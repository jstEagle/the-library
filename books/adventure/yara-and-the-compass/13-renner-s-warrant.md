## 13. Renner's Warrant

The rain came in off the sound like something with intent.

Sable had been watching it build all morning—a gray wall stacking itself over the western ridgeline, swallowing the peaks one by one—and had said nothing about it to Yara, because there was nothing either of them could do except tie the Kestrel down tighter and wait. They were holed up in a slough off the main channel, a finger of black water threading back into alder and rotting cedar, the kind of place a boat could disappear if you didn't already know it was there. Sable knew it was there. Had camped in it once, eleven years ago, running medicine for a midwife whose name Sable had never learned and never asked. The rules held even in memory: never stay, never ask, never carry anything worth stealing.

The rules were failing. Everything worth stealing now fit inside an oilskin roll and Yara's stubborn skull, and both of them were known from Kestrel Bay to the falls portage.

Sable sat in the stern under the tarp with the salvaged radio across their knees, coaxing a signal out of a sky that wanted none of it. The set had come out of the floatplane's burned carcass smelling of char and solder, and it worked the way wounded things work—reluctantly, with complaint. Static hissed and rolled like surf. Every few minutes a voice surfaced through it, half-drowned, and every few minutes Sable caught the same words riding the interference:

*Warrant issued. Warrant issued. All stations.*

Yara was forward, mending the jib by feel more than sight, her injured shoulder propped against the mast step in a way that would have made a proper boatwright wince. She'd stopped wincing herself two days ago. That worried Sable more than the shoulder did. Pain you complain about is pain you're managing. Pain you go quiet around is pain that's settling in like a tenant.

"Radio's saying my name," Sable said.

Yara's needle paused. "It would."

"*Theft of navigational property. Flight without registry. Obstruction of a lawful salvage operation.*" Sable read the litany flatly, the way you'd read a grocery list, because inflection was a luxury. "*Subject considered armed. Subject considered desperate.*"

"They got the armed part wrong," Yara said. "You don't even carry a gun."

"That's the point." Sable turned the dial a hair and the static sharpened into a woman's voice, clipped and official, reading the same words over and over on a loop. "They're not describing me. They're building me. Armed and desperate is a man you can shoot on sight and sleep fine after."

The needle went still. "And me?"

Sable hesitated. It was a small hesitation, a quarter-second, but Yara heard it the way she heard a loose plank in a hull—by the way the sound changed.

"Sable. And *me*?"

"*Accomplice,*" Sable said. "*Apprehend if practicable.*"

The word hung in the wet air between them. *If practicable.* Three syllables of legal silk stretched over a very simple idea: nobody was going to risk anything bringing her in alive. A warrant wasn't law. Sable had learned that young, standing in the dooryard of a house that no longer existed while men in magistrate's gray read out papers that made theft into custody and custody into ownership. A warrant was permission. It was Renner signing his name to what he'd already decided to do, so that afterward, when anyone asked, there would be paper. Paper was how men like Aldous Renner kept their hands clean while other people's blood did the work.

*Accomplice, apprehend if practicable.* Yara Voss, daughter of Ede Voss, deceased prospector, cause of death officially listed as exposure. Sable had read the coroner's transcription off the radio three nights ago and understood, with a cold clarity that hadn't left since, exactly how much of this had been arranged before they ever launched the Kestrel.

"He wrote it before we ran the narrows," Yara said quietly. She'd followed the same arithmetic; Sable could see it landing behind her eyes. "Before Miller's Landing. Before any of it. He had the warrant drafted while we were still—" Her voice snagged. "While I was still sanding the hull. He'd already decided what I was."

"Yes."

"And there's nothing—" She stopped. Started again, angrier. "There's a circuit judge in Kestrel Bay. There are courts. There's a whole apparatus built for exactly this, for when a magistrate goes past the line—"

"There is," Sable said. "And every inch of it runs on paper too. Paper he writes. Paper his clerks file. Paper his inspectors stamp." Sable folded the antenna wire down with deliberate care, giving their hands something to do that wasn't shaking. "I spent eleven years believing the opposite of what I'm about to say, so hear it from someone who paid tuition. You cannot appeal to a system when the man hunting you *is* the system. Not from a slough, not with a warrant live, not with his launches on the water. Justice isn't a door you knock on, Yara. Out here, right now, justice is something you take. Like water in a drought. Like ground under a failing plane."

* * *

The launch came at dusk, in the rain, running dark.

Sable heard it first as a wrongness in the water—the slough's surface, which had been talking to itself all evening in small rain-voices, suddenly developed a long low swell that didn't belong to weather. Then the engine, throttled down to a murmur, a big diesel idling the way predators move. Sable put two fingers on Yara's wrist and pressed once. Yara set down the jib without a word and killed the lantern.

They lay flat in the bilge among the spare lines and the empty fuel cans and listened to the patrol launch feel its way along the slough mouth. A searchlight swept the alders, white and indifferent as judgment, dragging its circle across the water, the mudbanks, the hanging moss. It passed over the Kestrel twice. Both times the boat was only a boat—a tarped shape among tarped shapes, a dead thing among dead things. Sable breathed through their mouth, slow and shallow, and thought about nothing at all, because thinking has a texture and searchlight operators can sometimes feel it.

The light died. Voices carried across the water, distorted by rain—one voice doing most of the talking, official and unhurried, the voice of a man reading from a document because the document mattered more than whether anyone heard it.

*"By order of Magistrate Aldous Renner, circuit authority for the interior waters: the persons known as Sable Winterbourne—"*

Sable closed their eyes. Eleven years of a different name, a name worn smooth as river stone, gone in one sentence spoken to an empty slough.

*"—and Yara Voss are declared fugitive from process. Any officer or licensed party may effect arrest. Resistance may be met with necessary force."*

*Necessary force.* Four words that meant whatever the shooter needed them to mean.

A paper rustled. Somewhere a man laughed briefly at something someone else said, and then the launch backed water, swung its bow toward the channel, and thumped away into the rain with its light burning again, sweeping the banks, patient as a lighthouse that hated people.

They lay in the bilge until the engine sound was indistinguishable from rain. Then longer. The cold came up through the planks and into their bones and neither of them moved, because moving would have been an admission that the world had started again.

Finally Yara spoke, her voice muffled against the tarp. "He read it to the trees."

"He read it to whoever reports the trees," Sable said. "Every trader, every fish camp, every kid with a pair of binoculars between here and the sound just became his constabulary. We're not hiding from launches anymore. We're hiding from *eyes*, and eyes are everywhere and free."

"So what does a fugitive do?" Yara rolled onto her back. In the dark Sable couldn't see her face, only hear the controlled steadiness that was costing her something. "Run for the border? Give him the map? Sink the evidence and let the river keep his secrets?"

"No." Sable sat up slowly, and the tarp shed its collected rain all down their back, cold as a verdict. "We do the one thing a warrant can't survive. Warrants work on people who are alone. People with no witnesses, no allies, no story but the one the magistrate tells. So we stop being alone."

* * *

They talked until the rain eased near midnight, heads together under the tarp, voices below the level of the dripping alders.

The plan, such as it was, had been assembling itself in Sable's mind since the deckhand's warning at the fleet's wake—*he isn't after treasure, he's after the proof*—and it finished assembling now, in the dark, with a warrant on the water and six tides down to four. Renner had made them outlaws on paper. Fine. Paper cut both ways. Ede Voss had spent years assembling paper of his own—ledgers, letters, a signed order in Renner's hand—and that paper, read aloud in front of the right judge, wouldn't just dissolve a warrant. It would dissolve a magistrate.

But first they had to reach it. One tide to the sound. One tide at the wreck. And somewhere ahead, Corvane's remaining launch, Renner himself come upriver, and a fog-bound maze of islets where a holed hull and a shingle bar were waiting with Sable's name on them, though Sable didn't know it yet.

"We can't outrun them," Yara said. It wasn't despair; it was navigation. "Not with the fleet's fast launch behind us and the sound ahead. So we make them come to us where we choose."

"The wreck surfaces at extreme low water," Sable said. "So does everything else. Every bar, every reef, every rock that's been eating boats on that coast for a hundred years. Renner's pilots know the charts. What they don't know is that Ede Voss spent thirty years learning what the charts leave off." Sable touched the oilskin roll, dry inside its wrapping against their chest. "His notes aren't just tide tables. They're a map of everything the water hides. If we run his marks and they run theirs—"

"They hit bottom and we don't."

"If the river loves us. Which it doesn't. But it respects preparation, occasionally, grudgingly, the way it respects anything that refuses to drown."

Yara was quiet a moment. Then: "My father used to say that. About the river." Her voice did something complicated. "I used to think it was just talk. Old man's poetry. Now I'm betting both our lives on it."

"That's inheritance," Sable said. "It always arrives late and costs more than expected."

* * *

Near dawn the rain quit, and the world came back in pieces: the alders dripping, the slough smoking with mist, a kingfisher staking its claim somewhere upstream with a rattle like a stick dragged along fence wire. Sable stood the first watch while Yara slept—not easily, but actually, her breath finally finding a rhythm that wasn't arithmetic.

Sable watched the channel and thought about names.

*Winterbourne.* Their mother had chosen it, or rather had chosen to surrender to it, the way you surrender to a current—here is what the water will accept, here is who we can be without dying. Eleven years of it. Eleven years of ferrying medicine and secrets under a name that fit like borrowed clothing, of being no one in particular in a country full of people who'd learned that being someone was expensive. It had worked. It had been, if not peace, at least the shape peace makes when viewed from a distance.

And Renner had erased it with one sentence read to an empty slough. Not because the name mattered—he didn't know it was false, or didn't care—but because erasure was simply what he did. He had done it to a village. He had done it, in his fashion, to Ede Voss, filing a murder under *exposure*. He had done it to Sable's family, turning land into ledger entries and a brother into a footnote, and he had done it all with paper, always paper, the clean white weapon that never stains the hand.

Well. Sable looked down at the sleeping girl who was becoming, tide by tide, something like a partner, and at the oilskin roll with its cargo of ledgers and letters and one signed order that was going to hang a magistrate or get them both shot in the reeds.

Paper, then. If that was the weapon, they'd take it up. They'd carry it into the sound and hold it up in the light where everyone could read it, and let Renner discover what every pilot learns eventually: that documents, like aircraft, are only trustworthy at the moment of commitment, and that the ground always gets a vote.

The kingfisher rattled again. Downriver, faint beneath the morning sounds, a diesel engine turned over—big, patient, coming on.

Sable put a hand on Yara's shoulder. "Wake up," they said. "One tide left. Time to go be criminals."
