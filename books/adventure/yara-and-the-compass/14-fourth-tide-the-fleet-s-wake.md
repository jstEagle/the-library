## 14. Fourth Tide: The Fleet's Wake

We heard the *Meridian* before we saw her.

That was the first wrong thing. A salvage flagship—Renner's flagship, Corvane's command—should have announced itself with engines, with winches, with the clatter of a hundred men making a hundred decisions. Instead there was only a sound like the river itself grieving: a low metallic groan rolling down the sound, rising and falling with the current, the way a hull cries when it's settling into the mud that will keep it.

"Kill the engine," Sable said, though I was already reaching for the throttle. We'd been sharing the wheel in shifts for two days, and I'd learned to read the small economy of Sable's movements—the way a hand lifted before a word was spoken, the way those gray eyes narrowed at the water before the water had done anything worth narrowing at.

The Kestrel drifted. Mist hung in rags off the headlands, and the tide was on the ebb, dragging us seaward with a patience that felt personal. Ahead, through a seam in the fog, the *Meridian* appeared all at once, the way large things do in fog—not approaching but simply *being there*, as though she'd been on the horizon our whole lives and we'd only just been allowed to see her.

She was aground. Hard aground, high on a gravel shoal at the mouth of the sound, listing to starboard at an angle that made my boatwright's stomach clench in sympathy. Her port side was exposed to the sky like a whale's flank, and her hull plating was peeled back in a long curl, bright metal gone dull already with river stain. Her name was painted across the bow in letters tall enough to read from a mile off, and someone had taken an axe to them. The *M* was gone. The *e* and the *r* were gone. What remained was a smear of bare primer and the word *idian*—half a name, half a nothing.

"She didn't run aground," Sable said quietly. "She was *put* aground."

I brought the Kestrel in slow, along the shoal's outer edge, where the water still held six feet beneath her keel. Up close the flagship was worse. Her lifeboats were gone—every one, the davits empty and swinging. Her wheelhouse windows were shattered from the inside. And her fuel lines—Sable saw it before I did, and made a sound low in the throat, the sound of a pilot looking at a murdered engine.

"They opened her seacocks," Sable said. "All of them. From the inside. And they scuttled her on the one shoal in forty miles of river where she'd ground out soft instead of sinking." A pause. "Somebody aboard knew these waters."

"Somebody aboard wanted her here," I said.

We came around her stern, and that was where we found the men.

Not men—what was left of them. A camp of wreckage on the gravel bar in the flagship's lee: a tarp strung between two oars, a cold fire ring, crates pried open and gutted. Three figures lay under the tarp, and they were not sleeping. A fourth sat propped against a crate with a blanket around his shoulders, and when he saw us he didn't reach for a weapon. He raised one hand instead, palm out, an old fisherman's gesture I knew from the Kestrel Bay docks: *no harm, no harm, I've got none left to give.*

Sable's hand was inside the coat anyway, on the grip of the pistol that lived there like a second heartbeat. "Easy," Sable said, to the man, to me, to the river. "Easy, all of us."

I brought us alongside the bar and held the Kestrel against the current with short bursts of the engine, my shoulder—still bound from the falls portage, still a bright wire of pain every time I turned the wheel—screaming its familiar complaint. The man watched us come with the flat patience of someone who had stopped believing anything good would happen and was merely curious what bad thing would arrive next.

He was young. That was the thing that caught in me. Twenty, maybe twenty-two, with a deckhand's rope-burned palms and a beard that couldn't decide to grow, and a wound along his ribs that someone had bound with strips of a salvage flag. He'd torn up his own ship's colors to make bandages. I didn't know yet what that meant. I would, before he finished talking.

"Water," he rasped. "Please. The river's right there and I can't—my hands—" He held them up. They were shaking, and not from cold.

Sable looked at me. I looked at the water jug lashed by the engine housing. Some decisions you don't make; they make themselves, and you find out afterward what they cost. I filled our tin cup and held it to his mouth while he drank like a man who'd been rationing himself for days, small controlled swallows, the discipline of the very thirsty.

"You're Corvane's crew," Sable said. It wasn't a question.

"Was." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "Name's Pell. Was Pell, anyway. Hard to say what I am now."

"How many made it off?"

"Eleven, when she grounded. The bosun and two others took the launch that first night—said they'd run for Renner's station and bring back help." His laugh was a terrible small thing, like a stone dropping down a well. "That was four days ago. Nobody came back. And the tide's been eating the ones too hurt to move, one a night. There's three of us left. There was three." He glanced at the tarp. "This morning there was three."

I looked at the tarp and then away. The fog had begun to lift off the water in long slow ribbons, and the sun came through it the color of old brass, and the *Meridian* groaned again above us, settling by inches into the gravel that would hold her until the river decided otherwise.

"Who scuttled her?" Sable asked. "Your own people opened her seacocks. I've seen the work. It was done from inside, with knowledge."

Pell closed his eyes. For a moment I thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up, the way the exhausted do. Then he said, "The bosun. And the chief engineer. They got orders. Radio orders, from Renner's station, in the magistrate's own cipher—Corvane showed us the codebook himself when we signed on, told us it meant we were working for the law, that the law would protect us." He opened his eyes and looked at Sable, and there was something in his face I recognized from my own mirror in the weeks after my father's letters stopped coming: the look of a person revising every assumption they'd ever made, all at once, and finding the new sum unbearable. "The orders said *ground her on the shoal, save what you can carry, and wait for the survey party.*"

"Was there a survey party?"

"There was a boat. Came at night, no lights. Four men. They took the strongroom charts, the pay chest, and the bosun." Pell's voice went very flat, the way voices go when they're carrying more weight than they can afford to show. "Then they took the launch's fuel. Left us the wounded, the tide, and forty miles of river."

Sable was silent a long moment. I watched my father's killer's employee—no. Not that. I watched a man who had been a gear in a machine I hated, sitting in the machine's wreckage, and I found that what rose in me wasn't satisfaction. It was something colder and more useful. Recognition. I'd spent my whole life believing the salvage fleet was a wolf and we were the rabbits, and here was the wolf opened up on a gravel bar, and inside it there were rabbits all the way down. Boys with rope-burned hands who'd signed on for wages and been spent like ammunition.

"Why?" I asked. "Why sink your own flagship? She was the biggest thing on this river. She was the whole operation."

Pell looked at me like I'd asked why rain was wet. "Because we'd seen the wreck's position, miss. Because we'd sounded the sound, and run the lines, and some of us had stood on the *Meridian*'s deck—on the old *Meridian*, the one under the water—and seen what was down there before the tide covered it again." He swallowed. "Corvane told us we were after cargo. Silk and plate, a fortune in the hold. But the bosun knew different. The bosun had a list. Names and dates and what was in the strongroom besides the pay chest. He read it off to us one night when Corvane was ashore, and he said, *boys, we're not salvage crew, we're a cleanup crew.*" His hands worked in his lap, twisting the blanket's edge. "Three days later the radio orders came, and the bosun was gone, and I understood which of us was cargo."

The fog had lifted enough now that I could see the whole sweep of the sound: the black-green water, the shingle bars, the far headland where the river narrowed toward the falls country we'd fought through. Somewhere out there was Corvane's remaining launch. Somewhere out there was Renner himself—I'd felt it since the falls, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. And somewhere under this water, at the bottom of the tide's long breathing, lay the wreck my father had spent twenty years finding and twenty years hiding, and whatever was in her strongroom that made a magistrate sink his own flagship to keep it from being read.

"You know why we're here," Sable said. It wasn't quite a question either.

"I know you're the ones the warrant's out for." Pell said it simply, without fear, and that absence of fear told me more than fear would have. "The whole fleet knew. *The bush pilot and the boatwright, six tides, don't let them reach the sound.* Corvane had us drilling on it—intercept maneuvers, like it was a war." He coughed, and the cough had a wet rattle in it that I didn't like at all. "Then the flagship went down and the war got called off, and now there's just the magistrate and whatever he wants down there. And you."

"And us," Sable said.

"Then listen to me." Pell leaned forward, and the movement tore at his bound ribs—I saw him go gray around the mouth and push through it, the way my father must have pushed through things for twenty years, and the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome and stayed. "You think Renner wants the cargo. Everyone thinks Renner wants the cargo. The cargo's nothing—silk rots, silver's just silver. What's down there, in the captain's strongroom, under the ballast, is *paper*." His voice dropped, and the river seemed to lean in to hear it. "The bosun's list had it. Manifests. Ledgers. A signed order, the bosun said, with a magistrate's seal, from before the wreck ever happened—papers that explain why a ship that was supposed to be in drydock in the capital went down in *this* river instead, with *this* cargo, and why no one ever came looking for her for twenty years." He looked up at Sable, then at me, and his young ruined face was absolutely steady. "He's not after treasure. He never was. He's after the evidence. And he'll burn this whole river to the waterline before he lets it surface."

* * *

We gave him what we had. It wasn't much—our stores had been counted twice since Miller's Landing, counted the way you count trust, in dwindling portions. But we left the tarp from the Kestrel's spare, and the last of the willow bark, and a full jug of water, and Sable splinted his two broken fingers with lath and tarred canvas, working with a quick economy that told me this wasn't the first wounded stranger Sable had patched in some fogbound nowhere. Sable didn't talk while working. Sable never talked while working. But when the splinting was done, Sable crouched so they were eye to eye, and said something too low for me to catch, and Pell nodded like a man receiving a sentence he'd already accepted.

I walked the gravel bar while they talked, because my shoulder ached and because I needed to be alone with what I was feeling, and what I was feeling had no name I wanted to say out loud.

The three under the tarp had been covered properly, at least. Someone had cared enough for that—Pell, I supposed, alone with the tide and the dying, doing the last office there was. At the edge of the camp, half-buried in gravel, I found a ship's bell, pried from its bracket and abandoned, the *Meridian*'s name cast into the bronze. I stood over it a long time. My father had taught me about bells, once, in the one summer before the silence started—*a bell's the voice of a ship, Yara; you can strip her masts and burn her decks, but as long as her bell rings, she's still somebody.* I crouched and worked the gravel away from it and heaved it into my arms, and my shoulder lit up like a struck match, and I carried it to the Kestrel anyway, because I decided that this ship would keep her voice, even if it was only to testify.

When I came back, Pell was watching the fog with an expression I couldn't read, and Sable was standing at the Kestrel's rail, looking at the water over the shoal, looking at the place where the river deepened and darkened and went down and down toward the thing we'd crossed a hundred miles of hell to find.

"Fourth tide," Sable said, to me, to the water. "Low water tomorrow, a little past dawn. After that, the spring ebb starts dying. Two more tides of any use at all, then she's gone under for the season."

"Then we're here first," I said.

"We're here first," Sable agreed, "and Renner knows it, and Corvane's launch is still on the water, and the man who sank his own flagship will not be gentle about the last tide." Sable turned, and in the flat pewter light I saw something in that weathered face I'd learned to read the way I read water: not fear. Calculation. The cold bright arithmetic of a pilot choosing a line through weather that has already killed better pilots. "Pell says the strongroom's under the ballast. Aft of the mast step. You'd need the low water just to reach her ribs, and a full day's work to get through what's above the papers."

"Then we start at dawn."

"Yes." Sable looked at Pell, and Pell looked back, and something passed between them that I only partly understood—two people who had both been used by the same machine, acknowledging it without a word. "He'll keep. The tide won't."

We pushed off as the fog closed again, and the last I saw of the *Meridian*'s grave was Pell raising his bandaged hand—*no harm, no harm*—and the broken-named flagship groaning once behind him, like a witness taking the oath.

I stood at the wheel and ran us up the sound into the deepening fog, and the bell sat at my feet on the deck boards, cold and heavy and certain. My father had known what was in that strongroom. He had carried it alone for twenty years, and died for it on a fogbound strip with a stranger as his only witness. And now the same man who'd killed him was coming up the river behind us, and the paper he wanted—the order with the seal, the reason a ship full of silk went down on purpose—was waiting under the fourth tide's lowest reach, and we had until dawn.

Sable appeared beside me at the wheel, silent, and after a while put two fingers briefly on my shoulder—the good one, the uninjured one, which from Sable was practically an embrace—and said, "Anchor in the narrows tonight. We'll want the dark."

The fog swallowed the headlands. Somewhere behind us, Renner's river was waking up.
