## 16. Fifth Tide: The Sound

The fog came off the sound like a held breath finally released, and Sable flew into it the way they did everything now—committed, no second chances waiting behind them.

They had left the falls portage at first light with Yara's shoulder strapped and her jaw set against the pain, and they had run the last stretch of river in a silence that wasn't empty but full, the way a drum is full before it's struck. The Kestrel rode low in the water. They'd burned through most of their fuel running the rapids below Miller's Landing, and what remained sloshed in the tank like the last of something precious. Sable had done the arithmetic three times and gotten the same answer each time: enough. Barely. If nothing went wrong.

Nothing ever went wrong. That was the joke Sable kept to themselves, the private humor of a person who had once watched a floatplane burn at anchor and learned that the world collected debts on its own schedule.

The river widened as they came down out of the hills, spreading into braided channels that wandered through gravel flats, and then it opened all at once into the sound—a long arm of gray-green sea reaching inland between shoulders of dark granite, forested to the waterline, the trees standing so close and so steep that their reflections made the surface look twice as deep as it was. Somewhere down that arm, under forty feet of cold water and a century of silt, lay the Meridian.

Somewhere down that arm too, if Ede's notes were right about the tides, was everything Renner had spent twenty years trying to drown.

"Smoke," Yara said.

Sable saw it a moment later—not chimney smoke, not campfire smoke, but the flat, industrial smear of a galley stove burning diesel, hanging in the still air above the tree line where the sound bent west. One plume. Maybe two, merged.

Corvane's launch. Already here.

Sable cut the engine and let the Kestrel drift, the current taking them sideways toward the eastern shore, and for a long moment there was only the sound of water lapping at the hull and Yara breathing carefully through the ache in her shoulder.

"He beat us here," she said.

"No." Sable studied the smoke, the way it leaned, the way it didn't move. "He's anchored. Look—no wake, no movement. He's holding position, which means he doesn't know exactly where the wreck is either. He knows the sound. He doesn't know the spot." Sable pulled Ede's oilskin from inside their jacket, unwrapped it just far enough to check the tide tables scrawled in the margin in the old man's cramped hand. "And he can't search until the fog lifts, because his depth sounder is worth nothing in water this full of kelp and rock anyway. He's waiting."

"For the tide."

"For the tide." Sable looked up at the sky, reading it the way they read weather, the way they read people—the cloud ceiling maybe six hundred feet and lowering, the fog thick enough to chew. "We have one extreme low left. Tomorrow morning, just after dawn. Tonight the water drops partway—not enough to raise her, enough to tell us where she isn't." Sable wrapped the map away again. "Which means tonight we find the spot, mark it, and tomorrow we're first over the rail while Corvane is still warming his coffee."

"And Renner?"

Sable had been avoiding that question since the patrol launch delivered the warrant two days ago, since the words *theft* and *resisting* turned them both into paper targets, free to be shot by any man with a badge or a grudge and no questions asked after. But the sound answered it for them. Because there, half-hidden in a cove on the western shore where the fog thinned for just a moment, Sable caught the gleam of glass—a window catching light—and beneath it, the long dark shape of a second vessel, larger than Corvane's launch, riding high and empty-looking at anchor.

A magistrate's barge didn't come upriver for salvage shares. Magistrates came to watch things end.

"He's here," Sable said quietly. "He came to see it done himself."

Yara's hand found the waterproof case strapped inside her jacket—Ede's journal, the ledgers' location memorized, the signed order they hadn't found yet but knew existed, waiting under the silt like a loaded gun. "Then we take it from him tomorrow."

"We survive tonight first."

* * *

They worked the eastern shore as the light failed, running the Kestrel engine-low through channels Sable charted by eye and memory and instinct, threading between islets that rose from the sound like the backs of sleeping animals. The fog never lifted; it only thinned and thickened, breathing with the tide, and Sable used it the way a thief uses a crowd—moving when it moved, freezing when it cleared.

Twice they heard the launch. Once it was just the idle rumble of a big diesel carrying across flat water, directionless, seeming to come from everywhere. The second time it was closer—a searchlight sweeping the fog in a slow white arc, the beam diffused into a glowing column that passed thirty yards off their bow and kept going. Sable killed the engine mid-wave and let them drift behind a granite shoulder, and Yara sat with her good hand pressed flat against the hull as if she could hold the boat silent by will alone, and the light crawled past, and the diesel faded, and neither of them breathed properly for a full minute afterward.

On the third islet they found what Sable was looking for.

It was nothing, to anyone else. A notch in the shoreline rock, a shingle bar reaching out like a finger, a stand of alders leaning over dark water. But Sable had spent thirty years reading country the way other people read faces, and the bar told a story: how the current split around it, how the silt had built up on the lee side, how the deep channel ran just off its tip before shoaling fast toward the eastern flats. Ede's notes described a wreck lying in the shadow of an islet with a split crown—"the twin-topped rock, east side, where the ebb runs hardest"—and when Sable raised their eyes from the map to the horizon, there it stood, two miles south, a low islet with two humps like a crouching animal, barely visible in the dying light.

"There," Sable said.

Yara followed their gaze. "That's it? That little thing?"

"That little thing has been hiding a hundred years of evidence under its skirt." Sable eased the Kestrel forward, engine at a whisper. "We anchor in the lee of the bar tonight. At slack water I'll drop a weighted line and sound the bottom. If Ede's right, we'll hit timber."

They were four hundred yards from the bar when the fog tore open.

It happened the way fog does—no warning, just a rift widening overhead as some upper wind found a seam, and moonlight poured through it, silvering the whole eastern half of the sound at once. And in that sudden, terrible clarity, Sable saw two things.

The first was the islet with the split crown, dead ahead, exactly where Ede had promised.

The second was Corvane's launch, coming around the southern point of it, a quarter mile out, running dark except for a single red running light, heading straight for the same stretch of water.

For one frozen heartbeat, boat and launch regarded each other across the silvered water like two animals surprised at a kill.

Then the launch's searchlight snapped on.

"Down!" Sable slammed the throttle wide and hauled the wheel hard to starboard, and the Kestrel leapt forward as the white beam slashed the water where they'd been. The night exploded into noise—the diesel roar of the launch winding up, a voice shouting across the water, the crack of something that might have been a rifle or might have been the sound of the world ending, and then another crack, definitely a rifle, and a bullet slapped the water ten feet astern with a sound like a fish hitting the surface wrong.

Sable flew the boat the way they flew planes—by feel, by memory, by the animal brain that had kept them alive through twenty years of lawless interior. Islet to port. Shoal water ahead where the current fanned out white over a gravel tongue. Searchlight swinging back, blinding, turning the whole world the color of bone—

"Rock!" Yara screamed.

Sable saw it a half-second too late to avoid it cleanly and a half-second early enough to not die. They threw the wheel over, and the Kestrel's port bow struck the sweep rock a glancing blow that rang through the hull like a church bell, and then they were past it, engine screaming, running blind into the fog bank on the far side of the islet with the searchlight behind them and the sound of pursuit filling the night.

Behind the fog wall, Sable throttled back to a crawl and listened.

The launch's engine idled somewhere south of them, searching. The searchlight probed the fog in slow sweeps, finding nothing but glowing columns of moisture. Voices carried across the water, angry, confused, calling positions to each other. And underneath it all, faint but unmistakable, came a third sound—one Sable had been dreading since the sweep rock rang the hull.

Water. Moving where it shouldn't. In a rhythm.

Sable lifted the floorboard. The bilge was taking water—not fast, not a gush, but steady, seeping through a seam that the rock's kiss had sprung along the port garboard strake. Not holed. Cracked. Cracked could be managed. Cracked could be pumped.

Cracked could also open.

"We can't run anymore tonight," Sable said quietly. "Every minute at speed works that seam wider. We beach her, patch what we can patch, and pray."

"Pray to who?" Yara's voice was shaking—with fear or fury or exhaustion, Sable couldn't tell, maybe all three. "The father who got us here? The magistrate who wants us dead?"

"To whoever's listening." Sable steered for the shingle bar, the one safe thing in a night full of unsafe things, and felt the keel grind up onto gravel with a shudder that ran through the whole boat and through everyone in it. "Because tomorrow morning, one of us is going to be first over that rail down there, and I intend for it to be us."

* * *

They worked by flashlight shielded under a tarp, mending what could be mended. Sable drove oakum into the sprung seam with a wooden caulking mallet, packing it tight, smearing it with tallow from their stores, while Yara bailed with a bucket and held the lantern and told Sable, in a voice gone flat and calm the way voices go when people are past panic, about the dream she'd had the night before—her father standing on a dock in good light, saying something she couldn't hear, smiling like a man who finally understood something too late to use it.

"Do you think he knew?" she asked. "When he gave you the map. Do you think he knew he was handing us a death sentence?"

"I think," Sable said, driving another wedge of oakum home, "he knew he was handing you the only weapon he had left. Men like Ede don't die easy because they lived easy. They die hard because they held on until the handoff." Sable sat back, wiped their hands, and looked at the seam. It would hold. It might hold. "Get some sleep. I'll take first watch."

"You haven't slept in two days."

"Neither have you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." Sable settled against the gunwale with the rifle across their knees and the fog pressing in around the little island of tarp-light like a wall. "Sleep. Tomorrow we finish what your father started."

She slept, eventually, curled against the gear with her bandaged shoulder rising and falling, and Sable watched the fog and listened to the water and thought about landings. About how every landing, eventually, comes down to the same moment—the moment you stop flying and commit to the ground, knowing you can't take it back, knowing the ground will do what it does whether you're ready or not.

Tomorrow at dawn, the tide would go out farther than it had gone in six months. The Meridian's ribs would break the surface for the length of one breath of ocean. And somewhere out in that fog, a man who had destroyed two families—one with fire, one with paper—was waiting to see what the water gave up.

Sable checked the rifle. Checked the bilge. Checked the sky.

One tide left. Everything on the line.

Out in the darkness, soft as a confession, the launch's engine started up again—and this time, it wasn't searching.

It was heading straight for the shingle bar.
