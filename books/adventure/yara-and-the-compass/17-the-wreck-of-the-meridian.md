## 17. The Wreck of the Meridian

The tide began to leave at first light, and it left like a confession—slowly, then all at once.

Yara felt the change in the Kestrel before she saw it in the water. The hull shifted on its mooring line, swung a few degrees east, and the current that had been worrying them all night suddenly slackened, as if the sound itself had exhaled. She had been awake since the small hours, sitting in the stern with Ede's compass open on her knee and her father's journal closed on top of it, watching fog come off the water in slow gray ribbons. Sable slept badly in the bow, one hand curled around the salvaged radio like a talisman, and Yara had not had the heart to wake them. Let them have another hour of not knowing what was coming. She would carry the knowing alone a little longer. It was, she was learning, a family inheritance.

But when the light came up enough to read the water, she woke them anyway.

"Sable." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Look."

Sable came up on one elbow, hair matted, eyes going from her face to the water and back with the quick economy of someone who had learned never to waste a glance. Then they followed her pointing finger, and Yara watched something move across their face that she had never seen there—not fear exactly, not hope either, but the place where those two things met and cancelled each other out into pure attention.

Where there had been open water yesterday, black shapes were breaking the surface.

At first they looked like driftwood, like the ribs of some drowned animal too large to name. But as the tide dropped further—as the sound gave up what it had been holding for sixty years—the shapes resolved into timber. Great curved timbers, silvered and furred with weed, rising out of the green water in rows like the skeleton of a cathedral that had fallen into the sea. Barnacles crusted them in white patches. Gulls were already landing on the highest ones, screaming their ownership.

"The Meridian," Yara said. The name felt strange in her mouth, half prayer and half accusation. Sixty years down, and here were its bones, surfacing twice a day for anyone patient or desperate enough to be waiting.

"Her ribs," Sable said quietly. They were fully awake now, moving through the boat with the practiced silence Yara had come to think of as their native language. "She broke up on the bar before she went down—that's why the charts called her unrecoverable. Everyone assumed she scattered." A pause. "Ede didn't assume."

"No," Yara said. "He measured."

They worked in the rhythm they had built over five tides, which was to say without speaking much, because everything that needed saying had been said in the quarrels and the quiet hours and the letter Yara still carried against her spine like a second heartbeat. Sable checked the tide tables scrawled in Ede's cramped hand and pronounced the window: four hours, maybe less if the wind backed around. Four hours of low water. Four hours in which the wreck's belly would stand open to the air, and in which anyone else who knew the secret could also reach it.

Yara shipped the oars. The Kestrel's engine stayed cold—they couldn't risk the noise—and she pulled them across the flat-calm sound toward the wreck's exposed flank, each stroke sending rings outward across water so still it seemed embarrassed by the disturbance. The fog was burning off in patches. That worried her more than she said. Fog was cover. Sun was exposure. And somewhere behind them, or ahead of them, or both, Renner's men were doing arithmetic of their own.

* * *

Getting aboard the Meridian was like climbing a whale's corpse.

The hull lay heeled over on its starboard side, and its port rail stood clear of the water at an angle that made the whole structure look like it was mid-collapse, frozen in the act of falling forever. Yara went over the side into thigh-deep water and hauled herself up the weed-slick timbers, finding handholds where the planking had sprung apart, and for a moment—straddling the rail, dripping, the whole drowned ship sloping away beneath her—she felt the weight of the thing. Not just the wood and iron. The weight of everyone who had gone down with it, whose names were in the ledgers they were about to steal back, whose village ash was under her fingernails from two tides ago.

"You all right?" Sable had followed, moving over the wreck with a sure-footedness that seemed almost disrespectful, except that Yara had learned it wasn't. Sable touched things carefully. Always had.

"It's bigger than I thought," Yara said. "The charts made it sound like a rowboat."

"Charts lie by omission. Same as people." Sable crouched, tapping the deck planking, listening. "Cargo hold will be forward. Strongroom aft, under the captain's cabin—if she's laid out like the coastal packets of her day. Ede's notes put the cache beneath the salvage, which means we go through whatever's left of the cargo first."

"What was she carrying?"

"Everything worth stealing sixty years ago." Sable's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Which is why nobody ever looked past it."

They went down into the ship.

Inside, the air was cold and mineral, thick with the smell of wet rot and old salt, and the darkness had texture—Yara kept reaching for the lantern before remembering, adjusting instead to the gray-green light that fell through gaps in the hull above. The cargo hold was a forest of broken crates and shifted ballast, everything furred green, everything softened by decades into shapes that only suggested what they'd been. Here the curve of a barrel stave. There the corner of something that might once have been furniture. The salvage fleets of three generations had written the Meridian off precisely because this chaos looked like ruin rather than riches.

Ede Voss had understood that the best hiding place in the world is inside something everyone else has already given up on.

Yara found the strongroom hatch aft, under a collapse of bulkhead, and together they levered it open with the pry bar Sable carried everywhere the way other people carried knives. The door beneath was iron, rusted to lace in places but solid at the lock, and Sable studied it for a long moment before simply putting the pry bar between door and frame and leaning their whole weight into it. The metal shrieked—a sound that went out through the wreck and out through the water beyond it, and both of them froze, listening, hearts hammering, waiting for it to be answered.

Nothing answered. Yet.

The door gave. Beyond it, a small square room, dry-ish, silted ankle-deep, and stacked with the salvage that had been the Meridian's public face: coin chests split and empty, their contents long since scattered by time or tide. Yara's stomach dropped. For one horrible moment she thought Ede had been wrong, or worse, that someone had beaten him here decades ago—

"Beneath," Sable said. "He said beneath. Help me move these."

They hauled the empty chests aside, and there, set into the deck beneath them, was a slab of lead sheeting, and riveted to the lead, a brass plate stamped with a mark Yara knew better than her own heartbeat now: the compass rose Ede had scratched into every map, every note, every page of the journal she had read aloud by lamplight until her voice went hoarse.

Her hands shook so badly she could barely work the plate's hidden catch. Sable reached to help and she shook her head. This part was hers. Whatever else happened, this part belonged to the daughter.

The lead came up. Beneath it, wrapped in tarred canvas sealed with pitch, was a box.

* * *

They opened it in the gray light of the strongroom, and the past came out breathing.

Ledgers first—three of them, fat with pages, their spines cracked but their contents legible, ink preserved by the pitch and the dark. Yara opened the first one with fingers that wouldn't quite obey her, and the columns of figures swam, and then sharpened, and then became unbearable. Names. Payments. Dates. Entries in a clerk's neat hand recording disbursements to men whose descriptions matched the burners, the evictors, the enforcers—and beside each entry, initials. A.R.

Aldous Renner. Signing off on arson the way other magistrates signed off on road repairs.

"There's more," Sable said, and their voice had gone very quiet, very flat, the voice Yara had learned meant they were standing on the edge of their own history looking down. "Letters."

She took them. Dozens, bundled, tied with wire. She read the top one and had to stop, press her fist against her mouth, breathe through her nose until the shaking stopped. It was a report—from a land agent to the magistrate's office, describing the clearing of a settlement, using words like *resolved* and *concluded*, describing people the way you'd describe a fence line. Dated eleven days after the night the village in Ede's journal had burned.

And beneath the letters, wrapped separately in oilcloth, a single sheet of heavy paper, official stock, bearing the seal of the magistrate's office and a signature Yara recognized from the warrant that had named them outlaws.

It was an order. Signed in Renner's own hand. Authorizing, instructing, commanding—the language was bureaucratic, deniable, but the meaning was naked—the removal of the occupants of Harrow's Reach by any means necessary, costs to be borne against future salvage recovery.

*Any means necessary.* Yara knelt in the silt of a dead ship and held the paper that proved a living man had ordered a village full of children erased from the world, and she thought of the toy she had found in the ashes—a tin whistle, small enough for a six-year-old's hand—and she understood finally, completely, why her father had spent twenty years being a stranger to her. He hadn't been carrying guilt. He'd been carrying this. He had known that the man who signed this order would burn a second village without blinking if it meant keeping it buried, and he had loved her too much, in his wordless sideways way, to let her name appear anywhere near it.

"Dad," she whispered, to no one, to the dark, to a man dead on a fogbound strip because he'd finally tried to move the weight. "You stubborn old fool. You should have told me. I'd have helped you carry it."

Sable's hand settled on her shoulder. Said nothing. Some silences are the loudest thing in a room.

"We have it," Yara said at last, wiping her face with the back of her wrist, salt water and fresh indistinguishable. "We actually have it. Ledgers, letters, the order itself—it's not suspicion anymore, it's his signature. No judge in the territory can look at this and unsee it."

"Yes," Sable said. And then, because Sable had survived this long by hearing things in the world before the world announced them: "Get it packed. Now. Quickly."

Yara heard it a half-second later. Oars. More than one set. Coming across the water with the crisp military cadence of men who rowed for a living, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the cough of an engine being kept just below idle.

Renner's men. Closing from both shores, exactly as Ede's final notes had feared—because of course Renner had known about the strongroom all along; he'd spent sixty years making sure no one else found it, and the moment Ede's map surfaced, the trap had begun closing. The wreck wasn't a treasure. It had never been a treasure. It was bait, and they had swum straight into it with the evidence in their hands, and now the sound was filling with boats.

Through a gap in the hull Yara saw them: two launches, one from the eastern islets, one from the shingle spit where the Kestrel lay beached and holed, converging on the wreck's exposed ribs like wolves on a carcass. Men in dark oilskins. Rifles slung across backs, not carried—which meant they expected compliance, or didn't care whether they got it.

"How many?" Sable asked, already moving, already thinking, eyes sweeping the strongroom like a man memorizing an exit.

"Two boats. Eight, ten men." Yara shoved ledgers, letters, the order into the waterproof case, lashed it shut, cinched its straps. "And the tide's turning, Sable. In an hour this whole ship goes under again—with us in it if we're still aboard."

"Then we're leaving through the water, not over the rails." Sable's grin, when it came, was the wildest thing Yara had ever seen on that careful face. "There's a flooded corridor on the port side, runs out under the waterline. We go in with lungs full and come up outside the wreck, behind their boats. Can you swim in the dark?"

"I grew up in a boatyard."

"Good girl." Sable hefted the pry bar. "Take a breath you'll remember. We're about to need it."

Above them, boots hit the wreck's ribs—one, two, a dozen—and a voice Yara had last heard across the water of a magistrate's launch called out, smooth and carrying, offering terms to the empty air:

"You have the papers. Come out, and no one drowns today."

Yara looked at Sable. Sable looked at the black water flooding the corridor's mouth.

Together, they dove.
