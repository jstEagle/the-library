## 19. The Choice at Low Water

**SABLE**

The tide had begun to turn.

Sable felt it before they saw it—a change in the sound's breathing, the water in the channel below the wreck's ribs going from glass-still to a slow, muscular shiver. Six hours of low water, and they'd spent four of them inside the *Meridian*, hauling ledgers and letters and that one signed order in Renner's hand out of Ede Voss's cache while Renner's men closed along both shores like a fist making itself.

Now the fist was nearly shut.

From the wreck's broken stern, Sable could see them: lanterns bobbing on the eastern shingle, maybe a dozen men working down toward the waterline where Renner's launch sat anchored in the last of the exposed channel. On the western shore, Corvane's remaining launch—what was left of it after the fleet ate itself—rode at anchor with only two figures visible on deck. The fog was coming in off the sound in ragged banks, thickening by the minute, and Sable thought: *fog is a door, if you're small enough to fit through it.*

But they weren't small. They were two people on a dead ship with a hull full of water and a hundred pounds of paper that mattered more than their lives.

"Yara." Sable kept their voice low, though the wind off the sound would have swallowed anything louder than a shout anyway. "How much did you get lashed down?"

"Everything. The ledgers, the letters, the order." Yara cinched the last strap of the waterproof case across her back, her injured shoulder held stiff against her side, her face gray with pain she wasn't spending time on. "It rides or I ride. Both."

"I know." Sable looked at the case a moment longer than necessary. Everything Ede Voss had died carrying. Everything Renner had burned a village for, murdered a prospector for, turned the law inside out for—all of it wrapped in oilskin and riding on the spine of a boatwright who should have been sanding planks in Kestrel Bay. "Listen to me now, because there isn't time to say this twice."

Yara turned. In the failing light her eyes were the same pale gray as her father's, and Sable had to look away from that, just for a second, because grief could wait but the tide couldn't.

"The hull," Sable said. "The *Meridian*'s hull. When we came through the strongroom I put my hand on the frames and they're gone—rotted through, every one below the third strake. She's been holding herself up on habit and the mud. And she's sitting on the shelf right above the deep channel." Sable pointed, tracing the line: the wreck's heeled flank, the shelf of gravel beneath it, and beyond, the black trench of the channel where Renner's launch lay at anchor, forty yards of open water between. "Ede knew it. It's why he cached the evidence here. He didn't just hide it in a wreck—he hid it in a trap."

Understanding moved across Yara's face like weather. "You want to bring her down."

"On top of his launch. On top of his men if they're under her when she goes." Sable felt the old cold clarity settle in, the pilot's arithmetic, altitude versus airspeed, weight versus lift—but this time the numbers were lives, and some of them would be boys with lanterns who'd never chosen any of this. "The tide's coming back. In an hour the channel will have four feet of water over the shelf. If we cut her loose now, while she's still light and the current's still running out, she'll slide. Gravity does the rest."

"And us?"

"We swim for the shingle bar. The *Kestrel*'s holed but she's beached above the high-water mark, and I can patch her enough to float if the fog holds and nobody's shooting at us." Sable said it evenly, the way you read a forecast, and watched Yara do the same math and arrive at the same place: *nobody's shooting at us* was doing a great deal of work in that sentence.

Yara looked west, toward Corvane's launch, then east, toward the lanterns. "Renner's men will hear her go. The whole sound will hear it."

"Yes."

"Then we'd better not be here when they finish counting." Yara pulled the knife from her belt—the one Ede had carried, the one she'd taken off his body without ever saying so—and handed it to Sable hilt-first. "You know ships better than you know swimming. Cut her free. I'll get us to the bar."

For a moment neither of them moved. The sound breathed around them, filling, patient. Then Sable took the knife, and the moment of no return passed so quietly that neither of them marked it—that was the thing about such moments, Sable thought later; they never announce themselves. You only recognize them by what you can never go back to.

* * *

**YARA**

The *Meridian* groaned like something waking from a long sleep, and Yara's hands shook as she worked the sea-cock lever loose from its crusted housing.

Her father had known this ship. That was the thought that kept surfacing while she worked, absurd and enormous: Ede Voss had stood where she was standing, forty years younger, with salt on his hands and no idea what his map would cost. He'd found the wreck, stripped the strongroom of its salvage, buried the evidence beneath it, and left a compass and a daughter behind him—one to point the way, one to pay for it. She'd hated him for six tides. Now, prying at a rusted fitting in the belly of a dying ship while armed men closed in from two shores, she found the hatred had quietly become something else. Not forgiveness exactly. More like inheritance. He'd left her the work because she was the only one left who could do it.

"Sable!" she called into the dark of the hold. "The seacock's seized!"

"Hit it sideways, not straight—she'll give." A pause, then Sable's voice again, closer, breathless from the cutting. "I'm through the lashings on the seaward side. Two more turns and she's ours."

Yara swung the knife's pommel against the lever. Once. Twice. On the third blow the crust cracked and the lever gave with a shriek of metal, and cold water began to gurgle somewhere below her feet—not flooding them, not yet, just finding its old path home, whispering through the ship's opened veins. Above them, through the shattered deck, the sky was going the color of wet slate, and the fog was moving in earnest now, pouring over the eastern headland in a slow white flood.

Somewhere across the water, a voice shouted. Then another. Lantern-light stuttered on the eastern shore.

"They've seen the fog shift," Sable said, sliding down the canted deck toward her, knife still in hand, face streaked with rust and bilge. "Or they've seen us. Doesn't matter. We're done here."

"We're done when she moves." Yara grabbed the last lashing herself, hauling on the rope with her good arm, teeth gritted. The *Meridian* shifted—an inch, a groan, a sigh of gravel beneath her keel—and settled again. "Come *on*," Yara whispered, and she didn't know if she was talking to the ship or to her father or to herself.

Then the tide decided for them.

It came as a long swell rolling up the sound ahead of the fog, the first true push of the flood, and it slid under the wreck's exposed flank and lifted her the way a hand lifts a sleeping child. The gravel sang. The hull rolled a degree, two degrees, and Yara felt the whole vast rotten mass of her begin to travel—slowly, majestically, gathering the terrible patience of heavy things.

"Now! Off! *Off!*"

They went over the seaward rail together, into water that hit like a slap, black and shocking after the ship's stale dark. Yara swam with the case strapped to her back, its buoyancy fighting her, dragging her half-sideways, and she kicked hard for the shingle bar where the *Kestrel* lay beached like a stranded bird. Behind her she heard Sable surface, gasp, strike out beside her.

And behind *that*—behind them both—came the sound she would hear for the rest of her life.

The *Meridian* came off the shelf all at once. Forty years of rot let go in a single tearing roar, and the old ship slid down the slope of the channel like an avalanche given a hull, gathering speed as the flood tide pushed her, and Renner's launch—anchored, lit, her crew scrambling on deck at the noise—had nowhere to go and no time to go there. The impact was less a crash than a detonation of wood and water. The sound went white with spray. The concussion rolled across the channel and slapped Yara's back like a wave, and when she twisted in the water to look, the launch was already gone under the wreck's settling flank, masts and rigging folding, lanterns snuffed, men in the water screaming in the fog.

"*Kestrel!*" Sable's arm hooked her collar, hauling. "Don't look—swim—"

She looked anyway. She owed them that much, the drowning men. Some of them were swimming. Some were not. And on the western shore, torchlight bloomed—Corvane's launch, her engine turning over, her bow swinging out into the channel.

Toward the screaming.

* * *

**SABLE**

Corvane's launch ran into the wreckage with her searchlight blazing, and Sable, hauling themselves onto the shingle bar beside the *Kestrel*, understood three things in the space of ten seconds.

First: Brann Corvane was pulling Renner's men out of the water. Every one he could reach.

Second: that meant Corvane had done his own arithmetic sometime in the last hour, alone on his half-crewed launch, and arrived at the sum Sable had reached at the burned village, at the grounded flagship, at every stop on this river: Aldous Renner spent people the way other men spent matches, and the matchbox always believed it was the fire.

Third: the searchlight would find them next, and everything depended on what Corvane did in the moment it did.

"Get her afloat," Sable said, already dragging the patch kit from the *Kestrel*'s flooded cockpit. "Canvas, pitch, the seam plate—go."

Yara worked without answering, and Sable loved her a little for it, in the fierce impersonal way you love someone in a foxhole. The hull wound was a splintered gash below the waterline, ugly but honest, and the canvas patch went over it slick with pitch, held by the seam plate and prayer. The fog was a living thing now, swallowing the shores, swallowing the wreck, swallowing the cries from the water until they seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere. Somewhere in that whiteness, Renner himself was alive—Sable had seen his coat, his unmistakable upright silhouette, on the shingle before the fog took him. Alive, ashore, and for the first time in twenty years, *unarmed*. The law he'd wielded was a warrant and a patrol launch, and both of those were at the bottom of the sound.

The searchlight found them.

It came through the fog like the eye of God, white and total, pinning them to the shingle bar—two soaked figures kneeling around a patched boat, a waterproof case gleaming on one bent back. Sable rose slowly, hands open and empty, and put their body between the light and Yara.

"Brann Corvane!" Sable's voice cracked across the water. "You want the map? Come take it!"

The light held. The engine idled. Water lapped and men coughed and retched aboard the launch, the saved and the half-drowned. Then Corvane's voice came out of the fog, roughened, and it was not the voice of the harbor tavern or the intercepting launch. It was tired. It was the voice of a man reading his own ledger and not liking the totals.

"You sank his boat."

"His boat sank itself. I just stopped holding it up."

A long silence. In it, Sable heard the sounds that mattered: oars, moaning, a man being hauled over a gunwale. Corvane was still picking people out of the water. Renner's own magistrate's guard, fished from the sound by the salvager Renner had bled dry.

"He promised me a third," Corvane said at last. "Twenty years I ran his rivers, and tonight he ordered my crew to stand on that wreck's deck and hold it steady while his divers went down. Stand *on* it. With the tide coming." A pause, and something in the voice hardened into iron. "He told me they were ballast."

Sable said nothing. There was nothing to say that the fog and the drowned weren't already saying.

The searchlight swung away from them, deliberately, sweeping wide across the empty channel—pointing, Sable realized with a jolt, at nothing, at fog, at a corridor of darkness left open like a door.

"East channel's clear for six minutes," Corvane called, already turning his bow back toward the wreck site, toward the crying in the water. "After that, my conscience comes back, and I hear it's got a warrant in its pocket. Go."

Sable moved. No hesitation, no ceremony—the moment of no return had already happened back in the hold, and this was just the ground rushing up to meet a committed landing. They shoved the *Kestrel* off the bar together, Yara at the tiller, Sable hauling the starter cord, and the little engine caught on the third pull like a heart remembering its work.

As the fog folded around them, Sable looked back once. Corvane's searchlight swept the water, patient and methodical, pulling Renner's drowning empire out of the sound one man at a time—and far off on the eastern shingle, small and upright and utterly alone in the white blindness, Magistrate Aldous Renner stood watching his wreck, his launch, his law, and his future all go down together into the rising tide.

He raised one hand. Not a threat. Something more like a summons—or a curse.

Then the fog took him too, and Sable turned forward, and the river ran dark and endless ahead of them, and the evidence rode dry against Yara's spine, and there was nothing left in the world but sixty miles of night water between them and justice.

"Hold her true," Sable said.

Yara held her true.
