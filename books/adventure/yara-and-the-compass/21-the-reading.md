## 21. The Reading

Kestrel Bay had never been quiet, but I had never heard it like this.

The harbor was full past sense—fishing boats rafted three deep off the government wharf, the Kestrel among them, her patched hull riding low and proud, renamed now, the letters *Meridian's Daughter* still smelling of fresh paint. Every deck that could hold people did. People stood on the breakwater. People stood on the roof of the customs shed. Someone had strung lanterns from the mast of a derelict schooner so the light fell gold across the water, and the whole bay seemed to be holding its breath the way water holds a hull: patiently, and with enormous force.

I stood at the edge of the crowd with my hood up, an old habit, and felt the hood for the first time as what it was—a shroud.

The circuit judge had come up from the coast on the mail steamer, a small grey woman named Judge Oyelaran with a voice like a cleaver and a reputation, the harbor master told me, for being the one magistrate in the territory Renner had never managed to buy, bribe, or bury. Renner had tried all three, the harbor master said, and Judge Oyelaran had fined his office for wasting her time. She had set up her bench on the wharf itself, a folding table, a clerk, a lamp, because she said justice that hid indoors was justice that had something to hide.

Yara stood at the table. She wore her father's coat, the oilskin one, too big in the shoulders, and she had the waterproof case open in front of her like a heart laid out for surgery.

I had not wanted to be there. I want to be honest about that, since honesty was the whole business of the evening. I had spent thirty years learning how to disappear—how to land at a strip, unload, take payment, and be a rumor before the dust settled. How to be the pilot people described as *tall, maybe, flew a blue-and-white, hard to say.* Invisibility was not cowardice. It was a craft, and I had been good at it, and I had built a life on it the way you build a cabin on permafrost: carefully, knowing the ground would take it only if you never asked too much of it.

But Yara had looked at me that morning, in the grey light of the boatshed, and said, "You carried the map. You carried me upriver. You're carrying this the last mile or I'll put you in the boat myself." And I had learned, somewhere between the narrows and the sound, that when Yara Voss decided a thing, arguing was like arguing with the tide. You could do it. You would lose.

So I stood at the edge of the crowd, hood up, and listened to the ledgers being read.

* * *

They read them by lantern light, page by page, because Judge Oyelaran refused to summarize. "A crime summarized is a crime softened," she said, and the crowd murmured agreement, and the clerk's voice went on and on into the cold.

The ledgers were Ede Voss's salvage—papers from the strongroom of the *Meridian*, forty years under salt water and still legible in their oilcloth, and beside them Ede's own journal, and beside that the thing that stopped the harbor's breathing: a signed order in Aldous Renner's hand, authorizing the clearing of the settlement at Graywater Bend. *Clearing.* That was the word he had chosen. As if the village had been furniture.

The clerk read the names of the ledgers' entries. Payments. That was the worst of it, I thought—not the burning, which everyone had always whispered about, but the bookkeeping. Renner had paid for it. Line items. *Disbursement, Graywater operation.* He had paid the men who carried the lanterns, paid the man who drove the boat, paid a constable to look at the water and see nothing. And every payment had a signature, and every signature was read aloud, and with each name I watched someone in the crowd go very still, because everyone in that harbor knew someone.

An old woman near me held her son's arm with both hands. When the clerk read the name of the man who had fired the first building, she said, quietly, "That's the one who carried my mother out. He carried her out and then he went back for the pay." Her son didn't answer. There wasn't an answer.

I had spent the whole run upriver thinking of the evidence as a weapon. Something you pointed. But hearing it read, I understood it was something else. It was a mirror held up to a whole territory, and the territory flinched, and then—this was the part I had not expected—it did not look away.

Yara read the journal herself. Her voice was steady except once, at the entry from Graywater Bend, the one where Ede wrote that he had stood on the ridge with his hands empty and his mouth shut, and that he intended to spend the rest of his life paying for the shut mouth. She stopped there. She looked out at the crowd, at the old woman, at the handful of grey-haired people scattered through the harbor who had come down from the interior valleys, survivors and children of survivors, and she said, "He couldn't say it to me. So he said it to this. I'm saying it for him now. I'm sorry it took forty years."

The old woman looked up at her for a long moment and then nodded, once, the way you nod at a landing done well.

* * *

The warrant came up somewhere near midnight.

Judge Oyelaran held the paper Renner had issued—the one naming me for theft, naming Yara as accomplice, the paper that had made us lawful targets from the narrows to the sound—and she read it aloud with the flat delivery of a woman reading a grocery list, which was exactly the right way to read it, because it had been exactly that kind of document. A shopping list for two lives.

Then she set it down on the folding table, took up a pen, and wrote across it in strokes I could see from where I stood, large and unhurried. She held it up.

"Void," she said. "Issued under color of office by a magistrate under investigation for murder, arson, and the perversion of justice. There is no warrant. There was no lawful warrant. If any officer of this territory acts on Renner's paper after tonight, they act as a private criminal, and they will be treated as one." She looked out over the harbor. "Sable Winterbourne. Yara Voss. You are cleared. Come forward if you wish."

I did not wish. My feet had opinions of their own, and their opinion was the tree line, the strip, the fog, gone. But Yara turned and found me in the crowd—found me the way she always did, as if my hood were made of glass—and she didn't call my name. She just held out her hand, palm up, the same gesture she'd used on the shingle bar with the hull coming apart under us, and I crossed the crowd the way you cross a current, committing, and took it.

The hood came down. I don't even remember deciding. It was just suddenly down, and three hundred people looked at the face they had spent a week inventing stories about, and the stories all died at once, and what was left was just me: a bush pilot with river mud still under their fingernails and no place left to hide.

It should have felt like drowning. It felt, instead, like setting something heavy down after so long you'd forgotten you were carrying it.

* * *

The restitution came last, and it came in Oyelaran's cleaver voice, and it was not enough, and everyone knew it was not enough, and she said so.

"The territory will return the Graywater lands to the surviving families. The salvage rights Renner granted himself and his partners are revoked. Seizures going back twenty years will be reviewed—every one of them." She paused, and the lantern light moved on her face. "I will not insult you by calling this justice. Justice would be the village back, and the people in it. What this is, is the territory admitting what it did, in writing, with names. That is the floor. You build on floors."

The old woman stood up on the deck of the fishing boat where she'd been sitting and said, in a voice that carried across the water like a keel through still water: "Then we build."

And the harbor—three hundred people, fishermen and boatwrights and traders and the quiet grey survivors of places like Graywater Bend—made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life. Not a cheer. Something lower than a cheer, deeper in the chest, the sound a crowd makes when it decides something. The sound of a heading being chosen.

Renner himself, they told me later, was already in custody downcoast, taken off a coastal steamer trying to run with a case of gold and a face like a man reading his own ledger. Corvane had given the statement that put him there. The salvage captain had walked into the constabulary office in Miller's Landing, laid Renner's private correspondence on the desk, and said, "He was going to burn us like he burned the village. I'd rather be a witness than fuel." Brann Corvane, it turned out, had his own half of a map, and his own arithmetic of betrayal, and he had done the sums.

* * *

The crowd thinned toward dawn. Lanterns went out one by one. The harbor settled into that grey hush that comes before the fishing boats start up, and I stood at the rail of the *Meridian's Daughter* with a tin cup of coffee going cold in my hands, and Yara came to stand beside me, and for a while neither of us said anything, because the river had taught us both the value of silence that isn't empty.

"Your name was read out loud," she said finally. "In front of everyone."

"It was."

"How does it feel?"

I thought about it honestly, because she would have known a lie. It felt like the morning after a storm, when you walk out and count what's left. It felt like the permafrost finally shifting under a cabin I'd built too carefully. It felt like a promise kept—Ede's, mine, hers—and like the price of it, all at once.

"It feels like the ground," I said. "You commit to it, or you never land at all."

Yara looked at me, and then out at the water, where the first light was coming up the sound the way it had come up the sound on the sixth tide, gold on grey. "I found a strip outside town," she said. "Flat, drained, an old logging field nobody's using. I was thinking—" she turned the tin cup in her hands, suddenly awkward, suddenly seventeen years younger— "a pilot needs a base. And a boat needs a home port. And I was thinking they don't have to be in different places."

I looked at her. At Ede's coat on her shoulders. At the compass hanging at the wheel behind her, Ede's compass, its needle settling north with the patient certainty of a thing that had finally been pointed at something true.

"The mail run upriver needs doing," I said. "Medicine, letters. Settlements that haven't seen a supply in a year. And there are people in the valleys who'll want to know they can come home to Graywater."

"So that's a yes?"

"That's a heading," I said.

She smiled—the first smile I'd seen on her that wasn't grief wearing a disguise—and out past the breakwater the tide turned, and the harbor began, all around us, to wake.
