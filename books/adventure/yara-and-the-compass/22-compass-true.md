## 22. Compass True

**Sable**

The river at dawn was a ribbon of hammered tin, and Sable flew above it the way a hand rests on an old scar—lightly, knowing every ridge beneath.

Three weeks since Kestrel Bay. Three weeks since the harbor had roared with the reading of the ledgers, since the circuit judge's voice had cracked like ice going out in spring, since the name *Winterbourne* had been spoken aloud in daylight for the first time in fifteen years. Sable still flinched when strangers said it. But they said it kindly now, or warily, or not at all, and either way the sky did not fall. That was the strangest part. The sky simply went on being the sky.

Below, the *Meridian's Daughter* ran the long straight reach above Miller's Landing, her patched hull throwing a wake like a white stitch through grey cloth. Yara stood at the wheel with her collar up against the morning cold, one shoulder riding a little lower than the other—the portage injury had healed crooked, the way some things do—and Ede's compass hung on its brass bracket beside her, its needle steady as a held breath.

Sable rocked the floatplane's wings once, twice. Acknowledgment. *I see you.*

Yara lifted one arm without looking up. She never looked up anymore; she'd learned to read the plane's shadow on the water instead, the way sailors read clouds. It was a small thing, but small things were what trust was made of. Sable had spent a lifetime learning that the hard way.

The cargo behind the seats was nothing worth stealing, which was to say it was everything: penicillin for the mission at Two Rivers, a crate of schoolbooks wrapped in oilcloth, letters from mothers to sons upriver, a bag of coffee beans for old Marta who traded fuel at Miller's Landing and had, since the reading, taken to charging Sable half price and refusing to discuss it. No gold. No maps. No secrets worth a man's life. Just the ordinary freight of people who had decided, all of them, that the river belonged to them again.

Sable banked south over the narrows where the sweep rock hid just under the surface on anything but a flood tide. From the air it showed as a faint pale smudge, a drowned ghost waving. They'd run it at night once, in terror, with a tide gained and a fuel drum lost. Now it was just geography. That was what survival did—it turned your nightmares into landmarks.

The radio crackled. Yara's voice, dry as driftwood: *"You're drifting east, pilot. Daydreaming?"*

"Navigation by sentiment," Sable answered. "It's a recognized technique."

*"Recognized by whom?"*

"People who've earned it."

A laugh came back down the static, short and real, and Sable felt it land somewhere under the ribs, in the place where fear used to live. Fifteen years Sable had flown this country alone, invisible, unnamed, holding the throttle the way you hold a door closed against something at your back. And now there was a boat below with a crooked-shouldered woman at the wheel and a dead man's compass pointing true, and the door was open, and whatever had been pressing against it had turned out, all along, to be morning.

They were running mail to Grayling Bend by noon, medicine to Two Rivers by dark, and tomorrow—Yara had pinned the note to the chart that morning, in her blunt carpenter's hand—*the Hollis settlement, past the falls. They sent word. No doctor since spring. A child with fever.*

Tomorrow. A heading, chosen. Not fled to. Chosen.

Sable had not known, until now, that those were different things.

* * *

**Yara**

The compass swung as she took the bend, then settled, and Yara watched it settle the way she watched everything her father had left behind now—with a grief that had stopped cutting and started carrying weight, like ballast.

She'd hated the thing for years. Hated it hanging in the boathouse among his tools, hated the way he'd trusted it more than he'd ever seemed to trust her, hated that when he finally died it was a stranger—a bush pilot with a face like winter—who carried it home to her instead of him. She'd wanted to throw it in the bay. She'd told Sable so, that first night, standing in the doorway with the oilskin packet unopened between them like a body.

Now it hung at her wheel because she'd drilled the bracket herself, and she checked it the way he must have checked it, a hundred times a day, out of habit, out of love, out of the simple animal need to know which way was true.

*I stayed away to keep you off his list.* That was what the journal had said, in his cramped prospector's hand, in the entry she'd read aloud to Sable in the burned village while the scout plane droned overhead. She could still recite it. Some sentences you carry like splinters until they work their way out or work their way in. His had gone all the way in and become bone.

He had watched Renner's men burn Ash Hollow for salvage rights and he had run—not from cowardice, though she'd called it that for twenty years, to his face, in letters she'd written and half-sent—but because a man who has seen what Renner does to witnesses keeps his witness far away. He'd kept himself far away too. He'd kept the proof in a strongroom under the sea and waited six tides and a stranger to bring it home.

Too late to say any of it out loud. That was the arithmetic of the dead; it never balanced. But last week, anchored below the falls, she'd carved his initials into the new rail she'd built where Corvane's men had holed the hull, and she'd found herself talking to him while she worked—telling him about the child at Two Rivers who'd waved at the plane until Sable waggled the wings, about Marta's coffee, about the way Sable hummed off-key during preflight checks and would deny it under torture. A one-sided correspondence. Better than none.

Grayling Bend came up on the starboard bow, smoke rising from three chimneys, kids already running down the shingle the way they did everywhere now when the *Meridian's Daughter* appeared. Word traveled faster than boats. *The wreck-boat's coming. The magistrate's dead. The river's open.*

Yara throttled down and let the current have the bow, easing her in toward the landing the way her father had taught her without ever meaning to—she'd watched him bring skiffs in as a girl, resenting every motion, memorizing every one.

"You're early," called the ferryman, wading out with a line.

"Tide's on our side," Yara said, and heard, in her own voice, how much that sentence had cost and how cheaply it was spent now. Six tides. They'd had six tides to save a truth, and they'd spent them all, and here on the far side of it the tides just kept coming, ordinary and generous, hauling the world in and letting it out.

She tossed the line. The ferryman caught it. Somewhere above, faint as a thought, the floatplane's engine turned a lazy circle over the bend, keeping watch.

* * *

**Sable**

Marta met them at Miller's Landing at dusk with fuel, coffee, and news, in descending order of importance.

"Fleet's done," she said, working the pump handle. "Corvane sold the launches. Kept one, they say. Him and his crew are running honest freight now, if you can believe it." She snorted. "Man covered your escape at the sound and then spent two weeks looking surprised that anyone noticed."

"He noticed," Sable said. "He just didn't want it counted against him."

"And the judge—" Marta leaned in, lowering her voice with the relish of a woman who had waited years for good news worth whispering. "Restitution's moving. Real money, real deeds. Ash Hollow survivors got their first papers Tuesday. There's talk of a memorial. Talk of rebuilding."

Talk. Sable looked north, where the river bent toward the black line of hills beyond which a village had been ash and a boy named Winterbourne had been a grave with no marker. Rebuilding was a word Sable had never let into the house of the heart. You didn't rebuild what was taken; you flew over it fast and low and didn't look down. That had been the whole religion.

But Yara had stood in the ashes of Ash Hollow and picked up a child's toy—a wooden bird, singed but whole—and put it in her pocket, and carried it three hundred miles, and last week she'd set it on the *Meridian's Daughter*'s dashboard next to the compass, and neither of them had said anything about it, and both of them looked at it every day.

Maybe rebuilding was just that. Carrying the bird.

"Marta." Sable dug in the coat. "The Hollis settlement. What do you know?"

Her face went careful. "Bad season. Fever in the kids, no doctor since spring, and the old supply line's cut since the fleet pulled out. They sent a runner down asking after medicine." She paused, and her eyes did the thing Marta's eyes did—weighed Sable, weighed the question behind the question. "It's past the falls. Rough water this time of year. You'd be running it in a patched hull with a bad shoulder aboard."

"We've run worse with less."

"I know." Marta handed over the coffee, unasked, uncharged. "That's what worries me about you two."

* * *

**Yara**

Night anchor below the falls, and for once the river was kind—no storm, just wind in the pines and the water talking to itself over the stones, that endless low conversation Yara had fallen asleep to her whole childhood and spent her whole adulthood trying not to miss.

She sat in the cockpit with the hatch open, mending a chafe in the jib sheet, and listened to Sable move around the deck doing the hundred small checks pilots and boatwrights share: lines, bilge, fuel, the anchor light burning its small stubborn yellow eye against the dark.

"You could sleep," Yara said. "I have the watch."

"So could you."

"One of us should be a good example."

Sable laughed—that low, rusty sound that had gotten less rusty every week since Kestrel Bay—and came and sat in the cockpit across from her, knees almost touching in the narrow space, and for a while neither of them spoke, because they'd gotten good at that too. Silence between them wasn't armor anymore. It was just silence, comfortable as an old coat.

"The Hollis child," Sable said eventually. "Fever cases this far upriver, this late—if it's what I think it is, medicine helps but only if it gets there inside two days. Plane can't land up there. No water deep enough for the floats except the pool below the falls, and the falls themselves—" A shrug that admitted everything. "Boat's the only way. Boat through that water with my cover overhead."

"You've already planned the approach."

"I've already planned three approaches. I don't love any of them."

Yara bit the thread, tied it off. "Show me in the morning. We'll pick the least terrible one together." She looked up. "That's the arrangement. We don't commit each other to ground anymore. We choose it."

Something moved across Sable's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under the hull. "You know," Sable said quietly, "for fifteen years my rule was never stay, never ask, never carry anything worth stealing. And I broke all three for a dying man in the fog, and it cost me the plane, the anonymity, the name I built to hide behind, nearly the boat, nearly—" The sentence stopped. Started again, lower. "Nearly you, at the sound, when the hull went."

"But?"

"But I keep waiting for it to feel like a mistake." Sable shook their head slowly. "And it doesn't. It feels like landing. You know what I mean? That moment when you're committed, wheels or floats touching down, and there's no go-around anymore, and every year of flying you've ever done is in that one second—and it's terrifying, and it's the only time the whole machine is actually doing what it was made for."

Yara reached over and touched the compass hanging at the wheel. Under her fingers the needle trembled and steadied, trembling and steadying, the way living things do.

"Ede used to say a compass doesn't tell you where to go," she said. "It tells you when you're lying to yourself about where you're going." She smiled. "I thought it was just something old men said. Turns out it's navigation."

Above them the stars wheeled slow over the falls, and the river ran on toward the settlements that needed them—Hollis tomorrow, and past Hollis whatever came next, because there was always a next; the interior was a wound the size of a country and it would take years and boats and planes and stubborn hands to close it, and someone had to carry the medicine and the mail and the names of the dead, and the someone, it turned out, was them.

"First light," Yara said. "We run the falls."

"Together," Sable said.

Not a question. A heading.

* * *

**Sable**

Dawn came up gold behind the eastern ridges, and the *Meridian's Daughter* took the falls channel with Yara's hands easy on the wheel and the compass needle steady and the wooden bird riding the dashboard, wings spread, singed but flying.

Sable's floats touched the pool below an hour later, and the medicine went ashore into arms that had been waiting, and a child's fever broke two days after that, and none of it made any history anywhere—which was, Sable understood at last, exactly the point.

That evening, moored at Hollis with lanterns lit on shore and children daring each other closer to the strange airplane, Yara climbed up onto the float and handed Sable a cup of tea and a folded paper.

"What's this?"

"Runner came in from upriver. Settlement past the divide—no mail in four months, no medicine in six." Her eyes were bright in the lantern light. "They heard there's a boat that comes."

Sable unfolded the paper. A heading. Coordinates. A need.

"Well, pilot," Yara said. "Where to?"

Sable looked at the compass hanging at the wheel of the boat they'd built out of a dead man's legacy and a live woman's hands, and found the needle already answering.

"North," Sable said. "Wherever it's needed. North."

And the *Meridian's Daughter*, loaded at first light, turned her bow upriver, and went.
