## 1. The Long Way Around

The pass had no name on any map worth trusting, which was exactly why Coral Delacroix charged what he charged.

He stood at the mouth of it at first light, breath steaming, and did the arithmetic he did every morning: eleven miles to the saddle, six more down the far side to the river crossing, then the long scree traverse where a man with bad judgment could die in under a minute. Seventeen miles. He measured his life in miles now. Miles were honest. Miles never asked him how long it had been.

Behind him, his client was taking too long with his boots.

"Mr. Ashcombe." Coral didn't turn around. "We move in four minutes or we lose the cold window."

"I'm paying you," Ashcombe said, "for an experience."

"You're paying me for a corridor of ice that stays frozen until nine o'clock. After that it's a waterfall with opinions." Coral checked the rope coil at his hip, ran a thumb along the sheath of his ice axe out of habit rather than need. Twenty years of habit. His hands knew things before he did. "Four minutes."

Ashcombe came up the trail huffing, a man built like a wine barrel in gear that had never touched anything rougher than a catalog page. Money coming off him in waves. That was fine. Money was the only language the legal world spoke to Coral Delacroix anymore, and even then only in whispers, through intermediaries, in countries where his name hadn't been entered into the wrong ledgers yet.

Two countries. Two blacklists. One wreck.

He put the thought down the way he'd learned to put thoughts down—carefully, face up, where he could watch it.

They moved.

* * *

The first three miles were scree and old moraine, the kind of ground that forgave nothing but also surprised no one. Coral set a pace just past comfortable and let Ashcombe's pride do the work of keeping up. It always worked. Rich men would sooner rupture something than admit a guide was managing them.

"You come here often?" Ashcombe managed, somewhere around the second mile marker—a cairn Coral had built himself, seven years ago, for no one.

"No."

"Where *do* you come from?"

Coral let ten steps go by. "Ahead," he said. "I come from ahead. You should try it."

Ashcombe laughed, breathless, charmed against his will. They all were, at first. The charm was part of the product. A man who couldn't sell his name sold the next best thing: competence wrapped in mystery, a guide who appeared from nowhere with no history and no references and took payment in cash or gold or, once, in a boat, which he'd sold within the week because boats went toward water and water went toward the sea and the sea—

He put that thought down too. Face up. Watched it.

The truth was simpler and uglier: there was nowhere he came from. There was a port town with his family's name on a customs house plaque, and there was a shipping company that had erased the rest, and there was a sister who had boarded a vessel called the *Meridian* twenty years ago this spring and never stepped off it, and there was an avalanche—the official story said avalanche—that had taken the ship and forty-one souls and Coral's good name along with them, because grief needs somewhere to land and the ledgers of two nations had decided it would land on him.

He'd been nineteen. He'd been asking questions. That was the whole crime, as far as he could ever reconstruct it: a boy asking questions about a wreck the company wanted closed, in a country that found it cheaper to close the boy instead.

Seventeen miles today. He counted them off like beads.

* * *

At mile five the ground turned to blue ice under a skin of powder, and Coral stopped being a personality and became an instrument.

"Ashcombe. Crampons on. Here." He knelt and demonstrated the strap tension without touching the man's boots—never touch a client without asking; another rule from another life, when he'd been trained by people whose names he still didn't say aloud. "Front points only where I step. Nowhere else. If I say *down*, you drop to your knees before you finish hearing the word."

"And if I fall?"

"You won't." Coral stood. "Because I've already fallen everywhere you're about to walk."

It wasn't bravado. It was inventory. Every route he guided was a route he'd tested with his own body first, alone, in seasons when the mountains kept no witnesses. That was the other thing exile bought you: freedom to be reckless in private. He'd spent years mapping passes no government wanted named, finding lines through country that officially didn't exist, and the knowledge sat in him now like ballast—useless to any legitimate life, priceless to the one he had.

They crossed the ice shelf in single file, Coral leading, the rope between them slack enough to insult and tight enough to save. Below their boots, through thirty feet of clear glacier, a boulder hung suspended like a fly in amber. Ashcombe looked down at it and made a small sound.

"How deep does it go?"

"Deeper than the light," Coral said. "Keep walking."

* * *

They reached the saddle at 8:40, inside the window, and Coral allowed himself—for the first time all morning—to look at the view instead of through it.

The range opened eastward in ranks: white shoulders, blue shadows, and beyond them, faint as a watermark, the coast. The sea. From this height it was only a color, a gray-blue seam stitched between mountain and sky, and it meant nothing, he told himself, it was just geography, except that his chest did the thing it always did, the slow clench, the tide pulling back from a shore inside him.

Twenty years since the *Meridian*. Nineteen since they'd told him he could never work coastal waters again in either nation that mattered. Eighteen since he'd stopped saying her name out loud, because saying it felt like digging, and the grave was already disturbed enough.

Maren.

There. Said. Once a year, whether he needed it or not.

"Extraordinary," Ashcombe breathed beside him. The rich man stood at the lip of the descent, wind pulling at his expensive hood, and for a moment his face held something unguarded—not greed, not conquest, just the naked awe of a man seeing past the edge of his own life. Coral recognized it. He remembered wearing it, at nineteen, planning voyages with his sister over cheap wine and stolen charts. *We'll see the horizon from the middle of it,* Maren had said. *Everyone else sees it from the shore. We'll stand in the middle.*

They'd stood in the middle of something, all right.

"Mr. Delacroix." Ashcombe was looking at him strangely. "You've gone somewhere."

"Mile twelve," Coral said. "Stay with me."

* * *

The descent was the dangerous half, and Coral gave it everything he had, which was to say he gave it nothing personal at all. Route-finding through the seracs below the saddle demanded a mind like a cold room: nothing left out on the counters, everything shelved, every door shut. He read the ice the way other men read weather, picked a line through the pressure ridges, called his holds in a low flat voice, and got them both down to the river crossing by noon with the sun still polite and the cornices still asleep.

It was only afterward—at the far bank, while Ashcombe posed for a photograph he'd have to explain to customs officials—that Coral felt the cost arrive, the way it always did. Not in the body. The body was fine. The body was a machine he maintained with suspicious devotion. The cost arrived in the silence after the job, in the space where a life was supposed to be.

No wife waiting. No crew. No letters, because letters required an address and addresses required a country and countries required a name that hadn't been struck through in two registries. He had a tent, a stove, a ledger of routes memorized so completely that burning his maps would change nothing, and a cash box buried in three locations he rotated seasonally.

He was, by any measure, the best guide working these ranges. He was also, by any measure, no one.

"Delacroix." Ashcombe had come up beside him at the bank, quieter now, the awe burned down to something more durable. "May I ask an impolite question?"

"You may ask."

"Why does a man with your skills work alone? I made inquiries, before hiring you. Through channels." The rich man smiled thinly. "Channels are how I do everything. And the channels say you don't exist. No guild. No license. No country that will stamp your passport. The channels say you're *blacklisted*, Mr. Delacroix, and yet here we are, above a river that will kill us both by dark, and I have never felt safer in my life. So. Why alone?"

Coral watched the water. White water doesn't hold reflections; it tears them apart. He'd chosen this bank for that reason, though he'd never admitted that to himself before.

"Because crews talk," he said finally. "And I don't."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one for sale."

Ashcombe studied him for a long moment, and then did the thing rich men sometimes did that Coral hated most: he saw straight through to the merchandise. "One day," Ashcombe said, "someone is going to offer you a job you can't refuse. Something with your name written all over it. And I suspect you'll discover that *alone* was just a word you were hiding behind." He handed over the payment pouch—heavier than agreed; Coral didn't comment—and started down toward the valley road where his car waited. At the edge of the rocks he paused. "There's a letter following you, by the way. I was asked to mention it. I was asked to say—" the rich man's mouth twisted, enjoying himself, "—that the past knocks politely."

Coral stood very still.

"Who asked?"

"A woman's handwriting on the envelope," Ashcombe said. "Ice on it, when it reached my office. Actual frost, in summer. Sent ahead through six hands so it couldn't be traced." He shrugged, pleased with the theater of it. "I was told you'd know who writes like that."

But Coral didn't know, and that was the trouble. Because only one person had ever written to him in that looping, left-slanted hand, pressing hard enough to emboss the paper, and she had been dead for twenty years—and yet some traitor part of him, the part that measured his life in miles precisely so he'd never have to measure it in years, had already begun counting the days backward, doing the terrible arithmetic, arriving at a number he'd sworn never to reach:

Zero miles left.
