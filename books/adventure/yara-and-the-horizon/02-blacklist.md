## 2. Blacklist

The client wanted stories.

They always did, eventually. Coral Delacroix had learned that about rich men on mountains: the altitude loosened something in them, some valve of vanity or loneliness, and by the second day above the treeline they started asking questions they thought were friendly. *Where'd you learn to read ice like that? How long you been doing this? You from around here?*

He gave them weather answers. Short, factual, useless. *Learned it the hard way. Long enough. Doesn't matter.*

But Herr Aldous Brandt—third-generation shipping money, soft hands, good boots he'd clearly been talked into buying—was not a man accustomed to weather answers. He was forty meters behind Coral on the switchbacks above the Veyra col, breathing in the ragged three-count rhythm Coral had drilled into him, and between counts he kept talking.

"They say you've guided every range on this side of the world," Brandt called up. "That true?"

"Depends who's saying it."

"The porter at the last village. He said you once took a party across the Skarn Icefield in whiteout conditions that killed two army patrols."

Coral adjusted his pace so the rope stayed slack. "The porter talks too much."

"He also said you can't legally guide here." Brandt's voice had gone careful, the way voices do when a man steps onto thin ice and feels it flex. "That this pass is closed to licensed operators. That you're—" he searched for the polite word and failed to find one, "—*unlicensed*."

The wind came off the col and pushed against them both. Coral let it fill the silence for a few strides before he answered.

"I'm the reason there's a rope between us," he said. "You want my history or my judgment? On this mountain, you only get one."

Brandt shut up for almost an hour. It was, Coral reflected, the most pleasant hour of the trip.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a granite shoulder, a shelf of flat stone just big enough for two tents and a stove, with the drop falling away south into blue shadow. Coral got Brandt fed and zipped into his bag while the light died, then sat outside with his own back against the rock, watching the temperature fall through the last visible degrees.

He didn't sleep well at altitude. Never had. Twenty years of practice had taught him what to do with the hours instead: he ran the route ahead. Tomorrow's crossing. The cornice on the eastern rib that would need testing. The descent gully where the rockfall risk went up after noon. He laid the mountain out in his mind piece by piece, mile by mile, because miles were countable and years were not, and a man could survive anything he could measure.

It was somewhere in that accounting—somewhere between the cornice and the gully—that Brandt's question finally caught him.

*Unlicensed.*

As if the word described a gap. As if somewhere there existed a license he'd simply neglected to pick up, a form left unsigned on a desk in some government office, and all of this—the exile, the aliases, the twenty years of guiding men like Brandt through passes their governments had sealed—was an administrative oversight.

Coral knew better. He'd read the actual documents. Both of them.

The first had come eleven months after the Meridian went down. He'd been twenty-four, working salvage crew on the southern coast, sleeping in a bunkhouse and drinking in a bunkhouse bar, when the maritime tribunal's findings arrived in a brown envelope with a wax seal he still sometimes saw in dreams. He hadn't opened it right away. He'd carried it around for two days, unopened, the way you carry a diagnosis.

Inside, in language designed by lawyers to sound like mercy and feel like a blade: *the tribunal finds no evidence of criminal negligence attributable to C. Delacroix.* A pause, a breath, the paper's equivalent of a hand on the shoulder. *However, given the totality of circumstances surrounding the loss of the Meridian, including the deceased's familial connection to voyage planning, the tribunal recommends—and the Ministry concurs—that Mr. Delacroix be prohibited from holding any maritime certification within these waters for a period of no less than ten years.*

*Familial connection to voyage planning.*

Maren had planned that voyage. His little sister, four years younger, smarter than him in every way that mattered, had spent eighteen months plotting the Meridian's route as junior navigator under Captain Ostrer. And Coral had signed on as deck crew specifically to be near her, over her objections, over their mother's objections, over the quiet screaming instinct in his own chest that had told him—*told him*—that the ship was wrong, that the cargo manifest didn't add up, that the company was running her heavy and fast through waters she had no business crossing in late season.

He'd gone anyway. Because Maren was going, and because he couldn't stand the idea of her out there without him.

And when the ship broke apart in the ice with two hundred and six souls aboard, and Maren went into the water with them, the tribunal had looked at the wreckage of the Delacroix family and found—not guilt, exactly. Something worse. *Circumstance.* They'd found circumstance, and they'd made it a wall.

The second ban came three years later, from the northern federation, and that one hadn't even pretended to be procedural. He'd crossed their border on a rescue—legally gray, morally clean, pulling two stranded surveyors off a glacier face that their own federation's guidelines forbade anyone from attempting in that season. He'd saved both their lives. The federation thanked him publicly and revoked his entry privileges permanently, citing, in the words of the interior ministry's letter, *a pattern of unauthorized high-risk activity inconsistent with regional safety standards.*

A pattern. As if the rescues were symptoms. As if the problem was him.

He remembered sitting on a dock in the federation's grey southern port, reading that letter for the third time, while the two surveyors he'd pulled off the ice stood nearby not knowing what to say. One of them had finally offered him a job. Off the books, cash, no names. And that had been the beginning of it—not a decision, nothing so dramatic. Just a door opening in a wall he'd assumed was solid, and Coral walking through it because the alternative was standing still, and standing still meant thinking, and thinking meant the ship, and the ship meant Maren.

So he'd become what the porter at the village whispered about. What men like Brandt paid triple rates for and never asked twice about. An underground guide. A ghost with a rope coil.

* * *

The rebuilding had taken years, and it had never once felt like building. It felt like burrowing.

The first rule he'd learned: never work the same range twice in a season. The second: take clients who needed discretion more than they needed cheap. The third—and this one he'd learned the hard way, in a border town where a customs officer had gotten curious about a name that appeared on two different watchlists under two different spellings—never use your real name where paper could touch it.

*Coral* survived because it was strange enough to be a nickname and common enough to be a coincidence. Everything else he'd let erode. Delacroix became a thing he'd been, the way childhood was a thing he'd been. He kept the surname locked in a drawer of himself, next to the tribunal letter and a photograph he never looked at and a journal he'd never opened past its first page.

The journal. Even now, twenty years on, his mind flinched away from it like a tongue finding a broken tooth. Maren's handwriting on the cover. Her initials pressed into the leather. He'd taken it from her cabin aboard the rescue vessel that recovered the survivors' effects—taken it without opening it, because opening it would have meant reading words she'd written while he was alive to write to, and he wasn't sure there was enough of him left to survive that.

One page. That was all he'd ever allowed himself. The first page, dated the week before departure, full of her looping, impatient script: *Coral says I'm overthinking the tide tables. Coral is wrong. Coral is always wrong about tides and always right about everything else, which is insufferable.*

He'd closed it and hadn't opened it since. Some doors you kept shut because you knew what was behind them. Others you kept shut because you didn't, and the not-knowing was the only version of her you could carry.

The underground trade had grown around him the way lichen grows on stone—slowly, invisibly, until the stone wore a green coat and everyone forgot the stone was underneath. Word traveled the way word travels among people who can't use official channels: quietly, in port bars and trailhead kitchens, in the shorthand of the dispossessed. *If you need to move through the closed country, ask for the man who reads ice. If he'll take you.*

And they came. Surveyors whose certifications had lapsed. Refugees with more money than papers. Rich men like Brandt, whose appetites outran what legal guiding would sell them. And once—a memory Coral kept in the same drawer as the photograph—a young woman with frostbitten fingers who'd reminded him so violently of Maren that he'd walked her off the mountain in silence and refunded her fee and not slept for a week afterward.

He'd built a life inside the shape of his exile, and the terrible truth—the truth he admitted to no one, barely to himself, usually only in the hour before dawn when the stove burned low—was that he'd gotten good at it. Not just competent. Good. The blacklist had stripped away everything soft and left the craft, pure and load-bearing, and he'd discovered that the craft was the only part of himself he still trusted.

Shame was armor if you wore it right. It kept people at the distance where they couldn't hurt you and you couldn't fail them. It justified the solitude, explained the aliases, made a philosophy out of what was really just a wound. *I can't go back, so I don't belong anywhere, so nowhere can reject me.* Clean logic. Load-bearing. He'd checked the joints a thousand times.

* * *

Below him, Brandt's tent rustled. The man slept badly at altitude too, apparently, or maybe the porter's gossip had followed him up the mountain and was keeping him awake.

"Delacroix." Brandt's voice, muffled through nylon. "You awake?"

"No."

A pause. Then, stubbornly: "The porter said something else. About a ship. That you lost someone on a ship, and that's why the licenses—"

"Your porter," Coral said, "is going to get himself blacklisted from my routes."

Silence again. But Coral found he was staring at the stars now instead of the route ahead, which was unusual, and his hands, laced over his chest, had gone tight, which was worse.

Because here was the thing he'd never told anyone, the thing that lived beneath even the shame, down where the load-bearing walls met bedrock: he wasn't sure the tribunal had been wrong. Oh, not the verdict itself—he knew, in the part of himself that still did honest arithmetic, that he hadn't caused the wreck. Cargo does what cargo does; ships break the way ships break; the sea doesn't consult family trees.

But he'd known. He'd *known* something was wrong with that voyage—the manifest, the speed, the season—and he'd boarded anyway, because leaving Maren alone on it had seemed unbearable, and being unbearable to his sister had seemed worse than being dead. His presence hadn't saved her. His presence hadn't changed anything. He'd been ballast. He'd been a brother-shaped hole in the water where a warning should have been.

If he'd refused the voyage—if he'd made noise, filed complaints, forced the company's hand the way he'd later force avalanches and rivers and governments to bend to his will—maybe the ship sails lighter. Maybe she takes the long route. Maybe two hundred and six people live, and maybe one of them is a girl with looping handwriting who thinks her brother is insufferable and right about everything except tides.

That was the arithmetic he did instead of counting years. Not miles. Debts.

And tonight, for the first time in a long while, the debt column wouldn't balance. Tonight the mountain wasn't enough. Tonight the route-ahead dissolved whenever he tried to lay it out, and what replaced it was a brown envelope with a wax seal, and a girl's voice saying *you worry too much, Coral*, and the specific, physical memory of the last time he'd seen her—on the dock, laughing, telling him the Meridian was the safest ship in the fleet because she'd plotted the route herself.

She'd plotted the route herself.

He'd forgotten that detail for years at a time. He'd had to forget it. Because if Maren had plotted the route herself, then the tribunal's phrase—*familial connection to voyage planning*—hadn't been about him at all. It had been about her. They'd hung the disaster's shadow on the whole family, and he'd worn it, because wearing it was the only way to keep her close.

The stove ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, the glacier settled with a sound like a long exhale.

Sleep took him eventually, the way it always did—reluctantly, and without offering any deals.

* * *

They summited the pass two days later, on schedule, without incident, and descended into the valley where Brandt's car waited with its driver and its cold coffee and its return to a world where names matched papers.

At the trailhead, Brandt shook his hand and held it a beat too long.

"You're wasted on this," he said. "Guiding smugglers' routes for men like me. Whatever it was that happened—it shouldn't have cost you everything."

"It didn't cost everything," Coral said. "It cost the parts that mattered. The rest was surplus."

Brandt studied him with the frank appraisal of a man who bought companies. "There's a rumor moving through the ports," he said. "Something about a fortune being assembled. Salvage rights, far north. Big money, bigger secrecy. They're hiring guides nobody else will hire." He paused. "Men like you."

Coral felt the mountain shift under him, though the ground hadn't moved.

"Enjoy the drive," he said, and turned up the trail toward home, already counting the miles.
