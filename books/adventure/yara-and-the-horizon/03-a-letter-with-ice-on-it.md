## 3. A Letter with Ice on It

The letter came up the mountain the way most things did—carried by someone who wanted something.

In this case it was a boy of maybe sixteen, breathless and blue-lipped, who'd climbed the last four hundred meters of the Kestrel Approach in running shoes because his uncle's mule had thrown a shoe at the treeline. He found me at the high camp where I was re-tensioning a client's tent lines against a wind that had opinions about canvas.

"You're Delacroix?" he said, though I was the only person within ten miles who wasn't him.

"Depends who's asking."

"Letter." He held it out like it might bite him. "From down-coast. Paid to bring it to your hand only."

I wiped the ice glaze off my fingers before I took it, which turned out to be a mistake, because the paper itself was cold. Not mail-cold. Cold like it had been kept somewhere deliberate—against ice, or near it—and the chill hadn't fully left it. The envelope was heavy stock, cream-colored, absurdly fine for a place where everything you owned eventually smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool. My name was written across the front in a hand I recognized even after twenty years: sharp downstrokes, a V that looked like it was trying to cut its way off the page.

Sable Voss.

The boy stood there waiting for a tip. I gave him one, mostly to make him leave faster, and watched him pick his way back down toward the trees until the white took him.

Then I sat on a supply crate with the letter in my hands and didn't open it for a long time.

That's the thing nobody tells you about exile. It isn't the borders that close. It's smaller than that. It's the way a certain handwriting can reach you at nine thousand feet, through two mountain ranges and a courier chain, and put frost on your spine anyway. Twenty years I'd built a life out of places other people wouldn't go, clients other agencies wouldn't take, routes drawn so far off every official map that the maps might as well not exist. I'd made myself unfindable by anyone who mattered.

And Sable Voss had found me anyway, because of course he had. Finding people was the one thing he'd always been better at than me.

* * *

I want to say I burned it immediately. That would be cleaner, and clean is what I sell clients—a controlled descent, no surprises, everyone home by dark. But the truth is I carried the thing around for half a day first, unopened, while I ran a rope line for a textile heir from Meridian City—I noted the irony of the city's name and decided not to have feelings about it—who paid triple rates precisely because my routes were illegal and therefore uncrowded. He wanted photographs of himself looking solitary against vastness. Solitude is easy to fake if you've got the money to buy real remoteness.

All day the letter rode in my chest pocket, over my heart, cold as a stone pulled from a stream. By the time we made the descent camp and the client was safely drunk on the good whiskey he'd portered up at ruinous expense, I still hadn't opened it. But I knew what it was. You always know. A letter that travels that far, that cold, addressed in that hand—it isn't correspondence. It's a summons. And summonses only come from two directions: the law, or the past.

With Voss, historically, it was both.

* * *

Here is what most people got wrong about Sable Voss, back when there were people around who knew us both: they thought we hated each other. We never hated each other. Hatred requires a kind of attention that neither of us was willing to spend. What we had was worse. We were the same animal wearing different collars—two kids from the salvage coast who'd learned to read ice the same winter, raced each other up the same frozen waterfalls, taken the same certification exams out of order just to prove the examiners were idiots. For six years we were closer than brothers, and then the *Meridian* went into the ice with my sister aboard, and the inquiry needed someone to blame, and the two most obvious candidates were the guide who'd planned her route and the rival guide who'd publicly called that route reckless three weeks before she died.

Voss walked away with his licenses intact. I walked away with nothing but a name that opened no doors in two countries and closed most of them in the rest.

I never learned exactly how he'd managed it. That question had teeth, and I'd spent twenty years refusing to put my hand in its mouth. Because there were only two possible answers, and one of them—that he'd simply been smarter, better connected, luckier—was bearable. The other one wasn't.

So when I finally sat down that night outside the client's tent, fed the stove, and slit the cream envelope with my knife, my hands were steadier than they had any right to be. Twenty years of practice at being steady. It's the first thing they teach you and the last thing you own.

The letter was short. Voss never wasted words; it was one of the things I'd resented about him, how much he could say with so little.

---

*Delacroix.*

*I'll assume this finds you, since I paid enough to guarantee it.*

*A job. One client, one window, one route only you can lead. Six tides, starting when the calving season opens the shelf road. The fee is forty thousand down, forty on completion, and I know what you'll say, so I'll say this first: it isn't the mountains I need you for. It's the memory.*

*You know the place. You've spent twenty years pretending you don't.*

*Come down to Greyharbor inside the month or don't. But understand that if you don't, someone else will lead it, and they will do it badly, and things will come out of the ice that should have come out under your hand or no one's.*

*You were always the best of us. It cost you everything. This is the one job that pays it back.*

*S.V.*

---

I read it twice. Then I fed it to the stove.

The paper caught reluctantly—good stock, dense fibers, the kind of luxury that burns slow—and I watched the ink curl and go brown and then black, watched my own name blister off the page, and felt absolutely nothing except the specific nothing I'd spent two decades perfecting. The stove ticked. Outside, the wind rearranged itself against the rock face in long exhalations, the sound the old Salish guides called the mountain breathing, though I'd stopped believing mountains breathed a long time ago. Mountains endure. That's their whole trick. Everything else just fails against them at its own speed.

Forty thousand down. Forty on completion. Enough to disappear properly, if a man still wanted to disappear. Enough to stop guiding rich amateurs up illegal passes and buy something flat and warm and anonymous near the sea.

He'd known I'd think that. That was the trap inside the offer—not the money, the *option* of the money. Voss understood that the cruelest thing you could give a man in exile wasn't punishment. It was a door, left unlocked, with light behind it.

I banked the stove and went to my tent and lay down in my bag and stared at the nylon ceiling and waited for sleep the way you wait for a bus in a country with no schedule.

It didn't come.

* * *

What came instead was Maren.

Not a ghost—I don't traffic in ghosts, ghosts are for people whose memories stay where they're put—but the ordinary machinery of recall, which is worse, because it runs whether you want it or not. She was nineteen in the version my brain chose, standing on the dock at Greyharbor with her duffel over one shoulder and her hair full of salt wind, laughing at something I'd said, and I remembered thinking, with the fat certainty of a twenty-four-year-old brother, that she'd be fine. That the *Meridian* was a good ship with a good crew and a certified route, that her berth as assistant purser was a stepping-stone, that the world was a place where careful people on careful ships arrived where they were going.

Eleven days later she was a name on a casualty list, and I was a name on an inquiry report, and the ship was a shape under the ice that fishermen pointed at and children dared each other about.

They said the cargo shifted. They said the manifest was wrong. They said a lot of things in that inquiry room, in that flat official voice designed to make catastrophe sound like arithmetic, and when the arithmetic was done, the remainder—the part that couldn't be explained—was assigned to me. Route planning. Judgment. *Delacroix's chosen line brought the vessel inside the shelf zone during a documented instability period.* As if I'd steered her there myself. As if a sister's death were a rounding error I'd made on purpose.

Two countries blacklisted me inside a year. The third just quietly stopped returning letters.

And here was the thing I had told no one, the thing I burned along with Voss's letter every time it surfaced, which was roughly once a year at three in the morning: the route hadn't been mine. I'd drawn the safe line. Someone above my pay grade had amended it—for schedule, for cargo tonnage, for reasons entered in a ledger I was never permitted to see—and the amended line was the one the *Meridian* sailed, and when I said so in the inquiry room, a man with a government lanyard looked at me with something close to pity and asked whether I understood how guilt worked in survivors.

I understood exactly how it worked. It worked by making the truth unsayable until it sounded like excuse.

Maren had known too. Her last letter to me—still sealed, still carried, still unread past the first line in twenty years because the first line was *Coral, you have to see the manifest*—was in the bottom of my gear kit, wrapped in oilcloth, riding with me up and down mountains like a splinter I refused to dig out. Some nights I took it out and held it. Most nights I didn't. Tonight I did, lying in the dark with the stove cold, turning the little oilcloth package over in my hands while the wind did its breathing act against the rock.

*You have to see the manifest.*

I'd read that sentence ten thousand times and never let myself read the next one. Reading the next one meant believing her. Believing her meant the inquiry had lied. The inquiry lying meant the blacklist was built on a lie, which meant my whole exile—every illegal pass, every cash payment, every year measured in miles instead of birthdays—was built on sand. And a man will defend his prison if the alternative is learning the door was never locked.

I put the journal away. I zipped the kit. I lay back down.

Sleep, when it finally took me near dawn, was thin and gray and full of ice groaning somewhere below the dream-line, a sound I knew professionally and personally, the sound a glacier makes when it's deciding what to let go.

* * *

I woke to the client asking for eggs and a view, in that order, and gave him both, because that was the job: you feed people their appetites and you keep your own buried somewhere above the snowline. But all through the morning—through the traverse, the fixed lines, the carefully staged photograph of him alone against the vastness—he kept talking, the way rich men do when they've bought themselves a captive audience, and he said the thing that lodged:

"Heard they're fitting out a fleet at Greyharbor. Salvage outfit. Big money, going after something in the shelf ice." He squinted north. "You know anything about that? You're the mountain man."

"No," I said.

But I knew. Of course I knew. Voss's letter hadn't been an invitation. It had been a courtesy—a courtesy with a deadline, *come down inside the month*, and fleets don't fit out for months, they fit out for weeks. Which meant either Voss had lied about the timeline, or he'd never expected me to accept at all, and the letter was something else entirely. A warning. Or a record. Proof, someday, that he'd offered the job to the man with the memory before he gave it to the men with the money.

Things will come out of the ice that should have come out under your hand or no one's.

I stood on the ridge with the client's camera clicking behind me and looked north, past the pass, past the border ridges I couldn't legally cross, toward the coast I hadn't seen in twenty years, and I did the thing I always did when the past knocked politely: I counted miles. Two hundred and forty to Greyharbor by the smugglers' trail. Ninety by the legal road I couldn't walk. Eleven days' hard travel to a town I'd sworn off, for a man I'd sworn off, about a ship I'd sworn off, carrying a letter I'd already burned.

Except the letter wasn't burned. That was the joke of it. I'd fed the paper to the stove, but paper was never the part that mattered. The part that mattered was sitting in my chest pocket where the envelope had ridden all day, cold as ever, and it said *you have to see the manifest* in my sister's handwriting, and it had been knocking for twenty years.

That night I broke my own rule. I took out the oilcloth package, unwrapped it, and read—to the end of the first page, then the second, hands not steady now, no practice in the world steady enough—until the stove burned low and the words swam and I had to stop with the journal open on my knees like a door I'd finally unlocked and didn't dare walk through.

Because the manifest she wanted me to see wasn't a shipping document.

It was a list of names. And halfway down it, in her small precise script, was a note in the margin: *This doesn't match what they loaded at Greyharbor. Coral, someone is lying, and I think it's the company.*

Someone is lying.

I sat in the dark with the stove ticking down and understood, with the calm of a man reading his own avalanche forecast, that the six tides were coming whether I moved or not. That Voss's fleet would sail. That the ice would open the shelf road for six tides and then shut it for another lifetime, and whatever was sealed in the *Meridian*'s hold—her truth, my exoneration, or something worse that I hadn't yet imagined—would either come out into the light or go down into the deep with the calving.

The past had knocked politely. Now it was picking the lock.

At grey dawn I struck camp, paid off the client with a refund and a lie about family business, and started down.
