## 4. The Crew That Shouldn't Exist

The letter was ash by morning, but the job wouldn't burn.

Coral spent two days telling himself that. Two days of checking rope inventory he already knew by heart, of re-waterproofing boots that didn't need it, of walking the harbor road at dusk and counting the ships at anchor the way other men counted sheep. On the third day he stopped lying to himself and started making calls.

Not calls, exactly. There were no phones where his people lived. There were notes left in agreed places, chalk marks on specific pilings, a folded page tucked into the third loose stone of a wall in Saltrow that hadn't been repaired in forty years because everyone who mattered knew what lived inside it. He sent four messages. Then he sat in his rented room above the chandlery with a map of the northern ice fields spread across the bed, and waited to see who would come.

He'd told himself he wasn't going to take Voss's money. He'd told himself that loudly, repeatedly, with the conviction of a man rehearsing a defense. But somewhere between burning the letter and the third sleepless night, the arithmetic had changed. Not because of the fortune—though the fortune was real, and Coral had spent twenty years being paid just enough to keep moving. It was the six tides. The window. The idea that the glacier was going to open a door for less than a week and then close it forever, taking whatever was sealed in the Meridian's hold down into the dark with it.

And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, something he didn't have a name for yet: the sense that the past had knocked politely, and if he didn't answer this time, it would stop knocking. And then there would be nothing left but the miles.

* * *

Ilsa came first, as she always did.

She arrived at the chandlery an hour before opening, and Coral found her already inside, sitting on a crate of shackles with her boots crossed at the ankle, reading his tide tables upside down from across the room.

"You reorganized your notation," she said, not looking up. "The old system was better."

"The old system got us lost off Sker Point."

"The old system got us *found* off Sker Point, which is different." She set the tables down and looked at him properly. Ilsa Reyen was fifty-one years old and had the face of a woman who had stopped wasting energy on anything decorative decades ago. Grey shot through her black hair like frost through soil. Her eyes were the pale grey-green of shallow water over slate, and they went through Coral the way they went through weather—cataloguing, measuring, unimpressed. "You look terrible."

"I've been sleeping fine."

"Then you should see someone about your face." She stood, brushed off her coat, and finally acknowledged the reason she was there. "Voss's letter. I heard. Half the harbor heard. The man has never learned to do anything quietly."

"He burned," Coral said.

"And yet here you are, asking me to chart a route nobody's charted in twenty years." She said it without accusation, which was worse. Ilsa never accused; she simply stated the shape of things until you had to live inside the shape or argue with geometry itself. "So. Are we doing this?"

Coral had planned a speech. He'd drafted it during the sleepless nights—a careful, reasonable case about money and timing and one last job. Looking at her now, he abandoned all of it.

"Why are you still talking to me?" he asked instead. "You could be navigating for anyone. You're good enough. You were always good enough. Ten years ago you could have had a fleet contract, a pension, a name on a chart somewhere. Instead you're sitting on a crate of shackles in a smuggler's chandlery waiting for a blacklisted guide to ask you a favor."

Ilsa considered him for a long moment. Outside, the harbor sounds built themselves up piece by piece—the first gulls, the first winch, the slap of water against hulls.

"Because in thirty years of navigation," she said, "I have worked with exactly two people who never once lied to me about the sea. One of them died on the Meridian." Something moved behind her eyes, fast and gone. "The other is standing in front of me looking like a man who hasn't decided whether to be honest yet."

"I'm deciding."

"Decide faster. The window won't wait." She picked up her bag. "I'll need three days and access to every hydrographic survey from the last decade, including the ones the survey office doesn't admit exist. And Coral—" she paused at the door, hand on the frame, not turning around, "—when you decide, decide all the way. I'm too old to follow a man who's still arguing with himself."

Then she was gone, and Coral stood alone in the chandlery feeling something he hadn't felt in years: seen, and not entirely displeased about it.

* * *

Tam and Bo arrived together, because Tam and Bo arrived everywhere together, like weather systems that had gotten tangled years ago and never separated.

They came up the harbor road at midday, loud before they were visible, and Coral heard them coming from two streets away—which was how it always was. Tam Rennick was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a laugh that carried across water and hands that could splice cable in a gale. Bo was smaller, quicker, younger by eight years, with a permanent half-smile and a way of moving that suggested the ground was a rumor he chose to believe only selectively.

They found him at the chandlery counter, and Tam's grin arrived before he did.

"Coral Delacroix," Tam boomed, slapping the counter hard enough to rattle the till. "Heard you were dead. Heard it twice, actually. Disappointed both times."

"Sorry to disappoint."

"Oh, don't apologize. Best news I've had all month." He dropped onto a stool that groaned under him. Bo leaned against the wall beside him, arms folded, saying nothing, watching everything—that was the division of labor between them. Tam filled the room; Bo mapped it. "So. Word is Voss put a fortune on the table and you burned it. Also word is you're asking around anyway. Both can't be true."

"Both are true."

Tam's eyebrows went up. Bo's half-smile deepened by a millimeter, which from Bo was equivalent to another man's open laughter.

"Well," Tam said, rubbing his hands together. "That's the most interesting thing anyone's said to me since the Kestrel job, and we all remember how that ended."

"We remember how that ended because you won't stop telling it."

"Because it's a *good story*. There was fire. There was a crane. There was a moment when you looked at me across a burning deck and said, and I quote, 'Tam, I have made a series of decisions.'—"

"—and then we got everyone out," Coral finished. "Which is the part you leave out."

"Because the fire's funnier." But Tam's grin faded into something more careful, and he leaned forward on the stool, elbows on knees, and for a moment the clown was gone and the professional was there underneath, the man who had rigged loads in conditions that would have made grown shipwrights weep. "All right. Serious voice. Why this job? You've spent twenty years not going near that glacier. People noticed. People like me notice things about the people we trust our lives to. So either Voss is paying enough to buy your ghosts, or something else is going on. Which is it?"

It was a fair question, and Coral respected him for asking it, and he hated having to answer it.

"The window closes forever," he said. "After this season, the calving takes the whole channel. Whatever's in that wreck goes under a hundred meters of ice and stays there. I'm not ready for that to happen yet."

"Not ready," Bo repeated softly, from the wall. First words he'd spoken. His voice was quiet and precise, a contrast to Tam's foghorn. "Interesting word. Not 'willing.' Not 'paid enough.' *Ready.*"

Coral met his eyes. Bo held them, mild and unblinking, and Coral understood—as he always understood with Bo, and never comfortably—that the man saw more than he performed.

"Yes," Coral said. "Ready."

Bo nodded once, as if a transaction had completed. "We're in."

Tam blinked. "We are?"

"We are. Weren't you listening? He said *ready.*" Bo pushed off the wall. "Terms?"

"Standard rates plus hazard share," Coral said. "Split five ways if Ferro signs on."

"Five ways." Tam whistled. "Ferro. Gods. Is he even still licensed?"

"No."

"Right." Tam nodded sagely. "Neither am I, technically. The Guild revoked my certification after the Kestrel inquiry."

"They revoked your certification because you testified against the salvage master who cut a loaded line."

"I prefer my version. It has more fire in it." Tam stood, joints cracking like small-arms fire, and stuck out his hand. Coral took it. Tam's grip was still the strongest thing in any room he entered. "One last job, Delacroix. Then I retire to a coastal town and become a nuisance to fisheries inspectors."

"You'll be bored in a week."

"I'll be bored in *four days*, but they'll be glorious days." He grinned again, and this time Bo smiled fully, briefly, like sun through cloud, and Coral felt the strange warmth of it settle somewhere under his ribs. Twenty years he'd worked with hired hands, temporary partners, men whose names he learned and forgot within a season. These two he'd known for eleven years, and they kept coming back, and he had never once asked himself why until now.

* * *

Ferro was the hardest call, and Coral saved him for last, which was cowardice dressed as strategy.

Doctor Emmerich Ferro had practiced medicine for nine years before losing everything in a single afternoon—not to scandal, not to incompetence, but to a decision. A mining town upcountry, a collapse, forty-one men underground and one field hospital and supplies enough for thirty. Ferro had triaged. He had chosen, openly, in front of witnesses, which lives to spend resources on. Thirty survived. Eleven did not, and one of the eleven had a brother with money and a grudge and time, and the license board had found its courage in someone else's grief.

Coral found him in a free clinic in the dock district, elbow-deep in a fisherman's infected hand, and waited among the smell of carbolic and cheap tobacco until the patient was dismissed.

"You look like a symptom," Ferro said by way of greeting. He was a narrow man, grey-faced, with the permanent stoop of someone who had spent decades bending toward other people's pain. "Sit down before you fall down. When did you last eat?"

"Ferro—"

"When. Did you last. Eat."

"...Yesterday."

Ferro closed his eyes as if praying for strength, produced a heel of bread and hard cheese from a drawer, and pushed them across the table. "Eat while you ruin my life. What is it?"

Coral ate. It seemed wise. "A salvage job. Northern ice fields. Six-tide window. High risk, high pay, and I need a medic who doesn't ask questions about why his patient might want to avoid port authorities."

Ferro listened with his hands flat on the table, the way he listened to symptoms. When Coral finished, the doctor was silent for a long moment.

"The Meridian," he said.

Coral's jaw tightened. "You know."

"Everyone knows, Coral. That's rather the point of a blacklist—it's public." Ferro rose and began straightening instruments that were already straight. "Twenty years ago I read the inquiry findings. All of them, not the newspaper versions. Do you know what struck me most?"

"What."

"That they blamed you for a decision no one can prove anyone made. Pilot error, they said. The pilot was dead, so the dead make convenient scapegoats." He turned around. "I've spent fifteen years treating dock workers and fishermen because one rich man's brother died under my care despite my best judgment. I know exactly what it looks like when an institution needs someone to blame more than it needs the truth." He picked up his bag. "When do we leave?"

Coral stared at him. "That's it? No questions?"

"I have questions. I'll ask them on the trail, where answers are useful." At the door he paused, and his voice softened into something almost gentle. "You sent four messages, didn't you. And you saved me for last because you were sure I'd say no."

"I wasn't sure."

"You were. It's written all over your face, along with malnutrition." Ferro opened the door onto the noise of the docks. "Here's what you missed, Coral, in twenty years of measuring miles instead of friends: none of us are employable *because* we're the kind of people who do the right thing at the wrong price. Tam testified. I triaged. Ilsa tells the truth to admirals. Bo—" he shrugged, "—Bo has never told me why, and I've stopped guessing. We are not a crew despite our records. We are a crew *of* them. Get some sleep. We leave when Ilsa says we leave."

* * *

That night the five of them sat around a scarred table in the back room of the chandlery, charts spread out, lamplight pooling yellow, and Coral watched his crew argue about routes and load limits and whether Tam could be trusted with the stove, and felt something crack in him—not painfully, the way ice cracks, but the way a fist cracks when it finally opens.

He had spent twenty years alone by design. Alone was safe. Alone meant no one to lose, no one to doubt him, no one whose disappointment could reach the place where Maren lived in his memory. He had told himself exile was a sentence imposed from outside, when the truth was he'd been serving it willingly, cell door open, warden absent.

And these four impossible, unemployable, loyal people had simply walked through the open door and sat down at his table like it was the most natural thing in the world.

"—so if we take the smugglers' trail north of the pass, we gain two days on the fleet," Ilsa was saying, tapping the chart. "But it costs us. The border runners tax that route, and they tax it in ways that aren't always money."

"Everything costs," Bo said. "The question is what currency."

"Speaking of which," Tam said, leaning back until his chair protested, "when does Voss expect an answer? Because a man like that doesn't send one letter. He sends reminders."

As if summoned, there came a knock at the chandlery's street door—heavy, deliberate, three strikes.

The room went quiet. Five people who trusted each other with their lives looked at each other across a lamplit table, and Coral felt the warmth in his chest cool by several degrees.

"It's past midnight," Ferro said quietly.

"Nobody knocks at midnight unless they already know we're all here," Bo said.

Coral rose, hand drifting toward the knife at his belt, and crossed to the door. Through the wood, muffled, came a voice he knew—smooth, unhurried, amused—and it said:

"Don't bother reaching for the blade, Delacroix. I counted your crew through the window. Four. Very cozy. Shall we discuss terms—or would you rather I discuss them with the harbor master, who is, I'm told, a light sleeper?"
