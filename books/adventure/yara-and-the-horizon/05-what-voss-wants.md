## 5. What Voss Wants

The knock came at four in the morning, which told Coral everything before he opened the door.

Nobody honest knocked at four. Honest people waited for daylight, for coffee, for the polite fiction that a man like Coral Delacroix kept regular hours. Four in the morning was for debt collectors and border police and old friends who'd stopped being friends somewhere around the turn of a century's worth of grudges.

He lay in the narrow bunk of the rented room above the ferry office and listened to the second knock. Three raps, evenly spaced. Patient. The kind of patience that had money behind it.

The lamp on the sill still held a thumbprint of oil. He lit it, watched the flame steady, and considered his options, which were: out the window, out the door, or through the floorboards into the fish cellar where he'd slept worse nights than this one.

Instead he said, "It's open," because the man on the other side of that door already knew he was awake, and pretending otherwise would cost him more dignity than it was worth.

Sable Voss filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley. Not large, exactly—Voss had never been large—but *present*, the way a man is present when he has spent thirty years making sure rooms rearrange themselves around him. Grey coat, grey eyes, hair gone silver at the temples in a way that looked less like age than like frost claiming high ground. He carried no bag. Men like Voss didn't carry bags; they arrived places as if the world had simply agreed to have them there.

"Delacroix." He said the name like he was testing an ice screw—gently, with weight behind it.

"Voss." Coral didn't get up. "You're a long way from your fleet."

"My fleet is a long way from me. That's rather the point of fleets." Voss stepped inside without being asked, closed the door softly behind him—the softness itself a kind of arrogance—and took the room's only chair, which meant Coral was left standing, which meant Voss had already won the first exchange without either of them touching anything. "You look well."

"I look tired."

"You look tired the way a blade looks tired. It's called an edge." Voss folded his hands. "I'll be brief, since you're clearly counting the minutes until you can throw me out."

"You've counted wrong. I finished throwing you out two minutes ago. You just haven't left yet."

Something moved at the corner of Voss's mouth—not quite a smile, but the memory of one, from a time when they'd been young enough to trade insults across a rope team instead of a room. Twenty-two years ago they'd shared a tent on the Skerwall traverse, back when Sable Voss was nobody's magnate and Coral Delacroix had never heard the word *blacklist*. Back when Maren was alive and the three of them had eaten frozen cheese and argued about whether the mountain owed them anything.

Coral put that thought down carefully, the way you set down something with a live edge.

"You burned my letter," Voss said.

"You read smoke?"

"I read everything. It's why I'm rich." He reached into his coat—an unhurried motion, telegraphed, deliberate—and produced a second envelope. Thicker. Sealed with wax instead of glue. He set it on the table between them and did not push it forward. "Before you burn this one too, hear the number."

"I don't want your money."

"Everyone wants someone's money. Yours is just pickier about whose." Voss leaned back. "Fifty thousand."

Coral laughed. It came out wrong, rusty, like a door on a house nobody lived in. "Fifty thousand for what? A guide who can't legally guide? You'd be paying me to break laws in two countries. That's not a salary, that's a bounty with extra steps."

"A hundred thousand."

"Stop."

"One hundred and fifty," Voss said, "and I haven't even told you what the job is yet."

And there it was. The trap laid open on the table, elegant as a snare line: not the money, never the money, but the *not knowing*. Coral felt the hook of it settle behind his sternum, cold and patient, and hated Voss with a clean, clarifying hatred, because Voss knew him. Knew that Coral Delacroix could refuse any sum, but couldn't refuse a question.

"The job," Coral said, "is nothing. Whatever's in that envelope is nothing. You found the one man in the world who doesn't care what's in it."

"Maren's journal says otherwise."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the harbor water slapped the pilings in its slow, indifferent rhythm. Somewhere below, the ferry office clerk coughed in his sleep.

Coral discovered he was standing very still, the way you stand still when the snowpack under you has started to whisper. "Say that again."

"No." Voss unfolded himself from the chair and crossed to the window, looking out at the black harbor as if the conversation were already settled and he were merely waiting for the tide. "I'm going to tell you what I want, Delacroix, and then you're going to tell me no, and then I'm going to leave, because we both know how this ends—we've played it out a hundred times in twenty years, usually at three in the morning, usually after drinking. So let's do it standing up and sober."

"Get out."

"In six weeks, the Vantar Glacier enters calving season. For six tides—six, no more—the meltwater channels along its eastern flank will run deep enough to float a shallow-draft boat through the ice fields to a place no ship has reached in twenty years." Voss turned. His grey eyes caught the lamplight and gave nothing back. "There's a wreck there, Coral. Sealed in the ice since the winter she went down. Half-buried, hull intact, hold welded shut from the inside. The glacier has been keeping her like a body keeps a bullet."

The floor did not move. Coral made sure of it. He gripped the back of the chair Voss had vacated and made absolutely certain the floor stayed where floors belonged.

"The Meridian," he said. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of that.

"The Meridian."

"There is no Meridian. There's a story about a wreck, and a blacklist with my name on it, and a grave with no body in it because they never found one. That's all that exists."

"That's what exists *officially*." Voss picked up the envelope, weighed it once in his palm, and set it back down. "Here's what exists actually. The Meridian didn't sink in open water. She went down in the shallows off the Vantar tongue, and the winter ice took her whole, and she's been in the glacier ever since—moving, year by year, downslope at the speed the ice moves, which is slower than grief and faster than forgiveness. Every survey that's flown over has logged her as a pressure ridge. I've paid for eleven surveys. I know exactly where she is."

"Why?" The word came out harder than Coral intended. Harder, and more afraid. "Why does a shipping magnate with three fleets want one dead hull in the ice?"

"Because of what's in her hold."

"Don't." 

"Cargo manifest lists forty tons of ore concentrate and general freight." Voss's voice dropped, went quiet, went *careful*, and careful was worse than loud. "But the Meridian wasn't carrying forty tons of freight the night she went down. She was carrying the Delacroix family's entire estate—deeds, ledgers, titles, the inheritance your father sealed aboard for safe passage because he trusted the company, and the company—" he paused, savoring it or maybe hating it, Coral couldn't tell, "—the company lost her. Deliberately or stupidly, that part's still an open question. And with her went every document proving what your family owned, and every document proving who was responsible. Both halves in one hold, Coral. The fortune and the truth. Welded shut from the inside by someone who had time to think, in the last minutes, and chose to seal it."

Coral's hands had gone numb on the chairback. He noticed this distantly, the way you notice weather. "You're lying."

"I'm many things, but I've never lied to you. Ask anyone who's worked for me. It's my one virtue and it's cost me millions."

"Then you're wrong. Or you're used. Someone fed you a story to get you to spend a fortune digging a dead ship out of a glacier, and when you crack that hold you'll find forty tons of wet ore and a hole where a family's ghost used to be."

"Possible." Voss nodded, agreeable, terrible. "Entirely possible. Which is why I'm offering you a hundred and fifty thousand to come find out with me. If it's ore, you've been paid handsomely to be wrong. If it isn't ore—" he let it hang, "—then somewhere in that hold is a paper trail that ends at whoever sank the Meridian, and a name on it that isn't yours. Twenty years, Delacroix. Twenty years you've been the answer to a question nobody asked correctly. I'm offering you the chance to correct the record."

"And what do you get?" Coral pushed off the chair, crossed the small room, put his hand flat on the envelope—anchoring himself, not taking it. "Men like you don't fund expeditions out of sentiment. What's your cut?"

For the first time, Voss looked away. Out at the harbor, at the dark water, at some middle distance where things floated that weren't ships. When he spoke again his voice had changed register—lower, stripped of the magnate's polish, and for one unguarded moment he sounded like the boy on the Skerwall traverse who'd cried from altitude sickness and sworn Coral to secrecy.

"What I get," Voss said, "is complicated. And it's mine. Take the job or don't, but don't stand there and tell me you don't want to know what's in that hold. I watched you burn my letter, and I watched you not sleep afterward—I know the innkeeper here, Delacroix, I know everyone—and a man who burns a letter and then lies awake listening to it is a man who's already halfway to yes."

Coral picked up the envelope. Held it over the lamp.

Voss didn't move. Didn't flinch. Watched him with those grey eyes and the patience of a man holding the other end of a rope.

"You know what I remember?" Coral said quietly. "I remember the night before she sailed. Maren sat at our mother's table doing figures by lamplight, checking the manifest, and she looked up at me and said, *Coral, if anything goes wrong, promise me you won't come looking. Promise me you'll stay on dry land.* And I promised. And then I broke that promise for twenty years in every way except the one that mattered, because I never went to the glacier, and I never stopped wanting to." He turned the envelope in the flame. The wax bubbled, blackened, let go. Paper caught, curled, went to ash in the dish below the lamp. "So no. The answer is no. It was no twenty years ago and it's no now, and if you send a third letter I'll burn that too, and the fourth, until you run out of wax."

Voss straightened his coat. Collected his composure the way other men collected hats. At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your sister checked that manifest twice. Found an error in the third column. Corrected it herself, in ink, initialled it. I have a copy of that page." Now he turned, and the lamplight did something unforgivable to his face, made him look almost kind. "She was good, Coral. Better than either of us. Think about who else might have known that."

The door closed behind him with barely a sound.

Coral stood in the small room with the smell of burnt paper and the sound of the harbor, and understood, with the clarity that comes only at four-thirty in the morning, that he had just made the worst mistake of his life or avoided it, and that he would not know which until it was far too late to matter.

He did not sleep. He sat by the dead lamp while the sky went grey, and somewhere past dawn he caught himself doing arithmetic he'd forbidden himself: six tides. Six weeks out. A hundred and fifty thousand. A page initialed in Maren's handwriting, sitting in Sable Voss's pocket like a coin he hadn't flipped yet.

*Promise me you won't come looking.*

He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until the dark went red.

By full light he was pulling his gear out from under the bunk, checking rope, checking stove, checking the list of names in his head—the crew that shouldn't exist, the people broken enough to say yes to a job with no legal shape. Because whether he took Voss's money or not, one thing had become unbearable overnight:

If that hold was real, someone else was going to open it first.

And Coral Delacroix had spent twenty years learning exactly what happened to promises when other men got there ahead of him.

* * *

He found Ilsa at the chandlery, arguing with the owner about chart corrections, her finger stabbed down on a coastline that had migrated half a mile since the last survey. Tam and Bo were loading crates onto the *Kestrel's* deck. Ferro was nowhere visible, which meant Ferro was everywhere, watching.

Coral stood at the dock edge with the wind coming off the water and said, "We need to talk about a job."

Ilsa looked up. Read his face in the two seconds it took her to lower the chart. "How much?"

"It's not about the money."

"It's always about the money."

"It's about the Meridian," he said, and watched the word land on each of them in turn—Tam going still over his crate, Bo setting it down too carefully, Ferro materializing at the rail like he'd been there all along, which he had.

Nobody asked what the Meridian was. They'd all heard the story; everybody in two countries had heard the story. The wreck that made a guide into a ghost.

"The route opens for six tides during calving season," Coral said. "Voss wants the hold. I told him no."

Ilsa rolled the chart slowly, eyes never leaving his face. "And?"

"And I need to know if any of you are fool enough to follow me anyway."

Bo grinned. Tam didn't. Ferro said, quietly, "Who else is going?"

Which was, Coral thought, the whole problem with the world, stated plainly at seven in the morning on a dock that smelled of tar and salt.

Because Voss hadn't needed to buy him. He'd only needed to show him the map. And now the map was in Coral's head, burning a hole shaped like a sister, and there were six tides coming whether he said yes or no, and a hundred and fifty thousand reasons, and one page initialed in ink, and a promise he'd already broken in every way but one.

The question was which way he'd break it last.
