## 6. Ilsa's Map

Ilsa waited until the dishes were done.

That was the tell. In four years of running the high passes with her, I'd learned that Ilsa Rehn did nothing without a reason, and she never did anything important in passing. She dried the last bowl, set it on the shelf with the others, squared its edge to the counter like she was squaring herself, and then she unrolled the map.

Not on the table. On the floor. She knelt and weighted the corners with four tin mugs, and the rest of us drifted over one at a time the way people drift toward a fire, pretending we hadn't already decided to.

The map was hers. Not a chart you could buy—those stopped being printed after the Meridian came down off the shipping lanes and into the ice, and anyway no printer alive would sell one to my crew. This was drawn by hand, ink on waxed paper, the lines fine as spider silk and just as strong. The coastline. The sound. And cutting through all of it, in red, a route.

I knew that route. I'd known it before I could read properly. My father had traced it for me with a blunt pencil when I was nine years old, back when the glacier still let ships through twice a year instead of six tides a decade, and he'd said, *Coral, this is the only door in the world that opens from the inside.*

"Where did you get this?" I asked, though I already knew. She'd drawn it. Every contour line was in her hand.

"I started three years ago," Ilsa said. "Interviewing runners. Old pilots. A lighthouse keeper who's been watching the calving face since before either of us was born." She tapped the red line where it crossed the first narrows. "He told me the ice sings different notes depending on what year it's going to give way. I thought he was drunk. He wasn't."

Tam crouched at the edge of the paper, arms on his knees. Bo stood behind him, chewing the inside of his cheek. Ferro stayed by the stove with his bad leg stretched out, but his eyes were on the map, and they weren't leaving.

"You charted the calving route," I said. "For a job nobody's hired us for."

"For a job somebody will," Ilsa said. "Six tides this season. Maybe seven if the pressure ridge holds. That window comes once, Coral. It hasn't come in eleven years, and the men who watched it come last time were too old or too broke to use it." She looked up at me then, and there was no apology anywhere in her face. "So I got ready."

* * *

Here is what I want you to understand about Ilsa Rehn: she navigates the way other people breathe. It isn't skill exactly, though her skill is real and hard-won. It's more that she believes, down in the bedrock of herself, that every landscape has a logic, and that the logic can be read, and that reading it is not a gamble but a discipline. When she looks at a glacier she doesn't see a monster. She sees an argument, and she intends to win it.

Which is why, when she said *I got ready*, I felt the floor tilt under me the way it does on a ship.

Because I'd spent two days telling myself the letter was ash. Burned in the stove, gone, Voss's offer drifting up the flue with the smoke, and Coral Delacroix still free, still invisible, still measuring his life in miles instead of years. But Ilsa had been ready longer than Voss had been writing letters. The past hadn't knocked politely. It had walked in through the back door while I was doing dishes, wearing the faces of the four people I trusted most in the world.

"The fleet," Ferro said from the stove. "Tell him about the fleet."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. That was another tell. Ilsa didn't tighten her jaw over small things.

"Voss isn't coming alone," she said. "The Meridian Salvage Consortium filed papers in Port Anselm eight weeks ago. Three ships. Heavy equipment. And they've bought guides." She pulled a folded list from inside her coat and laid it beside the map, and I made myself look at the names.

Half the region. Half the guides I'd grown up with, drunk with, buried friends alongside. Kessler. The Ondar twins. Old Marta, who'd taught me star-fixes when my hands were too small for a sextant. Names I'd have trusted on any rope team in the world, lined up on the other side of a line I'd sworn off for twenty years.

"They're not buying their skill," Tam said. "They're buying them out. Every guide who knows that coast, signed and paid so nobody else can take the job." He glanced at me. "Except us."

"We're not for sale," Bo said. "We're not even listed. We don't exist." He said it lightly, the way Bo said everything, but I heard the edge under it. Bo had spent enough years not existing to know what it cost.

"That's the point," Ilsa said. "Voss bought every legal guide in the region because he doesn't need legal. He needs someone who'll take the smugglers' trail, the runner routes, the ways that don't appear on charts. He needs someone blacklisted." She finally looked straight at me, and I understood that every word since she'd unrolled the map had been aimed here, at this sentence, waiting. "He needs you, Coral. And he knows it. That's why the letter came to you and not to me."

* * *

I walked to the window. There wasn't much to see—the yard, the woodpile, the grey shoulder of the ridge behind the cabin—but I needed my back to them, because my face was doing something I didn't want them to read.

Twenty years ago I'd stood in a hearing room in Port Anselm while men in good coats explained to me that the loss of the *Meridian* was, per the findings, attributable to navigational error, and that the navigator's brother was available for questioning should further clarity be required. My sister had been dead nine days. They'd used the word *attributable* like it was a kindness. Then two countries had closed their borders to me, quietly, the way you close a door on a room where something died, and I had built a life in the cracks between them.

And now the cracks were leading somewhere. That was the unbearable part. All these years I'd thought the blacklist was a wall. It turned out to be a corridor, and Sable Voss had measured it, and Ilsa had charted it, and both of them had known, before I did, exactly where it ended.

"There's no version of this where I say yes," I said to the window.

"Probably not," Ilsa said. "But there might be a version where you lose the vote."

I turned around. She was still kneeling at the map, patient as tide.

* * *

Ferro spoke first, which surprised me. Ferro rarely spoke first about anything; he was a man who believed in triage, and triage meant the loudest wound waits.

"I've read the letter," he said. "The one you burned. You didn't burn the envelope." He held it up—he'd taken it out of the stove ash, smoothed it flat, and I felt something twist in my chest that wasn't quite anger. "It's addressed to *the crew of the Delacroix line*. Not to you. To all five of us. Voss knew we vote on things."

"He's manipulative," I said.

"He's thorough," Ferro said. "There's a difference. I've worked for manipulative men. They lie to you. Thorough men put the truth in front of you and let you do the lying to yourself." He set the envelope down on the map's corner, replacing a mug. "I'm voting yes."

"On what?" I said. "Nobody's said what the job even pays. Nobody's said what's in that hold. Voss didn't say, and none of you are asking, and that should frighten—"

"It's the inheritance," Bo said.

The room went very quiet. Bo rubbed the back of his neck, uncomfortable, the first time I'd ever seen him unsure of his own mouth.

"My cousin works freight out of Anselm," he said. "Consortium paperwork goes through his office. The manifest addendum lists the hold contents as *family effects, Delacroix*, appraised for salvage rights. Family effects, Coral. Your name. Your family's name on a cargo manifest twenty years sealed in the ice." He shrugged, helpless. "Everybody in the port knows except you. I figured you knew and didn't want to talk about it."

I hadn't known. Twenty years, and the thing my father had lost, the thing Maren had boarded that ship to help secure—I'd let myself believe it was gone, dissolved, unwritten. And it had been sitting in the ice the whole time, forty miles north, with my name on it, waiting for a tide.

I sat down. I didn't decide to; my legs made the decision for me.

* * *

Tam voted next. He did it standing, arms crossed, looking at the map rather than at me, which was his way of being kind.

"I was on a consortium crew once," he said. "Before you. Before any of this. You know why they let me go?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Because I flagged a load rating. Told them a rig wouldn't hold. They ran it anyway, a man died, and when I raised it again they called it insubordination." His jaw worked. "Every guide on that list took consortium money knowing how they treat crews. Marta took it. Kessler took it. Good people, hungry people—it's not a crime. But it means the fleet is run by men who buy silence." He looked at me now. "You've never once told me to shut up about a rig. I'm voting yes, and I'm not ashamed of it."

Bo voted yes without ceremony—"Somebody's got to carry the heavy end"—and then it was three to nothing and Ferro looked at me with his medic's patience, the patience of a man who has watched people die of things that were treatable if caught early.

"Coral," he said. "You don't have to lead it. You don't even have to come. But if we go without you, we go onto that ice without the only man alive who's crossed the narrows in a calving year. People die of that. I patch people. I'd rather not spend this job patching preventable things." He paused. "Also—and I say this as your friend—you've been dying slowly for twenty years and calling it a career. This job might kill you quick instead. Some part of you already knows that's the better trade."

"Ferro."

"I'm a medic. I'm allowed to say prognosis."

* * *

Ilsa voted last, and she did it by turning the map around.

She'd drawn it for me. I saw that only then—all those years of quiet interviews, the lighthouse keeper, the runners, the old pilots, and never a word to me, because she'd known I'd have shut it down, and so she'd built the case in secret the way you build anything worth having. And now she rotated the waxed paper one hundred and eighty degrees so the route pointed at me across the floorboards, and she said:

"I'm voting yes. And I'm voting for you specifically, not the job. Because I've charted every mile of this route, and there is one crossing on it—one—that I cannot solve on paper. The second narrows, at mid-tide, when the meltwater runs under the bridge ice. I've interviewed everyone who's ever done it. Do you know what they all say? They say *ask Delacroix*. The blacklisted man. The one who can't be hired. You are the missing piece of my map, Coral, and I am asking you, as your navigator, to stop hiding the last contour line."

The stove ticked. Snow slid off the roof outside. Five people in a small room, and the room had never felt smaller or warmer.

"You understand what you're asking," I said. My voice came out lower than I meant it to. "That ship killed my sister. Whatever's in that hold, whatever Voss wants, whatever truth is frozen in there—it cost me Maren, and it cost me my name, and I have spent twenty years making sure I never had to look at that water again. And you four are going to sit there and vote to sail me straight into it."

"Yes," Ilsa said simply. "Because we think you'll live through it. And we don't think you're living now."

* * *

I want to record honestly what happened next, because pride would edit it and pride has edited enough of my life.

I sat on the floor of my own cabin, next to a map drawn by the best navigator I'd ever known, and I lost a vote in front of my own crew, four to zero, and it was the first time in twenty years that losing felt like being found.

They believed in me. That was the whole weight of it. Not in my legend—half my legend was rumor and the other half was wanted posters. They believed in the man who checked the rigs twice, who carried Bo's pack when Bo wouldn't ask, who'd hired every one of them when no one else would touch them. Tam with his flagged loads. Ferro with his leg and his discharge nobody would explain. Bo with his mouth and his debts. Ilsa with her maps nobody official would credit. I'd collected the unemployable the way a beach collects driftwood, and I'd told myself it was charity, and it had never once been charity. It had been recognition. They were mine because I was theirs.

And they were voting to walk into the place that had broken me, carrying me with them.

"One condition," I said.

Four faces came up.

"We take the job, we play it my way. Rules. Non-negotiable." I held up a finger. "First: nobody speaks my sister's name on the ice. Not in grief, not in argument, not as leverage. If there's truth in that hold, it'll keep until we're off the water. Second: Voss gets nothing from us until the hold is open—not information, not cooperation, not one honest answer. He wrote to *the crew* because he thinks he can split us. He can't. Third—" and here I looked at each of them in turn, because the third rule was really for me— "nobody dies for my name. If it comes down to the inheritance or the rope team, we drop the inheritance. Every time. Swear it."

They swore it. Tam gruffly, Bo flippantly, Ferro solemnly, Ilsa with a single nod that carried more weight than the other three together.

Then Ilsa turned the map right side up again, took a pencil from behind her ear, and drew a fifth mark at the head of the route—a small, precise figure, a stick of a man with a guide's staff.

"Departure in three days," she said. "Ahead of the fleet. There's a runner's trail out of the valley that the consortium doesn't know exists."

"How do you know they don't know?"

"Because I'm the one who sold it to the runners," Ilsa said, and for the first time all night, she smiled. "Twenty years ago. Long story. Get some sleep, Captain. You start earning your contour line tomorrow."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I lay in the dark listening to the cabin settle and thinking about doors that open from the inside.

My father's phrase. He'd meant the glacier. But lying there, I kept hearing it differently—as if it were about me, about the sealed thing in my own chest, welded shut from the inside the way—my mind supplied, cruelly, unprompted—the way a hold might be welded shut during a sinking.

I didn't know why that image came to me. I'd never heard any account of the *Meridian*'s final minutes that mentioned welding. There were no accounts. That was the whole horror of it; the ship had gone into the ice with her secrets, and the inquiry had filled the silence with the easiest story available, and the easiest story had needed a villain, and the villain's brother was available for questioning.

I got up, lit the stove low, and sat with the burnt envelope in my hands. *The crew of the Delacroix line.* Voss had known us before we'd known ourselves.

Three days, Ilsa had said. Somewhere north, a glacier was groaning toward its six tides, and a fleet was loading, and half the guides of my childhood were counting money, and my sister's name was on a manifest in the dark.

I fed the envelope back into the stove and watched it catch, and this time I watched it all the way down to ash.

Tomorrow we'd plan. Tomorrow I'd dig out gear I'd sworn never to touch. Tomorrow the miles would start counting themselves toward a place I'd spent twenty years walking away from.

Outside, far off, so faint it might have been wind, the ridge gave a long groan of settling snow—and I lay back down and pretended, for a few more hours, that I couldn't hear the mountains opening their one door.
