## 7. Six Tides

I didn't sleep after the vote. Not because of the decision—because of how easily they'd made it. Four hands up before I'd finished saying no. Tam had raised his so fast it looked like a reflex, like the body answering before the mind could talk itself out of anything.

By grey light I was at the table with Ilsa's map spread flat under a coffee tin and two mugs, and she was already there, already waiting, like she'd known I'd come down eventually. She poured me a cup without asking. That's the thing about Ilsa. She doesn't ask. She just makes space for you to arrive.

"Walk me through it," I said.

She tapped the chart where the fjord narrowed into the Serrano Reach. "The glacier's been retreating eight meters a year. Calving season opens the channel along the ice face—the meltwater backs up behind the terminal moraine, then punches through." Her finger traced a line that looked less like a route and more like a scar. "Six tides. Maybe five if the weather turns. The water rises fast enough to float a shallow-draft boat through the gap between the moraine and the calving wall, then drops again. After the sixth tide, the channel refreezes or collapses. Nobody knows which. Nobody's ever needed to know."

"And the Meridian."

"Here." Her finger stopped on a notch in the coastline west of the calving face. "The survey photos from last summer show her hull exposed. Forty meters of it. The rest is still in the ice." She looked up. "Coral. She's not buried anymore. She's *waiting*."

I drank my coffee instead of answering. It was terrible coffee. Ferro bought it in bulk from a supplier who I'm fairly sure also sells ballast. But my hands needed something to do, and the mug gave them an excuse.

"The fleet," I said finally.

"Three days out of Halden Harbor. Eleven vessels registered for salvage permits in the Reach this season—that's public record. Voss's lead ship, the *Cold Antler*, plus nine others flying company colors, one research charter that's probably not doing research." She pulled a second sheet from under the map, hand-drawn, dense with her small sharp notation. "Main channel puts them at the ice shelf in four days if they push hard. We take the smugglers' trail over the shoulder of Kestrel Ridge, we're there in three. Half a tide ahead, maybe more."

"You've already planned this."

"I started planning it the night you burned Voss's letter." She said it without apology. "You burn things slowly, Coral. You hold them first. I watched your face while the edges caught. That's not a man who wants the past gone. That's a man negotiating with it."

"I could fire you for insubordination."

"No, you couldn't. I'm the only navigator alive who's charted the Reach in winter." She smiled, and it was the smile of someone holding all the cards and enjoying the view. "Also you don't pay enough to fire anyone."

* * *

We spent the day on numbers, because numbers were safer than names.

Tide tables taped to the wall. Weather windows sketched in pencil and argued over in pencil, because ink implies commitment. Tam and Bo went through the rigging inventory twice, then a third time when Bo found a carabiner with a hairline gate crack that would have ended someone's life above open water. Ferro laid out the medical kit on the kitchen table like a surgeon preparing for war—sutures, splints, the good painkillers he keeps locked and the ones he doesn't, hypothermia wraps, a folding litter.

"We're carrying too much weight," Bo said, standing over Ferro's kit with his arms crossed.

"We're carrying exactly enough weight for the things that will go wrong."

"Then we should carry more, because everything goes wrong."

They bickered like brothers. They'd been crew for six years and still hadn't learned each other's rhythms well enough to stop, or maybe they had learned them and the bickering *was* the rhythm. I stood by the window and let the sound wash over me. It occurred to me, distantly, that I had built this crew out of people no one else would hire, and that somewhere along the way they had become the only people whose voices I wanted in a room.

Tam caught me watching. "Boss. Say the word and we start packing."

"Not yet."

"It's been 'not yet' since dawn."

"I know how long it's been, Tam."

He held up both hands, palms out. Tam's whole philosophy of life was palms out. He'd been a rigger on container ships for eleven years until the accident that wasn't his fault but was pinned on him anyway—he never talked about it, and I never asked, because that's the deal we all signed with each other. This crew runs on a mutual amnesty. Everyone here did something or was done to. Nobody checks the paperwork.

But Voss's letter had broken the amnesty's one rule without saying a word, because the letter wasn't about cargo or salvage rights or money. It was about the Meridian, and the Meridian was about Maren, and Maren was the thing I'd spent twenty years building walls around, and now four people I trusted were volunteering to climb those walls with me and none of them knew what was on the other side.

I called the meeting at dusk.

* * *

They came in smelling of rope oil and cold air, and they arranged themselves around the table the way crews do—loose, easy, chairs tilted, Ferro perched on the counter like always—and I stood at the head of the table and realized I had no speech prepared. I'd had twenty years to prepare one. All I had was a map with a dead ship on it and four faces waiting.

"Here's what I know," I said. "Voss wants the Delacroix inheritance. Deeds, papers, whatever my family locked in that hold before she sailed. He thinks I want it too. Maybe he's right. What I know for certain is that he's spending a fortune to get there, and men like Voss don't spend fortunes on sentiment. There's something in that hold worth more than the crossing costs, and he needs *me*—or someone like me—to get him through ice nobody else can read."

"So we beat him there," Ilsa said. "That was the vote."

"That was the vote. Now hear the terms." I put my palms flat on the table, and everyone went quiet, because they'd seen this posture before and knew it preceded rules. "If we go, we go as a salvage crew. Nothing else. No questions about the wreck's history. No questions about passengers, manifest, cause of sinking. Nothing about—" I stopped. Started again, quieter. "Nothing about my sister. Not her name, not the anniversary, not the official report, nothing. If a document surfaces with her name on it, you hand it to me unopened and you forget you saw it. That's rule one."

Silence. Ferro's eyes flicked toward Ilsa, and Ilsa's stayed level on mine, and I understood that some conversation had already happened between them that I hadn't been part of. Fine. Crews have their own weather.

"Rule two," I said. "Nobody dies for cargo. Not papers, not deeds, not gold if there is gold. If the mountain asks for a price, we pay in time and we pay in pride and we walk away. I've buried enough people. I won't bury any of you for a box of family secrets."

"Coral—" Bo started.

"That's the term. Take it or leave it."

"Rule three?" Tam asked, and there was a thread of a smile in it, because Tam always found the joke in the wreckage.

"Rule three is there is no rule three. Two rules are enough. Three means I'm enjoying myself."

Bo laughed first, then Ferro, then Tam, and even Ilsa's mouth bent at one corner, which from her was applause. And I stood there feeling something I hadn't felt in years, something I distrusted immediately and completely: I wanted to do this. Not for the inheritance. Not to spite Voss. I wanted to cross that ice with these four people, to be inside a plan that had their fingerprints on it, to move toward the thing instead of circling it for another decade.

Memory is a burden, I'd told myself for twenty years. But Ilsa had redrawn it as a map, and maps are made to be followed.

"Then it's settled," Ilsa said, already pulling the charts toward her. "Fleet leaves Halden in three days. We leave tomorrow, grey light, ahead of them."

"Tomorrow?" Bo sat up straight. "I thought—"

"The fleet leaves in three days. We need the margin. Tide waits for no one and neither does Voss." I rolled up the map. My hands were steady. That surprised me. "Pack light. Check everything twice. Ferro, double the thermal wraps—we'll be sleeping on ice. Tam, I want the rope doubled at every anchor point, no exceptions, no economy. Bo—"

"Yeah?"

"Don't argue with Ferro about the med kit."

"He's bringing too much—"

"Bo."

He shut up. Grinning.

Ilsa lingered when the others filed out. She stood at the window beside me, looking at the darkening street, the mountains behind the rooftops going blue and then black.

"You set the rules," she said, "but you didn't say why we're really going."

"You heard the vote. We're going for the salvage rights."

"Mm." She picked up her coat. At the door she paused, not turning around. "For what it's worth—I think you're not running toward Voss's money. I think you're running toward whatever's in that journal you keep at the bottom of your pack, the one with the strap worn through, the one you've carried since I've known you and never once opened where anyone could see."

The door closed behind her.

I sat alone at the table for a long time. Then I dug the journal out of my pack—leather gone stiff, the strap cracked nearly through, my sister's initials pressed into the cover in handwriting I'd have known blindfolded—and I set it in the middle of the map, on top of the Serrano Reach, on top of the little notch where forty meters of hull waited in the ice.

I didn't open it. Not tonight. Tomorrow, maybe, on the trail, in some cold camp with the stove ticking. Or maybe not until I was standing in front of the wreck itself, where the words would have somewhere to land.

Outside, the wind had shifted north—the wind that comes before calving season, the one the old guides used to say meant the mountain was clearing its throat.

Three days until the fleet sailed. One until we did.

And somewhere south of us, in Halden Harbor, Sable Voss was boarding a ship with my name already written into his plans—which meant he knew something about the Meridian that I didn't, and I intended to arrive before he could use it.
