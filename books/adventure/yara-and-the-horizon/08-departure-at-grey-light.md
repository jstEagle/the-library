## 8. Departure at Grey Light

They left before the light had a name for itself.

Coral stood in the yard behind Ferro's clinic at four in the morning and watched the crew load the sledges by headlamp, and he thought: this is the last hour in which I am still a man who said no. After this hour I would be a man who was on his way. The difference sounded small. It wasn't. He had spent twenty years building a life out of refusals—no to that pass, no to that client, no to that job, no, no, no—and a refusal was a wall you could lean against. A yes was a door with wind behind it.

"Delacroix." Ilsa's voice came low across the dark. "You're staring at your boots like they owe you money."

"They do." He shouldered his pack. The weight of it settled wrong, or right—he couldn't tell anymore which. "Load order. Tam and Bo take point sledges, Ferro rides middle, you navigate from second position. I'll sweep."

"You always sweep."

"I always sweep." It was true. The back of a line was where a guide could watch his people and not be watched himself. Twenty years of practice at being unobserved in plain sight.

The smugglers' trail left town through the drainage cut behind the tannery, a gap in the fence that had been a gap since before the border commission drew its lines, and Coral took them into it one at a time, counting heads the way he counted everything now—miles, knots, breaths, people. Four sledges. Five souls. Six tides.

He did not say that last number out loud. Nobody did. They all carried it anyway; he could feel it riding in the cold air between them like a fifth rigger.

* * *

The first three hours were the kind of ground Coral could have walked asleep, and part of him wished he were asleep, because then the trail would be doing what trails did in dreams—unspooling without consequence. Instead every step landed with the specific gravity of decision. The smugglers' route ran north along the river gorge, below the customs road, below the survey stations, below the whole tidy architecture of permission. It was a path made of other people's need: runners and refugees and men like him, men whose names had been struck from two countries' registries in the same season, men who had learned that borders were just lines drawn by people who'd never been cold.

Ilsa fell back beside him as the gorge narrowed. She didn't speak for a long while. That was one of the reasons he trusted her—she understood that silence was a tool, not a void to be filled.

Finally she said, "You're going to ask me why I charted the calving route before you agreed to the job."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to brood about it instead. You brood loudly." She adjusted her hood against the wind funnelling up the gorge. "So I'll save us both. I charted it because a navigator who can't chart the impossible route is just a woman with a compass. And because—" she paused, and Coral heard her choose her words with unusual care, "—because I've watched you carry something for twenty years, Coral, and I've never once asked what it weighs. But I know what it costs. I see the bill come due every time someone says the word *Meridian* within earshot of you."

Coral kept his eyes on the trail. The ice underfoot was old river ice, grey and crazed, and it made a sound like distant crockery when Bo's sledge crossed a pressure seam ahead.

"It doesn't cost anything," he said. "It's paid off."

"Liar," Ilsa said, almost gently, and dropped back to her position without waiting for an answer.

* * *

They stopped at dawn in the lee of a rockfall, and Ferro went down the line with water and salt tabs, checking hands, checking eyes. He was a compact man with a surgeon's stillness and a voice he seemed to ration like fuel. When he reached Coral he looked at him a moment longer than the check required.

"Your color's bad," Ferro said.

"My color is always bad."

"Not like this. This is the color of a man who hasn't slept." Ferro pressed a tab into his palm and closed Coral's fingers over it. "Eat it. Sleep debt is a liability I can't stitch."

Coral swallowed the tab and didn't argue, because arguing would have meant explaining that sleep had left him three nights ago, the night after Voss's letter, and that when it finally came back it came back wearing Maren's face. In the dream she was seventeen and sitting on the dock at Saltmere with a chart spread across her knees, tracing the route they'd planned together, the voyage that was supposed to make the Delacroix name mean something again. In the dream she looked up and said, *You're late, Cor.* And he woke with his heart going like a runaway sledge and the ceiling of his room pressing down like ice.

Tam appeared at his elbow, chewing something that might once have been jerky. "Fleet status," he said. "Bo's cousin works the harbor office. Three ships provisioned, guides signed on yesterday. They move on the main channel day after tomorrow."

"We're a day and a half ahead if we hold pace."

"If we hold pace," Tam repeated. He was a big man gone lean at the edges, with a rigger's forearms and a gambler's face, and he studied Coral the way he studied weather. "You know what I keep thinking? I keep thinking a man who's been blacklisted twenty years doesn't break the law for money. Money we'd split six ways barely covers the risk."

"What does he break it for, then?" Bo called from the sledges, grinning around a mouthful of bread.

"That's what I'm asking," Tam said, not taking his eyes off Coral.

Coral met the look. There was no cruelty in Tam—there never had been—but there was precision, and precision was worse. "Ask me at the glacier," Coral said. "If I still have an answer, I'll give it to you."

Tam held the look another beat, then nodded slowly, like a man filing a debt he intended to collect. "Fair enough. For now."

* * *

Midmorning brought the first hard ground: the Switchbacks, a stairway of broken scree climbing out of the gorge toward the high shelf, steep enough that they roped the sledges and hauled them in relays. It was work Coral loved—the honest arithmetic of it, weight against angle, grip against gravity—and for an hour the knot in his chest loosened for the first time in days. His body knew this country. His body had never been exiled from anywhere; only his name had.

Halfway up, Bo slipped.

Nothing dramatic—a foothold rolled, a boot skated, and suddenly eighty kilos of rigger was sliding toward the drop with a sledge line wrapped twice around his wrist. Coral moved before thought, dropped flat, got his axe pick seated behind a boulder, and caught the line across his forearm. The burn went up to the elbow. Bo swung, found the slope with his boots, and stopped.

For a moment nobody breathed. Then Bo laughed—that easy, unkillable laugh of his—and said, "Good catch, boss."

"Unwrap your wrist," Coral said. His voice came out flatter than he intended. "Line around your wrist on scree. We talked about this."

"We did." Bo unwound it, sheepish, rubbing the skin. "Habit."

"Habits are how mountains collect their fees." Coral coiled the line with hands that were not quite steady, and hated that they weren't, and covered it by turning away to reset the anchor. Behind him he heard Ferro murmur something to Bo, low, and Bo's laugh again, softer. The crew knitting itself tighter around its own small crisis, the way crews did, the way his crew had never gotten to do because he'd never let them close enough to have a crisis worth sharing.

That was the thought that climbed the rest of the Switchbacks with him: *you kept them at rope-length so nothing could cost you again.* And here he was, leading them into the one place where the interest on his old debt might come due in full.

At the top of the climb, the high shelf opened before them—the white road north, the calving route, the way to the Meridian. Wind-scoured ice running to the horizon under a sky the colour of unpolished steel. Somewhere out there, sealed in the flank of the glacier, was a ship with his sister's bones in it and a truth welded shut inside a hold.

Ilsa came up beside him and unfolded her charts against the wind, weighting the corners with stones. "Two days to the ice shelf if the weather holds. Fleet takes four on the main channel, five if it doesn't." She glanced sideways at him. "We're going to get there first, Coral. Say it out loud so it becomes real."

"We're going to get there first," he said.

It became real. He felt it settle over the crew like a change in the air pressure—Tam cracking his knuckles, Bo whooping once, sharp and brief, Ferro simply nodding as though confirming a diagnosis. Five people standing on a shelf of ice at the top of the world, committed.

And Coral stood among them feeling the strangest thing: not fear, exactly, though the fear was there, humming under everything like current through wire. What he felt was the absence of the wall. For twenty years the refusals had held the world at bay, and now there was no refusal left between him and the thing he'd sworn off, and the openness of that was vertiginous. A man could fall a long way into open country.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below the shelf rim, tents pitched in the lee of an old moraine, stove-light orange against the blue snow-dark. Rations, melt-water, gear checks. The rituals of the trail, performed in the order they were always performed, and Coral took a strange comfort in that—in the fact that some things stayed legible even when a man's life had stopped being so.

After the others turned in, he sat outside his tent with his back against the moraine stone and watched the north. The glacier was too far to see, but he could feel it the way sailors feel a coast in fog—an enormous presence off in the dark, patient, indifferent, holding what it held.

Twenty years ago he had stood on a dock and watched the Meridian's wake flatten into nothing and told himself his sister was dead because of him—because of the fight, because of the words he'd said, because he'd put her on that ship with anger still standing between them like a third passenger. The official inquiry had needed a cause and found one in the Delacroix name: the family that lost the cargo, the brother who'd pushed the schedule, the sister who'd signed aboard. Two countries had agreed it was tidier that way. He had let them agree. Shame had fit him so well by then that he'd worn it like issued gear.

Now Voss wanted him to walk back into that story and pull it open. And some traitorous part of Coral—not hopeful, he refused to call it hopeful—some part of him had already started walking.

"Coral." Ilsa's voice, quiet, from the next tent. Not emerging. Just speaking into the dark, the way old partners did. "You should sleep."

"In a minute."

"She'd want you to sleep."

He went very still. "You don't know who—"

"No," Ilsa said. "I don't. And I'm not asking. I'm telling you that whoever she is, whatever she is to you—she wouldn't want you walking onto that ice running on fumes and guilt. Guilt makes bad footing. I've buried people over less."

The wind moved along the shelf rim with a sound like a long exhalation. Coral looked north into the dark, at the place where the glacier must be, and found that his throat had gone tight around an answer.

"Goodnight, Ilsa," he managed.

"Goodnight, boss."

* * *

He slept, in the end—two hours, dreamless, black and merciful—and woke in the grey hour before dawn to the sound of Bo kicking snow off the stove pipe and Tam swearing softly at frozen buckles, and for a moment, half-asleep, Coral didn't know where the dread was coming from. Then it arrived, all of it, the whole freight of it: the fleet, the tide count, the ship in the ice, the sister in the ship. And underneath the dread, quieter but unmistakable, something else. Something that felt disgracefully like readiness.

They broke camp in the dark and moved out at first grey light, five figures and four sledges strung out on the white road, heading north into the open country where no refusal remained.

Behind them, far to the south, faint as a scratch on glass, the harbour horn sounded the fleet's morning shift change.

Three ships. Day after tomorrow.

Coral lengthened his stride and did not look back.
