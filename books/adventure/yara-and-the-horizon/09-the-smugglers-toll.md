## 9. The Smugglers' Toll

The smugglers' trail stopped being a trail two hours after dawn and became a suggestion instead—a seam of grey scree threading between black rock faces, climbing toward a col that didn't appear on any map worth trusting. Ilsa navigated it the way she did everything: quietly, precisely, with a folded paper chart she guarded like scripture. No GPS. Nothing that pinged. Tam had stripped every transmitter from our packs at the trailhead, down to the emergency beacons sewn into our jackets' collars, and Bo had complained about it for a mile before Ferro told him to complain quieter.

We crossed the border of the country that had banned me twenty years ago somewhere around mid-morning, though there was no line in the snow to tell you so. Just a shift in the wind's direction and the feeling I always got crossing that particular latitude—the sensation of walking into a room where everyone already knows your name and none of them are glad about it.

"Smile," Tam said, falling into step beside me. "We're about to meet your fan club."

"They're not my fans."

"No," he agreed. "That was sarcasm. It's how I cope."

* * *

The toll station sat where the smugglers' trail narrowed between two walls of ice-scoured granite—a place called the Throat by people who used this route and didn't want it known. There was a hut built from shipping crates and salvaged hull plating, a stovepipe leaking woodsmoke, and a rope stretched across the gap with tin cans strung on it like a wind chime that only played warnings.

Three runners came out to meet us. I knew the middle one.

Kess Ardaug had been running this border since before my blacklist, back when she was a courier with quick hands and a quicker mouth, and time hadn't softened either. She'd gone grey at the temples but her eyes were still the color of a blade kept oiled. She looked at me across forty meters of frozen ground and started laughing before she even reached us.

"Coral Delacroix." She said it loud enough for her two companions to hear, which was the point. "I heard you were dead."

"You heard wrong."

"I heard you were dead twice, actually. Once in the south, once after the Meridian business. People love saying you're dead. You're very easy to say dead." She stopped an arm's length away, hands loose at her sides in a way that wasn't casual at all. Her companions flanked wide—professional spacing, one high on the rocks with a rifle slung but not aimed, one low near the rope. "And now here you are, walking my Throat with four ghosts of your own, headed north in calving season, three days ahead of a salvage fleet that's been buying up guides like bread before a storm."

Word travels fast when money travels faster.

"We're paying the toll," I said. "Same as anyone."

"Oh, you're paying." Kess grinned. "The question is what with."

Ferro stepped forward and laid out our cash reserve on a crate lid—what remained after outfitting, after Ilsa's charts, after Bo's medical bills from a life I didn't ask about. It was maybe half of what the toll should have been. Maybe a third of what it would be, today, for five armed travelers trying to beat a fleet through a window that opened once a year.

Kess counted it without touching it. Then she looked up at me with something almost like pity.

"That's a down payment," she said. "On a much larger conversation."

* * *

They took us into the hut because the wind was rising and because Kess, whatever else she was, didn't let people freeze on her doorstep—it was bad for repeat business. Inside smelled of kerosene, wet wool, and old smoke. A stove made from a fuel drum ticked as it heated. Maps papered one wall, layered over each other like sediment, decades of routes drawn and redrawn in different inks.

She poured tea nobody asked for. Five chipped metal cups. Herself too.

"So," she said, settling onto a crate across from me while her people stood by the door and mine stood behind me, and the geometry of the room made it very clear who was visiting whom. "Tell me why Coral Delacroix is going to the Meridian."

The name landed in the room like a dropped tool.

Tam shifted his weight behind me. Bo stopped fidgeting. Ilsa went very still, the way she did when she was recalculating.

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said.

"Coral." Kess blew on her tea. "Twenty years ago you were the best mountain guide on this border, and then one night a passenger ferry went into the ice with your sister aboard and your name on the manifest as expedition lead, and two governments needed someone to blame, and they chose you. And you have not set foot north of the Grey Line since. Now you cross it illegally, off-season, ahead of a salvage operation whose backers have offered my people more money than I've seen in ten years just to report sightings." She sipped. "There is exactly one thing north of the Grey Line worth that arithmetic. So. Why?"

"The toll," I said. "Take the money."

"The money buys you passage. Information is a separate economy." She set the cup down. "Here's how this works now, Delacroix. Your cash covers the rope and the stove and my discretion about the five of you passing through. But my discretion has a shelf life. The fleet's advance scouts will reach the main channel tomorrow. When they do, they'll start asking border people about other parties on the ice, and my competitors will sell them everything they know, including you, because the fleet pays better than you ever will. Unless—" she spread her hands "—I decide you're worth protecting. And protection costs. Not money. Money I have enough of, or will, once this season's done. What I'm short on, this far north, is *useful things*. Names. Routes. Reasons."

"You want information."

"I want leverage," she corrected pleasantly. "Information is just leverage you can carry."

Behind me, Ferro said quietly, "Coral," — a warning, or a question.

I looked at the maps on her wall. Decades of ink. A whole life drawn in routes, the way I'd tried to draw mine in miles. And I understood, with the flat clarity that sometimes comes in cold rooms, that I had walked into this hut carrying exactly one currency, and it wasn't cash.

It was the story I'd spent twenty years refusing to tell.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about shame: it isn't heavy. That's the trick of it. It weighs nothing at all, which is why you can carry it everywhere, up every mountain, through every border crossing, and never notice the cost until you try to set it down—and discover it's fused to you, grown through you like rebar through concrete, and setting it down means tearing out a piece of yourself along with it.

I set the cup of tea down first. Small delay. Bought me a breath.

"In 1999," I said, "I guided a private expedition across the Vantaa Shelf. Eleven clients, two assistant guides. My sister Maren was one of them—not a client. Crew. She'd talked her way aboard because we were supposed to do the Horizon Traverse together afterward, the two of us, the route we'd been planning since we were kids." The words came out level. Practiced-level, which is its own kind of damning. "A storm closed in early. We diverted to the coast road. The Meridian was making the same run the same night—passenger ferry, ice-hardened hull, running late in the season because her owners were greedy about schedules. She took the inside channel to save six hours."

"And?" Kess said softly.

"And I was on the ridge above the channel when she went in. I saw the lights go under." I made myself keep looking at Kess, because looking away would have been worse than anything I could say next. "The inquiry found my diversion plan had put my party on terrain that forced the Meridian's captain to take the inside channel—my route report, filed with the harbor authority, was cited as contributing to the traffic decision. Two countries revoked my certifications. One prosecuted. Neither cared that the finding made no sense, because neither wanted to look at the Meridian's owners, who had friends in both capitals."

"And did it make sense?" Kess asked. "The finding?"

The question hung there. Behind me, Ilsa breathed out slowly through her nose—the sound of a navigator hearing a chart contradicted.

"No," I said. "It didn't make sense. My route report covered a ridge traverse eleven kilometers from the channel. There was no mechanism by which it affected ferry routing. I knew that at the time. I said so, loudly, in three inquiries, and it didn't matter, because the owners' lawyers were better than my grief, and grief makes a man sloppy." I picked the tea back up just to have something in my hands. "So I stopped arguing. I stopped being certified. I became what you see: a guide who works below the line, for clients who don't ask questions, in countries that don't extradite. Twenty years."

The stove ticked. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the Throat and made the tin-can rope sing, a thin discordant note that ran through the walls.

"Why now?" Kess said. "Why break the exile for this?"

"Because the wreck is surfacing. Because the glacier is giving it back whether I want it or not. And because—" I stopped.

Because there's proof in that hold. Because my sister welded a door shut from the inside and no one has ever asked why. Because I burned Sable Voss's letter and couldn't sleep afterward, which means some part of me already knew I'd come.

"Because I need to read a document that's in that hold," I finished. "One document. Everything else is negotiable."

Kess studied me for a long moment, and whatever she was looking for, she apparently found, because something in her face shifted from appraiser to something older and wearier.

"My brother ran cargo," she said. "South routes. Drowned in '94 when a company skiff went down overloaded, because the manifests lied and the inspectors were paid. They blamed the skipper. He was nineteen." She drank her tea. "So. Now we understand each other's currencies."

"Now we do."

"Here's the price, then, and it's fixed." She held up fingers. "One: your cash stands as the base toll. Two: your story—which I'll hold, not sell, provided you keep to terms I'll give you in a moment. Three: you tell me what the fleet's backers are after. Not the salvage. The *specifics*. Who hired them. What they expect to find. Because if there's a company name attached to this, I have a list, and I have long memories, and I would very much like to know if the Meridian's owners are on it."

"That's the part I can't give you yet," I said. "Because I don't know it myself. Voss kept the contract sealed. But—" I exhaled. "Sable Voss is fronting it. And Sable Voss doesn't spend his own money. Whoever's behind him has deep water under their keel, and if I learn the name before the fleet reaches the wreck, you'll have it within a day. Runner's word on it."

"A day's delay on a name." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Terms, then. You pass through my Throat tonight. You camp beyond the second moraine, not the first—I won't have your fire visible from the high trail. If the fleet scouts reach me asking questions, I sell them a false route east for as long as it holds, and when it stops holding, you're gone and I never saw you. And if your crew talks—" her eyes moved across them, unhurried, "—anyone, anywhere, anytime—if one word of this conversation leaves this hut through a mouth other than yours, I will make sure both governments know exactly where Coral Delacroix is standing, and I will collect the bounty personally. Do we understand each other?"

"We do."

"Then we're done." She stood, and so did everyone else, the room rearranging itself around her. "Rope drops in ten minutes. Take the left seam past the serac—it looks wrong but it's the right one; the right seam killed two men last spring." At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at me with the ghost of the grin. "Delacroix. For what it's worth—the story you just told? It's the first version of it I've heard that sounded true."

"It's the first version I've told."

"I know." She tapped the doorframe twice, some runner's superstition. "That's why it cost you. Come on, then. Night's short and the ice doesn't wait for grief."

* * *

Outside, the wind had sharpened. The light was failing into that blue-grey hour when the mountains stop being scenery and become fact. Ilsa fell into step beside me as we crossed to the rope, and for fifty meters she said nothing, which from Ilsa was a paragraph.

Finally: "You didn't have to do that."

"Yes, I did."

"There were other ways—"

"Not ones that held past tomorrow night." I checked the rope anchor out of habit, testing the tension. "She'd have sold us inside a day anyway. Better she owns the truth from me than buys a version of it from someone else."

"That's not what I meant." Ilsa pulled her hood tighter against the wind. When she spoke again her voice was lower, pitched under the gusts, meant only for me. "You carried that alone for twenty years. Through every job. Every campfire. We've eaten beside you for three seasons and you never—" She stopped herself, restarted. "I'm glad you put it down. Even if it was a toll."

I didn't answer right away. The tin-can rope sang above us. Beyond the Throat, the glacier field waited, white and enormous and indifferent, holding everything it had ever taken.

"It's not down," I said finally. "It's just... in someone else's hands for a while. Lighter that way."

We crossed. On the far side the land climbed steeply toward the second moraine, and we made our camp in the lee Kess had named, stoves lit low, fires screened. Tam took first watch without being asked. Bo was asleep almost before his bag was unrolled, Ferro checking his pulse out of some medic's habit even so.

I lay awake longer than I should have, staring at the roof of the tent, doing the accounting I always did—the miles behind, the miles ahead—except tonight the numbers wouldn't settle, because for the first time in twenty years I'd told the story forward instead of carrying it backward, and it had changed shape in the telling. Smaller, somehow. Or maybe just finally the right size.

Sleep came late. And when it came, it brought the dream it always brought—the lights of the Meridian going under—but for the first time, at the edge of it, another detail surfaced that had never been there before: a door, steel-grey, welded shut from the inside, and behind it, faint as a heartbeat through a hull, the sound of hammering.

Someone was alive in there. Someone had been sealing something in—or keeping something out.

I woke before dawn with my heart slamming and the cold sweat freezing at my hairline, and I lay listening to the glacier groan in the dark, and I thought: *Voss knew. He knew what happened inside that ship.*

Two ridges north, a scout's fire flickered on the high trail where no fire should be—too far east for Kess's people, too fresh for coincidence.

The fleet had found our scent a day early.
