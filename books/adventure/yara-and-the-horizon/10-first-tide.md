## 10. First Tide

Ilsa Vann had charted water for eleven years, and she had never once trusted a map she hadn't drawn herself. This morning, standing on a shelf of grey ice that groaned like an old ship settling, she distrusted her own.

The tide was coming in.

She could feel it before she saw it—a change in the pressure underfoot, the faint shudder that traveled up through her boot soles and into her knees, as though something enormous below were turning over in its sleep. She knelt, pulled off one glove with her teeth, and pressed her bare palm flat against the ice.

Cold bit through her skin in seconds. But beneath the cold there it was: the pulse. Slow. Rhythmic. The sea breathing against the underside of the world.

"Reading?" Coral's voice behind her, level as always, pitched low so it wouldn't carry across the shelf.

"Rising." She checked her watch against the tide tables she'd laminated herself, back in the smelly harbor town where they'd bought their last fuel. "Forty minutes to peak. Maybe less. The charts are from last season." She didn't say what that meant. He already knew. Last season, this channel hadn't existed at all.

She got up and walked to the edge.

* * *

The route—if you could call it that—opened ahead of them like a wound in the glacier. Two walls of ice, forty meters high, leaning toward each other the way drunks lean on doorframes, and between them a floor of sea ice that the rising tide would lift and flex and test. Beyond the first narrows she could see open leads of black water, seams of it threading through the white like veins through marble. In six hours those veins would widen. In twelve, if the calving forecast held even loosely, the whole floor would go to slush and then to sea.

And after that—nothing. No route. No retreat. The mountain would close its fist for another year.

Ilsa stood at the lip of it and felt the old familiar split inside her chest: half of her wanting to kneel down and worship the thing, half of her wanting to run.

She'd grown up on a fishing boat off the northern coast, a girl who learned to read water before she learned to read words. Her father used to say the sea never lied to you, it just told you the truth whether you wanted it or not. Ice was different. Ice lied constantly. It looked solid when it wasn't. It looked broken when it would hold. It made promises with its frozen face and broke every one of them from underneath. Every navigator who'd died in these latitudes had trusted ice exactly once too often.

So she did what she always did. She took out the notebook.

The notebook was her religion. Waterproof paper, grease pencil, measurements logged four times daily since they'd left the smugglers' trail: air temp, water temp under the leads, wind direction, cloud ceiling, the width of every crack they crossed, the sound each one made when tapped. Tam called it her prayer book. He wasn't wrong. It was the only conversation she knew how to have with a place like this—patient, repetitive, humble. You asked the ice the same questions over and over until it forgot to lie.

She wrote: *0612. Tide rising faster than table. Lead at marker 3 widened ~40cm overnight. Wind NE light. Barometer steady but I don't believe it.*

Then she closed the book and allowed herself thirty seconds—the whole thirty, no cheating—to simply look.

Because here was the other thing, the thing she'd never said aloud to anyone, the thing that lived in her next to the fear like two fish in the same net: it was beautiful. God help her, it was beautiful. The dawn light came down the glacier face in long pink blades, and the ice drank it and held it, glowing from inside like something alive. The pressure ridges along the channel walls reared up in blue-white waves, frozen mid-collapse, sculptures no human hand could make or ruin. And beneath everything, that pulse. That vast slow heartbeat. Standing here was like standing on the chest of something sleeping, and knowing—it was not going to stay asleep for your sake. Not for anyone's sake.

The mountain did not know they were coming. That was the truth she kept returning to, the truth she built her whole discipline around. They had deadlines. The tide had none. The fleet behind them had schedules and investors and radio chatter; the glacier had only physics, patient and total, indifferent as gravity. You did not race a mountain. You begged a temporary corridor through its indifference, and you paid whatever toll it named, and you thanked it afterward for letting you live.

"Ilsa."

Coral again. She turned. He stood ten meters back with Ferro, and behind them Tam and Bo were bringing the sledges forward in the staggered formation they'd drilled, one team always anchored while the other moved. Good. Her crew. Her strange, unemployable, stubborn crew.

"The narrows," Coral said. "Your call."

Her call. Four days ago that sentence would have been a joke. Coral Delacroix did not give away decisions; he rationed them like a man who'd learned the hard way that trust gets people killed. On the trail he walked point by default, silent, reading the ice with some instinct she suspected came from grief rather than skill. But somewhere past the border runners' camp, something had shifted—or maybe it had been shifting since the vote, since five people sat around a stove and decided to take the job with him or without him, and he'd looked at them with an expression she still couldn't name. Since then, when the route forked, he waited. When the ice spoke, he looked at her for the translation.

It terrified her more than the crevasses did.

"Narrows first," she said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt. "Single file, full spacing, probes on every step. We cross at the tail of low tide, while the floor is settled. Once we're through we don't stop until the high ground past the second lead." She pointed with her probe. "There. That rise. If the tide does anything ugly, that's our island."

Ferro squinted at it. "How long to reach it?"

"Two hours at loaded pace. Three if the floor argues."

"And if the floor argues harder than three hours?"

Ilsa met his eyes. Ferro was the oldest of them, gray at the temples, with hands that had stitched men together in places she didn't ask about. He asked questions the way doctors did—not to be cruel, but because pretending was how patients died.

"Then we swim," she said. "And swimming is not a plan. It's a eulogy with wet sleeves. So we don't let it come to that."

Nobody laughed. That was fine. It hadn't been a joke.

* * *

They crossed the narrows in the order she chose, which was itself a small revolution: Bo first, because he was lightest and quickest and if the floor failed she wanted it to fail on someone who might survive the fall; then Tam hauling the lead sledge; then Ferro; then herself, logging as she went; and Coral last, because someone had to be rear anchor, and because when she'd assigned him that position he'd held her gaze for a long moment and then nodded once, and she understood that he'd heard what she was really saying. *You are not expendable. None of us are. We do this my way or we don't do it.*

The floor argued almost immediately.

It began as sound—a crack like a rifle shot somewhere off to their right, deep in the channel wall. Then another, closer, under the ice itself, a long tearing groan that traveled beneath their feet from front to back like a word being read aloud. Bo froze mid-step, arms out, and Ilsa watched him hold absolutely still with a discipline that would have seemed impossible from the grinning fool who'd fallen into the crevasse field two days ago. He'd changed out there, hanging on Ferro's rope over all that blue dark. Everyone had changed. Fear was doing its work on them evenly, like weather.

"Keep moving," she said quietly. "Slow is smooth. The ice cracks because the tide lifts it, not because you stepped. Step anyway."

Tam muttered something that was probably profane in three dialects and hauled his sledge forward.

At the deepest point of the narrows the sea ice thinned until she could see down into it—black water under grey glass, and moving in the black, pale shapes. She stopped. Held up a fist. Waited.

Seals. A dozen of them, gliding beneath their feet in loose formation, curious, unhurried. One rose at a breathing hole near the channel wall and regarded the line of humans with big dark ancient eyes, exhaled a plume of fishy breath, and slid under again.

"They're using the channel," she breathed.

"So are we," Tam said.

"That's my point." She crouched, touched the ice. "Animals that live here don't travel on bad ice. If they've committed to the route, the route is holding—for now. It's the best endorsement we're going to get." She stood. "Move."

They moved.

* * *

The high ground, when they reached it, was barely worthy of the word—a dome of old compressed ice, blue as bottle glass, thrust up where two flows had collided decades ago and ground each other to a standstill. But it stood above the tide line, and its surface was ridged and rough enough to anchor ropes, and after the narrows it felt like solid land feels in dreams: too good to be true, and therefore true.

Ilsa planted her probe, drove two anchors, ran the safety lines, and then finally, finally let her shoulders drop a centimeter.

Bo flopped onto the ice and stared at the sky. "I want it noted," he said, "that I crossed an entire tidal narrows without falling into anything."

"Noted," Ferro said, checking his pupils anyway, because Bo had taken a head knock in the crevasse and Ferro had appointed himself the guardian of Bo's skull with a fierce quiet tenderness that nobody commented on, because commenting would have embarrassed them both.

Tam began rigging the sledges for the next leg, hands moving without looking at his work, eyes on the channel behind them. "We made good time," he said. "Better than the tables."

"We made better than the tables because the tables are dead paper," Ilsa said. "This year the melt started early. Everything's running fast. Which means—" She opened the notebook, checked, hated what she found, said it anyway. "Which means the tides themselves may be running fast. Our window might be shorter than six tides. Could be five. I won't swear to six."

Silence went around the circle. Coral stood apart, as he usually did, facing back the way they'd come, and Ilsa followed his look.

Far off, at the mouth of the main channel—the wide southern route, the honest road, the one the fleet would take—there was a smudge against the horizon. Too regular to be cloud. She raised her binoculars and steadied them against her knee.

Ships. Three, possibly four, hull-down behind the pressure ridges, engines making the faintest dark thread of smoke. Moving. Not idling at the staging grounds the way the intelligence had suggested. Moving now, hours ahead of any schedule she'd projected.

"Coral," she said.

He took the binoculars without a word. She watched him watch the fleet, and watched the thing pass over his face that always passed over his face when Voss's name came near—not anger exactly, not fear, something older than both, a scar flexing. He lowered the glasses.

"Early," he said.

"Early," she agreed. "Somebody sold them a better calendar than ours."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "Can we still beat them?"

Ilsa looked back at the route ahead. Past the high ground the channel bent north, narrowing, climbing through country where the ice grew younger and meaner—serac fields, bridge crossings, a traverse she'd been losing sleep over since the day she drew it. It existed. Barely. For now. The seals had voted yes; the barometer was a liar; the mountain had not been consulted and did not care.

She thought about the pulse in the ice, that vast indifferent heartbeat underneath everything, keeping time for no one. She thought about her father saying the sea tells you the truth whether you want it or not. She thought: *the truth today is that the window is closing faster than planned, and the fleet is ahead of schedule, and we have exactly one advantage left.*

"Yes," she said. "If we move now, and if nothing breaks, and if the mountain permits it." She snapped the notebook shut. "Three ifs. I've crossed oceans on worse odds. But we stop camping like tourists and start traveling like thieves."

Tam grinned, sharp and ready. Bo rolled upright. Ferro began repacking his kit with the calm of a man folding laundry during a bombardment.

Only Coral hesitated—and Ilsa caught it, the flicker, there and gone. Not doubt of the route. Something else. Something about the ships on the horizon, or the name attached to them. Then it sealed over, and he shouldered his pack, and said, "Lead on, Navigator," and she led.

Behind them, far to the south, the first of the fleet's smoke threads thickened. Beneath them, patient as ever, the tide kept rising, lifting the whole white world a finger's width at a time, asking its slow question.

By the next slack water, Ilsa knew, the question would demand an answer.

She wrote it in the book: *Day 5. First tide taken. Route holds—barely. Fleet ahead of schedule. Window shrinking.*

Then, because the notebook was also where she kept the things she'd never say aloud, she added one line more:

*The mountain doesn't hate us. It just doesn't know we're here. Pray it never looks down.*
