## 11. Fleet Smoke on the Horizon

I saw the smoke before I saw the ships.

That's how it works on ice—smoke travels farther than anything else, a gray thread stitched against the pale sky where no thread should be. I'd been reading that sky for twenty years. Clouds lie. Smoke doesn't.

Ilsa spotted it two minutes after I did, which for her was practically sleeping on watch. She came up beside me at the ridge line without a word and stood there with her binoculars pressed to her eyes until her knuckles went white around them.

"Main channel," she said finally. "Three masts. Maybe four."

"Distance?"

"Twelve miles. Fourteen." She lowered the glasses. "They're making better time than your chart said they would."

My chart hadn't said they'd make good time. My chart had said the main channel was choked with old pack ice this season, that any fleet pushing through would have to pick its way slow as a funeral. That estimate had been built on three-year-old survey data and the assumption that nobody with money to lose would gamble a hull on it.

Assumptions were how men like me ended up blacklisted in two countries. I knew better than to trust them.

"How fast are we moving?" I asked.

Ilsa did the arithmetic in her head, lips moving slightly. "If we hold this pace through the narrows, we're four hours ahead by second tide. If they hold theirs." She looked at me. "But they don't have to climb what we climbed last night. They've got steam winches and a channel with water in it."

Water was the whole argument of the north, when you got down to it. We were taking the high road because it was the only road left open, and the fleet was taking the low road because money buys you the right to risk other people's hulls. Both roads led to the same glacier. Only one of them led there first.

Below us, the crew was breaking camp in the grey half-light. Tam had the stove already kicked over and doused; Bo was coiling rope with the exaggerated care of a man proving he could still work after the crevasse. Ferro moved between them doing the quiet arithmetic medics do—counting fingers, checking color, deciding who was lying about being fine.

Six hours. That's what Bo's fall had cost us, six hours of rigging and hauling and Ferro's hands shaking only after it was over, never during. Six hours bought back one rigger's life, and I'd have paid six days. But the ledger didn't care what I'd have paid. The ledger only cared what was written in it.

And now the smoke said the ledger was turning against us.

"We push," I said.

"I figured," Ilsa said. "I already told them."

* * *

We made the narrows by mid-morning, a slot of blue shadow between two walls of pressure ice where the wind funneled through hard enough to lean on. You go through places like that in single file, roped, nobody talking except the person counting steps. I counted. It gives the mind something dumb enough to hold onto.

It was coming out the far side, blinking in the sudden open light, that Tam caught up to me.

He didn't say anything at first. He just walked beside me for a hundred paces, which from Tam was a speech. Tam was the kind of man who thought out loud only when he'd already finished thinking, and everything he'd finished thinking was sitting behind his eyes like a stone he wanted to hand me.

Finally he said: "You see the smoke."

"I see the smoke."

"They'll beat us to the shelf if that channel stays open." He said it flat, no drama. Tam didn't deal in drama; he dealt in loads, angles, breaking strengths. "Four hours becomes nothing if they find clear water past the point. Could be nothing by tonight."

"So we move faster."

"We're moving as fast as bodies move." He glanced back at Bo, who was walking with a hitch in his stride he thought nobody noticed and everybody noticed. "Bodies that have already given what they've given."

I stopped walking. That made him stop too, and for a moment we just stood there on the ice with the wind pulling at us both, and I understood that whatever he'd finished thinking, he was about to hand it to me whether I wanted it or not.

"Say it," I said.

"There's a choke point on their channel." His voice dropped even though there was no one close enough to hear, which told me everything about how long he'd been carrying this. "The Serpent's Teeth—you know it. Where the channel pinches down to fifty meters between those two grounded bergs. Every ship has to funnel through. Slow, single file, engines easy." He paused. "A charge set at the waterline on the eastern berg. Half a tonne of the powder we're already carrying for the salvage work. It wouldn't take much. The calving does the rest. Whole face comes down, blocks the channel for days. Maybe weeks."

The wind filled the silence while I let the picture build itself: the fleet stacked up behind a wall of fresh ice, our four-hour lead stretching into something unassailable, the Meridian's hold ours before Voss ever touched it.

It was a good plan. That was the ugly part. It was clean, cheap, and it worked, and every hour I'd spent in the mountains had taught me exactly how to do it. I could place that charge in my sleep. I'd placed charges in worse spots for worse reasons.

"You want me to order it," I said.

"I want you to consider it." Tam met my eyes. He wasn't ashamed. That was the thing about Tam—he'd carry a plan like that openly, weigh it honestly, and let someone else decide whether the weight was too much. "Consider it properly, Coral. Not the way you refuse things in your head before they finish being said."

"I'm considering it properly right now."

"Then consider this. Those aren't soldiers down there. It's a salvage fleet—deckhands, ice pilots, a couple of company men. Men hired for wages, same as us. Same as Bo, same as Ferro. You drop that face, you don't stop Voss. You bury whoever's in the first hull through the Teeth, and the ones behind turn around and go home rich anyway, because companies don't grieve, they re-budget." He held up a hand before I could answer. "And I know what you're going to say. I know you're going to say the mountain kills people all the time, that ice doesn't take sides. And that's true. But we'd be choosing to be the ice, Coral. There's a difference between weather and a man with a fuse."

I looked south, toward where the smoke stood against the sky. Twelve miles, maybe less now. Somewhere under that smoke were men who'd signed onto a job the way my crew had signed onto ours—for money, for a reason, for the simple animal need to keep moving. Some of them might even be guides who'd been blacklisted the way I'd been blacklisted, working the only work left to them.

Twenty years ago, on the night the Meridian went down, I had learned exactly what it meant when people treated other people as obstacles to a cargo. I had spent twenty years telling myself the story of that night in whatever shape hurt least, and every version of the story had one thing in common: somebody had decided that lives were cheaper than what was in the hold.

Tam was still watching me. Good man. Honest man. Asking an honest question.

"No," I said.

"Coral—"

"No." I said it again, quieter, because the first one had come out harder than I meant. "We don't touch their channel. We don't touch their hulls. We race them the way we are—on foot, on rope, ahead or behind, however it lands."

"It might cost us the job."

"It might."

"It might cost more than the job."

I turned and started walking again, and he fell in beside me, and for a while neither of us spoke. Then I said, "You know why I got blacklisted, Tam? Officially? Negligence. Bad judgment. A guide who took his sister onto a ship that sank. That's the story in two countries' worth of files." I kept my eyes on the route ahead, picking the line, because looking at him would have made the next part harder and I needed it to be easy. "Unofficially, the reason nobody says out loud is that after the wreck, I spent a year asking questions the shipping company didn't want asked. And the answers kept pointing at men who decided that what was in the hold mattered more than who was on the deck. So they buried the questions, and they buried me with them, and I've been walking on top of the burial site ever since."

Tam said nothing. He was good at that.

"If I set that charge," I went on, "then the file was right about me after all. Not the negligence part—the other part. The part where a man looks at a channel full of working stiffs and sees a math problem." I finally looked at him. "I've spent twenty years deciding what kind of man I am. Mostly alone, mostly in the dark, mostly by elimination. This—" I nodded south, at the smoke, at the whole shape of the choice hanging in the cold air between us, "—this is the first time in twenty years anybody's handed me the decision while I could still see it clearly. So I'm making it while I can see it. No sabotage. We win honest or we lose honest."

Tam chewed on that for a dozen paces. Then he nodded, once, the way he nodded at a load that had settled right.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I hoped you'd say no. I just needed to hear it out loud so I could stop hearing it in my head."

"That's what I'm for," I said. "Out loud."

He almost smiled. From Tam, that was a standing ovation.

* * *

We told the others at the noon halt, huddled in the lee of a serac with the stove hissing and everyone's hands wrapped around tin cups. I laid it out plain: the fleet was ahead of schedule, the gap was shrinking, and we were going to keep racing clean. Nobody suggested sabotage but everybody understood it was on the table, because Tam's face announced it had been discussed, and Tam's face was not built for secrets.

Bo flexed his frost-nipped fingers and said, "Good. Blowing that channel would've been the easy way, and the easy way's been lying to us since the crevasse."

Ferro said nothing, but she looked at me a moment longer than usual, and there was something in it I couldn't read yet—a question forming, maybe, or an answer she wasn't ready to give. I filed it away. Medics carried things. That was the job.

Ilsa just checked her watch, checked the sky, and said, "Then we need the high traverse before dark, or none of this matters. Four hours of climbing. Can they give me four hours?"

Everyone looked at Bo.

Bo looked at his own hands like they belonged to a stranger, then closed them into fists. "They can give you five," he said. "Don't tell Ferro."

"I heard that," Ferro said.

"Then you heard a brave man," Bo said, and got up, and we got up with him.

* * *

The high traverse was exactly as bad as Ilsa's map promised, which is the highest compliment I can pay a map. A ledge cut diagonally across a wall of blue ice, wide enough for one boot, dropping away on the left into a field of boulders the size of houses that the glacier had spat out sometime in the last century and hadn't finished apologizing for. We crossed it roped at ten-meter spacing, each of us pressing our shoulder to the wall, moving when the person ahead moved, stopping when they stopped, the whole crew breathing in one long shared rhythm.

Halfway across, I made the mistake of looking south.

The channel was visible from up there—a silver seam stitched through the white, and on it, small with distance but unmistakable, the fleet. Three ships in line ahead, funnels trailing their gray thread, nosing through the broken ice with the patience of things that cost too much to hurry. Even from twelve miles I could pick out the lead vessel by her lines. Company hull. Salvage rig on the foredeck, crane arm folded like a sleeping insect.

Voss's ship. Or Voss's employers' ship, which amounted to the same thing with extra paperwork.

I watched her for longer than a man crossing a ledge should watch anything, and I watched her pass the point where the Serpent's Teeth would be, and I watched her keep going. Clear water past the point. Just like Tam said. The channel was open, and the fleet was through, and our four hours had become two, and by dark it might become one.

There was a moment—I'll be honest about this, because a man who won't be honest with himself about a moment like that will lie about everything else—there was a moment when my hand remembered the weight of a charge bag. Twenty years of muscle memory doesn't ask permission. It just presents the option, polite as a waiter: the berg, the waterline, the fuse, the wall of white, the channel closed, the race won. All it would cost was everything I'd just told Tam I was.

I put my shoulder back against the ice and kept moving.

Because here was the thing I understood up on that ledge, watching Voss's hull slide through water I could have shut forever: the blacklist, the exile, the twenty years of measuring life in miles—it had all grown from one seed, and the seed wasn't the wreck. The wreck was weather. The seed was the decision, made by men whose names I'd never learned, that cargo outweighed conscience. Everything I'd lost traced back to somebody treating human beings as a line item.

I could spend the rest of my life avenging that. Or I could spend the rest of my life refusing it. I couldn't do both. Up on the ledge, with the fleet smoking along below me and my crew strung out behind me trusting me to choose, the refusal finally felt less like a sacrifice and more like a door.

Maren, I thought—not for the first time, but for the first time without flinching—I'm going to get there first, and I'm going to do it the way you'd have done it.

I didn't know yet how she'd have done it. I didn't know yet what she'd actually done. That knowledge was still sealed inside a hull of ice and a journal I hadn't opened past page one, waiting for me at the end of this white road.

All I knew was the direction. For twenty years, direction had been enough. Today, for the first time, it felt like it might actually be pointing somewhere.

* * *

We came off the traverse at dusk with the light going copper and the temperature falling like a verdict. Below us, the fleet had anchored for the night in the lee of the point—three sets of riding lights strung across the dark water like a town that had wandered in from somewhere warmer. They'd lost the daylight too. Whatever head start the channel gave them, the mountain was charging them for it in its own currency.

"One tide," Ilsa said, crouched over the map by stove-light, her breath fogging the page. "Maybe two. After that the routes converge at the calving wall, and it's a footrace on ice with the whole sky trying to kill us."

"Then we don't sleep long," I said.

Around the stove, nobody complained. Bo was already asleep sitting up, which after the day he'd had was less laziness than physics. Ferro covered him with the spare fleece without waking. Tam cleaned his rack with the concentration of a man performing a small ritual of forgiveness—for the plan he'd offered, or the answer he'd gotten, or both.

I took first watch, because I always took first watch, and stood at the edge of camp looking south at the riding lights.

Somewhere on that lead ship, Voss was awake too. I could feel it the way you feel weather coming—twenty years of rivalry had built a kind of barometer in my chest, and it was reading storm. He'd known I'd come. His letter had said as much, that smug, patient, terrible letter. He was counting on me to be exactly what I'd always been: fast, stubborn, and alone enough to be predictable.

Well. I glanced back at the camp—at Ilsa bent over her charts, at Tam's hands still moving, at Bo snoring under Ferro's fleece. Alone had been a choice once. It wasn't anymore.

The lights on the water burned steady. The glacier ahead groaned once, long and low, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Two tides, I thought. Hold the line, stay honest, and be faster than money.

Behind me, unheard, Ilsa rolled up her map and looked at the southern horizon a long moment—and frowned at something in the smoke that I, facing the wrong way, had not yet seen.
