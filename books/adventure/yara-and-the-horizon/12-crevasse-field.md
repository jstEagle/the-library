## 12. Crevasse Field

Ferro had a rule about fear: you were allowed exactly ten seconds of it, and then your hands had to start working. He'd learned it in a field hospital outside Kessarin, holding an artery closed with two fingers while a surgeon screamed for clamps that weren't there. Ten seconds. After that, fear stopped being information and started being weather.

He was at second forty when Bo went down.

It happened the way these things always happen—no drama, no warning shout, just a change in the texture of the rope team's movement ahead of him. Tam, on point, took one step that looked like every other step. Bo took the next one, and the next step wasn't there.

The snow didn't even crack. It sighed.

One moment Bo was a broad-shouldered silhouette against the white, hauling the sled line with his usual easy rhythm, joking about something Ferro hadn't caught. The next moment he was a shape dropping into the ice, and the rope between them snapped taut with a sound like a struck cable, and Ferro's whole body was yanked forward onto his knees before his brain finished the sentence *he's falling*.

"BO." Coral's voice, flat and enormous, from the rear. "Everyone freeze. Nobody moves."

Nobody moved. That was the thing people didn't understand about glacier rescue—the first act wasn't action, it was stillness. One wrong step and the whole rope team followed Bo down into the blue dark. Ferro knelt in the snow with the line burning across his shoulders and counted his own heartbeat and did not look over the edge, because looking over the edge was a luxury for later.

Below him, faintly, Bo said something.

"He's talking," Ilsa called. She was third on the rope, closest to the lip. "Bo. Can you hear me?"

A pause. Then, muffled but unmistakably Bo: "I can hear you fine. It's very quiet down here. Very blue."

Ferro let out a breath he'd been rationing. Talking meant conscious. Conscious meant airway. Airway meant they had time—not much, but some.

"How far?" Coral again, already moving up the line, anchor screws already out of his pack. Ferro watched his hands work and thought, not for the first time, that whatever else Coral Delacroix was—exile, ghost, a man who measured his life in miles instead of years—his hands belonged to the mountain the way a surgeon's belonged to the body.

"Twenty meters," Ilsa called back. "Maybe twenty-five. He's on the ledge."

"I'm on a ledge," Bo confirmed, with the note of mild surprise of a man reporting the weather. "It's a good ledge. Roomy."

"Are you hurt?"

Another pause. Longer this time. When Bo answered, the joke had thinned out of his voice. "My leg's not great. Left one. Something's—I don't think it's broken. But it doesn't want to be stood on."

Ferro closed his eyes for half a second. A leg injury at the bottom of a crevasse, twenty meters down, six hours behind schedule already, with a salvage fleet eating the main channel somewhere to their south and a tide window that would not wait for anyone's good ledge.

Ten seconds of fear, he reminded himself. Then hands.

* * *

They built the anchor while the wind picked up.

Coral drove two ice screws into the shelf above the lip, set the load-sharing system, and put Ferro on the brake line because Ferro weighed the least and had the steadiest hands—"and because if he codes down there, you're the one who keeps him alive until we get him up," Coral said, which was both a compliment and a sentence Ferro decided not to think too hard about.

The plan was simple, which is to say it was terrible: lower Ferro on the main line, rig Bo into the harness, haul them both out on the counterweight. Simple plans on glaciers are like calm seas—accurate right up until they aren't.

"Ferro." Coral crouched beside him at the lip, wind tearing at his hood. Up close, Ferro could see the thing under his face that was always there on the ice, the thing he wore the way other men wore scars: a kind of listening. Like the mountain was always saying something only he could hear. "You go down, you clip in, you assess, you call it. You don't play hero. If he can't climb, we haul. If the ledge is bad, we tell me and I decide. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Ferro." A beat. Coral's eyes flicked once toward the crevasse mouth, then back. "If the ice moves down there, you come up first. I mean it. We don't trade two for one. Not on my rope."

Something in Ferro's chest turned over. He'd worked with a lot of leaders—military contractors, expedition bosses, one memorable pirate who'd paid in antibiotics—and none of them had ever said *we don't trade two for one* like it was a law with their name on it. Most of them did the math the other way.

"On your rope," Ferro said, and went over the edge.

* * *

Down was another world.

The light came through the ice in shafts, blue-green and ancient, like being inside a cathedral built by something that had never heard of people. The walls of the crevasse rose sixty meters above him, striated with decades of seasons—dirty summer bands, clean winter ones—a calendar written in frozen water. His breath smoked. The rope hummed above him. And below, wedged on a shelf of ice no wider than a door laid flat, sat Bo, one leg stuck out at an angle that made Ferro's professional instincts wince, grinning up at him through a mask crusted with frost.

"Doc," Bo said. "Took you long enough. I was starting to take it personally."

"Shut up and let me work." Ferro swung down onto the ledge, clipped in, and got his hands on Bo's leg before either of them could say anything sentimental.

It wasn't broken. He was almost sure it wasn't broken. The swelling along the shin said deep bruise, maybe a hairline fracture the cold was politely hiding, and the ankle was the real problem—wrenched hard enough that Bo's hiss when Ferro rotated it ten degrees was entirely honest. Cold made everything worse. Cold turned sprains into non-starters and hairlines into collapses, and it turned a twenty-meter haul into a medical event.

"Well?" Bo asked.

"You're walking on it in a week. Maybe two. Today you're cargo."

"Cargo." Bo tried the word out, grimacing. "Tam's going to be insufferable."

"Tam's going to be relieved and then insufferable. There's a difference." Ferro was already rigging the haul sling around Bo's thighs and chest, checking each carabiner twice, three times, the way you do when the drop below the ledge is another thirty meters of blue nothing. "How's your core temp? Talk to me honestly."

"Honestly? My toes are rumors."

That was worse than Ferro wanted to hear. He dug the heat packs out of his kit, cracked them, shoved them against Bo's groin and armpits inside the harness where they'd actually count. "We're getting you up fast. The ledge is going to warm you or the rope is. Pick one."

"The rope," Bo said. "Definitely the rope."

Above them, faintly, the whole glacier groaned—a long, tectonic complaint that Ferro felt in his teeth rather than heard with his ears. Both of them went still. Somewhere in the ice around them, a crack propagated with a sound like a rifle shot, then another, then silence.

"That's normal," Ferro said, not sure he believed it.

"Sure," said Bo. "Everything's normal. Say it enough and it's true."

* * *

The haul nearly killed all of them.

It should have taken twenty minutes. It took ninety, because the lip of the crevasse was rounded and overhanging, and every meter Bo gained on the rope, the sling sawed against the ice edge and lost friction and gained it again, jerking like something alive. Ferro rode up beside him, calling the counts, feeding slack, keeping Bo's injured leg clear of the wall by main force. Above, he could hear Coral's voice running the haul team like a metronome—*walk, walk, walk, hold*—and Tam swearing with tremendous creativity every time the line jumped, and Ilsa's low, steady counters, and underneath it all the glacier talking to itself in the language of stress.

At fifteen meters from the top, the ice moved.

Not a groan this time. A shift—a settling of the whole crevasse wall, a shudder Ferro felt through his boots and then through his harness, and above him the overhanging lip shed a slab of ice the size of a door directly down the fall line.

"ROCK!" he screamed, which was wrong, it was ice, but the word did its job. He slammed himself and Bo flat against the wall as the slab went past them like a freight train, close enough that the wind of it slapped his cheek, and then it was gone into the dark below, and the boom of its landing came up the shaft a full second later, rolling away into the deep places of the glacier.

Silence. Rope hum. Breath.

"Status," Coral's voice, tight as wire.

"Both alive," Ferro called up. "Lip's unstable. Get us the rest of the way now."

They got them the rest of the way now. The last five meters were chaos—hands grabbing harnesses, hauling them over the lip and back from the edge, all four of them collapsing in the snow in a tangle of rope and heat packs and frost-crusted laughing. Because Bo was laughing, actually laughing, flat on his back with his ruined leg propped on Tam's pack, wheezing at the grey sky.

"Told you," he managed. "Good ledge. Roomy."

"You absolute—" Tam couldn't finish it. He settled for gripping Bo's shoulder hard enough to bruise and saying nothing at all, which from Tam was a eulogy and a love letter in one gesture.

Ferro lay in the snow and let his heart slow down and did the math he'd been avoiding. Time since the fall: two hours and change. Time to get moving again, with Bo half-cargo on the sled line: unknown. Distance to the tide route marker: a number Ilsa kept in her head and wouldn't say out loud, which told Ferro everything.

Six hours. The rescue had cost them six hours they didn't have, and everyone knew it, and nobody said it, and that silence was the loudest thing on the ice.

* * *

They moved again within the hour, because Coral gave them ten minutes to eat and then stood up, and standing up was the order.

Bo went on the sled line between Tam and Ilsa, weight-sharing, one boot wrapped and laced tight, hopping-assisting where the grade allowed. He refused the full litter—"I'm a rigger, not a parcel"—but Ferro watched him carefully all afternoon, pulse checks disguised as adjusting his collar, watching the color of his face and the clarity of his jokes. The jokes stayed clear. That was the metric Ferro trusted most. Pain made people quiet; hypothermia made them funny in the wrong way; Bo was funny in exactly his usual way, which meant the engine was still running clean.

It was late in the grey afternoon, the light going pewter, when Bo dropped back beside Ferro on the rope and spoke without looking at him.

"You came down for me."

"You fell. That's the job."

"No." Bo shook his head. "The job was the haul. You clipped in and went over the edge into a hole in the world, on a rope, with the lip shedding slabs. Nobody pays a medic enough for that. I've worked with guides who'd have voted to flag the position and send for gear." He paused. "Coral didn't even hesitate. None of you did."

Ferro adjusted his own collar, which was nowhere near Bo's neck, and said nothing.

"Why?" Bo asked. Simply. Not pushing. Just asking, the way you'd ask about the weather.

Ferro thought about Kessarin. About the field hospital and the missing clamps and the ten-second rule. About the long list of employers who had done the math the other way, who had weighed one medic against a schedule and found the schedule heavier. About why a man with steady hands and a clean record on paper ends up unemployable everywhere that matters, and what it does to you, and what it means when four strangers on a rope don't do the math at all.

"Because you'd have done it for me," he said finally. "And because Coral would have fired me if I hadn't. And because—" he exhaled, watching his breath tear away downwind, "—because for the first time in about six years, I'm on a rope with people who don't trade two for one. I'm not in a hurry to be anywhere else."

Bo looked at him for a long moment. Then he grinned, that easy grin, and knocked his shoulder against Ferro's, careful of the rope between them.

"Doc," he said, "you're stuck with us now. Hope you know that."

Ahead of them, Coral had stopped. He stood facing south, where the ice fields ran down toward the channel, and Ferro followed his gaze and felt the day's warmth drain out of him all over again.

On the horizon, thin as a pencil line, a smudge of smoke rose against the grey water.

Engines. Fleet smoke, moving faster than any of them had planned for.

Behind them, the storm clouds they'd been outrunning all day had begun, quietly, to organize.
