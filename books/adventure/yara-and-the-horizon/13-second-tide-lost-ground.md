## 13. Second Tide, Lost Ground

Ilsa Vinter had navigated by dead reckoning through whiteouts before. She'd once held a fishing crew together for eleven hours in the Denmark Strait with a broken compass and a bucket of nerve. But she had never done it while listening to a mountain erase the road home.

The wind came down off the icecap in long grey fists. It didn't howl so much as grind—a sound like a millstone turning somewhere above them, crushing everything loose into powder and throwing the powder sideways at forty knots. Visibility had collapsed from a horizon to a rope-length to almost nothing. The world was now the width of Coral Delacroix's back, three meters ahead on the lead line, a dark shape flickering in and out of existence like a signal lamp with a dying battery.

"Distance?" Tam shouted against her ear.

"Four hundred meters since the last marker," she shouted back, which was a guess dressed up as a number. She hated guesses. She'd built her whole second life on not guessing—on charts, on soundings, on knowing exactly where the water ended and the drowning began. Now every figure in her head was a soft pencil line smudged by weather.

Behind them, where they had come from, the glacier groaned.

Not the ordinary talk of ice—the pops and ticks that Bo called the mountain's knuckles cracking. This was deeper, structural, a sound Ilsa felt in her molars and in the soles of her boots and in the wet meat of her lungs. A long descending note, like a ship's hull taking water.

Then: the crack. Sharp, flat, enormous.

She turned into the wind despite the needles of snow it drove into her face. Behind them, maybe two hundred meters back along their own track, the shelf they'd crossed an hour ago was simply gone. She couldn't see it clearly—nothing was clear—but she could see the absence of it, a new darkness in the grey, and the spume rising off open water where there had been solid ground under their feet at first tide.

The route behind them was closing. Not narrowing. Closing.

* * *

They made camp because there was no choice left to make. Coral found a hollow in the lee of a pressure ridge, and they dug in with shovels and ice axes while the storm tried to take the shovels out of their hands. Ferro got the tent up with Bo holding the poles and swearing in two languages. Tam anchored the guy-lines with deadman burials, packing snow and stamping it hard, working with the grim economy of a man who'd buried things before.

Ilsa sat just inside the tent flap with her chart case open on her knees, protecting it with her body, updating the log by stove-light. Her handwriting looked like someone else's—shaken, cramped, honest.

*Second tide. Route astern calved at approximately 16:40 local. Retreat no longer possible. Forward only.*

She stared at the words. She had written retreat reports before. Once, off Hvaler, she'd written one that saved seven lives, turning back through weather everyone said was crossable because she knew it wasn't. Turning back was a skill. Turning back was sometimes the whole job. A navigator who couldn't say *no further* was just a passenger with instruments.

But there was nothing behind them now except the sea coming up between the ice blocks like something surfacing to breathe.

"You saw it," Coral said. He'd come in without her noticing—he moved quietly for a big man—and he crouched at the flap, looking back the way she was looking.

"I saw where it was," Ilsa said. "That's not quite the same thing."

"It's gone."

"It's gone."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, and she watched him do the arithmetic she was already doing. Six tides. They were inside the second. If the route closed behind them this fast—

"How far to the third-tide crossing?" he asked.

"Nine kilometers. Maybe eleven in this." She tapped the chart. "If the channel holds its shape."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we're standing on an island that hasn't finished becoming one yet."

Coral looked at her for a long moment. Snow had gotten into the creases around his eyes; he looked older than twenty years past the wreck, older than the mountains. Then he did something she hadn't expected. He smiled. Small, crooked, entirely without comfort.

"So we don't turn around," he said.

"No," Ilsa said. "We don't turn around."

* * *

Dinner was pemmican and melted snow, eaten in silence broken only by the storm hammering the fly. Bo lay propped against his pack, gray-faced but conscious, his rescued-from-the-crevasse ankle wrapped and splinted, refusing the stronger of Ferro's painkillers on the grounds that he wanted his head when the time came. Nobody argued with him. Arguing with Bo about wanting his head was like arguing with the tide.

It was Bo who broke the silence, in the flat voice of a man stating a fact he'd been sitting on for hours.

"We can't go back."

Nobody answered, because there was nothing to say that wasn't already written in Ilsa's log.

"My sister used to sail the Baltic in winter," Bo went on, staring at the ceiling of the tent. "Single-handed, little boat, no engine worth mentioning. People told her she was insane. You know what she said?" He laughed, thin and dry. "She said, *the sea doesn't care which way you're facing. Only the shore does.*"

Tam snorted. "Your sister sounds exhausting."

"She was." Bo's grin faded into something softer. "She drowned anyway. So maybe don't quote her too often."

The silence after that had weight. Ferro reached over and squeezed Bo's boot, once, and Bo let him.

Coral spoke from the shadows near the flap. "Third tide is our crossing. Nine, eleven kilometers. After that the route runs along the shelf for six more, then the final bridge at fifth tide." He laid it out the way he must have laid it out a hundred times in his head, the way Ilsa had, the way they all had. "The fleet takes the main channel. Faster going, worse exposure. We took the smugglers' trail to buy a day. The storm may have taken it back."

"The storm doesn't know whose side it's on," Tam said.

"Storms aren't on anyone's side," Ilsa said. "That's what makes them useful. They hit everyone equally. The fleet is getting hit right now too." She paused, checking herself, hating the next sentence even as she said it. "Probably."

*Probably.* There it was again. Guesswork. She pressed her palms flat against the chart until the paper warmed under her hands, as if she could transfer certainty into it by contact.

Ferro cleared his throat. "For what it's worth—Bo's fever's down. His ankle will hold for walking, not for climbing. If the crossing involves climbing—"

"It won't," Ilsa said. "It involves trusting me."

Everyone looked at her. She realized she'd said it louder than intended, and that the stove had chosen that moment to gutter, and that in the sudden half-dark she looked, apparently, like a person who deserved to be trusted, because nobody laughed.

Good, she thought. Because underneath the chart-case and the compass and the twenty years of dead reckoning, that was the truth of her situation: nine kilometers of untested ice, and four lives routed through her judgment like current through a wire.

She thought about the Denmark Strait. Eleven hours. The moment the compass died and the skipper looked at her—not at the sky, not at the waves, at *her*—and asked where. And she'd pointed, and pointed correctly, and understood afterward that navigation was mostly a confidence trick played on chaos: you couldn't actually know, but you could act as if you knew, and sometimes the acting kept people alive long enough for luck to arrive.

This was bigger than the Strait. This was a glacier instead of a sea, and the stakes weren't fish and wages but Maren Delacroix's name and Coral's freedom and whatever truth was welded shut inside a hold at the bottom of twenty years of ice.

And she was afraid.

She let herself feel it, fully, for exactly as long as it took the stove to flare back up. Dread, cold and complete, sitting in her chest like ballast. She examined it the way she examined weather: What is it doing? Where is it moving? Can I use it?

Because here was the thing she'd learned in thirty years of water and ice. Fear and attention were the same substance at different temperatures. Dread was just resolve that hadn't been given a heading yet.

She gave it a heading.

*Forward,* she wrote in the log, under the earlier entry. *Forward is the only direction. Third tide. 09:00 or first break in visibility, whichever comes later.*

* * *

The storm blew itself hoarse sometime after midnight. Ilsa slept in ninety-minute watches and woke each time to check the barometer she'd carried since the Strait, watching the needle climb out of its dive like a swimmer coming up for air. By 04:00 the wind had dropped to a mutter. By 05:30 the clouds were tearing apart overhead, showing scraps of black sky and more stars than she'd seen since the coast.

At 06:00 she crawled out of the tent into a world that had been remade overnight.

Their camp sat in a bowl of fresh powder a foot deep. The pressure ridge above them glittered, blue-white, absurdly beautiful. And to the south, where the route ran, the ice field stretched away toward the third-tide crossing—visible now, all of it, laid out like a diagram.

Except.

"Come look at this," she said quietly, and Coral was out of the tent before she finished the sentence, and then Tam, and then Ferro supporting Bo, who stood on his good leg and swore softly in both languages again.

The storm hadn't only closed the route behind them. It had rearranged everything ahead. The pressure ridges had migrated—or rather, the ice between them had shifted, plates the size of villages grinding into new positions, opening leads of black water where yesterday there had been white, and closing yesterday's leads under fresh rafted slabs. The channel they were meant to follow to the crossing point was still there. Roughly. In spirit.

In practice, it was a different river now.

Ilsa knelt at the edge of camp with her binoculars and read the ice the way other people read scripture, line by line, hunting for meaning. Old ice, young ice, rafted ice, rotten ice with the telltale dull grey of thaw-refrozen slush. A navigator's whole art was telling them apart fast enough to matter.

"There," she said finally, lowering the glasses. "See the ridge line running east-northeast? That's old pressure ice—it's been there longer than the storm. If we follow the spine of it, it should carry us within sight of the crossing. We'll lose the direct line. Adds maybe three kilometers."

"Three kilometers of what?" Tam asked.

"Mostly good ice. One section I don't like. Flat, featureless, recently flooded and frozen—I can't tell its thickness from here. We'll probe it. Ropes on. Slow."

"Slow costs us the tide," Coral said.

"Fast costs us Bo," Ilsa said, and let that sit. "The tide window at the crossing is four hours wide. We make it or we don't, but we'll make it with everyone, or there's no point having made it."

Coral studied her. She braced for the argument—the famous Delacroix drive, the man who'd crossed passes he shouldn't legally enter, who measured life in miles precisely so he wouldn't have to slow down and measure it in years.

Instead he nodded. "You have the route."

Something turned over in her chest. Twenty years of being the best navigator nobody would hire, the woman with the Strait on her record and a blacklist of her own—one country, just one, she'd always been modest about it—and here was Coral Delacroix handing her the lead like it was obvious. Like it had always been obvious.

"Ropes on," she repeated. "Single file. My spacing. Nobody moves unless I move." She looked at each of them in turn, ending on Bo. "Can you walk it?"

Bo rolled his bad ankle once, experimentally, and his face went the color of old candle wax, and he grinned through it. "I can walk anything for nine kilometers."

"Eleven," Ilsa said. "Maybe twelve. Let's find out."

* * *

They moved at 07:40, in light the color of pewter, along the spine of the old pressure ice. Ilsa led with Coral on the second rope position, then Ferro and Bo roped close together, Tam sweeping. The morning was glassy-still, the kind of stillness that on a glacier was less peace than held breath.

The good ice held. The bad section, when they reached it—an hour in, a vast pale plain of refrozen floodwater—held too, though it boomed hollowly under their boots, a drumskin stretched over darkness, and nobody spoke the entire kilometer and a half of crossing it. Ilsa counted paces the whole way, 340 double-steps, probing every tenth step, and when solid pressure ridge rose ahead of them again she realized her hands were shaking and made them stop.

They came within sight of the third-tide crossing at 11:15, three hours ahead of the window's opening, and Ilsa permitted herself one breath of something adjacent to relief.

Then she raised the binoculars one more time, out of habit, scanning the horizon the way she always scanned horizons, and stopped breathing altogether.

Southwest, far off across the ice fields where the main channel ran: smoke.

Not one plume. Three. Ship engines venting steam, or camp stoves, or both—tiny grey feathers against the white, unmistakable, moving.

"They're through the channel already," Tam said, coming up beside her. "Storm didn't stop them either."

"No," Ilsa said. "It didn't." She lowered the glasses. The plumes were distant enough to be ambiguous, close enough to be real. Somewhere under one of those feathers of smoke was Sable Voss, and whatever Voss knew about the Meridian, and whatever Voss had planned for the people racing him.

Coral stood beside her, saying nothing, looking at the smoke with an expression she couldn't read—hunger and horror sharing the same face.

And then Bo, leaning on Ferro's shoulder behind them, said quietly:

"Someone's coming. Fast. Single traveler, on the ice west of us—heading straight for camp."

Ilsa swung the glasses north. A lone figure, small at this distance, moving across the ice with the efficient stride of someone who knew exactly where he was going.

Straight toward them. Straight toward four people who were supposed to be invisible.

"He knows where we are," she said. "How does he know where we are?"
