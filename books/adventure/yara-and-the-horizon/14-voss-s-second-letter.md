## 14. Voss's Second Letter

The scout came at dusk, walking out of the white like a man stepping off a ship.

Coral saw him first from the ridge above camp—a single dark figure on the ice shelf, no sled, no rope team, moving with the unhurried confidence of someone who knew exactly where he was going. Nobody walked that way out here. Out here you moved hunched and careful, testing every step, or you didn't move long.

He watched the figure pick its way down toward their tents, and something cold settled in his chest before his brain caught up with the reason. He'd seen that walk before. Twenty years ago, across a crowded harbor in Halvard, a young man with salt-bleached hair had crossed a quay the same way—like the world was a room he'd been invited into.

"Sable Voss," Coral said to no one.

The figure stopped at the edge of camp and raised both hands, empty. "Just delivery," the man called up. His voice carried flat across the ice. "I'm not Voss. I'm his scout. Name's Pell."

Coral descended anyway, keeping the slope between them, axe loose in his grip. Up close the scout was younger than the walk suggested—mid-thirties maybe, windburned, sensible gear worn sensibly. A courier, then. But couriers for Sable Voss were chosen the way other men chose weapons.

"Letter," Pell said. He pulled a sealed envelope from inside his jacket and held it out. "He said you'd want to read it alone. He also said—" the scout hesitated, and Coral marked the hesitation, filed it, "—he said to tell you the tide tables don't lie, but men do."

"Charming."

"He pays me to be accurate, not charming." Pell glanced past Coral at the camp: Ilsa crouched over her charts by stove-light, Tam checking lines, Bo sitting propped against a crate with his ribs strapped, Ferro melting snow. Five people in a white wilderness, looking small even from ten meters. "You're behind," Pell said. "You know that."

"I know you're standing on my mountain without an invitation."

"It's nobody's mountain. That's rather the point of it." Pell set the envelope on a rock between them, stepped back three paces—the courtesy of a man who'd been briefed on how Coral Delacroix received things handed to him directly—and waited.

Coral let it sit there a full minute. Long enough to make a point to himself, if to no one else. Then he took it.

The paper was heavy stock, absurd out here, where a page was ballast. The seal was wax, black, pressed with a ship's wheel. Voss hadn't changed it since Halvard. Coral remembered the letterhead of the Meridian's passenger manifest under his thumb, twenty years ago, the same wheel stamped beside the company name, and his stomach did the old familiar drop-and-catch.

"You can go," he said.

"He'll want an answer."

"He'll get one when I have one."

Pell nodded as if this too had been scripted, turned, and walked back the way he'd come, unhurried, certain of his ground. Coral watched until the dark took him, then stood a while longer holding the letter, feeling its weight, which was nothing, which was everything.

* * *

He didn't open it by the fire.

That was the thing about grief—it taught you tactics. For twenty years Coral had known exactly which hours of the night his mind went soft, which memories ambushed him at which altitudes, which songs he could not allow himself to hum. He had built a life the way you build a winter camp: walls to windward, nothing left lying around that the weather of the past could pick up and throw.

So he took the letter and climbed.

Above camp there was a shelf of blue ice where the wind scoured clean, and he sat there with his back to the glacier and broke the seal with his thumbnail. The wax cracked like a small bone.

*Delacroix.*

*You're reading this by now, which means you took the smugglers' trail, which means Ilsa Reyen is navigating for you, which means you've already lost the argument with yourself and won the one with your pride. Congratulations on both.*

*I told you once I'd see you on the ice again. I keep what I promise. Ask anyone who's sailed with me—ask anyone who sailed with Maren.*

There it was. Third paragraph, first knife. Coral made himself keep reading.

*You'll be wondering how I knew your route. Don't waste the suspicion on your crew—they're better than you deserve, and you know it, and it's eating you alive that they believe in you. The mountains are full of ears and I own most of them. That's all. That should be enough. It won't be.*

*Here is what matters. When we reach the hold—and we will reach it, my ships are faster and my route is warmer—you and I are finally going to settle what happened aboard the Meridian. Not the version the commissions wrote. Not the version you've been carrying around like a stone in your chest for twenty years, measuring yourself against it every morning. The real one.*

*I was there, Coral.*

*Think about that. Think about who else was there. Think about why you've never once asked yourself the question that would end you: if the hold was sealed from the inside, who did the sealing?*

*Six tides. I'll see you at the wreck.*

*—S.V.*

*P.S. Your sister kept a journal. Everyone on that crew kept journals. Company policy after the Halvard inquiry. Hers was recovered. I've read it. Have you?*

Coral read it twice more. The third time his hands were shaking badly enough that the paper chattered against itself, a small dry sound swallowed instantly by the wind.

Below, the fire glowed orange in the blue dusk. His crew. His people. *They're better than you deserve.* Voss knew that too. Voss knew everything—that was the whole architecture of the man, he'd always been a builder of leverage, and now he'd built it out of Coral's own guilt, brick by brick, twenty years of it.

And the question. God, the question. *If the hold was sealed from the inside, who did the sealing?*

He'd never let himself ask it. That was true. In two decades of nightmares he had reconstructed the sinking a thousand ways—the storm, the cargo shifting, the panic, the black water coming up through the decks—but he had always stopped at the hold door. Sealed. Welded. The official finding said structural failure; the unofficial story, the one that had followed him through two countries' worth of blacklist hearings, said negligence, said the Delacroix charter, said *your family's ship, your family's fault*. And in all those reconstructions he had never once asked the simplest question, because the answer lived next door to a possibility he could not survive:

That someone had sealed it deliberately. That someone had been inside. That someone had—

"No," he said aloud. The wind took it.

Because there was only one person it could be, and Maren had died in the water, they'd told him she died in the water, they'd *found*—

They'd found what? He'd identified her effects. He'd been nineteen and concussed and half-dead himself, and a company clerk had handed him a box with her watch in it, stopped at 11:47, and he had signed a form, and the form had become the rest of his life.

Stopped at 11:47. The hold had been welded shut at some point that night. He had never asked when.

Coral sat on the blue ice until the cold worked through his layers and started talking to him in the language of numbness, and still he sat, because going down meant faces, and right now every face in that camp was a possible leak, a possible lie, a possible kindness being run on him like an operation. Voss had told him not to suspect them, which was either honesty or the most elegant misdirection Coral had ever been handed, and the hell of it was he couldn't tell which, and the worse hell of it was that Voss knew he couldn't tell which.

*Paranoia and grief,* he thought. *Same corridor. Different doors.*

* * *

Ilsa found him an hour later. She didn't climb; she appeared at the base of the shelf with a thermos and waited, which was her way of giving him a choice. He came down because the alternative was freezing to the ice out of spite, and spite was a fuel that burned dirty.

"Voss?" she said.

"Voss."

"What did he want?"

"To get inside my head." Coral took the thermos. Tea, over-sweet, the way Ferro made it, the way Ferro thought nobody noticed. "He wanted me thinking about the night of the sinking instead of the route ahead."

"Is that what you're doing?"

He looked at her. Wind-burned, competent, loyal in the specific stubborn way of people who had followed him into a job he'd refused to take. *Better than you deserve.* He wanted to ask her—flat out, right there—if anyone had sold the route, if anyone had talked, if any of them had ever taken money from a man with a ship's-wheel seal. He wanted to because the letter had put a splinter in him and splinters work toward the surface.

Instead he said, "How far to the serac field?"

"Two days at our pace. Fleet's a day behind us on the main channel, maybe less if the channel stays open." She studied him with those chartmaker's eyes that measured everything and gave away nothing. "Coral. Whatever's in that letter—"

"It's bait."

"Obviously."

"Then we ignore it."

"We ignore it," she agreed, "and you sleep with it under your jacket, and tomorrow you check the rope order twice, and you look at Bo like you're counting his fingers for a payment mark. I've guided with paranoid men before. They die last, usually of exhaustion."

"That's oddly comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be." She took the thermos back. "One thing. The scout—Pell. He looked at Bo's ribs. Longer than a look. Made a note."

The cold went through Coral fresh, a new seam cracking. "A note."

"A small one. Could be nothing. Could be Voss wanting to know how much weight we're carrying." She shrugged. "Or how slow we'll move."

When she'd gone, Coral checked the perimeter the way he always did, and then checked it again, which he never did, and hated himself for the second pass, and did it anyway.

* * *

He didn't sleep. Around two, he gave up pretending and dug to the bottom of his pack, past the spare rope and the emergency rations, to the oilcloth bundle he had carried through two countries and twenty winters without opening.

Maren's journal.

Page one only. He'd read page one the week after the funerals, standing in a rented room in Halvard with the blackout curtains drawn, and the handwriting had undone him—her handwriting, slanted left, letters leaning into the wind the way she did—and he had closed it and wrapped it and carried it ever since, a letter with ice on it of his own, addressed to a version of himself that no longer existed.

Voss had read it. *Hers was recovered. I've read it. Have you?*

Recovered. From where? From the wreck? From her body? And what was in it that Sable Voss, a rival since they were teenagers racing sloops in Halvard harbor, had bothered to read and then weaponize?

Coral held the bundle over the little flame of his private stove. The oilcloth began to warm. He could burn it. Burn it and the question died unanswered, and the official story stayed the story, and the stone stayed in his chest where it had lived so long he'd mistaken it for a heart.

*If the hold was sealed from the inside, who did the sealing?*

His hands wouldn't obey the burning. They lowered the journal instead, and unwrapped the oilcloth, and opened it—not to page one this time, but somewhere in the middle, to an entry dated four days before departure, and Maren's voice came off the page like she was sitting across the stove:

*Coral thinks I don't know he's scared. He checks the rigging three times and calls it habit. I love him for it and I wish he'd just say it. This is the last voyage for both of us—I've decided. One more season and then we take the inheritance and buy something small with sails and never charter for the company again. I found something in the cargo manifest that worries me. I'll deal with it myself. Don't want him carrying it too.*

*Don't want him carrying it too.*

Coral closed the journal with hands that were not steady. Four days before the sinking, Maren had found something wrong in the manifest. Something she'd hidden from him to protect him. Something she'd planned to *deal with herself.*

The wind rose outside, and somewhere up-glacier the ice groaned, long and low, the sound the calving wall made when it dreamed about falling. Below, his crew slept in a circle of firelight, trusting him. Behind him, a fleet was coming, led by a man who had read the same words Coral had just read—or worse ones, later ones, the last ones—and had built a fortune and a fleet and a twenty-year trap on top of them.

Maren had dealt with it herself.

Whatever *it* was, whatever she had done in the black hours of that night with a welding torch and eleven lives in her hands—Voss knew. Voss had always known. And he was dangling it one paragraph at a time, letting Coral bleed for it, because the cruelest thing about Sable Voss was that he never lied. He just made you do his remembering for him.

Coral repacked the journal, zipped it deep, and crawled out into the dark. To the south, faint past the ridgeline, where the main channel ran: a smudge of light that wasn't a star. Fleet lamps. Closer than Ilsa's numbers said they should be.

Either the channel had stayed open longer than charted—

—or somebody had told them exactly where the shortcut ended.

He stood in the wind a long moment, the splinter working toward the surface, and made himself say it, because saying it was the only way to stop it from saying itself:

"One of us is leaking."

Then he went down to wake Ilsa.
