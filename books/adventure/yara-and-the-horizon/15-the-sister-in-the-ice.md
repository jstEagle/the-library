## 15. The Sister in the Ice

The storm pinned them to the shelf for two days, and Coral spent both of them losing the argument he'd already lost twenty years ago.

He didn't sleep. Sleep was where Maren lived now, and he had learned to ration her. But with the wind screaming against the tent fly and Ferro dosing Bo and Ilsa running weather calculations by lamplight like a woman defusing a bomb, there was nothing left to do but lie in his bag and let the memory come. It came the way water comes through a cracked hull—slowly at first, then all at once.

* * *

They were nineteen and twenty-two, and they planned the voyage on the floor of their mother's kitchen with charts weighted down by jam jars.

That was the thing people never understood about Maren Delacroix. They saw the quiet one, the careful one, the girl who double-knotted her boots and counted the crew onto the boat and off again like a shepherd. They thought she was the cautious Delacroix and Coral was the reckless one, because he talked big and climbed fast and laughed too loud at the dockside bars. But Coral knew the truth that only siblings know: Maren wasn't cautious. She was *certain*. Caution is doubt wearing a coat. Maren never doubted anything in her life, including the Meridian.

"Look at this," she said, tapping the chart with a pencil worn down to a stub. "The northern route. Six days saved."

"The northern route is a rumor," Coral said. "Half ice, half wishful thinking."

"Half ice is still half open water." She grinned at him across the kitchen table, and it was his own grin looking back at him, which should have been a warning. "The company pays a bonus for early arrival. Enough for the boat, Cor. Enough for the *down payment*. You and me, our own hull, our own name on the stern."

Their mother's kitchen smelled of bread and diesel from Maren's coveralls, and outside the window the harbor cranes stood against a grey sky like patient animals. The Delacroix family had been losing money for three generations—first to bad luck, then to bad contracts, then to a shipping company called Ardent Line that had swallowed their last vessel in a debt restructuring so clean it looked legal. What remained was a deed to a warehouse nobody wanted and a name that opened doors only to show you the room was empty.

So when Maren signed on as second mate aboard the Meridian—the flagship of the very line that had eaten their family—it wasn't betrayal. It was infiltration. That's what she called it, laughing, pencil behind her ear. *Infiltration.* Nineteen years old and already planning to take the company apart from inside its own wheelhouse.

"You hate that ship," Coral said.

"I respect that ship," she corrected. "Hate comes later, after we've got what we need."

"What we need being?"

"Evidence." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "You think a company that strangles three generations of a family does it with clean books? I'm going to be standing in their cargo holds, Cor. I'm going to see exactly what they're carrying and exactly how they're carrying it, and one day that information is going to be worth more than any bonus for early arrival."

He believed her. That was the unforgivable part, looking back. He believed her completely, because she was Maren, and Maren's certainty was the load-bearing wall of his whole life. When she said the northern route would hold, the route held. When she said the pay was fair, the pay was fair. When she said *come with me next season, we'll run the Meridian together*, he started saving before she finished the sentence.

* * *

The fight happened on the last night. Of course it did. Fights always save themselves for the last night, when there's no time left to fix them.

It started small, the way the worst ones do. They were at the Anchorline, the dockside bar where the beer was cheap and the jukebox only knew four songs. Maren had one drink, because she always had one drink, and Coral had four, because he was twenty-two and immortal. Some deckhand from another vessel made a joke about the Delacroix name—something about the family fortune fitting in a matchbox—and Coral went over the table at him before Maren could finish saying his name.

She hauled him out by the collar, into the cold, into the smell of salt and fish oil, and shoved him against the wall hard enough to rattle his teeth.

"What is wrong with you?"

"He talked about Mom's—"

"I heard what he said. Everyone heard what he said. You want to fight every man who says our name wrong, you'll spend your whole life fighting and none of it living." She was shaking—not with anger, he realized later, but with something under the anger. "We have one chance to get out from under this name. One. And you're brawling in bars two weeks before your interview with Ardent Line."

"My interview's a formality. You vouched for me."

"And my name is the only currency I have with these people, and you just tried to spend it on a bar fight." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. For a moment she looked nineteen again, actually nineteen, actually scared, and then the certainty came back up like ballast. "I need you steady on this voyage. Steadier than you've ever been. Can you do that? Because if you can't—"

"If I can't what?"

She looked at him for a long time. The harbor lights moved on the water behind her, gold smeared across black.

"If you can't, don't come," she said quietly. "I mean it, Cor. This run matters. There are things on that ship I haven't told you about, things I'm still confirming, and I can't be watching my brother's temper on top of everything else. Stay home this season. Sign on with a coastal tug. We'll do it together next year, when I've got it all worked out."

The words went into him like a splinter working toward the heart. *Don't come.* His own sister, his other half, the person who had taught him knots and stars and how to be brave in the dark—telling him to stay ashore like a child.

"Fine," he said, because he was twenty-two and proud, and pride is just fear that learned to shout. "Stay home yourself, then. Run your precious evidence mission without me. See if I care."

"Coral—"

"No, you're right. Don't come, don't go, don't fight, don't feel anything. You've got it all figured out. Go be certain somewhere else."

He walked out. That was the last thing he ever said to her: *go be certain somewhere else.* Not goodbye. Not *be careful on the ice fields*. Not *I love you*, which he had said maybe four times in his life and meant every time and never once when it counted.

The Meridian sailed on the morning tide. He watched it from the breakwater with a hangover and a grudge, and he raised two fingers in something between a salute and an insult, and Maren—because she was Maren—raised her hand back from the rail anyway. He could see her smile even at that distance. Certain to the end.

Eleven days later the Meridian went down in the northern passage with thirty-one souls aboard, and the inquiry found the wreck caused by *navigational error in known hazardous waters*, and the name attached to the navigational error, in the rumor that outran the official report, was Delacroix.

Because Maren had been at the helm when she died. Because someone had to be blamed, and dead women make convenient scapegoats, and a shipping company with dirty cargo has every reason to let a dead woman carry it. Because Coral, blacklisted from two countries within the month, banned from the inquiry by a technicality he never stopped suspecting was purchased, had no way to stand in a room and say *you're wrong, you're all wrong, my sister wouldn't—that isn't who she was.*

Except he couldn't say it. Because he hadn't been there. Because he'd stayed ashore with a grudge, exactly as she'd asked, and so the only person who knew what Maren Delacroix really did that night was the sea, and the sea doesn't testify.

* * *

Twenty years of measuring his life in miles instead of years, because miles you can count and years you have to feel. Twenty years of guiding rich fools through passes he was legally dead in, building a reputation under a trade name because his real name was a warrant. Twenty years of not opening the journal in his pack past the first page, where Maren had written, in her square careful hand: *Day 1 — Northern route. Everything according to plan.*

Everything according to plan. He used to read that line like a curse. Lately, since Voss's letter, he read it like a clue.

* * *

Coral came out of the memory with frost on his sleeping bag and Ilsa's lamp still burning. She looked up when he sat up, and whatever she saw in his face made her set down her charts.

"There's coffee," she said.

"How's Bo?"

"Asleep. Real sleep, finally. Ferro thinks the fever broke." She poured him a cup without asking, and he took it, and for a while neither of them spoke, because the storm filled the silence better than either of them could.

Then Ilsa said, carefully, the way you step onto ice you haven't tested: "Voss's letter. The second one. You've read it six times."

"It's short."

"Short letters are usually the ones that matter." She turned back to her charts, giving him the mercy of not being watched. "My father used to write those. Three lines and a knife in them."

Coral dug the letter from his jacket. He knew it by heart, but he read it again anyway, because reading it was like pressing a bruise to check whether it still hurt. It still hurt.

*Delacroix. You'll come. You were always coming; the refusal was just the scenic route. Ask yourself why the Meridian's hold was sealed from the inside. Ask yourself who sealed it. Your sister kept records—I've seen them. When we reach the wreck, you can read them, or you can let the fleet's backers burn them with the rest. Your choice. It was always your choice. — S.V.*

*Sealed from the inside.*

He had read those five words perhaps two hundred times in the last week, and each time they rearranged something in him. A hold welded shut during a sinking means someone chose to stay. Means someone chose to contain something. Means the official story—a ship lost to error and ice—has a hole in it shaped exactly like his sister.

"She told me once there were things on that ship she hadn't told me about," he said aloud. It was the first time in twenty years he'd said it to another living soul. "Things she was still confirming."

Ilsa's pencil stopped moving.

"She was nineteen," Coral said. "Nineteen, and she was going to take down a shipping company from inside its own hull, and I told her to go be certain somewhere else." He stared into the coffee. "And then I spent twenty years believing the version of events that made it my fault. Because guilt is easier than questions. Guilt you can carry. Questions you have to answer."

"Voss could be lying," Ilsa said. "It's what I'd do, if I wanted you motivated."

"He could be." Coral almost smiled. "But here's the thing, Ilsa. If he's lying, I'll know it when I see the hold. And if he's telling the truth—" His voice caught on something and he cleared it away like snow off a chart. "If he's telling the truth, then my sister didn't die causing a disaster. She died stopping one. And there's a version of me that's spent twenty years walking in the wrong direction, and I'd like, just once, before the ice takes everything, to walk in the right one."

Outside, the wind dropped. Just slightly. Just enough that they could hear it—the deep groan of the glacier shifting, the sound Ilsa had told them meant the route was opening ahead of schedule.

Ilsa looked at the tent wall, then at her watch, then at him. "Storm's breaking early," she said. "Tide tables say we've got a window starting in four hours. Fleet will move on the same window."

Coral folded Voss's letter along its old creases and put it away. Then he reached into the bottom of his pack, past the rope and the spare mitts and the emergency flare, and took out the journal—oilcloth-wrapped, corner-battered, twenty years unopened. He held it in both hands like something that might still be warm.

"Not yet," he said, mostly to himself. "When we get there. I'll read it when we get there."

Isla nodded once and began striking the stove.

Four hours later they moved out into a world scoured white and silent, six small figures roped together on the shoulder of a glacier that was already beginning, far below and far ahead, to give up its dead—and Coral led them down into the calving field with Maren's journal riding against his spine, heavier than rope, heavier than iron, and lighter with every step.
