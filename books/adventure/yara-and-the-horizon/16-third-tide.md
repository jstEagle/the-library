## 16. Third Tide

Tam Oduya had a theory about luck that he'd built over eleven years of rigging loads on mountains nobody paid honest wages for: luck was a loan, and the mountain always collected.

He was thinking about that at dawn on the third tide, because everything felt too good. The wind had dropped overnight to nothing. Ilsa's route ran clean along the shelf like someone had drawn it with a ruler. Bo was walking again—stiff, gray around the mouth still, but walking—and Ferro had stopped hovering over him every ten minutes. Even Coral had loosened. Tam had watched the man laugh twice yesterday. Actually laugh, short and rusty, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in twenty years but still worked.

Halfway. That was the number in everyone's head. Ilsa had said it last night over the stove: *Third tide puts us halfway.* And halfway meant something to people who had spent six days expecting to die. It meant the gamble was paying. It meant they might actually do this thing.

That was exactly when, in Tam's experience, the loan came due.

* * *

The morning ritual had developed its own rhythm by now. Ferro checked Bo's color and made him drink water he didn't want. Bo complained with theatrical precision. Ilsa spread her charts on a sled deck and traced the day's line with a gloved finger while everyone crowded in pretending not to crowd in. Coral stood slightly apart, always, watching not the map but the glacier—the way a sailor watches weather rather than the compass.

"Today's the day," Ilsa said. "We cross under the serac band above the icefall, then climb out of the channel onto the high shelf before the tide turns." She tapped a line of blue hatching. "Two hours through the shadow of those towers, then we're clear."

Tam looked up at where she meant. The serac field hung off the shoulder of the glacier's tributary like a collapsed cathedral—a jumble of ice blocks the size of houses, some the size of churches, frozen mid-fall or waiting to finish falling. Morning light came into them sideways and turned them amber and blue, beautiful in the way that things are beautiful right before they kill you.

"Those have been sitting there since the last ice age," Bo said. "They're not going anywhere today."

"Nothing sits that long without meaning to fall," Ferro said quietly.

"Cheerful as ever."

"That's why you keep me."

Coral came over, studied the band for a long moment. Tam watched his face do the arithmetic it always did—slope angle, sun angle, temperature, time. "We go through single file, wide spacing," Coral said finally. "No stopping under the towers. Not for photos, not for water, not if someone's boot lace breaks. We move through it like we owe it money." A ghost of a smile. "Which, knowing this crew, we do."

There were laughs all around. That should have been the warning sign, Tam thought later. Nobody laughed on day two. Day two, everybody had been too scared to laugh. Fear was the cheapest insurance a mountaineer ever bought, and they'd all let their policies lapse overnight because Ilsa had said a nice word about distance covered.

They broke camp fast. Too fast, maybe—Tam noticed Coral coiling his rope with quick, careless loops instead of the flat, patient figure-eights he'd been drilling into them since the smugglers' trail. Small thing. Tam filed it away and didn't say anything, because he'd been guilty of the same haste himself, whistling while he rigged the sleds, taking corners on the runners at speed like a boy showing off on ice.

Halfway. The word did something to a person.

* * *

They entered the shadow of the serac band an hour after dawn, and Tam understood immediately why sailors called places like this haunted. The light died all at once. The air went ten degrees colder, smelling of old ice, that deep mineral coldness like standing inside a cave that had never been opened. Above them the towers rose four hundred feet, five hundred, leaning against each other in attitudes of exhaustion—blue walls streaked with ash from some eruption decades dead, split by veins of darker ice that had melted and refrozen a thousand times.

"Spacing!" Coral called back, and the word bounced strangely off the ice, arriving flattened and late.

They strung out. Tam took the rear sled, which was tradition now—heaviest rigger, best brake control, rear guard. Ahead of him: Bo, moving well, head up, even cracking jokes under his breath that Ferro, ahead of him, refused to acknowledge. Then Ferro, then Coral, then Ilsa breaking trail on the far point, her small figure sharp against the pale wall of the channel's far side.

For the first twenty minutes, nothing happened except the sound of their own movement—boot crampons, runner hiss, breath. Tam counted his steps the way he always did in bad ground, one to fifty and start again, a habit from a bad year on Denali's routes that he'd never managed to quit. Thirty-one, thirty-two. The towers slid past overhead. Forty-four, forty-five. Somewhere deep in the ice a groan traveled through the glacier's body, long and low, more felt than heard, and passed on southward like gossip.

Fifty. Start again.

It was Bo who broke the discipline first. Of course it was Bo.

"Hold up—hold up." His voice carried down the line, bright with discovery. "You've got to see this."

Tam looked up. Bo had stopped beneath one of the smaller towers, maybe sixty feet of leaning ice above him—not safe, but survivable, the kind of pause you could get away with—and he was pointing his pole at the tower's base where the morning sun had just crept far enough to catch it.

Inside the ice, suspended like a fly in amber, was a ship's lantern. Brass frame, glass panes intact, hanging forty feet up in solid blue ice. Someone's lantern, lost a century ago, maybe two, when these towers had calved off the parent glacier and rolled and been swallowed and refrozen. A whole dead moment preserved mid-air.

"Look at it," Bo breathed. "It's just—*there*. Like somebody set it down for a minute."

"Bo." Ferro's voice, low. "Spacing."

"One second. Ferro, look at it. When are we ever going to—"

And that was when Tam's step-count hit thirty-eight, and the mountain called in its loan.

He didn't hear the crack. Later, piecing it together, he decided there probably wasn't one—that was the horror of seracs, they didn't announce themselves the way rockfall did, they simply changed their minds. What he heard was Coral's voice, one word, "*DOWN*," already stripped of everything human, pure command, and Tam was moving before the word finished because eleven years of rigging teaches your body to obey certain voices faster than your brain can vote.

He threw himself sideways off the sled runners and got maybe three meters before the world ended above Bo.

Not the whole tower—just its crown, a block the size of a fishing boat, maybe eighty tons of ice that had spent centuries deciding and finished deciding in half a second. It came down without drama, without roar, almost politely, a soft colossal *whump* that Tam felt through the soles of his boots and in his back teeth, and the air went white.

Powder snow blasted down the channel like a horizontal blizzard. Tam curled behind his sled and waited, counting—one, two, three—as the cloud boiled past and began, slowly, to settle. Snow drifted down through what light remained, fat flakes with nowhere to hurry.

When the air cleared enough to see fifteen feet, Tam stood up too fast and his vision grayed at the edges.

Where Bo had been standing there was no Bo. There was no channel floor either, not really—just a churned crater of shattered ice blocks and powder, and the fallen crown itself, huge and smooth-sided, settled across the route like a door slamming shut.

"BO!" Ferro was already running, which was wrong, which was exactly the wrong instinct, and Tam grabbed her harness at the edge of the debris and hauled her to a stop hard enough to spin her. "Probe line! You go in there alone, you're two victims!"

"He was *right there*, Tam—"

"I know. Probe line." He was already uncoiling rope, hands steady, voice steady, everything steady, because that was the deal he'd made with the mountain years ago: *you take my fear, I'll take my hands.* "Ilsa! Anchor us. Coral—"

But Coral was already moving. Not toward the debris. Along its edge, reading it, fast, the way he read everything—slope, grain, the way the broken blocks had settled. "He's not under the main block," Coral said. "Air blast threw him. Look—" He pointed. There, downslope of the impact, a sled runner sticking out of the churned snow at an angle, torn free of its load. And beyond it, a dark shape, half-buried, not moving.

They reached him in ninety seconds that took a year. Bo was face-down, snow packed in his hood, one arm folded under him at an angle arms don't fold. But when Ferro rolled him, gently, gently, he coughed—coughed snow and swore with tremendous feeling—and blinked up at the five faces crowding over him.

"The lantern," he rasped. "Did anybody get a picture?"

Nobody laughed. Then Ferro laughed, one cracked note, and then everybody did, ugly and shaky and half-hysterical, and Tam sat down in the snow because his knees had submitted a formal resignation.

* * *

The damage inventory took an hour and got worse the longer they looked.

Bo himself: left collarbone fractured, Ferro confirmed it with her fingers and her face going carefully blank, the way she did when the news was real but not yet fatal. Shoulder strapped, sling improvised from a sling, painkillers administered with a lecture attached. He could walk. He couldn't haul. He shouldn't fall again, and everyone understood without saying that the mountain would not necessarily honor that request.

The gear was worse. The air blast had ripped the rear two sleds loose of their traces and tumbled them into the debris field. Tam and Coral dug for two hours, extracting equipment piece by piece from the ice rubble: a stove recovered, a fuel can dented and hissing faintly until Tam pinched the valve shut and swore at it; the tent gone—actually gone, buried under the main block under a hundred tons of ice they'd need blasting powder to shift; half the food stores scattered and unrecoverable; and both spare ropes shredded, cut clean as butcher's twine by the razor edges of the broken ice.

They stood over the salvaged pile in the early afternoon light, which had come back into the serac field and made it beautiful again, which felt like an insult.

"No tent," Tam said. "Three ropes down to one and a half. Half our food. Bo can't haul." He looked up at the towers, which leaned on in silence. "That's the bill."

"We camp here tonight, dig in behind that wall, bivouac bags and shared body heat," Ilsa said. She had her chart out but wasn't looking at it. She was looking at the sky, at a haze building in the northwest, thin as breath on glass. "Tomorrow we make the high shelf before the fourth tide turns. If we move."

"*If,*" Coral repeated.

"If Bo can travel. If the weather holds. If—" Ilsa stopped herself, jaw tight. Tam realized he'd never seen her run out of words before, and didn't like the view.

Bo tried to stand up, made it, swayed, stayed up through visible stubbornness. "I can travel," he said. "Ask me tomorrow, ask me every hour, I don't care. I'm not burying this expedition under a chunk of ice because my collarbone wanted a souvenir." He looked around at all of them, and Tam saw the fear under the bravado now, plain, and respected it more than the bravado. "But I want to say something first."

Nobody stopped him.

"I stopped under that tower for a *lantern*." His voice came out rough. "Ilsa called spacing and I heard her and I stayed anyway, because halfway made me feel immortal. That's on me. All of this—the tent, the food, Tam's rope, Ferro carrying my share—" His good hand gestured at the wrecked sleds, the scarred channel, the whole ruined arithmetic of their supplies. "One second of stupid. One second. That's all the margin is out here. I forgot, and you all nearly paid for it."

Silence. The glacier groaned somewhere, distant, indifferent.

"So I'm asking," Bo said. "Not for forgiveness. For a job. Give me something to carry that isn't weight. Navigation watch, stove duty, whatever keeps me useful, because the alternative is lying on a sled feeling like cargo, and I've seen how this crew treats cargo." A crooked grin. "It gets abandoned in crevasse fields."

Ferro made a sound that was half sob and half laugh and turned away to fuss with his sling strap that didn't need fussing with.

Coral stepped forward. Tam watched him—watched the man take off one glove and put a bare hand briefly on Bo's good shoulder, and Tam thought: *he hasn't touched anyone in six days.* Six days of guiding them through hell and he'd kept a hand-span of distance between himself and every human being, and here it was, broken over Bo's crooked collarbone.

"You'll take navigation watch with Ilsa," Coral said. "Every hour, you call the time and the tide. You'll be the reason nobody else stops under a tower." He paused. "And Bo—you were right about one thing. The lantern was worth looking at."

"It was," Bo agreed solemnly. "Worth eighty tons of ice. Reasonable rate."

* * *

They dug in at dusk behind a ridge of pressure ice that gave them shelter from the wind now picking up out of the northwest—that haze Ilsa had spotted had organized itself into something with intent. Tam rigged the surviving tent for the injured and the sleeping bags around it in a horseshoe, and they cooked the dented stove down to embers and ate half-rations without anyone having to propose it, which was its own kind of vote.

Later, when the others had wrapped into their bags, Tam took the first watch, sitting with his back against the ice wall, facing the serac field. In the last light the towers glowed rose-gold, serene as saints. Eighty tons had come off the big one and it still looked like it had all the time in the world. Because it did. That was the difference between them. The mountain had forever; they had three tides.

He heard movement and didn't reach for his knife—his hands knew the footfalls by now. Coral lowered himself onto the ice beside him, close, though not touching, and handed over a cup of something hot that tasted mostly of sugar and gratitude.

"You pulled Ferro up before she ran in," Coral said, after a while. "Under the fall zone. Didn't hesitate."

"Somebody had to be the smart one. You were busy shouting."

"I was busy being twenty-two years old again." Coral said it flatly, staring at the towers. "For about four seconds. Watching that ice come down, I wasn't here. I was somewhere else, on another ship, another kind of ice." He turned the cup in his hands. "You weren't anywhere else. You were right here, doing the next right thing. I'm noting the difference."

Tam drank his terrible sweet coffee and said nothing, because there was a shape underneath the words and he recognized it: a man telling you, obliquely, that he trusted you, which for Coral Delacroix was roughly equivalent to other men proposing marriage.

"Get some sleep," Tam said finally. "Fourth tide comes early."

Coral nodded and moved off toward the bags. Tam settled back against the ice and took up his count—one, two, three—out of habit, watching the towers lean against the darkening sky.

It was near midnight, the light long gone, when he saw it: away to the northwest, down the channel they'd refused to sabotage, a string of lights. Small, yellow, orderly, spaced like beads on a wire. Moving fast.

Headlamps. A dozen of them, maybe more, coming up the fleet's channel at a pace no honest expedition maintained at night.

Tam sat very still and counted them twice, and then went to wake Ilsa, because whatever else those lights meant, they meant the fleet wasn't resting either—and they'd just lost their tent, their ropes, and most of a day.
