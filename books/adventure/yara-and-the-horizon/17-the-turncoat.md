## 17. The Turncoat

The scout's letter had been burning a hole in Coral's pack for two days before he finally showed it to anyone.

He told himself it was strategy. Information was leverage; you didn't spend leverage early. But the truth was uglier and he knew it: he hadn't shown them because showing them meant admitting what the letter implied. That someone out there knew exactly where they were, exactly which line Ilsa had drawn across her chart, exactly which smugglers' trail they'd paid for in blood and secrets at the toll camp.

Someone had sold them.

He sat on his kit bag at the edge of camp with the letter open on his knee, watching the fourth tide drain out of the channel below, and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the fleet scout had skied away whistling. Three days from the toll camp to here, if you pushed hard. The fleet had appeared behind them on the fifth-tide sprint, close enough that Bo could count masts through his binoculars. Close enough that Voss's second letter hadn't needed wings—it had needed only a man on skis and a route map that somebody had copied.

There were four people who'd seen Ilsa's route. Five counting him.

* * *

He waited until Ferro had Bo's ankle wrapped and the stove was ticking over on its little flame, the blue light making everyone's face look like something dredged up from under the ice. Then he laid the letter flat on the tarp between them and said nothing, because saying nothing made other people talk, and talking was what he needed from them right now.

Ilsa read it first. She read fast, lips barely moving, and when she finished she read it again slower, and Coral watched her jaw tighten around each word like she was chewing gristle.

*"Knew you'd take the low trail,"* she said finally. *"He wrote 'low trail.' That's not a guess. There are two ways off the toll plateau and only one of them is low."*

"Only one way worth taking," Tam said. He was sitting with his back against a supply crate, arms crossed, doing the thing he did where he looked relaxed and was actually coiled. "Voss could've guessed."

"Voss doesn't guess." Ilsa tapped the paper. "This is my handwriting's worth of certainty. This is someone who saw my chart or heard me describe it."

The stove ticked. Out past the camp's edge, the glacier went on groaning its long slow note, the sound it made all night, the sound Coral had stopped hearing days ago and now heard all at once, like a door opening in another room.

"So we're asking," Ferro said quietly. She didn't look up from Bo's ankle. Her hands kept working, methodical, wrapping tape. "Who talked."

Nobody answered. That was the problem with a question like that—every silence was an answer, every non-answer was an answer, and Coral sat there feeling the question work through the group like cold water wicking up cloth. He watched it move. Tam's eyes going flat and calculating. Bo trying to sit up and failing because of the ankle, looking younger than his thirty years. Ilsa going very still, the stillness she wore when she was doing navigation math in her head except this wasn't math.

Ferro finished the wrap. Stood. Looked at Coral directly, and there was something in her face he couldn't read, some flinch held down under discipline, and later—much later—he would understand what that flinch was. But standing in that moment, with suspicion already souring his stomach, he read it wrong. He read it as guilt.

"I'm going to check the perimeter," Ferro said, and took her rifle, and left.

* * *

They talked for two hours without deciding anything, which was its own kind of decision.

Tam wanted to turn back—not retreat, he was careful to say, not retreat, but *reassess*, which meant the same thing with better posture. "We've got a leak. You don't run a rope-line traverse above open water with a leak in the team. That's how people die. Not from the ice. From each other."

"Or," Ilsa said, "we assume the leak happened back at the toll camp, which is where Voss's information would have had to originate, and we stop treating each other like suspects."

"The toll camp had forty runners in it," Tam said. "Any one of them could've sold the route after we left. Sure. Or one of us gave it to them *before* we left. Somebody knew the route before we ever hit the plateau. Somebody planned this leg."

"I planned this leg," Ilsa said.

"And you're the one who had the charts."

Coral heard it land—the ugly little implication riding inside a reasonable-sounding sentence—and watched Ilsa hear it too. She didn't flare. That was worse. She just looked at Tam for a long moment with an expression like she was filing something away, and said, "Noted," and started cleaning her compass with a cloth, very thoroughly, as if the compass had done something wrong.

Bo, from his bedroll, propped on one elbow: "This is stupid. All of it. Nobody here sold us. I've known you people—" he gestured vaguely at the tent walls, the ice, the whole doomed enterprise— "for years. Tam, you cried at my wedding."

"I did not cry at your wedding."

"You cried. Ferro has a photograph."

"Ferro has a photograph of *nothing*—"

And just like that the argument broke apart into bickering, and Coral let it, because bickering was at least warm, bickering was at least human, whereas the thing underneath it—the thing they were all carefully not saying—was that the letter named a price. Voss's scout had been paid in fuel and passage, standard runner terms, but the person who'd fed him the route had been paid in something else. The letter said so, in Voss's neat taunting hand: *Your guide's secrets travel cheap out here. Everyone sells eventually. Ask yourself what yours cost.*

Everyone sells eventually. Coral turned the sentence over all night in his sleeping bag while the glacier groaned, and hated how well it fit the shape of his own life. He'd sold things. Passage, silence, the occasional client's dignity. He'd built a career in the space between legal and not, and the currency of that space was information, and he had never once believed himself above spending it.

So why should any of them be?

* * *

Morning came grey and windless, which on a glacier is more ominous than a storm. Windless means the air is holding its breath. Coral was up first, melting snow, and when Ferro came back from the dawn watch she stood at the edge of the stove-light for a moment before sitting down, and he caught it again—that flicker, that half-second where her eyes went somewhere else before coming back to his face.

"You were out late last night," he said. Casual. Too casual; he heard it come out of his mouth wearing a costume.

"Perimeter check."

"After your perimeter check. Your lamp burned past midnight. I saw it from my bag."

Something closed in her face. Not guilt exactly—more like a door shutting against weather—but he was in no state to distinguish. Twenty years of reading clients' faces for lies had honed the skill and rotted the trust, and right now the skill was running the show.

"Just couldn't sleep," Ferro said.

"Me neither."

They looked at each other across the stove, and the silence between them had weight now, and he hated it, hated himself for building it, and did it anyway, because the alternative was to be the man who got his whole crew killed by trusting the wrong person, and he had buried enough people to know he couldn't survive being that man twice.

* * *

It got worse before it got better, the way things do.

Midmorning, crossing a pressure ridge, Tam found the cairn. Someone had built a stone marker at the ridge crest—a fresh one, stones stacked wet-side-up, placed within the last day. Beside it, scratched into the ice with a boot heel: an arrow pointing along their exact line of travel, toward the narrow way, toward the fourth-tide route.

"That's not a runner's mark," Ilsa said, crouched over it. Her voice had gone flat and professional, which was how she sounded when she was frightened. "Runners mark caches. This marks *us*. This says: they came this way, they'll continue this way."

"Scout's work," Tam said. "The one who brought the letter. He tagged our route for the fleet."

"Then why does the arrow point *forward*?" Bo said from the back, hopping on his good leg, wrapped ankle strapped to a ski pole crutch. "If the fleet wants to catch us, the scout tags where we've been. This points where we're *going*. That's not tracking. That's an invitation."

Nobody said anything. The windless air pressed down. Somewhere below the ridge, deep in the glacier's plumbing, something shifted with a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

Coral looked at the arrow and felt the suspicion finish its work, quiet and complete, the way frost finishes a crack. Because there was one person among them who'd been awake past midnight. One person whose lamp had burned while the rest of them slept. One person who'd volunteered for both dawn watches since the letter arrived, keeping herself apart, keeping her movements unaccounted for.

Ferro.

He didn't say it. He looked at her—she was twenty meters back, helping Bo over the ridge, her hand steady on his arm, her face set against the glare—and he didn't say it, and the not-saying was its own accusation, hanging in the clean cold air where everyone could feel it and nobody would name it.

Ilsa caught his eye. She'd seen him look. She raised one eyebrow, a small precise question: *Well?*

*Later,* he mouthed, and turned up the ridge, and led them forward along the arrow, toward the narrow way, carrying the poison in his own pack alongside the rope and the stove fuel and Maren's journal he still hadn't opened, and the poison weighed more than all of it.

* * *

Camp that night was the worst of the trip, and it was bad because of what didn't happen.

No fire-circle talk. No cards. Tam checked the rigging twice without being asked. Ilsa re-inked her chart by lamplight and angled the board away from the rest of them, an unconscious gesture that everyone noticed and nobody mentioned, and Coral watched her do it and thought: *look what we've become. Four people in a tent the size of a chapel, guarding a piece of paper from each other.*

Ferro sat outside on watch. Of course she did. Volunteered again. And Coral made his decision the way he made all his worst decisions—alone, quickly, and dressed up as decisiveness.

He went out to her.

She heard him coming—of course she did, she always did—and didn't turn, just kept scanning the white dark where the glacier met the sky. Her rifle lay across her knees. The temperature had dropped hard; her breath plumed and hung.

"Talk to me," Coral said.

"About what?"

"About whatever it is you're not telling me."

That got her. She turned, and in the starlight her face was older than it had been at the start of this, everyone's face was, and she said, "You think it's me."

"I think someone sold the route."

"Somebody at the toll camp, maybe. Forty runners, Coral. Half of them would sell their mothers for a tank of fuel."

"The lamp."

"What?"

"Your lamp burns past midnight. Two nights running. You volunteer for every watch. You go off alone. And yesterday morning you couldn't look at me." He heard his own voice and recognized it—the voice from interrogations, from border crossings, from the worst year of his life when everyone he met was either lying to him or about to. Cold. Procedural. "I'm not accusing. I'm asking."

Ferro stood up. Slowly, the way she did everything, a big deliberate woman who never wasted a motion. She slung the rifle. For one absurd moment he thought she might swing it at him, and part of him—the honest part—thought he'd deserve it.

"You want to know what I was doing past midnight?" she said. "I was writing a letter."

"To who?"

"To my daughter." The word landed in the silence like a dropped tool. "She's eleven. She lives with my sister in Tromsø because I got struck off the medical register for a mistake I didn't make, and I haven't seen her in three years because every job I take is illegal and every illegal job is one raid away from prison, and I write to her by lamplight at midnight because that's the only hour on this ice that belongs to me. That's my secret, Coral. That's the crime. I miss my kid."

The glacier groaned. Far off, a serac fell with a sound like a door slamming in a cathedral.

"Why couldn't you look at me yesterday morning?" His voice had gone rough without permission.

"Because you asked who talked, and I watched you look at each of us, and I saw you deciding." She stepped closer. Up close her eyes were wet and furious and steady. "I've worked for men like you. Good men. Men with reasons. And I have watched suspicion eat a team from the inside, and I will tell you what I told the last one: the mountain doesn't care who betrayed whom. It kills loyal teams and rotten ones at the same rate. The only variable is whether the team is still holding the rope when it happens."

She walked past him to the tent. At the flap she stopped.

"There's one more thing. Since we're trading secrets." She didn't turn around. "Nine days ago, before the first tide, I sent a distress ping. Short range, coded, medical channel. Bo's cough was turning into something bad and I wanted a medevac option on file in case he crashed. One ping. It's traceable. If anyone followed a signal to find us—" she shrugged, a small bitter motion— "it might have been mine. Not the route. Never the route. But the signal, possibly. So if you're keeping a ledger, put it in the book."

She went inside.

Coral stood alone under the stars for a long time, feeling the suspicion drain out of him and something heavier flood in behind it—shame, and under the shame, a colder thought, moving slowly, the way truth moves:

If Ferro's ping was traceable, then the fleet hadn't needed a turncoat at all. They'd needed only a receiver. Which meant the arrow on the ridge, the letter, the whole architecture of betrayal he'd spent two days constructing—

Might be built on nothing. Might be *designed*. Voss didn't guess. Voss planted. A letter naming a traitor who didn't exist, seeded into a team, left to grow.

And it had grown. God, how it had grown.

Coral looked back at the tent, at the lamplight where his crew sat guarding their charts and their letters and their children's names from one another, and understood that the real damage wasn't behind them on some runner's ledger. It was right here, already done, already inside the ropes.

Tomorrow they'd reach the narrow way—a single rope-line above open water, the fourth tide, no room for error and none for doubt. He'd lead them onto that line carrying what he carried.

First, though, he had to walk back into that tent and tell them everything. Including the part where he'd been wrong. Including the part where being wrong might have been exactly what someone wanted.

Including the ping—which meant, if he was honest, that the fleet might not need to follow their route at all.

They might already know precisely where they'd sleep tonight.
