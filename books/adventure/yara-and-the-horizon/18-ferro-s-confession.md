## 18. Ferro's Confession

Coral didn't sleep. He sat at the edge of camp with the rope coiled in his lap, running his thumb along its sheath like a man counting beads, and let the suspicion do what suspicion does best: grow teeth.

The turncoat theory had a shape now. Someone on the runners' side of the border had sold their route to the fleet—Voss's people had been waiting at the second channel with a confidence that no guess could explain. And there were only five people who knew the route. Four of them he'd have trusted with his life a week ago. The fifth was himself.

He turned each of them over in the dark like stones.

Ilsa had charted the route before Coral agreed to the job. She'd had it in her hands for days alone. But Ilsa loved maps the way monks love scripture; she didn't sell them, she *was* them. Tam and Bo were brothers in everything but blood, and Tam had suggested sabotage and accepted refusal without sulking—a man planning betrayal doesn't take no that gracefully. Ferro—

Ferro was the one who'd argued hardest against coming. Ferro who'd checked Bo's pupils twice a day since the crevasse. Ferro who kept to himself, kept records, kept a small transmitter case locked in his kit that Coral had never once asked about because medics carry radios, that's what medics do.

The thought arrived whole, ugly, and unwelcome: *the medic has a radio.*

Coral stood, joints cracking in the cold, and told himself he was going to check the perimeter. He wasn't. He crossed to the medical sledge where Ferro slept sitting up, back against the cargo, hands folded on his chest like a man posing for his own effigy. He wasn't asleep either.

"You're up," Ferro said.

"Perimeter check."

"At three in the morning." Ferro's voice was flat, tired, and something else underneath it—resignation, maybe, the tone of a man who'd heard footsteps stop beside his bed before. "Coral. Sit down."

"I don't need—"

"Sit down or stand there looking guilty, but either way I'm going to talk, and you're going to listen, because I've watched you circling this camp for six hours like a dog that smells smoke, and I know exactly which fire you think you've found."

Coral stood there. Then, because his legs made the decision, he sat.

Ferro reached into his coat—not fast, not slow—and brought out the transmitter case. Set it between them on the ice like a confession laid on a table. The little unit's status light glowed green. Sent log: four days old.

"Day two," Ferro said. "After the crevasse. After we got Bo out."

Coral said nothing. His pulse was loud in his ears.

"Bo was worse than we told you," Ferro went on. "Worse than I told anyone. Hypothermia goes in stages, and he'd skipped one. His core temp was low enough that I should have evacuated him. Proper evacuation. Medevac, shelter, the works. Instead I wrapped him in both my spare bags and told everyone he was fine, because I looked at your face when you pulled him out of that hole, and I understood that if I said the word *evacuate*, you'd order it—and if you ordered it, this crew turns around, and if this crew turns around, we lose the window, and you lose—" He stopped. Started again. "You lose whatever it is you're really out here for."

"That wasn't your call."

"No," Ferro agreed. "It wasn't. So I made a different call instead, which is how these things always go wrong. I sent a distress ping. Encrypted, short-range, medical band. There's a coastal station that monitors it—runners mostly, smugglers, the kind of people who answer a medical emergency because nobody else will. I asked for cold-weather pharmaceuticals and a rendezvous point off our route. No coordinates for us. No route data. Nothing about the job."

Coral stared at the green light. "And they traced it anyway."

"The ping bounces through relay buoys. One of those relays is owned by the same family of vultures that runs the toll crossing. They logged the burst, triangulated backward from the buoy, and sold the corridor—not our position, just the corridor—to whoever was paying. Which was Voss." Ferro exhaled, long and slow, fog curling in the lamplight. "I found out yesterday when I went back for the resupply drop. The station operator apologized. Said he assumed I'd want to know my signal had been read. That's the courtesy among thieves, apparently. Honesty after the fact."

The silence stretched. Somewhere out on the ice, the glacier groaned—a deep tectonic complaint, the mountain shifting in its sleep.

"So it was you," Coral said. Not an accusation. Just placing the stone down.

"It was me." Ferro met his eyes. "Not the fleet. Not Voss. Not a traitor in your crew. A medic who was scared for his patient and stupid about his equipment. If you want to court-martial me for it, do it in daylight, so I can defend myself properly."

Coral looked at the transmitter. At the man. At the four days of shared rope and shared storm and shared rations stacked behind them, and felt the whole architecture of his suspicion collapse—not gently, but all at once, like a snow bridge giving way.

Because here was the thing he couldn't say out loud yet: some part of him had *wanted* it to be Ferro. Wanted a traitor. A traitor was simple. A traitor meant the world still worked the way it had worked twenty years ago, when the disaster had a face and a name and someone to blame. If Ferro had sold them, then Coral's instincts were intact, his judgment sound, his isolation justified. Twenty years of walking alone would be proven correct by a single act of treachery.

Instead he'd gotten fear. Human, ordinary, defensible fear—from the most careful man on the ice, doing the most caring work there was.

*Memory is a burden,* he'd told himself once. But tonight it was worse than a burden. Tonight it was a lens, and everything seen through it came out warped.

"Ferro," Coral said finally. "Bo. Right now. How bad?"

"Recovering. Genuinely. The drugs arrived, the fever broke, and he ate a full ration last night and complained about the taste, which is how I know he's fine." A ghost of a smile. "But four days ago? Four days ago I'd have traded your whole route for his pulse. That's the truth of it. You can hate me for the method. Don't ask me to regret the reason."

"I don't hate you."

"You circled this camp for six hours."

"I know what I did." Coral rubbed his face with both hands, gloves rasping against stubble. "I did the arithmetic. I sat out there and I ran all four of you through it like suspects in a dockside hearing, and I want you to know how that felt afterward, finding out the answer was fear. It felt like being handed the bill for twenty years of a habit I thought was wisdom."

"And what habit is that?"

"Assuming the worst first. It keeps you alive on a mountain." He paused. "It kills everything else."

Ferro was quiet a moment. Then: "For what it's worth—the others don't know you suspected. Tam's been bragging about his knot work. Ilsa redrew the route twice today to shave an hour. Bo asked me this morning if you were angry with him for falling. They're out there trusting you completely, Coral. Whatever you decide to do with what I just told you, decide it knowing that."

That landed harder than any accusation could have. Coral nodded once, stood, and picked up the transmitter case. Held it out.

"Keep it. But it stays powered down until we're off the ice. If Bo worsens—if it's life or death—you send anything you need to send and I'll live with the consequences. Otherwise, we go dark."

"Agreed." Ferro took the case. Then, quieter: "You're not your father's son out here, you know. In case anyone ever told you otherwise."

"My father's dead."

"So is mine. Doesn't stop them from talking." Ferro settled back against the sledge. "Get some sleep, skipper. We make the narrows tomorrow, and Ilsa says the fourth tide won't wait for guilt."

Coral walked back toward his own tent, and the ice creaked beneath him, and for the first time in days the sound didn't feel like accusation—just the mountain, minding its own enormous business.

* * *

He did sleep, eventually—two hours of black, dreamless nothing—and woke to the smell of stove-heated water and Tam's voice arguing cheerfully with Bo about whether a man who'd fallen into a crevasse earned extra breakfast or forfeited it.

"Extra," Bo was saying. "Trauma surcharge."

"Forfeit. You dropped sixty meters of good rope into a hole."

"The rope sacrificed itself nobly."

"The rope was rented."

Coral emerged from his tent into grey morning light and stopped, just for a moment, to look at all four of them. Ilsa bent over her charts, pencil behind her ear, muttering elevations. Tam gesturing with a spoon. Bo propped on a crate, color back in his face, laughing without wincing. Ferro checking a dressing on Bo's wrist with the brisk tenderness of a man absolved.

They trusted him. Completely, stupidly, magnificently. And twelve hours ago he'd sat in the dark weighing them like contraband.

"Skipper," Ilsa said, not looking up. "Narrows are eight hours out. Fourth tide fills the channel at eighteen hundred. If we're not on the traverse when it does, we wait a full cycle on a shelf the size of a dinner table."

"Then we move early," Coral said. "Break camp in twenty."

Tam raised his spoon in salute. Bo started folding his sleeping bag with exaggerated care, milking his convalescence. Ferro kicked snow over the stove embers.

Coral took down the small tent, rolled it, strapped it—and found Ilsa standing beside him, chart tube under her arm, watching him with the particular patience she reserved for things she'd already figured out.

"You and Ferro talked last night," she said.

"We talked."

"Good." She handed him the chart tube. "Because whatever it was, you look lighter. And I need you light, skipper. The narrows don't forgive heavy men."

She walked off toward the sledges. Coral stood holding the tube, feeling the strange new weightlessness she'd named—guilt set down, even if not put away—and looked north, where the route bent toward the traverse: a single rope-line above open water, drawn on Ilsa's map as a thin blue thread across a white void.

Eight hours. One tide. Five people, all accounted for, all forgiven, all his.

He shouldered his pack and followed his crew onto the ice.

Behind them, far to the south, a smudge of smoke rose against the horizon—the fleet, closer than yesterday, closing the gap with every hour they'd lost to storms and crevasses and fear. One tide of advantage left. Maybe less.

Coral counted the miles instead of the years, and for once, the miles felt like they were taking him somewhere worth going.
