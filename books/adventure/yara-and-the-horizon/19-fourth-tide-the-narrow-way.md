## 19. Fourth Tide: The Narrow Way

Ilsa had charted the route three times in her head before she ever saw it, and all three times her mind had flinched at the same place—a gap in the shelf where the ice pinched down to nothing, a throat of blue-black water breathing between two walls of glacier. On the charts it was a pencil line. In person it was a verdict.

The fourth tide was coming in.

She could feel it before she heard it, the way you feel weather in your teeth. The water below the traverse had been slack and sullen an hour ago, gray as old pewter. Now it had a color to it, a green-black shine, and a sound underneath the wind like something large turning over in its sleep. The tide was filling the channel. In ninety minutes it would fill it completely, and the ledge they needed to cross would be a seam of wet stone and older ice with the sea chewing at its underside.

"Talk to me," Coral said behind her.

He'd been saying that for two days. Not *are we fine*, not *how long*—just *talk to me*, like her voice was another instrument he trusted more than his own eyes. She'd decided not to examine that too closely. Some things you ruined by looking at them.

"One rope line," she said. "Four hundred meters, maybe less if the melt's taken more of it. Single anchor points every sixty meters—old pitons from the smuggler era, so we test every one. Below us is open water at four degrees. You go in, Ferro has maybe eight minutes to get you out, and then we're done talking about your hands ever working again."

"And the order?"

She looked back at him. He stood with the wind flattening his hood against his skull, face raw from five days of cold, and he was watching her the way he watched terrain—not with fear, exactly, but with the total attention of a man who intended to survive whatever answer came next.

"You're last," Ilsa said.

Something moved through the crew behind him—Tam shifting his weight, Bo still moving stiffly on the leg the crevasse had nearly taken, Ferro quiet as always but present, which for Ferro was a kind of speech. Nobody argued. That was the strange part. Five days ago every one of them would have expected Coral Delacroix to take point, because Coral took point the way other men breathed.

"I've led this crew six days," Ilsa said. "You've led it twenty years, just never all in one place. But this pitch is mine. I charted it. I've crossed worse in the Svalbard channels when the fleet still thought I was employable." She let that sit. "And you're carrying Maren's journal in the inside pocket where you'll break a rib reaching for it if you slip. Last position. That's not punishment. That's arithmetic."

Coral held her gaze for a long moment. Then he did the thing she would think about later, on the far shore, in the years after: he nodded once, unhurried, like a man handing over a tool he knew was in better hands than his own.

"Orders, then," he said.

* * *

They rigged for forty minutes because Tam refused to rig faster. He tested each piton with the maul and his whole body weight, hanging off anchors drilled into ice by smugglers who'd been dead or dispersed for a decade, and where the iron was rotten he replaced it with his own gear without complaint, drilling new holes while the tide-sound grew beneath them like applause from a very large audience that wanted them to fail.

Bo went second-to-last instead of third, on Ferro's insistence—"I want my hands on his harness rope, not four bodies between us"—and Bo accepted it without joke, which told Ilsa how much the crevasse had cost him. Even now he kept glancing down at the water as if it owed him money.

Ilsa went first.

The traverse was exactly what she'd promised and worse than she'd described: a shelf of blue ice rarely wider than her boots, tilted outward, polished by a century of tides licking up to its lip twice a day. To her left the wall rose sheer and dripping. To her right, past the rope, the air simply ended, and below the air the channel opened its green throat, thirty meters of cold down to the first floes riding in on the tide.

She clipped in, said the old words under her breath—the ones her father used on the herring boats, half prayer and half arithmetic—and moved.

Sixty meters to the first station. She counted her steps the way she counted everything, in fathoms, in knots, in units her grandfather would have understood. The ice sang under her crampons, a high thin note like a wire tightening. At station one the old piton held. She clipped the running line through, called *"Good!"* over her shoulder, and felt the rope come alive behind her as the crew's weight joined hers.

That was the thing nobody told you about leading: you were never alone on a rope line. Every meter forward was a meter someone else would walk, trusting your meter. She had spent eleven years being unemployable because she'd once trusted a captain's chart over her own reading of the water, and nine people had learned what cold does. She had sworn she'd never carry anyone's trust again.

Then Coral Delacroix had shown up at her door with a burned letter's ashes still practically on his coat, asking for nothing, and somehow here she was, carrying four lives across a throat of the sea on a shelf that would not exist in a week.

*"Station two!"* she called. *"Hold!"*

It held.

* * *

Two hundred meters in, the shelf narrowed to a hand's width, and the wind found them properly.

It came down the channel like water finding a drain, funneling between the glacier walls, and it hit the traverse broadside with enough force that Ilsa went flat against the wall and stayed there, cheek to the ice, counting. Behind her the rope went taut and then slack in a rhythm—someone falling into the wall, recovering, moving again. She couldn't look. Looking cost balance, and balance was the only currency this pitch accepted.

*"Wind's gusting to forty!"* Tam bellowed from behind her. *"We wait it out or we push?"*

She measured it the way her father taught her: the sound of the water below, the interval between gusts. Forty seconds of screaming, twenty of mutter. Workable, if you moved in the mutters.

"We push in the lulls!" she shouted back. "Nobody moves in the scream! Count your own wind!"

And they did—five people spread along four hundred meters of ice, each of them alone with the rope and the wall and their own private count, advancing in bursts, freezing in place, advancing again. It was the strangest parade Ilsa had ever led: a procession of the unemployable crossing a seam of the world that would be erased by morning, moving only when the mountain allowed it.

At station four she found the problem.

The smugglers' piton had sheared clean off, leaving a rusted stub and a scar in the ice, and beyond it the shelf thinned to less than a boot-width for fifteen meters, curving slightly around a bulge in the wall where meltwater had carved the rock underneath. The tide was audible there—not distant anymore but directly below, a heavy breathing through a blowhole in the channel wall, spraying mist that was already re-glazing the shelf with a film of black ice.

She hung at the station and made herself look at it for a full minute. Fifteen meters of black glass over open water, no anchor, wind at her back pushing her toward the drop.

There was a way. There was always a way; that was the whole of her profession. But the way required the leader to free-solo fifteen meters of verglas above a rising sea, and then set a new anchor from stance, and everyone behind her would have to do it roped, sliding on their teeth essentially, praying to her anchor.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking and made herself be interested in that fact rather than afraid of it. Fear was data. Her father again. Fear tells you the size of the thing. It doesn't tell you yours.

*Ilsa,* she thought, *you have been waiting eleven years to find out.*

She backed up to the last good rock—the wall here was stone, not ice, a rib of black basalt poking through—and placed her own protection, two cams in a vertical crack, equalized. Then she unclipped from the rope line.

Behind her, faintly, through the wind's mutter, she heard Coral's voice: *"Ilsa. Report."*

"Anchor change," she called back. "Fifteen meters unprotected. I'm soloing it to set the next station. Everyone stays clipped until I call good."

A pause. She could feel him weighing it from two hundred meters away, the way you feel weather.

"Your pitch," he said finally. "We're on your clock."

* * *

She climbed it badly and she climbed it well, both at once—ugly, cautious, heel-hooking off micro-edges, pressing her body into the wall like a lover, refusing three moves her pride suggested because pride was how people died on verglas. The spray from the blowhole hit her left side in rhythmic pulses, timed to the tide's breathing, and she learned its rhythm within four moves and climbed in the dry gaps.

Halfway across, the tide surged harder than the pattern allowed.

The blowhole coughed a column of spray that caught her full in the face, and the world went white and silent, and her right crampon skated on fresh black ice with nothing under it but the channel.

Time did something strange. Later she would remember it in pieces: the wall against her palms. The sound of her own breath, very loud. The rope trailing loose behind her, connected to nothing, because she had unclipped. Her weight tipping past the angle where ice decides things for you—

—and her left front-points biting, one centimeter of old blue ice under the film, holding, holding, her whole life funneled into a single tooth of steel.

She didn't move for ten seconds. She let the tide finish its sentence. Then, in the next lull, she finished the traverse, reached the rib of sound ice beyond, and drilled two bolts with hands that would not stop trembling until she made them, screwing the hanger down while the sea exhaled below her.

*"STATION FIVE!"* she screamed into the wind. *"GOOD! SEND THEM ACROSS! CLIP THE NEW LINE, IT'S FRESH IRON!"*

* * *

They came across one at a time, in the lulls, and she anchored herself at station five and belayed each of them the last hard stretch personally, because that was what leaders did—they put themselves at the worst point so the others passed through the worst point attached to someone steady.

Tam came through growling at the ice like it was a personal enemy. Ferro came through silent and precise, checking Bo's knot twice before letting him onto the pitch. Bo came through swearing steadily under his breath, a continuous low recitation that Ilsa suspected was the only thing keeping him upright, and when he reached her stance he pressed his forehead against the ice for three seconds, just breathing, before he looked up and grinned at her with bloodless lips.

"Never," he said, "again."

"You said that about the crevasse."

"The crevasse had better manners."

Then the rope behind went taut in the slow, deliberate way of a careful climber, and Coral came around the bulge.

She watched him take the fifteen meters. She watched him climb it exactly the way she had—no flourish, no speed, just economy, a man who had clearly decided that the fastest way across was the way that guaranteed arrival. And when he reached her stance he didn't celebrate, didn't exhale dramatically, didn't do any of the things she'd seen expedition leads do after a crux. He clipped into the anchor beside her, looked back at the traverse, at the four hundred meters of impossible they had just folded up behind them, and said:

"That was the best lead I've seen in twenty years on this ice."

Ilsa laughed—a short, cracked sound, mostly adrenaline. "You're biased. You were terrified."

"Completely," he agreed. "That's why I know."

Below them the tide reached its height and paused, the whole channel going strangely still, the blowhole quiet, the water lying green and deep and patient against the glacier walls. The window was closing. In an hour the ebb would begin, and the route ahead—the reason they'd pushed through the traverse today and not tomorrow—would open for exactly as long as the sea allowed.

Ilsa checked her watch, then the sky, then the water, in that order, the order her father had taught her.

"Coral," she said. "Look north."

He followed her finger. Past the shoulder of the glacier, where the channel bent toward the deep field, the horizon was doing something horizons shouldn't do at this hour.

Smoke. A thin dark smudge of it, standing up straight in the cold air, too deliberate to be weather.

Tam saw it a moment later and said what they were all thinking:

"That's not a campfire. That's a fleet."
