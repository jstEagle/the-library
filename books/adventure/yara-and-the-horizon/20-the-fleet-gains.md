## 20. The Fleet Gains

We came down off the shoulder of the ridge at first light of the fourth tide, and that was when Tam saw it.

He didn't say anything at first. He just stopped, one boot planted on a shelf of blue ice, and tilted his head the way he did when he was listening to something the rest of us couldn't hear yet. Tam had a talent for sound. Twenty years running rigging on ships and cliffs had tuned his ears to frequencies most people missed—the creak of an overloaded line, the tick of ice about to let go.

"Smoke," he said finally.

I followed his chin. Below us, far down where the glacier's tongue met the grey water of the channel, a thread of black rose against the pale morning. Not campfire smoke. This was engine smoke, heavy and oily, the kind that hangs in cold air like a signature.

Ilsa was beside me in three seconds, pulling her glass from its case. She braced her elbow against a bollard of ice and held still for a long moment, so long I started counting my own heartbeats to keep from asking.

"One ship," she said. "Maybe two behind it, hull-down. It's taking the main channel."

"The channel we didn't burn," Tam said.

Nobody answered him. Nobody had to.

* * *

Two nights ago, crouched above that same channel with a satchel of thermite charges and a plan, I had made a choice. The fleet's lead vessel would have to pass through a narrows where the ice ran thin over fast water—a bottleneck any competent saboteur could crack open like an egg. Six hours' work with the charges, maybe less. The fleet would lose days digging their ships out or turning back for another route. Days we needed.

Tam had wanted it. Bo had wanted it—I saw it in his face, that hungry look he got when the job turned personal. Even Ferro hadn't argued, and Ferro argued about everything.

But I'd stood there looking down at the narrows, at the black water sliding under the ice, and I'd thought about what kind of man wins this race by breaking other men's ships under them. The channel wasn't just a route. It was the only way out for anyone who got into trouble on that side of the glacier. Seal it, and if Voss's crews hit weather—if anyone fell through, if anything went wrong—there would be no rescue coming. There would be no rescue possible.

Maren had died because eleven people had no way out. I wasn't going to build the same trap for strangers, even strangers racing to steal what was mine.

So I'd put the charges back in the pack, and Tam had looked at me like I'd handed away our lives along with them, and we'd climbed on.

Now the cost of that choice was rising out of the channel in a plume of honest, unburned diesel, and it was gaining on us.

"How far?" I asked.

Ilsa worked the glass. "Twelve miles as the ice flies. They're making good speed—no load, light draft, pushing hard." She lowered it. "They'll reach the calving wall by tonight. We'll reach it by tomorrow midday, if the route holds."

"So they take the wreck first." Bo said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "They'll strip the hold before we're even in sight of it."

"Not necessarily," Ilsa said. "The seaward approach is bad. Ice foot, pressure ridges. They'll need half a day to get landing parties ashore. That buys us time."

"Half a day," Tam repeated. He sat down heavily on his pack and pulled off a glove to rub his face. "Coral. I want you to hear me say this once, and then I'll never say it again. If we'd burned that channel, we'd be standing on the wreck right now."

"I know," I said.

"And you'd do it again?"

I looked at him. Tam was the oldest of us, grey in his beard, hands like tree roots, a man who'd been fired from more ships than he could count for refusing orders he considered stupid. That was why he was here. Every one of us was here because somewhere, sometime, we'd chosen the harder right over the easier wrong and paid for it with our livelihoods. If I told him yes, I'd do it again, I'd be telling him the thing he already knew about me—and about himself.

"Yes," I said. "Again. Every time."

Tam blew out a long breath through his nose. Then he stood up and shouldered his pack. "Then we'd better move faster," he said. "Because being right doesn't put us on that wreck first."

* * *

We moved.

The fourth tide was generous by the standards of these waters—high enough to float the ice bridges, low enough to leave the pressure ridges exposed and climbable—but generous wasn't the same as kind. The route Ilsa had charted threaded between serac fields where the glacier leaned over us in towers the size of churches, groaning as the sun warmed their flanks. We roped up in pairs and moved in silence, because silence was the price of passage through country like this, and because none of us had much to say that wasn't bitter.

The fleet stayed visible all morning. From the high ground we could see the whole channel laid out below like a grey ribbon, and the lead ship crawled along it, trailing its smoke, close enough now that Ilsa's glass picked out details: the crane on her foredeck, the orange of survival suits on her rail, the wake she threw off in a white fan.

"She's flying company colors," Ilsa said at the midday halt. She passed me the glass. "Look at the funnel."

I found the ship, steadied my elbows, and looked. The funnel was dark blue with a device painted near the top—too small to read at this distance, but I knew the shape. A compass rose inside a broken circle.

Halvard Marine. The same company whose flag flew over the Meridian twenty years ago. The same company whose official inquiry had concluded that a Delacroix girl's negligence had sunk one of their finest vessels, killed thirty-one souls, and cost them a fortune in cargo and reputation. The same company that had made sure the inquiry's conclusion traveled further and faster than any counter-evidence ever could.

"They're not sending a salvage crew," I said. "They're sending an eraser."

Ferro, crouched over the stove melting snow for tea, glanced up. "You think they know what's in the hold?"

"Voss knows. And Voss doesn't spend his own money." I handed the glass back. "Whatever documents Maren sealed in there, Halvard wants them melted down into slag before anyone else reads them. The inheritance is a bonus. The truth is the target."

Bo had been quiet since the channel. Now he spoke without lifting his head from the map he was studying. "Then burning the channel wouldn't have saved us anyway. They'd have come around the long way, lost two days instead of six hours, and we'd have burned our own exit behind us." He tapped the map. "This narrows here? It's the only way out on their side too. You didn't hand away the race, Coral. You kept the door open for everybody—including us."

It was the closest thing to absolution anyone offered that day, and I took it the way you take water in a dry camp: gratefully, and knowing it wouldn't last.

* * *

The afternoon went wrong slowly, the way afternoons do on a glacier—not with a bang but with a series of small negotiations between the ice and your confidence.

First the route narrowed. What had been a broad ramp of tide-washed ice funneled down to a ledge barely wide enough for one person, hugging a wall on the left and dropping forty feet to a jumble of boulder ice on the right. Ilsa went first, testing every step, calling back the holds. We crossed in single file, packs scraping the wall, nobody breathing much.

Then the ledge ended at a crevasse bridge that hadn't been in Ilsa's charts—a fresh collapse, snow bridging a gap that should have been open. She probed it for ten minutes with the pole, found a line that would hold, and sent us across one at a time while she watched each crossing with her jaw set like a woman defusing something.

Tam went last, as always. As always, the bridge held for him exactly as well as it had held for anyone, which was to say: completely, and only because the mountain hadn't decided otherwise yet.

By late afternoon we could see the calving wall itself—a cliff of ice two hundred feet high running along the head of the channel, blue-white and streaked with grey moraine, the face of the glacier where it met the sea. Somewhere behind that wall, entombed in the ice the glacier had swallowed and carried down the valley for twenty years, lay the Meridian.

And below the wall, anchored in the lee of a point of rock, lay the fleet's lead ship.

She'd beaten us by half a day, just as Ilsa predicted. Her boats were already shuttling toward the ice foot at the base of the wall. Through the glass I counted figures in orange suits wading ashore, hauling gear, setting up a camp with the crisp efficiency of people who did this for money rather than love. A floodlight mast was going up. They meant to work through the night.

"There," Ilsa said quietly. She passed me the glass and pointed with one finger.

At the edge of the landing beach, apart from the working parties, a lone figure stood facing the glacier. Even at this distance I knew the silhouette—the particular stillness of him, hands clasped behind his back, watching the ice the way other men watch a fire.

Sable Voss had come in person. Of course he had. He'd known I would come; he'd said so in his letter. Men like Voss don't delegate the endings of stories.

"He can't see us," Bo said. "Too far, and we're skylined wrong."

"He doesn't need to see us," I said. "He knows where we are. He's known since the border runners sold our route." I lowered the glass. "He's not watching for us. He's watching the ice. He's trying to find her."

Nobody asked who *her* was. They knew.

* * *

We made camp that night in a hollow two miles short of the wall, tucked behind a ridge of old moraine that cut the wind. Cold work, silent work. Ferro got the stove hissing and fed us barley and fat and salt tea, and we ate in the orange glow of the stove screen while outside the temperature fell through numbers I preferred not to think about.

From the hollow we could hear the fleet working—generators, the whine of a winch, voices carrying flat across the ice. And beneath it all, deeper, the sound the whole region made at night: the glacier talking to itself. Cracks like rifle shots. Long groans that started below hearing and climbed until your teeth ached. The calving wall settling into its moods.

"We've got one tide," Ilsa said. She spread her chart on a sled deck and weighted the corners with pitons. "The fifth tide opens tomorrow at noon and closes by evening. After that the window starts shutting—water drops, the bridges starve, and the route we came in on becomes a death trap in both directions. If we're not off this ice by the sixth tide, we're not off it at all until next season."

"And the fleet?" Ferro asked.

"They don't care about tides. They've got ships, fuel, cranes. They can sit on that wreck for a week." Ilsa's finger traced the last stretch of route. "Our only chance is the fifth tide. We cross the final ice field at high water, reach the wreck from the landward side—their landing's on the seaward foot, they won't expect an approach from the moraine—and get into the hold before they've finished cutting through the hull."

"That's assuming they haven't already found the fissure we're planning to use," Tam said.

"It's assuming a lot of things," Ilsa agreed. "But it's the plan we have."

I sat with my back against the sled, listening to the glacier grind, and thought about the shape of the next eighteen hours. One tide. One window. A race we were losing by half a day against men with machines, armed with nothing but a smuggler's trail, a dead woman's journal, and the stubborn arithmetic of people who had nowhere else to be.

Twenty years ago I'd have called that hopeless. But I'd spent those twenty years learning that hopelessness is mostly a failure of imagination, and imagination was the one tool nobody in the salvage fleet had bothered to bring.

"Coral." Ferro's voice, low. "You've been quiet."

"I'm counting," I said.

"Counting what?"

"Miles," I said. Then, hearing how it sounded, how it had sounded for twenty years, I corrected myself. "Hours. Just hours now."

Ferro studied me for a moment in the stove-light. Whatever he saw, he nodded and said nothing, which was Ferro's particular gift.

* * *

I took first watch, because I wanted to see the fleet's lights and I wanted to be alone with them.

They burned all night down at the ice foot—a string of yellow points against the black water, generators humming, floodlight mast blazing against the calving wall so that the glacier's face glowed like the inside of a lantern. They were beautiful, those lights. They were also everything standing between me and my sister's name.

I thought about the choice in the narrows. Two days ago, standing over that thin ice with thermite in my pack, I'd chosen not to become the kind of man who drowns strangers to win a race. It had felt clean then. It felt less clean now, watching the enemy's lights bloom fat and comfortable below the wall while we shivered in a hole in the moraine eating cold barley.

Integrity, it turns out, has a price tag, and the currency is time. I had paid in hours, and the hours had compounded into half a day, and the half a day had become a fleet sitting on the wreck while we watched from the dark. There was no version of the ledger where that trade came out even. There was only the version where I decided, again and again, that I could live with the exchange rate.

And the strange thing—the thing I kept turning over like a stone in my pocket—was that I could. Losing honestly sat better in my chest than winning dirty ever would have. Maren had died keeping a door open for other people. The least I could do was keep one open myself, even if it cost me the race.

Even if it cost me everything.

Behind me, boots crunched on frozen scree. Ilsa settled onto the rock beside me without a word, handed me a cup of tea gone lukewarm, and looked out at the lights with me.

"One tide," she said.

"One tide," I agreed.

Down at the wall, the floodlights swung, and for a moment the beams crossed the glacier's face and lit something deep in the ice—a dark shape, angular, impossibly regular, the kind of geometry that nature doesn't make.

A hull. Ninety feet up in the ice, black steel showing through blue, like a shipwreck hanging in the sky.

Ilsa saw it the same instant I did. Neither of us said a word. But I heard her breath catch, and I heard mine answer it, and somewhere below us the glacier groaned like something waking up.

The fifth tide would come at noon. The Meridian was already showing herself.

And Sable Voss was standing directly beneath her, waiting for us to arrive.
