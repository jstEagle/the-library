## 21. Night Camp at the Calving Wall

The glacier announced itself before we saw it.

We'd been climbing the frozen riverbed for two hours in the dark, headlamps cutting cones through blowing spindrift, when the sound reached us—a deep, tectonic groan rolling down the valley like a ship's hull flexing in heavy seas. Tam stopped dead. Bo grabbed his arm.

"What was that?"

"The wall," Ilsa said. She didn't slow down. "It talks at night."

We came around a shoulder of blue ice and there it was: the calving face, three hundred feet of glacier front glowing faintly green-white under a half moon, hanging over the frozen lagoon like the prow of some impossible ship. It looked solid. It looked eternal. And every few minutes it groaned, or cracked, or sighed—a sound like something enormous turning over in its sleep—and I understood why no one camped here willingly.

But the route ahead didn't open until the fourth tide, and behind us the storm had eaten our retreat, and this shelf of wind-scoured gravel at the base of the lateral moraine was the only flat ground for six miles. So we camped at the foot of the thing that could kill us fastest, because the mountain had left us no better room.

Ferro got the stove going in the lee of a boulder the size of a truck. Ilsa rigged the tent as a windbreak rather than shelter—nobody would sleep inside it anyway; you don't sleep in an enclosed space under a calving face, you sleep ready to run. We laid out pads and bags in a row against the moraine, backs to the rock, faces to the ice, and ate rehydrated stew out of shared pots while the glacier muttered overhead.

Nobody talked much at first. That was normal. But there was a different quality to the silence tonight—a looseness, almost. We'd lost the retreat option at the second tide. We were committed now, all of us, sealed into this route the way the Meridian was sealed into the ice. Maybe that's what did it. When there's no way back, people start telling the truth.

It was Bo who started it. Of course it was Bo.

"You know what's funny," he said, scraping his pot with his spoon. "I've been fired from eleven jobs. Eleven. I've counted."

"Twelve," Ferro said. "You forget the fish plant."

"The fish plant doesn't count. Nobody counts the fish plant." Bo pointed his spoon at her. "Eleven real jobs. And you know the common thread? It's not that I'm careless. Everyone always says he's careless, he's reckless, he doesn't think. That's not it. I think too much. I see the load shift two seconds before it shifts, and I say so, and nobody wants to hear it, and then it shifts, and suddenly I'm the guy who jinxed it." He shrugged. "Turns out companies don't pay you to be right early. They pay you to be quiet."

Tam snorted. "That's not why they fired you from the Kestrel Line."

Bo's spoon stopped moving.

I watched him across the stove-light. Big man, gentle hands, a face that did its thinking in public whether it wanted to or not. He'd carried sixty-kilo loads up routes that made fleet guides turn back, and he'd never once asked any of us what we'd done to end up here, on a crew made entirely of people the legitimate world had spat out. None of us had asked each other. That was the deal, unspoken, from the first day. You didn't ask. Asking meant answering.

But the glacier was groaning, and the storm had closed behind us, and Bo said, quietly: "Go on, then. Tell them."

Tam looked into his pot for a long moment. The ice groaned again—longer this time, a rising note that faded into a crack somewhere high on the wall, and a puff of snow slid off the face and caught the moonlight like smoke.

"I dropped a client," Tam said.

The words landed flat and hard. Even Bo didn't move.

"Not killed. Not even badly hurt, in the end—she recovered, married her physiotherapist, sends me a Christmas card every year, which is somehow worse." His voice stayed level, the voice he used for rope lengths and anchor checks. "Roped glacier travel, crevasse field, standard stuff. I'd run the route forty times. Forty. And on the forty-first I let my attention go somewhere else for maybe four seconds—four seconds, I've reconstructed it a thousand times—and she stepped on a snow bridge that was tired, and it gave, and she went in to her waist before the rope caught." He turned his spoon over. "She was fine. The report still ended my career. Because the report wasn't about her. It was about me, and what four seconds means, and once someone writes *lapse of attention* in an official file, that word follows you like a smell."

He looked up, and for the first time since I'd known him, Tam looked tired—not physically tired, but tired underneath, the kind you can't stretch away.

"So that's mine. Four seconds. That's what I'm unemployable for." He almost smiled. "Cheapest mistake anyone ever made, and it cost everything."

The stove hissed. Somewhere above us, deep in the glacier, something shifted with a sound like a door closing in an empty house.

I realized I was holding my breath, waiting for someone to look at me. Waiting for the question I'd forbidden on day one: *What about you, Coral? Why are you here?* They knew the shape of it—blacklisted twice, the Meridian, the sister—but knowing the shape isn't the same as knowing the wound, and I'd built twenty years of walls around the difference.

Nobody asked. That was its own kind of mercy, and its own kind of loneliness.

Ferro spoke instead. She sat with her knees drawn up, medic's bag within reach the way it always was, even here, even now—she slept with one hand near it, I'd noticed, like other people sleep near a weapon.

"My reason's boring," she said. "I want to say that upfront. Compared to dropping clients and getting fired twelve times, mine's boring."

"It's not boring," Ilsa said.

Ferro shrugged. "I reported a captain."

The wind moved through the boulder field, finding the gaps, making low notes of them.

"Container ship, med run. I was ship's medic, junior, three years in. One of the deck crew came to me with tremors—hands, mostly, then arms. Heavy metal exposure, classic presentation, and I knew exactly where it was coming from, because I'd seen the manifest discrepancy myself: drums listed as lubricant that were neither drum-shaped nor lubricant-colored, if you follow me. I reported it up. Then up further. Then, when 'up' stopped answering, out—to the port authority, the maritime board, whoever would take the call." She turned her hands over, studied the palms. "The company settled with the crew quietly. Paid medical, paid hush money, most took it. I didn't, so they didn't have to settle with me—they just made sure my certification lapsed at the worst possible moment, and by the time I'd fought the paperwork back into order, no shipping line would touch the medic who'd gone to the press. Which is funny, if you think about it. The one person on that ship who did her actual job correctly, and I'm the one who can't get work."

"That's not boring," Bo said. "That's the fish plant of injustices. Doesn't count as boring."

Despite everything, Ferro laughed—a short, surprised sound, like she'd forgotten the mechanism existed.

Ilsa went last. Ilsa, who charted routes in her head the way other people prayed, who had found us a smugglers' trail around two borders and a storm, who led on the narrow ways without ever once raising her voice. She was quiet so long I thought she'd pass.

"I was never fired," she finally said. "That's the problem. I quit. Publicly. Loudly. At a navigation conference, at a podium, with the regional survey director on the stage next to me." A ghost of a smile. "There was a ferry route. Winter crossing, well-documented ice hazard, and the survey office had signed off on it anyway because the operator donated to the right fund. I'd flagged it twice in writing. Both flags were removed from the final published charts—you can actually check, the drafts exist, I kept them. The ferry went down in November. Nine people." Her jaw worked. "Nine people, and the inquiry used my charts—the corrected ones, the ones they'd deleted—as evidence the hazard was *known*, and then the same office that deleted them offered me a promotion to keep quiet during the hearings. So I stood up at the conference and told four hundred navigators exactly what their charts were worth." She looked at the glacier. "They're still using my methods. They just don't use my name."

The fire popped. Above us the calving face caught a gust and shed a curtain of powder that drifted down the moonlight, silent, beautiful, indifferent.

Five people around a stove at the bottom of the world, each one carrying a version of the same story: they'd told the truth at the wrong volume, or too early, or to the wrong room, and the world had answered by erasing them. I'd spent twenty years thinking my exile was singular, particular, mine alone—a punishment fitted to my crime. Sitting there, listening, I felt the shape of it change. Not a cell. A club nobody joins on purpose.

And they hadn't asked me. So I did the thing I'd sworn off doing, the thing Voss's letter had been knocking at for weeks.

"Maren wanted to name her boat Yara," I said.

They all went still. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"She was my sister. Younger by two years, older by a mile—she was the one who should've been giving the safety briefings, running the routes, keeping me alive. Instead she signed onto the Meridian as crew, and I—" My throat closed. I made myself keep going, because these four had handed me their worst rooms tonight, and leaving mine locked would have been theft. "I was supposed to be on that ship. I gave up my berth three days out, over a fight. A stupid fight, the kind you have with family because family is the only place you can afford to be stupid. And the ship went down in the pass with all hands—or that's the story. Two survivors walked off the mountain, and both of us got blacklisted, and the official report says crew error, and for twenty years I have believed, down in the place you don't dig, that if I'd been aboard, I'd have caught whatever she missed. Or died trying. Either one would've been easier than this."

The glacier groaned—a long, grinding exhalation, the whole face settling a few millimeters, snow smoking off it in sheets. Nobody flinched. We were past flinching.

"So that's why I measure everything in miles," I finished. "Miles you can cover. Years just pile up."

Silence. The good kind, the kind with weight and warmth in it.

Then Bo said, gruffly, "For what it's worth, I don't think you'd have caught anything. No offense. I've met you. You'd have caused a second disaster arguing with the captain."

Laughter went around the circle—real laughter, ragged and disbelieving, the kind that only comes after truth. Even I felt it crack something loose in my chest. Tam clapped my shoulder once, hard, saying nothing, which from Tam was a eulogy and a blessing both.

We banked the stove and settled into our bags in a row, backs to the moraine, faces to the ice. Sleep came slowly. The glacier talked all night—groans, cracks, that deep hull-flexing moan—and sometime in the small hours I stopped hearing it as threat and started hearing it as speech, a language I'd been refusing to learn for twenty years, saying something patient and enormous just below the threshold of understanding.

I was nearly under when I heard boots on gravel. Quiet, deliberate. I opened my eyes to a shape crouched at the edge of the fire-light: Ilsa, sitting up, holding something in both hands, looking at it the way you look at a map of a place you thought didn't exist.

She must have felt me watching. She turned, and in the dim glow of the banked stove I saw what she held: a small book, water-stained, its cover stiff with age.

"Coral," she said softly. "This fell out of your pack when I was checking the straps. I didn't read it. I swear I didn't read past the flyleaf." She held it out. "The flyleaf says *Property of Maren Delacroix.*"

The glacier groaned above us, long and low, like something clearing its throat before it speaks.

I reached out and took my sister's journal in my hands, and for the first time in twenty years, I didn't put it away.
