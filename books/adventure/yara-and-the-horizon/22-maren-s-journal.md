## 22. Maren's Journal

The camp at the calving wall was the kind of place where you learned what your own heartbeat sounded like.

The glacier groaned all night—not the sharp cracks of shifting pack ice, not the boom of a serac letting go, but a long, low complaint, like a ship's hull working against a heavy swell. Like something enormous trying to remember how to move. We'd pitched the tents in the lee of a granite ridge two hundred meters back from the ice face, which Ilsa called "close enough to be efficient and far enough to be alive," and which Ferro called "the worst of both worlds" and then laughed at his own joke until nobody joined him and he stopped.

We'd earned the rest. Fourth Tide was behind us, the narrow traverse survived, and the fleet was a full tide behind—Voss's lead ship a smear of smoke on the southern horizon when Tam took the last watch of the afternoon. One tide of advantage. Maybe two, if the weather held. It wouldn't. It never did.

I was repacking the rope locker when I heard Ilsa say my name in the voice she used for things that mattered.

"Coral."

She was kneeling by my kit—the big external-frame pack I'd carried for twenty years through eleven ranges and two blacklists, the pack that had been searched by border guards on three continents and never once turned up anything worth confiscating. She was holding a book.

Not a book. A journal. Oilcloth cover, brass corners gone green with age, a leather strap wrapped twice around it and knotted in a bow that hadn't been touched in two decades. The knot was mine. I'd tied it the week after the funerals, in a rented room in a port town whose name I'd made a point of forgetting, and I had never untied it.

"I wasn't looking for it," Ilsa said. "The side pocket tore. The stitching finally went—I can fix it tonight, but it opened and this fell out." She turned the journal over in her hands like it might go off. "I didn't read it. The strap was still tied. I figured a tied strap means a closed door."

She held it out to me.

I didn't take it.

For a moment we just stood there in the blue light off the ice, the glacier groaning its long complaint behind us, and I watched her realize what she was holding. Ilsa was the best navigator I'd ever worked with and a worse liar than Tam, and her face did exactly what her face always did: it showed the arithmetic. The journal. The sister. The wreck we were walking toward. The knot.

"This is hers," she said. Not a question.

"Maren's."

"You've carried it twenty years."

"Twenty-one in March."

"And you never—"

"Opened it past the first page." I heard my own voice come out flat, the guide's voice, the one I used for clients on bad days and for myself on worse ones. "I read the first page the night I got it back from her effects. Then I tied the strap."

Ilsa looked down at the journal again, and I watched her make the decision I would have made in her place, which was to say the true thing even when it wasn't welcome. She'd told me once that navigation was mostly the discipline of admitting where you actually were instead of where you'd planned to be. She applied it to everything. It was infuriating. It was probably why I kept her.

"Coral. We're two tides from the hold. Tomorrow night, maybe the night after, you're going to be standing inside that ship. You're going to be standing in the corridor where she—" She stopped, recalculated. "You're going to be standing in the place you've spent twenty-one years not standing. And you're going to do it carrying a book you can't open."

"The book isn't the problem."

"No," she agreed. "The book is the symptom."

I took it from her then, mostly to end the conversation, and the weight of it was exactly the weight I remembered, which is to say it weighed nothing at all and everything at once. Two hundred pages of paper that had crossed an ocean and survived a fire and an avalanche and a man's whole ruined adulthood, and it weighed less than a carabiner.

"Thank you for not reading it," I said.

"I wanted to." She shrugged. "I'm not going to pretend I didn't. But some doors you knock on first." She picked up her sewing kit and the torn pack and left me alone with it, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for me in years.

* * *

The tents went quiet in the way they do when everyone is pretending to sleep. Tam snored with theatrical commitment, a performance he'd maintained for so long it had become genuine. Bo's breathing was still wrong—shallower than it should have been, six days after the crevasse, the ribs Ferro had strapped still complaining—and every time his breath caught I found myself counting, the way I'd been counting since we hauled him out, as if the arithmetic of his survival were something I could keep balanced by attention alone.

I sat outside my tent with my back against the granite and the journal in my lap and the stove hissing beside me, because Ferro had left it burning low on purpose, and we both knew it, and neither of us said so.

The glacier groaned. Somewhere up-slope, ice let go of ice with a sound like a door opening very slowly in an empty house.

I untied the knot.

It's strange, the things your hands remember. The knot came apart the way it had gone together, two turns and a draw, my fingers finding the sequence without consulting me, as if the part of me that tied it had been waiting all this time for the part of me that would untie it to catch up. The strap fell away. The cover opened.

The first page. I knew the first page. I could have recited it.

*Day one. The Meridian rides high and mean and the chief says we're overloaded but the manifest says we're not, and the manifest is the company's and the chief is mine, and Maren Delacroix knows which one to believe.*

Her handwriting. I'd forgotten how slanted it was, how she pressed hard enough to emboss the next page, how every *y* descended like she was driving a piton into the paper. She wrote the way she climbed—committed, over-gripped, furious at anything that suggested caution. Our mother used to say Maren had been born with the brakes off. Our mother said it fondly. Maren took it as a job description.

I read the first page again. Then I turned to the second.

And stopped.

Not because I couldn't go on. Because going on felt like a door I could only walk through once, and I wanted to walk through it awake, and I was—by my own honest accounting—not awake. I was a man sitting in the dark under a calving glacier with twenty-one years of carefully maintained ignorance between him and the truth, and the ignorance had been load-bearing. I had built my whole exile on top of it. The shame, the blacklist, the miles I measured instead of years—all of it had been erected on a foundation I'd never inspected, because I'd been afraid of what the inspection would find.

The official report said the Meridian's cargo shifted in heavy seas. The official report said the crew was negligent. The official report said, in the careful passive voice that institutions use to avoid saying *someone specific*, that the Delacroix siblings' charter had been improperly ballasted, and that the surviving brother's account of events could not be corroborated, and that his license was therefore suspended pending review, and that the review had concluded, and that the conclusion was exile.

I had spent two decades assuming the report was right about everything except the part where it ruined me. I had assumed Maren had trusted the manifest. I had assumed the overload was our fault, my fault, the fault of a family that cut corners because the inheritance was slipping away and we were young and greedy and in a hurry. I had assumed the fight we'd had the night before she sailed—about the cargo, about the route, about whether she should take the Meridian's contract at all—had been me being right and her being reckless, and that her recklessness had killed her and eleven others, and that my being right had been worth exactly nothing, because I'd stayed on the dock.

But the first page said *the manifest is the company's*.

The first page said she already knew.

I closed the journal. I pressed my palms against the cover until the brass corners bit into my skin, and I sat there under the indifferent stars and let myself feel the thing I'd been outrunning since I was twenty-six years old, which was not grief. Grief I could carry; grief had a shape and a weight and I'd gotten strong enough to haul it. What I felt now was the vertigo of a man discovering the map he'd been navigating by was drawn wrong—not the territory, the map. Twenty-one years of miles, all plotted from a false origin.

"Coral."

Ferro. He'd come out of his tent without a sound, the way he did, a big man who moved like a medic, which is to say like someone who had learned that noise costs lives. He sat down across the stove from me without being invited and without apology. He looked at the journal in my hands.

"That's the sister's," he said. Not a question either. This crew had stopped asking questions about Maren somewhere around the crevasse field, when I'd gone down after Bo and come up with blood on my hands and her name in my mouth, and they'd understood, in the way good crews understand, that some cargoes you don't ask the manifest about.

"Yeah."

"You going to read it?"

"I don't know."

Ferro nodded slowly, and fed a stick into the stove, and was quiet for a while. When he spoke again, he was looking at the glacier, not at me, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"I had a patient once," he said. "Back before I was unemployable. Woman with a growth in her belly, and she knew, and I knew, and for four months we both pretended the tests were ambiguous. She wasn't afraid of dying. She was afraid of knowing the date." He poked the stick down into the coals. "The day she let me tell her straight, she slept twelve hours. First real sleep in months. Woke up and said, *huh, that's the number, fine, now I know what I'm working with.*" He glanced at me. "Diagnosis isn't the wound, Coral. Diagnosis is where the treatment starts."

"You're saying I'm sick."

"I'm saying you've been carrying an undiagnosed thing for twenty-one years and it's metastasized into everything. The exile. The rules you make. The way you flinch when anyone says her name." He stood, brushing frost off his knees. "I'm not telling you to read it tonight. I'm telling you that you can't board that ship still pretending the tests are ambiguous. Voss knows what's in that hold. If he knows and you don't, he owns the conversation. And men like Voss—" he almost smiled, "—shouldn't own the conversation."

He went back to his tent. The glacier groaned. The stove ticked as it cooled.

I sat with the journal until the cold drove me inside, and I didn't read past the first page, and I hated myself a little for that, and I noticed the hating and set it down beside the rest of the luggage, because Ilsa had taught me that too: admit where you actually are, not where you planned to be.

Where I actually was: a man two tides from the truth, holding the truth in his hands, unable to turn a page.

Where I'd planned to be: anywhere but here.

* * *

I woke before grey light to the sound of the glacier clearing its throat—a deep, resonant boom that rolled through the granite and up through my bedroll and into my teeth, and lay there listening to the echoes sort themselves out, and thought: *soon*. The calving season was opening ahead of schedule. Ilsa would confirm it at first light with her instruments and her terrible calm, but I already knew. The mountain was done waiting, and it had never cared about our schedule, and the six-tide window was going to close whether or not the man who'd needed it most had finished being afraid.

I got up. I lit the stove. I made coffee the way Maren had taught me—strong enough to stand a spoon in, sweet enough to forgive it—and I took the journal out of my kit and set it on my knees and untied nothing, because the strap was already off, had been off since last night, and there was no knot left to hide behind.

The second page. Her handwriting, pressing hard, embossing the page beneath:

*The chief showed me the real manifest today. Not the company's—the real one. What they've got us carrying in the lower hold isn't on any list, and it isn't packed right, and when I asked the super about it he smiled at me the way men smile when they think you're too young or too female or too both to matter. Coral was right. I hate that he was right. I'm not telling him. He'd pull us off the contract and we'd lose the inheritance and the company would just find another crew stupid or hungry enough to say yes. At least this way I'm aboard. At least this way I can watch the hold myself.*

I read it twice. Three times. The coffee went cold in my hands and I didn't notice.

*At least this way I'm aboard.*

She'd known. From day one—day one—she had known the cargo was wrong, and she had stayed aboard anyway, and she had not told me, and every assumption I had built my exile on turned over like pack ice under a boot.

I turned to page three. My hands were not shaking. My hands were perfectly, unnaturally steady, the way they go steady in an emergency, and I understood, sitting in the stove-light with the glacier groaning its long complaint outside, that this was an emergency, that some part of me had declared it so, and that the steady hands were the hands of a man crossing a threshold he could not cross back over.

The third page began: *If anything happens, and I don't think it will, but if it does—*

Bo's voice came through the tent wall, hoarse with sleep and pain: "Coral? Why's the stove lit?"

And behind him, Ilsa, already awake, already reading the light: "Because he's been up all night."

I looked down at the journal open on my knees, at my sister's handwriting marching across the page like a woman driving pitons into the dark, and I heard myself say, in a voice I didn't recognize as mine:

"Get the others. It's time you all heard what she wrote."
