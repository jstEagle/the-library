## 23. Reading by Stove-Light

The stove ticked as it cooled, a small metal heartbeat in the dark of the tent. Outside, the calving wall groaned its long slow sentences—*crack, settle, crack*—and the glacier spoke to itself the way old men speak to empty rooms.

Coral sat with Maren's journal on his knees and did not open it.

He had carried it for twenty years. He knew its weight the way he knew the weight of his own hands: four hundred and twelve grams, cloth-bound, water-swollen at one corner where it had been fished from a lifeboat locker three days after the Meridian went down. He had carried it through two countries that wouldn't have him and eleven countries that didn't care. He had carried it up passes with no names and down rivers with too many. He had never read past the first page.

The first page said: *Day one. The sea is a rumor the mountains tell about themselves.* Her handwriting, slanted left, ink already smudged because she'd written it standing up against a rail. He had read that line so many times he could recite it in her voice, and her voice was the thing he could least afford to hear.

Ilsa had put it back in his pack two nights ago without a word. Just set it there, spine toward him, like returning a knife you'd found edge-up in someone else's gear. She'd known what it was. Of course she'd known. Twenty years of watching him check that one pocket before every camp, every crossing, every border. You didn't need to be a navigator to chart a man's one fixed point.

Now the crew slept around him—Tam snoring like a landslide in rehearsal, Bo breathing shallow but even, Ferro flat on his back with his arm over his eyes, Ilsa curled toward the tent wall the way she always curled, as if saving space for someone who wasn't coming. The stove light was down to coals, orange and patient.

Coral turned the journal over. Turned it back.

"You're going to do it or you're not," he said quietly, to himself, to her, to the glacier. "One or the other."

The mountain didn't answer. It never had. That was why he'd loved it.

He opened past page one.

* * *

Her entries came fast at first, the way young people write when everything is new and they haven't learned yet that new things end. Day three: the smell of the cargo hold. Day five: a card game with the second engineer where she'd cheated and gotten away with it. Day eight: *Coral would hate this ship. Too much metal, not enough sky. I keep telling him the horizon is just a mountain lying down, and he keeps telling me the sea is just a valley that gave up.*

He laughed. It came out of him like something breaking—a short, ugly sound—and Ferro stirred and then didn't wake. Coral pressed his knuckles to his mouth until the laugh finished dying.

She'd been twenty-three. He'd been nineteen, and he'd stayed behind to take the guiding certification because somebody in the family had to earn money that didn't come off saltwater, and she'd teased him about it for a year. *You'll die on a mountain, Cor. At least I'll die somewhere flat.* 

That was the joke. That was the last joke between them, and it had come true inside out, because the sea had climbed the mountain—the glacier had taken the wreck and kept it—and she'd died at altitude after all, frozen into a steel hull at two thousand meters, and he had spent twenty years believing he knew how.

He turned pages faster now. He told himself it was efficiency. It was cowardice wearing efficiency's coat, and he knew the difference, and he kept turning anyway, skimming the ordinary days—the weather, the food, a dolphin escort off the point, a letter home she never finished—to get to the days that weren't ordinary.

Day fourteen. The entry began mid-sentence, which meant she'd been interrupted writing it, which meant—

*I asked Vasquez what's in Hold Three and he looked at me like I'd asked what's in his mother's coffin. Which is an answer, if you know how to read people. Manifest says machine parts. Machine parts don't need their own ventilation schedule. Machine parts don't get loaded at night by a crew that isn't on the roster.*

Coral stopped.

He read it again. Then again, slower, the way you'd test ice—one more step, weight forward, listen.

*Machine parts don't get loaded at night by a crew that isn't on the roster.*

The stove ticked. The glacier groaned. Somewhere out in the dark, ice the size of a cathedral leaned a little further toward falling, and none of it mattered as much as a sentence a dead woman had written in blue ink twenty years ago.

Because Coral had read the official report. He'd read it forty times; he could have drawn it from memory. The Meridian, lost with all hands in the Kestrel Strait incident—no, not lost with all hands, that was the other lie, there were survivors, eleven of them, pulled from the water by a fishing boat that the report thanked in one line and forgot in every other. The cause: structural failure compounded by heavy weather. The cargo: agricultural equipment and machine parts. The inquiry: closed. The company: compensated, exonerated, still operating under a different name.

And the blacklist—that had come later, when Coral, nineteen years old and half-mad with grief, had stood up at the public hearing and said the words nobody wanted said: *the manifest is wrong, my sister checked the hold herself, ask the night loaders, ask them.* And the commission had looked at him with pity, and the company's lawyers had looked at him with something colder than pity, and six months later he couldn't get a guide's license in his own country, and a year after that the neighboring republic had followed suit, politely, with paperwork, the way you close a door you want to seem like it was always shut.

*Ask the night loaders.*

He had asked. Nobody would answer. So eventually he'd stopped asking, and stopping asking had curdled into something worse—he'd started believing maybe he'd imagined it, maybe grief had invented the whole thing, maybe his sister had died in an honest accident and he'd spent two decades dragging her name through hearings and bars and border towns for nothing.

Maren hadn't imagined it. Maren had written it down. In blue ink. On day fourteen.

His hands were shaking. He noticed it the way you notice weather—not immediately, but all at once, undeniable. He set the journal flat on his knees and pressed his palms against his thighs and breathed the way he taught clients to breathe at altitude: four counts in, seven counts out, the body lied to less when you made it count.

Then he kept reading.

* * *

Day fifteen. *Found the second manifest. Not hidden, exactly—filed wrong, which is how smart people hide things, because everyone searches for secrets and nobody checks filing errors. Hold Three: sealed drums, class 4.3. Unstable when wet. On a SHIP, Vasquez. They've loaded drums that eat water and give off poison gas on a ship whose job is being wet. I copied the numbers. If anything happens, the numbers are real even if I'm not.*

Class 4.3. Coral knew the classification the way he knew rope grades—it had haunted the margins of every document he'd ever filed, the technical footnote the official report had somehow omitted. Dangerous when wet. Water-reactive. The Meridian had gone down in heavy weather, taking green water across her decks for nine hours before she listed.

The report called it structural failure. Structural failure caused by what? By drums swelling and splitting in a flooded hold? By gas building behind a bulkhead? By cargo that was never supposed to be on any ship, loaded at night by men who weren't on the roster?

Or—his stomach turned over slowly, deliberately, like a stone shifting in a scree slope—by something done *about* the drums. Something done in a hurry, in the dark, by someone who understood exactly what class 4.3 meant.

He thought of chapter twenty-seven of this voyage, still ahead of him: the hold door welded shut. From the inside.

He hadn't let himself think about it yet. There hadn't been time; there was never time; the route kept moving. But the image had lodged in him like a splinter—*welded from the inside*—and now, sitting in coal-light with his sister's handwriting under his thumbs, the splinter moved.

Someone had sealed that hold while the ship was dying. Someone had stood in a corridor with water rising and put a weld bead across a door, sealing whatever was in Hold Three away from the rest of the ship. Sealing it away from the sea. Sealing it, maybe, away from the air.

And the official story said structural failure. And the official story said no one was to blame. And the official story had made sure that the only person who ever stood up in a hearing and said *the manifest is wrong* ended up unlicensed, unlisted, and invisible, measuring his life in miles because miles didn't ask questions.

*They've loaded drums that eat water and give off poison gas on a ship whose job is being wet.*

She'd been funny even when she was terrified. That was Maren. That was the whole of her, right there in one sentence.

* * *

Day sixteen was the passenger list.

He almost missed it. She'd tucked it between two mundane entries—weather, engine noise, a joke about the coffee—and it began without ceremony, the way the truest things usually do:

*Passenger list doesn't add up. Officially we carry six passengers. I can count. There are eleven berths occupied in B-deck and the purser's ledger shows eleven names, but only six are on the sailing manifest. Five extra souls, no tickets, no papers, boarded at the night port with the night crew. Vasquez won't talk. One of them—a woman, older, hands like a dockworker's—told me thank you for asking after the hold. Told me: "Some of us are cargo too."*

Coral read that line five times.

*Some of us are cargo too.*

Refugees. Or fugitives. Or witnesses—people the company needed moved quietly, people whose names could never appear on a manifest, people whose deaths, if the ship went down, would appear nowhere at all. Eleven survivors pulled from the water. Eleven extra berths. He did the arithmetic twice because the first time felt too large: the fishing boat had rescued eleven people, and the official record said the Meridian carried six passengers and a crew of thirty-one, and the inquiry had noted, in passing, that survivor accounts suggested "discrepancies in headcount attributable to confusion."

Discrepancies in headcount. Five human beings erased from existence by a comma.

His hands weren't shaking anymore. They had gone very still, the way they went still on bad ground, when movement was the enemy. He sat in the failing light of the stove and understood, with the calm that comes after long fear, that he had spent twenty years carrying the evidence of a crime and treating it like a wound. It wasn't a wound. It had never been a wound. It was a map. Maren had known she might not make it home, and she had done the one thing she could do: she'd written down the numbers, the names, the truth, and she'd sewn it into the one place guaranteed to survive—her brother's stubbornness.

*If anything happens, the numbers are real even if I'm not.*

"God, Mare," he whispered. "You knew."

The tent was silent except for breathing. Tam's snore had stopped; either he'd rolled over or he was listening. Coral didn't look up. Some things you couldn't do while watched, and this was one of them.

He turned to the last written page.

* * *

Day seventeen. The handwriting had changed—faster, the letters leaning harder, the pen pressing through the paper in places.

*Something's wrong with the ship. List to port since noon, and the pumps are running in a hold that isn't supposed to take water. Vasquez finally talked, or half-talked. Says the drums were breached two days ago, says the company knows, says we're making for the strait because it's shorter and the company wants us short. Says some of the night crew got paid to be quiet and some got paid to leave. I'm putting this in the locker with the emergency kit because lockers float. If you're reading this, Cor—I'm sorry about the fight. You were right about the money. You were right about everything. Take the mountains. I'll take the horizon and we'll meet in the middle.*

*Tell whoever finds this: it wasn't the weather. Check Hold Three. Count the passengers. Make them say our names.*

*Make them say our names.*

The page ended there. No day eighteen. No day nineteen. The journal simply stopped, the way she had simply stopped, somewhere in the dark of the Kestrel Strait with water coming through a hull the company had knowingly sailed broken.

Coral closed the cover with both hands, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

Then he sat there and let twenty years rearrange themselves.

It was not relief. Relief would come later, maybe, or maybe never; some debts the heart collects regardless of the facts. What it was, first, was vertigo—the ground-level shift of a man who has built his entire architecture of self on a false foundation feeling the foundation move beneath him. He had believed his sister's death was a mystery he'd failed to solve. Worse: in the black hours, the hours he'd never admitted to anyone, he had believed the whisper the company's lawyers had planted at the hearing and let grow wild—*the Delacroix girl was careless, the Delacroix family pushed, the brother protests because guilt needs a stage.* He had carried that whisper like a stone in his boot for two decades and called the limp a scar.

There had been no carelessness. There had been a fight about money—her wanting to send their share of the passage fee home, him insisting she keep it, both of them saying things that were really *I love you* in the ugliest available grammar—and then she'd boarded a death trap with her eyes open, copying manifests, counting berths, doing the math nobody else would do. She hadn't caused the wreck. She hadn't stumbled into it. She had walked toward it with a pencil in her hand because someone had to write the numbers down.

*Make them say our names.*

"They'll say your name," Coral said to the coals. His voice came out low and cracked and certain. "All of them. Every name in that book. I'll drag the whole fleet through the ice if that's what it takes."

Behind him, softly, Ilsa said, "Takes what?"

He hadn't heard her wake. He should have—twenty years of guiding and he'd missed a person sitting up three feet away—but his senses were all pointed inward, at the wreckage of his old map. She was sitting with her knees drawn up, hair loose, watching him with the particular patience of someone who had returned a journal unread and then waited exactly as long as it took.

"The Meridian," he said. "It wasn't the weather."

"I know."

He looked at her. "You *know*?"

"I charted the sinking coordinates against the storm logs two years ago, when Voss's first inquiry letter came through my old office." She shrugged, small, unapologetic. "The storm wasn't strong enough to do what the report claimed. I assumed corruption. Corruption's the default. But I didn't know there was proof." Her eyes went to the journal. "Until now, apparently, there was proof the whole time, riding in your pack."

"Page one was all I could—" He stopped. Started over. "I couldn't get past her voice."

Ilsa nodded like that was a reasonable answer, which, from her, it was. Then she reached over and fed two sticks into the stove, and the light came up between them, orange and honest, and she said the practical thing, because she was Ilsa and the practical thing was her love language:

"Fifth tide's in nine hours. The bridge holds maybe six. Whatever's in that hold—numbers, names, lists—we have to reach it before Voss does, and we have to carry it out ourselves, because paper burns and companies lie." She held his gaze. "So sleep, Coral. Two hours. Then we run."

He looked down at the journal in his lap—at the swollen corner, the blue ink, the last line pressed hard enough to read from the back of the page. *Make them say our names.*

For the first time in twenty years, the weight in his pack matched the weight in his chest, and both of them were light.

Outside, the calving wall boomed once, deep, like a door unlocking.

Coral lay back, closed his eyes, and slept without dreaming—which, for him, was itself a kind of miracle—and woke to Bo shaking his shoulder and the grey beginning of the fifth tide, and the last ice bridge waiting, thin as a promise, between them and the wreck.
