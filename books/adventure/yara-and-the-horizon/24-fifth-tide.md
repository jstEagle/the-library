## 24. Fifth Tide

Bo had never been afraid of heights. He'd told Tam that once, on a rig job in the southern ranges, hanging off a tower leg with nothing but air and a bad attitude below him, and Tam had laughed and said that wasn't courage, that was just a missing wire in his head. Bo had agreed. He figured the same missing wire applied to ice bridges.

Except this morning, standing at the edge of the last crossing between them and the far shore of the sound, Bo discovered the wire hadn't been missing after all. It had just been waiting for something worth being afraid of.

The bridge was maybe forty meters of glacier ice spanning a channel of black water that moved like it had somewhere to be. The tide was coming in—the fifth tide, Ilsa had said at dawn, her voice flat in the way it went flat when she was doing arithmetic she didn't like—and the water beneath the bridge was already climbing, already licking up into the old melt tunnels that riddled the crossing like wormholes in rotten wood. Sunlight came through the ice from above and from the sides, so the whole bridge glowed a deep, impossible blue-green, lit up like something alive. Like the belly of a fish. Like an eye.

"Beautiful," Ferro said behind him, and didn't sound like he meant it.

"It's a lie," Ilsa said. "It looks solid because light lies. It's honeycombed. We go fast, we go spread out, and nobody—*nobody*—stops on it. Not to look. Not to breathe. You feel it groan, you don't stop, you *run*." She looked at each of them in turn, ending on Coral. "You first or me?"

Coral was looking at the bridge the way he looked at everything now, Bo thought—like a man reading a letter he'd been afraid to open for twenty years. Since Maren's journal, since the entries about cargo manifests and passenger lists, he'd gone quiet in a new way. Not the old quiet, which had been armor. This one was closer to hunger.

"You lead," Coral said. "I'll take the rear."

* * *

They crossed in the grey-gold hour when the sun sat low enough to turn every shadow blue. Ilsa went first, rope paid out behind her, moving quick and light on her axes, testing each step with a single hard stamp before committing her weight. Then Tam, then Ferro with Bo's spare pack strapped over his own medical kit, then Bo, then Coral at the back with the coil of their retreat line, though there was no retreat anymore and everyone knew it. The storm two days ago had closed the route behind them like a door. There was only forward, and forward was this: forty meters of glowing, rotting ice above water cold enough to stop a heart in ninety seconds.

Bo counted his steps. It was a thing he did when the fear got loud—counted, because numbers were honest and fear was not. He got to eleven before the first crack came.

It wasn't loud. That was the worst part. It was a sound like a knuckle popping, somewhere under his left boot, intimate and small, and the ice under him shivered—not shook, *shivered*, like a horse flicking off a fly.

"Don't stop," Ilsa called, without turning around. Her voice carried that particular calm that Bo had learned meant she was terrified and had decided to be useful instead. "Keep your rhythm. It's settling, not failing."

"How do you know?" Tam asked.

"I don't. Keep moving."

Bo kept moving. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen. The glow under his feet brightened as the channel below narrowed and the water rose—he could see it now through a vein of clear ice, black water sliding past three feet beneath his boots, carrying slush and a chunk of brash ice the size of a table. Rising. The tide filling the space under the bridge, pressing up against the ice from below while the sun worked it from above, and the whole crossing was a sandwich being crushed between two appetites.

Nineteen. Twenty. Halfway.

That was when the far shore came into view properly for the first time—not the shore itself but what stood against the sky beyond it, down the coast, where the glacier bent around the headland. Smoke. Thin, dark threads of it, four or five of them, rising from the main channel where the salvage fleet had been grinding north for days.

"They're landing," Tam said. He'd stopped. Of course he'd stopped; Tam stopped when the world required commentary.

"TAM." Ilsa's voice cracked like a whip. "Move."

He moved. But Bo had seen it too, and now he couldn't unsee it: the fleet was here. Voss's ships had made the main channel, and they were putting men ashore on the same coast Ilsa was aiming for, and the six-tide window that had seemed like a fortune back in the lowlands suddenly felt like the cruel joke it probably always was. Five tides spent. One left. And the fleet was already pouring onto the ice like ants finding a crack in a jar.

Thirty-one. Thirty-two. The bridge groaned again, longer this time, a deep tonal note that Bo felt in his molars, and ahead of him Ferro stumbled, caught himself on his axe, and for one horrible second all Bo could see was the crevasse, six hours of hauling, Bo's own frostbitten hands on the rope, Ferro's voice saying *stay with me, stay with me* in the dark—

"Ferro." His voice came out higher than he wanted. "Ferro, you good?"

"Good," Ferro said, and started moving again, faster than before. "Hating this bridge. Moving."

Thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. The ice thinned toward the far side—Ilsa had warned them, the bridge feathered out into plates and slush—and Bo's boot went through to the ankle in a slush pocket, cold biting through his gaiter, and he yanked free and lunged and the plate behind him dropped half a foot into the channel with a slap like a door slamming.

Then gravel. Real gravel, moraine grit under his boots, and his legs nearly folded with the simple animal relief of ground that didn't shine.

Ilsa hauled him the last few meters by the pack strap. "Count anything good?" she asked. She was breathing hard, grinning, that wild-eyed grin she got when the mountain let her live.

"Forty-six steps," Bo said. "Bridge is garbage. Ten out of ten."

Coral came off the ice last, coiling the line without looking at it, his eyes already down the coast toward the smoke. He didn't grin. He stood there a moment with the wind pulling at him, and Bo watched him look at the wreck's direction the way you'd look at a house where someone you loved used to live.

Then he shouldered his pack. "Fleet's ashore," he said. "We've got one tide and maybe six hours of light. We run."

* * *

They ran.

Not the desperate flailing sprint of panic—Coral set a pace, that long metronome stride of his, and Bo matched it and found, to his own surprise, that his body was glad of it. This was the part Bo understood. This was the part where fear burned off like morning fog and left something cleaner underneath. He'd felt it on rig towers, on rock faces, in the two minutes after a load dropped and everyone realized they'd survived. The body saying: *you're alive, you're alive, use it.*

The coastline ran south toward the calving wall, a jumbled rampart of moraine and old pressure ridges, and the going was brutal—scree that rolled, ice patches hidden under fresh snow, ridges they had to chimney up with packs snagging—but Bo's blood was singing and his legs didn't care. Behind them, the smoke plumes grew. Once, cresting a ridge, he looked back and saw them plainly: figures on the ice, a string of them, roped in teams, moving along the fleet's route with the mechanical efficiency of men who were paid by the hour. A lot of men. More than Bo had guessed.

"How many you figure?" Tam asked, coming up beside him.

"Forty? Fifty?"

"And us."

"Us is better," Bo said, and meant it, and Tam barked a laugh that steamed in the cold.

They came down off the third ridge and the land opened, and there it was.

Bo had heard them talk about it for twenty years, had heard Coral say the name *Meridian* exactly twice in all the months Bo had crewed for him, both times like spitting. He'd built a picture in his head: a rusting hulk, broken-backed, the usual grave of a ship. What lay ahead of them in the long bowl between the moraines was nothing like that.

The glacier had released her slowly, grudgingly, over years—and what it gave back was whole. The Meridian lay canted on her port side in a valley of dirty ice, masts snapped but standing, hull lines clean and dark, the paint gone but the shape of her intact, preserved the way the cold preserves everything: perfectly, terribly, like a face under glass. Ice sheathed her to the rail. The evening light came down the bowl sideways and caught the frozen spray along her flank, and for a moment the whole wreck glittered like it was lit from inside.

Nobody spoke. Even the wind seemed to hold off.

"There she is," Ferro said finally, softly, the way you'd speak in a church.

"The hold's amidships," Coral said. His voice was steady but Bo could hear the effort in it, the way a rope goes taut. "Starboard side, below the waterline when she sank. If the ice has opened her anywhere, it'll be along the starboard fissure—we saw it from the pass." He pulled out his glass, swept the bowl, and stopped. Bo watched his shoulders change.

"What," Tam said.

"Smoke." Coral passed the glass. "Base of the headland. They're closer than I thought."

Bo took the glass when Tam handed it back. Found the smoke. Found, below it, movement—tiny at this distance, but unmistakable, a team of climbers traversing the headland's base on a line that converged, with horrible mathematical certainty, on the bowl. On the ship.

"An hour," Ilsa said, doing her flat arithmetic again. "Maybe less. They've got more ropes, more bodies, and no reason to be careful."

"We don't need careful," Tam said. "We need fast."

"No," Coral said. Everyone turned. He was still looking at the ship, and when he spoke again his voice had changed—quieter, and underneath the quiet something Bo had never heard in it before, something that sounded almost like hope wearing grief's old coat. "We need *first*. There's a difference. Fast gets us there sloppy. First means we're inside that hold, we have what's in it, and we understand it before Voss puts his hands on it. My sister didn't seal that hold so a salvage consortium could strip it for scrap value." He lowered the glass. "We go now. Straight down the bowl. Bo—you're lightest and you climb like a spider. You're on point with me."

Bo's heart slammed once, hard, against his ribs. Point. With Coral. Twenty years of exile and silence and the man was handing him the sharp end of the rope.

"Boss," Bo said, and couldn't get anything else out, so he grinned instead, big and stupid, and Coral—Coral almost smiled back. Almost. It was there and gone like a fish under ice, but Bo saw it, and he'd remember it.

* * *

The descent into the bowl was faster than it should have been and slower than Bo wanted, which was the story of his whole life. The moraine fought them, and then the ice shelf around the wreck fought them harder—pressure ridges thrown up like frozen waves, blue ice polished to glass by a century of wind. Bo went first with Coral a body-length behind, calling holds, and the ship grew and grew until it stopped being a picture and became a thing, a presence, a wall of dark iron and ancient ice that radiated cold like a stove radiated heat.

Up close, the Meridian smelled strange. Bo hadn't expected that. Old iron and sea salt and something sweeter underneath, organic, like the inside of an old wooden drawer. Time itself had a smell, apparently, and it lived here.

"Starboard fissure," Coral said. "There."

It opened in the ice sheathing the hull like a wound—a vertical seam, two meters wide, running down from the rail toward the waterline, its edges melted smooth and refrozen in curtains. Through it, dimly, the hull plating of the ship herself, and a darkness beyond that might have been a gap in the plating or might have been nothing.

Bo reached the seam first and put his hand on the ice curtain, and that was when he heard it.

Not from the ship. From behind them. Across the bowl, off the headland's shoulder—a sound he knew from rigging camps and salvage docks, small and human and completely wrong for this place: the rasp of a flare gun, and then a red star climbing the grey sky and bursting, hanging there, staining the whole bowl the color of an open wound.

Below the flare, at the mouth of the bowl, the first of Voss's teams came over the ridge.

They weren't an hour away. They were ten minutes.

"Coral," Bo said.

"I see it." Coral was already at the seam, axe drawn back, and his face in the flare-light was the calmest Bo had ever seen it, which scared Bo more than the fleet did. "Change of plan. Nobody's first anymore." He swung, and the ice curtain shattered, and cold air breathed out of the Meridian's hull—air that had been sealed in that dark for twenty years, air that had touched the last living moments of a woman Bo had never met and whose journal was riding in Coral's pack against his spine.

"Now," Coral said, and went in.
