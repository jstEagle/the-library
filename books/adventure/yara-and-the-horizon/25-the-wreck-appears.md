## 25. The Wreck Appears

The fifth tide had barely begun to fall when Ilsa stopped walking and said, "Coral."

Not his name the way she usually said it—flat, functional, a tool like any other in her kit. She said it the way people say a word they've only read about and never spoken aloud. He turned, following her gaze past Tam's shoulder, past Bo limping on Ferro's arm, past the white road of ice stretching ahead of them toward the calving wall.

And there it was.

The Meridian.

She lay on her side in a trench the glacier had carved open over twenty years, as if the ice had been slowly turning a page and had finally reached the one with her name on it. Three hundred feet of steel hull, black with age but whole—whole in a way that made Coral's stomach drop, because wrecks were supposed to be broken. Wrecks were supposed to be forgiven by time. This one hadn't been. The glacier had kept her. Preserved her like a specimen pinned under glass, every rivet and rail and rust-streaked plate exactly where it had been the night the sea came for her.

"There she is," Tam said quietly. "God. There she actually is."

Nobody moved. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath around the trench, funneling down into it and coming back changed, carrying a smell Coral hadn't smelled in two decades—old iron, old salt, the particular cold sweetness of things that died wet and froze before they could rot.

He had imagined this moment ten thousand times. In prison cells and border camps and cheap rooms above harbors all over two hemispheres, he had built it and rebuilt it: himself standing before the wreck, and the wreck meaning something. Justice, maybe. Or absolution. Or at least an ending, some clean line drawn under twenty years of running.

He had never imagined she would look like this. Not ruined. *Kept.*

"Distance to the hull?" he heard himself ask. His voice came out level. Twenty years of practice at level.

"Four hundred meters," Ilsa said. "Maybe less. The trench floor is stable ice—we can walk right up to her." She paused. "Coral. Are you—"

"Set camp here," he said. "We move on her at slack water. Ferro, Bo gets off his feet. Tam, rig lines. Ilsa—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Ilsa, chart us a path down. I'll be along in a minute."

They went. They were good crew; they knew what a minute meant when a man asked for it. Their footsteps faded behind him, and then it was just Coral and the ship and the wind, and twenty years collapsed into the space between one breath and the next.

* * *

He walked toward her alone.

The ice underfoot was ancient blue, shot through with bubbles frozen mid-rise, and it creaked beneath his boots with a sound like an old floor in an empty house. With every step the Meridian grew. Her name was still visible on the stern, half-eaten by rust weeping down from the letters like dark tears: MERIDIAN. Below it, smaller, the port of registry he'd once known by heart. Above it, the row of portholes along B deck—

He stopped.

B deck. Fourth porthole from the stern.

His legs simply quit. Not fear, exactly. Something older than fear—the body remembering what the mind had spent twenty years refusing to learn. Because he knew that porthole. He had stood on the dock at Yara Point the morning she sailed and pressed his palm flat against the glass while Maren laughed at him from inside, fogging the pane with her breath, drawing a smiley face in the condensation with one finger. *You're such a worrier, Cor. It's a coastal run. Six days. I'll bring you back a shell.*

Fourth porthole from the stern. He was looking at the window of the room where his sister had drowned.

The grief hit him physically, the way it always did—not sadness but impact, a blow to the chest that bent him forward and stole the air out of him. He put a hand against the ice to stay upright. The cold bit through his glove and he let it, because pain was simple, pain was honest, pain was at least *his* after all these years of having everything else taken.

*It wasn't your fault,* people had told him, over and over, for years, until the words wore smooth as river stones and meant nothing. The inquiry had blamed him anyway. The company had blamed him conveniently. Two governments had banned him for a crime no one could even properly describe—a guide who signed off on a manifest, a brother who waved from a dock. And so he had carried it, because someone had to, because guilt was at least a shape you could hold, and a man needs a shape or he comes apart.

But now he stood forty meters from the shape itself, and it looked back at him with a hundred dark windows, and it did not accuse him. That was the terrible thing. It didn't accuse him at all. It just *was*—rust and rivets and silence—and all his guilt suddenly had nowhere to stand.

"You're smaller than I remember," he said aloud. His voice cracked the stillness and he flinched at it. "Or I got bigger."

The ship said nothing. Of course she didn't. Ships don't answer. They just keep what they keep.

He walked closer. Close enough now to see details that made his hands shake inside his gloves: the mooring cleat where the last lifeboat line had been cut—he knew the story, everyone knew the story, though nobody knew it true. The scorch shadow above the cargo hatch, which the official report called a boiler flare-up and which Coral had always privately thought looked wrong, too low, too localized, like something that had burned *inside*. The sealed hold door on the starboard side, welded shut—welded shut, the survey teams had found, from within, though no inquiry had ever satisfactorily explained how or why, and the question had been buried along with everything else.

Welded from the inside. He stared at it now across the ice, that blunt dark seam of metal, and felt the first cold thread of something that wasn't grief.

Maren had been assigned to engineering auxiliary. Lower decks. Near the hold.

*Stop,* he told himself. *Not yet. One thing at a time.*

But the thoughts wouldn't stop. They came the way water comes through a cracked dam—first a thread, then a singing pressure, then the whole wall giving way. Twenty years of small wrongnesses he'd swallowed because swallowing was easier than drowning: the manifest discrepancies he'd been punished for noticing. The company's indecent haste blaming the crew. The way Voss's letter had said *I know what's in the hold* — not *what was*, not treasure-language, but present tense, certain, specific. The way Voss had written *your sister's name* in a letter that was supposedly about salvage rights.

Sable Voss didn't want gold. Gold doesn't need a Delacroix.

He sank down onto the ice, right there, forty meters from the hull, and sat with his knees drawn up like a boy, and for a long time he let himself do the thing he had forbidden himself for two decades: he remembered her fully. Not the funeral portrait version, not the guilt-shaped hole, but Maren—Maren arguing with the ticket clerk about the exchange rate, Maren teaching him to whistle through a blade of grass, Maren at sixteen standing on the roof of their father's boathouse with her arms spread wide facing the open water and saying, *When I'm on the sea I can feel the whole world turning, Cor. Doesn't that ever happen to you?*

"No," he said to the ice. "It never happened to me. That was always the difference between us."

The wind moved through the trench and the ship groaned—a long, deep, structural sound, steel settling in its cradle of ice, or maybe just answering. He chose to believe it was answering.

"I'm coming aboard," he told her. "I'm going to find out what they did to you. What they did to her. And then I'm getting everyone out alive, and I'm telling the truth if it kills me, and it might, because the fleet's a day behind us and Voss is Voss." He laughed, a short broken sound with nothing in it. "Twenty years I practiced what I'd say to you. That's what I've got. Sorry, Mare. You always said I was better with maps than words."

From up the slope, faint, came Ilsa's whistle—two notes, the signal for *slack water approaching, movement order.* The tide was turning. The window was doing what windows do.

Coral stood. His knees protested; his eyes burned in the cold. He took one last look at the fourth porthole from the stern, and he did the hardest thing he had done since the dock at Yara Point.

He turned his back on his grief—not abandoning it, not anymore, but carrying it now the way you carry a compass: pointed forward instead of down.

Then he climbed toward the light and his crew, and behind him the Meridian waited in her trench of blue ice, patient as only the dead are patient, holding her sealed door and her scorched hatch and whatever truth Maren Delacroix had welded shut with her own hands on the last night of her life.

Slack water would last ninety minutes.

The hold was waiting.

So, he suspected, was Voss.
