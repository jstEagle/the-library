## 26. Boarding Party

The Meridian came out of the ice the way a drowned man comes out of water—wrongly, unwillingly, all at once.

She lay on her port side in a bowl of blue shadow where the glacier had spent two decades carving around her and then, in one indifferent summer, decided to give her back. Her hull was black with old iron and new frost. Her name still ran along the bow in flaking gilt: MERIDIAN, and beneath it the homeport that had legally ceased to exist after the disaster, repainted by committee. Coral had seen photographs. He had seen the official diagrams, the inquiry sketches, the grainy salvage-survey images Voss's people had bought and sold for twenty years.

None of it was this. None of it was the size of her, or the silence coming off her like cold off stone.

"Say something," Ilsa said quietly.

He realized he'd been staring without speaking for the better part of a minute. "I'm deciding," he said, "whether I've wanted this my whole life or dreaded it my whole life."

"Can't it be both?"

"It can." He shouldered his pack. "That's the problem."

Tam crouched at the edge of the ice shelf above the wreck's exposed flank, testing the surface with an ice axe, and said nothing at all, which from Tam was its own kind of commentary. The three of them had drawn straws back at the calving wall—Ferro staying with Bo, whose leg Ferro had strapped and dosed and sworn about for an hour—and Coral had watched the straw come up in his own fist and felt, absurdly, like he'd cheated. As if he'd marked it somehow. As if some part of him had always known he'd be the one to go through that hull.

Below them, the fissure breathed.

It ran along the ship's starboard side where the glacier's retreat had cracked her open like a walnut—a seam of darkness eight feet high at its mouth, narrowing into the interior, its edges glazed with refrozen meltwater that caught the low sun and threw it back in long orange blades. Warm air, impossibly, moved out of it. The sea trapped beneath the ice shelf exhaled through the wound in the hull, and the breath smelled of brine and rust and something older, mineral, like the inside of the mountain itself.

"She's stable today," Tam said. "Tomorrow she might not be. Ice moves, steel doesn't."

"Ice wins," Coral said. "It always wins. Let's be quick."

They roped up—Coral on point, because nobody argued about that, not even Ilsa, who had led them across the narrow way on the fourth tide and whom he would follow anywhere now except here. Here he went first. Some doors you don't ask other people to open.

The descent to the fissure took twenty minutes of careful down-climbing on screws and slung axes. At the mouth of the crack, Coral stopped and put his gloved hand flat against the hull, and the metal was so cold it burned through two layers of fabric, and he held it there anyway.

Twenty years ago he had stood on a dock in another country and watched this ship's distress flare go up over the water, a red rose blooming and dying in the dark, and he had been nineteen years old and powerless and certain, with the certainty that only the young and the guilty can manage, that it was his fault. His plan. His route. His sister aboard because he had talked their inheritance into one last voyage—one last chance to make the family whole again before the creditors took everything.

The flare had gone out. The sea had kept going.

"Point?" Ilsa said behind him. Soft. Just checking.

"Just saying goodbye," he said, which wasn't true, and went in.

* * *

Inside, the light changed completely.

His headlamp cut a cone through air that was thick with suspended frost—millions of ice crystals hanging motionless, so that the beam looked solid, lookable, like a thing you could pick up and carry. The fissure widened after ten meters into a gallery between decks: the starboard corridor of what had been second-class accommodations, now tipped forty degrees and sheathed in ice, so that walking it was like walking through the inside of a frozen wave. Doors stood along the wall, warped open or swollen shut. Frost had grown over everything in feathers and ferns, white lacework on brass fittings, on a coat hook, on a child's boot that someone had left outside a cabin door twenty years ago and that no one would ever come back for.

Nobody spoke. There was nothing you could say in here that the room wouldn't make small.

Coral moved door to door with his lamp, and hated himself for it, and couldn't stop. Each cabin was a diorama. A berth with blankets frozen mid-tumble, as if sleepers had risen in a hurry. A washbasin with a cup beside it. On one bulkhead, pinned under a sheet of ice clear as glass, a photograph of a dog. He found himself cataloguing the passengers without meaning to—the inquiry files had made him memorize them once, in a courtroom corridor, in the years when he still believed knowing names was a form of penance. Second class, he thought. Families moving for work. People who had saved for these tickets.

"They got out," Tam said behind him, reading his silence correctly. "This deck evacuated clean. Eleven dead total, all forward and below. You know that."

"I know what the report says." He kept walking. "Reports are written by survivors."

Ilsa touched his shoulder once, lightly, and let go. They followed.

The corridor ended at a stairwell, and the stairwell dropped toward the heart of the ship, and here the ice grew stranger—thicker, bluer, layered in annual bands like tree rings, twenty winters stacked over the scene of the last hour. Their lamps pushed cones into blue depths. Somewhere below, water moved. They could hear it working against the hull, patient as erosion, the same sound that had been underneath every camp they'd made since the first tide: the ocean keeping its appointment.

"The hold is four decks down," Ilsa said, consulting the survey sheets Voss had included with the letter—the only part of that correspondence worth anything. "Forward of the engine spaces. The welded door is listed at frame ninety-two."

"You've memorized the frames?"

"I've memorized everything." A pause. "You don't have to do the arithmetic, Coral. Four decks down is where the eleven died. It's also where the answer is. Same place. It's always the same place."

He knew that. He had known it since the letter with the ice on it, since before. Grief and truth shared an address; that was the whole design of the thing, and whoever built the world had been crueler than any glacier.

"Then we go down," he said.

* * *

The engine spaces stopped him.

Not because of danger—though Tam called out a cracked stanchion and a ceiling of hanging ice that they gave a wide, respectful berth—but because of what the freezing had done to the moment of the sinking. Down here the ship had died fast, and the ice had preserved the dying. A wrench lay where a hand had dropped it. A ladder bore the prints of boots, pressed into frost that had never melted, climbing upward—dozens of prints, overlapping, the archaeology of panic and then, higher up near the escape hatch, of order: fewer prints, spaced evenly, disciplined. Someone down here, in the dark and the rising water, had organized the evacuation of the engine crew. Had counted them up a ladder. The eleventh survivor in the inquiry record was an oiler named Vasquez who had testified, wept, and been discredited in the same week.

Coral stood under the ladder for a long time, looking at those calm uppermost footprints, and felt something shift in his chest that had no business shifting yet—some load redistributing, some keel adjusting.

"Maren worked the engine decks," he heard himself say. It came out flat. "Cadet engineer. She wasn't supposed to be down here that night. She traded shifts."

Ilsa's lamp swung to him. "Why would she trade shifts?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He swallowed. "Everyone assumed she was where she was supposed to be. Everyone assumed—" He stopped. Twenty years of assuming, worn smooth as river stone. "Let's keep moving."

But his hands were shaking, and both of them saw, and both of them were kind enough to turn their lamps away.

They crossed the engine room along a catwalk furred with ice, past the great silent cylinders of the engines, which stood in their blue sheath like monuments in a crypt—which is exactly, Coral thought, what they were. This whole ship was a museum now. Not a ruin: a museum. Everything curated by cold. Every object labeled by its position, its angle, its distance from the stairs. And somewhere ahead, at frame ninety-two, was the exhibit the entire building had been raised around: a door welded shut from the inside, by hands that had run out of time, and the reason why.

He had spent twenty years walking through his worst memory in the dark, blind, guessing at the furniture. Now the lights were on. Now he could see everything, and the seeing was worse and better in equal measure, the way truth usually bills you.

* * *

Frame ninety-two announced itself before they reached it.

A smell—not decay; the cold permitted none of that—but iron. Old iron and brine and the faint chemical ghost of welding flux, preserved like perfume in a sealed bottle, and Coral stopped three frames short because his body knew the smell before his mind did and his body had opinions.

"There," Tam said, and his lamp found it.

The hold door was a slab of steel six feet wide, riveted at its edges, and it was not a door anymore. It was a seal. Welded shut from the inside—Voss's survey had confirmed it, and the inquiry had screamed about it for months, and the shipping company's lawyers had buried the question under structural reports until the question suffocated—but confirmation was nothing next to standing in front of it. The weld bead ran the perimeter in heavy, overlapping passes. Amateur work. Strong work. Work done by someone who understood metal well enough to know that a bad weld fails, and who had refused, with every pass of the torch, to fail.

And there, at chest height, low on the door where a kneeling person would reach, someone had scratched letters into the fresh weld before it cooled.

Coral stepped closer. His lamp trembled. Behind him, neither Ilsa nor Tam breathed.

Four words, gouged deep, in a handwriting he would have known anywhere—in the dark, in ash, in the bottom of the sea:

**HOLD FAST — M.D.**

M.D. Maren Delacroix.

The sound Coral made was not a word. Ilsa's hand found his arm and stayed there, and he let it stay, because his legs had become a rumor. Twenty years he had carried the guilt of a boy whose route, whose plan, whose ambition had put his sister on a dying ship—and the whole time, the whole twenty years, she had been down here in the dark doing the bravest thing anyone aboard had done, sealing herself in or sealing something in, and leaving him a message. Not a plea. Not a goodbye.

An instruction. *Hold fast.* She had known he'd come. However many years it took, however much ice—she had known her brother would eventually stand on the other side of this weld, and she had told him how to behave when he arrived.

"She welded it from the inside," Tam said quietly. "Which means she was already in the hold when she did it. Which means—" He stopped himself.

"Which means the door wasn't keeping water out," Coral said. His voice sounded like someone else's, scraped thin. "It was keeping something in. Or keeping something safe. Either way—" He put his bare hand against the weld, against the letters, cold enough to sear, and held it there the way he'd held it against the hull outside, paying a toll. "Either way she chose this door over the stairs."

The ship groaned. Long and low, through keel and frame, ice settling against steel, or steel remembering. Dust sifted from the seams overhead. In the lamplight, the scratched letters gleamed.

"We need cutting gear to get through this," Tam said. "Hours, maybe. The plasma rig's back at camp with Ferro and Bo."

"Then we go get it," Ilsa said—and then her head came up, sharp, like a deer's, and her lamp snapped off.

In the sudden dark, all three of them heard it at once: footsteps. Metal rang under boots somewhere aft—decks below or decks above, impossible to tell, the hull carrying sound the way water carries blood. More than one person. Moving with purpose. And threaded under the footsteps, faint but unmistakable, a voice on a radio, clipped and dry and familiar as a scar:

"—breach team to seaward entry. Voss confirms. Bring the lights. We're going to frame ninety-two."

Coral stood with his palm still flat against his sister's last words, twenty meters from the door she had died to close, and listened to the man who had sent him here come to take it away.

"Well," he said to the dark. "Now it's a race."
