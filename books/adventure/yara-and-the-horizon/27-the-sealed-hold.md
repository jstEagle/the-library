## 27. The Sealed Hold

Tam had rigged a hundred wrecks in his life and this was the first one that felt like walking into a church.

Not a good church. Not the kind with candles and singing. The kind his grandmother used to drag him to in Port Anselm, the cold stone kind where you lowered your voice without being told because something in the walls demanded it. The Meridian had been sealed in ice for twenty years, and the ice had done what curators do: it had kept everything. The corridor they moved through still held its furniture bolted down, its handrails frosted white, its emergency lamps hanging from the ceiling like dead fruit. Their headlamps cut through air so still it seemed offended by their presence.

"Watch your step," Coral said, though he didn't need to say it. He'd said it four times since the fissure.

Tam checked his anchor line for the fifth time in as many minutes and told himself the shaking in his hands was the cold. It wasn't entirely the cold.

They'd come through the fissure on the port side, a split in the hull wide enough for a body with a pack if the body was willing to exhale all the way through. Coral first, then Tam, then Ilsa. Ferro stayed topside with Bo, who was in no shape to climb and knew better than to argue about it. Bo had argued anyway, from flat on his back, until Ferro put a hand on his chest and said, *You want to be useful? Be alive when we get back.* That shut him up. Nothing else did.

The ship listed maybe twelve degrees to starboard, frozen mid-death. Everything inside was tilted toward the same corner of every room, as if the whole vessel were leaning in to whisper something.

"Distance to the hold?" Ilsa asked. Her voice came low through the comm bead at Tam's ear, though she was ten feet behind him. In here nobody wanted to speak at normal volume. The silence taxed loudness like a toll.

"Coral?"

Coral stood ahead of them at a junction where the corridor split, and he wasn't moving. His lamp beam rested on a door — a cabin door, ordinary, marked with a stenciled number that the frost had half-eaten. He just looked at it. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Long enough that Tam's skin started to crawl.

"That's her cabin," Ilsa said quietly. Not a question.

"Maren slept there." Coral's voice was level in the way a rope is level right before it takes the load. "Deck two, aft of the galley. She complained about the engine noise for the whole crossing out."

He turned away from the door and kept walking. Tam exchanged a glance with Ilsa. Neither of them said anything. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be a wound dressed up as comfort.

* * *

They followed the aft stairwell down one deck, then another, Coral navigating from a memory twenty years old and never once hesitating. That was the thing about Coral Delacroix that outsiders never understood, Tam thought. They saw the exile, the blacklist stamp, the man who took illegal clients through illegal passes because no legal work would have him. They saw a man running. But watch him move through a wreck he'd boarded exactly once, twenty years ago, as a teenager — watch him turn left at a junction he couldn't possibly remember clearly, duck under a pipe at exactly the right moment — and you understood he wasn't running from anything.

He was carrying it. Every mile of it. And a man who carries a thing that long learns its shape better than his own hands.

"The hold access is through cargo bay three," Coral said. "There are two doors. The main hatch faces forward, into bay two. The maintenance hatch is aft, smaller, through the pump room."

"Which one?" Ilsa asked.

"The small one. If the bulkheads held, the pump room will be dry-ish. Bay two flooded before she went down — I remember the water coming up the stairs." He paused at the bottom of the stairwell, lamp beam sweeping a landing crusted in ice like the ship had grown fur. "I remember it very well."

They went through the pump room sideways, single file, shoulders brushing pipes furred with hoarfrost. Tam went last, which meant he watched Coral's lamp disappear around the final bend and heard Ilsa's breath catch over the comm a half-second later.

Then he rounded the bend himself, and his breath caught too.

The maintenance hatch was there. Steel, round-cornered, the standard Delacroix Yards pattern — Tam knew the pattern, had installed knockoffs of it on three tramp freighters in his smuggling years. Wheel lock in the center, dogging levers at four points. A door built to keep water in or keep it out.

Except someone had made sure it would never open again.

"Welded," Ilsa said.

Tam played his lamp across the seam. The weld ran the full perimeter of the door, a fat, uneven bead of metal, laid in overlapping passes by somebody working fast and probably by feel — the beads sagged where the welder's hands must have been shaking, pooled thick at the corners, thinned to threads along the top where the angle got awkward. It was ugly work. It was desperate work. It was also, Tam realized with a slow cold feeling climbing his spine, thorough work. Whoever did it hadn't stopped until the seam was closed all the way around. No gaps. No quick job and run. They'd finished it.

"From the inside," Coral said.

His voice did something Tam had never heard it do. It didn't shake and it didn't go flat. It went *quiet* — quiet the way deep water is quiet, all the pressure underneath.

"You can't weld a hatch shut from outside and call it inside," Tam said. "The hinge side — look. The beads wrap around the barrel. Whoever ran those passes was standing on this side of the door."

He knew it the way he knew rope: by touch, by geometry, by twenty years of thinking about how things hold together and how they fail. You could fake a lot of salvage knowledge. You couldn't fake an understanding of seams.

"Someone was in the hold," Ilsa said slowly. "During the sinking. And they welded themselves in."

"Or welded everyone else out," Tam said.

Nobody answered that. The lamp beams sat on the ugly, beautiful, terrible weld like three people staring at a grave marker.

* * *

Here is what Tam Rook knew about the Meridian disaster, because everybody on the coast knew it, because it was the kind of story that got told in bars for twenty years until it wore smooth as sea glass:

The liner went down off the Verhagen coast in a storm that wasn't bad enough to explain it. Official inquiry blamed hull failure compounded by crew error, and named names, and one of the names belonged to a Delacroix — the daughter, Maren, junior officer, twenty-three years old, posthumously scapegoated in every bar from Anselm to the ice fields. Her brother had spent two decades banned from two countries for asking too loudly why the inquiry's math didn't add up. That was the story. That was the whole story. Everyone knew it the way everyone knows a song on the radio: without ever once listening to the words.

Tam had hired onto Coral's crew eighteen months ago, blacklisted himself — caught rigging loads he'd knowingly overloaded for a captain who threatened to report him either way. He'd never asked Coral about the Meridian. Nobody on the crew had. It was the rule Coral set and the rule they kept, and Tam had always assumed the rule existed because the wound was simple: his sister died, the world called her a killer, and he couldn't bear to hear it.

Standing in front of a hatch welded shut from the inside, Tam felt the shape of the rule change under him.

Simple wounds don't get welded doors.

"Hold up." Ilsa crouched by the wheel lock, chipping frost away from the plate around it with the edge of her knife. "There's markings here. Under the ice."

Coral knelt beside her. Tam did too, and pulled his multitool, and the three of them scraped at the steel in the lamplight like archaeologists on their knees, and what emerged made Tam's mouth go dry.

Stenciled letters, official yard paint, faded but legible:

**HOLD 3 — SEALED PER ORDER — NO ENTRY**
**MERIDIAN SHIPPING CO. — CARGO CLASS C-UNSTABLE**

And beneath that, scratched into the paint with something sharp — not stenciled, not official, human — a single line of handwriting preserved under the frost:

**IT WASN'T THE STORM. CHECK THE MANIFESTS. M.D.**

M.D.

Nobody said the name. Nobody had to. The letters hung in the frozen air between them, twenty-three years old and two days dead in ink, and Tam watched Coral read them, and watched him not move for a long, long time.

"She was here," Coral finally said. "After. She survived the flooding. She got to this door, and she—" His hand rose toward the scratch marks and stopped an inch short, hovering, like a man warming himself at a fire he doesn't trust. "She left me a message. Twenty years ago. She left a message and I spent twenty years on the wrong side of a border."

"We don't know it means—"

"Ilsa." Coral's voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture, and then he sealed it the way the door was sealed. "Check the manifests. That's what she wrote. So we're going to check the manifests."

* * *

Getting the hatch open took ninety minutes and every trick Tam owned.

The problem wasn't cutting the weld — Tam could cut through that bead with the plasma torch in his kit in twenty minutes flat. The problem was everything the weld implied. A sealed hold on a sunken, ice-imprisoned ship was, structurally speaking, a pressure vessel of unknown contents. If the hold had flooded and frozen solid, fine. If it hadn't — if somewhere in the last twenty years meltwater had found a way in and pooled against the far side of that door — then the moment Tam broke the seal he'd be opening a valve, and whatever was behind it would come through at whatever pressure twenty years of glacial hydrology had built up.

So they did it carefully. Tam drilled pilot holes at the four corners while Ilsa monitored with a pressure gauge improvised from Ferro's kit, and Coral stood back with his arms crossed and his jaw set, watching Tam work with an expression Tam couldn't read and decided not to try.

The first hole breathed out a long sigh of cold, dry air. Not water. Air that had been sitting in the dark for twenty years, tasting of rust and machine oil and something faintly sweet underneath, like old paper.

"No flood," Ilsa reported. "Dry hold. Or mostly."

The second and third holes confirmed it. On the fourth, the drill bit punched through into open space and the gauge needle settled at ambient, and Tam allowed himself one breath of relief before picking up the torch.

"Stand clear," he said. "This is going to be loud and it's going to be bright and neither of us should be proud of how we handle both."

He cut the weld in sections, working around the wheel lock last. The plasma hissed and spat orange sparks that died fast in the frozen air, and the smell of burning steel mixed with the sweet-paper smell of the hold's exhaled breath, and Tam thought, absurdly, of his grandmother's church again — the smell of candle wax, of old hymnals, of time itself going stale in a sealed room.

The last section of weld parted. The wheel, freed, resisted for one grinding second and then spun loose with a shriek of dry bearings that echoed down the corridors like something screaming. Tam dogged the levers back one by one, braced his boots, and pulled.

The hatch swung inward six inches and stopped, jammed on something. Cold light — grey, sourceless, glacier-filtered light leaking through some distant fissure — spilled out through the gap and fell across their boots.

Tam put his shoulder to the door and pushed. It ground against whatever blocked it, gave another inch, and stopped again.

"Something's against it," he said. "Barrel, crate, I don't know."

Coral stepped past him and put his eye to the gap. Tam watched his face in the reflected glow — watched the lamp-lit tension fall away from it and something else arrive, something older and more frightened than tension.

"There are crates stacked against the door," Coral said. "Deliberately. Stacked from the inside, wedged under the wheel. Someone barricaded this door after welding it." He was silent a moment. "They weren't keeping something in, Tam. They were keeping something safe."

"From who?" Ilsa asked.

"From whoever was sinking my sister's ship," Coral said, and pushed the door wide.

* * *

The hold opened before them like a throat.

It was bigger than Tam expected — liners carried cargo, everyone forgot that, but the Meridian had been built with a proper freight capacity, and hold 3 ran forty meters fore to aft, ribbed with frames, dark as a held breath. Their lamps reached maybe ten meters in before the darkness ate them. The crates stacked against the door were the nearest ones; beyond them the floor was a maze of pallets and strapping, rows of cargo going back into the black, all of it tilted twelve degrees with the ship, all of it dusted white, all of it perfectly, horribly preserved.

And everywhere — everywhere — stenciled markings. Tam swept his lamp across the nearest stack and read them without wanting to:

**CLASS C-UNSTABLE. HANDLE PER PROTOCOL 9. PROPERTY OF MERIDIAN SHIPPING CO.**

Protocol 9. Tam didn't know what Protocol 9 was, but he knew unstable cargo classifications, because every rigger who'd ever worked honest docks or dishonest ones knew them. Class C meant chemical. Class C meant the kind of manifest that got filed twice — once real, once fiction — depending on which customs office was asking.

"This isn't passenger luggage," Ilsa said. Her lamp tracked along a row of drums, each the size of a man's torso, each stenciled with the same class code and a serial number. She counted under her breath, lips moving. "Forty. Fifty-eight. There are dozens of these. This is commercial tonnage."

"On a pleasure crossing," Coral said.

"A *smuggled* crossing," Tam said, and heard his own voice come out strange. Because it was assembling itself in his head now, the whole picture, the one the inquiry had refused to assemble, the one Coral had spent twenty years shouting about from outside borders: a liner full of passengers, carrying unstable chemical cargo under false manifests, in a storm, going down. Hull failure. Crew error. A young officer blamed for all of it.

A young officer who had apparently survived long enough to reach this hold, find *this*, understand what it was, and weld herself in with it.

No — weld everyone else *out* of it. Away from it.

"Guys." Ilsa's voice had gone thin. "Come look at this."

She'd moved deeper into the hold, along the drum row, and her lamp was fixed on the deck between two pallets. Tam and Coral crossed to her, boots ringing on frozen steel, and looked down where she was looking.

A sleeping bag. Rolled out flat, twenty years flattened, but unmistakable. Beside it, a lantern, its fuel cell long dead. A tin cup. And arranged neatly against a cargo frame, squared up with the care of someone putting their affairs in order —

Eleven life vests. Folded. Stacked in a column, counted out like evidence.

Nobody spoke. The glacier groaned somewhere above them, a long tectonic complaint, and dust sifted down through their lamp beams like snow falling indoors.

"She lived down here," Ilsa whispered. "At the end. After the sinking. She *survived*, and she stayed."

"With the cargo," Tam said. His mouth was working faster than his judgment. "Welding the door so nobody could get to the drums. Keeping them contained. Keeping the proof—" he gestured at the stacks, the stencils, the serial numbers, the manifests that had to exist somewhere in this steel belly, "—keeping all of it intact. For twenty years the inquiry said crew error. Said her name. And the whole time the answer was sitting in a box she welded shut with her own hands."

He looked at Coral.

Coral was standing very still among the drums, in the middle of the maze his sister had built and guarded and died in, and Tam saw something happen to his face that he would spend the rest of his life failing to describe to anyone. It wasn't grief exactly. Grief he'd seen on Coral before, worn smooth by two decades of use. This was different. This was grief colliding with vindication at speed — the sound of a twenty-year lie breaking apart underwater, and a man realizing that everything he'd believed about his own guilt might be upside down.

"She saved eleven lives," Coral said. Almost inaudible. "That's eleven vests."

"Voss," Ilsa said suddenly. All the blood left her face. "Coral. Voss's letter. *'What's in the hold is worth more than you know, and worth less than you fear.'*" She recited it from memory, word-perfect, the way she recited charts. "He knows what's down here. He's known. He wasn't hiring us to salvage treasure."

"He was hiring us to open this door," Tam said.

And that was when the sound came — muffled, distant, rhythmic, transmitted through twenty meters of frozen steel and arriving in the soles of Tam's boots before it reached his ears. A sound he knew from a hundred harbors, a sound no sound should ever make inside a sealed hold entombed in a glacier.

Engines.

Someone was cutting through the outer hull.
