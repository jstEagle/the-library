## 28. Voss Arrives

The hold door stopped us for forty minutes.

Welded shut from the inside—Tam said it twice, then a third time, like repetition could make the metal confess something different. His lamp crawled across the seam, following the bead of weld down and around: rough, hurried work done with whatever torch was at hand, by someone who had no business doing it and every reason to.

"Whoever did this," Tam said finally, "was standing where we're standing. Inside."

"Or they welded it from the corridor and got trapped in here," Ilsa said. She didn't believe it. I could hear that she didn't believe it, the way you can hear a person not believing something even as they say it out loud, because hope is a language everyone can fake except under stress.

"Company policy on a foundering ship is to seal cargo spaces," Tam went on, quieter now. "Standard practice. But you seal them from outside. You don't weld yourself into a room full of whatever made this ship go down." He looked at me. "Unless you were keeping it in. Or keeping it from getting out."

I put my palm flat against the door. Cold came through my glove like a message. Somewhere behind this steel was the answer to a question I'd spent twenty years refusing to ask out loud, and now it had a texture—a weld bead, an hour of someone's desperate labor—and it felt less like a mystery than like a person. Like hands. My sister's hands, maybe. Or maybe I only thought so because grief will dress itself in any coincidence you leave lying around.

"We cut it," Ilsa said. "Plasma'll take twenty minutes once Ferro's rig is up here."

We were dragging the cutting gear through the wreck's spine when the sound came.

At first I took it for ice—the Meridian groaning inside its glacier shroud, the whole frozen museum settling another millimeter deeper into its long sleep. But Tam's head came up the way a deer's does, all at once and before the ears move, and he held up a fist, and we stood in the dark listening until there it was again, unmistakable:

A second torch. Cutting. From the seaward side.

"They breached," Ilsa breathed. "Voss is already aboard."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rage: it arrives with perfect clarity. Not the red mist of the stories. The opposite. Everything sharpens. You can count rivets. You can hear your own pulse ticking against your eardrums like a small clock counting down to something, and every object in the room acquires the crisp edges of a diagram, because some old animal part of you has decided that in about ninety seconds, everything you see may become either a weapon or an obstacle.

I heard Voss's crew moving through the lower decks the way you hear weather coming through a forest—not one sound but a pressure. Boots on frozen deck plates. The clink of gear. A voice giving orders in a tone I knew, low and unhurried, the tone of a man who has never once in his life needed to raise his voice to be obeyed.

"Coral." Ilsa's hand on my arm. "Coral. Look at me. We don't know how many."

"I know exactly how many." My own voice sounded strange to me, level and far away. "He runs six-man breach teams. He's run them for fifteen years. Four on the torches, one on comms, one watching the door." I realized I was reciting Sable Voss's operational habits from memory, the way you might recite the layout of a house you grew up in, and something about that frightened me more than the sounds below us did.

"You've worked with him," Tam said. It wasn't a question. Everyone in my crew knew the shape of that history without knowing its contents. "Before."

"Before," I said, and left it there, and started moving aft toward the stairwell that would bring us down behind them.

"Coral—" Ilsa again.

"He didn't come all this way to share," I said. "Whatever's in that hold, he intends to walk out with it and leave nothing for anyone else, including us. And if those documents are what Maren's journal says they are—" My throat closed on the name. I pushed through it. "Then he also intends to make sure no one else ever reads them."

"And if he just wants the deed?" Ferro asked. He'd come up the line quiet, medical bag over his shoulder, always the last voice and usually the sanest. "You said it yourself—inheritance, family papers. Maybe it's just money."

"Then why send scouts to taunt me with letters across four tides of ice?" I said. "Why hire half the guides in the region? Why tell me he knows what happened inside this ship?" I looked back at them, three faces in lamplight, and I heard myself say the thing I'd been carrying since the second letter, the thing I hadn't let reach daylight: "Because he wanted me here. He didn't need me for the route—he had the fleet. He needed me for whatever's in this hold. Which means whatever's in this hold needs me. Or ends me."

Nobody had anything to say to that. Good. I didn't want an argument. I wanted a stairwell.

* * *

We came down behind them through the officers' corridor, and I'll tell you plainly that walking those decks was worse than any fight could have been.

Twenty years of ice had preserved the Meridian the way bogs preserve bodies—perfectly, terribly, everything in its final place. A coat still hung over a stateroom chair. A chessboard sat mid-game in the wardroom, a bishop frozen mid-capture. Frost had grown fern-patterns up the walls like the ship growing new, white nerves. And everywhere the small details of ordinary life interrupted at speed: a dropped glove. A coffee mug fused to a table by its own spilled contents. Life, mid-sentence.

And over it all hung that smell—that deep-frozen nothing-smell, the absence of decay, which is its own kind of presence. A room where time refused to do its work is a room that remembers harder than any room should.

Maren had walked these corridors. That thought arrived with every step. She had walked these decks with her kit bag and her terrible jokes and her habit of tapping twice on every doorframe she passed through—for luck, she claimed, though she was not a lucky person and never pretended otherwise—and somewhere on this ship, on its last night, she had stopped tapping and started welding.

The voices ahead turned a corner. We flattened into a junction alcove, lamps killed, and I watched them come through the frost-light of their own torches: five figures in salvage rigs, moving with the economy of men who have breached wrecks the way fishermen cast nets. And at the rear, not hurrying, letting his people do the work while he read the ship—

Sable Voss.

He was older. Of course he was older; we both were. Grey had come into his beard the way frost comes into rope, gradually and then all at once, and there were lines around his eyes that hadn't been there when we'd shared a rope team on the Karst traverse, back when we were the two best young guides on the coast and too vain to admit we liked each other. But he walked the same. Weight on the balls of his feet. Head slightly tilted, reading a space before entering it.

I stepped out of the alcove and turned on my lamp, and the beam caught him full in the face.

To his credit—and Sable Voss would collect credit on his deathbed if someone offered terms—he didn't flinch. Didn't grab for the pry bar at his hip. He just raised one hand, and his team froze mid-stride, four lamps swinging toward me and then, at his small gesture, away again, down, disarmed by permission.

"Coral Delacroix," he said. "In the flesh. I'd say I'm surprised, but I paid good money to make sure you weren't."

"Voss."

"That's the greeting? Twenty years, and that's the whole speech?"

"Give me a reason to write a longer one."

Something moved through his face then—too fast to name, gone before it landed. He took a step forward and his team tensed and mine appeared out of the dark behind me, Ilsa and Tam flanking, Ferro hanging back with his hands visible, and just like that two crews stood in a frozen corridor with the chess game of a dead ship between them, and nobody breathing hard yet, everybody calculating.

"There it is," Voss said softly, looking me over. "There's the man from the mountain. I wondered if the exile had worn you down to something smaller. It hasn't. Good."

"Don't flatter me. You sent letters."

"I sent invitations."

"You sent bait." I took a step and Ilsa's hand brushed my elbow, not restraining, reminding. "You knew what this ship was to me. You knew, and you used it. So let's skip the part where we pretend this is a reunion. What's in the hold, Voss? Why does it need me?"

For a long moment he said nothing. Behind him, one of his torch-men shifted weight, and frost creaked somewhere overhead like the ship clearing its throat. Then Voss did something I had not seen him do in thirty years of knowing him, in rivalry and partnership and the long cold war between: he looked tired.

"Not here," he said. "Not in a corridor with my crew behind me and yours behind you, and every word I say becoming a lever somebody can pull later." He glanced down the passage, toward the bow, toward the sealed door we'd been cutting toward when his torches announced him. "How far along are you on that weld?"

"Forty minutes from through."

"My breacher says fifty from our side. Thicker steel over there—they built the seaward wall to take a grounding." Almost, almost, a smile touched his mouth. "You always did pick the efficient angle. Even at school. Especially at school."

"Answer the question."

"The question," Voss said, and his voice changed—dropped its polish, dropped its armor, dropped twenty years in the space of a sentence, "is not what's in the hold, Coral. The question is who welded it. And I can answer that one better than you can. Better than anyone alive can." He paused, and the pause had a shape, and I hated that I recognized the shape, because I'd heard men gather themselves to deliver bad news my entire career, and I knew the posture, and I knew what was coming, and I knew I couldn't stop it.

"I know because I was there," Sable Voss said. "Deck crew, Meridian, final voyage. I was Maren's crewmate. I was on watch the night she went down. And I've spent twenty years knowing what she did in these corridors while the rest of the world decided it was your fault."

The words went through me like a picket through snowpack—no resistance at first, just entry, and then the whole slope above beginning to move.

"You're lying," I said.

"I've lied to you plenty. Not about this."

"You weren't aboard. There's no Voss on any surviving manifest—"

"Because I bought my way off the survivors' list," he said flatly. "Third day after rescue. Cash to a company clerk who was already shredding half the paperwork. Ask yourself why, Coral. Ask yourself what a nineteen-year-old deckhand saw that night that made him willing to pay to disappear from the record." He took one more step, close enough now that I could see his breath ghosting between us, could see that his hands, at his sides, were not steady. Sable Voss's hands, not steady. Twenty years of dossiers and salvage fleets and cold empire, and his hands shook in a frozen corridor of the ship that made them.

"Your sister didn't cause the sinking," he said quietly. "She answered it. And the company has spent two decades making sure the difference between those two things never reaches shore—because if it does, the settlement collapses, the admiralty reopens the case, and certain very comfortable men go to prison. The documents in that hold prove all of it. Her logs. The cargo manifests. The testimony of eleven people whose lives she bought with—" His voice snagged. He cleared it like a man clearing ice off glass. "With herself."

Behind me, I heard Tam exhale. Heard Ilsa say my name, very carefully, the way you say a name to a person standing on ice you suspect is rotten.

And I stood in the corridor of my worst memory, in the ship that had eaten my sister and my reputation and my right to walk legally through two countries' mountains, and I looked at the man I had raced across a hundred miles of dying glacier, and I understood—with rage and revelation tangled so tightly together I couldn't tell which wire was which—that he had not come to beat me to the truth.

He had come because he couldn't carry it alone anymore.

"Say her name," I heard myself say.

Voss blinked. "What?"

"You've been dancing around it since the letters. Say her name, Voss. If you were really there. If you really knew her." My voice cracked down the middle and I let it. "Say it."

"Maren Yara Delacroix," he said, without hesitation, without ceremony, the middle name spoken soft as a hand laid on a grave. "She called herself Yara when she wanted to sound mysterious. She tapped doorframes twice for luck. She cheated at cards and confessed to it only when she won." His eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "Now are you going to stand there hating me, or are we going to open that door before my employers arrive and bury the truth in there with her?"

"Your employers—"

Boots on the upper deck. Many boots. And above them, faint but closing, the grind of hull-cutting equipment starting up at the seaward breach—his fleet, landing behind him, exactly on schedule.

Voss's face went white in the lamplight.

"They're early," he said. "Coral—we have a problem much bigger than each other."
