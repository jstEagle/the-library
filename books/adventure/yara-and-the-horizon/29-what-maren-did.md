## 29. What Maren Did

The corridor tilted.

Not the ship—the ship was dead, frozen mid-death for twenty years. It was Coral's balance that failed, some inner gyroscope knocked loose by Voss's last words: *I was her crewmate that night.*

He put a hand against the bulkhead. The metal was so cold it burned through his glove, and he welcomed it, because pain was a language he still spoke fluently and whatever Voss was about to say would not be.

"Say it again," Coral said. "Slowly."

Voss stood six feet away in the frozen dark, headlamp beam cutting between them like a drawn blade. Behind him, two of his salvage team had their lamps angled down—deliberately, Coral noticed, giving the dead ship the courtesy of shadow. Frost feathered the walls in white ferns. Somewhere aft, water moved inside the hull with a sound like slow swallowing.

"I was twenty-three," Voss said. "Junior mate aboard the *Meridian*, third season. You want to know what kind of man I was at twenty-three? I was the kind who took company money to look away from cargo manifests." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only rust. "So did everyone. That's the first thing you have to understand, Delacroix. The *Meridian* wasn't sunk by weather or by your sister's navigation or by any of the ten thousand lies they printed afterward. She was sunk by her own owners."

Coral's breath came out in a white plume. "The official finding was ice damage. Hull breach amidships during the storm."

"The official finding was written by the company's lawyers and blessed by two governments who'd both been paid to look elsewhere. Which is why your name ended up on a blacklist instead of a memorial." Voss stepped closer, boots crunching on frost. "You were nineteen. A deckhand with no standing, no witnesses willing to speak, and a family name the company needed buried. You were the cheapest scapegoat ever purchased."

"Stop." The word came out cracked. "Just—stop. Tell me what happened. In order. From the beginning."

Voss looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned and swept his lamp down the corridor, toward the heart of the wreck, and Coral understood he was being invited not just to hear the story but to walk into it.

They moved aft together, past doorways furred with ice, past a stairwell where a handrail still held the ghost-shape of decades-old grip tape. Ilsa and Tam followed without a word; Tam kept glancing back the way they'd come, toward the seaward breach where Voss's team had cut in, as if expecting the fleet to pour through it at any moment. Maybe they were. Coral found he couldn't make himself care. There was a door at the end of this corridor and behind it was his sister, and he had spent twenty years believing she had killed eleven people and himself along with her.

"Storm hit at 0300," Voss said, voice low, pitched for walking. "Bad one, but the *Meridian* had ridden worse. What made this one different was what we were carrying in hold three. Company charter, sealed manifests, no questions allowed. I signed off on the loading myself—I watched them bring aboard crates marked as machine parts that weighed wrong, sat wrong, and when one got dropped at the dock it didn't spill machine parts. It spilled crystals. Ammonium nitrate in a form I'd never seen, packed in drums, and the drums were already sweating."

"Blasting agent," Ferro said quietly, from the rear. "In quantity, that's not cargo. That's a bomb with a schedule."

"That's what our chief engineer said," Voss said. "He refused to sign. They transferred him off at the last port and blacklisted him too. Funny how that works." His lamp beam wavered, steadied. "So we're in open water, storm building, and hold three starts talking to us. Not leaking—*talking*. Drums shifting, knocking against each other. Every swell, a knock. Like something down there counting."

Coral stopped walking.

Because he remembered. He was nineteen years old and he remembered the knocking. He'd told himself for twenty years it was the hull flexing, the sea speaking, the ordinary grammar of a working ship in bad weather. But he had heard it, that night, lying in his bunk, and he had thought: *that sounds like something trying to get out.*

"Maren heard it too," he said.

"Maren heard it before anyone." Something changed in Voss's voice—not softness exactly, but the careful tone men use around wounds. "She was bosun's mate. She went down to look. Came back up white as sailcloth and went straight to the captain. Told him what was in the hold, told him the drums were compromised, told him we needed to jettison or beach her, anything. Captain said his hands were tied. Charter terms. Jettison the cargo and the company voids every insurance claim, fines the officers, ruins the crew. He ordered her to log it as sea noise and forget it."

"And she logged it," Coral said. "That's what the inquiry used. Her log entry. 'Unusual noises from hold three, attributed to sea state.' They used her own words to hang her."

"They used the entry they *made her write*," Voss corrected. "There was an earlier entry. She wrote it in her private book, not the ship's log. I know because after—" he stopped, jaw working. "After, when I was testifying to the inquiry under oath, I lied. I said I never saw any private record. Because I had seen it. She showed me. And I was twenty-three and terrified and the company's lawyer was sitting three feet away, and I buried it. Same as everyone buried everything." He turned to face Coral fully, and in the flat light his face looked older than the glacier, carved and tired. "That lie is why I'm here, Delacroix. Not the money. Well. The money too. But mostly the lie. Twenty years is a long time to carry a thing like that."

* * *

They reached the hold door at the end of the corridor, and Coral's legs nearly quit under him.

It was small—a freight access hatch, steel, unremarkable except for the welds. Beads of them ran across the seam in rough, overlapping rows, the work of someone welding fast and desperate and in the dark, sealing the door shut from the inside. The metal around the welds was scorched and bubbled. Whoever had done it had been standing in here when they struck the arc.

Tam ran a gloved hand near the seam without touching. "Welded from within," he murmured. "Somebody shut themselves in with it."

"Eleven people were in that section when she went down," Voss said. "Passengers and off-watch crew, berthed aft. When the hull breached—storm-driven swell, and the cargo shifted hard enough to split the frames at the bulkhead—hold three started to go. Not explode. Worse. The drums ruptured and the compound started reacting with seawater. Exothermic. Heat, gas, pressure building in a sealed space. Given ten minutes it takes the whole stern section with it, and everyone aft dies. Not eleven. Forty-one."

"So Maren went in," Coral said. His voice sounded far away to himself, like radio traffic from another life.

"Maren went in." Voss's lamp found the welds again, traced them. "With a portable rig and a cutting torch, and she sealed the aft bulkhead door behind her. Welded herself in with the cargo. Bought those eleven people time to get clear of the section before the pressure blew. The explosion was internal, contained, and it took hold three and everything in it—including her—and left the rest of the hull enough intact that forty-one souls made the boats. Eleven saved by the door she closed. Thirty survived overall. The inquiry knew all of this. The inquiry chose not to know it."

The corridor was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the glacier groaned—a long tectonic sigh, the mountain settling its weight.

Coral reached out and laid his palm flat against the welded door.

Cold. Of course cold. Everything here was cold; the cold had been keeping his sister's last work preserved like a fly in amber for two decades. But under his glove, through the leather, he could feel the beads of the welds, each one a decision, each one a second of her life spent buying someone else's. She had stood exactly here. She had held the torch exactly here. And she had known—he understood this suddenly, completely, the way you understand a thing you've always known—she had known that the door only worked if it stayed shut, which meant she had to stay inside, which meant—

"You said eleven," Coral managed. "Saved. How do you know the number?"

"Because I counted them onto the boats." Voss's voice had gone rough. "I was one of the last off. I stood at the rail and I counted. Eleven people came out of the aft section alive because of a door that should have killed them. And then the company told the world the bosun's mate had mislabeled volatile cargo, stowed it wrong, caused the disaster, and died running from her own mistake. And I let them say it." He pulled off one glove, pressed his bare hand briefly against the door beside Coral's, then pulled it back as if the metal had bitten him. "I was going to fix it, Delacroix. For years I told myself I was going to fix it. And then fixing it became expensive, and expensive became impossible, and impossible became a fortune sitting in a sealed hold that nobody could reach anyway. So I stopped being a man who was going to fix it and became a man who was going to profit from it, and I told myself those were different things. They're not. I know they're not."

"Why tell me now?" Coral asked. "Why not keep the lie? You had me beat to the hold. You had the fleet behind you. You could have taken everything and I'd have gone home with nothing but a look at a welded door."

Voss was quiet for a moment. His lamp drifted up to the frost-ferned ceiling.

"Because she showed me her private log the night before we sailed," he said finally. "And the last line of it was about you. *If anything happens, my brother doesn't carry this. Whatever the inquiry says, he doesn't carry it.*" He looked back at Coral, and for once there was nothing sharp in his face at all. "I broke that promise for twenty years. I'm not breaking it twice."

* * *

Coral didn't remember sitting down.

But he was sitting—back against the opposite bulkhead, knees up, in the corridor outside the hold where his sister had spent her final minutes, and his crew had formed a loose ring around him without anyone ordering it. Ilsa crouched nearest, saying nothing, which was the right thing. Ferro had his med kit open out of pure habit, though there was nothing in it for this.

Twenty years of guilt does not collapse all at once. It collapses the way a glacier calves—one crack, then another, then the whole face of it letting go at once with a roar you feel in your teeth.

"She didn't do it," he said, to no one, to everyone. "All this time. Every border, every blacklist, every job I took under a false name, every mile I walked to stay ahead of a thing that wasn't even true—"

"We know," Ilsa said simply.

"She *saved* them. She saved eleven people and they gave her a grave with a lie on it and gave me her sentence to serve." His hands were shaking. He let them shake. "I built my whole life on top of her crime. I measured every year against it. And she was—" His voice went. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "She was the best of us. She was always the best of us."

Ferro spoke gently, from the edge of the lamplight. "The documents. Voss said there are records in the hold. Manifests, correspondence, the private log entry. Enough to reopen the case?"

"Enough to burn the company down," Voss said. "Charter contracts, the engineer's transfer orders, dockside testimony about the dropped crate, and Maren's private log with the original entry in her own hand. The inquiry suppressed all of it. It's all still here. Paper keeps, in the cold." He paused. "Along with the other thing. The inheritance. The reason my backers funded this whole expedition."

Coral lowered his hand. "What other thing?"

Voss met his eyes, and behind the exhaustion in his face something new had surfaced—something that looked almost like apology, and almost like fear.

"The Delacroix deed," he said. "Your family's land claims. The coastal parcels your parents registered before you were born—parcels that happen to sit directly on the route the shipping company has spent twenty years trying to build. Your sister's evidence archive isn't just proof of a crime, Delacroix. It's leverage over the people who committed it. And those people are twelve hours behind us, and they don't want justice. They want the archive burned and the deed signed over."

From somewhere deep in the wreck—deep in the glacier, deep in the world—came a sound none of them had heard yet on this voyage. A crack, long and rising, like the first note of something enormous beginning to fail.

Tam's head snapped toward the seaward breach. "Tell me that was the ship settling."

Voss was already on his feet, lamp swinging toward the sound, and when he answered, his voice had shed every ounce of confession and regret and become pure command.

"That wasn't the ship," he said. "Calving season just opened early. And my backers' lead ship will be landing on the seaward ice in less than two hours—with orders to leave no paper and no witnesses."
