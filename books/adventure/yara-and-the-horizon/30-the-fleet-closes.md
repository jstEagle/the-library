## 30. The Fleet Closes

The sound came first—a diesel cough rolling up the channel, wrong for the ice, right for money.

Coral was still inside the Meridian's corridor when he heard it, one hand flat against a bulkhead furred with frost, Voss's words about Maren still ringing in his skull like a struck bell. Eleven lives. She welded it shut from the inside. He'd been standing there letting the truth rearrange twenty years of himself when the ship's old hull carried the vibration through the ice and into his bones.

He knew that engine note. Every guide who'd ever been undercut on a contract knew it: the heavy-throated idle of an Ardent-class salvage tender, the kind of vessel that showed up after someone else had done the dangerous work and made everything legal by force of paperwork and presence.

"They're early," Ilsa said. She was at the fissure mouth, binoculars up, breath steaming around her face. "Two tenders. No—three. And a work barge."

"How far?"

"Channel's open to them now. Ninety minutes if they push it. Less if they've got a pilot who knows the shelf."

Voss stood in the corridor behind them, and Coral turned to look at him properly for the first time since the revelation. The man looked older than his reputation. Twenty years of running salvage contracts for people with clean hands and dirty ledgers had carved him down to something lean and watchable, and right now what was watchable in his face was fear.

"You didn't tell them where we were," Coral said. It wasn't a question anymore. Ferro's ping had been the leak, and Voss had come alone—or nearly alone—and told him things no employer would have authorized.

"I told you," Voss said. "I came to see the hold opened. That was always mine. The rest—" He jerked his chin toward the corridor, toward the sealed door at its end, welded shut by a dead woman's hands. "The rest belongs to your sister."

"Then whose engines are those?"

Voss's jaw worked. "Meridian Lines never went under, Coral. They restructured. New name, same board, same families. They've spent two decades waiting for the glacier to give the wreck back, because everything that could convict them is in that hold. My contract wasn't salvage." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "My contract was retrieval and destruction. Recover the documents. Burn them. File a report saying the hold was empty, compromised by seawater, nothing recoverable. They've done it before. There are three other wrecks like this one scattered across the northern routes, and every one of them came back with nothing in the manifest worth investigating."

Tam appeared at the junction behind them, rope coiled over one shoulder, face gray with cold and something else. "Bo says the fleet's main landing party just hit the near shore. We've got maybe an hour before their advance team reaches the calving wall. And Coral—their lead tender's flying survey colors. Official ones. They're not even pretending this is private."

* * *

They gathered in the fissure mouth, the five of them plus Voss, wedged between walls of blue ice while the wind off the channel tried to take their heat away a handful of calories at a time. Below and behind, the Meridian lay in her cradle of melting ice, black hull emerging like something surfacing from a long dive. Ahead, out on the white road, three vessels were disgorging men and equipment.

Ferro had Bo propped against the ice wall, the kid's leg splinted, color bad but breathing steady. Ilsa had the route map spread on her knee, weighting the corners with stones. Nobody spoke for a moment. The glacier groaned overhead, a long tectonic complaint, and everyone flinched except Voss, who had clearly heard it enough times to stop hearing it.

"Options," Coral said.

"Run," Tam said. "Take the documents and run. We know the route better than they do. We got here first."

"With Bo on a splint and the fifth tide already turning?" Ilsa shook her head. "We can't outrun them on foot, and we don't have a boat. Running means they take everything anyway and we lose people doing it."

"Then we fight," Tam said. His hand drifted toward the pry bar on his belt—not a weapon, exactly, but not not one either.

"No," Coral said.

"Coral—"

"We didn't sabotage their channel. We're not starting a war on the ice with eleven people and a med kit." He looked at Voss. "But you haven't given us options yet. You've been standing there like a man doing arithmetic. So do it out loud."

Voss crouched by Ilsa's map. When he spoke, his voice had changed—flatter, faster, the voice of a man who had run contracts in rooms where the wrong sentence ended careers. "Their authority is contractual, not legal. Survey colors or not, they have no maritime jurisdiction out here until they file at the coastal registry, and the nearest registry office is two days' sail south. What they *do* have is muscle, equipment, and a document signed by a director of Meridian Lines authorizing 'recovery and disposal of hazardous materials.'" He tapped the map. "What we have is the documents themselves, physical possession of the wreck's interior, and—" he paused, and Coral watched him arrive at the decision he'd apparently been carrying since the corridor, "—my testimony."

"Your testimony's worthless," Tam said. "You're their man."

"I'm their man on paper. I've been theirs for nineteen years, which means I've seen the internal correspondence, the original incident reports, the insurance filings they thought were destroyed in the restructuring. I kept copies." He said it simply, the way a man admits to a tattoo he stopped being ashamed of years ago. "Not because I planned this. Because I couldn't sleep. Same reason you burned my letter and then couldn't stay home, Delacroix. Guilt keeps its own calendar."

"Why?" Coral asked. Not why the copies—why the turn. "Nineteen years working for them. Why break it now, on this ice, for me?"

Voss met his eyes. Out on the channel, a tender's floodlight swept the fog, and the light passed over both their faces without distinguishing between them.

"Because I was in the water that night," Voss said. "Lifeboat six. I watched your sister weld that door with the flooding coming down the aft stairwell, and I watched her finish, and I watched the ice take the stern before she could get to us. And then I stood at the inquiry and said nothing, because a director's lawyer put a contract in front of me and I was twenty-four years old and drowning in debt." His voice didn't crack. It went quieter, which was worse. "Eleven people lived because of her, and the official record says she caused it. I signed my name under that lie. I've been signing it every year since. Today the ice gave her back, and I find I'm done signing."

Nobody moved. Somewhere above them the serac field ticked and settled, the mountain keeping its own time.

"So we go public," Ilsa said finally. "Here. Now. Before they reach the wreck."

"That's the arithmetic," Voss said. "They need silence. Silence requires controlling the site, the documents, and every witness. Remove any one leg and the whole thing wobbles. We can't remove their muscle. But the documents are in that hold, behind a door only four people alive can breach safely"—he glanced at Tam—"and the witnesses are standing in this crack in the ice refusing to be quiet."

"They'll just take us," Tam said. "All due respect. Six of us, thirty of them."

"Not legally," Voss said. "And these aren't pirates. They're corporate security contractors with insurance policies and reputations. They'll posture, they'll threaten, they'll try to buy you—that's the first move, always, offer the guides triple rate to walk away—but they will not kill six witnesses on a documented route during calving season with satellite imagery of the channel. Their entire method depends on nobody ever having proof anything happened. We are going to make *happened* extremely expensive for them."

Coral looked out at the approaching lights. He thought about the kind of man he'd decided to be on the ridge, the day Tam suggested sabotage and he said no. That choice had cost them their tide advantage; the fleet had used the channel he wouldn't poison, and here they were, ninety minutes from a confrontation he couldn't win by strength.

Fine. Then not by strength.

"Then we hold the wreck," Coral said. "All of us. We get inside, we secure the documents, and when their advance team arrives, we meet them at the fissure with cameras running and Voss's name attached to everything. Ferro—can Bo move?"

"He can hop. Slowly. With help."

"Then he hops. Ilsa, how long to get everyone through the fissure and to the hold door?"

"Twenty minutes if the ice cooperates."

"The ice never cooperates," Tam muttered, and started coiling rope.

* * *

They crossed the shelf in a tight line, headlamps bobbing, the fleet lights growing behind them like a second dawn that meant harm. Coral carried Maren's journal inside his jacket, against his chest, and the exculpatory pages they'd recovered from the purser's cabin wrapped in oilcloth in his pack. The deed—the actual paper inheritance, the family land grant Voss had wanted all along—he'd left in the hold's strongbox, because some decisions you make once and don't rehearse.

At the fissure, the Meridian rose around them, and Coral felt the strange double vision of it: grief and strategy occupying the same corridors. This was the place his sister died. It was also, now, a position to be held. He hated how naturally the second thought fit alongside the first. Twenty years as an underground guide taught you to read every tragedy as terrain eventually.

They reached the hold door. The welds still gleamed under lamplight, Maren's bead pattern steady and deliberate even in the final minutes of a sinking ship. Coral put his palm against the metal, once, briefly.

"Hold fast," he told her quietly. "One more job."

Tam and Bo had rigged the breach point Voss had identified—a stress fracture two meters port of the original door, where the hull plate had begun to separate from the frame. It took forty minutes of careful cutting with the plasma lance, forty minutes in which the fleet's advance lights crept visibly closer along the shore, and when the plate finally swung inward, the air that came out was cold and dry and still, the preserved breath of a sealed room.

Inside: crates. Dozens of them, stenciled with Meridian Lines markings, and among them—the reason for everything—the document cases, the ledger safe, the company's own smuggling manifests packed for transport and never delivered. Maren hadn't just sealed the danger in. She'd sealed the evidence in with it, deliberately, a message mailed twenty years into the future addressed to whoever was stubborn enough to come.

"She knew," Coral said. His voice sounded strange in the frozen hold. "She knew the cargo would sink her, and she locked the proof in with it so it couldn't be quietly recovered later. She built this room to outlast them."

Voss stood in the doorway with his lamp raised, and for a moment the salvage man fell away entirely and what remained was a young crewman in a lifeboat, watching a welder work by lightning.

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly what she did."

* * *

The advance team reached the fissure ninety minutes later, twelve men in matching insulated gear with a survey drone humming overhead like an obedient insect. Their leader was a broad, unhurried man named Kessler, and he did exactly what Voss predicted: he offered them money first.

"Triple contracted rate," Kessler said, standing at the fissure mouth with the wreck at his back and the fleet lights strung out along the shore behind him. "Walk away now, sign a standard nondisclosure, and every one of you clears more than a season of guiding. No hard feelings. This is industry, not conflict."

Coral stood in front of the breached hold with his crew arrayed behind him—Tam with his arms crossed, Ilsa with the route log open in her hands, Ferro beside Bo's improvised crutch, and Voss slightly apart, deliberately visible, deliberately identifiable.

"Our answer's the same as it was on the ridge," Coral said. "No."

Kessler sighed, professional regret. "Mr. Delacroix. You're a blacklisted guide operating illegally in two national jurisdictions, in possession of material from a protected wreck site under a valid commercial recovery contract. You understand how this reads from where I stand."

"I do," Coral said. "Let me adjust the reading." He nodded to Ilsa, who held up the route log—timestamped, witnessed, documenting their legal passage under the calving-season window. He nodded to Ferro, who held up the medical tablet with Bo's injury report and the distress ping record, fully disclosed, dates and coordinates intact. And then he stepped aside so the lamplight fell full on the man behind him.

"And this," Coral said, "is Sable Voss. Contract holder. Who will be filing his own statement with the maritime registry at Port Calloway within seventy-two hours, along with nineteen years of Meridian Lines internal records, the original cargo manifests, and the testimony of eleven surviving witnesses to what happened in this hold the night of the sinking. You can burn the papers, Mr. Kessler. You've done it before—we know. But you'd have to burn us too, on camera, on a documented route, during calving season. And your employers did not budget for that."

Something flickered across Kessler's face—not fear, but recalculation, the rapid arithmetic of a man discovering the job had changed shape. Behind him, the drone's camera light blinked steadily, and Coral realized with a cold, calm clarity that the drone was recording *him*, and that this time he wanted it to.

Kessler keyed his radio. Spoke low. Listened.

When he turned back, his posture had shifted by degrees—the unhurried authority thinning at the edges.

"My directors would like to speak with Mr. Voss," he said.

"They know where to find me," Voss said. "Tell them the hold's contents are secured, the witnesses are unified, and the documents reach Port Calloway in three days whether I'm alive to carry them or not. Tell them I've retained counsel. Tell them—" and here his voice took on something almost gentle, almost cruel, "—tell them Maren Delacroix sends her regards."

Kessler stared at him for a long moment. Then he turned and walked back down the ice toward the lights, radio crackling, and the drone wheeled and followed him like a gull after a fishing boat.

For a moment nobody breathed. Then Tam let out a breath that was half laugh. "Did you mean that? Counsel? Records? Seventy-two hours?"

"I meant every word," Voss said. "I've been composing that speech for nineteen years. It came out better than the draft."

Above them, the glacier made a new sound—not the familiar groan but a sharp, percussive *crack*, followed by a rumble that rolled down the calving wall and out across the channel, and every head turned toward the ice face a kilometer north, where in the floodlit dark a seam had opened, vertical and gleaming, shedding a curtain of powder snow.

Ilsa checked her watch. Checked the water line lapping at the shelf edge. Her face, when she looked up, was the face of a navigator delivering news she'd already accepted.

"Fifth tide's peaking," she said. "And the calving season isn't opening in six days anymore. It's opening tonight. That seam is the first fracture of the season, and when the next tide turns, this whole shelf starts letting go." She pointed south, down the white road they'd bled across, now the only way out. "Everything we just won, we have to carry out. Starting now."

Coral shouldered the oilcloth-wrapped documents, felt Maren's journal press against his chest, and turned to face the long ice road home.

"Then we don't waste the window," he said. "Everyone on rope. We run."
