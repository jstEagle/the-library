## 31. Calving Season Opens

Ilsa heard the mountain before she understood it.

She was on watch at the mouth of the corridor they'd cut through the pressure ridge, a thermos of tea going cold in her hands, when the sound came up through the soles of her boots first—a deep, tectonic groan, like a door opening in a house that had been locked for a century. Then the air itself carried it: a crack that didn't end so much as travel, running away to the north along the calving wall until her ears lost track of it.

The glacier was talking.

Not the ordinary talk—the settling creaks and sighs she'd catalogued over six days like weather. This was different. This was a sentence being spoken all at once.

"Delacroix," she called down into the camp, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Get up. All of you. Now."

* * *

They'd made the far side of the calving wall by midnight, after the wreck, after Voss, after everything—thirty-one hours without real sleep, camped in the lee of a serac field two kilometers from the ice face because closer wasn't survivable and farther meant losing the route. The plan had been simple: rest four hours, run the last stretch during the sixth tide's window, be off the ice before the fleet's heavy equipment finished stripping what Voss's backers wanted stripped.

The plan had assumed the mountain would wait for them.

Ilsa had grown up on water, not ice—harbor charts, tidal tables, her father's fishing boat and the particular grammar of the sea—and she'd told Coral once, back before any of this, that ice was just water that had learned to hold its breath. She'd thought it was a clever line. Standing watch now, watching the calving wall in the grey pre-dawn, she understood it differently. Water holding its breath eventually exhales. And when it did, it wouldn't exhale politely. It would exhale the way the Meridian had gone down: all at once, taking whatever it wanted with it.

The wall was three hundred meters high where it met the fjord, blue-white and striated like the rings of some impossible tree, twenty thousand years of winters compressed into stone-hard ice. All week it had been shedding small pieces—the size of cars, once the size of a house—with reports that echoed off the far shore. Normal. Textbook. Glaciers calve; that's what glaciers do.

But the rhythm had changed overnight. The pieces were coming more often. And between them, the deep groans were getting longer.

Ilsa pulled out her watch and started counting intervals, because counting was what she did when fear arrived—her father had taught her that too, standing at a wheel in weather that wanted them dead: *Numbers are a rope, girl. Hold onto them and the storm can't have your hands.*

Fourteen minutes between events. Then eleven. Then nine.

By the fourth interval she'd stopped drinking her tea.

* * *

Coral came up the slope fast, pulling his jacket on wrong-side-out, Ferro and Bo behind him with Tam limping last on the ankle he'd turned in the wreck's corridors. They gathered around her at the ridge crest and looked north, and Ilsa watched their faces do what hers had done ten minutes ago: the slow recognition that the thing they'd been racing against all this time had never actually been the fleet.

"How long?" Coral asked. No preamble. He'd stopped wasting words somewhere around the fifth tide.

"I don't know." She hated saying it. She was the navigator; knowing was supposed to be her job. "Hours, maybe. Could be less. The interval's collapsing—fourteen minutes to nine since I started counting. That's not shedding, that's—" She searched for the word and found it and wished she hadn't. "That's failure."

"The route?" Bo asked. His voice was still rough from the crevasse, from the cold water he'd swallowed twice now on this expedition, but his eyes were clear.

Isla looked south, past the camp, to where the ice bridge spanned the channel—the same bridge they'd sprinted on the fifth tide with the fleet landing behind them, the narrow white road that was the only way out of this basin before the meltwater channels widened past crossing. In the dark she could just make out its outline, and even as she watched, something in its middle sagged. Not broke. Sagged. Like a spine settling.

"It's dying," she said. "It was dying when we crossed it. Everything out here is dying right now—the whole shelf is letting go. The tide comes up tonight and takes the last of it, or the calving does it sooner. Either way, we're on a schedule the mountain wrote, and it didn't consult us."

Tam sat down heavily on a supply crate. "So we run now. In the dark. Over a bridge that's already half-gone."

"We run now," Coral said.

"In the dark," Tam repeated.

"You want to wait for daylight?" Coral's voice wasn't angry. It was worse than angry—it was flat, arithmetic, a man doing sums he already knew the answer to. "Daylight is four hours out. Four hours is forty minutes of margin if the interval keeps collapsing, and it will keep collapsing, because that's what collapse means. We go now, we cross while there's still a bridge, and we put five kilometers between us and that wall before it lets go. Or we stand here and discuss it until the discussion doesn't matter."

Nobody discussed it.

* * *

Breaking camp took eleven minutes. Ilsa timed it, because numbers were a rope. Ferro struck the stove, Tam and Bo collapsed the tents, Coral distributed loads with the economy of a man who'd been packing other people's survival into bags for twenty years. Ilsa herself stood over the map one last time, headlamp low, and did the navigation she'd been dreading since the moment she'd heard the first groan.

The route out was brutal in the best conditions: five kilometers across the shelf, the ice bridge over the melt channel, then the climb to the moraine saddle where solid rock began and the glacier's jurisdiction ended. On the fifth tide, fresh and terrified, they'd done it in ninety minutes. Now they were carrying an injured man—Bo's ribs, cracked again in the wreck when a bulkhead gave under him—and hauling the salvage cases, because Coral had refused, absolutely refused, to leave Maren's archive behind, and no one had argued, because after everything they'd seen inside that hold, no one wanted to be the person who suggested leaving her evidence on the ice to drown twice.

Ilsa ran the numbers and got a number she didn't like: two hours, best case, if the bridge held and nothing else went wrong.

She folded the map and went to find Coral.

He was at the edge of camp, looking north at the wall. In the darkness it was just a paler blackness, but every few minutes it flashed faintly blue as another piece let go, and the sound rolled over them like a train passing underground.

"Ilsa." He said her name the way he'd started saying it somewhere around the third tide—not *navigator*, not a summons, just her name, the way you say the name of someone whose judgment you've decided to trust with your life. "Give it to me straight."

"Two hours. Best case. Bridge permitting."

"And worst case?"

"Worst case the bridge goes while we're on it, and the melt channel becomes open water, and we're on an island of ice with a calving wall for a neighbor." She paused. "There's also a version where the wall lets go while we're still in the basin, and none of the rest matters."

Coral nodded slowly. Twenty years ago, she'd heard, he would have hidden the calculation behind bravado. The man who stood in front of her now just absorbed it, the way rock absorbs rain.

"Then we don't stop," he said. "Whatever happens, we don't stop moving. Stopping is how the mountain gets you. It's always been how the mountain gets people."

"That's not navigation advice," Ilsa said.

"No," Coral agreed. "It's the only other kind there is."

* * *

They moved out at 0340 by Ilsa's watch, single file, roped at fifteen-meter intervals, Coral leading and Ilsa second so she could call the line. The salvage cases rode in a sledge Bo and Tam hauled in shifts, Bo refusing to surrender his turns despite the ribs, because Bo had decided somewhere in the last day that he was done being the one carried, and nobody was foolish enough to fight him about it.

Behind them, the calving wall kept talking. Louder now. The intervals were down to seven minutes, then six, and each event was bigger—Ilsa felt them through the ice under her boots before she heard them, a shudder traveling through the shelf like a pulse, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and beginning, slowly, to wake.

She navigated by dead reckoning and memory, because the markers they'd planted on the outbound run were mostly gone—swallowed by new crevasses, or simply vanished, the ice rearranging itself hour by hour into terrain none of them had crossed before. Twice she called a halt and swung left by degrees, feeling for the old line. Once she got it wrong, caught it within thirty seconds, corrected. Nobody blamed her. There was no time to blame anyone.

At kilometer two, the wall exhaled.

Ilsa heard it build—a rising roar, deeper than anything yet, a sound with mass behind it—and she was already shouting *"DOWN! FLAT!"* before her mind finished the sentence, dropping behind a serac with her arms over her head as the world behind them turned white.

The wave of displaced air hit like a physical hand, pressing her into the ice, tearing at her hood, filling the world with a grinding thunder that went on and on and on. Through the glare of her squeezed-shut eyes she saw nothing, heard everything: the crash of tons of ancient ice hitting the fjord, the secondary cracks chasing it, the shelf itself flexing beneath her body like the deck of a ship in swell.

Then it passed, and the silence afterward was the loudest thing she'd ever heard.

She raised her head. Snow-dust hung in the air, catching her headlamp beam like fog. Behind them, where the calving wall had been, there was a new geometry—a fresh scar of raw blue ice three hundred meters wide, and beyond it, out on the black water, a floating jumble of bergs the size of buildings, rocking in the swell they'd born.

"Everyone report," Coral's voice, hoarse, cutting through the dust. "Now."

One by one: Ferro. Tam. Bo. Ilsa. Five voices. All present.

"Move," Coral said.

They moved.

* * *

The bridge appeared out of the dark at kilometer three, and Ilsa's stomach dropped.

On the fifth tide it had been a white road, narrow but proud, spanning the melt channel with the confidence of something that had stood for a season. Now it was a ruin. The middle third had sagged into a shallow arch, cracked along its length like old porcelain, and the melt channel beneath had widened overnight—black water visible on both sides, moving, hungry, flecked with brash ice that clinked against the bridge's undermined edges like glasses at a funeral.

Ilsa knelt at the near edge and studied it the way she'd study a chart of a hostile coast. Rope-line traverse, she thought. One at a time. Weight spread. No stopping in the sagged section—it's already committed to its decision, and we don't get a vote.

"Coral." She waved him forward. "Single file. Fifteen meters apart. Fast feet, soft steps—if you feel it move under you, keep moving, don't freeze. Freezing concentrates weight. Moving distributes it." She looked at the others. "Sledge goes light—cases off, carried across on backs. Bo, you're second-to-last with me spotting. Questions?"

"Fifteen meters," Tam said, "on a bridge thirty meters long means we're basically crossing alone."

"Yes," Ilsa said.

"And if it goes under someone?"

Ilsa looked at the black water and said nothing, and her silence said it for her: *then we go in after them, and we'd better be quick, because cold like that gives you perhaps four minutes of useful consciousness, and the water gives you less.*

"Ropes stay attached," Coral said quietly. "All the way across. If someone goes in, the rope is the rescue. Cut only on my order." He looked at Ilsa. "You lead. Your bridge, your rules."

Her bridge. She felt the words settle onto her shoulders like a pack. Somewhere around the fourth tide, on the rope-line traverse above open water, Coral had followed her orders for the first time in twenty years of leading everything himself, and she'd understood then that he'd been handing her something deliberately, the way you hand someone a torch you can't carry anymore. He was handing it to her again now.

"All right," she said. "Ferro first. Then Tam. Then me. Then Bo. Coral anchors rear. Go."

* * *

Ferro crossed like a dancer, light and quick, her medic's hands steady on the guide rope, and made the far side in under a minute. Tam followed, heavier, slower, the sledge frame strapped to his back, and the bridge complained under him—one sharp crack, a shudder—but held. Ilsa went third, counting her own footsteps because numbers were a rope, feeling through her boots the strange hollow elasticity of ice that had stopped being ground and become, temporarily, a rumor of ground.

In the middle, in the sagged section, the bridge spoke to her.

Not cracked—spoke. A long, groaning syllable, the ice flexing down under her weight, and for one suspended second she felt the whole span shift, a lateral movement, the structure deciding something about itself. Her body screamed *freeze*. Her training said *move*. She moved—fast feet, soft steps, exactly as she'd ordered—and came off the far lip onto solid shelf with her heart slamming and her breath smoking and the bridge still standing behind her, barely, like a promise it was no longer sure it could keep.

"Bo," she called. "Your turn. Slow is smooth."

Bo crossed well for a man with cracked ribs—she could see him rationing his breathing, timing each step to the exhale. He was halfway, in the sagged section, when the next calving hit.

The roar built behind them and the shelf bucked and the bridge—already dying, already decided—shuddered hard, and a crack shot across its width directly under Bo's feet with a sound like a rifle.

"MOVE!" Ilsa screamed, and Bo moved, three lunging steps, four, and the section he'd just left dropped thirty centimeters with a wet crunch, tilting into the channel, and then he was off the far edge and into Coral's arms and the bridge stood broken-backed behind them, its middle sagging into the black water, still technically connected, technically passable, technically.

Technically was running out.

* * *

They didn't stop to celebrate. Coral counted heads—five, again, always five now, the number had become a prayer—and pointed up the slope toward the moraine saddle, and they climbed.

And that was when Ilsa, pausing at the top of the rise to check the line behind them, looked back south and saw the lights.

Out on the shelf, strung along the route they themselves had taken, a chain of headlamps was moving toward the bridge. The fleet. Voss's backers, their crews, the heavy teams with their winches and their barges and their lawyers' contracts—all of them retreating now, the way animals retreat, because the mountain had announced its intentions and even men who thought they owned the ice could read a death sentence when it was written in seven-minute intervals.

But between the fleet and the bridge, one set of lights had stopped.

Ilsa raised her binoculars. Found the figure. A single man standing at the near edge of the ruined span, looking across at them—or looking at the bridge, assessing it, doing the same arithmetic she'd done twenty minutes ago.

Even at this distance, even in the dark, she knew the set of those shoulders.

"Sable," she said.

Coral turned. Took the binoculars. Was silent for a long moment.

"He'll try the crossing," Ilsa said. "His team won't fit through the window—they're too heavy, too slow, they'll lose the route entirely. But he'll send himself across, or try to, because he's spent twenty years getting to that hold and he's not leaving empty-handed." She lowered her own gaze to the bridge, to its sagged and shattered middle, to the black water chewing at its edges. "And that bridge has maybe one crossing left in it. Maybe zero."

Coral lowered the binoculars. Behind them, the calving wall fired another salvo into the fjord, the boom rolling up the valley like a verdict, and the shelf trembled under all their boots—the mountain reclaiming its dead, all of them, everything, the Meridian and the route and the bridge and every plan ever laid upon the ice.

"He saved my life yesterday," Coral said quietly. "I owe him a crossing."

Ilsa looked at the bridge. Looked at the water. Looked at the man standing alone on the far shore with the fleet's lights streaming past him like a river he'd stepped out of.

"Coral," she said, "that debt is due *now*."
