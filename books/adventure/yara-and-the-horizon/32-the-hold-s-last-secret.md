## 32. The Hold's Last Secret

The hold door hung open like a mouth that had been waiting twenty years to speak.

Voss had cut the welds from the seaward side in the chaos of the calving warning—three torch lines, quick and ugly, the work of a man who'd stopped caring about preservation the moment the glacier started to die above us. The ice groaned through the hull like a living thing turning over in its sleep. We had minutes. Maybe less.

I went in first.

Not because I was brave. Because if I let anyone else go through that door ahead of me, I would stand in this frozen corridor for the rest of my life wondering what they'd found.

The air inside was older than I was. Cold that had never once been interrupted—no thaw, no season, no breath. It pressed against my face like a hand. My headlamp swept across cargo netting stiff with frost, across a stack of crates furred white as though the ship had grown fur in its long sleep, across—

A boot.

I stopped. Made myself look. It was just a boot, empty, laced, standing upright on the deck plates as if its owner had stepped out of it and walked away into the dark. Twenty years of nightmares had prepared me for worse than a boot. Twenty years of nightmares had not prepared me for how *tidy* everything was.

"Maren's station should be aft," Voss said behind me. His voice was wrong—stripped of all its usual lacquer, down to bare wood. "Second bulkhead. She kept the manifests there."

"You've been inside before." I didn't turn around.

"Once. The night it happened." A pause. "You want to hate me for that too? Get in line. I've been at the front of it since I was nineteen."

Ilsa's light swung past my shoulder, methodical, cataloguing. Tam stood at the breach with the pry bar and said nothing, which for Tam was a kind of prayer. Above us, through three decks of steel and ice, the glacier made a sound like a giant clearing its throat.

"Talk while we move," I said. "We don't stop moving."

* * *

Maren's station was exactly where Voss said it would be, and that fact hit me harder than the boot had. He knew her habits. He knew where she kept things. There had been a version of my sister's life that included this man, that had a desk and a system and a routine, and I had never been part of it because I'd been home being sixteen and certain she'd come back by spring.

The desk was a steel cabinet bolted to the bulkhead, and the drawer was locked—not welded, *locked*, a small brass padlock crusted in frost, the kind you'd put on a diary.

Tam cracked it in four seconds flat. He didn't even apologize.

Inside: no gold. No coin, no bullion, none of the Delacroix silver that had been the family legend, the inheritance my grandfather supposedly loaded aboard the Meridian when he fled the bankruptcy. Instead there were oilcloth packets, dozens of them, each one labeled in Maren's handwriting—small, slanted, impatient, exactly as I remembered it from grocery lists and margin notes in my schoolbooks.

*Ledger — Q3. Manifests, original vs. filed. Correspondence, Halvard Shipping. Photographs. Testimony (signed). Deed.*

My hands stopped working. That's the honest truth of it—they simply stopped, the way an engine stops, and Ferro had to take the packet out of my grip before I dropped it.

"Read the top one," Voss said quietly. "The testimony. Read who signed it."

I peeled back the oilcloth. The paper inside was cold enough to burn, and it was covered edge to edge in Maren's handwriting, dense and furious, and at the bottom, eleven signatures. Eleven names. And beneath them, in letters she'd pressed hard enough to tear the page:

*If we do not survive the night, let it be known: the Meridian was lost because Halvard Shipping loaded her hold with unstable blasting agent under false manifest, doubling the insured value and saving themselves a second voyage. The crew objected. The company overruled us. What happened next was not weather, and it was not our captain's error, and it was not the fault of any soul aboard.*

Below that, dated the day of the sinking, a final line:

*My brother will believe it was his fault. He always takes the weight that isn't his. Someone make him read this.*

* * *

I sat down on a crate of frozen blasting agent and read my sister's handwriting until the words blurred, and nobody hurried me, and somewhere far above us the glacier screamed and nobody hurried me then either.

Twenty years. Twenty years I had built my whole life on the foundation of a single certainty: that I had argued with her the night before she sailed, that I'd told her the Meridian was a coffin with a paint job, that I'd dared her to prove me wrong—and that the mountain had taken my dare literally. Every blacklist, every border crossed at night, every mile I'd measured instead of years—all of it had been penance for a sin I had invented, alone, at sixteen, because grief needs a shape and guilt is the easiest shape there is.

She'd known. Even drowning, even welding a hold shut around a cargo that was already cooking off, she'd known I would blame myself. She'd written a letter to whoever survived and addressed it to *someone*, because she couldn't trust me to ever open her journal.

And I hadn't. Twenty years, page one, dog-eared.

"Coral." Ilsa's voice, low. "The deed."

Ferro handed me the last packet. Inside was a document in a leather sleeve, and it was not what the legends said either. Not a shipping fortune. Not silver. It was land—a valley, upriver from the coast, forty hectares of timber and riverfront that my grandfather had bought with the last money he had, deeded jointly to his two grandchildren, Maren and Coral Delacroix, with a clause in my grandfather's cramped hand at the bottom:

*To be claimed only together, or by the survivor who has learned what the other knew.*

"What does that mean?" Tam asked. "Learned what the other knew?"

"It means he knew about the cargo," Voss said. His voice had gone very flat. "The old man. He was Halvard's accountant before the bankruptcy. He knew what they shipped and he got out and he bought land with hush money and he put his granddaughter on that ship anyway because Halvard offered her a wage no honest company would pay a woman her age." He laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "I spent fifteen years tracking that deed. Fifteen years thinking the Delacroix inheritance was the payoff. And it was here the whole time, sitting next to the evidence that would have burned Halvard Shipping to the waterline, and nobody ever filed it."

"Why not?" Ilsa asked. But I think she knew. I think they all did.

"Because filing it meant admitting what the hold contained," Voss said. "Meant the inquiry reopening. Meant every surviving crewman questioned again. Meant—" he looked at me, and for once didn't flinch, "—meant telling you."

Above us the glacier gave a long, tearing groan, and a fine snow of ice crystals sifted down through the breach, catching in our headlamps like static.

* * *

Here is the thing I understood in that moment, sitting in my dead sister's office at the bottom of a dying glacier, with the salvage fleet's torches probably already cutting through the seaward hull and Bo limping somewhere behind us and six tides become five become four:

There was treasure in this room after all. Two kinds of it, and only time to carry out one.

The deed was worth more than the ship. Forty hectares of riverfront, plus whatever the salvage rights to the Meridian herself might fetch—the lawyers could argue that number for a decade. With the deed in hand I could walk out of these mountains rich, buy back a name, hire the best counsel, end the blacklists with money instead of truth. Fast. Clean. No inquiry, no reopened wound, no years of testimony and appeals while Halvard's successors—because there are always successors; companies are the true immortal species—lawyered the evidence into dust.

Or I could carry out the packets.

"Coral," Voss said, and there was something new in his voice, something almost gentle, and I hated it. "The fleet's backers have men on the ice right now. If they reach this hold first, the evidence burns and the deed goes to them and you get neither. Take the deed. I'll carry the archive. We split the weight and we run."

"And when their lawyers get the archive off you between here and the coast?"

"I'm many things," Voss said. "I am not theirs. You saw me side with you against them in the corridor. Ask yourself why I've been chasing this wreck for fifteen years and never sold what I knew to Halvard's people, though God knows they offered."

I asked myself. And the answer, arriving late as always, was simple: because Sable Voss had been Maren's crewmate. Because he had watched her weld that hold shut. Because he had carried the same twenty years I had, except he'd carried them knowing the truth and powerless to prove it, and that, I decided, was the heavier load.

But that wasn't the choice. The choice was quieter than Voss, colder than the room.

The choice was this: my whole life I had traveled light. One pack, one route, one name I barely used. I had told myself it was freedom, and it was actually fear—the fear of carrying anything that could be taken, anything that could sink, anything I could fail the way I'd failed her. The deed was the old life. The deed was walking away with value in my pocket and the lie still standing.

Maren hadn't died for the deed. She hadn't even died for the silver that wasn't there. She'd died for eleven signatures on a piece of paper—for the truth, physically, in her own hand, weighted down with oilcloth so it would survive the water. She had chosen, in her last hour, what was worth preserving.

So I chose it too.

"We take the archive," I said. "All of it. Copies exist?" I looked at Ilsa.

"Three sets," she said. "Ferro photographs everything tonight, wherever we camp. Tam carries the originals in the waterproof drum. I keep the negatives on my body." She was already repacking, fast and sure, and I understood that she'd made the same calculation I had before I'd finished making it. That's why she navigates. She reads routes before other people see roads.

"The deed stays in the hold," I said.

Voss stared at me. "You're joking."

"My grandfather wrote a condition on it. *Claimed together, or by the survivor who has learned what the other knew.*" I folded the packet closed. "I know what she knew now. But I'm not claiming anything today, on a glacier that's actively trying to kill us, with a fleet behind us. The deed keeps. Ice kept it twenty years; it can keep another month. And if Halvard's successors find it—" I met Voss's eyes, "—it's worthless without the archive. You said so yourself. The evidence is the key. So the evidence comes out, and the lock stays buried."

For a long moment Voss said nothing. Then he did something I had never seen him do in thirty years of rivalry: he nodded slowly, like a man conceding a point, and it cost him visibly, and he paid it anyway.

"My backers will burn this ship looking for that deed," he said.

"Then let's make sure they're burning it while we're already over the horizon."

* * *

We moved. That's the whole of the next ten minutes—we moved, four people and a drum of paper, through corridors of frost and dark, past the boot standing its eternal watch, out through the ugly torch-cut breach into air that tasted like rock flour and ending. Behind us the Meridian sat in her melting cradle with her secret half-emptied, and I did not look back, because I had finally learned the difference between looking back and going back.

The glacier calved while we were still on the ice shelf below the wall—a chunk the size of a chapel shearing off and hitting the water with a sound I felt in my teeth rather than heard. The wave lifted the shelf under our feet like a slow breath. Tam fell; Ferro and Ilsa had him up before I'd finished turning around. Nobody spoke. We ran.

It was at the top of the moraine ridge, lungs full of broken glass, that Ilsa grabbed my arm and pointed down the valley.

Lanterns. Six of them, maybe eight, moving along the channel ice below us—fleet scouts, moving fast, moving toward the breach in the hull. And at their head, walking with a lantern held high like a man reading a map by it, a figure in a grey coat that I recognized even at that distance, because some silhouettes you'd know across twenty years and a frozen sea.

Halvard's people hadn't sent hired men.

They'd sent the man who signed the false manifest. The name was in Maren's ledger—I'd read it an hour ago by stove-light, pressed hard enough to tear the page.

He was heading straight for the hold. And the deed was still inside.
