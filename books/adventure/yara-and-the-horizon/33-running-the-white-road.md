## 33. Running the White Road

The first sound was not the ice. It was Coral.

"MOVE!" he bellowed, and his voice cracked across the white road like a whip, and every one of us obeyed before our minds caught up with our legs.

I had been walking beside Bo, one hand hovering near his elbow because his ankle had gone bad an hour ago and he was pretending it hadn't. Then the glacier behind us spoke—a sound I will carry to my grave, a groan so deep it wasn't sound at all but pressure, the mountain clearing its throat—and Coral turned and saw the calving wall let go.

I didn't look back. That was the discipline of it. You train your whole life not to look at the thing chasing you, because the thing chasing you is enormous and looking at it steals the half-second you need to live. But I heard it. A wall of ice the size of a church shearing off the glacier face and hitting the water, and the water answering with a roar that came up through our boots.

"White road, single file, GO!" Ilsa's voice now, ahead of us, her arm swinging like a signal flag. "Ferro, Bo, center! Tam, rear!"

We ran.

* * *

Let me tell you what the white road was, because the name makes it sound gentler than it was. It was a spine of old ice, maybe forty meters across in its best places, maybe six in its worst, running between open water on our left and a field of shattered seracs on our right. It was the only way off this side of the glacier before the whole route sealed or shattered, and now the calving season had opened early, and the road was dying under our feet.

The ice groaned as we ran. Not the deep tectonic groan of the calving wall—that was behind us, a sound with distance in it now—but a higher sound, a cracking, a series of small reports like someone snapping kindling under the surface. Every crack was a question. The road answered some of them by holding. The others it answered by opening.

"Crack left!" Tam shouted from the rear, and we all swerved right without breaking stride, because Tam had spent twenty years reading ice and we had spent six days learning to trust him absolutely.

Bo was falling behind. I could hear it in his breathing—the wet, hitching pull of a man whose ankle had gone from bad to structural failure somewhere in the last ten minutes. He wasn't complaining. Bo never complained; it was the one flaw in his character, if you could call courage a flaw. He was just slowing, and slowing, and the road behind us was closing like a zipper.

"Ferro." His voice, low, next to my ear. "Leave me. Take the case."

The case. Maren's evidence archive, wrapped in sailcloth and strapped to Coral's chest like a second heart. Bo had carried it for two hours while Coral scouted ahead, and now he was offering it back to me like a man settling his accounts.

"Shut up," I said. "You're not dying. You're limping. There's a difference and I'm the one who gets to name it."

"Ferro—"

"I said shut up and run."

He ran. For another two hundred meters he ran, and I ran beside him with my hand under his arm, taking more of his weight than he knew, and I thought: this is the job. Not the medicine. This. The arithmetic of hands. Every hand matters. Every hand that holds another hand is a hand that isn't holding a rope, isn't carrying a case, isn't free—but it's also a hand that keeps a man on the road, and a man on the road is worth more than a free man in the water.

Then Bo's ankle finished its argument with gravity, and he went down.

* * *

The ice where he fell had gone rotten—old blue ice shot through with melt channels, the kind that looks solid and lies. His boot punched through a crust the width of a dinner plate and his leg torqued, and I heard him make a sound I'd heard once before, on a mountain in Karakoram country, from a man whose femur had folded.

"Tam! Bo's down!"

Tam was back before the echo faded. That was Tam. Sixty-one years old, a rigging man blacklisted for a crane collapse that wasn't his fault, and he moved like a man half his age when the arithmetic demanded it. He dropped to his knees beside Bo, hands already moving, already reading the leg the way he read ice.

"Not the bone," Tam said. "Tendon. Maybe ligament. He walks on it, he loses the leg. He doesn't walk on it—" He looked up at me. "He doesn't walk."

Behind us, the calving wall roared again. A second collapse. The sound came up through the road and I felt it in my teeth, in the fillings I'd put in my own mouth in a border town in '09 because there was no one else to do it.

"Carry him," Coral said. He'd come back too—of course he'd come back, he was Coral, the man who measured his whole life in miles so he'd never have to measure it in years, and he was standing over us with the case strapped to his chest and the wind tearing at his hood. "Ferro, you take the shoulders. Tam, the legs. Ilsa, slow the pace, give us a corridor. Bo—"

"I know," Bo said through his teeth. "Arms in. Don't fight the carry. I've carried people, I know how it works."

"Then you know the worst part," Coral said. "It's the carried man who has to trust."

* * *

We lifted him. He was heavier than he looked—riggers always are, all that dense working muscle—and lighter than he should have been, because six days of rations had been coming out of all of us. His arms went around my neck and his breath came hot and ragged against my ear, and I felt him do the hardest thing I'd ever watched a man do: he went limp. He gave us his whole weight. He trusted.

We ran.

The road narrowed. Ilsa called it out ahead of us—"Twelve meters! Ten!"—and I understood what she wasn't saying, which was that the road was being eaten from both sides, water on the left where the swells from the calving were slapping up over the ice lip, broken serac field on the right where the shocks had toppled whole towers of ice into rubble. We were running down a corridor that was closing behind us and crumbling beside us, and somewhere ahead—I had to believe it was ahead—was the far shore and the bridge and the sixth tide.

The sixth tide. Ilsa had said the window closed with the sixth tide. We were in the fifth tide now, the final usable one, and the tide didn't care that Bo couldn't walk. The tide didn't care about anything. That was the first thing the mountains taught you and the last thing you learned to accept: the mountain is not against you. The mountain is not with you. The mountain is.

"Crack ahead!" Tam, from behind us now, watching the road we'd already crossed die. "Wide one! Jump it!"

I jumped it carrying half a man. I don't remember the jump. I remember landing, and the jolt going through Bo, and Bo not crying out—swallowing it, I felt him swallow it against my neck—and the crack behind us widening as we watched, ten centimeters, twenty, until it was a seam and then a gap and then a piece of our road simply tilting away into the water and gone.

"Faster," Coral said. Not shouting anymore. Quiet, which was worse. Quiet meant he was doing the math too.

* * *

Here is what I have never told anyone, and I tell it now only because the road was dying under us and dying men and dying roads make honest company: I sent the ping.

Day three. Bo's fever was climbing and I couldn't break it with what was in my kit, and I made the call every medic dreads—signal for help and risk the trace, or stay silent and risk the man. I sent the ping. It was traced. It's how the fleet found our route. It's how we got here, on a dying road, carrying a dying man.

Coral knows. I told him at the night camp under the calving wall, and he looked at me for a long moment and then he said, "You traded the route for Bo's life. That's not betrayal. That's triage." And then he said the thing that undid me: "I'd have made the same call. I'd have hated myself for it. But I'd have made it."

So when Bo's weight sagged against my neck and I felt him fading—not unconscious, not yet, but going somewhere inward where the pain couldn't follow—I carried him with the particular ferocity of a man paying a debt. Every hand matters. My hand most of all, because my hand was the one that had set this whole catastrophe in motion, and if the road wanted a price, it could take my strength and my breath and whatever was left of my pride, but it was not going to take Bo.

"Talk to me," I said into his ear. "Bo. Stay with me. Tell me about the boat."

"The—what boat?"

"Your boat. The one you're going to build. You told Tam about it at the night camp. Tell me."

A wet laugh against my neck. "You were listening."

"We were all listening. It was a good boat."

"Sloop," he said. His voice was slurring with pain but the word came out clear, because it was a word he'd been building in his head for years. "Thirty-two feet. Cedar on oak. I drew her on the back of a cargo manifest in a prison hospital in Valdez and the nurse stole the drawing and I drew her again. I'm going to name her—"

The road lurched.

* * *

Not a crack this time. A tilt. The whole white road, the spine of the glacier, the only way out, leaned to the left like a ship taking water, and the water came over the lip in a gray sheet and went across our boots, and Ilsa's voice came back to us thin and hard through the wind:

"RIGHT SIDE! Get to the right edge! The left half is going!"

We went. Tam and I with Bo between us, Coral ahead with the case, and behind us—I heard it, I will always hear it—the left half of the road let go. Not all at once. In slabs. Great blue slabs the size of barn doors peeling off and dropping into the water with a sound like the world's largest door closing, one after another, a drumroll of endings, and the wake from each one slapped the remaining road and shook it.

We were on six meters of ice now. Maybe five. Running on a ridge with the sea on one side and ruin on the other, and Bo's weight on my shoulders, and my lungs full of broken glass.

"Ferro." Coral, ahead, half-turned, running backward for three strides to face us. "How is he?"

"Alive. Shocky. Pulse is fast but it's talking to me."

"The leg?"

"The leg is his problem tomorrow."

Coral nodded once, and I saw something cross his face that I'd only seen once before—at the crevasse, when Bo went in the first time, when we spent six hours and nearly lost him. It wasn't fear. It was decision. Coral Delacroix, blacklisted from two countries, the man who had spent twenty years being invisible, looked at the road ahead and at the man on my shoulders and made a decision I could read from three meters away:

Nobody gets left on this road.

"Then we run," he said. "All of us. Every hand."

* * *

Every hand. I want to write that down properly, because it was the whole truth of the next hour and I don't trust myself to have seen it clearly, but I don't think I'm wrong.

Tam carried Bo's legs for a kilometer and a half and then Ilsa came back from the point—against every rule of route leadership, against her own doctrine, she left the front—and took a turn, because her shoulders were fresh and Tam's were shaking. Coral took the case off his own chest and strapped it across Bo's, so the weight sat on the carried man where it would do the least damage and the most good, because Bo had asked for it twice and Coral had heard him. And me—I carried the shoulders, the whole way, because I was the one who had put us here and because Bo's breath against my ear was the only vital sign I had, and I was not giving it up.

Four people, one stretcher of arms, and a road that was dying faster than we could cross it.

The calving wall behind us had gone from roar to continuous thunder. The whole face of the glacier was letting go now, the season opening all at once instead of in stages, the mountain doing in an hour what it usually did in a month, and the swells came down the channel in sets—three big ones, then a pause, then three more—and each set came over the lip of the road and ran across the ice and found the cracks and widened them.

Ilsa called the sets like a surf reader. "Wave! Off the ice—up on your toes, let it pass under you!" And we'd stop, all four of us, Bo between us, and let the gray sheet rush past our boots, and then we'd run again.

On the fourth set, the wave didn't pass under us. It took a slab from the right edge, a piece the size of a truck, and the road went from five meters to three, and Tam, bringing up the rear, had to jump a gap that opened under his own feet, and he made it—I saw him make it, I will testify to it forever—but he made it badly, and he came down on one knee, and when he stood up he was favoring the other leg.

"Tam," I said.

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. Give me the legs."

"You carry the shoulders and the legs and I'll carry your doubt," Tam said, and limped, and kept limping, and did not fall, and that was Tam all the way down to the bone: a man who had been blamed for a collapse once and had decided, somewhere deep, that he would never be the reason anything fell again.

* * *

The bridge came into view at what I judged—badly, my sense of time had gone to porridge—to be the last hour of the fifth tide.

It was worse than Ilsa's chart had promised. A single span of ice, maybe thirty meters, arcing between the white road's end and the black rock of the far shore, and it was thin—I could see the water through it in places, a green-gray glow, the light coming up from underneath. And on the far shore, small with distance but unmistakable, figures. Voss's team. Already across. Already waiting.

And behind us, the road.

The calving had reached the stretch we'd crossed four minutes ago. I turned once—just once, discipline be damned—and saw it: the white road unspooling behind us like a ribbon being cut, section after section dropping into the channel, and the wall of spray and ice dust where the calving front was advancing, and I understood with total clarity that the mountain was not chasing us. The mountain didn't know we were here. We were simply standing in the place where the mountain had decided to end, and the end was traveling at the speed of falling ice.

"Ferro!" Coral. "How far can Bo go?"

I looked down at the man on my shoulders. His eyes were open. Somewhere in the last kilometer he had come back from wherever he'd gone, and he was looking at the bridge, and he was doing his own arithmetic.

"Put me down," Bo said.

"No."

"Put me down. I'll hop it. Arm over your shoulder, Ferro. I can hop thirty meters."

"You'll tear the leg off the bone."

"Then I'll hop on the bone that's left." His grip tightened on my neck. "Every hand matters, right? That's the rule. So use my hand. I can't run. I can hop. Put me down."

I looked at Coral. Coral looked at Bo, and then at the bridge, and then at the wall of ice dust coming down the channel behind us, and I watched him weigh a man's leg against a man's life and find, to my lasting relief, that he refused to trade.

"Bridge first," Coral said. "We carry you to the rock. Then Ferro splints you and you hop or you crawl or you bite the rock, but you cross the bridge carried or you don't cross it at all. Nobody crosses that ice alone. Nobody."

* * *

We ran the last stretch with the thunder at our backs and the green glow under our feet growing brighter, and the bridge rose to meet us, thin as a held breath, and Ilsa went first because she always went first when the ground was lying to us, testing each step with her axe, calling the safe line: "Left! Two steps left! Now straight—don't look down—STRAIGHT!"

Tam crossed. Then me, with Bo, his arms around my neck, his weight forward against my back so we'd fall toward rock and not water if the ice spoke. Then Coral, last, the case against his chest, because Coral was always last; he'd been last the whole expedition, the man who checked the count, and I heard him behind me on the bridge saying the count under his breath like a prayer: "Ilsa. Tam. Ferro. Bo. Four. Four. Four."

And then rock. Real rock, black and wet and gloriously immovable, under my boots, and we laid Bo down against a boulder and I had the splint out of my kit before I'd fully registered that we'd made it, hands moving on autopilot, and behind us the white road finished its long argument with the sea.

The last of it went while I was tying off the splint. The whole remaining span, the road we'd run, the road we'd carried a man down—it calved all at once, a final groan and a roar and a wall of spray, and then there was no road. There was the bridge, thirty meters of thinning ice between us and the far shore where Voss stood watching, and there was nothing else. No way back. No way but across.

"Everyone alive?" Coral said. "Count."

"Ilsa."

"Tam."

"Ferro. Bo's alive. Pulse is strong. Leg's a mess but the leg was always the negotiable part."

"Four," Coral said. "Four."

Bo was looking at the bridge. His face was gray with pain and white with something else, and he said, quietly, so only those of us close could hear: "The ice on that span's been taking spray the whole time. It's thinner than it looks. Look at the middle—see how it's sagging?"

We looked. He was right. The bridge's midsection had a dip in it, a shallow green smile, the ice flexing under its own weight and the swell beneath.

"Someone still has to cross it," Ilsa said. "We're not done. Voss is over there, and the fleet's landing on the seaward side, and the sixth tide is the last window, and—" She stopped.

Because we all heard it at the same time. From the seaward side, around the headland, a sound we knew from the fleet smoke on the horizon days ago: engines. And a shout, thin across the water—a voice we recognized, a voice that had spent twenty years being Coral's rival and one day being something else.

Voss was on the far shore. And Voss was running back toward the bridge.

"Voss!" Coral was on his feet at the edge of the rock, hands cupped around his mouth. "VOSS! The middle span is failing—wait for the swell to pass—"

But Voss was already on the ice, running, and behind him on the headland his own backers were shouting at him to stop, and the bridge dipped and flexed under him like a living thing, and the sixth tide was coming in.

"Every hand," I said, to no one, to everyone. "Every hand matters."

Coral was already pulling rope off his shoulder.
