## 34. The Bridge Goes

The calving started as a sound I felt before I heard it—a deep, intestinal groan rolling up through the ice beneath my boots, like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Then the glacier face, a kilometer behind us, began to shed itself into the sea.

We were running. All of us. Bo on Ferro's shoulder, his leg wrapped in a splint made from a tent pole and two ratchet straps, his face grey but conscious. Tam hauling the evidence case against her chest like it was a child. Ilsa out front, reading the white road the way she'd read it all voyage—by faith and mathematics in equal measure.

And Voss twenty meters ahead of me, because of course he was. Even now. Even running for his life from the people who'd paid him, he moved like a man leading a race nobody else had agreed to enter.

"Bridge!" Ilsa's voice cracked across the ice. "Single span, sixty meters! We go one at a time—no, we go *fast*, two at a time, spacing! It won't hold weight standing still!"

The bridge was exactly what it sounded like: a tongue of ice that had survived the fracturing, arched across open black water where the route had already begun to die. On the far side, the shore rose in a ramp of rubble ice toward the high ground where the boats waited. Behind us, the sound of the glacier giving birth got louder.

I looked at that water and my body understood what my mind refused to say: cold like that doesn't kill you slowly. Cold like that takes you the way you take a breath.

"Voss first," Ilsa said. "Then Coral, then Ferro with Bo, then Tam, then me."

"The hell with that order," Tam said.

"It's not sentiment, it's physics. He's lightest on his feet."

Voss didn't argue. That should have warned me. He stepped onto the bridge at a run and the whole span flexed under him—a long, shuddering wave traveling its length, ice singing a note I felt in my teeth—and kept running anyway, arms out, balanced like a man crossing a fallen tree over a canyon because he'd done it a hundred times and expected to do it a hundred more.

He was ten meters from the far side when the glacier face let go.

Not the small stuff—the powder and growling we'd been hearing for an hour. This was the real voice. A section of the calving wall the size of a city block sheared away all at once, and the displacement wave came down the channel toward us like the sea changing its mind.

"OFF THE BRIDGE!" Ilsa screamed.

Ferro shoved Bo flat onto the rubble ice and threw himself over him. Tam dropped the case and flattened beside them. I was still on solid shelf—I'd hesitated half a second too long, watching Voss, always watching Voss—and I saw it happen the way you see a car crash through a windshield: complete, detailed, and entirely beyond your power to stop.

The wave hit the channel.

The bridge didn't break so much as *dissolve*. One moment it was ice; the next it was confetti. The wave lifted the fragments, rolled them, swallowed them, and took Voss with them. I saw him go under—one instant upright and reaching for a handhold that wasn't there anymore, the next gone, the water closing over him with a slap like a door.

The world went very quiet and very simple.

Later, I would try to reconstruct the decision. Ilsa would tell me there was no decision, that I was moving before the wave finished breaking. But that's not true. There was a decision, and it took less than a heartbeat, and here is everything that was inside it:

Twenty years of hating this man. Twenty years of measuring myself against him, of hearing his name in every port like a stone dropped down a well that never hit bottom. Twenty years of blaming him—for surviving, for knowing things about Maren's last night that he never told me, for being the one person alive whose account of the disaster mattered and who hoarded it like a miser.

And underneath all of it, older than the hate, one fact I'd never once let myself think clearly: Sable Voss was the last man to see my sister alive. And for twenty years, some part of me had known that if he ever drowned—if he died out here, in these waters, on this ice—I would never know. The truth would sink with him. Everything Maren had done, everything she'd welded shut in that hold, everything she'd died protecting—it would stay at the bottom of him forever.

So when I ran back toward the edge, I wasn't running to save Sable Voss.

I was running to save the truth. Or that's what I told myself, later, telling the story in warm rooms with a drink in my hand.

The truth is simpler and worse. I ran back because he was in the water, and he was mine—not mine to keep, mine to answer for. Because twenty years ago, when the Meridian went down, he had reached for someone in the dark and missed, and he had carried that miss across two decades the same way I'd carried mine. And because if I stood on that bank and watched the sea take the last person who could testify to what my sister did with her final hour, then I deserved the exile I'd been living in. I deserved every mile of it.

Bo grabbed my harness as I passed. "Coral—"

"I see him!"

I didn't. Not yet. The water was churning with brash ice, white plates grinding together in the swell, any one of them enough to crack a skull or pin a man under. I skidded down the rubble slope to the waterline, ripped the coil off my shoulder, and tied off to a boulder of grounded ice that Tam, without being asked, had thrown his whole weight against.

"CORAL!" Ilsa's voice. "Rope! Thirty meters, that's all you get!"

Thirty meters. The churn zone was forty wide.

Fine. Thirty would do. Thirty had always been enough.

I went in.

* * *

Cold is a country. You cross its border once, and after that you're a citizen whether you want to be or not.

I've been in water this cold exactly twice in my life. The first time was twenty years ago, the night after the Meridian, searching the wreckage line in a survival suit that failed at the seams. I remember floating among debris—luggage, hull fragments, a child's shoe—and feeling nothing, absolutely nothing, which is how I knew I was dying, and not caring, which is how I knew I'd already lost something worse than heat.

This time I felt everything.

The water took my breath like a punch. It squeezed my chest, lit every nerve I owned, and informed me, with total authority, that I had perhaps three minutes of useful movement and ninety seconds of good decisions left. Fine. I'd done more with less.

"VOSS!" My own voice sounded foreign, bounced back at me off the ice plates. "SABLE VOSS!"

Nothing. Swell lifting and dropping the brash around me. Somewhere behind me, on shore, Ilsa screaming my name and the count of seconds—she was counting, of course she was counting, Ilsa counted everything—"TWENTY SECONDS CORAL, TWENTY-FIVE—"

A white plate shifted ahead of me and I saw it.

His hand. Just his hand, fingers hooked over the edge of a floe the size of a dinner table, the rest of him under it, held down by gear and by the drag of his soaked parka trying to become an anchor. His knuckles were already the wrong color.

I swam. Three strokes. Got a fist in his collar and hauled, and he came up gasping in a way I'll hear for the rest of my life—that raw, drowning-man intake, the sound of a body renegotiating with the world—and his eyes found mine and they were clear. Terrified, freezing, but clear. And in them, for one bare second, was something I'd never seen in twenty years of rivalry:

He was glad it was me.

"Don't talk," I said. "Breathe. Kick."

He kicked. Between us we got his torso onto the floe, and then the problem became arithmetic: two men, one slab of ice barely buoyant enough for one, a rope trailing thirty meters behind me through grinding brash, and a swell that wanted the whole arrangement broken up and distributed along the coast.

"On three," I said. "We roll. Both of us, onto your side, weight low."

"You'll capsize us."

"On *three*, Sable."

Something crossed his face—recognition, maybe, that I'd used his first name for the first time in our entire acquaintance—and he set his jaw and nodded.

"One. Two." I hooked my arm through his harness. "Three."

We rolled. The floe groaned, dipped, shipped a sheet of water over both of us—and held. Barely. We lay there cheek to cheek on the ice like brothers sharing a bed in winter, breathing fog at the sky, while the rope went taut behind us and Ilsa and Tam hauled with everything they had, walking us across the churn zone hand over hand, the floe grinding and spinning beneath us, until the brash thinned and the near-shore rubble rose around us and hands—Ferro's, Bo's leaning on one leg, Tam's—pulled us out of the water and onto the shelf.

I came to on my back with Ferro stripping my parka and someone forcing a heat pack against my throat. The sky was doing something complicated above me. The glacier was still roaring. And Sable Voss was lying two feet away, being handled roughly by Tam, coughing seawater and laughing—actually laughing, a broken, wheezing, disbelieving sound.

"You absolute *idiot*," Tam was saying to both of us, in the tone she saved for men she'd decided not to let die. "You absolute—thirty seconds more and we'd have been fishing out two bodies—"

"He'd have done it," Voss said. Coughed. Said it again, to me, directly, his voice wrecked. "You'd have done it. For anyone. That's the whole— that's why she—" He stopped himself. Looked away.

"Why she what?" My teeth were chattering too hard for the question to sound as hard as I meant it.

But Voss just shook his head, and Ferro was wrapping me in the emergency bivvy, and Ilsa was already turning to the fleet's lights—because yes, they'd landed, their ships were in the channel behind us, small and patient and coming—and the moment folded itself away like a letter half-read.

* * *

They walked us the last stretch to the boats. I don't remember much of it. I remember Bo insisting on walking unaided, out of pride, and Ferro allowing it because Ferro understood that some kinds of medicine are administered by letting a man limp. I remember the evidence case riding safe in Tam's grip, dented, intact. I remember Ilsa falling into step beside me and saying nothing for a long time, and then saying, quietly:

"That was the stupidest thing I've ever watched anyone do."

"Yes."

"We have a rule against it."

"Yes."

"Good rule." She looked straight ahead. "Keep it."

And then, because she was Ilsa: "Your thirty meters weren't enough. You went past them. You always go past them. Someone has to be holding the rope when you do, and I'm telling you now, it's going to be me, every time, so get used to it."

I didn't trust my voice. I put my hand on her shoulder instead, briefly, and she allowed it, the way you allow weather.

At the boats, wrapped in foil and blankets, steam rising off all of us like we were the ones calving, Voss sat down heavily next to me on the gunwale. For a while neither of us spoke. The Meridian's channel was sealing behind us—we could hear it, the ice shifting, the route that had existed for six tides closing like a book.

"Twenty years ago," Voss said finally, staring at the water, "in the dark, on that ship, I reached for someone and missed. Your sister. She pushed eleven of us through a hatch and the deck tilted and I grabbed for her hand and got her sleeve and it tore." His voice stayed flat, recitation-flat, the way a man talks when feeling would drown him. "I've been reaching for her sleeve for twenty years, Delacroix. Every job. Every salvage. Every fortune I chased. I thought if I got rich enough, powerful enough, I could buy the past back and fix the reach."

He turned and looked at me, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady.

"And today you jumped into freezing water for the man who missed. So either you're a fool, or—" his mouth twisted, not quite a smile, "—or the debt ran the other way all along, and neither of us knew it."

"It's not settled," I said.

"No," he agreed. "It's not. Because there's something I haven't told you. Something about the cargo manifest—about what the company was really carrying in that hold, and why they'll burn coastal towns to keep it quiet. And the people chasing us—" he nodded back at the fleet lights, closer now, "—they're not here for gold, Delacroix. They're here because your sister's archive names names. Very high ones. And one of those names is on a boat in that fleet, arriving within the hour."

The ice groaned behind us. The last window was closing.

"Who?" I said.

Voss pulled the blanket tighter and looked out at the approaching lights, and for the first time since I'd known him, Sable Voss looked afraid.

"Get everyone in the boats," he said. "Now."
