## 35. Sixth Tide: Last Window

The water was rising when we reached the last stretch of the white road, and it rose the way a debt collector knocks—patiently at first, then all at once.

I had stopped counting the hours somewhere behind the calving wall. What I counted instead was the sound of the ice: a low groan that never quite stopped, like a ship settling in its grave, and beneath it the tick and crack of smaller failures, hundreds of them, the glacier shedding its skin one fracture at a time. The Meridian's tomb was opening whether we liked it or not, and every piece of it that fell into the sea narrowed the road we were standing on.

"Two hundred meters to the shelf," Ilsa called from the front. Her voice carried flat and clear over the ice, stripped down to its working parts. She hadn't raised her voice once since the calving started. She didn't need to. Fear had already done that for all of us; what she offered now was something better than volume. "Then forty meters of shingle above the tideline. After that, rock."

"And after that?" Bo asked. He was walking between Tam and Ferro, his left leg bound tight under the improvised splint Ferro had built from a tent pole and two ratchet straps, his weight distributed across their shoulders in a three-legged shuffle that would have looked absurd any other day. Today it looked like the only honest picture of us I'd ever seen.

"After that," Ilsa said, "the world."

* * *

We had come out of the hold's last secret with less than we'd gone in for and more than we'd ever imagined carrying. No gold. No chests of coin. Maren's archive—waterproof tubes of documents, photographs, a ledger with the shipping company's own stamp on pages that should never have existed—and the family deed, folded into oilcloth, riding against my ribs where Voss had wanted it and where, in the end, he'd told me to keep it. *You'll need it more than I will,* he'd said, which was either generosity or penance or both, and I hadn't had time to ask which.

Voss walked twenty meters behind me. Not because I'd asked him to. Because after the bridge went, after I'd hauled him out of the black water with his coat frozen to my fists, neither of us had found a way to arrange ourselves differently. Twenty years of measuring, and now there was nothing left to measure. Just distance. Just the road.

The tide was the problem. Sixth tide—the last window, Ilsa had said back when windows were still a thing you could plan around. The route through the ice fields existed only in the hours when the sea pulled back far enough to expose the causeway of old moraine and pressure ridges that ran from the wreck site to the mainland shore. On the seventh tide, the route would seal. Not close—seal. The calving season would finish what the winter had started, the whole face of the glacier would let go in slabs the size of city blocks, and everything we had crossed in six days would become open water and floating wreckage.

Behind us, as if to demonstrate, the glacier spoke.

It wasn't a groan this time. It was a shot—a crack so sharp and total that I felt it in my teeth before I heard it in my ears—and then the long thunder of something enormous deciding to be somewhere else. I turned. Halfway down the calving wall, a seam had opened, white flesh parting along a blue line, and a section of the ice face the size of a cathedral leaned out over the bay with the slow, terrible patience of a thing that has all the time in the world and no interest in ours.

"Move," Ilsa said. Not loudly. But everyone moved.

* * *

The white road had been wide enough for four abreast when we came in. Now it was a lane. Chunks of calved ice lay scattered across it like the debris of some ancient battle, and meltwater ran in channels along its spine, and at its edges the sea lapped up in long gray tongues that licked higher each time we looked. The air smelled of salt and cold stone and, faintly, of the kerosene from Voss's fleet ships, still anchored off the wreck site behind us, their crews presumably watching us go and calculating, in the way of men who work for money, whether the documents in our packs were worth dying for.

They weren't. That was the strange arithmetic of it. Voss's backers had wanted the deed, the salvage rights, the leverage. What they'd gotten instead was a man they'd paid to find treasure handing the truth to maritime authorities, a glacier eating their prize, and a rival they now owed their lives to. Somewhere behind us, lawyers were no doubt already drafting the paperwork that would make all of that someone else's fault. The sea didn't care. The sea just kept coming.

"Ferro." I dropped back to walk beside her. Bo was on her other side, sweating despite the cold, his face the color of old candle wax. "How is he?"

"He's fine."

"Ferro."

She glanced at me. Ferro's face was built for poker and field surgery, and right now it was doing neither. "He's not fine. He's ambulatory. There's a difference, and I'm choosing to believe in it until we reach rock." She adjusted the strap across Bo's chest. "If the leg goes septic, I'll tell you. Until then, let him pretend, because pretending is the only medicine keeping his pace up."

"I'm right here," Bo said.

"You're cargo," Tam said cheerfully, from the other side. "Beautiful, complaining cargo."

Bo laughed—a real laugh, cracked and breathless—and I understood in that moment why people climb mountains in companies rather than alone. It wasn't safety. It was that laughter sounds different at altitude, thinner and truer, like a bell rung in an empty church. You can't fake it up here. You can't fake anything up here. The mountain strips you down to whatever you actually are, and what we actually were, all seven of us, was tired and frightened and still moving.

Seven. I did the count again, the way I'd been doing it every ten minutes since the bridge. Coral. Ilsa. Tam. Bo. Ferro. Voss. And Voss's remaining man, Ketch, who had stood on the seaward side of the collapsing span and watched his employer go into the water and then watched me go in after him, and who had said nothing since except "rope" and "here" and "yes"—a vocabulary I found increasingly trustworthy.

Seven alive. One bridge dead. One glacier dying. One sister twenty years drowned and one week vindicated.

I kept walking.

* * *

The seam in the calving wall let go while we were still eight hundred meters from the shelf.

There was no warning shot this time. The cathedral-sized slab simply finished its decision, and the sound it made leaving the wall was not thunder but something older and wetter, a vast exhalation, and then the displacement wave hit the causeway.

Ilsa felt it before any of us—felt it through her boots, in the vibration of the ice, the way she always could, the navigator's sixth sense that had gotten us across five tides of ground no chart admitted existed. "Wave!" she shouted, and this time there was volume, and this time volume was correct. "Off the road! High ground! NOW!"

There was no high ground. There was a pressure ridge to our left, a humped spine of ancient ice maybe three meters tall, and we went at it like animals, hands and boots, Tam shoving Bo up ahead of himself, Ferro climbing with Bo's pack and her own, Ketch going up the ridge like it was a ladder he'd been born on. I got Ilsa's elbow in my palm and hauled, and we came over the crest together just as the water arrived.

I will not describe the wave accurately, because I don't think language holds it. It was not tall—it was perhaps two meters where it struck the causeway—but it was *heavy*, the whole bay shrugging, and it went over the white road like a hand smoothing a bedsheet. When it withdrew, dragging gravel and ice chips hissing back into the gray, the road was still there. Mostly. A gap had opened thirty meters ahead of us, ten meters wide, black water churning in it, and beyond the gap the road continued toward the shelf, and beyond the shelf the shingle, and beyond the shingle the rock, and beyond the rock everything.

Nobody spoke. The gap spoke for itself.

"Tide turns in ninety minutes," Ilsa said. Her eyes were on the water in the gap, reading it. "When it turns, that fills. When the calving really starts, that becomes a strait. We cross now or we write letters home."

"With what?" Tam asked. He was looking at our rope. Our rope was short. Our rope had been sacrificed, in pieces, to the crevasse rescue and the traverse and the bridge, and what remained was forty meters of good line and a prayer.

"Not with rope," Ilsa said. She pointed. "With that."

*That* was a grounded floe—a slab of calved ice, house-sized, wedged against the near lip of the gap, tilted like a door ajar. It was exactly the wrong shape and exactly the right position, and I watched her mind work across it the way she read charts, and I hated her for it, and I loved her for it, and I said, "That's a coffin with ambitions."

"It's a bridge with a personality disorder," she agreed. "One at a time. Weight low. Fast. Coral first—he's lightest on his feet and he can test the far lip. Then Ferro and Tam with Bo. Ketch. Me last—I'm the best swimmer, and if it goes with someone on it, better me than the medic."

"That's not bravery, that's arithmetic," I said.

"Everything worth doing is arithmetic," Ilsa said. "Go."

* * *

The floe held for five of us.

I went first, weight low, boots finding the tilt, the ice flexing under me with a sound like a bowed string, and I leapt the last meter to the far lip and rolled onto solid road and turned and waved the others on. Ferro crossed with Bo half-carried between her and Tam, the floe groaning, dipping, holding. Ketch crossed like water finding its level, effortless, and even managed to look bored doing it, which I decided was a survival trait and not an insult.

Tam went back for the second trip—someone had to carry Bo's share of the archive tubes, and Tam had appointed himself by simply picking them up. He was halfway across when the second wave came.

Smaller than the first. But the floe had already given everything it had, and the wave found the flaw. I saw it happen in the particular slow clarity that disasters grant: the slab tilting, Tam adjusting, adjusting again, the ice rotating under him with terrible gentleness, and then the gray water taking him at the knees, at the waist, at the shoulders, and the archive tube strap sliding off his shoulder into the sea, and Tam's hand slapping the water twice—

—and then Voss.

He was moving before I was. Twenty years my senior, half-frozen, dragged out of a bay an hour ago, and Sable Voss went down the near lip of that gap and into the water after Tam like a man diving for a coin he'd dropped as a boy. He caught the strap. He caught Tam's collar. Ketch and I hauled the line we'd thrown, and the two of them came up the ice together, streaming, gasping, Voss's grip locked on the archive tube like it was a child and Tam was incidental.

"The papers," Voss wheezed, pushing the tube into my hands, "are drier than I am. Someone fix that."

Ferro was already moving with the emergency blanket. Tam sat on the ice coughing bay water and laughing at the same time, which is a sound I hope never to hear again and will never forget. And I knelt there with Maren's evidence dry against my chest and Sable Voss shivering in front of me, and I thought: *this is what she died building.* Not the welding. Not the sealed hold. This. Eleven strangers alive because one person decided the count mattered more than the cost.

"Maren would have liked you," I said to Voss. Stupid thing to say. True thing.

"She'd have hated me," Voss said. "Everyone did. That's how you know she was smart." He let Ferro wrap him. "Get up, Delacroix. Your tide's leaving."

* * *

We crossed the gap on the rope, one at a time, Tyrolean-style off a bollard of ice Ketch cut with a patience I will always associate with him afterward. Ilsa went last, as promised, and when she stepped off the rope onto the far road the floe behind her sighed, settled, and slid into the gap like a coin into a well, and the road behind us was no longer a road at all.

Sixth tide. Last window. And the window was closing at the speed of water.

The final four hundred meters we ran—or shuffled, in Bo's case, at a pace Ferro later described as "aggressive limping"—as the calving wall behind us gave up its pretense of gradualness. Whole battlements of ice were going now, one after another, the reports rolling across the bay like artillery, and each collapse sent its wave chasing up the causeway, and each wave took a little more of the road with it when it left. I ran with Maren's journal in my jacket pocket—I'd been carrying it since the night camp, since Ilsa put it in my hands unread, since page one became page forty became the whole terrible beautiful truth of her—and with every stride I could feel it there, small and square and heavier than the deed, heavier than the tubes, heavier than twenty years.

The shingle came up to meet us. Real ground, loose and gray and gloriously ugly, and then the rock, black and barnacled and permanent, and Ilsa's hand on my shoulder pulling me up onto it, and then—

—we were on the far shore.

I turned around.

The route was sealing. I want to say it happened slowly, majestically, with the dignity of geological time, but it didn't. It happened like a door closing on a bad argument. The waves from the calving wall swept the causeway clean in three passes, and the third pass didn't give the road back. Black water stood where the white road had been, and the floes shifted and ground and resettled into a new arrangement that no chart would ever record, and somewhere under all of it, entombed past any salvage, past any fleet, past any ambition of men, the Meridian settled another degree into the dark and kept her secrets the way Maren had welded them shut: completely.

I stood on the rock and watched the mountain bury my sister for the second time, and this time I let it.

* * *

Ferro called the headcount.

It was her idea, and she did it formally, the way she did everything medical—out loud, touching each of us as she named us, a field ritual I suspect she invented on the spot and would deny inventing forever. "Delacroix." Her palm flat on my sternum, brief, professional, checking a pulse she didn't need to check. "Alive."

"Ilsa."

"Present," Ilsa said, "and insufferably correct about the floe."

"Tam."

"Damp," Tam said, "but alive."

"Bo."

"Alive," Bo said, "and legally entitled to complain about it for the rest of my natural life."

"Ketch."

A nod. Ferro touched his shoulder anyway. He allowed it.

"Voss."

Sable Voss stood apart, wrapped in the emergency blanket, hair frozen into gray spikes, looking younger and older than he had any right to look. "Alive," he said. Then, quieter, almost to himself: "Eleven."

"What?" Ferro said.

"Nothing." He shook his head. "Counting an old debt. Go on."

"Ferro," she said, touching herself last, completing the circle. "Alive. Seven for seven." She looked up at the sky, at the smoke-gray light, at nothing. "Seven for seven," she said again, and her voice did something none of us had heard it do, and none of us mentioned it, because that's what crew means.

Survival as grace. I'd heard the phrase somewhere, years ago, in a church I'd only entered to get warm, and I'd filed it under things other people believed. Standing on that rock with the route sealing behind us and seven heartbeats accounted for, I finally understood it. Grace isn't being spared. Grace is being *counted*. Being one of the number. Being, after everything, still on the list.

Maren was on the list too. She had been the whole time. I just hadn't known how to read it.

* * *

We camped that night on the rock shelf above the tideline, because nobody had the strength to go farther and nobody suggested it. Ferro re-splinted Bo properly. Tam built a stove-fire out of driftwood and the last of the fuel tablets and made tea that tasted like bark and salvation. Ilsa spread her charts on a flat stone and marked the route we'd crossed with a single line and then, after a moment, wrote across it in her small square hand: *CLOSED — DO NOT ATTEMPT — REMEMBERED.*

"Remembered," Tam said. "That's a new classification."

"Some routes deserve maps," Ilsa said. "Some deserve warnings. This one gets both."

Voss sat at the edge of the firelight, outside the circle and inside it at once, and I brought him tea because somebody had to and because I'd decided, somewhere on the white road, that the war was over whether or not peace had been declared. He took the cup with both hands.

"The fleet will pull out tomorrow," he said. "My backers' lawyers will spend a year untangling the insurance claims, and they'll lose, and they'll hate us both, and it won't matter." He blew on the tea. "What will you do with it? The archive."

"File it," I said. "Every page. With the maritime authority at Correnness, first boat that runs."

"They'll drag it out. Committees. Hearings."

"I know."

"You understand your blacklist doesn't lift until they finish."

"I know that too." I looked at the fire. "Twenty years I thought the truth would set me free. Turns out it's slower than that. Turns out it's paperwork."

Voss laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge unused for decades. "Welcome to honesty, Delacroix. It's terribly bureaucratic." He drank. Then he set the cup down and looked at me across the flames, and whatever he said next, he said carefully, the way a man handles something welded shut. "Your sister saved eleven people. I've carried the other number for twenty years—the ones I couldn't reach. You carry the eleven now. It's a better number. Take care of it."

"I will," I said.

And I meant it the way you mean things on the far side of a glacier: completely, permanently, with your whole weight on it.

* * *

I slept without dreaming, or if I dreamed, I dreamed of nothing I remembered, which may be the same thing. And in the gray hour before dawn I woke to find Ilsa sitting at the edge of camp, facing the bay, and I went and sat beside her because that's what you do.

The Meridian's resting place was invisible now—just water, just weather, just the calving wall grinding through its long demolition a kilometer offshore. Somewhere under that gray surface, a ship waited with my sister's welds holding her secrets, and would wait forever, and that was right. Some things are meant to be carried out. Some things are meant to be carried *away from*, and the carrying is the point.

"Correnness ferry runs Thursday," Ilsa said, not looking at me. "I checked the board at the last village. Two days from now."

"Thursday," I said.

"There'll be clerks. Forms. Probably a man who enjoys stamps more than justice." She finally turned, and in the pre-dawn gray her face was the map of everything we'd crossed. "You won't be alone in that room, Coral. Whatever they ask, however long it takes—you won't be alone."

I looked at my crew—Tam and Bo asleep back-to-back like children, Ferro awake as always and pretending not to be, Ketch standing watch nobody had assigned him—and I understood that she was right, and that this, too, was part of the inheritance. Maren had left me the truth. The crew had left me the strength to carry it. And on Thursday morning, in a room that smelled of ink and institutional coffee, I was going to set twenty years of lie down on a government table and walk out the other side of it a free man, or at least a man whose freedom no longer depended on a fiction.

The sun came up over the mainland hills, ordinary and gold, lighting a world that had no idea what had crossed out of its ice.

"Thursday," I said. "Let's go file a miracle."
