## 36. Testimony

The town was called Greywater Landing, which was a lie twice over—the water was green-grey at best, and nothing about it landed so much as clung to the coast like a barnacle that had given up on ambition. Three streets, a cannery that smelled of brine and diesel, a church with a steeple leaning like a man listening hard, and one long wooden building at the end of the harbor road with brass letters bolted above its door:

**MARITIME AUTHORITY — OFFICE OF INQUIRY & RECORDS**

I had walked past buildings like this for twenty years. In Port Callas I'd crossed the street to avoid one. In Halden I'd watched a blacklisted engineer go in with a folder under his arm and come out an hour later with his hands empty and his face older, and I'd told myself then that those doors were where careers went to be weighed and found wanting.

Now I stood in front of them with a waterproof satchel against my ribs, and inside the satchel was everything.

Maren's journal. The passenger manifest she'd copied by hand in the last hour of her life, or near enough—her handwriting went ragged toward the bottom of the page, as if the ship were already tilting under her pen. The cargo declarations showing forty tons of unstabilized ore concentrate listed as "machinery parts." The company telegrams Voss had carried out of the wreck in his own coat, the ones proving the Meridian's owners knew what they were shipping and ordered it shipped anyway. And the photograph of the hold door, welded from the inside, that Tam had taken two days before the glacier took the wreck back.

Eleven names. That was the number Voss gave me, kneeling in a flooding corridor with the ice groaning overhead like something enormous turning over in its sleep. Eleven people who lived because my sister decided, in the dark, with water rising around her ankles, that the hold needed to stay shut—and made sure of it with a welding torch and whatever time she had left.

Twenty years I'd believed she caused it. Twenty years the story had been simpler than the truth, the way stories usually are when they're built to carry weight.

The bell above the office door hadn't been oiled since the last government that cared about anything. It announced me like a crime.

* * *

The clerk's name was Ostergaard, according to the little brass plate on his desk, and he had the complexion of a man who had not seen direct sunlight since childhood. He looked up from his ledger with the expression of someone expecting either a complaint about fishing licenses or a bribe, and got neither.

"I need to file documents," I said. "With the Inquiry Board."

Ostergaard's eyes did a slow inventory: the salt-stiff coat, the rope-burn scars on my hands, the satchel, the four other people dripping on his floor behind me because Ilsa had said we weren't splitting up again until everyone was dry. "Inquiry Board convenes Thursdays," he said. "This is Tuesday."

"Then I'll wait until Thursday."

"That's two days."

"Yes."

He looked at me the way clerks look at men who have clearly never dealt with clerks before. "There are fees for expedited review. There is also a fee for document intake, a fee for witness registration—" he flipped a page "—and if you're filing on behalf of a deceased party, a fee for that too."

"How much?"

"Altogether?" He did arithmetic with his lips moving. "More than you appear to have."

Behind me, Bo snorted. Ferro elbowed him into silence, which lasted approximately four seconds.

"We have salvage rights," Ilsa said, stepping forward. She put the certificate on his desk—that beautiful, absurd piece of paper Voss's own lawyers had drafted in the hours after the calving, the one granting us rights to anything recovered from the Meridian, now a legal fiction attached to a wreck at the bottom of a fjord full of fresh icebergs. "Pending valuation."

Ostergaard picked up the certificate, held it at arm's length, and read it with genuine interest, which was the first honest thing I'd seen him do. "You're the Delacroix party." It wasn't a question. Word travels faster than ships along this coast; the calving had been heard forty miles out to sea, and every harbor between there and here already had its version. In most versions I was dead. "The Meridian. The one from the disaster."

"The one from the disaster," I agreed.

"And you're filing—" he glanced at my satchel again, more carefully now "—what, exactly?"

"Documents," I said. "Concerning the cause of the sinking. Concerning the cargo. Concerning eleven survivors whose survival was never entered into the record." I set the satchel on his desk. It made a sound like a dropped anchor. "And concerning one crew member of the Meridian who was blamed for all of it, whose name has been on your blacklist for twenty years, and who is standing in your office asking you to strike it."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere outside, the cannery whistle blew noon, indifferent.

Ostergaard looked at the satchel, then at me, and something shifted behind his eyes—not sympathy, nothing so warm, but the recognition of a category of paperwork he had almost certainly never processed and would get to process exactly once in his career. Clerks live for this. I understood it suddenly, completely, the way you understand a current once you're in it: somewhere under the dust and the fees, this man had spent thirty years keeping records straight in a world that preferred them bent, and here was a chance to straighten one of the bent ones.

"Intake," he said quietly, pulling a form toward himself. "Witness registration. Deceased-party filing. And a petition for record amendment." He stamped each form as he named it, four small thumps like a heart remembering how. "That's the whole path. No expedite. Thursday."

"Thursday," I said.

"You'll want lodging," Ostergaard added, not looking up. "The Anchorline has rooms. Tell them Ostergaard sent you and don't pay more than eight a night; they'll try for twelve."

It occurred to me, walking out, that this might have been the first act of kindness anyone in an official capacity had shown me in twenty years, and that it had cost him nothing but a sentence, and that I would remember it longer than I remembered half the mountains I'd climbed.

* * *

We took five rooms at the Anchorline and spent two days doing nothing, which turned out to be the hardest work any of us had done in weeks.

Tam fixed things. There was no fixing needed anywhere in the Anchorline, but that didn't stop him; by Tuesday evening he had rehung the innkeeper's sagging door, re-caulked a window, and gotten into a low-grade argument with the cook about the draft on her stove, which he won, technically, though she banned him from the kitchen anyway. Bo slept fourteen hours the first night and ten the second and ate like a man making up for the entire ration deficit of the expedition at once. Ferro sat in the common room with a cup of tea going cold and wrote letters—to a sister in the south, she said, and then, when Ilsa raised an eyebrow, to three sisters, and then stopped counting.

Ilsa read charts. Of course she did. There were no charts to read, so she drew them from memory—the route, the ice fields, the fjord, the place where the bridge had gone—with a pencil stub on butcher paper, annotating depths and timings in her small exact hand. When I asked why, she said, "Because next time someone asks if a route exists, I'd rather answer from evidence than from hope," and I thought about how many times in my life I'd answered from hope, and what it had cost.

And I read.

Not the journal—I'd finished that on the boat down the coast, all of it, every page, including the entry from the night before she sailed that I'd been afraid of for twenty years. *Coral says the mountain will keep,* she'd written, in handwriting steadier than mine would ever be. *He's wrong. Mountains keep, but ships don't, and neither do chances. I'm going, and when I come back I'm going to make him come with me next season, and he'll pretend to hate it.* She'd been right about all of it except the coming back.

No—I read the newspapers. The innkeeper kept a stack of them for wrapping fish, and I bought the recent ones off him with coin I didn't count, and I sat by the window and read what the world had decided had happened while I was still on the ice.

Mostly it was wrong. The salvage fleet's backers had gotten their version out first, as money always does: a routine salvage operation, tragically interrupted by glacial activity, assets lost, inquiries pending. My name appeared in one account as "the guide who led the unauthorized party," which was true, and in another as "Delacroix, of the Meridian family," which was truer than the writer knew. Nowhere did it say what was in the satchel. Nowhere did it say that the shipping company—Corvane Maritime Lines, dissolved and reconstituted twice under new names in twenty years, still paying dividends to shareholders who had never once wondered where their dividends came from—had loaded unstable ore onto a passenger vessel to save on freight costs and let eleven people drown in a sealed hold to protect the insurance claim.

Thursday, I thought, folding the paper. Two days. Let them have their version until Thursday.

* * *

Wednesday evening, Sable Voss came to the Anchorline.

I saw him through the window first, standing across the street in the blue-grey light with his collar up, looking at the inn the way a man looks at a house where he once attended a funeral. Then he crossed the road and came in and stood dripping on the mat, thinner than I remembered him being even a week ago, with a healing cut above his eye that Ferro had dressed and he had clearly picked at since.

"They have a stove here," he said, by way of asking.

"There's a chair," I said.

He sat. The innkeeper brought him tea without being asked, having apparently decided that anyone associated with the Meridian party was either famous or cursed and deserved feeding either way. For a while we drank tea and listened to the cannery wind down for the night, the great machines going silent one by one until there was just the water against the pilings.

"I leave tomorrow," Voss said. "North. There's a commission—survey work. Legitimate." He said the word *legitimate* carefully, tasting it, like a man testing whether a floor would hold his weight. "My employers and I have parted ways. Permanently. Their lawyers were very interested in the documents. Interested in the way sharks are interested."

"They didn't get them."

"No." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the saucer. "They wanted me to sign a statement saying the hold was breached by impact. That the welding was post-mortem damage. They had a forensic examiner lined up, retired, respectable, willing. All I had to do was corroborate." He looked up. "I told them what I told you. On the record, if they want it on the record. Under oath, if it comes to that."

"Why?" I'd asked myself this question on the ice and hadn't answered it. I asked it again now, watching his face. "You spent twenty years on the other side of this, Voss. You built a career on knowing where bodies were buried and selling shovels. Why stand up now?"

He was quiet long enough that I thought he wouldn't answer. Outside, a gull argued with another gull about something neither of them would win.

"When the water came in," he said finally, "she told me to get them out. Eleven people, and she was holding the door closed with a weld and telling me which ones couldn't swim, which ones to carry." His voice stayed level the whole way through, which somehow made it worse. "I've spent twenty years being the man who got out. And every year it got easier to be him, and every year I told myself she'd have wanted me to move on, and every year that was a slightly bigger lie. You at least carried it, Delacroix. Wrong, but you carried it. I sold mine by the pound."

He finished his tea and stood.

"Tomorrow," he said. "When you file—they'll ask if you have witnesses. Corroborating witnesses. People who can testify to what happened aboard." He reached into his coat and put a card on the table, cream-colored, heavy stock, an address in the capital and nothing else. "I'll be reachable at that address for one month. After that, north, and difficult to find." At the door he paused, not turning around. "Your sister welded better than any man I ever sailed with. I want that in the record too. Not just that she saved them. That she was good."

The bell rang him out, unmourned.

I sat for a while after he was gone, turning the card over. Blank on the back. I put it in the satchel with everything else, and it fit, and I thought: twenty years ago these two pieces of paper—a rival's card, a sister's journal—could not have existed in the same container without one of them burning.

* * *

Thursday came up grey and clean, the kind of coastal morning where the light comes in flat and silver and makes even the cannery look dignified.

I wore the only shirt I owned without holes in it. Ilsa came with me; so did Ferro, as medical witness to the condition of the recovered remains, which was a thing that had to be stated formally and could only be stated by someone licensed. Tam and Bo stayed at the inn, ostensibly to guard the gear, actually because Tam had heard the Inquiry Board could take all day and had pronounced the chairs in Ostergaard's office "hostile to the human spine."

The hearing room was smaller than I expected. A table, six chairs, a high window throwing a parallelogram of grey light across the floorboards, and three board members who filed in at nine sharp carrying folders and the expressions of people who had expected a routine dispute over mooring rights and had instead been handed the Meridian.

The chairman was a woman in her sixties named Halvorsen, with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried manner of someone who had spent decades listening to liars and had developed an ear for truth the way old sailors develop an ear for weather. Ostergaard sat in the corner with the intake forms, radiating the quiet pride of a man whose paperwork had generated all this.

They asked me to state my name.

"Coral Delacroix."

"And your relation to the vessel Meridian, lost with all hands, [date struck from the record] twenty-two years past?"

There it was. *Lost with all hands.* The official phrase, stamped across the official record, the lie in its cleanest form—because it wasn't even a dramatic lie, just a lazy one, a box checked, eleven survivors erased by a single word: *all.*

"My sister was second officer aboard," I said. "Maren Yara Delacroix. I was sixteen when she died. I was told she caused the sinking. I was banned from the waters of two nations on the strength of that story. And twenty-two days ago I stood inside the wreck of the Meridian and learned that every word of it was false."

Halvorsen didn't write anything down. She just watched me over her glasses and waited, and I understood that the waiting itself was part of the procedure: say your piece, and then keep saying it, until you run out of prepared words and start telling the truth.

So I told it. I told them about the cargo manifests and the telegrams, and I laid them on the table one by one like cards, and I watched a woman board member's face change when she read the tonnage figures. I told them about the hold door welded from the inside, and slid Tam's photograph across, and the room went still around it. I told them about Voss, and produced his statement, signed and dated, and gave them his address. Ferro testified for six minutes about the remains and the positions of the eleven, and her voice never wavered once, and I thought: this is why I brought her, and also this is why she's unemployable, and both of those facts are the same fact.

It took four hours. Nobody shouted. Nobody pounded the table. Once, when I described the moment Ilsa had returned Maren's journal to me unread—how I had carried it for twenty years and never opened it past page one because page one was addressed to me and I couldn't bear to know what she'd written—Halvorsen removed her glasses, cleaned them slowly on her sleeve, and put them back on, and I recognized the gesture for what it was: a professional giving herself a moment to be a person.

At the end, the chairman folded her hands.

"Mr. Delacroix. This board can amend the record. We can enter the eleven survivors, we can refer the cargo findings to the prosecution service, and we can recommend the lifting of your exclusion." She paused. "What we cannot do is make any of it fast. The companies involved have successors with lawyers. There will be resistance. Some of it will reach you personally. Are you prepared for that?"

I thought about the question honestly, which was more than I'd have managed a month ago. I thought about twenty years of measuring life in miles. About a letter with ice on it. About a rope-line traverse above open water, following Ilsa's orders, and a bridge going down with Voss on it, and swimming.

"Madam Chairman," I said, "I have been running from this for twenty-two years. I'd like to see what it looks like standing still."

Something that was almost a smile moved at the corner of her mouth. She pulled the final form toward her, dipped her pen, and wrote.

*Record amended,* it would say, in ink that would outlast everyone in the room. *Exclusion lifted. Subject cleared.*

* * *

Outside, the tide was coming in, the way it does everywhere on this coast, patient and total.

Ilsa was waiting on the steps, and when I came out she didn't ask how it went, because she'd heard it through the high window, all of it, sitting on the harbor wall within earshot on the pretense of sketching. Instead she said, "Well?" and I said, "It's done," and she nodded once, the way she nodded at a bearing confirmed, and then—this was the part that undid me, quietly, on the courthouse steps in front of God and the cannery—she put her hand on my shoulder for exactly two seconds and took it away, and that was the whole ceremony, and it was enough.

"So," she said, when I could speak normally again. "Free man. Blacklist ended. Famous, or soon to be. What does Coral Delacroix do next?"

I looked down the harbor road, past the Anchorline, past the cannery, to where the water opened out toward the horizon in that flat silver light. Somewhere out there was the rest of my life, which had been waiting twenty-two years for me to show up, and had apparently gotten quite comfortable in the meantime.

"Actually," I said, "there's something I need to buy. A building, if the town has one. Big rooms, high ceilings. Near the water."

Ilsa's eyebrows went up. "For what?"

"For teaching people how to do what we just did," I said. "Rope work, ice rescue, crevasse recovery—properly, legally, for anyone the fleet industry won't hire. Bo knows rigging, Ferro knows field medicine, you know—" I gestured at the entirety of her "—everything else. Tam can fix the roof."

She was quiet for a moment, working it through, and I watched her arrive at the same place I'd arrived somewhere over the last few miles of white road: that this wasn't a memorial. Memorials look backward. This was a map.

"She'd have hated the fuss," Ilsa said.

"She'd have loved the students," I said. "Especially the ones nobody else would take."

The gulls wheeled over the masts. Down the street, Tam's voice rose in renewed argument with the Anchorline's cook about the draft on her stove, and Bo laughed, and the tide came in over the flats the way it had come in for ten thousand years, indifferent and faithful, erasing and revealing in the same motion.

I had a name for it already. I'd had it since the boat, since page one.

But that was tomorrow's business. Today the record was straight, the sun was climbing, and for the first time in twenty-two years, nowhere on any coast of any sea was there a list with my name on it that I needed to outrun.

I started walking down toward the water, and Ilsa fell in beside me, and we counted nothing at all.
