## 37. What the Inheritance Buys

The lawyer's office in Harrow Point smelled of old varnish and newer money, and Coral sat in a chair too fine for his coat and listened to a man read numbers aloud like they were weather reports.

"Salvage rights, confirmed by the maritime court," the lawyer said. "The Meridian's cargo manifest, the company correspondence, the testimony of Mr. Voss—all of it entered into record. The estate of Maren Yara Delacroix is recognized as the rightful claimant of the recovered archive and the associated salvage award. As her surviving kin, Mr. Delacroix, the award passes to you."

Coral had spent twenty years measuring his life in miles. He had never once measured it in money, mostly because there had never been any. The figure the lawyer read out was large enough that it didn't feel real—it felt like a tide, something that would rise and pull back and leave the beach unchanged.

Except it wouldn't. That was the strange part. For the first time in his adult life, something was going to stay.

"Mr. Delacroix?"

"I heard you." He looked out the window at the harbor. Grey water, grey sky, the masts of fishing boats standing in rows like patient trees. Somewhere out there, under a hundred fathoms of cold and a glacier's worth of time, the Meridian was settling into her final berth. He'd said goodbye to her on the far shore, standing in the spray while the calving wall came down like the end of an era, which it was. "I want to know what I can't do with it."

The lawyer blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"Every fortune has rules. What are mine?"

The lawyer smiled the way men smile when they've been asked a question they enjoy. "Very few. The salvage award is yours outright. The deed to the Delacroix holdings—the house at Verrow Bay, the land—it reverted to the estate twenty years ago under the presumption of death. That's been reversed. It's yours too."

The house at Verrow Bay. Coral had been eleven the last time he'd stood in it, before his parents lost it in the slow-motion shipwreck of their own finances, before Maren had drawn maps on the backs of mortgage notices and sworn she'd buy it back one day. *We'll get it back, Coral. You and me. I've already planned the route.*

She'd planned the route. She'd just never planned on the ice taking her before she could sail it.

"I'll want to see it," he said.

"It's been vacant a long time. Squatters, weather, the usual." The lawyer slid a key across the desk, an old iron thing, heavy as an apology. "Though I'm told the roof held. Houses built by people who expected storms tend to."

Coral took the key. It was colder than the room.

* * *

He told the crew on the dock, because it seemed wrong to tell them anywhere with walls. They stood in a half-circle among the crates and coiled line, the five of them looking like what they were—people the sea and the mountains had chewed on and spat back out, still standing.

"The award's mine," Coral said. "Legally. Maren's estate, my name on the paper. But it isn't mine." He'd rehearsed this part and now found he didn't need the rehearsal at all. "She bought it with eleven lives. She welded a door shut and stayed on the wrong side of it so other people could live. I've spent twenty years being ashamed of a woman I should have spent twenty years being proud of. So we're going to divide it the way she would have. Evenly. Five ways."

"Six," Ilsa said.

Coral looked at her. "Voss has his own money. Dirty money, most of it, but his own."

"Six," Ilsa said again, in the voice she used when a route was a route and arguing with it was a waste of breath. "He turned on his own backers for us. He crossed a bridge that fell down behind him because you went back for him, and he knows what that cost you. He testified for a dead woman against people who could ruin him. That's not dirty money anymore. That's blood money, and it's ours to give, and we're giving it."

Tam cracked his knuckles, which was his way of voting. Bo, arm still in a sling from the bridge, nodded with his whole body the way he did everything. Ferro said nothing, but Ferro's silences had weight, and this one landed on the side of six.

Coral exhaled. "Six, then. But I'm spending my share before I divide anything, and I want you all to hear it first, because I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you so nobody's surprised when I disappear for a year."

"Disappearing's your native language," Tam said. "Go on."

"I'm buying the house at Verrow Bay. The land with it—the headland, the old boathouse, the water. And I'm building a school."

He'd said it out loud for the first time and the words fit his mouth better than he'd expected, like a boot broken in on a long trail.

"A rescue school. Mountain and sea. Real certification, real instructors, open to anyone—including the ones with records, the blacklisted, the ones every fleet and every guild won't touch. The ones like us." He looked at them one at a time. "Maren spent her whole short life planning voyages for other people. She kept a notebook of routes for sailors who couldn't afford charts. I found it in her journal—pages of it. Tide tables she worked out by hand for harbors she'd never seen. She was going to teach, someday. She just never got the chance to start."

The harbor noise filled the pause—gulls, halyards, the groan of a boat knocking against its fenders.

"You're naming it," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question. She'd read the spine of the journal before she'd handed it back unread; she knew what name lived there.

"Yara," Coral said. "Her middle name. She hated it when she was young—said it sounded like a bird nobody had ever seen. She was right. Nobody had ever seen it." He looked out at the water. "They'll see it now. Painted on a boathouse door."

* * *

The house at Verrow Bay took two days to reach by road and twenty years to reach in every other way.

Coral walked the last mile alone. The lane was overgrown but walkable, and the house rose out of the headland grass the way it did in his memory, which unsettled him more than if it had changed—grey stone, steep roof, chimneys like raised fists against the sky. His parents had built it to outlast storms. It had outlasted everything else too.

The iron key turned on the second try. The door swung into a hall that smelled of dust and salt and, faintly, impossibly, of cedar—his father's cedar chests, still sealed, still doing their work.

He walked through it room by room. He didn't cry until the kitchen, where a pencil line climbed the doorframe with dates beside it: his own height, year after year, and then Maren's, higher, always higher, her handwriting triumphant even in graphite. *M., age 9. M., age 11. BEAT YOU.*

He stood with his fingers on the pencil line for a long time. Then he took out his own pencil, right there in the empty kitchen of a house that had been dead for twenty years, and marked a new line above his sister's.

*Beat you,* he wrote. *Finally. Took me long enough.*

* * *

The crew came up from Harrow Point in stages over the following weeks, and the school grew the way Coral had watched glaciers grow—imperceptibly, then all at once.

Ferro arrived first and claimed the kitchen, and by claiming it turned the whole ground floor into a place where people were fed whether they asked or not. He'd taken his sixth of the award and sent most of it north, to a clinic in the border country where he'd once practiced without a license and saved forty-one people before the authorities noticed. "They'll waste it on bandages and good coffee," he said. "It's what I know how to do with money. Ruin it on other people."

Tam and Bo took rooms in the village and started on the boathouse, which had a hole in its roof you could see stars through. Tam turned out to be a better carpenter than he was comfortable admitting. "Rigging's just carpentry that argues back," he said, driving nails like they owed him money. Bo, sling off at last, hauled timber and told anyone who'd listen that he was going to be the school's first instructor in crevasse rescue, on the grounds that he had more firsthand experience of the bottom of one than anyone alive.

"I fell," Bo said, "so that others may not fall. That's a whole philosophy. I'm going to put it on a sign."

Ilsa came last. She'd gone east first, to settle her mother's affairs, and came back with a sea chest and a look on her face that Coral had learned to read as *the map has changed and I've already redrawn it.* She took the chart room—there was a chart room, because of course there was, Maren's father had been a navigator before the money went—and started drawing.

"Curriculum," she said, when Coral asked. "Three tracks. Mountain, sea, and the one nobody teaches: judgment. When to go, when to turn around, when the mountain's telling you something the weather report isn't." She tapped the paper. "You had it and you learned it the hard way. Most people learn it the way Bo did, or the way the Meridian's passengers did. We can teach it the cheap way instead. On paper. In a classroom. With rope they can let go of."

Coral looked at her charts for a long time. "Maren had a page like that," he said. "In her journal. Rules for turning around. I never read them until this month." He'd read the journal now, all of it, twice. It lived on the shelf above the kitchen door, next to the pencil marks. "She wrote: *The summit is optional. The descent is mandatory. Anyone who tells you different is selling something.*"

Ilsa wrote it at the top of her curriculum without asking whose line it was. She knew.

* * *

The first students arrived in the spring, and they were exactly who Coral had built the doors for.

A rigger dismissed from the southern fleets for a strike he'd helped organize. A girl of nineteen who'd crewed smugglers' boats since she was fourteen and could read water better than anyone Coral had ever seen, including Ilsa, who'd tested her and said so with visible reluctance. A former guide from the far coast, blacklisted like Coral, who stood in the doorway of the boathouse on his first day and said, "I heard this was the place where they don't ask," and Coral said, "We ask everything except that," and the man came in.

Coral taught the mountain track himself. He was, he discovered, good at it—not the way he was good at guiding, which was solitary and silent, but good at standing in front of people and handing them what he knew. It turned out twenty years of survival had compounded into something. It turned out shame, once you set it down, weighed exactly nothing, and what you'd been carrying underneath it all along was the actual cargo: the knots, the readings, the thousand small judgments, the knowledge of what cold does to a promise.

Some evenings he taught in the old kitchen, because the stove there drew well and the students liked the pencil marks on the doorframe. He never explained them. The students made up their own stories, and the stories got better every year, and he let them.

* * *

Voss's share went out by courier in the autumn, along with a note Coral wrote four times before he got it right. The final version said: *Your sixth. Ilsa's arithmetic. Don't argue with her, you'll lose.* Voss's reply came back on good paper: *I never argue with a navigator. Tell her the harbor at Verrow Bay has a sandbar that the charts miss. I hit it at nineteen. Consider it my first donation to the school.*

He didn't come in person. Coral understood why. Some men could turn against their employers but not against their own reflection, and Coral was the reflection. It was fine. It was more than fine. There would be time—or there wouldn't, and that was fine too. The bridge had been crossed. What you did on the far side was your own business.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the calving, Coral walked down to the water at grey light, the hour he'd always liked best, the hour he'd once used for leaving.

He stood at the tideline and looked north, toward the ice fields, toward the white road that no longer existed, toward the mountain that had taken his sister and then, twenty years late, given her back to him. The salvage fleet's old channel was empty. Somewhere beyond the horizon the Meridian lay under new ice, sealed now by the sea instead of by Maren's welding torch, and Coral found that he could think of it the way you think of a grave you've visited and tended: sad, and finished, and at peace.

Behind him, the boathouse door banged open. Bo's voice, carrying across the yard: "Coral! First boat's coming in—new students, six of them, and one of them's asking for you by name. Says she knows you." A pause. "Says she's got a letter with ice on it."

Coral didn't move for a moment. The words landed in him like a stone dropped down a well, and the well was deep, and he listened all the way to the bottom.

Then he turned from the water and walked toward the door, into the light of the school that bore his sister's name, and the tide came in behind him, right on schedule, keeping its own time the way it always had.
