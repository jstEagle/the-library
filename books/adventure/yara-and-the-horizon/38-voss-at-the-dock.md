## 38. Voss at the Dock

The harbor town was called Greywater, which Coral had always thought was a lie, because in the late afternoon the water turned the color of hammered tin and then, if you were lucky, briefly to gold. He had been lucky that afternoon. He'd watched it happen from the end of the public dock with a cup of terrible coffee going cold in his hand, and he'd thought: *Maren would have said something clever about this.* She always had a line ready for light on water. It was one of the hundred small thefts grief commits—how you start collecting the sentences a dead person would have said, and carrying them around like loose change.

The paperwork was done. Filed, stamped, copied, witnessed. Three clerks had handled Maren's documents with the delicacy of people who suspected they were touching something radioactive, and by noon the maritime authority had quietly unmade twenty years of Coral's exile with two signatures and a form whose header read *Correction of Record*. The clerk—a young woman with ink-stained fingers and an expression of careful neutrality—had looked up at him once and said, "You understand this will be published."

"I'm counting on it," he'd said.

Now the sun was dropping behind the headlands, the tin-gold light was fading off the water, and Sable Voss was walking down the dock toward him.

Coral heard him before he saw him—that particular gait, uneven on the left, a souvenir from the ice fields that Voss wore like a man wears a scar he's decided to be proud of. He didn't turn around. He let Voss come the whole length of the planks, boots knocking hollow against old wood, until he arrived at the rail beside him and stood there, looking out at the same water.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Twenty years of habit is not broken by one glacier, apparently.

"You look terrible," Voss said finally.

"You should see the other guy."

Voss huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. His hands were wrapped around the rail, knuckles still scabbed where the rope had burned through them on the last crossing—the bridge, the water, the moment Coral had gone back for him when every sane cell in his body had voted otherwise. "I owe you my life," Voss said. "I've been trying to figure out how to say that without it sounding like a debt I intend to repay."

"Then don't say it."

"It sounds worse unsaid. Unsaid, it becomes a thing between us." Voss turned his head. Up close, the years had been less kind to him than the photographs suggested—or maybe the photographs had been kind because they were taken before. There was grey in his beard now, and lines around his eyes that hadn't come from smiling. "And we have enough things between us already."

"We do," Coral agreed. "We have a wreck, a company, eleven names on a passenger list, a hold welded shut from the inside, and about forty miles of bad ice. That's plenty."

"Forty-two," Voss said. "You always rounded down. Drove me insane."

That surprised a real laugh out of Coral, short and rough, like something dislodged. He set the coffee on the rail. Somewhere behind them a gull screamed its opinion of everything.

* * *

They had found each other again, after the calving, the way survivors find each other: too much to say, no language for any of it, so they defaulted to logistics. Voss had handled the fleet—scattered it, bought off the backers' lawyers with the very documents he'd spent a decade positioning himself to steal, burned his own reputation as thoroughly as Coral's had ever been burned, though in Voss's case it had been voluntary, which made it somehow more obscene. The backers would rebuild. Men like that always did. But their claim on the Meridian was dead, drowned in testimony and public record, and the salvage rights now belonged to a crew of five unemployables and a dead woman's archive.

"You could have kept it all," Coral said now, watching a fishing boat come in under a rag of sail. "The deed. The rights. You wanted them long enough."

"I wanted them when wanting them was the only honest thing left to want." Voss shifted his weight off his bad leg. "You know what I did for twenty years? I built a life on top of a hole. Every year the hole got bigger and the life got thinner, and I told myself the hole was ambition. It wasn't. It was a night in a flooding corridor and a woman welding a door shut while I pounded on it from the wrong side."

Coral didn't answer. The fishing boat passed below them, nets dripping silver.

"She ordered me out," Voss went on, quieter. "Did she tell you that? In her journal, or wherever you keep her voice these days. She ordered me out of the corridor. Said someone had to carry the count. Eleven souls accounted for, get them to the boats, don't come back. And I carried the count. I've been carrying it ever since, and I told myself it was grief, but it wasn't grief either. It was obedience. I did what she said and I hated her for it and I loved her for it and I spent twenty years trying to buy my way back through that door." He exhaled. "You spent twenty years trying to pretend the door never existed. We were both cowards. Just in opposite directions."

"That's generous," Coral said. "To both of us."

"I'm not being generous. I'm being accurate. There's a difference, and it took me a shipwreck and a glacier to learn it." Voss straightened, wincing. "Here's what I came to say, and then I'll go, because I know you have a crew waiting and a school to name and all the rest of the redemption tour."

"Don't cheapen it."

"I wouldn't dream of it. It's the only genuine thing either of us has done in decades; I'd sooner cheapen a funeral." He pulled something from inside his coat—a flat oilcloth packet, tied with cord, worn soft at the edges from handling. "This is yours. Or hers. It was in the fleet's manifest vault—I pulled it the day before we sailed, when I still thought I was the villain of this story and wanted a souvenir."

Coral took it without moving. His heart was doing something complicated and slow.

Inside the oilcloth, when he worked the cord loose with fingers that suddenly felt borrowed, was a photograph. Old stock, silvered at the edges. Two young people on a dock not unlike this one, squinting into sun. A girl with wind-ruined hair holding a coil of rope like a trophy, grinning like the horizon owed her money. And beside her, taller, awkward, half a smile he barely recognized as his own face—himself, at nineteen, before.

On the back, in pencil, in Maren's handwriting: *C & M — the day we signed on. He thinks the sea is a map. She knows it's a door.*

Coral stared at it for a long time. The dock swayed gently beneath them. Somewhere far off, the tide was turning.

"I didn't know this existed," he said finally. His voice came out sanded down.

"Neither did I, until I went looking for ammunition and found this instead." Voss looked away, out at the water, giving him the privacy of not being watched. "I was going to use it against you, Coral. At some point in the ice fields, when it suited me. Leverage. That's what I told myself it was. And then Bo fell, and Ferro pinged, and you forbade sabotage, and you went back for me on that bridge—and somewhere around the fourth tide I realized I'd stopped competing with you years ago and had just been losing to a ghost of my own construction ever since." He shrugged. "So. Souvenir returned. Ammunition decommissioned."

"Why give it to me now?"

"Because tomorrow you won't need it, and today you might." Voss pushed off the rail, testing his leg. "One more thing, and then I'm gone. The backers sent feelers. They want to talk to you—not threaten, talk. They're frightened men now, and frightened men with money are dangerous in a boring way. Watch the quiet ones. The loud ones already showed you what they are."

"Noted."

"And—" Voss paused at the top of the gangplank, silhouetted against the last of the gold light, and for a second Coral saw him at thirty, at twenty-five, at the regatta where they'd first raced each other across open water and hated how much they enjoyed it. "The measurement's over, Delacroix. You understand that? Twenty years I measured myself against you and came up short, or long, or sideways—but I measured. And you measured right back, even when you pretended you weren't measuring anything but miles." He tapped the dock plank with his boot. "There's no race anymore. There never really was. There was just two men standing in the same wreckage, counting the same dead, pretending the counting was a contest."

He held out his hand.

Coral looked at it. Then he took it—gripped it hard, the way you grip a rope over open water, the way you grip the only solid thing in sight. Voss's hand was cold and scarred and steady.

"Not forgiveness," Coral said.

"No," Voss agreed. "Forgiveness is for people who are sorry. I'm just finished."

"Finished works."

"Finished works." Voss released his grip, retrieved his balance, and started up the dock toward the lamplit street. After a few steps he stopped, without turning. "Delacroix. The school. When you name it—"

"Yara," Coral said. "I know."

A pause. "Good name," Voss said, and walked on, uneven on the left, getting smaller against the lights, until the town swallowed him and the dock was just the dock again, ordinary wood over ordinary water.

* * *

Coral stood there a while longer with the photograph in his hands.

The light had gone fully now, tin-dark, the gold spent. He turned the picture over and read Maren's penciled line again, mouthing it like a man learning a language: *He thinks the sea is a map. She knows it's a door.*

Twenty years he'd carried a journal he couldn't open past page one. Now he carried a photograph he could look at forever, and it didn't hurt the way he'd braced for. It hurt the other way—the way a splint hurts coming off a healed bone. Not damage. Proof of mending.

Behind him, footsteps on the planks—light, quick, familiar. Ilsa came down the dock with her chart case under her arm and her collar up against the evening chill, and stopped when she saw his face.

"Well?" she said. "Is it settled?"

"He's gone."

"That's not what I asked."

Coral looked at the photograph one more time, then folded the oilcloth over it with the care you give a chart you'll need again. "It's settled," he said. "No more measuring. From here on, we just sail."

Isla studied him a moment, then nodded slowly, and something in her shoulders loosened that had been tight since the crevasse field. "Good," she said. "Because Tam found a boat."

Coral raised an eyebrow. "Tam found a boat."

"Tam found a *steal* of a boat. Owner's desperate, hull's sound, engine's ugly but Ferro says she'll run. Bo's already named her."

Coral felt something in his chest that he hadn't felt in twenty years and recognized, slowly, with something like wonder, as eagerness. "Let me guess."

Ilsa grinned. "*Yara's Door.*"

Coral laughed—really laughed, out loud, alone on a dock in the dark with a dead woman's photograph in his pocket and a living crew waiting up the street. The water below slapped the pilings, patient as tide tables.

"Tell them," he said, turning toward the lights, toward the crew, toward whatever came next, "we sail on the morning tide."
