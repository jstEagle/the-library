## 39. Yara and the Horizon

The sign went up on a Tuesday, in weather too good to trust.

Ilsa had painted it herself—*YARA RESCUE & MOUNTAIN SCHOOL* in letters tall enough to read from the harbor road—and she'd hung it crooked on purpose. "So people know we're not the kind of outfit that takes itself seriously," she said, standing back with her hands on her hips, paint on her knuckles and something dangerously close to pride on her face.

"It's crooked because you were cold," Coral said.

"It's crooked because it's honest." She wiped her hands on her trousers and looked at him sideways. "You hate it."

He didn't. That was the strange part. He stood in the gravel lot of the old cannery they'd leased with salvage money—the first money he'd ever held that didn't feel like it was borrowed against his own name—and he looked at the word *YARA* and felt nothing break open in his chest. No grief. No guilt. Just a warmth, low and steady, like a stove in a small room.

Maren's middle name had been Yara. He'd forgotten that for years—not forgotten exactly, but buried it so deep under the weight of *Delacroix* and *blacklisted* and *the man whose sister died* that digging it out had felt like excavation. It took Voss saying it, offhand, at the dock in chapter after chapter of their long unraveling—"Your sister's name was Yara Delacroix, did you know that? Not Maren. Yara"—before Coral could even hear it without flinching.

Now it hung over a door, crooked, in Ilsa's handwriting, and it looked like it belonged there.

"First expedition leaves when?" Tam asked, coming around the side of the building with a coil of rope over one shoulder and Bo limping behind him, still favoring the leg the ice bridge had nearly taken. Bo's ankle had healed wrong and then been re-broken and set right by Ferro, who now occupied the back room they'd converted into an infirmary and complained about constantly, loudly, and with obvious contentment.

"Spring tide," Coral said. "Six days."

"Legal?" Bo asked, grinning.

"Permitted," Coral corrected. "Stamped. Filed. There's a number on the permit. There's a *name* on the permit."

"Coral Delacroix," Tam said, savoring it like a rare vintage. "Licensed guide. Two countries cleared him. Do you know what the odds were?"

"I don't gamble."

"You used to. You gambled your whole life on staying invisible."

Coral let that sit. It was true, and it deserved to be true out loud. For twenty years he had moved through mountain ranges the way water moves through stone—patiently, invisibly, wearing his own shape down to fit whatever crack would have him. He'd measured his life in miles because miles were honest. Miles added up or they didn't. Years just accumulated, and every year had carried the same question at the bottom of it: *what did you do, the night the Meridian went down, and why won't anyone say your name except as a warning?*

The testimony had changed all of it. Three hours in a maritime authority office in a coastal town that smelled of fish and diesel, reading Maren's documents into the record while a clerk with ink-stained fingers transcribed his voice into history. The shipping company's smuggling operation. The unstable cargo. The welded hold. Eleven lives saved by a nineteen-year-old deckhand who'd understood, faster than any adult aboard, that the only way to contain the disaster was to become its walls.

And then the quiet part, the part that had undone him: the clerk looking up and saying, "Mr. Delacroix, your blacklist status is hereby voided. Both jurisdictions." As if twenty years of exile could be lifted with a stamp. As if the mountains he'd been forbidden had simply opened their gates.

They had. That was the thing nobody told you about freedom—it arrived without ceremony. No trumpets. Just paperwork, and then the pass was legal, and then the range was legal, and then there was nowhere left he wasn't allowed to go, and he'd stood outside the office holding a folder of documents and felt the strangest vertigo of his life: *if I can go anywhere, where do I go?*

Here, it turned out. Here, to a crooked sign and a cannery full of unemployable people who had become, without his noticing, the only family he had left.

* * *

The night before departure, they laid out gear in the main hall the way armies lay out ammunition—methodically, superstitiously, each item checked twice. Ferro inventoried the medical kit with the grim thoroughness of someone who'd pulled two men out of crevasses in one season and intended never to be surprised again. Tam tested every carabiner individually, holding each up to the lamplight like a jeweler. Bo sat with his bad ankle propped on a crate, re-packing the rope bags because it was the one job he could do sitting down, and complaining about it because silence was not in his nature.

Ilsa spread the route map across the trestle table and weighted the corners with mugs.

"The Verren Pass approach," she said. "Three days in, two days of instruction on the glacier, two days out. Eight clients. All vetted. All paying." She tapped the map. "All expecting a licensed guide with a clean record, which is what they're getting, which is still the part that makes me laugh."

"Say it louder," Bo said. "The neighbors haven't heard yet."

"The neighbors are fish," Ilsa said. "They don't care."

Coral listened to them the way he'd once listened to weather—with attention, with respect, with the understanding that he was not in charge of it so much as responsible to it. Somewhere in the last year the crew had stopped being his crew and started being a crew, full stop. They voted on routes. They argued about rations. Ferro had opinions about the coffee that bordered on doctrine. And when Coral gave an order now, they followed it not because he was the blacklisted legend who'd outrun two governments, but because he was usually right and always willing to be argued with.

That second part had taken longer to learn than the first.

He thought about the rules he'd set at the start of the Meridian job. *No talk of my sister.* He'd made them swear it, and they'd sworn it, and then they'd broken it gently, one conversation at a time, until talking about Maren became not a wound but a window. Ferro asked questions no one else dared. Ilsa had found the journal and returned it unread—a gesture he still measured other kindnesses against. Even Bo, who feared nothing on ice and everything in conversation, had sat with him one night at the calving wall and said, "She sounds like she was better than all of us," and Coral had said, "Yes," and neither of them had needed to say more.

The journal lived in his kit now. He'd read it twice through since the glacier, and parts of it a dozen times. The last entry was the one he returned to:

*If anything happens, tell Coral the horizon isn't a wall. It's a door. He'll know what I mean.*

He hadn't known, for twenty years. He knew now. Or he was learning. The distinction seemed to matter less than it used to.

* * *

They left at grey light, because some things don't change.

Eight clients met them at the trailhead—teachers, a retired ferry captain, a young engineer who'd survived a building collapse and wanted, she said, "to make peace with frozen things." Coral briefed them the way he wished someone had briefed him twenty years ago: honestly. The mountain is indifferent. The route is safe because we make it safe, not because it is. If you follow instructions, you will probably come home. Probably is the best word in the language, if you treat it right.

Tam raised an eyebrow at the speech. *Probably,* he mouthed later, amused. *Very inspiring.*

"It's the most inspiring thing I know," Coral said. "Everything else is a lie we sell at sea level."

The first day was easy country—forest trail, then scree, then the long moraine shoulder where the Verren Pass opened ahead of them like a sentence finally finished. Coral walked point out of habit, though Ilsa had charted the route and technically led. He'd offered her the lead formally, at the dock. She'd refused.

"You lead," she'd said. "I navigate. We both know which one of us is better at pretending to be humble."

So he walked point, and behind him came eight strangers learning to walk like a team, and behind them his four impossible people, and the whole procession moved through the pass at the pace of the slowest member, which was the pace Coral had chosen deliberately, because for twenty years he had traveled at the pace of the fastest escape and it had gotten him nowhere he wanted to be.

At the high shoulder, the client—the engineer, Petra—fell into step beside him.

"They say you used to guide this pass illegally," she said. "Before."

"I did."

"What was different?"

Coral considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. Below them the valley dropped away in green folds; above, the glacier tongue gleamed like something freshly sharpened. Somewhere beyond the northern ridge, thirty miles of ice fields away, the Meridian lay entombed under a hundred feet of new snow, sealed forever, carrying its dead and its truth and its eleven saved lives deeper into the mountain's keeping. He'd visited the calving wall once since—stood at a respectful distance with Ilsa and said goodbye properly, the words he'd never gotten to say at any grave.

"Different," he said finally. "Before, I counted miles to get away from years. Now—" He watched Petra's face, waiting for the joke to land, watching instead as she nodded slowly, like a woman who'd survived a building collapse and understood exactly what he meant. "Now I count them to spend them."

Petra smiled. "My therapist says things like that."

"Your therapist steals from better writers than me."

Ahead, Bo whooped—something about a ptarmigan, or maybe just joy; with Bo it was often indistinguishable—and the line of climbers snaked upward into the bright cold air, and Coral Delacroix, licensed, permitted, named, followed his own crew toward the horizon at the pace of the slowest among them.

It was, he thought, the fastest he had ever moved.

* * *

Camp that night sat on a bench of stone above the glacier, wind-still, stars brutal in their clarity. Ferro built the stove-fire small and blue. Tam dealt cards. Ilsa sat beside Coral at the edge of the light, both of them watching the ice field glow faintly under the moon—the same ice fields, give or take thirty miles, where everything had ended and everything had begun.

"Voss sent a letter," Ilsa said.

Coral turned. "When?"

"This morning. Harbor master handed it over. I put it in your kit." She paused. "Didn't read it. Figured that's yours."

"He's not coming, is he."

"He's asking if you need a second instructor for the winter courses." She almost smiled. "Says he's 'between employments.' Says his former employers 'no longer return his correspondence.'"

Coral laughed—a real laugh, from the bottom of the old wreck of himself, echoing out across the ice. Twenty years of rivalry, one shared catastrophe, one drowning man pulled from black water, and now the man wanted a *job*.

"Tell him the pay is terrible," Coral said.

"Tell him yourself. He included a return address."

Coral looked out at the ice. Somewhere in his kit, next to a journal he no longer needed to carry but carried anyway, was a letter from Sable Voss asking for work. The past knocking politely. But this time, when he opened the door, it wouldn't be a ghost standing there.

It would be a man. Cold, difficult, half-drowned, and asking to come in from it.

"Tell him winter's hiring in September," Coral said. "And tell him—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell him the horizon isn't a wall."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment, moonlight on her face, understanding arriving in her eyes like dawn coming up over a ridge.

"Yeah," she said softly. "I'll tell him that."

Below them the glacier ticked and settled, ancient and indifferent and, tonight, almost gentle. Above them the stars burned their indifferent holes in the dark. And Coral Delacroix sat between the two with his crew around the stove-light, counting nothing, measuring nothing, spending everything—

—and toward the north, where the ice fields slept, the horizon waited, patient as a door left open.
