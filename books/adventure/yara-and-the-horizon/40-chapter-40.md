## 40. Chapter 40

The morning of the first expedition, Coral Delacroix woke before his alarm and lay still for a full minute, listening to the harbor.

Gulls. Rigging ticking against masts. The diesel mutter of the ferry that ran the coast route from Saltmere to the headlands. Somewhere below his window, Tam was arguing with a winch, which meant the *Yara* was loaded and ready, which meant Bo had promised to check the winch and had instead gone to buy pastries. Some things were fixed points in any coordinate system.

Coral got up, made coffee in the dented pot Ferro had confiscated from him years ago—"You boil it into tar, I'm medicating you whether you consent or not"—and carried it to the window. Across the water, the school stood on its new pilings: two long low buildings, a drill pond, a rope tower, a boathouse with the name painted on it in letters big enough to read from the far shore.

*YARA RESCUE & MOUNTAIN SCHOOL.*

Ilsa had chosen the font. She'd presented four options at a crew meeting with the gravity of someone briefing a border crossing, and they'd all pretended not to notice when her voice caught on none of them except quietly, on all of them.

Twenty years ago he would have said a building couldn't hold a person's name. He'd have said names were what you put on graves.

He drank his coffee and watched the light come up over the school and revised the opinion without ceremony. Names were what you built things under.

* * *

The dock at six was chaos of the good kind. Fourteen students, ages nineteen to fifty-one, standing in an uneven line with packs they hadn't learned to pack yet—everything heavy riding high, straps dangling, one woman with a coil of rope slung bandolier-style across her chest like she expected to be shot at by mountains.

Coral walked the line slowly, saying nothing, doing the thing he'd hated as a student and now understood completely: letting them wonder what the guide saw.

He saw a lot. He saw the kid who'd lied about his climbing experience—he could tell by the boots, too new, soles unscuffed—and decided to let him confess it himself by day two. He saw the older man with the surgical scar along his knee who'd written *former fisherman* on his application and meant *injury ended my career and I need the sea to mean something else now*. He saw the woman with the rope-bandolier, whose application listed no qualifications at all but whose hands were nicked and rope-burned in the specific pattern of someone who'd been teaching herself in secret for years.

He'd been her once. Standing outside the industry, pressing his face to the glass, learning anyway.

"Rules," Coral said, and the line went quiet. "One: nobody moves faster than the slowest person, and I decide who that is, and it's usually me. Two: if I say a word twice, we stop and talk about why. Three—" he paused, and let them see him pause, because rule three deserved the weight, "—nobody on my mountain is ever left behind. Not for speed. Not for schedule. Not for anything."

The kid with the new boots shifted. "What if leaving someone is safer for everyone?"

Coral looked at him. "Then you carry them," he said. "That's the whole job. Everything else is technique."

Ferro snorted from the back of the dock, where she was checking medical kits with the tenderness other people reserved for infants. Tam nearly dropped the pastry box. Bo, mouth full, raised a hand like a schoolboy with a question, and Coral ignored him on principle.

They boarded. The *Yara* pulled out of Saltmere harbor at 6:41 in grey-gold light, fourteen students, four staff, one ship's cat named Meridian—Bo's doing, sentimental and unrepentant—and a course plotted by Ilsa toward the headlands where, in eleven days, they'd run drills on glacier approach, crevasse rescue, and cold-water extraction.

Coral stood at the stern and watched Saltmere shrink.

He measured the distance without thinking about it, the way he'd measured everything for twenty years—miles, always miles, miles as armor against years. Then he caught himself doing it, and smiled, and turned to face forward instead.

* * *

The headlands were kinder than the ice fields. Kinder, but not kind; Ferro had drilled that distinction into the students before the gangplank came down. "The mountain doesn't hate you," she'd told them, pacing the deck while the coast scrolled past. "It doesn't love you either. It simply does not know you exist. Your entire profession consists of behaving accordingly."

By day three, the kid with the new boots had confessed—in a blurt, around a camp stove, that he'd climbed exactly twice, both times indoors, both times terrified. Coral thanked him in front of the group, assigned him to Bo for basic movement work, and watched something unclench in the boy's shoulders that reminded Coral, painfully, of himself at nineteen, telling a hiring manager a lie about experience he didn't have because the truth—that he needed this, needed it like air—seemed like weakness.

It wasn't weakness. It had never been weakness. It had taken him forty years and one dead sister's journal to learn the difference between needing a thing and being ashamed of the need.

Day five, the rope-bandolier woman—her name was Sefa, thirty-four, ex-warehouse, self-taught off library books—led a mock crevasse extraction better than some guides Coral had employed. He watched her anchor system, clean and slightly unconventional, and afterward asked her where she'd learned the variant.

"Nowhere," she said, wary. "It just made sense. Is it wrong?"

"It's better than the standard," Coral said. "Teach it tomorrow."

She stared at him. He'd seen that stare before, on Ilsa, on Tam, on Bo, on every person the industry had told to stay in their lane until they forgot there was anywhere else to go. He'd worn the stare himself, in two countries' worth of exile.

"Go on," he said. "Before I come to my senses."

* * *

On day eight, a supply boat came out from Saltmere, and the pilot handed Coral a letter with ice on it.

Not literal ice—the season was wrong for it—but the phrase arrived in his head fully formed, and he stood on the shingle turning the envelope over and laughing at himself, alone, like a madman, because he knew the handwriting before he knew anything else.

Sable Voss wrote the way he sailed: direct, economical, no flourish wasted.

*Coral. Your students' drill smoke is visible from the channel; your site selection is sloppy and your fire discipline is worse. If the fleet ever sails north again, you'll want to fix that.*

*I've taken a berth running survey charters out of Greywater. It pays badly and suits me perfectly. My employers from the Meridian affair are ruined—you'll have read about the indictments; the papers spelled your sister's name correctly, which I checked personally, three times.*

*I'm writing for one reason. A family came through Greywater last week asking after guides. Their daughter died last winter on the Verren Pass—avalanche, bad call by a guide who should never have been certified. They're not angry. That's the strange part. They're starting a foundation, and they asked me who runs honest training in this country, and I gave them your name, and then I stood there feeling like a man handing over a live grenade, because I remembered what your name used to cost you and what it costs you now, and I thought: he'll hate me for this, or he'll understand it, and either way I'd do it again.*

*So. Hate me or don't. The foundation's money is real and clean and comes with no strings except one: they want a scholarship seat, every season, for someone who can't otherwise afford the training. Someone like the person you used to be.*

*Tell them yes or tell them no. Either answer is yours to give, which is more than either of us got, once.*

*—V.*

Coral read it twice. Then he sat down on a rock above the tideline and read it a third time, slower, and found his eyes stinging in a way the wind did not explain.

Hate him. Understand him. Voss had finally learned to tell the two apart, and it had taken losing everything to teach him, and Coral recognized the tuition because he'd paid it himself.

He wrote the reply that evening by stove-light, on the back of a drill schedule, because the expedition stores had run short of paper and he refused to waste good rope on stationery jokes even though Tam suggested it.

*V. Tell them yes. Name the scholarship seat after whoever they want—I'll suggest Yara, but only if they agree to one condition: the recipient trains here, works here, and when they're ready, teaches here. Nobody gets rescued out of their own life and sent away. They get brought in.*

*Also: your fire-discipline critique was correct and I hate that. Come see the school when the ice allows. Bring charts. Ilsa wants to argue with your survey data and she's been practicing her arguments since spring.*

*C.*

He sealed it, set it in the supply boat's mail satchel himself, and stood watching until the boat rounded the point.

Behind him, someone cleared their throat. Ilsa, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a navigator who has been eavesdropping out of pure professional duty.

"You invited Voss here."

"I did."

"To the school named after your dead sister."

"That's the idea."

Ilsa considered this for a long moment, wind pulling at her collar. "Good," she said finally. "Because Bo's been trying to figure out how to invite him for months and lacks the nerve, and Tam wants to arm-wrestle him, and Ferro says he eats like a heron and she finds it fascinating." She turned back toward camp. "Also your fire discipline actually is sloppy. We'll drill it Thursday."

* * *

The last night of the expedition, Coral took the watch alone.

It was a thing he still did, though the school had rules against solo watches and Ferro had formally protested them in triplicate. But once per expedition, on the final night, he kept the old habit: sitting up while the others slept, keeping the fire, watching the dark do whatever dark does.

Tonight the dark was doing very little. Clear sky, hard stars, the sea making its patient argument against the shore. Behind him, the tents breathed in sleep-rhythm—fourteen students, four staff, one cat curled inside Bo's jacket against all posted regulations. Ahead, across the black water, the horizon held its usual silence.

Maren had loved this hour. He knew that now, properly knew it, the way you know a person after reading their own words in their own hand: she'd called it *the blue hour when the world admits how big it is.* Her journal sat in the footlocker behind him, spine cracked soft from rereading, page one finally just a page among pages.

He'd stopped counting miles somewhere on the white road, he realized. Somewhere between the calving wall and the far shore, between carrying Voss out of the water and filing Maren's documents in a coastal office while a clerk stamped papers that undid twenty years of lie—the arithmetic had changed. He still measured distances. Every guide did; it was craft. But the numbers pointed forward now. They were spending, not escaping.

A shape stirred at the edge of the firelight. The kid with the new boots—they were scuffed now, properly broken in, and he wore the wear like a medal—came and sat down at a respectful distance, the way students learn to approach Coral eventually, or never.

"Couldn't sleep," the kid said. "Is that allowed?"

"Nothing's allowed on the last night," Coral said. "That's what makes it the last night."

The kid was quiet awhile. Then: "Can I ask what you look at? Out there. You're always looking at the horizon."

Coral considered the question seriously, because the kid had earned a serious answer, and because he'd never once tried to say it aloud.

"A line," he said at last. "Between where the map ends and everything else begins. When I was young I thought the job was crossing it. Then for a long time I thought it was staying on the right side of it." He fed a stick to the fire. "Turns out the job is standing on it. One foot in the world you can chart, one foot in the part nobody's mapped. You keep people alive right on that line. That's the whole trade."

The kid nodded slowly, the nod of someone filing the words away to understand them in ten years.

"Get some sleep," Coral added gently. "Tomorrow we go home, and home has paperwork."

The kid grinned and crawled back into his tent. The fire settled. The stars wheeled their slow, indifferent, beautiful business overhead, and the sea kept its time, tide after tide, each one opening a window and closing it, each one indifferent and each one, somehow, an invitation.

Coral sat his watch. And when the sky went grey at the edges, he rose, kicked the fire apart with a professional's thoroughness, and woke his crew with the oldest call in the trade—the one his father had used, the one Maren had loved, the one he'd taught fourteen strangers to answer to:

"Up. Tide's turning."

And it was.
