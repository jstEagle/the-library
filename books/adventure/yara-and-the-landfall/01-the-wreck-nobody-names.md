## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The chart said *unidentified obstruction*, and the navy's letter said nothing at all, which was how Rosalind Joubert knew the wreck off Greyhaven Point was worth diving.

She'd been working salvage for eleven years, long enough to read the silences between official documents the way other divers read current tables. A charted-but-denied site meant someone had found it, catalogued it, and then buried the paperwork under a classification stamp. It meant cargo worth protecting or a story worth suppressing. Usually both. And both paid the same.

The *Kestrel's Wake* rode a flat grey sea two miles off the point, her crane arm folded like a sleeping bird. Below Rosalind, through forty feet of cold North Atlantic water, the sonar image resolved into a hull lying on its starboard side, silted to the gunwales, broken amidships as if some enormous hand had snapped it over one knee.

She checked her gauges a final time. Forty-one meters to the deck. Bottom time twenty-two minutes. Doable, if she didn't linger.

"Bottom's quiet," Dev said from the dive platform, handing her the tool bag. He was her best man—steady hands, no curiosity, which in this trade was rarer than steady hands. "No current to speak of. Visibility maybe three meters."

"And the tide?"

"Slack in ten. You'll have your window."

Rosalind spat into her mask, rinsed it, and pulled it down over her face. The world went green and rubber-framed. She fitted the regulator between her teeth and let herself fall backward off the platform, the sea closing over her like a door.

* * *

Descent was a kind of forgetting. Ten meters down, the boat's diesel clatter faded to a heartbeat in her chest. Twenty meters, and the water turned from green to bruised blue. Thirty, and the light thinned until her torch beam became the only truth left in the world—a pale blade cutting through suspended sediment, revealing nothing but more water, more dark.

Then the wreck rose up to meet her.

It was bigger than the sonar had suggested. A coastal freighter, by the lines of her—hundred and forty feet, maybe, riveted plates furred with a decade of soft coral, anemones waving in the faint surge like pale hands. Her nameplate had been scraped clean. Not corroded—*scraped*, chisel marks still legible under the growth, deliberate as a signature on a confession. Someone had come down here with tools and made this ship anonymous.

Rosalind ran her gloved fingers along the gouges and felt something cold settle behind her sternum that had nothing to do with the water temperature.

Ships got renamed. Ships got scrapped. Ships did not get their names chiseled off their hulls unless somebody very much wanted them forgotten.

She swam the length of the exposed deck, torch sweeping. The break amidships gaped like a wound, and inside it the cargo hold yawned, half-filled with silt and the collapsed skeleton of decking. Crates, rotted to lace. Barrels collapsed into hoops and staves. And near the forward bulkhead, where the silt had washed thin, the glint of metal.

Not ship's metal. Ship's metal was iron and bronze, functional, ugly. This was brass, and it was shaped.

She dug with her dive knife, careful as an archaeologist, though she told herself she wasn't one. Silt clouded and settled, clouded and settled. Her air gauge ticked down. Fourteen minutes of bottom time gone.

The object came free all at once, small enough to fit in her palm: a fitting, a decorative escutcheon off a cabin door or a captain's trunk, its surface crusted white. She thumbed the crust away—and stopped breathing around her regulator.

A crest. Stamped deep in the brass, worn at the edges but unmistakable: a gull rising over three waves, beneath a chevron and a star.

Her family's crest. The one that had hung over the door of the Joubert house in Greyhaven before the house was sold out from under them, before the name became a thing people said quietly, the way you say *drowned* or *bankrupt*. The one her grandfather had carried on his survey instruments, stamped into the brass plate of his theodolite case, the case she'd last seen the summer she was nine years old, the summer everything ended.

Her heart slammed against her ribs, and the gauge needle swung, and she made herself breathe. In. Out. The regulator hissed. The wreck held its silence around her.

*Coincidence,* said the reasonable voice in her head, the one that had kept her alive through eleven years of black water and bad contracts. *Brass fittings were mass-produced. Crests were copied. Half the shipping families on this coast used gulls and waves.*

But her hands knew better. Her hands remembered the weight of her grandfather's compass, the same star worked into the case, the same chevron. Her hands knew this stamp the way they knew their own knuckles.

She looked at the fitting sitting in her glove, small and bright and impossible, and felt the past rise through the water toward her like a body coming up from a depth too fast, expanding, unstoppable.

* * *

She should have left it there.

That was the rule—the only rule she'd never broken in eleven years. Salvage meant cargo, cargo meant money, money meant the next contract and the next port and another town where nobody knew what a Joubert was. Personal effects went back where you found them. Personal effects were anchors. She'd watched men ruin themselves over wedding rings and lockets and letters, diving the same wrecks again and again until the sea took its toll, because they couldn't stop looking for whatever they'd lost on the bottom.

Rosalind had always prided herself on traveling light. No photographs. No keepsakes. No home port. The sea didn't care who you'd been, and that was the whole point of it.

She closed her fist around the fitting anyway.

On the ascent she kept it tucked against her wrist, inside her glove, and told herself she was carrying it up to examine it properly, in good light, with reference books. That was procedure. That was due diligence. Provenance mattered in salvage; a misattributed artifact could void an insurance claim. There was nothing personal about it. Nothing at all.

Dev's face appeared above her as she broke the surface, haloed in grey sky. "Good dive?"

"Quiet," she said, and handed him the tool bag with the fitting zipped into its inner pocket, and climbed aboard the *Kestrel's Wake* with her heart going like a bilge pump.

* * *

She waited until evening to look at it properly.

The crew had gone ashore to Greyhaven for supplies and beer, and the harbor had gone quiet, just the slap of water against pilings and the far-off clang of the buoy bell off the point. Rosalind sat in the wheelhouse with the fitting under the desk lamp, a soft toothbrush in one hand and a cup of tea going cold at her elbow.

The crust came away in flakes. Underneath, the brass was bright where it had lain against wood, dark where the sea had worked it, and the crest stood out sharp as the day it was struck. Around the rim, almost invisible, a maker's mark: *J&H Fairweather, Greyhaven.* 

Fairweather. Her grandmother's maiden name. Two families braided into a piece of brass the size of a coaster, sitting in the wheelhouse of a salvage boat anchored off the coast she'd spent a decade pretending didn't exist.

She set it down carefully, as if it might go off.

Here was what she knew, laid out in the lamplight: the wreck was old enough to be pre-war, stripped of its name, classified into silence by an admiralty that didn't return calls. Her family's crest was on its fittings. Her grandfather—Edmund Joubert, marine surveyor, drowned man, ruined man—had worked these waters his whole life. And she had taken a contract to salvage this particular wreck not because of any rumor, but because the concession notice had crossed her agent's desk with a navy file number attached, and she'd followed the silence the way she always did.

Or so she'd told herself. Standing in the wheelhouse with her family's name in her hand, she had to admit there'd been something else. Some pull she hadn't examined. She'd grown up four hundred miles inland, in a rented house full of boxes that were never unpacked, listening to her mother refuse to say the word *Greyhaven*. And when the contract came through with that name on it, she'd signed it within the hour, and called it business.

The harbor lights of Greyhaven strung themselves across the dark water, yellow and indifferent. Somewhere in that town were people who remembered her surname, and what it had cost them, or what it had cost her family—she'd never known which way the story went, only that everyone told it in lowered voices and her mother never told it at all.

Instinct said: put the fitting in a weighted bag, drop it over the rail tonight, log the dive as unremarkable, finish the contract, take the money, leave. Instinct had kept her alive for eleven years. Instinct had never once steered her wrong.

But instinct hadn't counted on the maker's mark. *J&H Fairweather, Greyhaven.* Somebody had made that fitting here, in this town, for a ship that came here, that sank here, that somebody had tried to erase here. And her grandfather's instrument cases—the ones in the boxes her mother never unpacked—had borne that same stamp.

She thought about the chisel marks on the nameplate. Thought about the navy file number. Thought about her mother, who had left this coast at nineteen and never once looked back, and who had died still looking away.

Rosalind picked up the fitting and turned it in the light. The gull caught the lamp flame and seemed to lift.

"Damn it," she said quietly, to the empty wheelhouse, to the harbor, to the dead.

Because here was the thing about running: she'd gotten good at it, but she'd never once figured out what she was running *from*. Every town blurred into every other town. Every contract ended the same way, with her pulling anchor and feeling nothing but relief. And now, for the first time in eleven years, the sea had handed her something with a shape to it—a question with her own name on it—and she could feel the old reflex gathering in her legs like the urge to swim.

She wouldn't run. Not yet. Not until she knew what this was.

Tomorrow she'd go ashore alone, without Dev, without the crew, and start asking questions no sane salvager would ask about a wreck the navy wouldn't name. Tomorrow she'd find out what a Joubert had been doing on a ship that somebody wanted forgotten.

Tonight, she wrapped the fitting in a chamois cloth and hid it in the false bottom of her chart drawer, under the charts of waters she'd never dived, and went out onto the deck to stand her watch under a sky the color of wet slate.

Off Greyhaven Point, the buoy bell rang, and rang, and rang, like something counting down.
