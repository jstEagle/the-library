## 2. Six Tides

The harbor master's office sat at the end of the stone pier like a lighthouse that had given up on being tall—a squat granite building with a green door, salt-scoured windows, and a weathervane in the shape of a cod that pointed stubbornly into the wind no matter which way the wind blew. Rosalind had watched it from the deck of the *Kestrel* for two days, telling herself she'd go ashore when she was ready.

She went ashore at low water on the third morning, alone, wearing her least memorable jacket and carrying nothing but a canvas satchel and the lie of her own name.

Greyhaven woke around her as she walked the quay. Gulls arguing over fish heads. A fuel barge coughing diesel into the morning air. An old man mending nets on an upturned dory, his hands moving with the patience of someone who had done the same work for sixty years and would do it for sixty more. He looked up as she passed, and his eyes—pale, watery, old—lingered on her face a beat too long before returning to his knots.

*They remember faces here,* she thought. *That's the problem with towns. They keep everything.*

The green door was unlocked. Inside, the office smelled of tar, old paper, and coffee that had been kept warm past all decency. Charts papered the walls, some of them older than the building, their coastlines drawn in faded ink by hands that had never seen the shore from above. A pot-bellied stove ticked in the corner. And behind a desk scarred by a century of boots and elbows sat Elias Vane.

She'd seen him twice from a distance—once on the pier when they'd tied up, once standing at the harbor mouth watching her boat with the expression of a man counting other people's sins. Up close he was younger than his reputation suggested, maybe mid-thirties, built like the pier itself: weathered, functional, not going anywhere. His hair was dark and cut short, practical, and there was a white scar through one eyebrow that looked like it had a story attached and no intention of telling it.

He didn't look up when she came in. He was writing something in a ledger, the nib of his pen scratching in the quiet, and he let the silence stretch until it became a statement.

"Rosalind Marsh," he said finally, without looking up. "Master of the *Kestrel*, out of Portsmouth registry. Salvage concession, site seven." He turned a page. "You're three days into a permit nobody expected anyone to take."

"The wreck's charted," she said. "I checked."

"It's charted," he agreed. He set down his pen and looked at her then, and his eyes were gray-green, the color of the sea over sand, and entirely unfooled. "Charted and denied. There's a difference. The chart says the *Meridian* is there. The admiralty file says there's nothing at site seven worth anybody's time. You signed an acknowledgment of that discrepancy when you took the permit. Page four, paragraph two. I wrote the paragraph myself."

"I read page four."

"Then you know why I'm surprised to see you standing here instead of sailing away." He stood, and she saw he was taller than the desk made him look, and moved like a man who'd spent his life on decks that pitched. From a drawer he took a single sheet of heavy paper, folded once, sealed with grey wax, and laid it on the desk between them without ceremony. "Concession notice. You're required to receive it in person. That's why you're here—or part of why."

She didn't touch it. "And the rest of why?"

"The rest is courtesy." Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been considered and rejected. "Permits expire in six tides. Twelve high waters, twelve lows, starting with tomorrow's dawn tide. After that, this office reverts site seven to open tender, and Marrow & Finch's survey fleet takes position off the point. They've been waiting in Portsmouth roads for a month. They'll be here inside a day of your expiry, and they don't dive politely."

Six tides. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Three days. Maybe four if she stretched the definition of a working tide, which every salvager did and every harbor master knew they did.

"That's not enough time to survey a hull, let alone—" She stopped herself.

"Let alone what?" He tilted his head. "Recover cargo? File claims? Prove anything?" He came around the desk, and up close she could see the tiredness under his composure, the kind that came from holding a line alone for a long time. "Ms. Marsh, I'm going to say something now that I'm not required to say, and I'd thank you to forget it immediately after. The navy doesn't want that wreck touched. The council doesn't want it touched because the navy pays half this town's mooring fees. And Marrow & Finch wants it touched so badly they've already bought two votes on the harbour board. You are standing in the middle of something that has been buried very carefully for forty years, and six tides is exactly enough time to drown in it."

"And yet you issued me the permit."

"I issue permits. It's my job." He said it flatly, but she heard the seam in it, the hairline fracture under the words. "What people do with them afterward is between them and the sea."

She picked up the notice. The wax was cool under her thumb. "You could have refused it. Denied the application, cited the discrepancy, sent me home. Nobody would have questioned it."

"No," he agreed. "Nobody would have."

"But you didn't."

For a moment neither of them moved. Outside, the buoy bell at the harbor mouth rang twice, lazy, unhurried, keeping its own count. Somewhere down the quay a hatch cover banged. Vane looked at her with that unfooled gray-green gaze, and she had the sudden, disorienting certainty that he knew—not everything, not yet, but something. That behind the official face was a man doing sums of his own.

"Why did you come ashore today, Ms. Marsh?" he asked quietly. "A smart salvager would've stayed on her boat, waited out the notice, and let the clock do the work. Coming here tells me you want something the permit doesn't cover."

"Information."

"About?"

"A ship that sank forty years ago off Greyhaven Point. Officially a naval auxiliary lost with all records sealed. Unofficially—" she paused, testing him, watching his face the way she'd watch a gauge needle, "—unofficially, a wreck whose cargo manifest doesn't match its registered tonnage. Somebody misdeclared something, Mr. Vane. Misdeclared it badly enough that the navy sealed the file. I want to know who."

Something changed in his face. It was small—the way light changes on water when a cloud moves that isn't quite overhead yet—but she caught it, because catching small things was how she'd stayed alive underwater for ten years.

"My grandfather worked these waters," he said at last. "Harbor master before me, and his father before him. Forty years ago, a ship went down off the Point in weather that shouldn't have sunk her, carrying cargo that shouldn't have been aboard, and the town that helped pull eleven bodies out of the surf was told to forget it by men from the city." He picked up his coffee, tasted it, and grimaced as though it had personally betrayed him. "Some of us forgot. Some of us didn't. My grandfather was one of the ones who didn't, and it cost him his pension and most of his friends. So you'll forgive me if I don't leap at the chance to help a stranger dig it all back up."

"I'm not asking you to help. I'm asking you not to stand in my way."

"That's the same thing, dressed worse." He set the cup down. "Six tides, Ms. Marsh. Dive your site, log your hours, file your reports—and understand that every report you file crosses my desk before it goes anywhere else. If you find something on that wreck that shouldn't be there, I'll know about it before you've finished drying off. Whether I do anything with that knowledge depends entirely on what it is, and on whether you're the kind of diver who lies about what she brings up."

"And if I am?"

"Then I'll run you off this coast myself, permit or no permit, and sleep fine afterward." He said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. Then, as she turned for the door: "One more thing. The Joubert family used to work salvage out of this harbor. Before the disgrace. Before my time. Their name comes up in the old files now and again—usually crossed out." A pause, measured, deliberate. "You wouldn't know anything about that, I suppose."

The room went very still. Rosalind kept her hand on the door latch and made herself breathe slowly, the way she did at thirty meters when the plan stopped matching the reality.

"Should I?" she said.

"No reason you should." He returned to his ledger, pen scratching, conversation closed. But as she stepped out into the cold bright morning, she heard him add, almost too low to carry over the gulls: "Watch the third tide, Ms. Marsh. Spring low. The water off the Point does strange things when it drops far enough. Things get given back."

* * *

She walked the quay instead of going straight back to the dinghy, because walking was thinking and thinking was safer than the alternative. The notice rode in her satchel like a stone. Six tides. She'd had longer deadlines for smaller wrecks, but never one with this many watchers, this many hands reaching for the same drowned thing.

At the end of the pier, the net-mender was still at his dory. As she passed, he spoke without looking up, his voice a gravel rumble worn smooth by salt.

"You've got her jaw," he said. "The Joubert chin. Old Tomas had the same way of standing, feet apart, like the deck might argue back."

Rosalind stopped. Her pulse ticked up two beats, steady, controlled, the diver's discipline holding. "You're mistaken. My name is Marsh."

"Course it is." The old man drew a knot tight, bit the twine free. "My name's Aldous Petrie. I was crew on the *Meridian*'s last escort run. I was nineteen. I've been mistaken about a great many things for forty years, but not about faces." He finally looked up, and his eyes were kind and terrible. "Your grandfather died trying to clear that wreck's name, girl. Whatever you're diving for out there—he'd have wanted it found by blood."

She said nothing. There was nothing safe to say, and Petrie, reading that, nodded once and went back to his nets, and the conversation closed like water over a diver.

But at the dinghy, crouching to cast off, she found wedged under the bow line a scrap of oilcloth, folded around something small and hard. She glanced up the quay—Petrie was bent over his work, apparently absorbed—and opened it in her palm.

A brass fitting. Identical in make to the one hidden in her chart drawer, stamped with the same crest, the same motto. Except this one was newer, cleaner, and engraved along the edge in a careful hand:

*T.J. — third tide — ask the cave.*

Her heart slammed once against her ribs and settled. She looked up again. Petrie hadn't moved. The buoy bell rang, and rang, counting down toward the spring low, and Rosalind Joubert pushed off from the pier with a dead man's message in her fist and six tides left to learn what her grandfather had died trying to tell her.
