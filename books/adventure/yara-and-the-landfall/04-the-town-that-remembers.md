## 4. The Town That Remembers

Elias Vane had been harbor master of Greyhaven for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned one rule that outlasted every regulation in the admiralty code: the sea kept its own records, and the town kept its own grudges.

He was reminded of both before eight o'clock, when he found old Bram Tully waiting on the harbor office steps, cap in his fists, weathered face set in the expression of a man rehearsing an argument he'd been having for forty years.

"She's still out there," Bram said, jerking his chin toward Greyhaven Point, where the *Kestrel's* mast light bobbed at anchor. "That woman. That *Joubert*."

"You know her name."

"Everyone knows her name." Bram spat over the rail, an old habit, ceremonial rather than contemptuous. "My father hauled bodies out of that water in '71. Eleven of them. And the man who sent the *Merrow* out overloaded, out of season, with a cargo manifest nobody would show the coroner—that man's granddaughter is anchored over the grave of it like she owns it." He turned, and his eyes were wet and furious. "You going to let her?"

Elias had the concession notice in his desk drawer, signed and stamped, six tides written on it in his own hand. He had served it the day before. By the letter of his office, he had done his duty.

"I've served her the notice," he said. "Her permits expire. Marrow & Finch comes after."

"Marrow & Finch." Bram said the name the way other men said a curse. "And there it is. The company that bought the *Merrow's* insurance payout before the bodies were cold. That's who we hand it to when the Joubert girl leaves. One thief for another."

"Nothing's been handed to anyone yet."

"No." Bram settled his cap back on his head, and the fury banked down to something colder, something Elias liked less. "But I've lived here long enough to know how these things go. The town forgets who drowned. It never forgets who it decided to blame." He went down the steps slowly, favoring his bad knee, and paused at the bottom. "Your father would have understood. He was on the rescue boats too."

Then he was gone, and Elias stood in the doorway a long while, watching the *Kestrel* ride at anchor, thinking about the moment yesterday when he'd read the name *Joubert* off the concession papers and looked up into Rosalind Fairweather's face—*Rosalind Fairweather*, the name she'd filed under, and he'd known it was false, and said nothing.

He was still trying to decide what that silence had cost him.

* * *

The council chamber above the fish market smelled of old varnish and newer money. Councilman Aldous Prentice had redecorated it two years ago—new chairs, new blinds, a framed chart of the Greyhaven approaches that cost more than Elias made in a season—and the town had noticed, and said nothing, because Prentice's family had run the cannery and then the cannery's successor and then the land holdings, and that was simply how Greyhaven worked.

Elias had been summoned by note, not phone. Prentice liked notes. Notes didn't exist afterward.

"Sit," Prentice said, not looking up from a folder on the table before him. The folder was heavy stock, corporate gray, a logo embossed on the corner: a stylized anchor wrapped in rope. Marrow & Finch.

Elias sat.

"You served the notice." Prentice turned a page. "Six tides. Good. The survey fleet arrives on the seventh, weather permitting, and we want the site clear. Clean handover. No complications."

"The concession expires. That's the extent of my involvement."

"Your involvement," Prentice said, and now he looked up, and his smile was the one he used at fundraisers, "is whatever I tell you it is. The harbor office reports to the council. The council has entered into a preliminary agreement with Marrow & Finch Salvage for recovery operations on the *Merrow* wreck site. There's a fee to the town. Schools, seawall, the things this town actually needs instead of sentiment."

"The wreck's under admiralty jurisdiction pending claim. You can't sell what you don't own."

"I can sell the *harbor's cooperation*," Prentice said mildly. "Access, survey support, a quiet word in the right ears. And you're going to give me all three." He slid a single sheet across the table. "One more thing. The diver. Rosalind Fairweather."

Elias didn't touch the paper.

"Her certification's under review. There's a question about her qualifications—where she trained, whose name she dove under. Marrow & Finch's surveyor has raised it formally. Callum Reyes." Prentice let the name sit. "He's asked that she be barred from the site on safety grounds. Given her—history—I'd like you to enforce that. Keep her off the wreck until the fleet arrives. Consider it an instruction."

"From the council."

"From me, speaking for the council." Prentice closed the folder. "You've been harbor master a long time, Vane. You're good at the job. It would be a shame if the council had to reconsider the position. Your father held it before you, I remember. Family tradition. I'd hate to see another Greyhaven family lose what's theirs."

The threat was gentle as a tide, and just as irresistible, and they both knew it.

Elias took the paper. He didn't promise anything. Prentice, satisfied, didn't seem to need the promise, and that frightened Elias more than an explicit order would have—because it meant Prentice believed the harbor master's obedience was already bought and paid for, and Elias had eleven years of quiet, correct service to thank for it.

* * *

He walked the seawall that evening, the paper folded in his jacket like a stone, and found himself at the memorial without meaning to.

It stood where the harbor path met the cliff walk: a granite block, salt-scoured, with eleven names cut into it. The crew of the *Merrow*, lost March 1971. Elias had walked past it a thousand times. Children played on it. Tourists photographed the gulls on it.

Tonight he read the names.

His father had been seventeen when the *Merrow* went down, one of the young men who rowed out into the storm because nobody told them not to. He'd never talked about it, not once, except the night before he died, when he'd gripped Elias's wrist with a strength that surprised them both and said: *The manifest was wrong, lad. Whatever they tell you, whatever they wrote down—the manifest was wrong. Old Joubert knew it. That's why they buried him.*

Elias had been twenty-four and grieving and had filed it away as a dying man's confusion. Old Joubert—Aurel Joubert, the harbor master before Elias's father, the man whose survey charts every pilot in Greyhaven had learned from, the man blamed for the overloaded manifest, the man who drowned trying to reach the wreck himself three weeks later, alone, in a borrowed skiff.

Aurel Joubert. Grandfather of the woman now anchored over the wreck under a false name.

Elias stood at the memorial until the light went copper and then gray, and the arithmetic assembled itself with the terrible simplicity of all true things: the town had needed someone to blame, the company had needed a scapegoat, and the man who'd kept the harbor's records—the man whose job this was, whose chair this was, whose office held forty years of his ledgers—had been the most convenient target in Greyhaven.

And his granddaughter had come back to prove it.

* * *

He found her on the *Kestrel's* deck just after dusk, coiling line with the economy of someone who'd done it ten thousand times, and he came alongside in the launch and called up before she could pretend not to see him.

"Fairweather."

"Harbor master." She didn't stop working. "Come to serve me something else?"

He climbed aboard. Up close she had the particular tiredness of a diver at the end of a long day, salt in her hair, and she watched him with those level gray eyes that gave away nothing except that they were always calculating.

"I'm going to say some things," Elias said. "And you're going to decide how much of them you heard."

That got her attention. She set down the line.

"Prentice is negotiating with Marrow & Finch. The council hasn't voted on it publicly because there's no vote—it's being done in a folder. They want you off the site before the fleet arrives. There's a certification complaint filed against you by a Callum Reyes, and Prentice wants me to enforce a bar on your diving the wreck." He held up the paper, let her see it, and then, while she watched, he folded it in half, and in half again, and put it in his pocket. "I served you the concession notice yesterday. That was real, and it stands. Six tides. Everything else he gave me today—" he looked out at the dark water, at the point where the wreck slept beneath it, "—I haven't read yet."

Rosalind was quiet for a long moment. "Why?"

Because of a dying man's grip on my wrist. Because of eleven names on a granite block. Because Bram Tully's father hauled bodies and hated the wrong ghost. Because the harbor office still runs on your grandfather's charts, and I've been navigating his corrections my whole career without knowing I owed him an apology.

"Because I read the names tonight," he said instead. "On the memorial. All eleven. And then I read the ones that aren't on it, and there's one missing, and it's yours."

Something moved behind her eyes—the first crack he'd seen in the armor. "My grandfather's name isn't on it because he wasn't crew."

"He was harbor master. He was the town. And the town erased him." Elias turned back to her. "I'm not telling you to trust me. I'm telling you the clock you're racing just got shorter, and the man who's supposed to stop you is standing on your deck telling you he hasn't read his orders. Do what you're going to do. But do it fast."

He went down the ladder to the launch, and at the bottom she called after him—quietly, the first time he'd heard her voice come close to unguarded:

"Vane. The cave. Under the point, when the tide drops far enough. If you knew anything about what's down there—"

"I'd tell you the same thing I just did," he said. "Do it fast."

He ran the launch back across the black water, the folded order in his pocket, and behind him the *Kestrel's* anchor light burned steady over the grave of the *Merrow*—and over whatever else the low tide was about to give back.
