## 5. Night Work

The tide turned at 11:47, and Rosalind wanted to be on the wreck before it did.

She stood at the rail of the *Kestrel* while Elias killed the running lights and let the boat drift the last hundred meters to the buoy. Greyhaven Point was a black shoulder against a sky bruised purple with cloud, and the water below lay flat and oily, holding what little light remained like something cupped in both hands.

"Visibility will be nothing down there," Elias said. He wasn't arguing, exactly. He was giving her every reason to call it off, the way a man tests a rope by loading it.

"Then I'll bring my own light." She checked the gauge on her tank for the third time. Full. Checked the backup light clipped to her harness. Checked the line reel. "You hold station. If I'm not up in forty minutes—"

"I know."

"If I'm not up in forty minutes, you go back to the harbor and you report this as an unauthorized night dive by a diver you tried repeatedly to stop. You make it convincing. There's a career to protect here that isn't mine."

Elias looked at her across the dark deck, and she saw him decide not to answer that. He'd been deciding not to answer things all week—the concession notice folded in his jacket pocket like a knife he kept forgetting to sheathe, the way he'd said *Joubert* once, quietly, when he thought she couldn't hear, and then never mentioned it again.

"Forty minutes," he said instead. "I'll run the timer. And Rosalind—" His voice caught on something. "The squall forecast moved up. Weather service says it could hit before midnight now. If the wind comes up while you're under, I can't hold this position."

"Then I'll come up somewhere else." She pulled her hood over her head. "That's what I do."

"That's not what I meant."

She paused with one leg over the gunwale, the black water breathing below her. "What did you mean?"

He didn't say it. She hadn't expected him to. That was the shape of them: two people standing on opposite sides of a rule, leaning closer and closer to the same edge without ever quite admitting they were looking at the same thing.

She rolled into the sea.

* * *

Down, the world contracted to a coin of lamplight.

Rosalind dropped through cold layers of water, equalizing, watching her depth tick past thirty meters, forty, until the wreck rose out of the dark beneath her like a drowned cathedral. The *Meridian Star*. Forty years on the bottom had given her a skin of anemones and rust weeping orange into the current, but her bones were still sound—hull plates, cargo cranes, the great gash along her starboard side where the navy's official report said she'd struck uncharted rock.

Her grandfather had died proving otherwise.

She swam the length of the gash, following the survey notes from the chest in the cave, the ones written in his cramped hand: *cargo manifests filed as machine parts, ballast listed as ore. Ask why the holds were sealed before the inquiry.* The strongroom was aft, below the bridge, behind a watertight door that had held its seal for four decades because no one had ever bothered to open it. No one, apparently, except the navy divers who had surveyed the site in the first week and reported—per the documents Elias had pulled from the harbor archive—*nothing of evidentiary value recovered.*

They'd opened it. They just hadn't taken everything.

The door was swollen in its frame. Rosalind worked her pry bar into the seam and leaned into it, bubbles streaming from her regulator, the lamp beam shuddering with her effort. Metal groaned somewhere deep in the wreck, a long complaint like a ship talking in its sleep. Then the door gave six inches with a shriek of corrosion, and her light fell into the space beyond.

Papers. Even after forty years, even sodden and fused into bricks, there were papers—because whoever had sealed them in oilcloth had done it to last. She filled the waterproof bag strapped inside her suit, working fast, hands clumsy in the cold, taking bundles by feel and marking them against her grandfather's inventory scrawled on a dive slate tied to her wrist. Manifests. Charter agreements. A ledger with a company name stamped on its cover that she recognized from the concession notice in Elias's pocket: **MARROW & FINCH SHIPPING**.

The same company that would be anchored over this wreck in five days, with permits signed by men who thought no one would ever read what was in this room.

Her timer buzzed against her wrist. Thirty-five minutes. She kicked for the gap in the hull and the long climb toward a surface that suddenly felt very far away.

* * *

She came up fifty meters off the stern, which was wrong—the buoy should have been dead ahead—and the first thing she understood was the wind.

It came off the point in a hard gusting shove, flattening the swell into whitecaps, and the *Kestrel*'s riding light was nowhere in the dark. Rosalind treaded water with the document bag pressed to her chest and felt the cold begin its patient work on her hands and feet. She swept her lamp across the water. Nothing. Swept it again, slower.

A sound reached her—engine, throttled low, hunting. Then a flashlight clicked twice, three times, a stuttered rhythm that wasn't any signal she knew, and she realized it didn't matter what it meant. It meant *here.*

"Elias!" Her voice cracked apart in the wind.

The engine surged. The *Kestrel* materialized out of the murk bow-first, driving straight at her, and Elias was at the wheel with his collar whipping and rain starting to sting sideways out of the squall's leading edge. He brought her alongside close enough to grab, and she got one hand on the rail and he got his fist in her harness and hauled, and for one suspended second she hung between the sea and him, weightless, the whole ocean dropping away beneath her boots—

—and then she was on deck, gasping, and his hands were still gripping her arms like he'd forgotten they were attached to a person.

"You were supposed to stay on the buoy," he said.

"The buoy dragged. So did yours, by the look of it."

"The buoy dragged," he agreed, and something in his face broke loose—not quite laughter, more like the thing laughter is made from before it decides what to be. Rain ran down both of them. He didn't let go. Neither did she.

"Forty-one minutes," he said finally. "You're getting slow."

"Get us out of this squall, Vane, and I'll dive faster next time."

He let go then, and went to the wheel, and she sat down hard on the deck locker with the bag clutched in her lap and her heart going like a bilge pump. Through the salt-frosted glass of the wheelhouse she watched him work the boat—watched the competence of his hands, the economy of him, a man doing exactly the thing he was built for in exactly the conditions that should have made it impossible. The harbor master sworn to enforce the rules, running illegal surface cover through a squall for a Joubert.

She thought: *this is how it starts.* Not with declarations. With someone holding station for you in bad weather.

* * *

They ran for the lee of the point with the rain hammering the deck and neither of them speaking much, because there was nothing to say that the wind wouldn't take anyway. When at last the *Kestrel* settled at anchor in the sheltered water behind Greyhaven Point, Elias came aft and found her already prying open the first oilcloth bundle under the cabin light.

Documents spread across the chart table like evidence at a tribunal, which was precisely what they were. Cargo manifests in three hands. Charter amendments initialed by men whose titles mattered more than their names. And on the top sheet, a declaration of cargo—machine parts, agricultural equipment—beside a stowage plan showing ore concentrates loaded in the same holds, dense enough to sink the stability calculations of a ship her size.

"They misdeclared the cargo," Rosalind said quietly. "Overloaded her, falsified the manifest so the insurers would never ask questions, and when she went down, the navy helped bury it because Marrow & Finch held the salvage concession then too. Same company. Same families. My grandfather found out and they took his name away for it."

Elias stood very still, reading. She watched him reach the page where the inquiry had been closed—*no evidence of corporate negligence established*—and watched his jaw set.

"My father signed that," he said. "Deputy harbor master, 1989. He used to drink himself quiet every March and never told me why." He looked up. "This is why. He knew."

"So now you know too."

"Yes." He said it the way other men say vows. "And now it has to survive five days, Rosalind. Callum Reyes lands with the fleet in five days, and if these papers are aboard your boat when he arrives, they'll end up at the bottom of the harbor."

"Then they don't stay aboard my boat."

"No," he agreed. "They don't." He gathered the bundles carefully, wrapping them in oilskin again, and held them a moment before handing them back to her—an odd little ceremony, a man surrendering custody and acknowledging it wasn't his to keep. "There's a place behind my office wall where the old tide gauges used to mount. Nobody remembers it's hollow."

"You're hiding contraband in a government building."

"I'm hiding the truth in a government building," he said. "There's a difference, and on Friday everyone in Greyhaven is going to learn it."

Outside, the squall was moving off, dragging the clouds east, and between the broken ranks of them the moon found the water. Rosalind looked at Elias across the chart table—at this man who had served her a permit expiry notice with one hand and held his boat steady for her in a squall with the other—and felt the last of her instinct to run go quiet, the way a sea goes quiet after weather.

"Thank you," she said. "For coming after me."

"I'd have come after you in a hurricane." He said it flatly, like a fact about tides, and then seemed to hear himself, and looked away first.

She smiled at the chart table, at the ghost-ink of a dead man's handwriting, at the town she still refused to call home and was beginning, traitorously, to love.

Five days. On the sixth tide, the fleet would come—and now she knew they were bringing more than barges.

Because folded inside the last bundle, half-hidden beneath the manifests, was a letter on Marrow & Finch letterhead, dated three weeks ago, addressed to a name she recognized from the harbor office roster:

**Councilman Aldous Pryce — re: advance payment, expedited concession transfer.**

Someone in Greyhaven had already sold them the wreck.
