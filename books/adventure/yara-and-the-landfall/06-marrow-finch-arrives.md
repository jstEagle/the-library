## 6. Marrow & Finch Arrives

They came in before dawn, three days ahead of any permit that could justify them.

Rosalind heard them first—the deep, patient throb of diesel engines idling off Greyhaven Point, the sound of money waiting to be spent. She was on the *Kestrel's* deck with a mug of tea going cold in her hands, watching the horizon turn from charcoal to pewter, and there they were: four vessels in echelon, hulls black against the last of the night, running lights still burning like eyes that hadn't decided to blink.

The lead ship flew no flag. That was the message. Marrow & Finch didn't need flags. They needed nothing so decorative as permission.

She counted masts, counted cranes, counted the flat gray shapes on the aft decks that could only be ROV launch rails. Then she went below and woke Devlin by shaking his shoulder once, hard.

"Company's here," she said. "Early."

Devlin sat up in his bunk, hair flattened on one side, and looked at her with the particular dread of a man doing arithmetic he already hated. "Permits don't run out for three more tides."

"They know that."

"Then what are they—"

"Anchoring," Rosalind said. "In our site. All four of them."

By full light it was worse than she'd guessed. The fleet had taken up station in a rough arc across the wreck grounds themselves, anchors down on the very seabed where the *Vigilant* lay broken, and their anchor chains would be dragging furrows through two hundred years of silt and history while lawyers on shore sorted out whose fault that legally was. A survey launch—white hull, unmarked tender—was already circling the point, its transducer arm lowered into the water like a finger testing a bath.

Elias Vane's harbor launch came out to meet them at eight. Rosalind watched from her rail as the little boat ran the line of the fleet, and even at this distance she could read the geometry of it: Vane holding position between the survey tender and the wreck site, radio antenna whipping in the wind, a man making a stand that everyone involved understood to be symbolic. The fleet didn't answer him for twenty minutes. When they finally did, they simply moved the tender another hundred meters east.

Rosalind turned away before she saw anything else, because something else was happening on her own deck. A RIB had detached from the lead vessel without fanfare—a small black inflatable riding low and fast—and it was coming straight for the *Kestrel*, throwing a wake like a drawn blade.

She knew the silhouette of the man steering it before she could see his face. She'd known it for eleven years, across four oceans and a dozen dive sites, back when she'd been someone else entirely.

"Devlin," she said quietly. "Go below."

"Who is—"

"*Below.*"

* * *

Callum Reyes came up the ladder the way he did everything—easily, like the world owed him handholds. He was broader than she remembered, silver shot through the black at his temples now, wearing a Marrow & Finch fleece over salt-stained canvas trousers, and when he smiled at her from the deck of her own boat it was the same smile from the old days: warm, knowing, faintly amused, the smile of a man who always knew more about you than you wanted.

"Rosalind Fairweather," he said, tasting the name. "Or should I say—"

"Don't." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. She made herself lean against the rail, arms crossed, as if this were a social call. As if her pulse weren't slamming against her wrists. "You're anchoring on a protected site, Callum. Permits expire in three tides. Your survey tender is dragging gear through a grave."

"The permits are paper, Roz." He spread his hands. "Paper gets rewritten. That's why I'm here instead of a lawyer. I asked for this meeting specifically."

"You asked?"

"I told them I knew the local diver." His eyes moved over her face with unhurried familiarity, cataloguing changes—the scar above her eyebrow he didn't know the origin of, the lines the last decade had carved. "I said: I've worked with her. She's the best salvage diver on this coast, maybe on any coast. And she's reasonable. She'll listen to numbers."

"There are no numbers you can afford."

"Everyone has a number." He stepped closer, and dropped his voice, and the warmth in it became something almost gentle, which was worse. "Yours, though—I remember yours. You were never in it for money. You were in it for distance. For being nobody, somewhere new. Am I wrong?"

Rosalind said nothing. The tide slapped the hull. Out past the point, the fleet sat on her grandfather's grave like gulls on a carcass.

"So here's the shape of it." Callum pulled a folded sheet from his jacket—not a contract, just figures, handwritten, which somehow made it more insulting. "Stand down. Hand over whatever you've recovered, sign a consultation agreement, advise our survey team through the cave systems—you know that water better than anyone alive. In exchange: twelve percent of gross, paid offshore, no name attached. You keep being Rosalind Fairweather. You keep your little boat, your little crew, your clean anonymous life." He tapped the page. "That's worth more to you than the wreck is. And we both know it."

"You think you know what I am," Rosalind said.

"I *know* what you are." The gentleness fell away, all at once, like a mask catching on something. "I certified half those cards in your bag, Roz. The deep mixed-gas ticket. The saturation endorsement. I watched you log dives you never made, sites you never touched, because you needed a résumé fast enough to disappear behind, and I signed my name to it because—" he paused, and something almost human crossed his face, "—because you were the best natural diver I ever saw, and it seemed like paperwork was the only thing standing between you and the water. I never held it against you. But it's held over you, every day since. By me. Because I kept the file."

The world narrowed to the space between them, six feet of wet decking and eleven years of silence.

"You kept the file," she repeated.

"I keep everything. It's a professional habit." He shrugged, and the shrug was an apology and a threat in the same motion. "One phone call, Roz. One email to the maritime board, the insurance syndicates, the concession registry—all of it unravels. Every job you've worked under that certification becomes fraud retroactively. Every captain who trusted you gets sued. Every crew gets audited. Your friend Devlin loses his livelihood because he dove with a ghost." He let that sit. "So take the twelve percent. Take the exit. Be smart about disappearing, one last time. That's not a threat. That's me trying to save you."

And God help her, some cold animal part of her brain—the part that had kept her alive in black water and bad marriages of convenience and a decade of false names—actually considered it. Twelve percent, offshore, unnamed. She could be gone within a week. New port, new name, new set of forged papers; she'd done it before, she knew exactly how it was done. It would cost her Elias, and the chest in her cabin with her grandfather's handwriting in it, and the strange raw feeling of a town that had started, without asking her, to feel like hers. It would cost her everything she'd only just realized she wanted.

It would be easy. That was the horror of it. Running was always easy for her. It was the one skill her family had left her.

Rosalind looked at Callum Reyes, her oldest friend in the industry, the man who had taught her to mix gas and read a wreck and lie beautifully, and she felt the fear move through her like cold water through a flooded suit—and then, beneath it, something harder settle into place.

"No," she said.

"Roz—"

"No." She pushed off the rail and stood square, and found that her hands were steady. "Here's what you've got wrong, Callum. You think the certifications are the thing I'm afraid of losing. They're not. I've been dead before—two other names, two other ports. I know how to be nobody. I'm *good* at it." She stepped toward him. "What I'm not going to do is sell out a town that took me in without asking who I was, to a company that's anchoring on a mass grave three days before its permits allow. What I'm not going to do is help you strip that wreck and call it salvage. And what I'm really not going to do is pretend, for one more hour, that staying invisible was ever the same thing as staying safe."

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him recalibrate—the old partner giving way to the company man, regret giving way to procedure.

"That's your answer, then."

"That's my answer."

"Then the file goes out tonight." He said it without heat, which was how she knew he meant it. "Board, registry, insurers. You'll lose the boat, probably. Possibly face charges. I'm sorry, Roz. I genuinely am." He turned for the ladder, then stopped, one hand on the rail, and glanced back with the ghost of the old smile. "For what it's worth—the Joubert girl I knew would have taken the money."

"She drowned," Rosalind said. "A long time ago. In water shallower than this."

He went down the ladder and the RIB pulled away, and she stood at the rail until it was a speck against the fleet's black hulls, doing the math she couldn't avoid anymore. Tonight the file went out. Tomorrow she'd be a fraud on paper, unlicensed, unemployable, possibly indicted—and none of it would matter if Marrow & Finch stripped the *Vigilant* before the hearing could happen, before the manifest in her cabin and the survey in her grandfather's chest could be laid on a table and believed.

Three tides. Maybe fewer, now, because companies that anchored early wouldn't wait for tides either.

Behind her, Devlin emerged from the cabin, pale. "I heard enough," he said. "Through the hatch. I'm sorry, Rosalind. About all of it. But mostly I'm sorry about what I have to tell you."

She turned. "Tell me what."

Devlin pointed east, toward the point, toward the cliffs where the cave mouth lay exposed only at extreme low water—and where, right now, the fleet's white survey tender had come to rest, dead on station, its crane swinging a sonar array out over the water directly above the entrance.

"They're not surveying the wreck," he said. "They're surveying the cave. Somebody told them it was there."

And Rosalind already knew who, because there was exactly one person besides herself and Elias who'd seen the chart notation in her grandfather's logbook—one person she'd shown it to, three nights ago, over brandy and trust: Councilman Aldous Pryce, who'd promised to raise it at the next session.

The file would go out tonight. The cave might not last until morning.

Rosalind reached for the radio and called the harbor office, and got no answer but static.
