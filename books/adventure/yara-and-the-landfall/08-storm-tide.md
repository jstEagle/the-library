## 8. Storm Tide

The barometer had been falling all night, and by dawn the harbor wore a bruised grey light that made every mast look like a splinter driven into the town's ribs. Rosalind stood on the *Kestrel's* deck with her grandfather's logbook zipped inside her drysuit, against her sternum, where it had lived for three days now like a second heartbeat.

Two tides. That was all the concession had left, and the storm had eaten one of them already.

She could hear the fleet before she saw it—diesel generators coughing to life out past the point, the flat metallic clang of a decompression chamber being bolted down on a barge deck. Marrow & Finch hadn't waited for the permits to lapse. Callum Reyes had never in his life waited for anything he could simply take.

"They're running dive lines," Elias said beside her. He'd come aboard without knocking, which was its own kind of message. His oilskin was beaded with spray and his jaw was set at the angle she'd learned meant he'd spent the morning arguing with someone who outranked him. "Councilman Hale signed them a temporary work order at six this morning. Called it an 'emergency stabilization.' The ink wasn't even dry."

"Can he do that?"

"He can do anything until someone makes him stop." Elias looked out at the fleet—three vessels riding high and hungry beyond Greyhaven Point, their crane arms folded like mantises waiting for something to walk into range. "I filed the objection. It'll take forty-eight hours to process. We have two tides."

"So we don't file." Rosalind pulled her hood up. "We dive."

Elias turned to her then, and what was in his face wasn't surprise. She'd told him about the strongroom two nights ago—the captain's safe below the aft hold, the one place on the wreck that would hold documents heavy enough to survive salt and time: sealed manifests, the misdeclared cargo manifest, whatever orders the navy had issued and buried. Everything else they'd recovered pointed toward it. Everything else was circumstantial.

"The surge is already running four meters in the gullies," he said. "By slack water it'll be worse. A storm surge doesn't give you slack water, Rosalind. It gives you a rumor of one."

"I know."

"You nearly drowned at Tresco. I read the incident report when I was checking your—" He stopped himself, but not fast enough.

"My falsified certs," she finished flatly. "Yes. I know what's in my file, Elias. I wrote half of it." She held his eyes. "I'm asking you to run surface cover. Not to approve. Not to forgive. Just to keep the line and the radio while I go down and get the thing that proves my grandfather didn't sink that ship."

The wind gusted, walking white fingers across the harbor. Down the quay, the first of the old-timers were gathering outside the chandlery, watching the fleet the way you watch a fire cross a ridge toward your house. Mrs. Aldous from the bakery stood with her arms crossed. Old Tam Weller, who'd cursed the Joubert name in Rosalind's hearing her very first week in town, stood apart from everyone, staring at the water.

Elias exhaled through his teeth. "Slack water in ninety minutes. You get twenty at depth, no more. If the line goes taut twice, I'm hauling you up whether you're holding proof or holding rocks."

"That's fair."

"And Rosalind." He caught her arm before she turned away. His grip was careful, the way he handled everything—like a man who'd learned that most things break easier than they look. "Come back up. Whatever's down there, it's been down there sixty years. It doesn't need you to die for it. It needs you to *live* and carry it into that hearing."

She nodded, because if she spoke her voice would have cracked, and cracked voices don't get to command dives.

* * *

The world beneath Greyhaven Point in a storm was nothing like the world she'd trained in. It was a war between directions. The surge came in long rolling pulses, shoving her sideways across the wreck's spine, then sucking her back as though the sea wanted to swallow its own dead twice. Visibility came and went in brown curtains. Her torch beam swung wildly, catching glimpses—hull plating furred green, a deadeye hanging by one rope, the brass fitting stamped with the Joubert crest still lashed where she'd secured it as a marker, glinting like an accusation.

She followed her own guideline down through the broken quarterdeck, fins finding the memory of the route even when her eyes couldn't. The logbook pressed against her chest. *Hold fast, girl,* she thought, in her grandfather's voice, the voice she'd reconstructed from a dead man's handwriting. *The truth keeps. But only if someone carries it.*

The strongroom hatch was where the plans said it would be—under the collapsed chart table, behind a plate she'd found and marked on the night dive with Elias running lights above. Her knife scraped thirty years of concretion off the hinge bolts. The surge hit again, a full-body blow that tore her mask askew and ripped her glove against plating; she felt the cold bite in before she felt the pain. She pressed herself flat against the hull and let the pulse pass over her like a train.

Breathe. Work. Breathe.

The third bolt gave. The fourth seized. She hammered it with the knife butt, once, twice—and on the third strike the whole hatch frame groaned and shifted, and the sea reached in through the gap and took the hatch cover out of her hands entirely, slamming it against the bulkhead with a clang she felt in her teeth.

Inside: blackness, and sediment, and a steel box bolted to the deck, crusted but intact. The captain's strongroom. The captain's safe.

Her gauge said eleven minutes left. The line above had gone slack, then taut, then slack—Elias paying it out and taking it back with each surge, keeping her tethered to the living world by a thread of braided rope and, she suspected, sheer fury.

The safe's dial wouldn't turn. Of course it wouldn't. Sixty years underwater. She jammed the pry bar into the door seam and threw her whole weight against it, and the surge threw its weight against *her*, and for ten seconds she was nothing but a body being argued over by forces that did not care about manifests or hearings or names.

The seam cracked. The door peeled open like a tin.

Inside, wrapped in tarred canvas that had somehow held: a lead-lined document case. Heavy. Real. She hauled it out, clipped it to her harness, and checked her air—eight hundred psi, and the swim back through the collapsing quarterdeck suddenly looked like a mile of open ocean.

That was when the line went taut and stayed taut.

She signaled—two sharp pulls, *problem*—and got nothing back but tension singing in the rope. Then the tension *changed*, jerking her upward, hard, and she understood: Elias was hauling. Something had gone wrong above, and he was pulling her out of the deep whether she was ready or not.

She kicked for the guideline. The surge caught her mid-stroke and drove her into the wreck's superstructure shoulder-first. Pain bloomed white. Her regulator bumped against a plate and spat a mouthful of silt into the demand valve, and she coughed, and coughing underwater is how divers die, and for three full seconds she was choking, blind, alone, the document case dragging at her hip like an anchor chained to the truth itself.

*Live and carry it.*

She got her thumb on the purge. Cleared the valve. Found the guideline with her free hand and *pulled* herself along it, hand over hand, not swimming anymore, just climbing the rope out of hell. The light above came down in shafts—grey, then silver, then the boiling underside of the surface, and then she broke through into rain and chaos and Elias's hands closing around her harness and hauling her over the gunwale like she weighed nothing at all.

* * *

She lay on the deck of the launch, coughing seawater, the document case locked in both arms like a child. Elias crouched over her, cutting her harness straps because her fingers had stopped working, and his face was the color of old rope.

"Tender," he said, when she could breathe. "Marrow & Finch put a tender in the water twenty minutes ago. They're running a survey line right over the wreck. I put the launch between them and the site and told them the concession was still valid. Their coxswain didn't care. Their *surveyor* did." His mouth twisted. "Callum says hello. Says if you're done playing hero, he'll buy you breakfast and a lawyer."

Rosalind sat up. The harbor was heaving, rain coming in horizontal sheets, and beyond the point the fleet's floodlights burned through the murk like eyes. The tender was still out there, circling, patient as a shark.

"How long do I have?" she asked.

"Hearing's tomorrow, nine o'clock, admiralty court, moved up because of the storm." Elias took the document case from her arms—not taking it from her, she noticed, just holding it steady while her hands shook—and wiped the canvas clean with his sleeve. "Whatever's in there, it better be enough."

She unzipped her drysuit with numb fingers and drew out her grandfather's logbook, swollen with salt and three days of her body heat, and laid it on top of the case. Two objects. Sixty years between them.

"It's enough," she said. And then, watching the tender's running lights swing closer through the rain: "But Callum knows where I am, Elias. And he knows what I'm carrying. He won't wait for the hearing to try to stop me."

Elias started the engine. "Then we'd best not wait either."

The launch surged forward into the storm, toward the harbor lights, carrying the last proof of the Joubert name home through the dark.
