## 9. The Hearing

The admiralty court had been a customs house once, back when Greyhaven still mattered to the shipping lanes, and it had never quite shed the habit of smelling like wet rope and old money. Elias Vane stood at the back of the room with his hat in his hands and his harbor master's insignia in his coat pocket, where it had lived since yesterday, when he'd peeled it off and set it on Councilman Aldous Pryce's desk like a man laying down a losing hand.

Pryce hadn't even looked up. "You'll regret this, Vane. Marrow & Finch doesn't forget."

"Good," Elias had said. "Neither do I."

Now the room filled around him—counsel in dark wool, admiralty clerks with their ledgers chained to their wrists, and behind them the whole breathing weight of Greyhaven: fishermen who'd come in off the water without changing their boots, Mrs. Trelawney from the chandlery wearing her late husband's good coat, the Okafor sisters from the lightkeeper's cottage, three generations of them stacked on one bench like weathered pilings. The town had shut down for this. The fleet sat idle in the harbor, and every dory that could float was tied up because nobody wanted to be anywhere but here.

Rosalind Joubert sat at the claimant's table with her hands folded and her spine straight, and Elias thought she looked like a woman standing at attention before a firing squad she'd already decided not to flinch from. She'd dressed for the occasion in a plain grey jacket, her hair pinned back, no jewelry except—and he'd noticed this, because he noticed everything about her now—the small brass compass on a cord at her throat. Her grandfather's compass. The one that had spent forty years in a sealed chest at the bottom of a sea cave and still pointed true.

Across the aisle, Callum Reyes sat with the Marrow & Finch delegation, immaculate in a charcoal suit, and when his eyes found Rosalind they held something that wasn't quite cruelty. It was worse than cruelty. It was certainty.

The presiding commissioner was a dry stick of a man named Wexler, flown in from the capital, who opened proceedings by observing that this was an irregular hearing convened under irregular circumstances, and that he intended to proceed irregularly but thoroughly. Then he called the first witness, and the machinery began to turn.

* * *

They came at her the way he'd known they would.

Marrow & Finch's counsel—a sleek, expensive man named Harrow—opened with the concession itself. The permits were expired. The site was contested. The company held prior documentation of survey interest dating back eleven years. He spoke in the smooth cadence of a man reading a grocery list, and Elias watched the commissioners nod along, because paperwork has its own gravity and it pulls everything toward the company that can afford to generate it.

Then Harrow paused, adjusted his glasses, and said, "Before we address the question of the wreck's disposition, we must address the question of the claimant herself."

Elias's jaw tightened.

"The claimant," Harrow said, "is not who she presents herself to be." He produced a folder with the unhurried grace of a magician producing a dove. "Rosalind Joubert is, I grant you, her legal name. But her professional credentials—" he let the word hang, savoring it "—are not what her résumé suggests. We have obtained records from the Southern Maritime Certification Board. The dive certifications held by Ms. Joubert were issued under a name that does not appear in any registry. Further certifications were amended retroactively. Depth ratings altered. Log hours inflated."

He turned to face Rosalind directly, and the room turned with him.

"She is a competent diver, I'm sure. Perhaps even an excellent one. But she is not a certified salvage master, and every hour she spent on that wreck—every dive, every recovery operation conducted under these falsified credentials—was performed illegally. The commission should understand that the woman asking you to award this wreck to Greyhaven is asking you to trust her judgment. And her judgment, as documented, includes a decade of deception."

The silence afterward had texture. Elias could feel it pressing against his eardrums.

Wexler peered over his spectacles at Rosalind. "Ms. Joubert. Is this accurate?"

Elias watched her take a breath. Watched her hands stay folded. When she spoke, her voice was level and clear and carried to the back of the room without effort, the way voices carry when they've spent years talking through water.

"It's accurate."

A ripple went through the benches behind him. Someone said her name—not Joubert, the other one, the one the old families used like a curse—and Mrs. Trelawney shushed them hard enough to be heard in the harbor.

"I falsified my certifications," Rosalind continued. "I was nineteen years old, my family name was worth less than nothing in this industry, and I wanted to work. So I worked. Under other names, other papers, for ten years. I've logged more bottom time than half the certified masters in this room's Rolodex, and I've brought up more bodies and more cargo than most of them will see in a career. But yes. The papers are false. I won't insult this commission by pretending otherwise."

"And yet," Harrow said smoothly, "you ask us to accept your word regarding the contents of a chest recovered from a cave, regarding manifests whose provenance you alone can attest to—"

"No," said a voice from the back, and the room turned.

Old Tomas Okafor was on his feet, ninety if he was a day, holding the edge of the bench with both hands. His granddaughter tried to pull him down; he shook her off with the ease of long practice.

"No, she doesn't ask that. Because she isn't the only one who can vouch." He looked at Wexler. "Commissioner. My father crewed on the *Meridian*. He was one of the six who got off alive. He spent the rest of his life saying the cargo manifest was wrong—that ship was carrying naval stores marked as ballast, and everyone on that dock knew it and nobody would say it, because the company that owned the freight was the same company that owned the harbor leases and half the council. My father died saying it. Half the old families here grew up hearing it at supper tables. It wasn't a secret. It was just a thing nobody could prove."

He pointed a knotted finger at Rosalind's table.

"Until she proved it."

* * *

They brought out the evidence after that, and the afternoon belonged to the dead.

The chest came in first, carried by two constables and set on the trestle table like a relic, salt-crusted and iron-banded, the lock green with forty years of sea. Inside it, laid out under glass now for the commission: Edouard Joubert's original hydrographic survey of Greyhaven Point, dated thirty-one years before the first Marrow & Finch permit application. The ink had faded to brown but the lines were crisp as conscience—the cave charted, the approaches sounded, the wreck site plotted with a precision that made one of the junior commissioners sit forward despite himself.

"He surveyed it first," Rosalind said, standing. "My grandfather charted that cave and that wreck before this company existed. Before the harbor authority existed. Under the coastal claims act, an original survey by a licensed hydrographer establishes priority of discovery. That's the law. You can read it yourselves—I didn't write it."

"You are not a licensed hydrographer," Harrow objected.

"No. But he was. And the license number is right there in the margin. Check it."

Wexler checked it. A clerk was dispatched to the registry office, and while they waited, the manifests came out—the ones Rosalind and Elias had pulled off the wreck in the squall, nearly dying for them, though Elias noticed she left that part out entirely. Naval ordnance stores, misdeclared as stone ballast, insured twice over through shell companies that traced, through four layers of paper, to the corporate ancestor of Marrow & Finch. The cover-up wasn't a theory anymore. It was arithmetic.

And then the families testified.

Mrs. Trelawney spoke about her grandmother, who'd kept the lighthouse lamp burning the night the *Meridian* went down and swore until her death that the distress rockets came from the wrong bearing—that the ship was inside the point, not outside it, exactly where a captain would put her if he were trying to shelter rather than run. The Okafors produced letters. The Marchetti brothers, who ran the boatyard, read aloud from a ledger their great-uncle had kept of the salvage rights sold off in 1911 for a fraction of their value, to buyers whose names matched the shell companies in the manifests.

Piece by piece, all afternoon, the town laid its memory on the table like a hand of cards, and Elias stood at the back and watched forty years of silence come apart at the seams.

Harrow fought rearguard actions. He questioned provenance. He questioned chain of custody. He questioned whether testimony from descendants constituted evidence or folklore. Wexler, dry as driftwood, answered each objection with variations on the same sentence: "The commission will weigh it."

But it was Callum Reyes who landed the last blow, and he did it standing, quietly, without Harrow's polish.

"For the record," he said, "Marrow & Finch withdraws its objection to the historical evidence. Our position is simple. Whatever happened in 1911, the company operating today bears no responsibility for it, and the current concession framework governs current assets. If the commission awards this wreck to the town, so be it. We'll contest the valuation elsewhere." He gathered his papers. "We're done here."

It should have felt like victory. Instead it felt like a door closing softly somewhere far away, because Elias knew—as everyone in that room who'd watched Callum look at Rosalind knew—that a man like Reyes didn't concede anything. He deferred. There was a difference, and the difference had teeth.

* * *

Wexler delivered the finding at dusk, reading from his notes in a voice like a tide table.

The commission found the original Joubert survey valid and controlling. The commission found the documentary evidence of misdeclared cargo sufficient to establish the wreck's historical significance. The commission found, further, that the cave system constituted an integral part of the site, and that the whole—wreck, cave, and approaches—was hereby awarded to the incorporated town of Greyhaven in perpetuity, free of commercial salvage claim, designated for heritage preservation.

The room erupted. Old Tomas Okafor was crying openly. Somebody started singing—an old hauling song, low and ragged—and half the room joined in before Wexler banged his gavel and failed, entirely, to stop them.

Elias didn't move. He was watching Rosalind.

She stood very still amid the noise, one hand closed around the compass at her throat, and her face was doing something complicated—relief and grief arriving on the same wave, breaking together. She'd won. Her grandfather's name was clean. The wreck belonged to the town.

And then Wexler removed his spectacles, and the room quieted, because commissioners only remove their spectacles for the sentences that hurt.

"One matter remains," he said. "The claimant's certifications. The falsification of professional credentials is not within this commission's jurisdiction to forgive, and the withdrawal of Marrow & Finch does not extinguish the underlying regulatory violations. The certification board has been notified. Criminal referral is possible. At minimum, Ms. Joubert, your salvage master's standing is void, and you are barred from commercial diving operations pending review." He met her eyes, and there was something almost gentle in it. "You did a great deal of good, by the record. You also broke a great many rules to do it. This commission awards the wreck. What happens to you is another matter entirely."

The singing stopped.

Elias pushed through the crowd toward her, but the room was between them—constables, clerks, well-wishers, the whole tide of Greyhaven surging forward to touch her hands and say her name, her real name, some of them saying *Joubert* for the first time in forty years without spitting. By the time he reached the table she was gathering her grandfather's chest, and her eyes when they lifted to his were calm in the way he'd learned to distrust, the way of a woman checking her escape routes.

"Well," she said. "That's that."

"That's not that. They'll suspend the charges. Wexler basically said—"

"Wexler said it's out of his hands." She smiled, and it was the smile she wore like armor, the one that meant she was already halfway to the door in her head. "Ten years, Elias. Ten years I stayed ahead of exactly this. One hearing and I'm right back where I started—no papers, no name worth having, no—" She stopped. Looked down at the compass in her hand. "I should go before the board files whatever they're going to file. Traveling's easier before the paperwork catches up."

Something went cold in Elias's chest, slow and certain as a flood tide.

"You just spent six tides fighting for this town," he said. "You just gave it back its history. And your plan is to be over the horizon by Thursday?"

"My plan," she said quietly, "has always been to survive it."

She picked up the chest. Behind her, through the tall windows, the harbor lights were coming on one by one, and the *Meridian* lay black and patient in the swell, waiting to see whether the woman who'd saved her would stay to watch what she became—or slip away with the morning tide, the way the Jouberts always had.

Elias stepped into her path.

"Not this time," he said.
