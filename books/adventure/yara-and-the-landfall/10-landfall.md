## 10. Landfall

The hearing room smelled of old varnish and older grudges, and when it emptied, Rosalind sat alone at the long table for a full minute, listening to the tide against the seawall below the courthouse windows. Suspended sentence. Stripped certification. The admiralty magistrate had delivered it like a man reading a grocery list—no cruelty in it, just procedure, the machinery of consequence grinding on without malice. She had broken rules. She would pay for breaking them. That was fair, or as close to fair as the sea ever let anyone get.

What she couldn't shake was how light she felt.

Ten years of running, and the sentence that should have ended her freedom instead felt like an anchor dropped into good holding ground. She turned her hands over on the table. Salt still lived in the creases. It always would now.

Outside, Greyhaven was doing what Greyhaven did: arguing loudly about fish prices, quietly about everything else. But today the arguments had changed shape. She could feel it in the way Mrs. Okonkwo from the chandlery nodded to her across the street—not the careful nod reserved for outsiders, but the other kind, the kind that said *you're one of ours whether you like it or not*. The oldest families had testified for her. Old Man Trelawney, ninety-one and mostly deaf, had stood up in that hearing room and said, "The Jouberts built half this harbor before anyone decided to hate them," and the room had gone quiet in a way rooms rarely do.

She walked down to the water because her feet knew the way before she did.

* * *

Elias Vane was on the harbor office steps with two mugs of coffee and the expression of a man who'd rehearsed something and hated every draft.

"You look terrible," he said.

"Thanks. I nearly drowned Tuesday."

"I know. I was there." He handed her a mug. Steam curled between them. Below, the *Marrow & Finch* fleet was gone—all four vessels, weighed anchor at dawn without ceremony, leaving only oil slicks and a survey buoy nobody had bothered to retrieve. The wreck of the *Corvina* sat beneath them in forty feet of green dark, hers now. The town's. In perpetuity, the deed said, which was a word lawyers used and divers understood differently.

"Suspended sentence," Elias said. "Stripped cert."

"That's the official version."

"What's the unofficial one?"

Rosalind looked out at the harbor. Morning light came off the water in long silver bars. Her grandfather's compass was in her jacket pocket, heavy as a held breath. She'd carried it since the cave, since the chest, since the logbook that had rewritten her family's history in his own cramped hand. *They will say I sank her. Let whoever finds this know the cargo was never what they declared.*

"They gave me six months suspended," she said. "No commercial diving. Which means no salvage work anywhere that checks papers, and everyone checks papers." She laughed, short and without much humor. "I've spent ten years being nobody from nowhere. Turns out all I had to do to lose that was almost die in front of witnesses."

"You could run," Elias said. "South Pacific. Nobody asks questions past the third reef."

"I know." She sipped the coffee. It was terrible, harbor-office coffee, burnt twice over. "I've been running the route in my head since the courthouse. Fiji by Thursday. New name by Friday."

"And?"

"And I'm tired, Elias." The word came out of her like ballast released. "I'm so tired of being a stranger everywhere I go. You know what the strangest part is? I've been here three weeks and this is the first place in ten years where people say my name right. Where they say *Joubert* like it's a thing that belongs to the town instead of a thing that ran from it."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he reached into his coat and produced an envelope, soft-cornered, handled by many hands. She recognized the town council's seal.

"This came for you this morning," he said. "I could've mailed it. I didn't want to."

She opened it. Read it once fast, then again slow, because the first reading couldn't be trusted.

*The Greyhaven Heritage Trust, having secured title to the wreck of the S.S. Corvina and the adjoining sea cave, hereby offers employment to Rosalind Joubert in the capacity of Site Conservator and Dive Supervisor...*

Dive supervisor. On a stripped certification. They were offering her the job anyway—the town, in its stubborn, ungovernable way, simply declining to let a piece of paper from the admiralty have the last word. Restoration of the wreck as a heritage site. Mooring protection, artifact recovery, guided dives once the structure was stabilized. A salary. A cottage above the boatyard, rent deferred until she was "settled," whatever that meant to a council that had spent fifty years cursing her family name.

"They can't do this," she said. "Legally."

"Almost certainly not," Elias agreed. "Trelawney says the trust predates the current maritime code and dares anyone to check. He's been waiting fifty years for a fight with the admiralty and he intends to enjoy it."

She read the letter a third time. Her eyes caught on the signature line and stayed there. Beneath the chairman's name, someone had added a note in pencil, in handwriting she now knew better than her own: her grandfather's survey notes, photocopied, with one line underlined twice.

*The Joubert name belongs to this harbor. Come home and prove it.*

* * *

She signed the papers that afternoon at the kitchen table of the cottage, because the council chamber felt too much like a courtroom and she'd had enough courtrooms. Trelawney brought the contract himself, along with a bottle of something amber and illegal, and watched her sign with the satisfaction of a man closing a circle sixty years wide.

"Your grandfather drew the original survey of this harbor," he said, tapping the table. "Every mooring, every channel marker. Did you know that?"

"I know it now."

"He did it for free. Said a harbor isn't infrastructure, it's a promise." The old man stood, joints cracking like rigging in a blow. "We're just keeping his appointment."

After he left, Rosalind sat with the pen still warm in her hand and listened to the gulls argue over the boatyard. Through the window she could see the harbor office, its lamp already lit against the early dusk, and the small brass plaque Elias had apparently ordered without asking anyone: *GREYHAVEN HARBOUR MASTER — EST. 1897 — E. VANE.* Understated. Stubborn. Like him.

She walked down as the light went copper.

* * *

The harbor office smelled of chart ink and diesel and, faintly, of the coffee he refused to improve. Elias was bent over the tide tables, and he didn't look up until she set her grandfather's compass on the desk between them.

It was a beautiful thing even now, salt-pitted, the glass clouded, the needle swinging lazily toward a north only it believed in. Her grandfather had carried it through thirty years of surveys and one final dive from which he hadn't returned. It had waited in a sealed chest in a cold cave for longer than Rosalind had been alive. Some things kept.

"What's this?" Elias asked, though she could see he understood.

"A loan," she said. "Not a gift. I'm not giving things away anymore—I'm staying, and staying means keeping things." She turned the compass so its face caught the lamplight. "He surveyed this harbor. It seems right that it should watch over it. And it seems right that it should hang somewhere I'll see it every day, which means somewhere you are, which—" she stopped, exhaled, started over. "That's the actual point of this conversation, so let me say it plainly before I lose my nerve."

Elias leaned back in his chair. Waiting. He was good at waiting; it was half his job.

"I expected to run," she said. "I had the route planned. I've always had the route planned—that's the whole architecture of my life, exit routes dressed up as plans. And then the town handed me a job I'm not legally allowed to hold, and my grandfather handed me a compass, and you—" her voice did something unsteady and she let it, "—you held a boat steady in a squall for a woman you'd served a violation notice to three weeks earlier, and I thought, if I run now, I'll spend the rest of my life diving in warm clear water and wondering about a cold gray harbor and a man who makes terrible coffee."

"So don't run."

"So I'm not running." She looked at him straight on, the way she'd learned to look at surge and bad visibility and every other force that rewarded honesty. "I'm signing on here. Wreck restoration, five years minimum, probably the rest of my life if the paperwork holds. And I'm asking what you're offering, because you've had that expression for two days and I'd like to stop guessing."

For a moment he said nothing. Then he opened his desk drawer and took out a key. Not a dramatic key—a small brass one, dockside-ordinary, worn smooth.

"The boathouse," he said. "It leaks. The stove smokes. The previous tenant left a cat I haven't been able to catch." He set it beside the compass. "I'm offering Tuesday dinners. Bad coffee every morning. Someone who knows your name and uses it right. Nothing grand. Just—" he shrugged, and the shrug cost him more than the speech had, "—landfall. A place to come into from the water."

Rosalind picked up the key. It was warm from the drawer, from his keeping it there, from however long he'd been carrying it around waiting for her to ask.

"Tuesday dinners," she repeated.

"And Wednesdays, if the weather's foul."

"Deal."

* * *

She hung the compass herself, on the nail above the chart table where the harbor master's predecessors had hung their barometers and their photographs of ships that never came home. The needle swung, steadied, settled. North, more or less. North enough.

From the doorway, Elias watched her step back to inspect the work. Outside, the tide was turning, the water in the harbor slack and glassy for twenty minutes before it began its long pull back out to sea. Somewhere beyond the point, the *Corvina* lay in her new role—not a salvage claim, not a scandal, but a story the town would tell its children, with a Joubert's name in it at last for the right reasons.

"First day of work is Monday," Elias said. "Trelawney wants a full condition survey by spring."

"Then I'd better find a certification the admiralty doesn't know about," Rosalind said, and grinned when he raised an eyebrow. "I'm kidding. Mostly. There's a heritage-site exemption clause in the trust charter—Trelawney showed me. The old man has been planning this legal ambush for decades. I'm just the diver he finally got."

She took one last look at the compass, its needle holding true above the charts of a harbor her grandfather had measured and loved and died beside. Ten years of running, and it had ended here, in a room that smelled of diesel and ink, with a brass key in her pocket and a standing invitation to Tuesday dinner.

The tide began to move. Rosalind Joubert, site conservator, dive supervisor, daughter of Greyhaven, stepped out of the harbor office and into the evening, and did not look back at the road south.

But the compass, hanging in the lamplight, swung once—gently, like a hand waving—and pointed steadily out toward the open water, where the *Corvina* waited, and where, come Monday, there would be work.
