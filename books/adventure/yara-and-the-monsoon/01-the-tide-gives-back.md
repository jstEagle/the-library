## 1. The Tide Gives Back

The tide went out so far that morning it seemed the sea had died.

Jonah Iversen stood on the shingle at Greywater Cove and watched the water retreat like something ashamed, pulling back past the kelp beds, past the black rocks that had never seen air in his lifetime, until it exposed a long shelf of seabed glittering with stranded fish and old rope. The locals had come down to gawk. Children ran out to poke at sea stars. And there, at the far edge of the exposed shelf, a dark mouth opened in the cliff — a cave that appeared on no map Jonah had ever drawn or read.

He'd come to the cove for the same reason he came anywhere now: because it was cheap. The boarding house in Greywater charged four silver a week and didn't ask questions, which was more than could be said for most towns. His name was still poison in the guiding trade. Three years since the Kestrel Pass incident, and men who'd once fought for his services now found urgent business elsewhere when he walked into their offices. *The guide who lost them.* That's what they called him. Never mind that the coroner's report said avalanche, act of nature, no fault assigned. Names stick. Faults stick harder.

He was counting coins on the flat of his palm — eleven, and one of them suspect — when she walked up.

"You're Iversen." Not a question. She was maybe thirty, wind-burned, with a canvas satchel slung across her chest and the particular way of standing that people get when they've spent years measuring the world: feet planted, weight even, like ground was something you negotiated with rather than trusted. "Yara Voss. I need a guide."

"Then you need someone else."

"I need someone good, and everyone good is either dead or refuses to work this coast in autumn." She pulled a folded paper from her coat. "You come recommended anyway. By someone who doesn't like you much."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. "I'm not taking contracts."

"You're broke." Her eyes flicked to his open palm, to the coins, and she had the decency not to smirk about it. "I'll pay double the going rate. Triple if we finish inside two days."

He should have walked away. He told himself later that he stayed because of the money, but that was a lie he kept in a drawer with all the others. He stayed because of what she did next: she turned toward the cave mouth at the edge of the retreating sea and looked at it the way he used to look at unclimbed faces — with hunger, yes, but also with respect. Like it might be dangerous. Like it deserved to be.

"That cave," he said. "It's not on any chart."

"It's not on any chart because no one alive has seen it. The tide tables say an ebb like this comes once in sixty years. My instruments say tomorrow it comes back and takes the entrance with it for another sixty." She looked at him. "I'm a cartographer, Mr. Iversen. A place that exists for one day only — that's not a job to me. That's a debt."

"A debt to who?"

She hesitated, just a fraction, the first crack in her certainty. "To my father," she said. "Come or don't."

* * *

They went out across the seabed at mid-morning, when the ebb bottomed out. It was like walking on the floor of a drowned world — ribbons of bladderwrack, a rusted anchor chain thick as a man's arm, pools left behind in the rock holding shrimp and light. Jonah moved carefully, reading the ground the way he read snow: where it gave, where it held, which way the water would want to return. Old habits. The mountain had taken everything else from him, but it couldn't take the reading.

Yara kept pace without complaint, which he noted and approved of. She stopped every hundred meters to shoot bearings with a brass instrument she handled like a relic, sketching in a waterproof notebook with quick, sure strokes.

"You survey everything?" he asked.

"Everything. Land changes faster than people think. Rivers move. Cliffs fall. Maps are just arguments about where things were." She glanced up. "My father used to say a map is a promise you make to strangers. You'd better be sure you can keep it."

"What happened to him?"

Her pencil paused. "He made a promise somebody wanted broken. We can talk about that after, if we're both still breathing."

The cave mouth was taller than a church door and breathed cold salt air at them. Inside, the floor rose quickly above the tideline, dry as bone, and Jonah lit the lantern while Yara chalked marks along the wall — depth gauges, her own private system. The passage bent inland, climbing, and the smell changed from brine to stone to something older. Wax. Paper. Dust.

Then the light found the shelves.

They were cut into the living rock, three tiers of them, and stacked on those tiers were oilcloth-wrapped bundles gone stiff with age. Yara unwrapped the nearest one with hands that had suddenly stopped being steady. Ledgers. Dozens of them, bound in cracked leather, their spines stamped with a crest Jonah knew from every waystation sign between here and the capital: the Vale Mining Company. The company whose concession owned half this coast. The company whose payroll kept Greywater fed.

Yara opened the first ledger and went very quiet.

"Jonah." Her voice had changed. "Look at the dates."

He looked. Survey permits, land grants, transfer deeds — pages of official signatures, official seals. But the seals were wrong. He wasn't a lawyer, but he'd spent enough years dealing with permit offices to know that ink doesn't age evenly, that paper yellows from the edges in, and these documents were yellowed uniformly, artificially, like they'd been baked. Forged. All of them. The entire legal foundation of the Vale concession, laid down thirty years ago on paper that had never passed through a government office in its life.

"This is why your father—"

"Died disputing these permits. Yes." She was flipping pages fast now, photographing each one with her eyes. "They called it an accident. A fall on the Verrell scree. Everyone who looked into it too closely afterward had accidents too, smaller ones. Lost contracts. Lost homes." She looked up at him, and there was something raw under the cartographer's calm. "I was sixteen. I've spent fourteen years learning to draw maps well enough that a company would hire me to walk right into their territory and nobody would ask why."

"And the ledgers?"

"Proof. Real proof, with real dates and real handwriting, that the whole concession is built on nothing." She began wrapping bundles in her spare oilcloth, working fast. "We take what we can carry. The rest gets hidden better than it's been hidden for thirty years."

It was Jonah who spotted the other signs, because Jonah was the one who'd learned to look for them — the ones who come after you. Boot prints in the dust near the innermost shelf, recent, the tread pattern military-adjacent. A candle stub, still soft. And scratched into the wall beside the empty spot where one bundle clearly used to sit, a tally mark and a date from nine days ago.

Someone had been here already. Someone had taken one ledger and left the rest — or hadn't found the rest, or was coming back for them.

"We need to go," Jonah said. "Now."

Yara looked at the prints, then at him, and whatever she saw in his face made her stop arguing. They wrapped six ledgers into her satchel and his pack, reburied the remainder under collapsed rock from the shelf's edge, and were halfway back through the passage when the sound reached them — faint, carried over the returning water.

Voices. On the seabed outside.

Jonah killed the lantern and pulled her into a side hollow as the first wave slopped into the cave mouth below, cold and black and rising. Through the gap he could see figures out on the shelf, four of them, moving with purpose toward the entrance. Company coats. One of them held a lantern high, and its light swept the cave ceiling above their heads.

"They can't know we're here," Yara whispered.

"They don't have to know. They just have to be here when the tide does." He checked the water crawling up the passage — twenty minutes, maybe less, before the exit sealed. "Question is whether we wait them out in the dark, or find another way out before this becomes a tomb."

Above them, the lantern light swung closer, and gravel crunched under boots that had decided, today, to search again.
