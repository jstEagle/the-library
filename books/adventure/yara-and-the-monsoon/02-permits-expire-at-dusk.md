## 2. Permits Expire at Dusk

The smoke reached us before the flames did.

I was sketching the contour lines above the tideline when the wind changed, carrying that sweet, wrong smell of burning canvas and paraffin. Jonah saw it too. He didn't say anything — he just went still the way animals go still, head tilted, listening to something I couldn't hear yet.

"That's our tent," I said, though he already knew.

We'd left the camp below us because I wanted the elevation for the survey — a full hour's climb up the shoulder of the headland, enough to fix the cave's position against three visible peaks. An hour. That was all the distance between my instruments and whatever was down there eating them.

"Stay low," Jonah said. "Watch."

I watched. Through the heat shimmer I counted four figures moving through our camp with the unhurried efficiency of men doing a job they'd done many times. They weren't looting. Looting is greedy and fast. This was methodical: one of them fed pages from my field case into the fire while another held open a leather folio, checking things off against it. Checking off my maps. My father's maps, the ones I'd traced and re-traced until I knew every line by heart.

"They're taking inventory," I whispered. "Why would anyone take inventory of what they're about to burn?"

"Because someone above them wants proof it's gone." Jonah's voice was flat, but his knuckles had gone white around the strap of his pack. "That's not muscle. That's administration."

The word landed strangely in his mouth and stayed in mine. Administration. A tidy term for men burning a woman's life on a beach at dusk.

One of them straightened and turned toward the headland. Even at this distance I felt his gaze travel up the slope, patient as a lighthouse beam. He said something to the others. Two of them started climbing.

"Move," Jonah said.

* * *

We ran north along the ridge with the light dying behind us, and I hated how much I loved him at this — the way he read the ground like a sentence, choosing the line of least resistance without ever appearing to hurry. He'd been blacklisted from these ranges for six years, everyone said, run out of the guiding trade after some disaster he never spoke of. But his feet hadn't forgotten anything. Every scree slope, every goat track, every place the mountain offered a shortcut or a trap — he took them like a man returning to a house he'd once lived in.

We dropped into a ravine where a stream cut dark under alder roots, and there, with the fire glow finally hidden behind the ridge, he let us stop.

"Who are you?" I asked. It came out harsher than I meant. I was shaking, and anger is easier than fear. "Not the disgraced guide story. The real answer. Because those men back there aren't chasing a cartographer. Cartographers are boring. Nobody burns a boring woman's tent."

Jonah crouched by the stream and drank from cupped hands like the water might tell him something. When he spoke, he spoke to the current.

"I guided a company crew up the Kestrel Glacier eight years ago. Vale Mining's advance survey. There was an argument on the ice about route safety, and I lost the argument, and two men died. At least that's the version in the tribunal record." He wiped his mouth. "The true version is that the route was safe. The orders were wrong. They were mapping a corridor for heavy equipment that no sane engineer would have approved, and when I filed a dissent, the dissent disappeared, and so did my certification. So yes. I know exactly how their administration works."

The cold coming off the stream settled into me. "And the ledgers we found in the cave—"

"Are the same kind of paper that ruined me," he said. "Which means you've known since the tide went out that this was never a survey. So it's your turn. Who are you, Yara?"

I had rehearsed this confession for eleven years, in classrooms and hiring offices and rented rooms across three provinces, and every rehearsal had failed. It came out now all at once, ugly and plain, the way truths do when they're finally dragged into daylight.

"My father was Aldric Vane-Serrano. The surveyor."

Jonah looked up sharply. Everyone in the coastal ranges knew that name, or used to. Aldric Vane-Serrano, who had contested the original Vale concession before the regional board, who had produced evidence that the permit boundaries had been altered after signing — and who had died in a solo crossing of the Serrat Pass the following winter, officially, an accident.

"He didn't die in an accident," I said. "Or if he did, it wasn't an accident that he was up there. He was going to the tribunal himself. Carrying his own copies. His body was found, Jonah, but his satchel was never found. Never. And I've spent eleven years learning to draw maps well enough that someone would hire me to come back here and look for them."

"The ledgers in the cave—"

"Were his. Or copies of them. Same hand, same annotations, same ink batch — I checked, I spent half the night checking. He cached them there before his last crossing. Which means the permits Vale has been operating on for thirty years are forged, which means everything downstream of those permits — the town, the jobs, the whole economy they keep holding up like a shield — was built on paper my father tried to stop."

I heard my own voice crack and despised it. Jonah said nothing for a long moment. Above us the first stars came out, indifferent and sharp.

"So burn the ledgers," he said quietly. "Hand them over. Tell them you're just a mapmaker. They'll believe it. You could still walk away from this."

"No," I said.

It surprised me how little it cost. Eleven years of running the shape of this moment through my mind, and when it arrived, the answer was simply there, worn smooth as river stone from handling.

"Those men burned my father twice tonight. Once in the fire and once in the ledger of things worth destroying carefully. If I walk away now, they win the way they always win — slowly, politely, administratively. No. I'm going over those passes with those documents, and I'm reaching the tribunal at Meridian Falls before the snow closes the passes, and I'm putting my father's name back on the right side of the record. Whatever it costs."

Jonah studied me the way he studied terrain — looking for the weakness, the crevasse hidden under snow bridge. Then, unexpectedly, he almost smiled.

"You realize what you're describing," he said. "Meridian Falls in nine days. The high passes in November. We'd be weeks ahead of any sensible schedule, racing the first storm of the season, carrying evidence that makes us worth killing."

"Yes."

"And you want me — the guide who's famous, locally, for losing people on a glacier — to lead you there."

"I want the only guide who ever told the truth about Vale to lead me there," I said. "They can't blacklist the truth. It's the one thing they've never figured out how to forge."

He laughed — short, rusty, like a door opening in a house long shut. Then he stood, shouldered his pack, and nodded north toward the black mass of the range.

"All right. But we do this my way. Night marches, cold camps, no fires above the treeline. And Yara—" he paused, and for the first time since the tide gave us back that cave, he looked at me not as a client but as something harder to name. "Whatever's in that satchel of yours besides the ledgers, whatever else you haven't told me — tell me before the glacier. Not after. Up there, secrets weigh more than rope."

"I don't have secrets," I lied, and watched him decide to accept it, for now.

* * *

We climbed through the night. By dawn we were above the last farms, above the last fences, in country where the only law was weather. Behind us, far down the coast, I could see a smudge of smoke still rising from the beach — or maybe it was just sea fog; I chose to believe it was smoke. I wanted to remember what they'd taken. Anger keeps better than grief at altitude.

The passes opened ahead of us like a book whose ending I refused to guess. Snow already streaked the highest saddles, thin and early, weeks ahead of pattern. Jonah stopped at the top of the first col and studied the sky with an expression I was beginning to learn: the look of a man doing arithmetic with clouds.

"Weather window's closing," he said. "Three days, maybe four, before the first real system comes through. After that, the high route becomes a rumor."

"Then we move fast."

"We move *exactly* fast," he corrected. "Speed kills more parties in the shoulder season than storms do."

As we descended toward the glacier approach, I caught myself touching the waterproof sleeve inside my jacket where the most important pages rode flat against my ribs — not just the cave ledgers, but the letter I'd found folded inside them, in my father's hand, addressed to a daughter who had been seven years old when he wrote it. *If you are reading this, then the mountain kept its promise even if the men did not.*

Jonah glanced back at me. "You all right?"

"Ask me again on the other side," I said.

He grunted — approval, or the closest thing he allowed — and turned north. Somewhere behind us, faint but unmistakable now, engines. Not on the coast road. On the valley track below us, gaining.

They knew which pass we'd chosen. Of course they did. They'd burned the maps that would have told anyone else.

Permits expire at dusk. My father had written that once, bitterly, in the margin of a disputed boundary line. Ours expired somewhere back down on that burning beach, and everything ahead of us now was unpermitted ground — the mountain, the storm, the truth itself.

I tightened the straps and followed Jonah into the high country, and I did not look back again.
