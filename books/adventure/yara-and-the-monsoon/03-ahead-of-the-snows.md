## 3. Ahead of the Snows

**JONAH**

The storm came down the valley like something with a grudge.

Jonah had watched weather his whole life — read cloud lines the way other men read faces — and he'd never seen a front build this fast. One hour the sky over the Col de Verre was the pale, indifferent blue of high autumn. The next, the western ridgeline simply ceased to exist, swallowed by a wall of gray that ate the light as it came.

"That's not a storm," Yara said beside him. "That's a deadline."

They stood at the toe of the Serrac Glacier, breath steaming, packs heavy with the only copies of the ledgers left in the world. Three days since the enforcers burned their camp at the coast. Three days of climbing with the smell of ash in their clothes and the memory of Torvald's voice crackling over the radio: *Burn the maps. Burn the camp. Burn them, if it comes to that.* Three days of Yara refusing to slow down, refusing to eat more than she had to, her father's name folded flat against her chest like a letter she couldn't stop rereading.

"We cross now," Jonah said. "Or we don't cross."

The Serrac was the last obstacle between them and the ranger station at Halloway Pass — a squat stone hut with an old emergency transmitter, the kind regional tribunals still monitored out of habit. Forty years ago, before the mining concession swallowed the valley's economy, the rangers had run a proper weather watch out of it. Now it was a locked box on a map, and the only thing between them and it was four kilometers of glacier and a rope bridge some dead guide had strung across the Serrac's central crevasse a generation back.

Jonah tested the first anchor with his boot. The ice was old, blue, trustworthy. The sky behind them was not.

"Yara." He turned. She was watching the storm, and he watched her watch it, and he understood that she was doing the same math he was — that the weather didn't care about tribunals or dead fathers or forged permits. The mountain only cared about weight and angle and time.

"Say it," she said.

"If we get caught on the ice when this hits, we're not witnesses anymore. We're a news item."

"Then let's not be caught."

She stepped onto the glacier first. That was the thing about her, Jonah thought, following. She never asked for courage. She just spent it.

* * *

**YARA**

The crevasse field was worse than the map promised.

Yara had drawn half these maps herself — she'd surveyed the coastal range for three seasons, back when surveying was just a job and not a weapon — and she knew how maps lied. They made the ice look flat. They made the distances look kind. What they couldn't show was the way a glacier breathed, opening and closing its wounds with the temperature, so that a bridge anchored in September could be hanging over a gap twice as wide by October.

The bridge was there. Barely.

It sagged across the crevasse like something exhausted — three frayed ropes and a ladder of slats, half of them missing, the whole structure glazed with a skin of rime. Beyond it, the far side of the glacier rose toward Halloway Pass, and behind them the storm had begun to make its voice heard, a low grinding roar like a millstone turning somewhere out of sight.

"Spindrift's starting," Jonah said. He'd rigged their packs for a crossing — ledgers sealed in oilcloth, lashed to his own harness, because he'd said, in that flat way of his, *I'm heavier, I'll take the swing better.* "I go first. Then you. Fifteen feet apart, no closer. If I call, you freeze."

"You always say that like I'm the one who panics."

"You haven't yet. I'm keeping it that way."

He stepped onto the bridge, and Yara's stomach dropped with him. She watched him move — slow, deliberate, his weight rolled onto the ropes rather than the slats, an old guide's economy of motion — and she thought, not for the first time, that whatever had happened to break this man, it hadn't touched his hands. His hands still knew exactly what they were doing.

He was ten feet out when the wind arrived.

It came off the glacier in a single flat shove, hard enough to lean the whole bridge sideways, and Yara heard it — a sound she would hear for the rest of her life, she was sure of it — the dry, splintering *crack* of the far anchor letting go.

"Jonah!"

The bridge swung. He dropped to his knees and wrapped both fists in the rope, and the whole structure pivoted on the near anchor like a door on a hinge, swinging him out over the crevasse, out over a blue darkness that went down farther than her headlamp could reach.

The ledgers. He'd lashed the ledgers to his own harness.

She didn't decide anything. Later she would try to reconstruct the moment and find only this: she was already moving. She hit the near anchor and dropped to her belly, one arm hooked through the rope, the other reaching down the swinging lines, and the storm screamed across the ice and Jonah's weight came around on the pendulum and his hand found hers — cold through the glove, impossibly strong, a grip like he was holding the mountain instead of her.

"I've got you," she said. It came out as a roar, or a prayer. "I've got you. Climb."

He climbed. Hand over hand up her arm, up her back, while she anchored herself against the anchor post and the bridge bucked beneath them both, and when his boots finally hit solid ice he didn't say thank you. He knelt there a second, breathing hard, and looked at her with an expression she'd never seen on him — like a man reading a map he'd had upside down for years.

"You could have let go," he said.

"You could have let the ledgers go." She was shaking now, the fear arriving late, the way it always did. "We're even."

"We're not even," Jonah said. "You just paid for the next twenty years."

* * *

**JONAH**

They made Halloway Pass as the storm made landfall.

The last kilometer was white chaos — spindrift hissing across the ice, the wind stripping the heat out of them faster than they could make it. Jonah navigated by compass and count, by the memory of a route he'd guided a dozen times in another life, and when the ranger station finally materialized out of the murk — a dark stone shoulder with a door — he nearly wept at the sight of it.

Inside: a stove, half a cord of split wood left by some prudent dead ranger, and the transmitter. An old Halloway-model, brass fittings, hand-crank generator. Jonah had seen one like it in a museum. Yara had already opened the panel.

"It'll reach the regional seat," she said. "Barely. Storm interference, but the tribunal keeps a monitored band for exactly this — emergency declarations from the high country." She was pulling the ledger pages from the oilcloth, laying them flat, working with the focused calm of someone who had rehearsed this moment for years without knowing it. "We send copies. Coordinates, page numbers, the permit signatures, the comparison with my father's survey. Everything."

"And when the company monitors the same band," Jonah said, "which they will—"

"Then we'd better be fast."

He got the stove lit. She got the generator turning. Outside, the storm hit the station like a siege, wind screaming through the stones, and inside, in the small yellow light, Yara fed page after page into the transmitter's scanner while Jonah cranked the generator in long, steady strokes, and the documents of Vale Mining's great fraud went out into the blizzard one thin packet at a time — forged signatures, falsified boundaries, the whole rotten architecture of the concession, broadcast to a tribunal that would either save them or sell them.

The last packet went at dusk. Yara keyed the confirmation request, and the band crackled, and a voice came back — thin, static-shredded, but real: *Receipt confirmed. Halloway relay. Tribunal session convenes at month's end.*

Yara sat back on her heels. She didn't cheer. She just closed her eyes and pressed her hand flat against her chest, against her father's name, and Jonah, still holding the crank, understood that he was watching a woman finish a ten-year sentence.

Then the light died in the stove, and the wind changed its note, and Jonah heard the other sound underneath it — carried on the storm, faint but unmistakable.

Engines. Snow machines, coming up the pass road.

He moved to the window. Down through the whiteout, three sets of headlamps, climbing.

"Yara," he said quietly. "They knew about the transmitter. They didn't come to stop the broadcast."

He turned from the window, and the door of the station shuddered under the first blow.

"They came to make sure nobody's left to testify."
