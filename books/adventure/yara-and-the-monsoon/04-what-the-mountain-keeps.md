## 4. What the Mountain Keeps

The storm held us three days in the ranger station, and I learned that Yara hums when she sleeps — a low, tuneless sound, like wind testing a pass it has no intention of crossing.

I sat by the stove most of those nights, feeding it slats we pried from the bunk frames, and watched the snow bury the windows until the world outside was just a rumor of white. The ledgers stayed dry in their oilcloth, pressed between my chest and the wall like a second heartbeat. Twice I woke reaching for them before I remembered they were safe, and then lay there listening to Yara breathe and wondering when exactly I had stopped being a man who ran.

On the fourth morning the sky cleared so hard and blue it felt like an apology. We dug out the door, shouldered our packs, and started down.

* * *

The town of Vale looked different under snow. Smaller. Softer-edged, the way grief makes a face look younger than it is. But there were people in the streets — more people than a dying mining town had any right to muster in late autumn — and they weren't looking at us. They were looking at the notice board outside the mercantile, where someone had pinned page after page of photocopied paper, curling already at the edges.

The ledgers. Our ledgers. Copied at the ranger station's ancient fax machine and beamed to the tribunal in Greywater, and apparently also to every printer within forty miles, because somebody in this town had decided that secrets kept for sixty years were done keeping.

An old woman stood at the board with her mittened hand flat against one of the pages, not reading it so much as touching it. I recognized her — Marta Osei, whose husband had died in the Kestrel shaft collapse the same year Yara's father filed his first dispute. She'd been one of the voices calling him a troublemaker back then. A man who wanted to hurt the town that fed him.

She turned when our boots crunched behind her. Looked at Yara a long moment. Then at me, and I braced for it — the blacklisted guide, the coward of the Corren traverse, the name that got spat in guide houses from here to the coast.

"You're Iversen," she said.

"I am."

"My boy says you pulled two clients off the ice at Whitefall seven years ago. Before." Before the thing I'd actually been blamed for. Before the inquiry that found fault with everything except the truth.

"That was a long time ago."

"So was this," she said, and tapped the ledger page under her hand. "Sixty years, they told us the permits were clean. Sixty years my husband went down that hole on paper that never existed." Her voice didn't break. It just went very quiet, which was worse. "You brought this down the mountain."

"Yara carried it," I said. "I mostly fell."

That got something out of her — not quite a laugh, but the shape of one. She looked at Yara again, longer this time. "Your father's girl."

"Yes," Yara said. Just that. And Marta Osei took her hand off the notice board and put it on Yara's shoulder instead, and around us the street had gone still, thirty or forty people pretending not to watch while something sixty years old began, very slowly, to thaw.

* * *

The tribunal came up from Greywater nine days later, three officials and a stenographer and an escort of rangers, because the company's enforcers had not simply given up — they'd made threats, formal ones, the kind that get written down. The hearings were held in the schoolhouse because it was the only building with enough chairs.

I testified on the second day. I stood in front of a room full of people I'd spent a decade avoiding and told the truth in order: what we found in the cave, what we found in the ledgers, what the men who burned our camp looked like and how they moved and who paid their wages. My hands shook through the first ten minutes. After that they didn't, and I understood it wasn't courage. It was that for the first time in years, standing still was the harder thing, and I have always been better at hard things than easy ones.

Yara testified after me. She laid her father's original dispute filings beside the forged permits and let the dates speak — the "approved" concession dated four days *before* the application it supposedly answered. A child could see it. A tribunal, it turned out, could too.

The suspension came down on the eleventh day. All Vale Mining operations halted pending full review; all permits referred to the regional registrar as suspected forgeries; the Kestrel collapse reopened as a matter of corporate negligence. And then the chairman read the last item in a voice that made the whole schoolhouse lean forward:

"The tribunal further finds that Tomas Reyes, cartographer, acted throughout in good faith, and posthumously clears his name of all charges of falsification and sedition."

Yara didn't cry. She did the thing she does — went very still, the way she goes still on a ridge before committing to a crossing. But her hand found mine on the bench between us, and hers was shaking, and I held on.

Afterward, outside, the snow was starting again, fat slow flakes that meant the season turning honest at last. The enforcers had left town the night before, quiet as smoke. Nobody would say where they'd gone. Nobody needed to. Men like that only matter as long as the paper holds, and the paper was ash now.

* * *

They offered me the vanishing again. That's how I thought of it, anyway. A ranger named Okonkwo, who'd read my file and somehow not hated me, caught me stacking chairs after the last session and said, casual as weather: "Greywater will want you deposed formally, weeks of it, your name in every record. Or—" he shrugged "—there's a southbound freight Thursday. Some men do better gone."

I thought about it honestly. That's the part that surprised me — that I gave it a real hearing, the old dream of the clean slate, the new valley, the name nobody knew. I'd rehearsed that exit so many times it fit me like a boot.

But I looked across the schoolroom and Yara was at the big table with the town surveyor, unrolling her maps, already redrawing the range with the cave marked and the old smugglers' trail restored, talking fast with her hands, and half a dozen Vale kids were crowded around pretending to help while they memorized contour lines like treasure maps. And Marta Osei was telling anyone who'd listen about the day the tide went out farther than anyone living had seen, like the coast itself had decided to give back what was taken.

Nobody in that room needed me. That was the strange, unbearable gift of it. For ten years I'd only ever been useful running toward or running away, rope and ice axe and panic. These people had stood up on their own. The evidence had done its work because Yara had carried it, and because a town had chosen, finally, to believe.

And still — when Okonkwo cleared his throat, I heard myself say, "When does the deposition start?"

He smiled like he'd bet on that answer. "Monday. Nine sharp. Wear something warm; the courthouse heat's on the fritz."

* * *

We walked up to the headland the evening before I was due to testify again — properly, officially, the last of it. The cave was sealed now, the tide risen and the winter claiming it, but you could see where it had been, a dark seam in the cliff face below the snow line. The mountain keeps what it keeps. It had kept the ledgers sixty years, waiting for a low tide and two people stubborn enough to walk out onto the exposed seabed.

"Okonkwo offered me a freight south," I said.

Yara didn't look surprised. "Thursday?"

"Thursday."

"And?"

"I'm testifying Monday."

She nodded, watching the water. Then: "There's work, come spring. Surveying the upper passes for the new cooperative — the town voted it through yesterday. Real maps, honest ones. It doesn't pay much."

"It pays more than running."

That earned me the look — the one she gives the mountain when it's finally told her the truth. "Jonah Iversen," she said, "standing still. Write the date down. Someone should map it."

I laughed, and the sound went out over the water and didn't echo, because some things don't need to come back to know they landed.

Home, I'd always believed, was a place you found or failed to find, a stroke of luck like weather. Standing on that headland with the snow coming down soft and the whole valley lit gold below us, I finally understood it was nothing of the kind. Home was a choice, made daily, made in front of witnesses. I had just made mine.

Below us, at the edge of town, a light came on in the schoolhouse — early, someone preparing, the way towns do when they've decided tomorrow matters. Yara saw it too.

"Come on," she said, turning back down the trail. "If we're staying, we should probably learn everyone's names."

I followed her down into the falling snow, and for the first time in ten years, I wasn't going anywhere.
