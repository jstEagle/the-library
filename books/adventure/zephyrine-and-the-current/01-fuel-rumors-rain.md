## 1. Fuel, Rumors, Rain

The rain started an hour before dawn and Paloma Montrose was already awake to hear it arrive, because Paloma Montrose was always awake an hour before dawn, seven years running, as if sleep were a debt that could be called in without warning.

They lay in the cot above the hangar office and listened to the drum of it on the corrugated roof and did the arithmetic they did every morning. Fuel in the drums behind the fence: enough for four runs, maybe five if the headwind stayed honest. Money in the coffee tin under the floorboard: enough for the engine overhaul if nothing else broke, which something always did. Days until the permit expired: twenty-three. The permit was the whole architecture of the life—without it, the blue-and-white de Havilland on the apron outside was just a target, a thing men in pressed shirts would come to look at with clipboards.

Paloma got up, put water on the ring burner, and stood at the window watching the rain walk across the field in gray curtains. Somewhere downriver, thunder cleared its throat.

The medicine crates had come in on Tuesday's barge, stenciled with a clinic's name that wasn't really the clinic's name, bound for a settlement up the Cobre branch that didn't appear on any map worth owning. That was the trade. Not smuggling, exactly—the boxes held penicillin and antimalarials and rehydration salts, all of it legal, all of it paid for by mission money and mining wages pooled in coffee tins not unlike Paloma's own. But it moved through channels that officialdom preferred not to see, and so it needed a pilot who preferred not to be seen either.

Seven years of that. Seven years of being excellent at a job whose first requirement was that nobody remember your face too well.

* * *

By six the rain had thinned to a steady hiss and Paloma was walking the plane, doing the preflight with hands that knew every rivet the way other people knew the lines of their own palms. Left wing: clean. Right wing: a new dent near the strut, acquired at some unnamed strip from some unnamed event, because asking about dents violated Rule Three. Tires: acceptable. The fabric patch over the third window: holding, barely, the stitching gone gray from sun.

The engine was the problem. It always was, lately. She'd been burning oil like a drunk burns money, and the cylinder pressures were down two numbers on the gauge, and every flight was becoming a negotiation between Paloma and the machine, conducted in temperature and vibration, in the language of small warnings. The overhaul quote sat in the office drawer under a rubber band. Four hundred more than the tin contained.

That was why the rules existed, Paloma reminded themself, pulling the prop through and listening to her complain. The rules kept the overheads low and the profile flat and the name unspoken. Rule One: no names given, none asked. People could call the pilot whatever they liked—Montrose worked fine, it was even on the license, though the license belonged to a man who'd died of fever in Port Anselm years ago and looked nothing like the person wearing his paperwork now. Rule Two: no stop longer than an hour. An hour was long enough for fuel or cargo but not for friendship, and friendship was where questions came from. Rule Three: no questions answered. Not evasively—that invited curiosity—but smoothly, completely, the way a wall answers a thrown ball.

Rule Four, unwritten: never fly anything you can't drop out of the sky if you have to. So far, in seven years, Paloma hadn't had to. The rule was there anyway, the way a railing is there on a high balcony.

The engine caught on the fourth blade swing, coughed twice, and settled into its rough idle. Paloma stood in the prop wash and let it blow their hair back and thought, as they thought every morning, *one more season*. One more season and the overhaul gets done and the tin fills and maybe—

Maybe what? The thought never finished. It never finished on purpose. Finishing it meant deciding what the life was for, and that was a door Paloma kept painted shut.

* * *

The Cobre branch took ninety minutes in good weather and the weather today was merely adequate, low cloud sitting on the ridgelines like a lid, visibility down to a mile in the wet places. Below, the river ran wide and brown and swollen, weeks past flood crest but still fat with everything the hills had given up. Whole islands had drowned. Paloma flew the channel from memory and from the color of the water—brown over brown meant depth, brown over green meant sandbar, brown boiling white meant something was under the surface that used to be a tree.

This was the part of the work that was still pure, if anything in the life was pure anymore. The flying itself. Two hands and a machine and three hundred miles of country where no one could reach you. Up here at eight hundred feet, with the rain beading on the windshield and the wiper—hand-cranked, secondhand, squeaking on every pass—clearing a fan of droplets, Paloma Montrose was exactly competent and exactly alone, and those two facts were welded together so thoroughly now that it was hard to say which one made the other possible.

A settlement knows when the plane is coming. Word moves faster than aircraft in the interior; it moves by drum and by radio and by the simple fact that everyone watches the sky because the sky is where help comes from. By the time Paloma swung wide over the clearing and lined up on the grass strip, there were thirty people along the treeline, and the clinic orderly—a heavyset woman named Berta who had decided years ago that "no names" applied to everyone except herself—was waving both arms, which meant the icebox shipment, which meant someone was sick enough that the vaccine mattered today.

Paloma landed long to use the slope, taxied to the usual spot under the big kapok, and shut down. Rule Two clock started now.

Berta met them at the door of the shed with a manifest and no small talk, which was one reason Berta remained tolerable after six years of these runs. She counted boxes aloud, initialing each line while Paloma unloaded, and only at the end, with the last crate of saline stacked, did she pause.

"You heard about the river?"

"The river does many things."

"It dropped." Berta set down the clipboard. "Two meters. Overnight. After all that flood, holding high for a month—then Sunday night it falls off like somebody pulled a plug. Father Anselmo says he's seen it once before, forty years back, and that time—" she stopped herself, which was unusual for Berta, and glanced toward the trees as though the conversation might have listeners. "That time things came up with it. Out of the ground. The old people talked about it for years after. Anyway. Watch your bars. Sandbars are moving like cattle."

"Thank you," Paloma said, and meant it, and gave her nothing else, because Rule Three was Rule Three even for friends. Especially for friends. Friends were how names happened, and names were how the man from Port Anselm had found Paloma's face attached to a seven-year-old newspaper clipping about a charter that went into the water short of the runway with a dead passenger aboard.

Forty years back. Things came up out of the ground. Paloma filed it the way they filed everything in this country—under *not my business*, in a drawer that was getting crowded.

Fifty-one minutes after touchdown, the wheels left the grass.

* * *

The return leg put them back over the main stem of the river by mid-afternoon, and that was when Paloma saw what Berta had meant.

The floodline was written on the banks in mud—a bright band of fresh silt two meters above the current surface, a bathtub ring miles long. Trees stood in it, their lower trunks scoured pale. And the sandbars, yes: bars that had been charted in Paloma's memory as safe emergency ground were underwater, while others had surfaced where no bar should be, raw and new, steaming faintly in the breaks of sun like the backs of animals.

The river was giving things back. You could see it happening. A canoe hull, split and ancient. Half a fence. A row of posts that might once have been a dock, standing in water that hadn't touched them since before Paloma was born.

Paloma's hands stayed easy on the yoke. Paloma's eyes did not stay easy anywhere—they swept, checked, swept again, the habit of a person who had learned that the world occasionally surfaces things you'd rather it kept. Seven years of practice at not looking directly at the past, and here was the whole landscape doing it instead, hauling its drowned history up into daylight and laying it out in the mud for anyone flying over.

*Don't look*, Paloma told themself, and looked, because that was the other habit, the older one, the one the running had never managed to kill.

At the mouth of the Cobre, where it rejoined the main river, there was a long collapsed bank—a scar of raw earth where the flood had eaten fifty meters of shoreline in a single night—and as Paloma banked around a rain cell and passed over it at six hundred feet, something down in that scar caught the light.

Not water-glare. Not a fish jumping. Metal. A flat, deliberate glint, the kind of shine that manufactured things give off and rivers don't.

Paloma held the turn two seconds longer than necessary. Long enough to see the shape of it: rectangular, man-made, half-buried in the slumped earth like a coffin the bank had swallowed decades ago and was now, politely, returning.

Then the cloud closed and it was gone, and Paloma leveled the wings and pointed the nose home and told themself, firmly, in the voice of Rule Three applied inward: *No questions answered.*

The voice did not sound convinced.

* * *

The field at dusk was wet and empty, and Paloma tied the plane down in the last light, checking the tie-downs twice because the forecast said squalls. The hangar smelled of oil and canvas and the particular damp of a building that has stood beside a river for sixty years and lost every argument with the humidity.

Paloma was heating beans over the ring burner when the truck came up the track.

Headlights first, bouncing through the potholes, then the grind of bad gears. Paloma turned the burner down—not off, never off, the flame was a habit of readiness—and stood in the doorway with the light behind them, watching the truck stop at the edge of the apron.

Two figures. A driver who stayed in the cab. And a passenger, a woman by the build, who stepped down into the mud with a suitcase in each hand—one hard-sided, one soft—and stood looking at the blue-and-white plane the way people look at a door they've traveled a long way to knock on.

She walked to within ten feet of the hangar and stopped, which showed sense. Rain dripped off the hangar roof between them.

"I need a charter," she said. Her accent was capital-city, educated, careful. "Upriver. Survey work."

"No surveys," Paloma said. "Cargo, mail, medicine. Passengers to places that exist."

The woman smiled slightly, as if Paloma had said something cleverer than intended. "Places that exist," she repeated. "Then I suppose we'll get along."

She set the suitcases down, deliberately, in plain view, and Paloma's eyes went to them the way eyes go to a wound—one hard case, latched, the kind that holds instruments or documents; one soft bag, bulging, the kind that holds everything a person couldn't bear to leave. Too much luggage for a survey. Too little for anything else.

"How much?" Paloma asked, because the question underneath—*who are you*—was against the rules, and the price was the only legal question left.

"Double your rate. Cash. Plus fuel." The woman reached into her coat and produced an envelope, thick enough that Paloma's stomach performed a small, shameful calculation involving engine cylinders and rubber bands.

Rain ticked on the tin roof. Downriver, thunder muttered, patient as debt.

Paloma looked at the envelope, and at the suitcases, and at the woman's face—which was tired in the specific way of a person carrying something heavier than luggage—and heard themself say, against every rule that had kept them alive for seven years:

"When do you want to leave?"

"In the morning," she said. "Before the river drops any further. It's giving things back, you know. Someone should be there when it does."

The beans burned, quietly, behind them.
