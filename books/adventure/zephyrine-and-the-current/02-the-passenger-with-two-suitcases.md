## 2. The Passenger with Two Suitcases

The woman arrived at the airstrip on the morning mail truck, which was itself a small miracle, because the mail truck came twice a month and never on time. Paloma watched her climb down from the cab with two suitcases—one hard-sided, gray, scuffed at the corners like it had crossed more borders than its owner; one soft leather bag that she carried by the handle with a care that suggested the contents mattered more than anything else in her possession.

She stood at the edge of the strip in a linen jacket too clean for this latitude and looked around as if checking whether the place matched its description. It didn't. It never did. The airstrip was a scar of crushed coral and red clay cut into the treeline, the grass at its edges grown tall enough to hide a fence post, a fuel drum rusting into the shape of modern art beside the windsock. The windsock itself had faded from orange to the color of weak tea and hung limp in the still air, which meant no flying weather worth trusting, which meant nothing to Paloma, who trusted no weather anyway.

Paloma wiped their hands on a rag and walked out to meet her. Rules first. Always rules first.

"No names," Paloma said, before she could speak. "You can call me Pilot. I don't need yours unless we file a flight plan, and most of my flights don't get filed."

The woman blinked once. She had dark hair going gray at the temples, cut short and practical, and eyes that did the thing Paloma distrusted most: they looked at you like they were reading a document and noting discrepancies.

"That's fine," she said. "I'd rather not use mine anyway."

* * *

Ines—she would not give the name aloud, but she gave it to herself, silently, the way you touch a bruise to confirm it's still there—watched the pilot's hands.

That was how her grandmother had taught her to judge people, back when there had been a country to teach such things in. *Hands tell you what faces are paid to hide.* The pilot's hands were brown and nicked, a crescent of old scar across the left thumb, knuckles swollen in the way of hands that had been broken at least once and set without much ceremony. They moved over the engine cowling with an economy Ines recognized from archives: the economy of someone who has handled the same object ten thousand times and no longer needs to look at it.

The pilot opened the cowling and stood looking into the engine's throat for a long moment, and Ines saw the shoulders drop half a centimeter—a small surrender, quickly corrected.

"Cylinders three and four are eating themselves," the pilot said, not turning around. "I've known for two months. I've been pretending otherwise because an overhaul costs more than I've made since the wet season started." Now the pilot turned, and the face above those competent hands was watchful, neutral, younger than the hands but older than the eyes. "So. You want a survey flight. What are you surveying?"

"Riverbanks," Ines said. "Erosion patterns after the flood. I'm doing archival work for—" She stopped herself, which was a mistake, because stopping yourself mid-lie is more visible than telling it smoothly.

"For?" the pilot said.

"A historical society," Ines finished. "Private. Small. Poorly funded, obviously, or I'd have hired a proper charter firm."

The pilot looked at her for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then down at the suitcases. Then back up.

"Show me the money."

Ines had prepared for haggling. She had prepared a figure with room to be talked down, a story about institutional budgets, a modest demeanor. What she had not prepared for was the pilot simply naming a number that was fair—fair to the point of being cheap—and then standing there waiting while Ines, off-script, blurted out nearly double.

The pilot's eyebrows rose. Not greedily. Suspiciously.

"That's too much," the pilot said.

"It's what the budget allows."

"The budget allows double the going rate for a survey flight with no filed plan, no names, and a passenger who lies badly about who sent them." The pilot wiped their hands slowly on the rag, buying time, thinking. Ines watched the thought move through the face like weather. "What aren't you telling me?"

"That I need to leave tomorrow," Ines said, "and that where we're going, I'd prefer not to be asked about again."

A long silence. Somewhere past the treeline, a boat engine worked against the current and faded. The windsock stirred, considered it, and died again.

"I take the money," the pilot said finally, "because I need it. That's the whole transaction. You're cargo that talks. We fly tomorrow at first light, before the thermals build. One stop maximum, under an hour. You don't ask me questions, I don't ask you any more than I already regret asking. And if anyone official asks what we're doing, I'm a medicine run and you're a nurse."

"I'm not a nurse."

"You are today. Nurses don't get shot at. Much." The pilot held out the scarred hand. "Half now."

Ines shook it. The grip was careful, controlled—not testing her strength, just confirming she existed, that the money existed, that the deal had weight. Her grandmother would have said the hands were honest even if the mouth wasn't. Ines wasn't sure yet which was which.

* * *

Paloma spent the evening doing what they always did the night before carrying a stranger: inventorying the stranger.

The gray suitcase went into the hold without complaint—it was heavy, denser than clothes had any right to be, and when Paloma tilted it, something inside shifted with the flat slide of paper, a lot of paper, bound and stacked. Notebooks? Files? Nobody did erosion surveys with forty kilos of paper.

The leather bag the woman kept with her. Of course. Everyone kept one thing close—the thing they couldn't afford to lose or couldn't afford to be found. Paloma knew the taxonomy well, having lived out of a single go-bag for seven years: documents, cash, weapon, or photographs. Sometimes all four. The bag never left the woman's shoulder, not at dinner, not when she slept on the bench in the strip office. She slept sitting up, actually, jacket folded under her head, waking instantly when Paloma came in at midnight to check the tie-downs. Neither of them pretended to have been asleep.

"You always sleep here?" she asked.

"I always sleep light," Paloma said.

They sat in the strip office with its single bulb and its map of the river system pinned to the wall, annotated in three different hands across fifteen years, some of the ink gone brown. The woman studied the map the way she seemed to study everything—thoroughly, hungrily, like a person rehydrating dried meat.

"You know the upper reaches," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I know where the water is deep enough to land and shallow enough to kill us. That's the extent of my scholarship."

"The flood last month. They say the river dropped two meters overnight in places. All that water pulling back—" She traced a stretch of bank north of the old mission station, a bend where the map showed nothing at all, just white space and a dotted line that might have been a channel or might have been a guess. "Rivers give things back. When they take a bank, they trade."

Paloma looked at her. "Trade what?"

"Whatever they took before." She said it lightly, but her finger stayed on the white space a beat too long, and Paloma—who had survived seven years by noticing exactly these beats too long—filed it away.

"Surveying erosion," Paloma said. "Right."

"Is that so strange?"

"It's strange that you know the exact bend you want before you've seen it. It's strange that your 'historical society' pays double and travels with forty kilos of paper. And it's strange that you flinched—just now, small, but you flinched—when I said *trade*, like the word meant something specific to you."

The woman withdrew her hand from the map. For a moment the office was very quiet, and the two of them regarded each other across the desk like card players who had both just realized the other was counting.

"Tomorrow," she said at last, "you'll see why the permits matter and the questions don't. Until then, Pilot, I'll make you a professional promise: everything I'm hiding is legal. Can you say the same?"

It was a good line. It was a very good line, delivered with just enough tiredness to sound true, and Paloma felt it land exactly where it was designed to land—in the part of a person that wants to believe they're not the only one carrying something.

"First light," Paloma said, and turned off the bulb.

* * *

Ines lay awake on the bench listening to the night sounds of the strip—frogs, the tick of cooling metal, the pilot's footsteps making slow rounds of the plane like a shepherd counting sheep. She had made mistakes today. She knew them all by name. She had paid too much, lied too thinly, shown too much interest in the bend of the river that mattered. A better operative would have spread the money across three smaller payments and let curiosity do the work.

But there was no time for better operatives. Nineteen days—no. She wouldn't count tonight. Counting made the number smaller.

Her grandmother had crossed this same river system in 1951, in the last year the maps still said San Marisol, carrying a leather bag much like this one. Ines had grown up on the story of what was in it: deeds, censuses, photographs, the paper skeleton of a nation that four governments had agreed to forget because forgetting was cheaper than honoring. Her grandmother had spent fifty years waiting for the world to look again. She had died waiting. The bag was on Ines's shoulder now, and the white space on the pilot's map was where her grandmother's village used to be, and tomorrow she would ask a stranger with honest hands and hidden crimes to fly her into the middle of the largest act of erasure on the continent.

She listened to the footsteps outside. Steady. Careful. The walk of a person who checked tie-downs at midnight because sleeping soundly was a luxury they'd traded away somewhere, for reasons Ines very much wanted to know.

*Everyone hiding something,* her grandmother used to say, *is just someone whose papers haven't been demanded yet.*

Outside, the frogs went quiet all at once, the way they do when something moves through the grass. Ines heard the pilot stop walking. Heard a long stillness. Then, softly, the scrape of a case being unlatched—the pilot's own go-bag, the one that lived behind the seat of the plane and never got explained—and the whisper of paper being counted, or read, or simply touched.

Then the latch closed, and the footsteps resumed, and the frogs decided it was safe.

Ines stared at the ceiling and smiled without warmth. Tomorrow, then. Two liars in a tin box over a river full of secrets.

At least neither of them would be bored.
