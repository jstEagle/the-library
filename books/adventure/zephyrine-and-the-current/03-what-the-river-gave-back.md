## 3. What the River Gave Back

The river had been misbehaving all morning, and Paloma trusted it the way you trust a dog that's bitten someone—carefully, and with an eye on its teeth.

They were forty minutes into the survey run, following the water south from the medicine strip, when Ines Vidal leaned forward between the seats and said, "Lower."

"Lower costs fuel."

"I'll pay for the fuel. Lower."

Paloma glanced at her. She had both suitcases wedged at her feet like they might walk off without her, and she was pressed to the window with an expression Paloma hadn't seen on a passenger before—not fear of flying, but hunger for what was underneath it. Most people looked at the canopy and saw green. Ines Vidal was reading it.

"Bank's collapsed up ahead," Paloma said, because saying it out loud made it a decision instead of a drift. "Flood cut the east bank around the bend. I'm not putting wheels in mud that was riverbed yesterday."

"A low pass, then. Just a look."

"Just a look," Paloma agreed, and didn't believe either of them for a second.

The settler at the fuel stop had said the river dropped two meters overnight after the flood, the way it sometimes did—gorged itself, then vomited everything back. Two meters of new country exposed along every bank: trees on their sides with roots like torn hands, mud the color of old bruises, fish dying in pools that used to be deep water. The river giving back what it had taken, plus interest.

Paloma brought the plane down along the bend at two hundred feet, throttle eased, watching the collapsed bank slide beneath them. It was ugly work, fresh scar tissue half a kilometer long, and then—

Metal.

Not the dull orange of rusted roofing or the white flash of a dead fish's belly. This was a clean glint, deliberate, geometric. A corner of something square catching the sun through a smear of mud, winking once as the shadow of the wing crossed it.

Paloma's hands did the math before their brain finished the sentence. Wind down the valley, light on the water, the sandbar two hundred meters past the collapse firm enough to hold weight if the flood hadn't undercut it. They'd landed on worse. They'd landed on better and died anyway, some of them.

"No," Paloma said, to themselves, and began the turn.

"What are you doing?"

"Landing."

"You said—"

"I know what I said." The words came out flat, already regretted. Seven years of rules, and here was rule number one going over the side for a glint of metal in the mud, because the alternative was flying home tonight and lying awake hearing that wink of light behind their eyes until it drove them back tomorrow anyway. Better to spend the daylight now. "Stay buckled."

The sandbar took the wheels with a soft crunch and held. Paloma taxied to the upstream end where the ground rose toward the bank, shut down, and sat for a moment listening to the engine tick and the river move against the far channel. Somewhere in the green, a bird screamed twice and quit.

Ines had her door open before the prop stopped.

"Wait." Paloma caught her sleeve. "Ground rules. We stay together. We don't touch anything we don't have to. And if anyone comes—anyone—we're a survey crew that got curious about erosion. Nothing else."

"Understood." She wasn't really listening. Her eyes were on the bank.

They crossed the two hundred meters of sandbar on foot, boots punching crusty mud, and climbed the broken lip of the collapse. Up close the damage was worse—a whole slice of forest floor sheared away and folded down into the muck, saplings buried to their crowns, the smell of drowned earth thick enough to chew.

The metal was fifteen meters down the slope, half-buried where the flood had peeled the bank open and then abandoned it.

It was a chest.

Not a crate, not a drum. A chest—iron-strapped, wood gone black with decades of wet, but the straps still bright where the mud had scoured them. It lay tilted on its corner like something dropped mid-step, and across its lid, under a skin of dried silt, there was a stamped seal the size of a dinner plate.

Paloma slid down the last meter, worked fingers under the lid edge, and scraped. Mud came away in curls. Underneath, the seal resolved: a circle, a rising sun split by three lines of water, letters worn but legible around the rim.

*San Marisol.*

Paloma knew the name the way everyone knew it—from the old men in river towns who mentioned it only when drunk enough to forget they weren't supposed to, from the blank space on the schoolroom maps where a country should have been, from the word *concession* that filled that space now. San Marisol had been erased forty years ago. Not conquered, not renamed—erased. Redrawn off the map so thoroughly that asking about it in certain offices got you a visit from certain men.

Nobody's property sat in a sealed chest with that seal on it by accident.

"Ines." Their voice came out lower than intended. "You should see this."

She was already coming down the slope, and when she reached the chest and read the seal, Paloma watched something happen to her face that they had no name for. Color left it in stages, like a tide going out. Her hand went to the lid and stopped just above it, not touching, trembling slightly, the way you hover over a sleeping animal you're afraid to wake.

"That's—" She swallowed. Started again. "That's not possible."

"It's possible. It's right here."

"You don't understand." Her voice had gone thin and fast, the composure from the airstrip cracking straight down the middle. "That seal was destroyed. Everything was destroyed—the records, the registers, the archives. When the concessions were signed, there was a decree. Officially San Marisol never existed. There is no archive anywhere with that seal because every single one was burned. My grandmother used to say—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"Used to say what?"

"Nothing." She crouched, wiped more mud from the lid with the heel of her hand, methodical now, almost violent. "Help me lift it."

"It's locked."

"It won't be. Look at the hasp—the strap's corroded through on this side." She was right; forty years of river water had done their work. "Please. Help me."

Every instinct Paloma owned was standing in a row telling them to walk away. Leave the chest. Fly home. Forget the glint. Rules existed for exactly this moment, and every one of them said: whatever a vanished nation buried in a riverbank is none of your business, and the people who erased it are still in business, and business like theirs does not forgive witnesses.

But Ines Vidal was on her knees in the mud with her grandmother's country under her hands, and she was looking up at Paloma with an expression that made refusal feel like a small, mean kind of murder.

"We don't open it here," Paloma said. "Open ground, midday, visible from the water. If it's what you think it is, we don't want an audience." They tested the chest's weight—one end, grunting. Heavy. Paper-heavy, or metal-heavy, and either way too much for two people to carry far. "We take it to the plane, we fly it somewhere with walls, and then we decide whether we ever opened anything at all. Understood?"

"Yes." Too quick. Too eager.

They wrestled it up the slope together, sliding twice, Paloma's shoulder screaming by the top. On the sandbar they rested, both breathing hard, the chest sitting between them like a third passenger.

"Do you know what's in it?" Paloma asked.

"No." Honest, at least. Then, quieter: "I hope I do."

They loaded it into the cargo bay, strapped it down, and Paloma stood a moment with their hand on the fuselage, looking back at the collapsed bank. From here the scar in the earth looked small, almost innocent. The river had kept its secret for forty years and then, in one night of flood, given it up to the first pair of eyes that happened past.

Which meant the river could give it up to any other eyes too. Patrol boats ran this stretch. Meridian Concessions ran this stretch.

"Ines. Before we take off—who else knows you hired me? Who else knows this route?"

She hesitated one beat too long.

"Ines."

"My brother knows I was coming north. Not why. Not with whom." She met Paloma's eyes, and there was something in hers that wasn't quite a lie and wasn't quite the truth—something being carried and set down and picked up again. "He works in an office. He doesn't fly."

Everybody's brother worked in an office. Everybody's brother didn't fly. Paloma filed it away with the too-high payment and the too-few questions, in the drawer where they kept things they'd deal with later.

Later was getting crowded.

They lifted off the sandbar at noon, the plane heavier than it had come, and turned south toward the derelict mission station Paloma knew of—an hour's flight, walls, a roof, no reason for anyone to go there. Below them the river unspooled, brown and patient, and Paloma found themselves checking the banks the whole way, scanning every stretch of exposed mud for other glints, other chests, other secrets the flood had coughed up.

Because if the river gave this one back, it might have given others back too. And whoever had buried it would be wondering, sooner or later, what else the water remembered.

Behind them, in the cargo bay, the chest rode in silence, carrying a country that had never officially existed—and, Paloma suspected, carrying the end of the life they'd built to stay invisible.

They felt it the way you feel weather changing: not yet rain, but the pressure dropping, the birds going quiet, the whole sky holding its breath.

Three weeks. That was how long the permits had left. Paloma didn't know yet why that number mattered, or how soon it would matter—but the current had already turned, and they were in it, and the river does not ask permission before it takes you downstream.
