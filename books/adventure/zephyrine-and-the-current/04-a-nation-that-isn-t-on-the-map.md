## 4. A Nation That Isn't on the Map

The chest sat between them in the grass like a thing that had been waiting.

Paloma crouched beside it, not touching it, the way you don't touch a snake until you've decided whether it's the kind that strikes. The mud around it was already drying in the sun, cracking into plates, and the seal on the lid caught the light—wax gone amber with age, pressed with a crest: a bird over water, wings spread wide, and beneath it a ribbon of lettering worn almost smooth.

Ines Vidal knelt on the other side. She hadn't spoken since they'd landed. Her hands hovered over the seal without landing, the way people hover over things in museums.

"You know what this is," Paloma said.

"I know what it looks like." Ines's voice came out thin. She cleared her throat and tried again. "The bird is a frigatebird. Over a river bend. And the words—" She leaned close, squinting at the worn letters. "It's old San Marisolan script. Pre-standardization. They changed the orthography in the last decade before—" She stopped herself.

"Before what?"

"Before there was no more San Marisol."

Paloma sat back on their heels. "There's no such country."

"There's no such country *anymore*." Ines finally touched the lid, one fingertip, as if testing an iron for heat. "San Marisol. Republic of. Recognized 1911, dissolved—absorbed, partitioned, whatever word the maps use—forty years ago. It was upriver from here. Where the concession land is now."

"The Meridian concessions."

"All of it. Every hectare Meridian holds north of the falls sits on what used to be San Marisolan territory." Ines looked up, and her eyes were doing something complicated—bright and frightened at once, a woman standing at the edge of a hole she'd been digging toward her whole life. "My grandmother was born there. In a village called Puerto Cimarrón. It doesn't appear on any map printed after 1985. I've spent eleven years looking for documents from that country, and every archive I've ever accessed has a gap where it should be. A clean, deliberate gap. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Countries don't get erased by accident. Somebody erased this one, and then somebody paid to keep it erased."

Paloma said nothing. The river moved past the collapsed bank, brown and patient, carrying its usual cargo of branches and secrets.

"And now," Ines went on, quieter, "the river gives me a chest with the national seal on it, sitting in mud two hundred kilometers from any archive, any consulate, any record of any kind." She laughed—a short, unsteady sound with no humor in it. "Do you believe in coincidence, Montrose?"

"I believe in weather. Weather does this." Paloma gestured at the raw wound of the bank, the exposed roots, the slumped earth. "Floods bury things and floods find them. That's not destiny, that's hydrology."

"Then open it and let's see what the hydrology brought us."

"No."

The word came out flat and final, and Ines stared at them.

"It's not ours," Paloma said. "Whatever it is. Salvage law on state waters says recovered property gets reported to the provincial registrar within seventy-two hours. We log it, we file the report in Puerto Andes, and it becomes somebody else's problem."

"Somebody else's—" Ines stood up so fast she staggered in the soft ground. "You want to hand it to the *registrar*? The registrar's office is funded by a Meridian development grant. I read the annual report. Their salvage division operates out of a building Meridian built for them."

"Then it goes to someone who isn't."

"To whom? Name one person in this province whose hands you'd trust it to."

Paloma could have named three, actually, but all three were the kind of people who charged for trust, and saying their names out loud would only invite questions. Instead they stood too, brushing mud from their knees, and looked downriver.

That was when they saw it again—the thing they'd both been pretending not to have seen.

The patrol boat was still there.

It had come around the far bend twenty minutes ago, white hull, company colors, the Meridian lightning-bolt insignia visible even at this distance. It wasn't approaching. It wasn't leaving. It was holding position mid-channel, the way boats do when the men aboard are looking at something through binoculars.

"They've been watching us since we landed," Paloma said.

"I know."

"You knew and you didn't say anything?"

"I was hoping I was wrong." Ines's jaw tightened. "I've been wrong about boats before. Once, off the coast, I flagged down what I thought was a fishing trawler and it turned out to be—"

"That's not a fishing boat." Paloma's voice dropped into the register they used for bad news delivered calmly. "That's a Meridian patrol vessel, class forty-footer, twin screws, probably a crew of four and at least two of them armed. They run these reaches twice a week checking permits and 'salvage activity.' Which means they saw a blue-and-white bush plane make an unscheduled landing on a collapsed bank next to a chest that just surfaced out of forty years of river."

"So we go talk to them. We explain. We're surveyors, we're—"

"We're nothing. We're nobody. That's the whole point of how I fly." Paloma was already moving toward the plane, talking fast, mind running the arithmetic it always ran. "If we walk away from that chest right now, get airborne, file nothing, tell no one, then in six months it's a story we half-remember. But if we stand here another ten minutes, if those men come ashore, if they see our faces next to that seal—" They stopped. Turned. "Here's what happens. They take the chest. They take photographs of us. Our names go into a Meridian field report, and field reports don't die, they circulate. And somewhere down the line, when it matters, someone pulls the file and finds out exactly who was standing next to the last proof that San Marisol existed."

Ines had gone very still. "You think it's proof of something."

"I think a sealed national archive chest doesn't wash out of a riverbank by accident either." Paloma hated themselves for saying it. Hated the part of their brain that had already catalogued the chest's weight, the lock's age, the way the wax seal had never been broken. "I think countries that get erased leave enemies behind, and enemies hide things, and rivers find them. But knowing that and wanting it are different things. You should want to be wrong about this, Vidal. Being right about this is dangerous in ways you clearly haven't thought through."

"I've thought about nothing else for eleven years."

"Eleven years of reading about it in archives. Not eleven years of it being real." Paloma pointed at the boat. "*That* makes it real. So here's my professional advice, free of charge: we leave it. We lift off, we fly south, we pretend today was a fuel stop and a look at a riverbank. The chest stays in the mud. The mud keeps its secrets. Everybody lives."

"And if they dig it up tomorrow?"

"Then it's their problem."

"And if they burn it?"

Paloma didn't answer.

"Because that's what happens to things like this," Ines said. Her voice had changed—lower now, steadier, the tremor burned off by something harder underneath. "Things like this don't get filed. They get destroyed. My grandmother's whole country got destroyed, Montrose. Every document, every record, every photograph—there is *nothing*, do you understand? Eleven years, and I have found parish ledgers with pages torn out, census books with lines struck through, a single photograph of a street in Puerto Cimarrón that some family hid inside a Bible for forty years. That is all that exists. And you're telling me the correct move is to walk away from the first intact artifact of my grandmother's nation because it might be inconvenient?"

"Yes," Paloma said. "That's exactly what I'm telling you."

They held each other's gaze across the chest. Somewhere overhead a hawk rode the thermals, indifferent. The patrol boat sat in the channel, patient as arithmetic.

"Why did you really hire me?" Paloma asked suddenly. "The survey flight. Nobody pays triple rate for a survey flight and brings two suitcases full of—what, gloves? Brushes? You packed like someone going to handle documents. You knew something was out here."

Ines didn't flinch. "I knew the flood would change the banks. I've been flying this reach every dry season for four years, chartering whoever would take me, asking pilots to fly low along the cutbanks. Most of them wouldn't. Too risky. Then your name came up—Toma said you'd fly low for money and ask no questions."

"Toma talks too much."

"Toma talks exactly enough." Ines crouched again, resting her hands on her knees. "I didn't know about the chest. I swear that. I thought—I hoped—that forty years of flooding might finally give up something. A ledger. A marker stone. Anything. I'm an archivist, Montrose. I don't excavate. I *wait*. Waiting is the whole profession. And I have waited eleven years for a country to stop being dead." She looked down at the seal. "And then the river hands it to me in an afternoon, and the first thing I learn is that the world will send armed men to take it back."

Paloma exhaled slowly. The arithmetic was still running, and it kept arriving at the same ugly sum: the boat had seen them. The boat had photographed them, or would. There was no version of this where they walked away clean, because the plane's registration was on file, the flight plan was logged, and a blue-and-white bush plane making an unscheduled landing beside a San Marisolan seal was already a data point in somebody's system.

Leaving the chest didn't erase them from the story. It just left them defenseless in it.

"Say I'm wrong," Paloma said at last. "Say we take it. Say we somehow get it past that boat, off this river, into hiding. What do you think is in there that's worth what comes next?"

Ines reached out and laid her palm flat on the lid, over the frigatebird.

"Treaties," she said. "If I'm right—if this is what I think it is—it's the original treaties. The ones San Marisol signed with the colonial administration, and then the ones the concession company claimed to inherit. Because here's what the history books won't say plainly: Meridian's entire legal claim to this land rests on transfer documents from a government that officially ceased to exist *before the ink was dry*. The grants were backdated. The signatures were forged. Everyone in the ministries knows it and no one can prove it, because the originals vanished the same year the country did." Her fingers curled slightly against the wood. "A chest like this doesn't hold souvenirs, Montrose. National archive chests hold the things a country couldn't afford to lose. And if the treaties are in there—real, dated, signed—then every Meridian concession north of the falls is built on paper that was never legal. Forty years of land grants. Gone. In one afternoon."

The wind moved through the grass. Downriver, the patrol boat's engine note changed—a deepening, a turn.

"They're coming in," Paloma said. "Decision time. We leave it, or we take it, and either way we're in the water now."

"If we leave it, they take it and destroy it, and my grandmother's country dies a second time."

"If we take it, they hunt us for it, and I've spent seven years getting good at being hunted. Trust me, it's overrated."

"Then you already know how to survive this." Ines stood, and there was something almost gentle in how she said it, which made it worse. "I don't. I've never run from anything in my life. I've only ever waited. So I'm asking you—not as your passenger, not as the woman paying your invoice—as the person who knows what's in that chest matters more than what happens to either of us. Help me carry it to the plane."

Paloma looked at the chest. At the boat, now maybe four hundred meters out and closing. At the sky, which was clear for the moment but wouldn't stay that way this time of year.

Every rule they had—no names, no stops longer than an hour, no questions answered—had been designed for exactly this moment. Rules were what kept you alive out here. Rules were the wall between you and the current that pulled everything downstream eventually.

But rules assumed you got to choose what you carried.

"Three minutes," Paloma said. "We lift in three minutes. You ride with it on your lap. If we go down, you let it sink—you swim, the chest doesn't. Swear it."

"I swear."

"And Vidal." Paloma bent, gripped the iron handles, and felt the true weight of the thing—denser than it should be, packed tight, a country's worth of paper compressed into sixty kilos. "This doesn't make us partners. This makes us two people in the same current. Don't confuse the two."

"I won't," Ines said, lifting the other side. "Partners trust each other. We'll settle for surviving."

They carried it to the plane through the drying mud, each step sucking at their boots, the patrol boat's wake spreading white across the brown water behind it. Paloma's hands shook slightly on the handles—not from the weight—and they were grateful Ines couldn't see their face.

Some things, once picked up, could not be set back down. Seven years ago they had learned that lesson the hard way, in a night of rain and a falsified logbook, and had spent every day since pretending otherwise.

Now the river had handed them the same lesson a second time, in iron and wax and a dead nation's seal.

The engine caught on the second try. Behind them, the chest rode wedged between the seats, and ahead the channel opened south, toward weather, toward the interior, toward every place the maps had agreed to forget.

Paloma pushed the throttle forward and did not look back at the boat, or the bank, or the mud closing wetly over the place where the chest had lain.

But they both knew the men in the white hull had cameras.

And cameras, unlike rivers, never gave anything back.
