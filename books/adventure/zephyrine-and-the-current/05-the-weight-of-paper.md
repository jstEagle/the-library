## 5. The Weight of Paper

The mission station appeared out of the green like a shipwreck that had decided to become a building.

Paloma had passed it a hundred times at altitude—a gray tooth of masonry on the east bank where the river bent wide and slow—but they had never landed there. No reason to. The roof was half gone, the chapel's bell tower leaned like a drunk against its own wall, and the jungle had begun the long patient work of taking it all back. But it had walls, and walls meant shade, and shade meant they could open a chest that had spent forty years underwater without an audience.

They set down on the shingle bar below the ruins just before noon, Paloma easing the plane onto the gravel with the tenderness of someone handling something already broken once. The engine ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the trees, a bird made a sound like a hinge that needed oil.

Ines stood on the bank with her hands on the chest and didn't move.

"You want to do this here?" she said. "Or do you want to fly another hour to somewhere with a door?"

"A door has two sides," Paloma said. "This way, anyone coming has to cross open ground first." They nodded upriver, then down. "I'll see them before they see us."

Ines looked at the open ground, the water, the sky. She was doing arithmetic—Paloma could see it moving behind her eyes—and she didn't like any of the totals. But she picked up her end of the chest without arguing, and together they carried it up through the grass to what had been the mission's refectory: a long room with one standing wall, a collapsed section of roof that let in a column of white light, and a floor of packed earth swept clean by forty years of wind.

The light fell across the chest like a spotlight nobody had asked for.

* * *

It took twenty minutes to get it open. The lock was fused; the hinges were worse. Paloma worked a flat screwdriver around the lid's seam while Ines knelt opposite, wiping mud from the seal with her sleeve in small, careful circles, the way you'd clean a face.

"San Marisol," she said again, when the coat of arms emerged fully—the sunburst over water, the motto beneath it worn but legible. *Del río la verdad.* From the river, truth. She said it like she was translating for someone who wasn't there. "My grandmother used to say that. I thought it was a family saying. A private joke." Her thumb stopped on the metal. "It was a national motto. It was on their stamps. Their passports. And I grew up thinking my grandmother invented it in her kitchen."

"People invent countries in kitchens all the time," Paloma said. "Most of them don't last."

"This one lasted ninety years and then got erased in an afternoon." Ines sat back on her heels. "Do you understand what I'm saying? There are maps—official maps, survey maps—in government archives where this river valley is labeled *unorganized territory*. Like nobody ever lived here. Like the towns never existed. My grandmother came from a town called Puerto Solano. You can look for it. It isn't there. The word isn't there."

The screwdriver found the gap it wanted. Paloma leaned their weight into it, and the lid gave with a groan of tearing rust and a smell that rose up out of forty years of dark: river silt, old iron, and paper—that dry, sweet, ghostly smell of paper that has been kept dry against every odd.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, were bundles.

Not gold. Not weapons. Paper.

Ines made a sound low in her throat, halfway between a laugh and something else, and reached in with both hands.

* * *

She worked like a surgeon and read like a mourner, which is to say both at once, and Paloma watched her do it while pretending to watch the treeline instead.

The first bundle was treaties. Three of them, bound in ribbon that had once been red. Ines unrolled them across the refectory floor, weighting the corners with stones, and the column of light lay across the signatures like a finger pointing.

"Look at the dates," she said. Her voice had changed—flatter, more careful, the voice of someone carrying something heavy across ice. "1908. 1921. 1946. These are land cession agreements between San Marisol and—" she stopped, and tapped the page twice, hard. "And Meridian. Or its grandfather company. Same name, different letterhead. Look at the terms. Every treaty concedes more territory than the last. Every one is signed by the same five men for San Marisol. And look—" she turned the last page toward Paloma, angling it into the light. "Look at the signatures themselves."

Paloma looked. Five names, five flourishes. On all three treaties, spread across thirty-eight years, the ink was identical. Not similar. Identical. The same pen pressure, the same blot where the same hand had rested too long on the same letter.

"They signed these after the country stopped existing," Ines said quietly. "The 1946 treaty is dated nine years after San Marisol was dissolved. Somebody needed legal cover for the concessions, so somebody forged a nation's signature on paper the nation never agreed to. And then the courts upheld it, because the courts had already been paid, or already been frightened, or already been dissolved themselves." She smoothed the page with both palms, gently, as if calming it. "This isn't evidence of a crime. This is the crime. Preserved. Wrapped in oilcloth. Delivered to us by a flood."

Paloma said nothing. They were looking at the second bundle, the one Ines hadn't opened yet, because its label had caught the light: a strip of paper, typed, still legible.

*CENSO — POBLACIÓN CIVIL — 1949.*

A census.

* * *

The census was a ledger, and the ledger was worse than the treaties, though Paloma couldn't have said why until Ines began reading the columns aloud.

Village names. Headcounts. Occupations. Then, in a second hand—a clerk's hand, hurried, the entries crammed into margins—annotations. *Departed.* *Relocated.* *Unaccounted.*

Puerto Solano: 1,240 souls. Unaccounted: 1,240.

Arroyo Claro: 311. Unaccounted: 311.

Santa Rosa de las Aguas: 89. Unaccounted: 89.

"There were forty-one villages," Ines said. She wasn't reading anymore. She was reciting, and her voice had gone somewhere far away and very level, the way voices go when the alternative is breaking. "I've spent eleven years in archives looking for these names. Eleven years. And they were in a box under a riverbank the whole time, waiting for the water to give them back." She closed the ledger with exaggerated care, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "Forty years ago, everyone who signed these pages was disappeared. Not killed—we'd have found graves, someone would have talked. Disappeared. Moved. Scattered. Erased so thoroughly that even the erasure was erased."

"And nobody noticed a whole country leaving?"

"Everybody noticed. Nobody was permitted to say so." Ines looked up, and her eyes were dry and furious. "That's the trick of it, Paloma. You don't need to hide a massacre if you can hide the vocabulary. If the villages aren't on the map, and the mapmakers answer to the concession, and the concession answers to the ministry, and the ministry answers to—then nothing happened. Nothing can have happened. There's no *there* there." She gestured at the chest. "Except now there is. Now there's ink."

Paloma turned back to the third bundle, because looking at Ines's face had become difficult, and because the third bundle was photographs, and photographs were always the worst part.

* * *

There were perhaps sixty of them, sepia going silver at the edges, stuck together in places and separated with a patience that cost Ines visibly. Mission schoolchildren in rows, squinting. A river landing with barges stacked with crates. A church with a bell tower that did not lean. Men in suits shaking hands in front of a flag with a sunburst on it, everyone smiling the stiff smiles of people who suspect they are being lied to but haven't yet confirmed it.

And then a series that made Ines stop breathing for a moment: photographs of documents being signed. Ceremonial signings, pens raised, officials seated at long tables. And in the corner of one frame, half-cropped, a man holding a camera of his own, documenting the documentation.

"He knew," Ines whispered. "Whoever assembled this chest knew what was happening and photographed the proof of it and sealed it and sank it—or hid it where he thought no one would look, and the river finished the job. He built a time capsule addressed to whoever dug it up." She laughed, short and disbelieving. "Addressed to me. Forty years early."

"Could be addressed to anyone," Paloma said. "You're just the one who bought the ticket."

"No." Ines shook her head slowly, turning a photograph of the church over in her hands. On the back, in pencil, faded: a date, and a single word. *Solano.* "He labeled things. He wanted them findable. That's not an accident, that's a will." She looked up. "Someone died making sure this survived. The least I can do is—"

She stopped. Because Paloma had gone still.

It was the kind of stillness that has weight. Ines had known them three days, and even she felt the temperature of the room change.

"What?" she said.

Paloma was holding the census ledger again. They had opened it back to the village list, and their finger was resting on a line near the bottom, on a name printed in the same clerk's hurried hand as all the others.

*Alto Ceibo: 204. Unaccounted: 204.*

"Where is Alto Ceibo?" Ines asked.

"North fork," Paloma said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them, and cost them, which didn't. "Confluence of the north fork and the main channel. There's a bend there. Deep water, bad current on the outside of the turn." They were quiet a moment. "There's a wreck there, too. Half-buried in the bank. Old floatplane. Nobody ever salvaged it."

Ines waited. She was good at waiting; it was probably an archivist trait.

"I put it there," Paloma said.

* * *

The wind moved through the broken roof and stirred the treaties on the floor, and Paloma watched the pages ripple and thought about how paper was supposed to be the lightest thing there was, and how wrong that was.

"Seven years ago I flew a charter out of Alto Ceibo," they said. "Overloaded. I knew it was overloaded—I did the weight and balance myself, and then I let two more passengers on because the money was good and I owed money, which is the whole story of my life compressed into one sentence. We lifted off from the village strip at dusk. One of the passengers was a surveyor for the concession company. Meridian, or whatever it was called then—it doesn't matter, it's the same animal. He was doing boundary work. Marking out which trees belonged to whom, except none of them belonged to anybody, and everybody flying cargo in those days knew the surveys were cover for something, and we all took the money anyway."

Ines had not moved. The photograph of the church lay face-down in her lap.

"We went down in the bend," Paloma said. "Right where the north fork meets the main channel. Engine didn't fail. Wing didn't fail. *I* failed—I misjudged the turn with too much weight, and the aircraft stalled, and we hit the water on the outside of the curve. The surveyor drowned. His name was Emil Ríos. I never learned anything else about him. I didn't want to know anything else about him, because knowing would have required staying, and staying would have required—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "I falsified the logbook. I reported engine failure. There was an inquiry, and I left before it concluded, and I've been flying cargo into places with no addresses ever since."

"The census says two hundred four people vanished from Alto Ceibo," Ines said carefully.

"I know what it says."

"After your crash. Within a few years of it."

"I know what it says, Ines." Paloma set the ledger down with more force than intended, and dust jumped in the column of light. "I flew medicine into that village for two years. I knew the midwife's name. I taught two kids to make paper airplanes in the shadow of my wing. And then I crashed a plane into their river, killed a man, ran from the inquiry—and sometime after that, the whole place stopped existing. I told myself for seven years that the two things weren't connected. That the crash was mine and the vanishing was theirs and the company's, separate sins, separate ledgers." They looked at the census, at the neat hurried handwriting of a dead clerk. "But nothing here is separate. That's the whole point of this chest. It's all one ledger. It always was."

Ines was silent for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its archival distance entirely.

"You could have told me any of this three days ago."

"No."

"Why tell me now?"

Paloma looked at the photograph Ines had turned over. *Solano*, written in pencil by a dead man who had also decided, at some point, that hiding was no longer survivable.

"Because yesterday this was your country's ghost," Paloma said. "Today it's mine too. And if we're going to carry this thing out through whatever's coming, you should know exactly how much of my reason for running is sitting in this box."

* * *

They repacked the chest as the light went long and amber through the broken roof, wrapping each bundle the way it had been wrapped, though neither of them could have said why—the original order suddenly seemed sacred, or at least load-bearing. Ines copied the census index into her notebook, village by village, number by number, her pencil moving fast. Paloma walked the perimeter of the ruin twice, checking the sightlines to the shingle bar, and on the second circuit stopped at the chapel doorway and looked at the leaning bell tower for longer than the task required.

"You're thinking about Alto Ceibo," Ines said, coming up behind them.

"I'm thinking about the north fork," Paloma said. "It's on our route south. Twenty minutes' detour."

"To the wreck."

"To the wreck."

Ines studied them. "Why?"

"Because the chest came out of the river," Paloma said, "and I've spent seven years telling myself the river only takes." They turned back toward the refectory, toward the plane, toward the last hour of usable light. "Come on. We should be airborne before—"

They stopped.

Downriver, faint but unmistakable on the evening air, came the sound Paloma knew better than their own heartbeat: an outboard motor, running at an efficient cruise, not hurrying, not hiding.

Two strokes. Big engine. Coming up the channel.

Ines heard it too. Her hand found Paloma's sleeve.

"Company?" she breathed.

Paloma listened again, eyes on the empty water, and felt the old cold professional certainty settle over them like a coat.

"No," they said. "Worse. That's a boat that wants to be heard."

*End of Chapter 5*
