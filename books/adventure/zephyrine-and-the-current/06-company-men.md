## 6. Company Men

The mission station had a bell that hadn't rung in years, and Paloma heard the boat before they saw it—the flat diesel knock of an outboard running heavy with load, not speed. They were on the veranda re-lacing a fuel line when the sound came upriver through the rain-softened morning, and their hands stopped moving on their own, the way a deer's legs stop.

Ines was inside with the chest open, papers fanned across a table under a kerosene lamp even though it was daylight. She'd been photographing everything with a small camera she kept in a oilcloth pouch, page by page, muttering catalog numbers under her breath like prayers.

"Company," Paloma said through the window.

She looked up. "How do you know?"

"Because nobody else runs two engines at six in the morning to visit a dead mission."

* * *

There were two of them, as advertised by the sound. The boat eased against the rotted pilings below the station and two men climbed the mud-slick steps to the veranda where Paloma stood alone, having told Ines—firmly, in the tone of someone giving a preflight briefing—to stay inside with the lamp out and the chest closed.

The first man was big in the way of men who eat at company tables: soft through the middle but broad in the shoulders, wearing a khaki shirt with the sleeves rolled and a Meridian Concessions patch sewn over the pocket like it meant something out here. He had a sunburned neck and a smile he'd practiced in a mirror. The second man was smaller, younger, and said nothing at all. He stood one step down from the veranda with his hands loose at his sides, and his eyes never stopped moving—veranda, roofline, the blue-and-white tail of the Maule visible past the station's east wing where Paloma had tucked it under a strangler fig.

Paloma had spent seven years learning what that second man's stillness cost, because Paloma had once been paid to be exactly that still.

"Morning," the big man said. "Name's Corben. This is Ruiz." A nod at the quiet one, who didn't acknowledge it. "You the pilot?"

"I'm the pilot."

"Saw your plane come in yesterday evening. Low pass over the bend." He said it pleasantly, conversationally, the way you mention weather. "Thought maybe you were surveying."

"Ferrying medicine down to Santa Rosa. Storm caught me. Figured I'd overnight here rather than bend something on a night approach."

Corben nodded slowly, taking this in, and Paloma watched him take it in and understood that the nod wasn't agreement—it was filing. Somewhere behind those eyes a card was being written up: *pilot, blue-and-white Maule, claims medical run, claims weather.* The card would be compared later against other cards. That was how professionals worked. They didn't argue with your story; they collected it.

"Medicine run," Corben said. "That's decent work. Who's the outfit?"

"Church contract. Diocesan air service, out of Puerto Viejo."

"You don't look like a church man."

"I don't charge like one either."

That got a laugh, or the shape of one. Corben leaned against a veranda post that groaned softly under him. "Here's my situation, friend. Meridian holds salvage rights along this stretch of river. Flood moved a lot of ground last week—banks caving, old sites coming up. We're obligated to document anything of historical value that surfaces, on behalf of the government." He produced a folded paper from his shirt pocket and held it up without quite offering it. "Official authorization. Signed."

Paloma didn't reach for it. "Congratulations."

"So we're asking everyone on the river: seen anything come up? Metal, wood, anything with markings? Old sites especially. There was a settlement around here once—before the concession, way before. Most folks forgot about it." He smiled again, wider. "Most folks forget most things."

"River took whatever there was to take," Paloma said. "I flew over the collapse yesterday. It's all silt and snags. You want to dive it, be my guest. Water's brown as coffee for another month."

Ruiz spoke for the first time, and his voice was mild and unhurried, which made it worse. "Your passenger. The woman who hired you in Puerto Viejo. She come along for the medicine too?"

The question landed exactly where a professional would aim it—not at the story, at the seam of it. Paloma felt the old cold start behind the sternum, familiar as a heartbeat, and let it spread outward until the hands went steady and the voice went level. Seven years ago this feeling had been a job skill. Now it was just survival, and survival had gotten very good at pretending to be a job.

"No passenger," Paloma said. "I fly solo. Cheaper insurance."

Ruiz looked at Paloma for a long moment. Not hostile. Almost kind. It was the look of a man checking a lock to see if it was already open.

"That's funny," Ruiz said, "because the harbor master at Puerto Viejo says a woman booked a survey charter on a blue-and-white Maule three days back. Paid cash. Overpaid, he thought."

"Harbor masters see a lot of planes. Half of them are blue-and-white."

"Not many Maules doing survey work this far up. And not many pilots who lie about passengers." Ruiz said it without heat, almost apologetically, like a man pointing out a stain on your shirt. Then he shrugged and stepped back, yielding the veranda to Corben again, and Paloma understood the structure of the thing: Corben was the face, the friendly pressure, the paperwork. Ruiz was the instrument. The instrument had already taken its reading.

Corben held out the folded paper after all, and this time Paloma took it, because refusing it would have been louder than reading it. It was a genuine government form, properly stamped, granting Meridian authority to recover items of historical significance within the flood zone. Someone in a ministry office had signed it without looking, the way officials sign things. The forgery, if there was one, wasn't in the document. It was in the word *recovery*, which meant *removal*, which meant *theft with a receipt*.

"If anything does surface," Corben said, "you're legally obliged to report it. Salvage rights are serious business. There's a reward for cooperation." He named a figure that would keep a bush pilot fueled for a year. "And consequences for the other thing. Nobody wants the other thing."

"Nothing surfaced," Paloma said, handing the paper back. "But if I find a chest of gold sitting in the mud, you'll be the first to know."

Corben laughed for real this time. "Gold. Sure. Gold." He turned to go, then paused on the top step, and the practiced warmth dropped off his face like a tool set down. What remained was just tired professional attention. "One more thing. Your plane. Tail number ends in four-one-seven?"

Paloma said nothing.

"We photographed it yesterday," Corben said. "From the water. Standard procedure—every aircraft operating in the concession zone gets logged. Nothing personal." He smiled the mirror-smile again. "Fly safe, friend. River's high. Things come loose in high water."

They went down the steps. The outboard knocked twice and caught, and the boat slid out into the brown current and shrank to a wake, and then to nothing.

* * *

Paloma stood on the veranda until the sound was fully gone, counting to two hundred out of habit, because professionals sometimes idled downstream and listened. Then they went inside.

Ines was standing pressed against the wall beside the shuttered window, and she had heard every word—of course she had; the shutters were thin—and her face had gone the color of the paper she'd been photographing. Her hands were shaking. She noticed Paloma noticing and shoved them into her pockets like a child hiding a broken toy.

"They know," she whispered. "The harbor master talked. They know about me, they know the plane—"

"Sit down."

"Don't tell me to sit—" She didn't sit. She started instead toward the table where the documents lay in their careful rows, and began gathering them with quick, desperate hands, stacking pages crooked, misordering a census she'd spent two days sequencing. "We have to move now, tonight, we have to—"

"Ines." Paloma crossed the room and put one hand flat on the table between her and the papers. Not grabbing. Just a hand, a border. "Look at me."

She looked. Her eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once, and underneath them was something harder that Paloma recognized from mirrors: the part of a person that refuses, that will not hand over the thing, no matter what.

"They're gone," Paloma said quietly. "They ran two engines because they wanted us to hear them coming. Everything they did out there was theater. The paper, the reward, the friendly talk—that's the soft approach. It costs nothing and it works most of the time. When it doesn't work, they come back differently, and next time there won't be a conversation." Paloma picked up the crooked stack, squared it against the table edge, and set it back down in order, one hand steadying hers when it hovered. "So we do this the way I do everything. Slowly. In order. Because panic is how people die in the bush, and it's also how evidence gets confiscated."

"He knew the tail number." Her voice cracked on it. "He recited it. Four-one-seven. He'd looked at a photograph of your plane while talking to you about it. That means—"

"It means they photographed us before they ever knocked." Paloma had been holding this since the veranda, turning it over, letting it get heavier. Now they said it aloud and watched it land on both of them. "Which means the patrol boat on the river yesterday, the one we waved at, the one I judged harmless because it was moving away—it wasn't moving away. It was holding position long enough to shoot us with a camera. Long lens. One frame is enough. Tail number, paint scheme, my face at the controls if they're good." Paloma sat down slowly on the bench, and for a moment the competent loneliness of seven years opened under them like the collapsed bank, all the way down to the water. "I used to do this. Did I ever tell you that? Before. I was the one in the boat. You log the asset, you file the frame, you let somebody else decide what the frame means. And the person being logged never knows how much they've already lost until the day it arrives."

Ines lowered herself onto the bench across the table. The lamp between them threw their shadows huge on the mission wall, over faded whitewash where some long-dead priest had painted a cross that time had half-eaten.

"So they have my name," she said. "Or enough of it. Vidal, Puerto Viejo, the booking. They'll pull the harbor records, they'll find Mateo—" She stopped. Paloma watched her stop, watched the door close behind her eyes on whatever she'd been about to say, and filed it away the way Ruiz would have, gently, for later.

"They'll build a file," Paloma said. "Names, dates, movements. Then someone above Corben decides whether we're worth a floatplane and four men or just a phone call to the right police captain. Either way, the clock starts today. Whatever we're going to do with these papers, we now do it on their schedule, not ours."

"The permits expire in nineteen days," Ines said. "If the original treaties surface before then—if they enter a courtroom before the concessions renew—Meridian loses everything. Forty years of land grants, voided. So they can't let these documents reach a judge. Which means—" She laughed, short and bitter, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Which means we're not cargo anymore, are we. We're the schedule."

Outside, the river ran high and brown and indifferent, carrying whatever the flood had given back toward the sea, past checkpoints and patrol boats and men with cameras. Paloma looked at the chest—at the seal of a country that officially had never existed, stamped in wax older than either of them—and felt the hunter's chill settle into its final shape. Not fear, exactly. Arithmetic. Every hour the Maule sat under the strangler fig was an hour someone in a Meridian office was connecting dots with a ruler.

"We fly tomorrow, first light," Paloma said. "South, before they expect movement. And Ines—" They waited until she met their eyes. "When they come back, and they will, there won't be a form to read. So tonight you learn where everything is in that chest blindfolded, because if we're separated, the papers can't be allowed to be in the wrong hands. Yours or mine. Whichever of us keeps them has to disappear with them completely."

"And the other one of us?"

Paloma looked out at the darkening river, at the place where the boat had vanished, and answered honestly, because the chest had already made lying expensive.

"The other one of us buys time. However they can."

That night, while Ines drilled herself on the contents of a nation that wasn't on the map, Paloma walked down to the plane in the dark and checked the tail by feel, tracing the stenciled numbers like a scar. 417. Somewhere in a filing cabinet in a company town, that number already had a folder. Paloma had built folders like it once, and knew exactly what the first entry in a new folder always was:

*Subject unaware.*
