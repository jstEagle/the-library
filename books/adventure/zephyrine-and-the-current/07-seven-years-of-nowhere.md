## 7. Seven Years of Nowhere

The rain started an hour before sunset and never really stopped for three days.

Paloma remembered that first, the way they always did — not the passenger, not the debt, not even the sound the plane made when it came apart against the water, but the rain itself, patient and gray, falling on everything equally like it had no opinion about any of them.

Seven years later they could still stand in a downpour at some fuel strip on the middle river and feel that night press up through the wet ground under their boots.

They had never let themselves look at it straight before. That was the trick of running: you kept the bad thing in your peripheral vision, where it stayed small. You flew with your eyes fixed on the horizon ahead, which was always just far enough away to be survivable.

But Ines Vidal was asleep in the tent ten meters away, breathing slow, and the chest sat between them in the dark with its seal facing up, and Paloma lay awake on a foam pad listening to the river work the sandbar, and for the first time since the rain stopped falling seven years ago, they turned their head and looked directly at it.

* * *

His name had been Aurelio Sandoval, and he weighed more than his manifest said he did.

That was how it started, the way these things always start: with arithmetic. Paloma had been flying charter out of Puerto Cabral for eleven months by then, and eleven months of bush work had taught them what every pilot learns eventually — that the manifest is a suggestion, that the scale at the strip is a liar, and that the only real weight limit is the one your conscience sets, and conscience is the first thing poverty eats.

Paloma's debts were not dramatic. No gambling, no woman, no knife fight over cards. It was worse than that; it was boring. The engine overhaul they'd deferred twice. The hangar fees. The loan from a man named Ferro who smiled too much and charged interest that recalculated itself whenever Ferro felt like it. Fourteen hundred pesos, compounding monthly into something that no longer had a shape Paloma could picture. Every flight Paloma took went backward. Every day the plane sat on the ground, it cost money to exist.

So when Aurelio Sandoval came to the strip office at dusk with cash and a story — a mining engineer, he said, needed to reach San Rafael de los Ríos by morning, equipment and all, willing to pay triple rate because the scheduled flight had canceled — Paloma looked at the money and then at the sky and then at the money again.

"How much equipment?"

"Two crates. Not heavy."

Everything about him was slightly wrong. His clothes were good but creased like they'd been packed in a hurry. His accent was capital-city, but he said *San Rafael de los Ríos* the way locals did, swallowing the second syllable, and he flinched — barely, a twitch of the eyelid — when Paloma repeated the destination back to him. He paid half upfront in bills so crisp they might have come off the press that morning.

Paloma knew all of this. Paloma catalogued all of this, standing there in the office with the lamp buzzing and the rain beginning to tick on the tin roof, and then Paloma took the money anyway.

That was the part they had never let themselves think about clearly. Not the storm, not the overload, not the falsified logbook. This moment: the knowing, and the taking anyway. Because it was easier, afterward, to tell yourself the accident had been weather, had been fate, had been the river's doing — anything but the truth, which was that a person had stood in a lit room doing arithmetic with another man's life in the right-hand column and decided the number worked.

"Weight limit's nine hundred kilos with full fuel," Paloma said. "You and the crates together."

"We're maybe two-fifty," Sandoval said, and smiled, and the smile was also slightly wrong.

Later, loading the crates by flashlight while the rain thickened, Paloma felt the weight of them and knew. Two hundred fifty? Each crate alone was close to that. They were stenciled with a name Paloma didn't read closely enough — didn't *want* to read closely enough — and when Paloma asked what was in them, Sandoval said "survey instruments," and Paloma, holding a crate that clinked with a sound survey instruments do not make, set it in the cabin and said nothing.

There was a version of Paloma, standing there in the rain, who closed the cargo door and said *find another plane*. Paloma had thought about that version many times since. Had built whole nights around that version, imagining the life where the debts got worse and the plane got repossessed and Ferro sent men around and still — *still* — it would have been better, wouldn't it? Wouldn't it?

The honest answer, examined now at last, lying flat on a sandbar with a nation's dead paperwork six feet away: yes. Yes, it would have been better. That was the unbearable part. There had been a door marked *poorer but clean*, and it had been open, and Paloma had walked past it.

* * *

The Cessna lifted off Puerto Cabral at 21:40, forty minutes after last light, into weather that any pilot with a functioning survival instinct would have called unflyable.

Paloma had flown in worse. That was the lie pilots tell themselves — that skill is a currency that buys exemption. The storm cell sitting on the middle river that night wasn't the worst Paloma had seen. It was merely the worst Paloma had ever chosen to enter, loaded two hundred kilos over gross, at night, with a stranger whose papers didn't hold up to a second reading, carrying cargo that clinked.

The first hour was fine. That's the thing nobody tells you about disasters: they mostly begin with things being fine. The wipers worked. The radio crackled along. Sandoval sat in the right seat saying nothing, watching the black country slide beneath them, and once, when lightning lit the interior like a flashbulb, Paloma saw his hands — gripping his knees, knuckles white, a man praying or counting or both.

"You've done this run before?" Paloma asked, to fill the noise.

"No," he said. Then, after a mile of silence: "Have you?"

"Everywhere on this river."

He nodded slowly. "Then you know what's down there." It wasn't quite a question, and Paloma didn't answer it, and the lightning came again and showed him looking down at the water with an expression Paloma couldn't read and hadn't tried to.

The engine coughed at 23:05.

Not dramatically. A hesitation, a stumble, a catch — the kind of thing you log and mention to your mechanic and forget. But Paloma had been ignoring that engine's complaints for months, deferring the overhaul, stretching the oil changes, and they both knew what the cough meant the way old married couples know what a certain silence means.

Paloma began a descent toward the river. Standard procedure: get low, find water, put it down while you still have light and altitude to trade. The river below was running high with the rains, brown and fast, but wide here, forgiving-looking.

The second cough came before they'd lost five hundred feet, and this time the engine didn't catch.

What Paloma remembers of the next ninety seconds has never arrived as a sequence. It arrives as fragments, the way heavy things arrive: the propeller windmilling with a sound like a card in bicycle spokes. Sandoval's voice saying something short and ordinary — "ah," possibly, or a word in a language Paloma didn't recognize, which bothered them for years until they finally admitted it might have been a prayer. The altimeter unwinding. The black water rising with terrible patience. Paloma's own voice, level and almost calm, saying "brace, brace" to a passenger who had nowhere to brace except against the end of everything.

The left wing caught the water first. Or the float strut. Or the tail — the inquiry report, if there had ever been a proper inquiry, would have argued about it forever. What Paloma knows is that the world went from air to noise to white to cold, and then there was water coming through the broken windshield, and then there was a sandbar, and then there was Paloma dragging themselves out onto it, and then there was the long, long time of calling Aurelio Sandoval's name into the rain and hearing nothing come back.

They found him at dawn, forty meters downstream, snagged in a fallen tree. He had made it out of the aircraft — the right-side door was open, jammed open, his shoulder had done that — and the water had taken him anyway. He was thirty-one years old. In his jacket pocket, wrapped in plastic, was a folded paper that the rain had reached anyway: a letter, in careful handwriting, addressed to someone named Rosa, and the letter began *By the time you read this I will have done what I can,* and Paloma stood over it in the drizzle and understood, in a single nauseating drop, exactly what kind of man they had carried and what kind of errand he had been running and why his crates had clinked, and none of that knowledge changed the fact that he was dead, and none of the fact that he was dead made Paloma less guilty, though for years Paloma would try to spend it that way.

* * *

Here is what happened next, laid out flat, without the fog Paloma had kept around it:

Paloma walked to the fishing camp two kilometers downstream. A family named Ocampo lived there. They fed Paloma, dried Paloma's clothes, lent a canoe, asked nothing. Paloma told them there had been an accident, that the passenger was lost, that the authorities should be notified — and then Paloma got in the canoe and paddled upriver instead of down, away from the telegraph office at San Rafael, and by the third day had talked themselves into a plan that was not a plan but a direction: away.

The logbook took an hour to falsify, hunched in the canoe under a tarp. Fuel uplift altered. Departure time moved forward two days, into weather that had already cleared. Weight and balance figures rewritten in different ink. It was clumsy work — Paloma was a pilot, not a forger — and Paloma knew it was clumsy and filed it anyway, because the alternative was a signature on a truthful page, and a truthful page was a road that led to a courtroom, and a courtroom was a room where a jury would ask questions and Paloma would have to say out loud, in front of strangers, *I knew, and I took the money anyway.*

That sentence. That was the whole thing. That was the load-bearing wall of seven years of running, and Paloma had never once said it aloud, had barely allowed it to form as words even inside their own skull. It lived lower than language, in the body — in the way Paloma checked mirrors, in the way they never used their full name at fuel strips, in the rules they'd built like a shell around their life: no names, no stops longer than an hour, no questions answered. Everyone on the river assumed those rules were caution. Some of them were. But most of them were penance wearing caution's clothes, because if you stayed anywhere longer than an hour, people started to matter, and if people mattered, then eventually someone would ask who you were, and the answer was a woman who had killed a man with arithmetic and run from the bill.

Standing trial would have meant stopping. Stopping meant the questions. And the questions — this was the discovery of this sleepless night on the sandbar, this plain and awful inventory — the questions were not about whether Paloma could survive a courtroom. They were about whether Paloma could survive the honesty the courtroom required. Seven years of flight had been built on the belief that confession was the dangerous thing, that exposure was death. And it had worked, technically. Paloma was alive, solvent, respected at a dozen strips, known by the useful alias of competence.

But alive was not the same as free, and Paloma had begun, somewhere in the last weeks — since the flood, since the chest, since a woman with two suitcases looked at them like she could see the ledger behind their eyes — to suspect that the running had never actually been cheaper. That it had simply billed in smaller installments. Interest, compounding monthly, into something that no longer had a shape anyone could picture.

Ferro's debt had taught them nothing. They'd learned it all over again with a human life.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the nylon. The river had dropped another handspan overnight; the chest's shadow lengthened beside the tent like a witness.

Ines stirred, sat up, pushed her hair back, and looked at Paloma for a long moment before saying anything. Archivist's eyes. She'd spent her life reading documents that other people had tried to alter, and she had apparently decided that Paloma was one.

"You didn't sleep," she said.

"Thinking."

"About the documents?"

"About a man named Aurelio Sandoval," Paloma said, and heard their own voice do something it hadn't done in seven years: stay steady while telling the truth. "And about a logbook I wrote in two kinds of ink."

Ines went very still. Outside, the current slid past, going somewhere, taking its secrets with it unless the flood chose otherwise.

"The charge," she said quietly. Not a question. She had read Palomo's file somewhere, or guessed, or simply recognized the species — another keeper of records that someone powerful wanted erased. "Manslaughter. Puerto Cabral district. Seven years ago."

"You knew."

"I suspected. Now I don't have to." She pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the silence was the first honest one they'd shared.

"If we take this chest to the capital," Paloma said, "it ends in a hearing room. Papers on a table. People asking questions in front of each other." They looked at the river, at the country it had uncovered, at everything that had stayed buried for forty years until the water gave it back. "I've spent seven years making sure I was never in a room like that."

"And now?"

Paloma thought about the door marked *poorer but clean*, still standing open somewhere behind them all this distance, patient as weather. Thought about how every installment of running had been deducted from the same account, and how the balance had finally come due in a place like this: dawn, a river, a box of truths belonging to people who had never been permitted to keep theirs.

"Now," Paloma said, "I suppose we find out what stopping costs."

Ines reached over and touched the chest's seal, lightly, like touching a gravestone. "First," she said, "we need fuel. And there's only one man on this stretch of river who'll sell it to us without asking why a blue-and-white Cessna suddenly matters to so many people."

"Toma," Paloma said.

"Toma," she agreed. "And Paloma — Toma talks. If we're going to owe him, we should decide now what we're willing to let him know."

Paloma looked downriver, where the smoke of burn-off season was already smudging the horizon, and began, for the first time in seven years, to make a plan that pointed toward trouble instead of away from it.
