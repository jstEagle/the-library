## 8. The Permit Clock

The mission station had a table that had survived everything except termites, and by lamplight it became a war room.

Ines had the documents spread in rows, weighted at the corners with stones from the collapsed wall, and she worked through them the way Paloma imagined she must have worked through everything in her life—methodically, hungrily, with her sleeves pushed up as if the past were a stubborn engine cowling that would only open for someone willing to get grease under her nails.

Paloma sat across from her with a mug of chicory gone cold and watched the lamplight move on the paper. They should have been planning a route south. Fuel stops, weather windows, the long dogleg around the Meridian checkpoint at Puerto Cobre. That was the work they knew how to do. Instead they were watching an archivist read a dead country back into existence one page at a time.

"Say it again," Ines said, not looking up. "The part about the permits."

"You already know it better than I do."

"I want to hear it in your voice. You're the one who flies these rivers. I want to know if it sounds true to you."

Paloma turned the mug a quarter turn. "Concession permits expire every twenty years unless renewed. The big ones—the Meridian blocks, the timber, the dredging rights—all came up for renewal the same season because they were all granted the same season, forty years ago. Right after San Marisol stopped being on maps." They shrugged. "Renewal's mostly paperwork. Nobody reads the originals. Everybody knows the company pays the fees and the ministry stamps the book and life goes on."

"And when does the current term end?"

"Nineteen days. Maybe eighteen by now. There's a hearing window—if nobody contests the renewal before then, it's automatic. Rubber stamp. Another twenty years of Meridian owning half the watershed."

Ines finally looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely awake.

"That's not a deadline," she said. "That's a door."

* * *

She spent the next hour showing him why, and she did it like a pilot doing a preflight walkaround—touching each document, naming what it was, what it could bear, where its stress points were.

"This is the Treaty of Almendral." A heavy sheet, foxed at the edges, two signatures at the bottom in ink gone brown. "Signed between the Republic—which absorbed the territory after the dissolution—and the chiefs of eleven San Marisolan villages. Except look at the date." She tapped it. "Three weeks after the last census recorded those villages as depopulated. Three weeks after the company moved everyone off the land to build the dredge works. You cannot sign a treaty with people who have been removed. So either the signatories were coerced, or—" she flipped to a photostat, a clerk's hand, neat and bureaucratic, "—or the names were copied out of a census roll by someone who needed them to exist on paper and nowhere else."

"The second one," Paloma said.

"The second one. And here—" a bundle tied with rotted ribbon, "—the original grants. The concessions themselves. Every land claim Meridian holds in this watershed descends from these instruments. And every one of them recites the same chain: granted pursuant to treaties freely concluded with the lawful inhabitants. Freely concluded. Lawful inhabitants." She laughed, short and bitter, a sound with no humor in it anywhere. "My grandmother was a lawful inhabitant until the day she wasn't. Then she was a statistic in a file that got sealed. Then she was nothing."

Paloma said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the dark a night bird made its one note over and over, patient as a metronome.

"So here's the mechanism," Ines went on, and now her voice changed—flattened, professional, the voice of someone who had spent years in reading rooms learning to keep feeling on the far side of the desk. "A concession is a contract. A contract is only as good as its foundation. If the foundation—the treaty—is forged, the grant is voidable. Not automatically. Courts are slow and companies are fast. But voidable means there exists a legal instrument, filed properly, presented by a proper party, that can collapse the whole structure. And the hearing window closes in nineteen days. If these documents enter the record before then—filed, authenticated, attached to a formal objection to renewal—Meridian doesn't just lose the renewal. They lose the grants. Retroactively. Every acre, every dredge lease, every timber block. Forty years of paper empire, and it all rests on treaties that never happened."

"And you can prove they never happened."

Ines spread her hands over the table like a priest over an altar. "I can prove the census was falsified. I can prove the signatories were dead or displaced on the dates of signature. I have the company's own internal correspondence—look, here—a memo from a Meridian survey officer, 1949, instructing a subordinate to 'prepare suitable instruments reflecting agreements reached with local leadership.' Suitable instruments. He meant forgeries. He wrote it down. Companies always write it down; they just don't think anyone will ever read it again." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, bright and thin. "I've read it."

Paloma leaned back. The chair creaked, a small domestic sound in the middle of something enormous.

"There's a problem," they said.

"One problem?"

"Several. But the first one is this: none of it matters unless it gets in front of a judge before the window closes. Which means flying it to the capital. Which means—" they gestured vaguely southward, toward the smoke and the checkpoints and the blue-and-white shape of their own plane sitting on the mission's grass strip like a confession waiting to be read aloud, "—that flight."

"Yes."

"With a patrol boat already photographing us. With whatever those two men report back tonight. With a company that has nineteen days of reason to make sure this chest reaches the bottom of a river instead of a courtroom."

"Yes," Ines said again, softer.

"And you're smiling."

"I'm not smiling." She was. "I'm—do you know what I was going to do, Paloma? Before the river gave this back? I was going to spend my life writing footnotes. Footnotes about a country no one admitted existed, in journals no one read, arguing with men who'd say, interesting theory, but where's the evidence? And the evidence was sealed, or burned, or scattered, or sunk—and I'd die with a bibliography and nothing else." She pressed both palms flat against the table, against the treaties, as if checking whether the wood was real. "And now the evidence is sitting in front of me with mud still drying on it. Nineteen days. Do you understand? For the first time in forty years, the truth has a schedule."

* * *

Paloma walked outside after that, because the room had gotten too small for what was happening inside it.

The night was thick and warm and smelled of river rot and woodsmoke from the mission's cook fire, banked hours ago. The plane sat where they'd left it, tied down against a wind that hadn't come, and Paloma put a hand on the cowling the way you'd touch a horse's neck—habit, reassurance, a conversation held without words. The metal was cool. The engine underneath was tired. It wanted an overhaul they couldn't afford, which was the whole reason any of this had started, which struck Paloma suddenly as either very funny or very sad, and they decided it was both.

Here was the thing they kept circling and refusing to land on: seven years of rules, and rule number one had always been *away*. Away from checkpoints, away from registries, away from anything that smelled like official attention. Trouble was a current, and you flew upstream of it or you drifted, but you never, ever pointed your nose into it.

And now some part of their mind—the part that plotted fuel stops and weather windows, the part that was good at this—was already drawing the line south. Puerto Cobre checkpoint, avoid. Burn-off smoke over the Serranía, thread it low. Sandbars for emergency strips, marked. Nineteen days was enough time. Barely. If the engine held. If nobody sold them out. If—

If they wanted to.

That was the part that scared them. Not Meridian's enforcers, not the patrol boat, not even the chest itself. The wanting. Because wanting a destination was how you got caught. Wanting was a fixed point, and fixed points were where running pilots died.

Footsteps behind them. Ines, wrapped in a blanket against a chill she probably didn't feel, holding two mugs of the terrible chicory.

"They'll come for it," she said, handing one over. "Meridian. You know that better than I do."

"I know that."

"How bad will it be?"

Paloma drank. The chicory was awful and grounding. "Bad enough that I want you to hear this clearly, because I'm only going to say it once. Those men tonight weren't the effort. They were the receipt. Somewhere upriver there's a man whose entire job is making problems disappear, and right now we are a line item in his budget. He has boats, planes, money, and lawyers, and he has nineteen days. He does not need to kill us. He needs the chest. Killing is what happens when the cheap options run out." They looked at her. "So we don't let the cheap options run out. We stay ahead of them, we stay boring, we stay invisible, and we spend our invisibility on distance, not cleverness. Can you live like that for three weeks?"

"For forty years I've lived like something less than that."

It wasn't self-pity. It was arithmetic. Paloma found they respected it.

"There's one more thing," Ines said. "The filing. Documents alone aren't enough—I told you, they need a party. Someone has to stand in front of the court and object to the renewal. Someone has to be the name attached to all this." She hesitated, and for the first time since the sandbar she looked uncertain. "I can be that name. My family has standing—or had it, before the erasure; there are arguments to be made. But I'm one archivist with a foreign surname and no lawyer yet, and if I'm the only name on the objection, Meridian can bury me in motions until the window closes. What this needs is—" she searched for it, "—weight. More than one voice. Someone the court can't ignore."

"Someone with a clean record," Paloma said, and heard the flatness in their own voice.

Ines didn't answer. She didn't have to. The silence between them filled up with everything Paloma hadn't told her yet, the whole seven-year ledger of it, and somewhere in that silence a decision began forming that Paloma didn't examine too closely, because examining it would mean admitting it was already made.

"We fly south tomorrow," they said instead. "First light. Before those men's report travels further than their boat."

"South," Ines repeated, tasting the word.

"Toward trouble." Paloma drained the mug and set it on the cowling, where it rocked once and settled. "Get some sleep. You'll want your eyes sharp. There's weather building over the Serranía, and we're going to be threading it with the most important cargo this river's carried in forty years."

They stood a while longer after she went in, hand on the plane, listening to the river move in the dark. It sounded different tonight. Or maybe that was just them.

Nineteen days. For seven years the calendar had been an enemy—renewals, registrations, expirations, every date a trapdoor. Now, for the first time, a date was a door, and Paloma Montrose found themselves standing on the wrong side of it, key in hand, actually considering walking through.

Somewhere downstream, a radio crackled. Faint, almost lost under the water-sound—but a voice in it, clipped and professional, saying words Paloma couldn't make out and didn't need to.

The clock had started for both of them.
