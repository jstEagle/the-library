## 9. Old Friends, New Prices

Toma's trading post sat where the Cuará bent east and slowed, a stilted sprawl of tin roofs and drying nets that smelled of diesel, fish, and the particular sweetness of overripe plantains going soft in the sun. Paloma had been coming here for six years—fuel drums, spare parts, a night on the covered dock when the weather turned mean—and every visit had followed the same choreography. Land downriver, taxi to the mooring, climb the ladder, wait for Toma to finish whatever he was pretending was more important than you.

Paloma did all of it now, and waited.

The morning was thick with heat already, the kind that made the air feel like something you had to push through. On the water below, a boy hauled in a net by hand, coiling it with the automatic grace of someone who'd done ten thousand times. Somewhere behind the main house, a generator coughed and caught. Normal sounds. Ordinary morning.

Except that somewhere behind them, a floatplane full of Meridian men was regrouping after failing to kill them at dawn, and Ines Vidal was sitting in the shade of the post's lean-to with the chest between her feet like a woman guarding a sleeping animal that might wake up angry.

"You're early," Toma said, appearing at the top of the ladder with a mug of coffee he did not offer to share. He was broader than Paloma remembered, or maybe Paloma had gotten thinner. His beard had gone from black to salt without bothering with gray in between. "You're never early."

"Fuel."

"That's not why you're here."

"Fuel," Paloma said again, "and a favor."

Toma looked past them, down the ladder, at the blue-and-white bush plane riding low on its mooring lines, and then further, at Ines, who was watching him back with the careful blankness of someone who had learned to make herself unmemorable. Then he looked at the chest.

He looked at the chest for a long time.

"Toma." Paloma kept their voice level. "Inside."

* * *

The trading post's back room was Toma's sanctuary and his ledger room both: a desk scarred with twenty years of knife marks, a wall of clipboards holding debts owed to him from here to the capital, and a single window screened against mosquitoes but not against heat, so the room cooked slowly through every afternoon like something being proofed. Toma shut the door, sat, and didn't offer Paloma a chair.

"Six years," he said. "Six years I sell you fuel, no questions. You pay cash, you pay fair, you never bring trouble up my ladder. That was the arrangement."

"It still is."

"No." Toma set his mug down with a small, deliberate click. "Because trouble is sitting on my dock with a San Marisol seal on it, and there are men paying money in three river towns for word of a blue-and-white plane."

The words landed the way Paloma had known they would, the way they'd been landing since the mission station: not as surprise but as confirmation, the sick settling weight of a thing finally said aloud. Paloma had spent two days hoping the patrol boat's photograph hadn't traveled. Hope was cheap. Information moved faster than planes out here; it moved on the current itself.

"How much?" Paloma asked.

"Enough," Toma said. "Enough that half the river would sell their own mother's location for it, and the other half would sell yours." He leaned forward. "I'm going to ask you one question, and you don't have to answer it. But I want you to hear yourself answer it, either way."

He tapped the desk once.

"What did you pull out of that river?"

Paloma held his gaze. Six years of arrangement said: nothing, a salvage curiosity, papers belonging to a passenger. Six years of arrangement said: no names, no stops longer than an hour, no questions answered.

But Toma had fueled the medicine runs. Toma had carried insulin through a flood when the government boat wouldn't go. Toma had never once asked what Paloma was running from, which meant he had always known there was something, and had decided the debt ran the other way.

So Paloma told him. Not everything—the logbook, the charge, the seven years stayed locked where they lived—but the shape of it. A chest out of a collapsed bank. The seal. An archivist named Ines Vidal who believed the documents proved the concession company's entire land empire rested on treaties that were never real. Men with guns who had already shot at them once and would shoot again, because nineteen days from now, permits expired, and if those papers reached the capital before then, Meridian Concessions stopped existing as anything but a bad memory.

Toma listened without moving. When Paloma finished, he was quiet long enough that the generator outside cycled twice.

"The company," he said finally, "buys its fuel from me too. Did you know that?"

"I know."

"Their barges stop at my dock four times a month. Their men eat at my tables. And now their money is walking around the river asking about your plane." He spread his hands. "You see my problem."

"I see it."

"So here is mine." Toma stood and went to the window, looking out at the water where the boy was still hauling his net, patient as tide. "I owe you. Not money—you always paid. I owe you the other thing, the thing that can't be priced, and I've owed it since the flood year, and I don't like owing. It sits in a man like river water in a boot." He turned around. "So I'll say yes. Fuel, drums, a place to keep the chest tonight, my cousin's barge south as far as Santarem bend if it comes to that. Yes."

Relief hit Paloma physically, a loosening across the shoulders they hadn't known they were carrying. They let exactly none of it show.

"But," Toma said.

There was always a but. Out here, *but* was the whole economy.

"But you should understand what you're asking me to spend." Toma came back to the desk and lowered his voice, though the door was shut and the nearest ear was forty meters away. "If Meridian finds out I helped you—if one of my own boys sells the information, and someone will, because someone always does—then I lose the company trade, which is half my business. Maybe I lose more than that. Their enforcers don't send letters." He paused. "And I want you to notice what I just told you. I told you their price. Which means somebody else already knows theirs."

Paloma went cold in a way the heat couldn't touch. "Who?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Toma sat back down. "Two nights ago, before you ever landed here, a man came through on the northbound packet boat. Asked careful questions—not about you, about a chest. Described the seal. Said he represented parties interested in historical salvage." He shrugged elaborately. "I told him nothing, because I knew nothing. But he'd already been to Puerto Lobo and the fish camp at Dos Ríos before he got to me. Somebody talked, Paloma. Before you ever flew over this stretch of river, somebody described that chest well enough that strangers are hunting it by description."

Ines. The thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome—the confession on the sandbar, the brother in Meridian's legal office, the one person she'd tipped off. Mateo Vidal had found them via radio chatter once already. Maybe the trail started there. Maybe it started somewhere worse, some settler on the first day, some eyes at the collapsed bank Paloma had never even seen. It didn't matter who. What mattered was the shape of it: the secret had been leaking since the moment the river gave the chest back, and every hour it leaked wider.

"Thank you for telling me," Paloma said.

"Don't thank me yet." Toma pulled a clipboard off the wall—a habit, pricing things even when the price was a friend's life—and wrote nothing on it, just held the pen. "Here's how it goes. You take fifty drums' worth, on credit until this is finished, because if you win, you'll be able to pay, and if you lose, we'll both be poor anyway. The chest sleeps in my strongroom tonight. Your plane goes in my boathouse, under canvas, so it stops being visible. At first light I put word out that you flew north."

"And did we?"

"No." Toma's teeth showed briefly under the salt-and-pepper beard. "We fly south, low, on the far channel, after dark. There's a moonless window around eleven. My nephew guides you past the Dos Ríos checkpoint with a lamp signal—green twice, pause, green once. If you see red, you turn around and pray."

"And the barge?"

"Backup, only if the plane won't fly or won't hide. My cousin leaves Thursday. Cargo deck, under tarps, among the dried fish nobody wants to inspect too closely." He finally set the pen down. "That's everything I have. After tomorrow, I never saw you. If anyone asks, you came for fuel, argued about prices, and flew north, and I was glad to see the last of you."

It was more than Paloma had hoped for and less than they needed, which was the exact size of every gift on this river.

"One more thing." Toma opened the desk drawer and took out something wrapped in oilcloth, sliding it across. Inside lay a pistol, old but oiled, and a box of cartridges gone slightly green at the seams. "Yours, I think. From the medicine run two winters ago. You left it saying you'd never need it again."

Paloma looked at it and did not touch it. "I remember."

"You'll want it tomorrow."

"I didn't need it then."

"You weren't carrying a country in a box then." Toma pushed it an inch closer. "Take it. Consider it part of the debt. I'd rather you owe me a gun than owe your ghost an apology."

Paloma took it. The weight of it in their hand was familiar and ugly, a small dense argument about the kind of person they were willing to become before this ended. They wrapped it back in the oilcloth and put it inside their jacket, against the ribs, where it sat like a second heartbeat with worse intentions.

Outside, the heat had built into a shimmer. Ines was where they'd left her, but standing now, talking quietly with the boy who'd been hauling nets—no, not talking, listening, while the boy gestured at the river and she nodded. When she saw Paloma come down the steps, she excused herself and crossed to them fast.

"The boy," she said, low. "He asked me if we were the ones from the reward."

Paloma's stomach dropped through the dock boards. "What did you tell him?"

"That we were surveyors, and that reward descriptions get everyone excited, and that his uncle would be angry if he wasted net-hauling time on gossip." She glanced back at the house. "I think it held. But Paloma—the reward. Everyone down this whole reach knows about it. Every child on every dock. How long before someone decides a few hundred is worth more than being polite?"

"That's what I came to find out." Paloma looked at her—at the archivist who'd hidden treaty pages in her clothing, who'd bluffed a lock inspection with bureaucratic fluency, who'd betrayed them once in a small way and been paying for it since—and made the decision the way you make all decisions out here: quickly, and then live inside it. "We fly tonight. South, dark, far channel. Toma's arranging it. Until then, we sit in the boathouse with the plane, out of sight, and you tell me everything your brother might have told Meridian about our route. Everything. Assume he assumed we'd come here."

Ines went pale but didn't argue, and Paloma noted that too—how quickly she'd stopped arguing, somewhere between the sandbar and the bullets, how the two of them had worn each other into something like trust without ever signing for it.

They carried the chest up the dock together, past the boy with the net, who watched them go with wide eyes and the terrible innocence of someone counting money in his head. Paloma met his gaze and smiled, and the boy smiled back, and Paloma thought: *there it is. That's the price. Not the fuel, not the risk—that smile costing more than it looks.*

That night, in the boathouse under canvas, with the river slapping the pilings and the chest lashed beneath the cargo net, Ines laid out a hand-drawn map of everything Mateo could know, and Paloma memorized it while cleaning a gun they'd sworn never to need again.

At eleven, the sky went moonless and black.

At eleven-ten, Toma's nephew stepped into a skiff with a shuttered lamp.

And somewhere downriver, at a checkpoint neither of them could see, someone was already deciding whether the reward was worth more than the silence—because out here, loyalty wasn't a virtue.

It was a line item.
