## 10. Detour Through the Smoke

The smoke showed up first as a smudge on the horizon, the way weather always did out here—patient, indifferent to your plans. By the time Paloma had the river's last big bend behind them and the southern channel opening out below, the smudge had become a wall, a brown-gray curtain hanging from about four thousand feet down to the treetops, and beyond it, somewhere in that murk, the Meridian checkpoint strip at Puerto Cobre.

Paloma had been avoiding Puerto Cobre for seven years without ever having a specific reason to. Now they had one.

"Radio said burn-off started three days ago," Ines said from the right seat. She'd learned to hold the chest between her boots instead of on her lap; it made her look less like a courier and more like a woman with heavy feet. "Farmers clearing for dry season."

"Farmers don't clear forty kilometers of it in three days." Paloma eased the throttle forward a centimeter and watched the manifold pressure like it might lie to them. It wouldn't. The engine was the only honest thing left in their life. "Meridian burns what they want burned when they want it burned. That smoke is a fence."

"A fence made of weather."

"The best kind. Nobody shoots at weather." Paloma tapped the chart spread across the panel, the pencil line they'd drawn at dawn—a dogleg east, then south through the gap where two tributaries met, threading between the burn and the checkpoint strip, coming at the capital corridor from an angle no flight plan would predict because no flight plan existed. They hadn't filed one. Filing meant a trail, and trails were how professionals found you.

They were professionals now. Paloma knew the shape of the hunt from the inside: the quiet phone calls, the fuel manifests pulled and cross-checked, the man at the airstrip café who remembered your tail number and got paid fifty dollars for remembering it harder. Somewhere behind them, probably already airborne or soon to be, was a floatplane with men who did this work the way Paloma used to do it—competently, without appetite, without hate. That was the part people got wrong about enforcers. They didn't need to hate you. Hating took energy you needed for the checklist.

"How long through the smoke?" Ines asked.

"Eleven minutes if the engine cooperates." Paloma looked at her. "If it doesn't, we turn around and land at Toma's and think again."

"And if we can't turn around?"

"Then I'll be too busy to answer questions."

Ines nodded and said nothing else, which Paloma had come to appreciate about her. Seven days ago she'd been a passenger with two suitcases and too much money. Now she was a woman who held a national archive between her boots and asked exactly one question at a time, and waited for the answer before spending the next one. Paloma had flown missionaries, geologists, diamond buyers, a fleeing bishop, a honeymooning couple who should never have married. Ines Vidal was the first passenger who understood that the plane was a small country with two citizens and strict laws.

They crossed the tributary at six hundred feet. Below, the water ran the color of milk tea, swollen still with the flood's retreat, carrying whole islands of torn reeds downstream. And ahead—the smoke, close enough now that Paloma could smell it seeping through the vents, sweet and acrid, burnt cane and burnt leaf litter and something underneath like charred wood, like a house fire scaled up until houses stopped mattering.

"Oxygen's marginal in there," Paloma said. "Windows stay shut. If I go quiet for more than ten seconds, say my name."

"What happens after ten seconds?"

"You'll find out why you're saying it."

Ines almost smiled. Almost. Her hand moved once toward the chest and came back to her knee.

Paloma turned them into the smoke.

* * *

It was like flying inside a lung.

The world went amber, then brown, then a color Paloma had no name for—visibility collapsing from a kilometer to two hundred meters to less than the length of the runway they'd learned on, decades ago, in another life. Ash ticked against the windscreen like slow rain. The horizon vanished. The river vanished. There was only the compass card swinging gently and the sound of the engine, which Paloma listened to the way other people listen for a child crying in the next room.

Two minutes. Three. Sweat ran down Paloma's spine despite the cool air. Flying instruments in clear sky was arithmetic. Flying instruments in smoke with ash clogging the intake filters was faith, and Paloma had never been good at faith.

At minute five the engine coughed.

Not dramatically. Just once—a hiccup, a missed beat, the manifold needle flicking down and back up like a pulse skipping. Paloma's hands were already moving before the conscious mind caught up: mixture rich, carb heat on, fuel selector checked, gauges scanned. The engine smoothed out. Then coughed again, twice, and caught with a sound like a man clearing his throat before deciding whether to speak.

"That's not normal," Ines said.

"No."

"What is it?"

"Either ash in the induction system or water in the fuel from Toma's drums, and I won't know which until—" The engine stumbled a third time, longer, the propeller's note sagging, and Paloma felt the aircraft settle a hundred feet like a tired animal lying down. "Until it tells me."

Ahead, through a rift in the smoke, the river appeared—wide here, braided, strewn with sandbars the flood had built and not yet reclaimed. One of them, maybe eight hundred meters long, sat clean and pale in a pocket of clearer air, and Paloma made the decision the way they made all decisions now: fast, alone, without asking permission of anyone including themselves.

"We're landing."

"On water?"

"On sand. Better than water. Water forgives nothing." Paloma dropped the nose, traded altitude for airspeed, ran the pre-landing checks by memory because the smoke kept eating the light. "Straps tight. Hold the chest against your ribs, not your knees—if we dig in, it'll try to keep going without us."

The engine coughed a fourth time and then, insultingly, ran smooth all the way down, as if it had only been testing them. Paloma flared over the sandbar's upstream tip, touched with a chirp of tires on wet-packed sand, and rolled out through a foot of standing water that threw twin rooster tails and soaked everything the wheels couldn't shield. Then they were stopped, ticking, in a gray-gold evening world with the smoke wall hanging offshore like a storm that had forgotten how to storm.

Paloma sat with both hands on the yoke and breathed.

"Well," Ines said.

"Don't." Paloma held up a finger. "Don't say anything encouraging yet. Encouragement jinxes engines. That's not superstition, it's field data."

* * *

Dusk came down fast, the way it did near the equator, dusk being less a season than a door closing. Paloma drained the fuel strainer into a glass jar and held it up to the last light: water in the bottom, a cloudy lens of it, maybe a centimeter. Toma's drums. Or condensation from the cold nights and hot days. Either way, fixable—sump the tanks, drain the lines, wait for morning light to see if anything worse was hiding in there. Not fixable tonight. Night flying onto an unmarked sandbar with a sick engine and a cargo worth killing for was not a plan, it was an obituary with extra steps.

So they camped.

Paloma had done this a thousand times—tarp strung from wing strut to a driftwood stump, thermos of coffee, canned sardines and hard biscuits, mosquito coil burning its slow incense under the wing. What they'd never done was do it with someone else who knew how to be quiet. Ines built a small fire below the high-water line, using driftwood that burned almost without smoke, and sat beside it with the chest open on her knees, going through documents by firelight with the concentration of a woman reading letters from the dead. Which, Paloma thought, she was.

The river talked to itself all night, the way big rivers did—slurping at the sand, shifting loads of silt, muttering about everything it carried and would never give back. Except this year it had given something back. Paloma looked at the chest. Forty years underwater, or buried, or hidden—nobody knew—and the seal on the lid still legible, the eagle-and-star of San Marisol, a country that had existed the way a person exists: officially, briefly, and then not at all.

"Ines."

She looked up.

"When we get to the capital. What happens to me?"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean I'm the pilot of record. My name goes on whatever petition you file, whatever court takes this up. The documents prove Meridian forged treaties. They also prove somebody flew them out of the interior, and that somebody has a face and a tail number and—" Paloma turned the coffee cup in their hands. "I've spent seven years making sure my name appears nowhere. You're about to write it down in triplicate."

Ines closed the folder slowly. For a while she just watched the fire.

"There's a way to do it that limits exposure," she said. "Intermediaries, third-party filings, a journalist holding copies so the original source matters less than—"

"Ines."

"Yes. All right." She set the papers down. "You'll be visible. There's no version of this where you aren't. I've known that since the mission station, and I let you fly anyway, and I'm not proud of how little I warned you." She said it flatly, without performance, and somehow that was worse than an apology. "But I'll tell you something true, since we're apparently doing that now. Nobody survives being invisible forever. My grandmother tried. Whole country tried. Erasure feels safe right up until you realize it means nobody can find you to help you either."

Paloma drank the coffee. It had gone bitter from sitting too long on the fire.

"That's not the speech I'd have picked," they said.

"It's the one I had."

* * *

Later, with the fire banked and the mosquitoes reduced to a background complaint, Ines spoke again from across the embers, and her voice had changed—smaller, careful, the voice of someone stepping onto ice they'd been avoiding for days.

"There's something you should know. Before it finds us instead."

Paloma didn't move. "Go on."

"The night after we opened the chest. At the mission." Ines drew a line in the sand with one finger, some geometry only she could see. "There was radio at the mission. Old, but working. And I sent a message. One message. To one person."

The river filled the silence, patient, enormous.

"To whom," Paloma said. Not a question. A door being held open.

"My brother." Ines lifted her chin, meeting it head-on, refusing the mercy of looking away. "Mateo Vidal. He works in Meridian's legal office in the capital. Contracts division. He's not—they don't send him out with guns, he sits at a desk and makes theft look like paperwork, that's the family talent, apparently, we all got some version of it." A breath. "He's the only person alive who knows our grandmother's name besides me. The real name. The San Marisol one. When I saw that seal on the chest, I—I didn't think. I sent word that I'd found proof. That it was real. That it could bring the names back."

Paloma set down the cup. Very carefully, the way you put down something when the alternative is throwing it.

"He works for them."

"He works for them."

"So the company knows."

"He wouldn't tell them." Ines leaned forward into the ember-light. "Paloma. Listen to me. Mateo kept grandmother's name alive for thirty years inside that building. He files the concession contracts with a straight face and then he goes home and lights a candle for a country that isn't on any map. He is the reason I became an archivist. He is—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, Paloma could see her hating it, and pressed on anyway. "He is the one person I have ever trusted completely, and yes, he works for Meridian, and yes, I told him, and I have been waiting four days to tell you because every time I started I heard how it sounded."

"How does it sound?"

"Like betrayal." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears more than ashamed of them. "And it isn't. That's the thing I need you to understand. It isn't betrayal. It's me being a person instead of a courier. I couldn't carry this alone and I chose the one person I'd trust with my own grave, and he happens to collect a salary from the enemy, and I know, I *know* how that sounds—"

"Ines."

"—and if you want to put me off this sandbar tomorrow and finish it alone, I'll walk, I'll take the chest, I'll—"

"*Ines.*"

She stopped.

Paloma looked at her across the dead fire for a long moment. Thought about Toma, who owed them and would sell them for the right number. Thought about the nuns at the mission clinic, who'd remember kindness but whose neighbors remembered money. Thought about seven years of trusting no one and how much territory that had actually protected—not the life, just the loneliness.

"Does he know where we are?" Paloma asked quietly.

"No. I gave him a route and a window, not coordinates. He'd have to work to find us, and if he works, it's because he's coming to help." She hesitated. "Or because they made him."

"There it is."

"There it is," she agreed, miserably.

Paloma fed a stick to the coals and watched it catch. The smoke wall offshore had gone invisible with full dark; somewhere beyond it, Puerto Cobre's lights would be burning, and men with clipboards and men with guns would be comparing notes about a blue-and-white bush plane seen heading south. Somewhere behind them, a floatplane. Somewhere ahead, a brother with a desk job and a candle.

"Here's what I know about people who work inside places like Meridian," Paloma said. "I worked inside places like Meridian. Before. The ones who survive are the ones who learn to hold two truths—one for the office, one for the mirror. Most of them eventually drop the mirror truth because it's heavy and nobody pays for it." They looked up. "Your brother kept a dead country's name lit for thirty years on a thief's salary. That's not a man who dropped the mirror."

Ines stared at them. "Is that forgiveness?"

"It's a risk assessment." Paloma lay back against the tire, arms behind their head, and looked up at the one patch of stars the smoke allowed. "We'll know by morning. If he told them, they'll come at dawn—that's how enforcement likes to operate, first light, when everyone's slow. If he didn't, we drain the tanks, we fly, and someday I buy him a drink and ask him what our grandmother's name was."

"And if they come at dawn?"

Paloma was quiet long enough that Ines thought they'd fallen asleep.

"Then," they said finally, "we'll wish we'd slept. Get some rest. First light comes early, and I intend to be airborne before it, whichever way the truth lands."

Downriver, faint but unmistakable, an engine droned—high, steady, wrong for this hour, wrong for this river.

Neither of them slept.
