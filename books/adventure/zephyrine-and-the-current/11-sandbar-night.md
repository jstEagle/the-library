## 11. Sandbar Night

The sandbar held them the way a hand holds a moth—gently, and without any intention of letting go.

Paloma had put the Cessna down at last light with maybe four minutes of fuel margin and no better options. The river had split around this spit of gray sand for as long as anyone could remember; it showed up on no chart Paloma trusted, which was exactly why they'd chosen it. Now the plane sat nose-high on the shingle, tied to a dead tree trunk that had washed down in some earlier flood, and the water slid past on both sides, brown and patient, carrying the smell of drowned forest.

The engine had coughed twice over the smoke. Not died—coughed, like an old man clearing his throat before deciding whether to keep talking. Paloma had heard that sound enough times to know what it meant: something loose in the fuel system, or water in the lines, or worse. None of it fixable by headlamp in the dark without parts. So they'd landed, and now the dark was total except for the small blue ring of the camp stove and the enormous noise of the current.

Ines sat on an overturned crate with her arms wrapped around her knees. She hadn't spoken since the landing—not from fear, Paloma thought, but from the particular silence of someone recalculating everything.

"Engine will run tomorrow," Paloma said. "Probably."

"You said 'probably' like a man telling me the bridge might hold."

"I'm not a man."

"No," Ines said. "You're not." A pause. "The bridge might hold?"

"The bridge might hold."

She almost smiled. It didn't quite make it. She looked out at the water instead, where the last of the light was draining off the far bank, and Paloma saw her shoulders do the thing shoulders do when a person decides to stop pretending the night is going anywhere else.

* * *

They ate rice and tinned sardines and drank coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in. The chest sat between them like a third passenger, padlocked, patient. Paloma had covered it with a tarp against the dew, which was absurd—it had spent forty years under a river—but the gesture felt necessary anyway. You didn't leave things like that open to the sky.

"My grandmother," Ines said, without preamble, "was born in a town called Puerto Almendro. Population, in 1959: eleven hundred and forty. There are census records. Parish registers. A photograph of the market day, everyone squinting into the sun, my grandmother fourteen years old in the front row holding a basket of what I think is guava."

Paloma said nothing. On the river you learned when silence was the polite response.

"In 1963, San Marisol stopped existing. Not destroyed—*erased*. The treaties were renegotiated, the maps redrawn, the name struck from every registry in the capital. My grandmother's birth certificate became a forgery by definition. Her marriage license. Her death certificate, forty years later—they couldn't even record her dying properly, because officially she had never lived." Ines turned her cup slowly in her hands. "My mother grew up stateless in her own country. Do you understand what that does to a family? Every document is a wound. Every form you fill out has a line where your history should go, and the line is blank, and some clerk looks at you like *you're* the error."

"And the chest," Paloma said.

"Is the line filled in." Ines's voice went quiet and fierce at once. "If those treaties say what I think they say—if the concessions were built on paper Meridian forged after the fact—then the erasure wasn't just cruelty. It was a crime with a paperwork trail. And paperwork is the one thing I know how to fight with."

The stove hissed. Somewhere across the channel, a bird made a sound like a hinge.

"Why you?" Paloma asked. "An archivist, alone, hiring a bush pilot she found through—" They'd never asked how she'd found them. That had been deliberate. No questions answered; rule two.

"A retired customs officer who owed my mother a favor. He remembered a pilot who flew medicine runs and never asked about cargo." Ines looked at them directly for the first time since the engine coughed. "He said you were expensive, unreliable, and completely trustworthy. In that order."

"He got the order wrong. Trustworthy first."

"That's what he said too. He just liked the joke of it."

Paloma laughed—a short, surprised sound, the kind that escapes rather than gets released. Then the laugh faded, because Ines was still looking at them, and there was a question in the look that had been there since the mission station, since the village name in the census, since Paloma had gone still as a snagged fish reading that list of disappeared places.

*Villa Serena*, the document had said. Dispersed 1961. And Paloma had known the place—not the 1961 version, but the modern one, the failed cooperative settlement on the upper tributary where seven years ago a charter flight had ended badly in the rain.

"You recognized something," Ines said. "In the documents. I watched you."

Paloma fed a stick into the stove and watched it catch. The firelight did its work on their face, half shadow, half confession, and Paloma thought about the arithmetic of the situation: stranded, hunted, photographed, committed. The cost of lying had gone up. The cost of truth had come down. That was the strange gift of a sandbar—you couldn't fly away from a conversation.

"There's a charge," Paloma said.

Ines waited. She was good at waiting. Archivists probably were.

"Manslaughter," Paloma said, and the word came out of their mouth like a stone they'd been carrying in their cheek for seven years, finally spat into the fire. "One count. Involuntary. That's what the lawyers called it—involuntary, like the flying had happened *to* me."

* * *

The story came out in pieces, the way it had been stored.

Seven years ago, Paloma had been twenty-six and broke in the specific way pilots get broke—engine fund eaten by a debt, debt created by a season of bad contracts and one very good liar in the freight office. A charter offer came through: Villa Serena to the capital, six passengers, medical referral, cash up front. Rain forecast. Paloma took it anyway. Counted heads at the dock—six—and did the weight math fast and dirty, the way tired people do, rounding down.

"We were overweight. Not catastrophically. Enough." Paloma's hands moved while they talked, unconsciously flying the old approach: flare, sink, the sickening float, the stall warning singing its one note. "I knew it on takeoff. The climb rate told me. I should have set it back down on the river and waited out the front. Instead I climbed through the rain because the money was already in my pocket and shame is a terrible co-pilot."

"What happened?" Ines asked softly.

"Nothing dramatic. That's the part nobody believes. No fire, no spin. I just ran out of sky over the treeline and put it down in a clearcut, and the airplane came apart the way airplanes do, and one of the six—a woman named Rosa Ampuero, sixty-one years old, going to the capital for a heart procedure—didn't survive it. Everyone else walked away. I walked away. She didn't." Paloma stared into the stove. "The investigation would have found the overweight takeoff. Probably would have called it pilot error, maybe criminal negligence. I'll never know, because I falsified the logbook that same night—fuel load, passenger count, weather briefing—and I was on a southbound barge before the civil aviation board opened its file."

Ines didn't flinch or lean back. She leaned *in*, slightly, the way people do toward a fire.

"Rosa Ampuero," she repeated. "That name was in the census annex. The disappeared villages list. Villa Serena."

"Her family was from there. Original families. She told me about it on the flight—she talked the whole way, she was nervous, she talked about the town her parents lost, the orchards, the church bell they buried so the company couldn't melt it down." Paloma heard their own voice go flat and strange. "I've thought about that bell more times than I can count. She carried that town with her onto my airplane, and I killed her with arithmetic."

"You didn't kill her with arithmetic. You made a bad decision in bad weather, and it killed her, and you've been paying for it ever since—just not in the currency the law wanted."

"That's a generous ledger."

"It's an accurate one. I'm an archivist. Accuracy is the whole job." Ines set down her cup. "Why are you telling me this? You've kept it seven years. You could have kept it seven more. I'd have never known."

Paloma considered the question honestly, which was itself new. "Because of the chest," they said finally. "Because I watched you open it at the mission and I saw what it did to you—the way your hands shook, and then stopped shaking, like you'd decided something. And I realized I've been doing the opposite of that for seven years. Deciding nothing. Keeping the logbook lie sealed like evidence in a case I'm afraid to open." They looked up. "You're about to walk into a courtroom against the biggest company on the river with a box of paper. The least I can do is stop lying in a tent."

The word *tent* was generous. It was a tarp strung between the wing strut and the dead tree, and the rain started on it ten minutes later, drumming soft and steady, and neither of them slept much anyway.

* * *

Around midnight the rain eased to a whisper and the clouds tore open briefly, and the moon came through and turned the sandbar silver and the river into a road of hammered tin. Ines stood at the edge of the shingle, and Paloma came to stand beside her, and for a while they just watched the current move.

"My grandmother used to say the river remembers," Ines said. "Everyone thought it was superstition. Old woman talk. But she was right, wasn't she? It held that chest for forty years and gave it back the week we needed it. It dropped two meters overnight and showed us the mud." She glanced sideways. "It put an engine problem in your fuel line and stranded us here long enough for you to tell me about Rosa Ampuero. Maybe the river is an archivist too."

"Maybe the river is just a river."

"Maybe. But I've learned not to argue with things that keep better records than governments."

Paloma huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then, quieter: "What happens to me? After. If the documents work, if the permits collapse, if all of it goes the way you want—there's still a warrant with my name on it. There has been for seven years. Helping you doesn't erase it."

"No," Ines said. "But it changes what it means. A fugitive who runs is a fugitive forever. A fugitive who delivers a nation's stolen history to a judge—that's a different animal. Courts forgive a lot of things, but what they forgive best is usefulness." She paused. "I'm not saying it's fair. I'm saying it's real. And I'd testify for you. Whatever you decide."

Nobody had offered Paloma testimony in seven years. Nobody had even known there was anything to testify about. The offer sat in their chest, heavy and warm, like a stone left near a fire.

"Get some sleep," Paloma said. "We lift at first light if the engine agrees."

Ines went to the tarp. Paloma stayed at the waterline a while longer, listening to the current, thinking about Rosa Ampuero's bell buried in the dirt of a town that wasn't on any map, waiting forty years for someone to dig.

Then they heard it—faint, from the north, threading under the river-noise: an engine. Not theirs. Deeper-throated, rhythmic, mechanical. A boat motor working upstream against the current, in the dark, at one in the morning, on a stretch of river where nothing legitimate moved at night.

Paloma killed the stove flame and stood very still in the moonlight, counting seconds between the sound's pulses, judging distance and speed the way they'd once judged weather. Five minutes, maybe less. Coming straight up the channel.

Behind them, Ines stirred. "Paloma?"

"Don't make a light," Paloma whispered. "Someone's coming."
