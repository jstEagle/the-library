## 12. The Brother's Offer

Dawn on the sandbar came the color of old bone.

Paloma had been awake for an hour, watching the river rebuild itself out of the dark, when the radio crackled. Not static—speech. A voice calling a channel that bush pilots used and nobody else bothered with, the one frequency everyone in the interior knew and everyone pretended they didn't.

*"Blue-and-white Cessna, sandbar at kilometer two-forty. Blue-and-white Cessna, this is Mateo Vidal. I know you're listening, Inés. I know because I taught you that channel when you were nine."*

Paloma's hand was on the radio before the name finished. Ines was already sitting up in the tent, hair pressed flat on one side, eyes fully awake. She'd been awake too, then. Neither of them had really slept.

"Don't answer," Paloma said.

"He'll keep talking."

"Then let him talk. Every word he says tells us something. Every word you say tells him everything."

Ines looked at the radio the way you look at a snake you once kept as a pet. Then she reached past Paloma's hand and pressed transmit.

"Mateo."

Just the name. Nothing else. Paloma closed their eyes briefly. So much for letting him talk.

*"You're on a sandbar forty kilometers south of the checkpoint line with a dead-mag engine and a chest that weighs more than your plane's useful load,"* Mateo said. His voice was smooth, unhurried, the voice of a man reading from notes he'd written himself. *"You have fuel for maybe ninety minutes. The nearest strip with a working pump is Meridian's. I'm telling you this not because I'm a monster but because I'm your brother and I'd rather you hear the arithmetic from me than from the people who'll be doing it at dawn."*

"What people?" Paloma asked, before they could stop themselves.

A pause. Then, warmer, more careful: *"You must be the pilot. Montrose. My sister has expensive taste in felons."*

"Fugitive," Paloma said. "Get it right."

*"I intend to. That's rather the point."* Papers shuffled somewhere far away, in an office with air conditioning, Paloma guessed, a room where the river was an abstraction on a lease map. *"Here's the situation, plainly. Meridian Concessions is prepared to make this problem go away. All of it. The chest comes back to us—unopened, as far as anyone will ever know—and in exchange: full immunity for the pilot. The manslaughter charge, the falsified logbook, seven years of flight from it. Gone. Not pardoned. Erased. There's a difference, and I suspect you of all people appreciate the difference."*

Paloma's mouth had gone dry. They were aware of Ines watching them, and they hated being watched, and they hated more that the number in their chest—the one that had done the arithmetic of running for seven years—had just leaned forward and paid attention.

"And for me?" Ines said.

*"For you, Inés, the company will restore the family name."* A breath. *"The Vidal name goes back into the registry. Your grandmother's land title, recognized. Compensation for the erasure—forty years of it, calculated at current valuation. A public statement. An apology, if you want it, though I'd take the compensation. Apologies are cheaper than land and companies know it."*

The tent was very quiet. Down the sandbar, the river slid past with its low constant voice, indifferent as a witness who'd heard everything before.

"On the company's terms," Ines said.

*"On terms that exist,"* Mateo said, and for the first time something came through the radio that wasn't smooth—something raw underneath, like a bone showing through a sleeve. *"Inés. Listen to me. I fought for this. I sat in a conference room for six hours with men who call our grandmother 'the claimant' and I made them write down a number. I made them say the word Vidal out loud in a room where that word has been a liability for forty years. It isn't everything. I know it isn't everything. But it's the first time anyone has offered you anything at all instead of nothing, and you're going to refuse it from a sandbar, over a radio, holding a box of paper that will get you both killed."*

Ines's hand was trembling. Paloma saw it, saw her see it, saw her close her fist until it stopped.

"You read the documents," she said. "You told me on the phone you read them."

*"I read summaries."*

"You read them. The treaties. The census." Her voice was steady now, and that was worse than trembling. "Two hundred and eleven villages, Mateo. Our grandmother's village is number one-eighty-seven. They signed papers at gunpoint and then they erased the guns and the paper and the people, and Meridian has been farming the erasure ever since. And your offer is that they'll give us back our *name* if we hand them the proof and let them keep the *land*."

*"I know what the documents say."*

"Then you know what they mean."

*"I know what they cost!"* The radio hissed with the force of it. Somewhere on the other end, a door closed—a man stepping out of a room to shout at his sister in private. When he came back, his voice was low again, controlled, and somehow that was the saddest thing yet. *"I know what they cost. I've been paying Meridian's price my whole career, Inés. I know exactly what things cost. That's my job. That's all I am. And what I'm telling you, as the only Vidal who ever got inside the room, is that the price of holding that chest past dawn is one you can't pay. The enforcement team flies out of Puerto Cangrejo at first light. Two men I used to play cards with. They don't negotiate. They're not coming to talk."*

"Then talk fast," Paloma said. "You've got until we're done fueling."

There was no fueling. There was a fuel drum, half-full, and a hand pump, and a plane that needed an engine overhaul being asked to fly on faith and fumes. But Mateo didn't know the exact state of the aircraft, and Paloma let him believe the sandbar was a refueling stop rather than a stranding. In the interior, information was the only currency that never inflated.

"Here's what I can do," Mateo said. *"I can delay the team. Not stop it—delay it. Six hours, maybe eight, if I manufacture a weather concern at Puerto Cangrejo. I can have a fuel barge at the confluence by noon with no questions asked. I can put you both on a boat to the coast and the chest goes into the river and nobody ever speaks of San Marisol again, and you live. Both of you. Old. Bored. Alive."*

"And if we say no?"

Silence. Long enough that Paloma checked the radio was still transmitting.

*"Then I did my best,"* Mateo said quietly. *"And at dawn, so will they."*

* * *

Ines set the handset down and stood at the mouth of the tent, looking at the river as if it might answer for her.

Paloma gave her a minute. Then: "Your brother's good."

"He's the best in his office. He told me that himself, when we were young. I thought he was boasting." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "He was warning me."

"You believe him? About dawn?"

"I believe he believes it. And Mateo's beliefs are always accurate about terrible things. It's the hopeful things he gets wrong." She turned. Her face in the gray light looked older than it had three weeks ago, when she'd stepped off the dock with two suitcases and too much money. "He wasn't lying about the offer, either. The immunity would be real. Meridian doesn't make fake offers—they make offers so good you can't refuse, and then they own you. That's their whole architecture. That's how the concessions work. That's how the treaties worked." She looked down at her own hands. "He offered me my grandmother's name like it was his to give."

"It isn't?"

"It's mine to give back," Ines said. "There's a difference, and I suspect you of all people appreciate the difference."

Paloma huffed. Thrown back at them, their own line, and it landed harder coming from her. They busied themselves with the fuel drum, dragging it toward the plane, working the hand pump with a rhythm that kept their mouth from saying anything stupid.

Because the truth was the offer had teeth. It had gone in deep. Seven years of looking over a shoulder, seven years of one-hour stops and no names, seven years of being competent at nothing except disappearing—and here was a voice out of the morning saying *all of it, erased, gone*, the way the river erased a bank, the way the maps erased a country. Paloma knew what erased meant. Paloma had done it to themselves. And the voice had been kind, that was the trap of it, kind and precise and full of arithmetic, and some tired animal part of them had wanted to say *yes, take the box, give me my boring old life.*

"Say it," Ines said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you're not saying while you pump that fuel. You've been not-saying it since he mentioned your charge."

Paloma pumped. The drum gurgled. Downriver, the mist was burning off, which meant the mist had been cover, and the cover was going.

"I'm saying," Paloma said finally, "that he's right about the arithmetic. Fuel, distance, engine, dawn. Every number he gave is true. And I've spent seven years living by numbers like that, and here's what I learned that your brother hasn't: the numbers are always true and they never once told me what to do. They just told me the price. Choosing is separate." They wiped their hands on their trousers. "He's offering to erase me. I did that already. Didn't take."

Ines came and stood beside them at the pump, shoulder to shoulder, watching the fuel line jump.

"When I was nine," she said, "Mateo taught me the radio channels because he said a Vidal should always have a way out. He built me exits my whole life. Doors out of rooms. And I loved him for it, and I never once asked him what room *he* was standing in." Her voice caught, just once, and she let it, and kept going. "He's not betraying us. That's the thing you have to understand. He thinks he's saving us. He'd die before he'd sell us out—he sold us out six hours ago and he'd still say it was rescue. That's what Meridian does to people. It doesn't make them cruel. It makes them certain."

"Certainty's worse."

"Certainty is *cheaper*," Ines said. "Cruelty costs a conscience. Certainty doesn't."

* * *

They worked through the morning in the rhythm of the doomed and the determined: fuel, engine, documents. Ines had already sewn the thin sheaf of the most damning pages into the lining of her jacket—Paloma had watched her do it by headlamp and said nothing, because some preparations you don't interrupt. Now she repacked the chest, and Paloma lashed it into the cargo bay behind the seats, and the plane sat on the sand like a tired bird deciding whether the sky still wanted it.

The engine was the question. Paloma had pulled the cowl at first light and found what they'd feared: the cough from yesterday was a fouled plug and something worse behind it, a hesitation in the mag that might be nothing and might be everything. They cleaned, gapped, tested. The engine turned over and ran with a sound like a man clearing his throat before a speech. It would fly. Probably. The interior ran on *probably*.

At noon exactly, the radio spoke again.

*"Fuel barge at the confluence,"* Mateo said. *"Last offer. It waits one hour."*

Ines picked up the handset. Paloma watched her hold it, watched all forty years of a country and all thirty years of a family pass through her face and settle into something quiet.

"Mateo. Tell me the name of the enforcement lead."

A pause. *"Why?"*

"Because if I'm going to be hunted at dawn, I want to know who by. Grandmother's rule. Know the name of the man at your door."

Another pause, longer, and when Mateo answered, his voice had gone carefully flat, the flatness of a man handing over a knife handle-first. *"Renn. Aurelio Renn. He flew river interdiction for the government before Meridian hired him. He's methodical. He doesn't chase—he waits where you'll be."*

"Thank you." Ines set the handset down. Then she picked it up again, and this time when she pressed transmit, her voice was the steadiest it had been all day. "Mateo. One more thing. When this is over—however it's over—there will be a record. Court records, public ones, with the treaties in them and the census and the photographs. Our grandmother's village will be on a page that anyone can read. And when that happens, I want you to remember that you offered me a name in exchange for burying hers, and that I said no, and that I was right."

Static. Long static. Paloma was reaching for the off switch when Mateo came through one last time, and his voice had lost all its polish, and it was just a brother's voice, wrecked and young.

*"Inés. The team leaves at dawn. Don't be here at dawn."*

The channel went dead.

Paloma stood in the noon heat with fuel on their hands and a half-sick engine at their back and looked downriver, toward Puerto Cangrejo, toward a man named Renn who didn't chase but waited. The mist was gone. The sky was wide open and empty and lying about it.

"Six hours," Paloma said. "Maybe eight, if his weather story holds." They were already moving toward the plane, pulling the chocks, mind running the river south like a chart. "There's a mission clinic at Santa Ana, two hours' flight if the engine cooperates and three if it doesn't. Storms building over the ridge by the look of the clouds, which is bad for flying and good for everything else—Renn's floatplane won't like that weather either."

Ines climbed in beside them, jacket on, documents against her ribs, and buckled the harness with a click that sounded, in the small cabin, like a door being locked from the inside.

"And if the engine doesn't cooperate?" she asked.

Paloma fed the primer, said a word to the engine they'd learned from a mechanic in a border town who'd believed engines had ears, and hit the starter.

"Then we'll have a different problem," they said, as the propeller caught and the whole airframe began to shake with the effort of wanting the sky, "and we'll solve it at the bottom of whatever field we fall into. Hold on."

The sandbar fell away. Behind them, far to the north, a dawn was already being loaded onto a floatplane with their names on the manifest—and ahead, the ridge wore a crown of cloud that looked, from two hundred feet, like a door closing.
