## 13. Dawn Departure

The sandbar was already turning grey when Paloma opened the engine cowling and understood how bad it was.

Not catastrophic. Not a hole in the case or a sheared valve. Something worse in its way: a fuel line that had chafed against a bracket until it wept, and a carburetor gasket that had swollen in the humidity and gone soft, and a magneto that was thinking about quitting the way a tired man thinks about lying down in the road. Any one of them fixable. All of them at once, in the dark, on a riverbank, with an enforcement team lifting off at dawn—that was the arithmetic Paloma did while the last light died behind the western hills.

"You're smiling," Ines said. She was crouched by the chest, wrapping documents in oilcloth by the light of a single candle stub.

"I'm not."

"Your mouth did something."

"That was a grimace." Paloma pulled the cowling off the second side and laid it flat on the sand, where it caught the last of the sky like a dark mirror. "The line I can fix. The gasket I can cut from spare sheeting. The magneto—" They stopped. Touched the points with a fingertip, feeling the pitting. "The magneto I can clean and pray over. I'm not a praying man, so it'll probably hold."

"How long?"

"Working till dawn. Maybe past it."

Ines looked upriver, then downriver, then at the plane, as if the answer to something might be written on the fabric. "And if they come before you finish?"

"Then we're a story someone tells later." Paloma said it flat, the way you'd read a weather report, and saw Ines flinch, and didn't apologize. There was no version of comfort that fit inside these facts. "Get the documents ready. Whatever you can't afford to lose, keep on your body. The plane can be replaced. The chest can't."

"They'll be looking for the plane."

"They'll be looking for a blue-and-white bush plane with a dented cowling. By morning I'm going to make it harder to describe." Paloma reached into the baggage compartment and came out with a can of grey primer they'd bought two years ago in a moment of optimism and never used. "Hold the light."

* * *

The work was good in the way that only desperate work is good. It filled the hands and shut the doors in the mind that wanted to open onto seven years of corridors. Paloma stripped the fuel line, cut a fresh section from spare tubing, flared the ends with a tool that had belonged to a man they'd flown for in another life, and sweated the fittings tight. The river ran past them, low and brown and patient, carrying the smell of the burned country to the south.

Ines worked beside them without being asked. She held the headlamp steady when Paloma's hands were full, and when they weren't, she fed them tools by name—screwdriver, the good pliers, the safety wire—learning the vocabulary of the engine the way she'd learned the vocabulary of treaties, completely and in one night.

"The most damning ones," she said, somewhere past midnight. "You said keep them on my body. Which ones?"

Paloma sat back on their heels and thought about it. The headlamp threw their shadow huge across the sandbar, a giant hunched over a small machine.

"The treaty of 1938. The one with the thumbprints instead of signatures—that's the forgery Meridian's whole claim hangs on, so it's the one they'd burn first. The census page for the villages on the lower reach. And the photograph of the commissioners at the signing." Paloma looked at her. "You know why the photograph matters."

"Because faces are evidence." Ines had it already: three packets of oilcloth, each flat as a letter, each sewn into a different part of her clothing with thread she'd pulled from the hem of her own skirt. The treaty went flat against her ribs. The census page into the waistband seam. The photograph, wrapped twice, into the crown of her hat. "My grandmother sewed money into her coat when she left San Marisol," she said. "She said a country you can carry is a country they can't take."

"Your grandmother sounds like she'd have made a good bush pilot."

"She'd have made a terrible one. She never forgave a road for existing." Ines tied off the last stitch with her teeth and sat back. "There. Now I'm the archive."

Paloma looked at her—this small, stubborn woman with a nation folded into her clothing—and felt something that wasn't quite hope and wasn't quite fear and lived in the same place both of them did.

"Then we'd better get you to a courthouse," they said, and went back to the magneto.

* * *

The sky went from grey to the color of old pearl, and the magneto held, and the gasket sealed, and Paloma closed the cowling on an engine that was no longer beautiful but was, they judged, honest enough to make one hard flight. The primer had dried to a dull grey on the nose and the dented cowling panel; from two hundred meters, in bad light, the plane was no longer the plane in the patrol boat's photograph. It was a tired grey thing, like half the aircraft in the territory.

"Fuel check," Paloma said. "Then we go."

Ines was already loading the chest. She'd repacked it so the empty space where the three documents had been was filled with folded canvas—weight and shape preserved, in case anyone ever weighed it. Paloma hadn't thought of that. They looked at her doing it and thought, not for the first time, that the company had made a mistake underestimating her, and that the mistake had probably started with her brother.

They were hand-propping the engine when Ines went rigid.

"Listen."

Paloma heard it under the wind: a sound like a wasp in a bottle, far off to the north, rising and falling. Not a boat. Boats didn't have that pitch. A floatplane, running its engine up in a warmup, or coming in low over the water.

"Get in," Paloma said. "Belt. Now."

They swung the prop, once, twice—the engine caught on the second swing, coughed, caught again and settled into its rough, uneven idle, and Paloma was up over the wing and into the seat before the sound had grown any louder. The river ahead ran straight for two kilometers, then bent west into the hills. Two kilometers of takeoff run on a sandbar that was maybe nine hundred meters long at this water level.

"It's coming around the bend," Ines said. Her voice was very steady, which Paloma would think about later and be grateful for.

"Then we're leaving first." Paloma ran the engine up, watching the gauges with the whole of their attention, and the oil pressure wobbled and held, and the fuel pressure held, and they turned the nose into the wind that came down the river valley at dawn, cool and clean and smelling of smoke. "When I say now, say nothing and hold on."

The floatplane came around the bend at that moment—still three kilometers off, a dark shape against the pale water, but unmistakably a floatplane, and unmistakably coming this way—and Paloma pushed the throttle to the stop.

The old girl rolled. The sandbar drummed under the wheels. The engine coughed once at half-power and Paloma's heart went into their throat and came back down as it caught and pulled, and the airspeed needle climbed off the pin—forty, fifty, sixty—and the tail came up and the river's far bank swung closer, too close, and then the wheels left the sand and the plane was flying, heavy and low and ugly, climbing out over the water with the engine sounding like a man arguing in his sleep.

Behind them, the floatplane's engine note changed. It had seen them.

"Paloma." Ines's voice. Not steady now.

"I know."

The first bullet went through the tail with a sound like a whip cracking, and the plane shuddered, and Paloma felt it through the controls—a flutter in the rudder, nothing more, a flesh wound. The second and third went into the water to the left, throwing up white threads that vanished. The floatplane was still a kilometer back and closing, and its pilot was a professional, and a professional would not shoot at a plane he might want to force down intact—but he would shoot at the tail, at the controls, at the engine, at anything that made landing the only option.

Paloma did not land.

They put the nose down instead, trading altitude for speed, and dropped toward the river so fast the water seemed to leap up to meet them, and then leveled off ten meters above it, in the floatplane's own blind spot, in the smoke haze that lay along the valley like a second river. The bullets stopped. Behind them, faint through the din, they could hear the other engine, throttled back, searching.

"They've lost us in the smoke," Ines said.

"For now. He'll guess south. He'll guess wrong, because I'm going north." Paloma's hands were steady on the yoke. Their heart was going like a drum, and their mind had gone, for the first time in seven years, perfectly, terrifyingly clear. "Hold the map. We're crossing the hills at the lowest pass, then turning south on the far side. It'll cost us fuel and it'll cost us time, and it's the last thing anyone will expect, because it's the stupidest route available."

Ines looked at them. "You're smiling again."

"This time I mean it."

The plane droned north into the smoke, tail leaking a thin whistle of wind through the bullet hole, carrying a chest of paper and a nation sewn into a woman's clothing, and Paloma flew it the way they had not flown anything since the night that had broken their life: with nothing held back, and nothing left to lose, and the whole sky open ahead.
