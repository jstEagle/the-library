## 14. Grounded

The storm came out of the west like a debt collector.

Paloma saw it first as a bruise on the horizon, then as a wall of green-black cloud eating the river valley whole. The kind of front that didn't negotiate. They had maybe eight minutes before it swallowed the sky, and the nearest strip was forty minutes behind them and an hour ahead of anything else.

"There." They pointed with two fingers off the yoke, at a thread of clearing stitched into the treeline northwest of the river bend. "Santa Remedios."

Ines leaned toward the glass. "A town?"

"A mission. Clinic, chapel, a dirt patch they call a runway because the sisters asked God for one and he sent surveyors instead." Paloma's hands were already moving—mixture lean, carb heat, eyes flicking between the wall of weather and the treeline rushing up to meet them. "Hold on to your suitcases."

The wind found them before the rain did. It shoved the plane sideways like a hand flat against the fuselage, and Paloma fought it down in a crabbed, ugly descent, wings rocking, rain arriving all at once in sheets that turned the windshield into a river of its own. The strip appeared and vanished beneath the wipers—a brown smear, a fence line, three cows ambling off it with the unhurried contempt of animals who had seen worse landings.

Paloma put it down hard and long, hydroplaning through standing water, tail swinging, and brought the blue-and-white Cessna to a stop twenty meters short of the mission's gate with the engine coughing mud out of its own intake.

Silence, except for the rain hammering the roof like applause from an enemy.

"Nice," Ines said faintly. Her knuckles were white around the chest's strap where it sat wedged under her legs.

"That wasn't nice. That was survival." Paloma killed the mags and sat for a moment, listening to the drumming, feeling the particular exhaustion of a body that had spent ten minutes being smarter than the weather and knew the margin had been thin. "Get the chest under the tarp. We may be here a while."

* * *

The sisters were expecting no one, but they acted like they'd been expecting everyone.

The door of the clinic opened before Paloma finished knocking, and there stood a nun built like a dockworker, sleeves rolled, flour on one forearm, who took in the two soaked strangers and the tarp-wrapped bundle between them and said only, "You'll want dry clothes before you want explanations. Come in. Mind the cat."

Inside smelled of woodsmoke, iodine, and bread. The clinic was one long room: six cots, most of them occupied by river people recovering from fevers, a broken arm, something with bandages that Paloma chose not to study too closely. A wood stove ticked in the corner. A teenage girl with a fever slept under mosquito netting while another sister changed her compress with movements so practiced they looked like prayer.

And then the Mother Superior came out of the back room, drying her hands, and Paloma felt seven years fold up like a chart.

Sister Beatriz was smaller than memory made her—grey now where she'd been iron-grey, shoulders narrower—but her eyes hadn't changed at all. She looked at Paloma for exactly two seconds.

"Montrose." Not a question.

"Sister." Paloma kept their voice level, though something in their chest had gone strange and hot. "You remember me."

"You delivered penicillin through a storm in the wet season, four years ago, when the radio was down and every other pilot said wait." She said it plainly, the way she might report a temperature. "Half this village would be deaf or dead without that flight. I don't forget faces that arrive when the weather says stay home." Her gaze slid to Ines, to the bundle. "Though I notice today you arrived running *from* something rather than toward."

Paloma said nothing. Ines said nothing. The rain filled the silence.

Beatriz sighed like a woman whose faith had long since made room for disappointment. "Dry clothes. Soup. Then we will discuss what you are carrying and why the river is about to hear about it whether I like it or not." She turned for the kitchen. "Word travels fast here. That is not a threat. It is geography."

* * *

They ate in the kitchen while the storm worked itself into a fury against the shutters. The soup was cassava and dried fish and better than it had any right to be. Sister Beatriz sat across from them with tea she didn't drink, and Paloma told her as much of the truth as seemed survivable: storm, forced landing, cargo they couldn't discuss, people looking for them.

"People with money," Beatriz said.

"People with money," Paloma agreed.

"And the cargo?"

Ines set down her spoon. Whatever she'd decided in the last thirty seconds, she'd decided completely—the way she did everything, Paloma was learning. "Documents," she said. "Treaty documents. From San Marisol."

The spoon did not clatter. But Beatriz went very still, and in the stillness Paloma watched something old move across her face—not surprise. Recognition. Grief, maybe, of the archival kind, the kind you keep in a drawer.

"The sisters taught San Marisolan children," Beatriz said at last. "Before the maps were redrawn. Before the company explained to everyone that those villages had never existed at all." She folded her hands. "We buried eleven of them in the churchyard. The ones the fever took, the year before the erasure. Their families were moved somewhere the government denied having. We were told to strike their names from the register." Her voice stayed level; her hands did not. "I struck the names. And then I wrote them again, underneath, in Latin, where nobody official reads."

Ines made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost the opposite.

"So." Beatriz stood, collected her untouched tea, and became briskly administrative. "You will sleep in the infirmary. You will help me restock shelves in the morning—you look like pilots, not guests, and idle fugitives gossip. And whatever happens next, that chest does not leave my sight until it leaves my roof. Understood?"

"Yes, Sister," they said together, and it sounded absurdly like school.

* * *

The storm blew itself out overnight, which meant the world could reach them again.

It reached them by radio, naturally. Paloma was under the Cessna at dawn with a wrench, draining water from the fuel strainer—the engine had swallowed half the river on that landing—and heard the clinic's set crackle to life inside. By the time they walked back in, wiping their hands, Sister Marta, the young one, was standing at the radio with an expression like a woman holding a snake.

"It's a man," she reported. "From Meridian Concessions. He wants to speak to whoever's in charge."

"Everyone wants to speak to whoever's in charge," Beatriz said. "Put him through. Speaker."

The voice that filled the kitchen was smooth as varnish. It introduced itself as Señor Aldous Renner, Regional Liaison for Community Partnerships, and it congratulated the mission on its "vital work," and it mentioned—it happened to mention—that Meridian's annual grant cycle was opening, and Santa Remedios had been flagged as a priority candidate. New clinic roof. A proper generator. A motorboat for patient transport. A figure followed, and it had more zeroes than the mission probably saw in a decade.

"All we ask," Renner said, warm as bathwater, "is assistance in a small administrative matter. Two travelers may have sheltered with you during last night's weather. One of them is transporting property belonging to our client portfolio. Misappropriated cultural materials—we're quite sure they mean no harm, but provenance matters, doesn't it? If you could simply confirm their presence, or telephone us if they appear, our team can collect them and the paperwork, and your grant review proceeds unimpeded."

The kitchen was quiet. Rain dripped off the eaves. The cat washed its paw on the windowsill.

"Señor Renner," Beatriz said pleasantly, "this is a clinic. When sick people arrive, we treat them. When wet people arrive, we dry them. I do not inventory them, and I do not sell them." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Was there a medical question? No? Then good day to you."

She clicked off the radio. For a moment nobody breathed.

"He knows," Paloma said quietly. "Or he suspects. That call wasn't a bluff—he'll have someone driving out here within the hour to see for himself."

"Probably," Beatriz agreed. She didn't seem frightened. She seemed tired, in the way of a woman who had been fighting institutions for fifty years and had learned the fight's rhythm the way sailors learn swell patterns. "He will offer more money. They always do, once the first number fails. And then he will threaten the diocese, and the bishop will write me a letter full of sorrow."

"Sister." Paloma stepped closer, lowered their voice. "You should give us up. I mean it. Whatever that grant is worth, it's more than we are. Take the money, tell him we left in the night, point him south. We'll be gone before—"

"Montrose." Beatriz looked at them the way she must have looked at stubborn patients refusing medicine. "Do you know what Meridian's predecessor company did, the year they erased San Marisol? They offered this mission a generator. Same pitch, same warm voice, different decade. All we had to do was sign a paper confirming that the villages upriver were uninhabited." Her jaw tightened. "We signed it. I was young. Sister Agnes signed it. We told ourselves it was just paper, just ink, that the generator would save lives and the villages were far away and surely it didn't matter."

She crossed to the window, where the morning light was coming up gold over the flooded fields, and the churchyard was visible beyond it, rows of small crosses.

"Ink always matters," she said. "That is the first lesson of any archivist and the last lesson of anyone honest. So no—I will not sell you for a roof." She turned back, and now there was something fierce in the small frame, banked and steady as the stove. "But I will not pretend to be stupid either. How long do we have?"

Paloma checked the light, the sky, the wet strip gleaming outside. "Engine needs an hour to dry out and run clean. Call it ninety minutes if nothing goes wrong."

"Then you have eighty-nine of them, and in the last one you'll wish me luck and be gone." She was already moving, pulling keys, giving orders to Marta in rapid Spanish—fuel cans from the shed, bread wrapped for travel, the truck to tow the plane to the high end of the strip where the ground was firmest. "And Montrose—" She stopped them at the door with one hand, brief, dry, surprisingly strong on the sleeve.

"When you deliver whatever that is," she said, "deliver it properly. All the way. No half-landings. I've watched too many people drop the truth at the edge of trouble and call it done."

Paloma held her gaze. "Yes, Sister."

* * *

It took seventy minutes. It should have taken sixty-five, but the engine ran rough for the first ten after start-up, spitting residual water, and Paloma refused to leave until it smoothed out—instruments checked twice, mags checked three times, the whole preflight litany performed aloud like a rosary while Ines loaded the chest and Sister Marta stood at the wingtip with the chocks, not quite smiling.

Beatriz came out to the strip as they ran up the engine. The wind of the propeller pushed at her habit and she ignored it. She leaned in at Paloma's window, shouted over the noise:

"The girl in cot four—Tomasa—her grandmother came from a village called Yuracuna!"

Paloma's hands went still on the throttle. Yuracuna. Page nine of the census. Forty-one souls, recorded, then erased.

"She's alive," Beatriz shouted. "The grandmother. She remembers songs nobody else remembers. When your papers win—" and she said *when*, not *if*, and Paloma felt it land somewhere under the ribs, "—bring them back to her. That's my price. Not a roof. Names returned."

"Names returned," Paloma repeated.

Beatriz stepped back, raised one hand—not a wave, a blessing, or possibly just dismissal—and they rolled.

The strip was soft but long enough. The Cessna lifted through air scrubbed clean by the storm, the whole world rinsed and glittering, and Santa Remedios shrank below: the tin roof, the churchyard crosses, one grey figure at the gate with her hand still raised.

Beside them, Ines had the chest strapped tight and her jaw set hard.

"Renner knew we were close," she said. "Which means Mateo told him the sandbar coordinates, or the lock agents logged the barge, or—"

"Or Toma sold us twice." Paloma banked south, away from the river main, toward the long empty stretch where the map showed nothing but green. "Doesn't matter yet. What matters is he knew fast, which means the net isn't behind us anymore. It's ahead."

Ahead, the sky was clear. Ahead, the capital waited with its ministries and its expired stamps. And somewhere ahead too, unseen, unhurried, Meridian's enforcement floatplane was already in the air.

Paloma pushed the throttle forward and flew toward all of it.
