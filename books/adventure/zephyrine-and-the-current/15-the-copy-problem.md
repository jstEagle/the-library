## 15. The Copy Problem

The rain had stopped by morning, but the clinic's tin roof kept ticking with the last of it, a sound like someone counting slowly to no particular number. Paloma stood at the window of the room the nuns had given them—a storeroom, really, shelves of gauze and iodine pushed against one wall—and watched the airstrip dry. Puddles steamed in the early sun. The Cessna sat where they'd left it, blue-and-white, tail still carrying the memory of Meridian's bullets in two neat holes that would need patching before they flew again.

Behind them, Ines was counting.

Not money. Paper.

Paloma could hear it: the soft slide of documents being lifted from the chest, squared against the table, counted, set down again. She'd been at it since before dawn, working by lamplight while the Mother Superior's kitchen below them sent up the smell of rice and woodsmoke. Every few minutes she'd stop, and Paloma would hear her breathing change—the small hitch of a woman reading something that made her angry or sad or both—and then the counting would resume.

"You should eat," Paloma said without turning around.

"I'll eat when I know how many there are."

"How many what?"

"Pages that can end this." A pause. "Two hundred and eleven so far."

Paloma turned. Ines sat at the table with her sleeves rolled up, her hair pinned back with what looked like a pencil, her glasses fogged from the lamplight. The chest lay open beside her like a mouth. Around her, the documents were arranged in stacks with a precision that bordered on ritual: treaties here, census pages there, photographs face-down in a shallow tray because she couldn't bear to look at more than a few at a time.

"And when you know?" Paloma asked. "What then?"

Ines took off her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "Then we talk about copies."

"We've talked about copies."

"No," Ines said. "You've talked about why we shouldn't make any. I've listened. That's not the same as talking."

* * *

They'd been at the mission four days. Long enough for the storm to pass, long enough for Sister Bernadette to patch the tail with fabric and dope from a supply crate older than either of them, long enough—Paloma knew—for word to travel downriver. It always traveled. The mission had a radio; the radio had an audience; somewhere downstream, a man whose job was making problems disappear was adding a name to a list.

The nuns had chosen their side already. Paloma hadn't expected that. Kindness delivered seven years ago, in the form of penicillin and insulin flown through weather that grounded everyone else, had apparently accrued interest. But gratitude was a currency that spent differently depending on who was doing the spending, and Meridian's offer—funding for a new wing, a generator, a boat—had been generous enough to make the Mother Superior's refusal cost her something visible. She'd told Paloma about it plainly, the way she told everything: "I told them God does not take bids. Now we pray He agrees with me."

So they had shelter, food, and maybe three days before the shelter became a siege. Which meant the argument couldn't wait any longer.

"Copies mean hands," Paloma said now, crossing the room and sitting down across from Ines. "Every copy is another person who can be found, pressured, bought. You want to give a set to a journalist? Fine. Then some reporter is carrying two hundred pages that Meridian has already shot at an airplane over. You think they won't do worse to a man with an office in the capital?"

"The journalist I have in mind doesn't have an office," Ines said. "He works out of a print shop behind the fish market. He's published three stories about Meridian's land clearing and survived all of them."

"Survived *so far*."

"Yes. So far. That's how survival works, Paloma. Nobody gets a guarantee."

"A single chest can disappear," Paloma said. "That's my point. One chest, one plane, one pilot, one handoff. No chain. You put this evidence in five places and you've built five doors for them to kick."

"And if the one place fails?" Ines's voice didn't rise, but it changed register, dropping into something flat and hard that Paloma hadn't heard from her before. "If the chest disappears—which is exactly what Meridian wants, exactly what their fixer offered you in the capital, exactly what every instinct you have is telling you to arrange—then forty years of erasure succeeds. My grandmother's country stays dead. Two hundred villages stay missing. And we flew all this way to help bury them deeper."

"I never said anything about letting it disappear."

"You didn't have to." Ines put her glasses back on. Her hands, Paloma noticed, were perfectly steady. "You've been flying away from things for seven years. It's not a criticism. It's just a fact about you, the way it's a fact about me that I trust paper more than people. We're both doing what we know."

The words landed harder than a shout would have. Paloma looked at the table, at the treaty pages with their faded ribbons and wax seals, at the census columns where whole settlements were recorded and then struck through with administrative strokes—*relocated, dissolved, consolidated*, the polite vocabulary of disappearance.

"That's not fair," Paloma said quietly.

"No," Ines agreed. "It isn't. Fairness left this conversation somewhere over the burn-off smoke. We're past fair. We're at arithmetic." She spread her hands over the stacks. "One copy exists right now. If we lose it, everything ends. Two copies halve the risk of total loss. Three quarters it. This isn't philosophy, it's redundancy. Any pilot should understand redundancy."

"Redundancy in engines doesn't get tortured."

"In engines, the backup system also carries risk," Ines said. "A second engine is weight, complexity, another thing to fail. Pilots fly twins anyway, because the alternative is betting everything on one crankshaft. You're asking me to bet forty years of dead people on one crankshaft."

Paloma wanted to argue. The argument was right there, fully formed, loaded: names, faces, the fish-market journalist's family, the cousin in the hills who'd hide the second set, every one of them a thread Meridian could pull until something tore. But underneath the argument was the other thing, the thing Paloma had learned in seven years of professional evasion, and it was this: secrets don't survive by being hidden well. They survive by being *spread*. A secret one person holds is a hostage. A secret ten people hold is a rumor, and rumors are almost impossible to kill.

Paloma had run alone for seven years. It had worked, technically. It had also cost everything, and it had very nearly ended on a sandbar with a floatplane coming in at dawn.

"Who's the journalist?" Paloma said finally.

Ines blinked. "Emil Roque. He wrote the series on the Río Blanco clearances. He's careful, he's stubborn, and he hates Meridian for reasons that predate us by a decade."

"He keeps the copies where?"

"Not at the print shop. Somewhere he rotates—I don't know the details, and I don't want to. Plausible deniability runs both directions."

"And the second set?"

"My cousin Aurelia. She lives outside San Rafael de los Llanos, three days' walk from the nearest road. She buried family papers during the last purge and they're still in the ground and still legible. She knows how to keep things underground." Ines paused. "She also owes our family. She'll do it."

"She'll do it *if* you ask her. Which means contacting her. Which means a letter, a telegram, or a visit, and every one of those leaves a trail."

"I've thought about that."

"Have you thought about what happens to *her* when Meridian traces the trail?"

The question hung there. Ines looked down at the photographs, face-down in their tray, and for a moment Paloma thought she wouldn't answer. Then she turned one over—a row of people standing in front of a church, squinting into a sun that had set forty years ago—and set it back down.

"Yes," she said. "I've thought about it every night since the sandbar. I've thought about Mateo finding us because of what I did, and I've thought about what Meridian's men do to people who are merely in the way, and I've decided that the answer is still yes. Not because I'm brave. Because the alternative is that we protect ourselves by guaranteeing their victory, and I can't—" Her voice caught, steadied. "My grandmother used to say that San Marisol didn't die when the maps changed. It died when the last person who remembered stopped writing it down. I am the last person writing it down, Paloma. If I choose safety over memory, I'm the one who kills it."

* * *

They fought about it for two more days anyway, because the decision deserved to be fought for.

It wasn't shouting. Neither of them was a shouter. It was worse than shouting: it was two careful people dismantling each other's plans line by line, over meals, over engine work, over the mending of the tail fabric while Sister Bernadette pretended not to listen and occasionally offered theological commentary ("In my experience," she said at one point, holding the dope brush, "the arguments that last longest are the ones where both sides are right").

Paloma laid out the operational case: contact points, interception risks, the specific ways Meridian's enforcement team worked—they'd seen it at dawn on the sandbar, professional, unhurried, thorough. Every additional custodian was a liability with a name and an address and people who loved them.

Ines laid out the historical case: she'd read the archives of three other erased places, and in every single one, the records that survived were the ones that had been copied, scattered, duplicated beyond any authority's ability to gather them up. Single copies died in fires, in floods, in filing errors, in one man's conscience failing at the wrong moment. "The chest is a miracle," she said. "Miracles are not a storage strategy."

On the third evening, the Mother Superior sent up a plate of fried plantains and a message: a launch had come upriver asking questions at the trading post twelve kilometers south. Questions about a blue-and-white plane. The trader had told them nothing, but he'd charged them double for fuel, which meant he'd tell someone else something soon, because that was how the river worked.

The clock, which had been ticking since the permits, got louder.

"All right," Paloma said. They were sitting on the floor of the storeroom, backs against the gauze shelves, too tired to sit in chairs. "Compromise. But it's my compromise, so hear all of it."

Ines turned her head. Waited.

"One set goes to Emil Roque. Your journalist. But it goes by hand—you carry it, or I fly it, no letters, no telegrams, nothing that predates the handoff. Until it's in his hands, nobody knows it exists, including him."

"Agreed."

"Second set—not to your cousin. Not yet. We make it, we seal it, and it stays *with us*. Split between the two of us, on our bodies, wherever we go. If we make it to the capital and the hearing happens, it becomes insurance—if the chest vanishes, or the judge stonewalls, or Meridian pulls something, the second set surfaces. If we're taken, it's on both of us, which means they'd need to stop both of us, which buys time."

Ines was quiet for a long moment. "And Aurelia?"

"After. When the hearing's done, when the permits have collapsed or haven't, when whatever's going to happen has happened—then she gets her set, and she becomes the archive's deep vault. But not before. I'm not sending your family into the fire before the fight starts."

"That's not full redundancy."

"It's full redundancy *with staging*. You taught me the word. Don't pretend it doesn't apply."

Ines almost smiled. It was a tired, crooked thing, but it was real. "You realize what you've just agreed to. You'll be carrying forty years of a dead nation inside your jacket."

"I've carried worse," Paloma said. "I carried a lie for seven years. Paper's lighter."

* * *

They worked through the night.

Sister Bernadette produced supplies with the unflappable competence of someone who had smuggled medicine through three different governments: oilcloth, wax, a camp stove for the sealing, and a camera—an old twin-lens reflex, missionary issue—that Ines cleaned and loaded with hands that shook only once, when she framed the first photograph.

They photographed everything. Treaty pages under lamplight, angled to catch the seals. Census sheets, column by column. The photographs, one at a time, Ines turning each over with the care of someone handling bones. Paloma ran the timer and the log, recording frame numbers against document numbers in a ledger that would itself become part of the record.

Somewhere around two in the morning, Ines paused over a photograph of a village headman—erect, formal, wearing a medal from a government that would soon erase his town—and said, without looking up:

"Why did you change your mind? Really."

Paloma considered lying. It was the old reflex, smooth and fast, and for once it came easily to refuse.

"Because I've been the only copy," Paloma said. "Of myself. Seven years, one version of events, held by one person. And you know what I learned? A secret one person keeps isn't safe. It's just heavy. It doesn't protect you. It bends you, year by year, until you're shaped around it." They fed another page under the lens. "Ten copies of the truth aren't ten risks. They're ten chances that somebody, somewhere, gets to set it down."

Ines looked at them across the lamp for a moment. Then she nodded, once, and turned the next page.

By dawn they had two complete sets: the originals repacked in the chest, and ninety-six photographs split into two oilcloth packets, wax-sealed, each packet thin enough to wear against the body and thick enough to hold a country. Ines sewed a pocket into the lining of her jacket with stitches fine as surgery. Paloma took the other packet and, after a moment, slid it into the inner pocket where the falsified logbook page had lived for seven years, until the sandbar confession had emptied it. It fit as though the pocket had been waiting.

They were sealing the wax on the final packet when the knock came—two raps, urgent but controlled—and Sister Bernadette's voice through the door, low and level:

"Pilot. Archivist. There's a launch at our dock. Two men in company khaki, and they're not asking about salvage rights this time."

Paloma was on their feet before she finished the sentence, one hand closing around the sealed packet, the other reaching for the flight bag. Outside, the mist was lifting off the river, and the airstrip was drying, and the Cessna waited with its patched tail like a patient who'd been told to walk.

"They're early," Ines whispered, stuffing the ledger into her shirt.

"No," Paloma said, moving for the door. "We're late. There's a difference, and it's about to matter."
