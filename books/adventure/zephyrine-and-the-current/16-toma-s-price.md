## 16. Toma's Price

Toma's barge sat at the mouth of the Caño Verde like a thing that had been there since the flood, which in a sense it had. Forty meters of rusted steel deck stacked with fuel drums, dried fish, outboard motors, and crates of beer nobody would admit to owning, the whole arrangement moored under a tarpaulin the color of old teeth. Paloma had traded with Toma for nine years. Toma had never once asked where the medicine went or where the money came from, and Paloma had never once asked why a man with two functioning hands and a working engine chose to push cargo upriver at a walking pace instead of flying it.

That unspoken contract was about to be renegotiated.

They found him in the wheelhouse, barefoot, drinking coffee thick enough to stand a spoon in. He was sixty-something and built like the barge itself—wide, slow, and impossible to sink. He looked at Paloma, then at Ines, then at the duffel bag Ines carried with both hands, and his face did something Paloma had never seen it do: it closed.

"You brought company to my boat," Toma said.

"I brought a problem to the only man who can solve it."

"That's worse." He set the cup down. "Sit. Both of you. And before you say anything, know that I already know."

Paloma's stomach dropped. "Know what?"

"A blue-and-white Cessna made an emergency landing at the Santa Ana mission two nights ago. A floatplane with no registration followed the river south the next morning, slow, like a man looking for a dog. Meridian's paying two thousand for word of where that plane is now." He said it flat, the way he quoted fuel prices. "The bartender at Puerto Lempira already sold a description. Not a location—a description. There's a difference, and right now that difference is the only thing keeping you breathing."

Ines sat down slowly, the duffel in her lap. "Then you know why we're here."

"I know you need to get to the capital without touching an airstrip, a checkpoint, or a phone. I know you can't pay what that costs, because nobody can. And I know—" Toma looked at Paloma, and there was something in his eyes Paloma had never seen there before, something that wasn't anger and wasn't greed, something worse. It was disappointment. "I know what you're carrying. Not the papers. The other thing."

The wheelhouse was very quiet. Down on the deck, a crewman coiled rope, the sound of it faint and rhythmic, like a clock in another room.

"Toma—"

"Nine years." Toma's voice stayed level. "Nine years you've landed on my dock, bought my fuel, eaten my food, and never once lied to me, because you never once told me anything at all. That's a kind of honesty. I respected it. I told myself, this one has reasons, this one keeps quiet the way the river keeps quiet, and the river doesn't owe anyone an explanation." He picked up the coffee and didn't drink it. "But now you're asking me to carry you and that bag past Meridian's agents at the Dos Bocas lock. You're asking me to bet my boat, my crew, and twenty years of careful neutrality on you. And I don't know what I'm betting on. Nobody does. Not even you."

"I'm betting on the truth," Ines said.

Toma didn't even look at her. "The truth is free. Everybody's got one. What I want to know is what yours cost."

Paloma stood there with the wheelhouse door at their back and the river ahead of them, brown and slow and patient, and understood, finally, what Toma was doing. It wasn't a shakedown. It wasn't even a price, not really. It was a door, and Toma was standing in it, and he was not going to move until Paloma walked through it as a whole person or not at all.

"Name it," Paloma said.

"The plane."

The word landed like a dropped wrench. Paloma heard it, understood it, and still said, "No."

"The plane," Toma repeated, "and the truth. Both. You give me the Cessna—papers, logbook, the whole history of that aircraft, which I will register under my cousin's name in Guaviare before the week is out—and you tell me, here, now, to my face, what happened seven years ago. All of it. Not the river version. Not the version where you're a good pilot who had bad luck. The real one. And then I decide whether I carry you."

"The plane is my livelihood."

"The plane is a beacon. Every man on this river knows its silhouette now. It's worth more to me as scrap and cover than it will ever again be worth to you as wings." Toma set the cup down with a small, final click. "And the truth is worth more than the plane. Because if I'm going to lie to Meridian's face for three days, I need to know exactly what kind of liar I'm lying for."

Paloma looked at Ines. Ines looked back, and said nothing, and that nothing was the loudest thing in the room. *Your choice. Your past. Your price.*

Seven years Paloma had kept the story sealed. Not told it to no one—told it to no one *whole*. Pieces leaked out over the years, the way secrets do, the way floodwater finds every seam: a charter, an accident, a charge. But the shape of it, the weight of it, the part where Paloma had stood in the rain beside a dead man's plane and made a decision that turned a tragedy into a crime—that had belonged to Paloma alone. It was the one possession that had never been traded, never spent, never offered.

And now a man who owed them nothing was asking for it as fare.

"Give us a minute," Paloma said.

"No," said Toma. "You've had seven years."

* * *

Paloma walked out onto the deck because the wheelhouse was too small and the story had always been too big for small rooms. The river moved past, carrying its usual freight of branches, foam, and things better not identified. Ines followed and stood a few feet away, arms crossed against the heat, saying nothing, offering nothing, which was its own kind of respect.

"You don't have to listen to this," Paloma said.

"Yes, I do. If he's going to carry us, I need to know what he's carrying." She paused. "And if I'm honest, I've been wondering since the mission. You fly like someone being followed. I thought it was Meridian. It isn't, is it. It never was."

"No." Paloma gripped the rail. The metal was hot enough to sting. "It's a man named Aurelio Fuentes. He was a passenger. He's been dead seven years, and I still fly like he's in the seat behind me."

Ines said nothing. The river said nothing. Paloma took a breath and started.

"I was twenty-six. I owed eleven thousand dollars to a man in Puerto Lempira who'd lent me money against the plane, and he'd stopped being patient. A charter came through—freight and one passenger, Aurelio Fuentes, a surveyor for a company I didn't ask enough about. The load was over gross by a hundred and forty kilos and I knew it. I signed the manifest anyway, because the fee covered the debt with four hundred left over, and four hundred dollars is what my life was worth to me that day. That's the first thing you should understand. It wasn't desperation in the abstract. It was arithmetic."

Ines's face didn't change. She had spent her life with documents, Paloma realized. She knew how to read a confession.

"Storm cell over the Serranía. I could have turned back. I'd turned back before, a dozen times, and eaten the loss. That day I didn't, because the fee was contingent on delivery and because I was twenty-six and because I told myself the cell was moving east, which it was, mostly. I went through a gap in it that was there when I entered and wasn't there when I needed it. Downdraft took four hundred feet in seconds. I recovered it. Any pilot would tell you recovering it was the skilled part. But we'd come out of the cell low over the ridge, and the load—my load, my decision—meant I didn't have the margin to climb out of the valley. I put it into the trees on the far slope. Controlled. As controlled as it could be."

"And him."

"And him." Paloma's hands were steady on the rail, which was somehow the worst part, that they could be steady now. "Seatbelt anchor point failed. Or the impact was simply beyond what any anchor survives—I've never known which, and I've stopped pretending the difference matters. He was alive when I got out of my harness. He was dead four minutes later, on the forest floor, while I held his hand and told him a rescue was coming. A rescue was coming. That part was true. What wasn't true is what I told them after."

The rope-coiling sound from the deck had stopped. Paloma didn't look to see whether anyone was listening. It didn't matter anymore. That was the strange thing—after seven years of guarding the story, the telling of it felt less like opening a vault and more like setting down something that had been carried so long the arms had forgotten their own shape.

"The company's investigators came, and I told them the load was legal. I showed them a manifest I'd already altered. I let them blame the weather, the cell, the gap in the ridge. There was an inquiry. It found pilot error under conditions—my error, but the forgivable kind, the survivable kind. And then, a year later, Aurelio Fuentes's family hired their own investigator, a man who found the original fuel and weight tickets from Puerto Lempira. Overload. Falsified manifest. In that jurisdiction, with a dead passenger and paper proof, it wasn't an accident anymore. It was manslaughter. They issued a warrant." Paloma turned to look at Ines. "I had forty-eight hours' warning from a friend in the civil registry. I flew north on a Sunday night with a bag and a grudge against myself I didn't have words for yet, and I've been flying cargo into places with no names ever since."

"And the family," Ines said quietly. "Fuentes's family. Did they ever—"

"His daughter wrote me a letter, three years ago. It reached me through Toma, actually." Paloma nodded toward the wheelhouse. "I never answered it. I told myself answering it would be dangerous, that any contact could expose me. That was true. It was also a lie, because the true reason was that I didn't know what to say that wasn't either an excuse or a wound. So I kept the letter in my flight bag and I read it maybe forty times and I never answered it, and that is the single most cowardly thing I have ever done, and I once falsified a federal manifest, so the competition was stiff."

Ines almost smiled. It was a small, sad thing. "Why are you telling us this now? Toma's price. But you could refuse. You could find another way to the capital. You could—"

"Run." Paloma looked at the river. "I could always run. That's the one skill I've truly mastered. But I've been carrying this story the way you've been carrying that chest, Ines. And somewhere between the sandbar and the mission I understood that the chest and the story are the same weight. Proof of something that happened, sealed up, kept from the people it belongs to. His daughter belongs to my truth the way your grandmother's villages belong to those treaties. And I can't very well stand up in a courtroom and testify about Meridian's forgeries if I'm a forger myself who never paid for it. They'd eat me alive, and worse—they'd be right to."

Down the deck, Toma was leaning in the wheelhouse doorway. How long he'd been listening was impossible to say, and Paloma suspected that was the point. He came forward slowly, barefoot on the hot steel, and stopped an arm's length away.

"The man in the letter," Toma said. "Aurelio Fuentes. He was surveying for whom?"

Paloma's blood went cold, because Paloma had never said, had never let the thought fully form even alone at night, and now it arrived whole: *Meridian. The survey that became the concession maps. The maps built on the forged treaties.* "Meridian Concessions," Paloma said. "Preliminary survey, the eastern block."

Toma nodded slowly, as if a long division had finally come out even. "Then it isn't a price at all, is it. It's a fare, and you've paid it, and now I'll tell you something for free." He looked at the duffel in Ines's arms. "Seven years ago, a Meridian surveyor dies in a crash caused by an overloaded plane and a falsified manifest, and the company buries the investigation because a dead surveyor asking questions about land boundaries is worth more to them dead than alive. I heard that story years ago from a fuel man in Lempira. I never connected it to you, because the pilot in that story was a man." He shrugged. "Names travel badly on this river. Truths travel worse."

Ines had gone very still. "He was surveying the eastern block. The same block the treaties cover. Paloma—your accident wasn't an accident next to their story. It was *in* it. You were there. You've been carrying a piece of their paper trail without knowing it."

"The manifest," Paloma said slowly. The old copy, the true one, the one that would hang them—had they kept it? Of course they had. It lived in the flight bag, behind the letter, because some part of Paloma had known for seven years that a day would come when the truth would need its receipts. "The original manifest is in my bag. Weight tickets, fuel chits, the works. It proves what I did. And it also proves Meridian's own surveyor was flying their eastern block seven years ago—before any of their permits were filed. Before the land was legally theirs to survey."

Toma smiled, and it was not a comforting sight. "So. The plane, the truth, and now the manifest goes in the bag with the treaties. That's the fare. Agreed?"

Paloma looked at the Cessna sitting on the water at the edge of the caño, blue and white and patient, seven years of survival parked at a dock. It hurt more than Paloma had expected—like watching someone else drive away in your own skeleton. But the plane had been the running. Every rivet of it. Letting it go wasn't a price. It was the first installment on stopping.

"Agreed," Paloma said. "One condition. When this is over—if it's over, if I'm not in a cell—you teach me to run the barge. I'm going to need work that stays on the ground."

Toma spat thoughtfully over the rail. "You'd be a terrible deckhand. You'd want to go fast." He stuck out his hand. "We load at dark. Dos Bocas lock at dawn. You two sleep below, among the fish crates, and you don't come up no matter what you hear. And Paloma—" his grip was dry and strong and did not let go quickly, "—the daughter's letter. When you get to the capital, you answer it. That's not part of the price. That's free advice, and it's the most expensive thing I've given you."

Paloma nodded, because speech had temporarily failed.

* * *

They loaded at dark, as promised. The Cessna was stripped of anything identifiable—registration plates pulled, the little blue-and-white silhouette that every man on the river now knew covered with a tarp and lashed among the fuel drums like cargo, which was what it was now. Paloma did the work alone, hands moving through the familiar rituals one last time: drain, cover, tie off. Ines stood on the dock with the duffel and watched, and when Paloma finally stepped off the float for the last time, she said nothing, which was right.

Below decks, among the fish crates, the two of them sat shoulder to shoulder in the dark while the engine turned over and the barge pulled away from the dock, heading south, toward the capital, toward the lock at Dos Bocas, toward whatever came next.

"Seven years," Ines said into the dark. "And you gave up the plane in ten minutes."

"It took seven years and ten minutes. Those are different measurements." Paloma leaned back against a crate that smelled of salt and long voyages. "What about you? When this is done—your grandmother's name restored, the villages on the map again—what do you do with a country that exists again?"

"Finish the archive," Ines said simply. "Countries don't come back all at once. They come back a document at a time."

The barge pushed south through the dark. Somewhere ahead, at the Dos Bocas lock, Meridian's agents were checking papers, and in three days they would be checking Toma's. In the flight bag between them, the true manifest lay against the daughter's unopened letter, and Paloma found, to their own surprise, that the weight of it all had changed.

It was no longer the weight of something hidden.

It was the weight of something about to be set down.
