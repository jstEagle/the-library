## 17. Downriver

The barge was called *Doña Remedios*, which Paloma took as a good sign and then immediately distrusted, because nothing in their life had been a good sign in seven years.

She was forty meters of rust-streaked steel, half cargo hold, half open deck stacked with timber and drums of diesel, with a wheelhouse the size of a closet and an owner named Berto who shook Paloma's hand once, hard, and said: "You work. You don't talk. You eat last." Then he handed them a canvas jacket that smelled of fish oil and someone else's cigarettes.

Paloma had flown over this river for seven years and never once ridden it. From the air it was a brown ribbon with sandbars like spilled sugar. From the deck it was something else entirely — wide, slow, muscular, carrying whole trees past them sideways, the water folding and unfolding against the hull with a patience that made Paloma's skin crawl. The river didn't hurry. The river didn't have to. It always arrived.

Three days to the capital. Three days below the flight paths, below the checkpoints, below everything Meridian Concessions owned or pretended to own. Toma's price had been paid — the plane, the truth, both surrendered at his dock two nights ago — and now there was nothing left to do but be nobody.

Paloma was, it turned out, bad at being nobody.

* * *

The first day was the worst, because the first day was all chest.

The documents were gone from Ines's clothing now, redistributed through the barge itself — pages sealed inside oilcloth and slipped into diesel drums Berto had agreed to carry, photographs flat beneath a false floor in the galley, the census of disappeared villages rolled tight inside a hollowed mop handle that leaned, innocently, against the wheelhouse wall. Ines had done the hiding herself, methodical as a surgeon closing, while Paloma stood watch on the dock and hated every second of it.

"Paper floats," Paloma had said, when Ines sealed the first drum.

"Oilcloth doesn't," Ines said, "if you seal it properly."

"And if we sink?"

Ines had looked up at them then, kneeling among Berto's drums, and her face had been very calm. "Then the river gets to decide," she said. "It already decided once. It gave us the chest."

Now she sat amidships under an umbrella Berto's wife had lent her, wearing a plain gray dress and reading glasses Paloma had never seen before, annotating a ledger of agricultural import permits with the serene intensity of a woman auditing a neighbor's taxes. Nobody questioned a woman with a ledger. That had been Ines's idea. *Give me paperwork and I become furniture,* she'd said. *You're the one who can't sit still.*

She wasn't wrong. Paloma spent the first day coiling rope that didn't need coiling, scraping rust that would outlive them all, checking the fuel lines of Berto's ancient generator because hands needed work and idle hands checked horizons. Twice they caught themselves scanning the sky — habit of a lifetime — and twice they forced their eyes back down to the deck, where the only things falling were wood shavings.

"You'll wear a groove in your neck," Ines said, late afternoon, not looking up from her ledger.

"No one's looking for a deckhand."

"Someone's looking for a blue-and-white bush plane. You are no longer a bush plane. You're a person who used to be one. Those are different problems."

Paloma drove the scraper into the rust. "I've been different problems my whole life."

"Yes," Ines said mildly. "I know. I read your file."

"You read my—"

"The manslaughter charge. The falsified logbook. The passenger, what was his name—" She paused delicately. "Elias Ferrer."

The name landed in Paloma's chest like a dropped wrench. They hadn't heard anyone say it aloud since the night it happened. Everyone else in their life had agreed silently to pretend the man had never existed, and here was this archivist saying it on a barge deck like a line item.

"Don't," Paloma said.

"He'd be about fifty now," Ines went on, relentless, gentle. "If he'd lived."

"I said don't."

Ines closed the ledger. For a long moment the river filled the silence, slapping the hull, patient as arithmetic. "You told me on the sandbar," she said finally. "You don't get to take it back just because daylight makes it heavier. That's not how confession works."

"That's exactly how mine works."

"Well." She reopened the ledger. "Mine doesn't. So one of us is going to keep talking, and history suggests it will be me."

Paloma almost laughed. Almost. Instead they went back to the rust, and Ines went back to her permits, and the *Doña Remedios* slid downriver through the gold light with its secret cargo humming quietly in every drum, every floorboard, every hollow handle.

That night Paloma slept in the hold between pallets of timber, and dreamed of Elias Ferrer boarding a plane he should never have boarded, and woke with the taste of river water in their mouth.

* * *

The lock at Vado Seco came up on the second morning like a bad memory.

Every boat going downriver passed through it — a concrete throat in the river, gates fore and aft, and standing on the catwalk above the near gate, in matching khaki shirts that hadn't been issued by any government, were four men with clipboards.

Berto saw them before anyone else did. He was at the wheel, and he didn't say anything, but his shoulders changed, and Paloma had spent enough years reading pilots' shoulders to read a captain's.

"Meridian?" they murmured, coming up beside him.

"Contract security. They check papers at the locks since the flood. Officially it's customs." Berto spat over the rail, unhurried. "Unofficially it's whatever Meridian wants it to be. Usually it's a toll. Sometimes it's worse."

"What do they usually find worse?"

"People who look nervous." He glanced down at them, and his eyes were old river eyes, unimpressed by catastrophe. "So don't."

The gates closed behind them with a groan, and the water in the lock began to drop, and there was nowhere in the world to go.

Ines appeared at Paloma's elbow with the ledger open and her glasses on, and she looked, impossibly, bored. "Papers," she said. "Good. I've been rehearsing."

"You've been *what?*"

"Bureaucracy is a language," she said, watching the khaki shirts descend the ladder toward the deck. "My grandmother spoke three of them before they erased her country, and I decided early that if they could erase a nation with paperwork, I could resurrect one the same way. Watch."

The first agent reached them — big, sunburnt, clipboard held like a weapon he'd been assured was a tool. Behind him came a thinner one with a folder, and behind him, worst of all, a third man in civilian clothes with clean boots and the stillness of someone paid to notice things.

"Papers," the big one said.

Ines stepped forward before Berto could answer, adjusting her glasses. "Finally," she said, in a tone of exquisite administrative grievance. "We radioed ahead from Puente Claro on Monday. Manifest verification for the Ministry of Agriculture, Directorate of Import Control. Do you have the confirmation number or has it been lost again? Because I am *not* doing this form twice. Last time your office lost the duplicate, I lost two weeks, and my directorate lost a grant cycle."

The big agent blinked. The thin one flipped his folder. "Confirmation number?"

"AC-4471-DELTA," Ines said, without a heartbeat of hesitation, and recited a string of figures after it that sounded so much like real bureaucracy that Paloma felt their own pulse slow just hearing it. "Cross-referenced against manifest page two. Cargo is agricultural machinery parts, duty-exempt under Schedule Nine, and before you ask, yes, I have the exemption certificate, and no, you may not keep it, because the last officer who kept one is currently explaining himself to my directorate."

The thin agent actually flinched. He found the number in his folder. It was, by some miracle of Ines's preparation — or perhaps by the ordinary miracle of nobody ever checking anything — listed. Someone at Puente Claro had stamped something, weeks ago, for a shipment that may never have existed, and Ines Vidal had known it would be there the way she knew where every erased village lay along this river.

"Schedule Nine," the thin one repeated, mollified.

"Initial here, here, and here. Not there — that's for hazardous materials, and if you write in that box I will have to file a correction, and neither of us wants that." She handed over the pen like a queen dispensing mercy.

And it was working. It was genuinely working. The big agent was initialing, the thin one was stamping, the clean-booted man had drifted toward the drums but seemed satisfied by the smell of diesel and the tedium of it all —

—and then the fourth voice came from behind Paloma, from the bow, where they'd stationed themselves holding a mooring line like any other deckhand.

"*No. No way.*"

Paloma turned.

A man stood at the foot of the ladder, heavyset, sweating, wearing the uniform of a lock inspector and a face Paloma knew. Knew intimately. Señor Rubén Alcántara, rubber exporter, Puente Claro, who for two years running had paid Paloma in cash and contraband brandy to fly freight that never appeared on any manifest. Who had shared their table. Who had once, drunk, called Paloma *hermana* and meant it.

Who was staring at them now with dawning, delighted, catastrophic recognition.

"Paloma Montrose," Alcántara said. "Flying Montrose. On a *barge*—"

"Never heard of her," Paloma said, too fast, too high, and Alcántara's delight curdled instantly into confusion, because everyone on this river knew Flying Montrose, and Flying Montrose did not deny themselves.

The clean-booted man turned his head. Slowly. Like a heron deciding.

Ines moved.

She didn't run — she *strolled*, ledger under her arm, inserting herself into the gap between Paloma and the agents with the practiced ease of someone redirecting traffic. "Is there a problem with my crew?" she asked. "Because if there's a problem with my crew, there's a problem with my manifest, and if there's a problem with my manifest, I need it in writing, signed, today, with your name on it, and I will need three copies."

"It's—" Alcántara began.

"My deckhand," Ines said crisply. "Hired in Puente Claro eight days ago. References available. Is there a *problem*?"

The clean-booted man looked at Paloma. Looked at the scraper burns on their hands, the fish-oil jacket, the cap pulled low. And Paloma, whose lies had once come as easily as breathing, found the old fluency returning one word at a time, like an engine turning over cold.

"Deckhand since Puente Claro," they said. Flat. Dumb. A voice with no history in it. "Eight days. Never flown nothing in my life."

Silence. The lock water settled against the gates. Somewhere above them a bird screamed and got no answer.

Then the clean-booted man smiled — a small, professional smile — and nodded at the thin agent. "Manifest checks out. Stamp them through." And to Ines, almost courteously: "Your directorate sounds exhausting, señora."

"They are," Ines said. "Welcome to my life."

The gates opened. The *Doña Remedios* groaned forward out of the concrete throat into open water, and only when the lock was a kilometer astern did anyone breathe.

Alcántara found Paloma at the rail that evening, alone, and pressed something into their hand — a flask, cold and heavy, brandy inside. His voice was low and shaking.

"Flying Montrose, scrubbing decks and lying about it," he whispered. "Whatever you're in—" His eyes flicked toward the drums, toward the wheelhouse, toward everything. "I said nothing. I said *nothing*. But you get off this river, hermana. Whatever it is — get off."

He walked away before Paloma could answer.

Paloma stood at the rail a long time, flask untouched, watching the last light go off the water. Behind them, footsteps: Ines, wrapping her shawl tighter against the river damp.

"He recognized you," she said. "And he lied for you anyway. Why?"

Paloma turned the flask over in their hands. "Because two years of contraband makes us family," they said. "Or because people are better than I've been giving them credit for." A pause. "Either way, Ines — he saw me. At a Meridian checkpoint. If he talks to the wrong person, if that clean-boots man runs a deckhand description through Puente Claro—"

"Then we're ahead of them," Ines said. "By one day, maybe two."

"Maybe less." Paloma finally unscrewed the flask and drank, and the brandy burned like honesty. Ahead, far downriver, the capital's lights were beginning to prickle the dark horizon — a smear of orange against black, like a fire deciding how hungry to be.

"One more night," Ines said quietly. "One more day. Then ministries, stamps, judges."

"Then judges," Paloma echoed.

Neither of them mentioned the other thing waiting in the capital — the thing with no papers, no schedule, no lock to pass through. Neither of them said *the charge*, or *the trial*, or *seven years*. The river carried those words downstream faster than the barge could, and both of them knew that at the end of this water, Paloma Montrose would have to stop being nobody — permanently, publicly, on the record.

They drank to that in silence, the brandy passing between them, while the *Doña Remedios* pushed on through the dark toward the city of forgetting — and somewhere behind them, unseen, a radio crackled to life in a lock keeper's hut, and a voice described a deckhand with a pilot's hands.
