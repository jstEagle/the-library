## 18. Capital of Forgetting

The capital smelled of diesel and cut stone, the same as it had seven years ago when I'd landed here at night with a falsified logbook and a plan to disappear before dawn. It had grown since then—more glass on the ministry buildings, more checkpoints at the river gates—but underneath it was still a city built for forgetting. You could see it in the way the plazas were named: Reconciliation Square where the prison used to be, Unity Avenue where the old San Marisol quarter had stood before they renamed it twice and paved it once.

Ines carried the chest herself through the customs shed at the barge terminal, refusing every offer of help, her knuckles white on the rope handle. Toma's men had smuggled us upriver past two locks and a Meridian fuel depot, and we'd stepped off among crates of dried fish with nothing but our papers and each other.

"Ministry of Lands first," she said. "Then the archive. Then—" she checked a list she'd rewritten four times since the sandbar "—Judge Arriaga."

"And if the ministries don't want to know?"

"They'll have to know." She said it like a woman reciting a prayer she'd stopped believing but couldn't put down. "Expired stamps, lost files, officials who've never heard of San Marisol—I'm prepared for all of it."

I wasn't sure anyone could be prepared for it. But I'd learned something about Ines Vidal over three weeks and one night on a sandbar: she prepared for everything, and then the world surprised her anyway, and she got back up and prepared again.

* * *

The Ministry of Lands occupied a building that had once been a palace and now smelled like wet paper. We queued for ninety minutes behind a man trying to register a boundary dispute that had outlived him—his father had started it, he said, and he'd inherited it like furniture. At the counter sat a clerk with a green visor and the eyes of a man who had heard every story ever told about land and believed none of them.

Ines laid out her request with bureaucratic precision. Historical concession records. The Meridian grant of forty-one years ago. Underlying title documentation.

The clerk stamped something without looking up. "Concession files are held by the Concessions Directorate."

"The Concessions Directorate referred me here."

"Then there's been an error." He stamped something else. "File a correction request at Window Four."

"Window Four is closed."

"It reopens after lunch."

"It was closed after lunch yesterday," Ines said. "And the day before. Your own notice says temporary closure pending renovation. There's no renovation happening. There's a man playing cards in there."

The clerk finally looked up. He looked at Ines, and then at me, and then at the canvas bag where the corner of the chest showed through, and something moved behind his eyes—not sympathy exactly. Recognition. He'd seen people carrying things before. Things that made windows close.

"Madam," he said quietly, "the concession archives were transferred to regional storage eleven years ago. The regional facility flooded." A pause. "Everything from before the consolidation period is considered unrecoverable."

"You mean destroyed."

"I mean unrecoverable. There's a distinction. It matters to the auditors." He lowered his voice further. "If you possess historical materials of legal significance, Window Seven handles private deposits. Tuesdays only."

It was Monday. Ines thanked him with a courtesy so brittle it nearly cracked in her hands, and we walked out into a courtyard full of pigeons and a statue of someone whose face had worn smooth.

"Tuesdays only," she repeated. "Nineteen days until the permits expire. They're going to make us spend them standing in lines."

"That's how it works," I said. "You don't need to burn the documents anymore. You just need everyone who might care to die of paperwork first."

She turned on me, and for a second I thought she was going to say something sharp, but what came out was worse—quiet, almost wondering. "Is this what your seven years were like? Doors?"

"Some of them. Most of mine just didn't open at all." I nodded toward the gate. "Come on. Let's go see a judge."

* * *

Judge Tomás Arriaga worked out of a room the size of a generous closet in the old courthouse annex, because the new courthouse had been built by a construction consortium with Meridian money, and Arriaga had refused to move into it. Everyone knew this. It was the kind of integrity that made you famous and kept you poor, and in the capital it made him a curiosity—a judge known for never taking the convenient path, which meant he got the cases nobody wanted and the visitors nobody sent.

We waited three hours on a bench outside his door while clerks drifted past pretending not to look at us. Finally the door opened and a voice like gravel in honey said, "The archivist and the pilot. Come in before someone decides you're a security matter."

His office was books and a desk and a photograph of a river town on the wall—a town I recognized, because I'd flown medicine into it for six years. San Marisol. Nobody had taken the picture down; they'd just stopped saying what it was.

Arriaga poured coffee from a dented pot without asking. "Inés Vidal. Granddaughter of Elena Vidal, registrar of the San Marisol territorial survey. I signed the order that sealed those records when I was a young lawyer and too frightened to object." He set the pot down. "I've been waiting twenty years for someone to come ask me about it."

Ines opened the chest right there on his desk.

I watched his hands as she unwrapped the oilcloth—the hands of a man who had spent a lifetime touching other people's disasters—and they did not shake, but they went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes. Treaties signed under duress. The census of the disappeared villages. Photographs of officials shaking hands over documents that, according to every record the state preserved, had never existed.

"These are the originals," he said.

"With seals intact," Ines said. "Provenance documented. I can walk you through every page."

He was quiet for a long time. Outside, a trolley rang its bell. Somewhere down the hall a typewriter hammered away at some smaller injustice.

"Do you know what happens to documents like these?" Arriaga said at last. "Nothing. That's what happens. They sit in a chest. They sit in my drawer. They sit in an archive. Documents don't testify, Señora Vidal. Documents are evidence, and evidence requires a witness willing to stand in a courtroom and attach their name to it—publicly, permanently, with everything that follows." He looked at Ines. "You can petition. You can file. And Meridian's lawyers will bury it under procedural objections while their fixers work the other end, and in nineteen days the permits renew, and this goes back in a box for another generation. Unless."

"Unless," Ines said.

"Unless someone stands up in that hearing and testifies. Under oath. On the record. Someone the court cannot dismiss as a sentimental descendant or a foreign agitator. Someone with standing—or at least someone with enough truth in them that dismissing them becomes its own scandal."

* * *

Later—I don't remember walking down the stairs, only being halfway across the courtyard when it caught up with me—Ines took my arm.

"There's a witness provision," she said. "Any person with direct knowledge of relevant facts may testify at a permit review, even without standing. Even undocumented. Even—" she hesitated "—even under investigation. The court can't refuse a voluntary confession entered alongside material evidence. It's in the procedural code. Arriaga confirmed it."

"Say the rest of it."

"He means you, Paloma."

"I know he means me." I pulled free and kept walking, fast, toward the river gates because the river was the only thing in this city that pointed somewhere else. "A manslaughter charge. A falsified logbook. Seven years as a fugitive. You want me to walk into a courtroom full of Meridian lawyers and hand them all of it, wrapped, with a bow."

"I want you to hand them the truth," Ines said. "All of it. Because right now we have treaties and photographs and a census, and they will call it hearsay. They will call it forgery. They will say the seal proves nothing, that anyone could have buried a chest. But a witness—" her voice cracked and she steadied it "—a witness makes it real. That's what Arriaga is telling us. That's what he's been waiting twenty years to hear someone understand."

"So I confess to a killing and lose everything, and maybe the documents survive, and maybe they don't."

"Yes," she said. No softening it. She'd learned that about me on the sandbar—that I trusted people who didn't flinch more than people who did.

We stood at the rail above the river. Barges loaded with timber. Kids swimming off the pilings despite the signs. The current going about its business, taking everything downstream whether it was ready or not.

"If I do this," I said slowly, "Meridian doesn't need to kill me or buy me. I'll have done their work for them. I'll be a confessed criminal testifying alongside disputed evidence. Every word I say becomes suspect. Everything I touched becomes contaminated. Including the chest."

"Or," Ines said, "you become the only honest person in the building, and honesty has a way of making liars look tired."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. "You really believe that."

"I believe my grandmother believed that," she said. "She kept these documents safe through an erasure, through floods, through forty years of a country not existing. She believed a piece of paper with a true name on it could outlive an army. I'm not brave enough to believe less than she did."

* * *

That night I sat on the roof of the pension where Toma had stashed us, watching the lights along the river and doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the courthouse.

Seven years ago I'd run because staying meant trial, and trial meant the logbook, and the logbook meant admitting I'd flown overloaded to pay debts and watched a passenger die because of my own signature. Running had cost me everything except the running itself. It had cost me my name, my license, every friend who'd ever covered for me, and every morning the small cold work of becoming someone with no past.

And now the choice was back. Same shape, different courtroom. Run south with copies and hope, or stand up and say my name out loud in front of the whole machinery that had spent forty years grinding names smooth.

Below me, in the pension yard, Ines sat up late with the documents spread on a table, cataloguing, protecting, preparing for everything. My chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with engines.

There was no version of this where I stayed invisible. I understood that now. Either I surrendered myself on my terms, inside a courtroom, with the chest in evidence—or Meridian's men found me first, in some doorway, and the documents became a rumor and Ines became a widow of the cause and none of it had ever mattered.

The trap wasn't Meridian's. The trap was that there was only one honest option left, and it was shaped exactly like everything I'd spent seven years outrunning.

Down in the yard, Ines looked up as if she'd felt me decide, and raised the lamp a little, and waited.

I started down the stairs to tell her yes.
