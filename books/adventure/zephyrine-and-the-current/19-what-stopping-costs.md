## 19. What Stopping Costs

The fixer found Paloma at the Hotel Aurora's breakfast room, which told Paloma everything about how long they'd been watched. Nobody came to the Aurora for the food. The coffee tasted like it had been brewed during the previous administration, and the eggs had the texture of an apology from someone who didn't mean it.

He was already seated at the corner table when Paloma came down at seven, a cup in front of him and a newspaper folded to the shipping pages. Grey suit, good shoes, the kind of face you forgot while you were looking at it. He'd been on the barge at the lock—Paloma was almost sure of it now, remembering a man reading cargo manifests with the patience of someone who already knew what they said.

"Señor Montrose." He didn't stand. "Or should I say Señor Reyes? You've used both this week. I admire the discipline."

Paloma kept walking toward the door.

"The offer travels better sitting down." The man turned the newspaper around. On the back page, half-hidden by a fuel price listing, was a photograph of a blue-and-white bush plane on a sandbar, taken from altitude. Below it, a phone number. "It also travels worse. Your choice."

Paloma sat down. Not because of the photograph—the photograph changed nothing—but because standing in doorways was how people got followed into alleys, and alleys were where offers stopped being offers.

"I have coffee coming," Paloma said.

"You have forty minutes before your friend leaves for the courthouse district again." The man checked his watch, an expensive one worn like it was cheap. "So let's be efficient. My name is Corvo. I do difficult arithmetic for Meridian Concessions."

"What kind of arithmetic?"

"The kind where I add up what something costs to destroy versus what it costs to buy." He slid a plain envelope across the table, unsealed. "You've been reading the papers. You know the hearing is Thursday. You know Judge Ferrán has been assigned, and you know what that means. Meridian knows what it means too. So we arrive at the question: what is the chest worth to you?"

Paloma didn't touch the envelope. "You're asking the wrong person. It's not mine."

"No. It's hers. But she can't fly it anywhere, and you can't land it anywhere, and every airstrip within six hundred kilometers has been given your tail number and a description of two faces." Corvo smiled without warmth. "You are, at present, the most well-documented fugitives in the country. Congratulations. That took resources. Resources make people impatient."

Inside the envelope: a bank draft, made out to cash, for a number with enough zeros that Paloma's eyes did the thing eyes do—stuck for a second, recalculating seven years of engine overhauls paid for in installments, of medicine runs flown at cost because the mission clinics couldn't pay more, of sleeping in the plane because hotels were paper trails.

"There's more than money," Corvo said. "There's the charge. Manslaughter, 2019, Puerto Cielo district. A passenger named Elena Duarte. A logbook amended after the fact." He said it flatly, no cruelty in it, which somehow made it crueler. "We've had that file for eleven days. We could have used it sooner. We chose not to, because threats are crude instruments and we prefer to start with gifts. Here is the gift: the draft, a passport under any name you like, a charter contract in a neighboring country that pays well and asks nothing. And the file—" he tapped his temple, "—goes into a drawer. Not destroyed. Drawers can be reopened. But drawers stay shut for men who cooperate."

"And if I don't?"

"Then Thursday morning the prosecutor receives the file, the draft goes back in the vault, and you get your trial. Both trials. The one you want and the one you don't." He folded his hands. "I'm told the manslaughter sentencing guidelines here run four to eight years, mitigated by surrender. Add obstruction, flight from justice, falsification of records. Call it eight to twelve, served consecutively, in a facility where the concession company holds the catering contract. I don't say that as a threat. I say it as arithmetic."

Paloma looked at the draft. Seven years ago, a number like that would have bought the whole escape before it started—would have paid off the debts that put the extra weight on the plane in the first place, would have kept Elena Duarte's name out of a coroner's report, would have—

No. That wasn't true, and Paloma knew it wasn't true, and knowing it was the whole point of the last three weeks. The debts hadn't been the weight. The lie had been the weight. Everything since had been interest.

"You're quiet," Corvo observed.

"I'm doing arithmetic too."

"Then you'll want time. You have until Wednesday midnight." He stood, left a bill under his untouched cup, and paused at the door just long enough to say, without turning around: "For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much—I've read the Duarte file twice. It reads like a man who made one bad decision and then spent seven years making the same one over and over, faster. Some of us notice these things."

Then he was gone, and the eggs arrived, and Paloma ate them anyway because the body keeps its own ledger regardless of what the soul is deciding.

* * *

Paloma walked.

Not toward anything. The city in the late morning was all noise and diesel and fruit sellers shouting prices that changed by the hour, and Paloma moved through it the way water moves through a net—present everywhere, held by nothing. Past the ministry buildings with their flags going soft in the heat. Past the courthouse district, where Ines was somewhere inside right now, arguing with clerks about certified copies, her grandmother's name spoken aloud in government rooms for the first time in forty years. Paloma didn't go in. There were photographers near the steps, and a blue-and-white bush plane pilot was a recognizable animal now.

Past the riverfront, where the water ran brown and patient, doing the only thing it knew how to do.

Seven years. Paloma tried to hold the number honestly, the way you check a load manifest twice. Seven years of no names. No stops longer than an hour. No questions answered. Seven years of flying medicine to villages that never asked where the pilot slept, of being competent and useful and completely alone, of telling Toma everything except the truth, of watching Ines Vidal open a chest in a derelict mission and go white at a seal and choose—instantly, without hesitation—to walk toward the danger instead of away from it.

That was the part that wouldn't sit still. Not the fear. The ease. Ines had looked at that seal and her hands had shaken and her voice had not. She'd been running from erasure her whole life and the moment she had a weapon against it she'd picked it up like it weighed nothing.

And Paloma had picked up the chest too. Had carried it through smoke and bullets and a storm, had hidden it under fish crates on a barge, had lied to agents at a lock with a steady voice. For her sake. For the documents' sake. For the villages in the photographs whose names Paloma had read aloud once, at night, on the sandbar, and heard one of them answer back across seven years like a bell struck underwater.

San Rafael de las Corrientes. The settlement where the overloaded charter went down. Where Elena Duarte died. It had been in the census of disappeared villages, line forty-one, population 212, date of removal: the year the concessions began. The company hadn't just forged treaties. It had erased the place where Paloma's own crime happened, wiped it from the record so thoroughly that even the manslaughter trial had referenced a location that officially no longer existed—which was part of why the case had gone soft, witnesses scattered, the site itself unfindable, the whole tragedy dissolved into paperwork fog.

Meridian had buried Paloma's accident before it happened. They just hadn't known they'd done it.

Paloma stood at the river railing a long time, working that through, feeling it rearrange things.

* * *

Evening came, and the streetlights, and the smell of frying plantain from the corner stands. Paloma walked through the old quarter, past shuttered consulates and a church with a cracked bell, and did the accounting properly, line by line, the way you'd weigh a plane before a short-field takeoff.

Option one: take the draft. Fly out tomorrow under a new name, chest abandoned or handed over or burned—it hardly mattered which, because the result was identical. Live. Genuinely live, with money, with the file in a drawer instead of a warrant in a system. Keep flying. Keep being good at the only thing that had ever made the loneliness bearable.

Cost: Ines's family stayed erased. Two hundred and twelve people in San Rafael stayed erased. Forty years of forged treaties stayed load-bearing under every concession permit in the interior. And the drawer stayed shut only as long as Paloma stayed useful, and usefulness expires, and then the file comes out anyway, later, when there's no chest and no leverage and no judge named Ferrán waiting with a reputation for integrity.

Corvo had said it himself, almost kindly: some of us notice these things. Meridian noticed everything. That was their entire business model. Buying silence wasn't mercy; it was deferred payment.

Option two: Thursday morning, walk into the courthouse, surrender, testify, hand over the chest and the copies and the truth about the logbook, all of it, in one motion, in front of the press and the judge and whatever cameras Meridian couldn't keep out.

Cost: eight to twelve years, maybe. Maybe less—Ferrán was known for weighing confession heavily, and the evidence Paloma carried would gut the company's entire legal position, and prosecutors were politicians underneath, and politicians loved a gift-wrapped scandal. Maybe four. Maybe community service and a suspended license and a lifetime of being the pilot who testified. Maybe more. The number was not knowable in advance, and that was precisely what made it terrifying, and precisely what made it honest. Every other number in Paloma's life had been negotiated in advance. This one would be earned.

But the chest survived. The names came back onto maps. Ines's grandmother became a person again in the official record instead of a rumor in a family's memory. And Elena Duarte—Elena got what she'd never gotten, which was a pilot who stood in a room and said her name and said *it was my fault* and did not leave the room afterward.

Paloma stopped under a streetlight outside the church and laughed, once, without much humor in it, because the arithmetic had finally finished and the answer was embarrassing in its simplicity.

Running had always been the more expensive choice. Seven years of payments on a debt that never reduced the principal. Every hour of invisibility had cost exactly as much as the original crime, compounding daily, and the interest had been loneliness, and the collateral had been everyone who might have been a friend if Paloma had ever stayed in one place long enough to become one. Toma. The nuns at the mission clinic who remembered the medicine runs. Even Ines—especially Ines—who had traded truths on a sandbar with a stranger because the stranger's lies had finally gotten heavy enough to set down.

The draft in the envelope wasn't an offer. It was a bill. Meridian was offering to pay Paloma to keep paying them forever.

A woman selling roasted corn nearby glanced over at the sound of the laugh. Paloma bought a cob from her, overpaid, ate it standing up, and watched the river darkening past the rooftops, going wherever rivers go, carrying everything and holding nothing.

* * *

Midnight found the hotel again, but Paloma walked past it, around the block, checking the way professionals check—not because anyone was following, but because tomorrow there would be no more checking, ever, and it seemed right to do it one last time correctly.

Ines's light was on in her room, third floor. Through the gap in the curtains: her silhouette at the desk, still working, still cataloguing, still treating the documents with the tenderness other people reserved for children. Paloma stood in the street below for a moment, envelope in jacket pocket, draft inside it, and thought about the weight of paper. Everyone talked about the weight of gold, the weight of lead, the weight of engines. Nobody warned you about paper. Forty grams a sheet, and it had nearly sunk a plane, a friendship, a nation.

Wednesday. One day left on Corvo's clock, and nineteen days had become one, and the permit hearing was Thursday at nine.

Paloma climbed the stairs quietly, knocked once on Ines's door, and when she opened it—glasses on, hair down, looking older and younger at once—held out the envelope unopened.

"He offered me everything," Paloma said. "Money, a new name, my old charge buried. All of it, if the chest disappears by midnight tomorrow."

Ines went very still. "And you brought it to me because—"

"Because I need a witness." Paloma's voice was steadier than it had any right to be. "Tomorrow I'm going to tear this up in front of you, and then I'm going to call the prosecutor's office myself, before Meridian decides midnight means something else. And Thursday morning I'm going to stand up in front of Judge Ferrán and tell him about a falsified logbook and a woman named Elena Duarte and a village called San Rafael de las Corrientes, and I'm going to keep talking until they either believe me or arrest me. Probably both."

Ines looked at the envelope, then at Paloma, and something in her face shifted—the archivist's look, the one that assessed documents for authenticity. Whatever she saw passed inspection.

"They'll take your license," she said. "Maybe years."

"I know what stopping costs." Paloma stepped into the room and closed the door on the city, on the river, on seven years of nowhere. "I've been paying it since 2019. I'd like to settle the account."

Ines sat down slowly at the desk, pulled the blank paper toward her, and began, in her flawless bureaucratic hand, to draft the letter to the prosecutor—witness list, exhibit inventory, voluntary surrender, requested hearing date. Her pen didn't hesitate once.

Outside, far off toward the port, a ship's horn sounded, long and low, the way they do when they're leaving—or when they're finally, after a very long time, coming home.
