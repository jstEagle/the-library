## 20. Testimony

The hearing room smelled of floor wax and old paper, and Paloma thought: I have landed in worse places than this. It was a strange comfort, but it held. Seven years of bad airstrips had taught them to read a room the way they read weather—by what it was about to do.

The Ministry of Lands and Concessions occupied the fourth floor of a building that had been a bank before the war, then a hospital, then whatever it was now. The hearing chamber kept its marble teller counters along one wall like a scar it was proud of. A public gallery ran along the back behind a rail of dark wood, half-filled with clerks who had come for the drama and journalists Ines had promised copies to, and—Paloma spotted her in the third row, hands folded, spine straight as a surveyor's plumb line—a nun from the mission clinic upriver.

Ines sat at the petitioner's table with the chest open beside her, documents fanned out in protective plastic she'd bought from a stationery shop two days ago. She wore the same gray suit she'd worn into the interior, pressed now, and she looked exactly like what she was: an archivist with forty years of paper and nowhere left to be quiet about it.

Paloma stood at the back by the doors, wearing their only clean shirt, hands empty on purpose. No bag. No plane waiting on a strip outside. That had been the whole point of last night, walking the city until dawn made the decision for them by arriving.

The presiding officer was Judge Anselmo Reyes—the judge Ines had chosen, known for integrity, which in this capital meant known for having survived three governments without bending enough to break. He was small and gray and he looked at Paloma first, before anyone else spoke, as if he'd already read the room's manifest.

"Before we begin," Reyes said, "the record should note that Meridian Concessions has petitioned to adjourn this hearing pending—"

"Pending nothing," said the Meridian counsel, a broad man named Ferrero with a voice built for boardrooms. "Pending verification of the provenance of certain documents whose chain of custody is—"

"The chain of custody," Reyes said mildly, "is a woman who has spent eleven years cataloguing colonial archives, sitting right there with her credentials. We'll get to your objections, Counselor. First—" and here his eyes came back to the door, to Paloma, patient and terrible—"there appears to be someone standing who hasn't been called."

The room turned. Paloma felt every eye the way you feel crosswind on final approach—not dangerous yet, but full of information.

They walked down the center aisle at an unhurried pace, because hurrying would have looked like running, and running was over. At the rail they stopped, and heard themselves say, in a voice steadier than they had any right to expect:

"I'm surrendering."

The word went through the room like a stone through still water. Somewhere in the gallery a pen stopped moving.

"There's a warrant," Paloma said. "Manslaughter. Seven years old. Falsified logbook, falsified weight-and-balance, a charter flight out of Puerto Cielo that killed a passenger named Augusto Ren." The name came out whole, unbroken, the way it never had in seven years of saying it silently. "I flew the plane. I signed the logbook. I chose to leave rather than stand trial. I'm done choosing that."

Reyes didn't move for a long moment. Then he said, quietly, "Court security is outside. You'll wait there until we call you." And then, softer, almost off the record: "You could have waited until the end of proceedings."

"No, Your Honor," Paloma said. "That's how it starts. Waiting until the end."

* * *

They took the statement in a small room off the chamber, a court clerk with a typewriter older than the building's second life, a bailiff by the door, Reyes himself presiding because he wanted it firsthand. Paloma told all of it. Not the version rehearsed in the dark of the sandbar night, not the version shaped for sympathy or strategy—the actual thing, in order.

The debts after the family's medical bills. The charter offer that paid triple because no one else would take four passengers and freight in a plane rated for less. The storm cell that wasn't on any forecast. The overloaded takeoff roll that went on too long, the trees at the end of the strip, the stall, the ground coming up honest and final. Augusto Ren, a schoolteacher going home, dead on impact. Paloma crawling out with a broken collarbone and a head full of static, and then—the part that had lived under their tongue for seven years, tasting of copper—

"And then I walked to a village, borrowed a phone, called nobody, and opened the logbook with a pen."

"Why?" Reyes asked. Not cruelly. Like a man checking a load calculation.

"Because if I wrote the truth, I'd have to stay for what came after. And staying meant—" Paloma looked at their own hands, spread flat on the table where the clerk could see them weren't shaking. "I'd spent my whole life moving. Flying away from things was the one skill I trusted. So I did the thing I knew how to do. Which turns out to be the definition of cowardice, when you write it down honestly."

The clerk's keys clattered. The bailiff shifted. Outside, rain had started against the tall windows, soft as static.

"What happened to the aircraft?"

"Wrecked on site. I reported it as weather, weeks later, from a town two rivers away. They found it. They found him. They just never found me."

"Until now."

"Until now," Paloma agreed. "Your Honor, I need to ask for something, and I know how it sounds."

Reyes folded his hands. "Ask."

"The reason I'm here today—the reason I walked in instead of being dragged—is sitting on that table in your hearing chamber. Forty-one kilograms of paper that prove Meridian Concessions' land grants are forged, that San Marisol existed and was erased, that treaties were signed at gunpoint and then burned from the record. If I go to a cell before this hearing finishes, those lawyers will bury that chest so deep it'll take another forty years to dig up. Ines Vidal will be discredited by Friday. I'm asking to testify. Publicly. While I'm still—" Paloma smiled, and it hurt. "While I'm still a free man for another hour or two."

Reyes studied them for a long moment. Rain ticked on the glass.

"You understand," he said, "that confessing does not purchase leniency. That is not how I work."

"I understand that better than anything, Your Honor. I'm not buying anything. I'm paying."

Something moved behind the old judge's face—approval, maybe, or just recognition. He rose. "Then let's not waste your hour."

* * *

The hearing chamber, when they re-entered it, had changed temperature entirely. Word had traveled; the gallery was full now, standing room, and Ferrero was on his feet objecting before the doors closed behind the bailiff.

"Your Honor, this is theater. This individual is a confessed felon with an obvious motive to—"

"To what, Counselor?" Ines was on her feet too, and Paloma saw for the first time what eleven years in archives had built in her: a patience with teeth. "To hand you the exact evidence your company has spent six weeks and at least two armed enforcement teams trying to destroy? Yes. Devastating motive. Let's examine the documents anyway."

Reyes rapped once for order. "Ms. Vidal, proceed. Document by document. Slowly. Mr.—" a glance at his notes— "Montrose. You'll testify after, under oath, and everything you say goes into the transcript that Counselor Ferrero will wish, by the end of the day, did not exist."

What followed took four hours, and Paloma watched it from the witness bench like watching weather build across a valley—inexorable, magnificent, and impossible to stop.

Ines laid out the treaty of 1958 first. Original signature page, ink analysis already commissioned overnight by the court's own chemist, seal of San Marisol pressed in wax that predated the republic's annexation-by-merger. Beside it, the "original" grant Meridian's title chain rested on—and its date was wrong by nine months, its signatory dead before the signing ceremony his own signature commemorated, the paper itself machine-made in a mill that hadn't operated until a decade after the grant date.

Ferrero tried twice more to interrupt. The second time, Reyes said, "Counselor, every time you object to evidence, you make me curious about why. Continue, Ms. Vidal."

The census came next. Villages listed by name, population, year of last contact. Eleven villages. Four marked *relocated*, seven marked simply *closed*, in the bureaucratic shorthand that means disappeared. Ines read the names aloud, each one, slowly, and the gallery went so quiet Paloma could hear the rain.

And then she reached the photograph folder, and Paloma's stomach dropped through the floor, because they knew what was coming and couldn't stop it.

"These are aerial surveys," Ines said, "conducted by the concession company itself in 1961, documenting the villages prior to 'resettlement.'" She held one up toward the bench. "This is the settlement of Río Claro."

Paloma had delivered medicine to Río Claro for three years. Paloma's charter had gone out of Río Claro the night of the accident. The name was in the chest, in the census, in a document Meridian had buried in a riverbank—and the room was about to learn that the confessed criminal testifying in ten minutes had been flying into these erased villages for years, which cut both ways, and everyone in the gallery understood it at the same moment Paloma did.

"Your witness, Ms. Vidal," Reyes said. "Or shall we hear from Mr. Montrose?"

"We'll hear from him," Ines said, and her eyes found Paloma's, and what passed between them wasn't reassurance. It was permission. *Tell it true. All of it. Even the parts that hurt us.*

Paloma took the oath with their hand on a book of laws instead of scripture, and found, oddly, that this suited them fine.

* * *

The testimony took ninety minutes and covered seven years. Paloma gave it straight down the middle—no varnish, no self-pity, and crucially, no hiding. The manslaughter. The falsified logbook. The flight from jurisdiction. And then the part Ferrero had been waiting for:

"So you admit," Ferrero said, on his feet during questioning, "that you are a fugitive who discovered these documents under circumstances involving salvage of property in disputed waters, that you evaded a checkpoint operated lawfully by Meridian Concessions' contracted security personnel, that you received assistance from individuals now exposed to legal jeopardy because of you, and that your entire testimony today is constructed to trade your cooperation for leniency. Why should this court credit a single word from a man with everything to gain?"

Paloma considered the question honestly, the way you consider a fuel gauge reading lower than you'd like.

"Because everything I've done for seven years was designed to avoid this exact chair," they said. "If I were constructing testimony for leniency, I'd have constructed something cheaper than confessing to manslaughter. Counselor, I've read the arithmetic. Confession costs me. What I want you to explain—what I'd genuinely like to hear—is what Meridian gains from these documents being real, because from where I sit, your entire title chain dies the moment the court accepts them, and everything you've done since the river gave that chest back tells me your company knows it."

"That is not—that is speculation—"

"It's testimony from experience," Paloma said. "I used to work for people like you. Before the accident. Cargo that didn't get logged, manifests that didn't match contents, flights into villages that weren't on any permit. I didn't ask questions then either. It cost a man his life and it cost me seven years. I recognize the shape of the operation, Counselor. I recognized your floatplane crew's discipline on the sandbar. Professionals. Somebody trained them. Somebody signed their expense accounts." Paloma turned to the bench. "Your Honor, I'd suggest the court subpoena Meridian's internal correspondence from the past six weeks. Reward postings for a blue-and-white bush plane aren't the act of a company confident in its own paperwork."

The silence afterward had texture. In the gallery, the nun from the clinic was crying without any noise at all, and the journalists were writing like the pens were on fire.

Ferrero sat down. It was the sitting-down that finished it, Paloma thought later—the particular deflation of a man realizing the flood has receded and everything he buried is lying in the mud, gleaming, in plain view of everybody.

Judge Reyes let the silence run a full ten seconds. Then:

"Court finds sufficient grounds to admit the San Marisol documents into evidence. Court further orders subpoenas issued to Meridian Concessions for internal records relevant to the provenance of the 1958 grant instruments. Court notes for the record that the permit applications currently under review are suspended pending resolution of underlying title questions." He removed his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose, suddenly looking every one of his years and somehow lighter for it. "Finally. The bench acknowledges the voluntary surrender of Paloma Montrose, and refers the matter of the 2017 warrant to this court for disposition following conclusion of these proceedings." His eyes lifted, found Paloma's. "Mr. Montrose. You will remain available. You are not free to leave the district."

"Understood, Your Honor." Paloma stood. Their knees ached from the bench, their throat was raw, and underneath all of it was a feeling they had no name for yet—something like the stillness after touchdown, wheels weighted, engine winding down, the strange quiet of having actually arrived somewhere.

Outside the chamber, while clerks packed the chest under guard and Ines argued gently with a journalist about embargo timing, the bailiff stepped close and touched Paloma's elbow.

"Processing tomorrow morning, eight o'clock. Don't make us come find you."

"I'll be there," Paloma said, and meant it, and the meaning of it settled over them like ballast—heavy, stabilizing, theirs.

Ines appeared at their side. For a moment neither spoke. Down the corridor, Ferrero was on his phone already, voice low and fast, and somewhere in the upper floors of this building men were shredding paper or failing to shred it fast enough.

"You knew," Ines said finally. "When you walked in this morning. You knew you might talk your way into a cell."

"I knew I'd already lived seven years in one," Paloma said. "It just had more sky."

Ines laughed—an actual laugh, brief and cracked—and then pressed the back of her hand briefly against Paloma's sleeve, the archivist's equivalent of an embrace. "Eight o'clock," she said. "I'll be there. Somebody should witness it properly."

She walked off down the corridor with her gray suit and her forty years of paper, and Paloma stood alone in the marble light of a building that used to be a bank, waiting—for the first time in seven years—not to leave.
