## 21. Sentencing

The courtroom smelled of floor wax and old paper, and Paloma sat in it for four hours before anyone said their name.

They had dressed for it the way you dress for weather you don't trust—a shirt with a collar, borrowed from Toma, still carrying faintly of diesel and river. Ines had pressed it herself the night before with an iron she'd found somewhere, and neither of them had commented on that. Some kindnesses are easier to survive unacknowledged.

Judge Ferreira read from a folder without hurry. He was a heavy man with reading glasses too small for his face and a voice like gravel poured slowly. Around them the gallery held maybe forty people: lawyers for Meridian Concessions in gray suits arranged like a defensive formation, two journalists Ines trusted, a cluster of San Marisolan descendants who had come up from the capital's southern districts wearing white flowers pinned at the shoulder. The nuns had sent Mother Superior Cecilia herself, which surprised Paloma until they remembered that Cecilia had never once done the expected thing.

"—the respondent, Paloma Montrose," Ferreira said, "is charged with falsification of flight records, operation of an overloaded aircraft contrary to airworthiness directives, and the death of one passenger, Mr. Elias Renard, occurring seven years ago this month."

The name landed in Paloma's chest like ballast dropped from height. *Elias Renard.* They hadn't heard the name spoken aloud since the night it happened. Everyone since—the traders, the fuel men, the company enforcers—had known them as a rumor, a callsign, a blue-and-white plane on the horizon. A person without a past is convenient for everyone. Hearing the dead man's full name spoken into a microphone, recorded by a court, written into a transcript that would exist forever, felt less like punishment than like being handed back a piece of themselves they'd forgotten they'd left behind.

"Montrose. Stand."

Paloma stood.

"You confessed voluntarily. You surrendered yourself to this court carrying evidence you were under no obligation to preserve, at considerable personal risk, while actively pursued by parties with significant resources." Ferreira looked up over the glasses. "Is that accurate?"

"Yes, sir."

"Did anyone pay you to do this?"

A ripple of something—laughter, or its suppressed cousin—from the gallery. Even Ferreira's mouth moved a millimeter.

"No, sir."

"Did anyone threaten you into it?"

"The opposite, sir."

Ferreira nodded as if this confirmed a long-held suspicion about the world. He turned pages. The courtroom waited. Outside the high windows the capital went on being loud—buses, a vendor's bell, the particular honk of a city that has never once been patient—and inside, everything held still around the sound of paper turning.

* * *

The hearing on the concession claims had ended three days earlier, and it had not so much concluded as collapsed.

Paloma had watched it from the public benches beside Ines, watching the forged treaties go up on the projection screen one after another—the 1954 Grazing Concession, the 1961 Timber Compact, the 1968 Mineral Trust—each bearing signatures of San Marisolan chiefs who, the archival record now made plain, had been dead before the ink dried, or illiterate, or eleven years old. Each treaty "witnessed" by colonial administrators whose own dispatches, recovered from the mission station files, described the signing ceremonies as negotiations conducted at gunpoint over villages already burning.

Meridian's lead counsel had stood at the podium for forty minutes arguing procedural objections—that the documents' provenance was irregular, that an archivist's chain of custody did not constitute legal authentication, that testimony from a confessed felon could not be weighted equally with corporate records. And then Mateo Vidal had walked in through the public doors carrying a banker's box, and set it down on the counsel table without asking permission, and said, in a voice that carried further than his slight frame suggested it should:

"I'm Mateo Vidal. Until yesterday I was senior counsel in Meridian's legal office. These are internal memoranda, twelve years of them, documenting that the company's own due-diligence review in 2011 concluded the treaties were likely fabricated—and that the finding was buried because the concessions were collateralized against the parent company's debt restructuring."

He had worn no white flower. He had worn his gray suit like everyone else's, and he had stood there looking like exactly what he was—one of them, turned inside out—and Paloma had understood, watching Ines's hands grip the bench rail, that whatever else this cost him, he had chosen it the way Paloma had chosen the courtroom door: standing still, finally, in the one place running could never take you.

The permits didn't expire quietly. They died in open session, revoked one by one while the gray suits packed their leather folios with the faces of men doing arithmetic about severance packages.

* * *

Now Ferreira closed the folder, and took off the glasses, and looked at Paloma directly for the first time.

"I have read your confession in full. All thirty-one pages. I note that you did not soften it." He paused. "You wrote that Mr. Renard asked you twice whether the load was within limits, and that you lied both times, and that you told him the fog would lift by the ridge line when you had no instrument rating for night conditions and no current weather report. Is that accurate?"

"It's accurate."

"Why write it that way? Most people in your position would have—" he gestured vaguely, "—compressed. Abbreviated. Made themselves smaller in the account."

Paloma had thought about this question every night for weeks, in the mission clinic, on the barge, in the cell that wasn't really a cell because Judge Ferreira had arranged for them to be held at a church hostel rather than the remand block—an arrangement that scandalized exactly the right people. They thought about it now, standing, collar itching, forty faces waiting.

"Because I spent seven years telling the story small," Paloma said. "Small enough to live with. And it nearly killed me anyway. So this time I told it the size it actually was."

Silence. Somewhere in the gallery a woman with a white flower exhaled audibly.

Ferreira put the glasses back on. "Here is what weighs in this room," he said. "A falsified logbook is a crime. An overloaded aircraft is a crime. A dead passenger is a tragedy, and the law does not distinguish well between tragedy and crime, which is a failure of the law, not of you. Against all of that, we place the following: voluntary surrender. Full confession without mitigation. And the recovery and presentation of documentary evidence that has resulted in the voiding of land claims affecting—" he checked a page, though Paloma suspected he knew the number cold, "—four hundred and eleven square kilometers, and the restoration of nationality records to some nine thousand persons, several hundred of whom are in this jurisdiction and have been, until last week, legally nonexistent."

He let that sit.

"The law requires me to sentence you. It also permits me to weigh what you brought in with you against what you carried out seven years ago. I am permitted to do that, and I am going to do it, and there will be letters to the editor about it, and I have read worse."

He announced it plainly, without theater, which somehow made it heavier:

"License suspension, aviation, duration eighteen months, commencing today. Community service, five hundred hours, assigned to the civil aviation authority's safety-education program—you will teach, Montrose, which I suspect will be more punishing than any labor. Restitution to the Renard family trust, per the schedule agreed with their solicitor, whom I note is present and has signed off." A thin man near the aisle raised a hand briefly; his face was unreadable and Paloma couldn't look at it for long. "No custodial term. Probation, five years, conditions attached in writing."

Ferreira removed the glasses again and folded them, and for a moment he was just a tired man at the end of a long case.

"For the record, and because I believe the record matters: the court does not consider this a light sentence. The court considers it a proportionate one. There is a difference, and you will feel the difference, and that feeling is part of the sentence." He struck the desk once with a small wooden gavel that seemed almost apologetic. "It is done."

* * *

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, the light was the flat gold of late afternoon and the city noise came back all at once, like water filling a hull.

Ines found them before the journalists did. She was holding a document folder against her chest the way she'd held it on the sandbar, out of habit, though there was nothing left in it that anyone wanted to steal. Her family records had come through that morning—certified, stamped, entered into the national registry. Nine thousand names. Her grandmother's name among them, restored to a country that had spent forty years insisting she'd never existed.

"They gave me a copy," Ines said. "Official. With the seal." She laughed, and it cracked halfway through. "It's just paper. It's the same information I've had my whole life, that my family kept alive in kitchens and hymnals and—"

"And now it's true," Paloma said.

"Now it's *on file,*" Ines said, wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist, furious at herself for crying, which was the most Ines thing Paloma had ever seen her do. "There's a difference. God help me, after all this, I've learned there's a difference."

Mateo came down the steps behind her, carrying the empty banker's box under one arm like a man who no longer knew what to do with his hands. He stopped a careful distance away. The three of them stood in a triangle that six months ago would have been impossible.

"I resigned formally Friday," he said. "Effective immediately. They offered me a retention package that would have funded a very comfortable retirement." He looked at the box. "I filed the memos instead. All of them. Including the ones that implicate the office I ran."

"Mateo," Ines started.

"Don't," he said—not harshly, just finished. "I did the math a long time ago, Ines. I just kept getting the answer wrong." He extended his hand to Paloma, and after a beat Paloma took it. His grip was dry and brief and honest. "For what it's worth—I read your confession. It was circulated internally during the hearing. My former colleagues considered it a tactical masterstroke, a calculated play for leniency." His mouth twisted. "I've read a lot of calculated documents. That wasn't one."

He left them there, walking down the steps into the crowd with his empty box, and Ines watched him go until the crowd took him, and neither sister said anything, because some reconciliations are still under construction and words poured on wet concrete only make marks.

Toma appeared at the bottom of the stairs leaning against a lamppost as if he'd been there for hours, which he probably had. He didn't congratulate anyone. He looked Paloma up and down—the collared shirt, the free hands, the strange lightness of a person no longer carrying anything illegal or otherwise.

"So," he said. "Eighteen months grounded."

"Eighteen months."

"What does a bush pilot do for eighteen months in a country where everyone knows their face?"

"Teaches, apparently. Safety education." Paloma grimaced. "I'm going to stand in front of rooms full of young pilots and tell them not to lie about their weight and balance."

Toma considered this with the gravity he gave all commercial matters. "Terrible business model," he said. "You'll be giving away for free the exact thing I charge for." Then, gruffly, clapping Paloma once on the shoulder hard enough to mean it: "Five hundred hours. Come drink one beer when you hit two-fifty. Not before. I don't celebrate unfinished work."

* * *

That evening they walked out to the airfield—not to fly, there was nothing to fly, the blue-and-white Cessna belonged to Toma now and always would—but to look at it, parked on Toma's tie-down strip at the edge of the river field, catching the last low sun.

Ines stood beside Paloma at the fence for a long time.

"Eighteen months," she said eventually. "And then?"

"And then I don't know." Paloma turned the wire of the fence idly between finger and thumb. "Seven years I knew exactly what I was doing every day. Fuel, cargo, weather, gone. Now the whole map's blank and I keep reaching for the old one."

"The old one's burned."

"The old one's burned," Paloma agreed. "That was sort of the point of all this."

Ines dug in her bag and produced a letter—real paper, official letterhead, the seal of the national archives commission. She held it out.

"This came to the hostel this afternoon. Addressed to both of us." She watched Paloma take it. "The repatriation project. The restored villages want their records returned—physically, ceremonially, by air if possible. The archive can't spare a crew. But they're authorized to contract licensed civilian transport." She paused, letting the trap in the word land. "*Licensed.*"

Paloma read the letter twice. Sixteen months from now, it would be legal. Sixteen months of teaching weight-and-balance to teenagers who wouldn't listen, of restitution payments deducted monthly, of probation check-ins with a supervisor who smelled of peppermint. Sixteen months of standing still.

And then a flight north, upriver, with a hold full of homecoming.

"There's a village on the manifest," Ines said quietly. "San Rafael de la Corriente. It's listed in the census of disappeared villages. It's also—" she hesitated, first time Paloma had ever heard her hesitate over a document, "—it's the settlement where your accident happened. They're rebuilding. They requested their records first."

Paloma looked at the plane across the field, blue and white in the dying light, and felt the old current moving underneath everything—not pulling them away anymore, not chasing them anywhere. Just moving. The way a river moves when you stop fighting it and put your oar in.

"Sixteen months," Paloma said.

"Sixteen months," Ines said. "Think you can stand still that long?"

Paloma folded the letter carefully along its crease and put it in the breast pocket, over the heart, where pilots keep the things they intend to keep.

"I flew seven years without ever once arriving anywhere," they said. "I think I can manage sixteen months of learning how."
