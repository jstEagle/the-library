## 22. The Current Again

The river had dropped back into its banks by the time they flew north, and from two thousand feet it looked the way it always had—brown, patient, indifferent to everything that had happened on it. Paloma found that comforting in a way they couldn't have explained a year ago. The river didn't hold grudges. It just carried things.

The Cessna was different now. New engine, overhauled twice since the trial, painted over so many times the blue-and-white had become something closer to a bruise. But it was theirs again, legally theirs, license hanging on a nail above the fuel drum at the airstrip like a certificate of survival. Paloma still touched it sometimes when they walked past. Just to check it was real.

Ines sat in the right seat with the chest between her feet, exactly where it had ridden the first time, except now it was open half the time and she was cataloguing as they flew, calling out contents to herself in a low voice: *treaty of the lower confluence, 1931; census fragment, San Rafael district; photograph envelope, forty-one images.* She'd been doing this for three days straight, on the ground and in the air, and she hadn't tired of it once. Some people got energy from sleep. Ines Vidal got it from paper.

"Village is rebuilt," she said, not looking up. "Forty-two houses. A school. They put the school first, did you know that? Before the clinic."

"I heard."

"You heard because I told you." She glanced up, smiling. "You never listen until I've said things twice."

"That's not true. I listen once. Then you say it again anyway."

"And then you remember it." She went back to her list. "So really I'm teaching you how to listen."

Paloma let it go. This was their rhythm now, a year of it—the archivist who narrated everything and the pilot who answered in single sentences, and somehow between them the whole truth of San Marisol had been spoken aloud, filed, stamped, entered into the record of a country that was slowly remembering itself. Not a nation restored—that was too grand a word for what was happening, which was mostly paperwork and arguments and old women correcting dates—but a name returning to maps. Paloma had seen one of the new maps. San Marisol sat there in small gray letters like it had never left, like forty years of erasure had been a printing error someone finally caught.

Below them the floodplain opened up, green and drowned-looking even in dry season, and Paloma brought the nose down a little without deciding to. There it was. The collapsed bank. Healed over now with new growth, the scar of the flood smoothed into ordinary mud, no glint of metal anywhere. As if the river had only lent the chest out and taken nothing back.

"You're looking at the bank," Ines said.

"I'm looking at the channel. Sandbar moved."

"You're looking at the bank," she repeated, gently, and Paloma didn't argue, because she was right.

* * *

They landed at midday on the grass strip the village had cut themselves, machetes and fire and stubbornness, and half the town came out before the propeller stopped. Children first, always children first, running alongside the plane like it was a large friendly animal. Then the elders, slower, and among them an old woman in a white dress whom Ines had spoken of for a year without ever quite naming—her grandmother's sister, the last person alive who remembered the village under its own flag.

The unloading took an hour, though it should have taken ten minutes. Everything took an hour here, and Paloma had learned—had been taught, patiently, by a woman who explained things twice—that the hour was the point. The chest came out last. Four men carried it to the schoolhouse, which stood at the center of the village with its fresh timber still pale, and inside, on a table built for the purpose, they set it down among the returned archives: the treaties, the census, the photographs of villages that had existed and then officially hadn't. Forty years in a box in a riverbank, and now sunlight through a new window falling across the oldest treaty, the one signed under duress, the one that had undone Meridian Concessions in a courtroom while Paloma Montrose stood at a witness table and confessed to manslaughter.

The trial felt far away now, the way a storm feels far away once it has passed over you. Six weeks of testimony. The falsified logbook read aloud in their own voice. The sentence—suspended license, community service, the manslaughter charge reduced because the confession weighed more than the crime—and then the strange quiet afterward, walking out of the courthouse into a city that had watched them do it, nobody spitting, nobody applauding either. Just people letting them pass. It had been enough. It had turned out to be enough.

"Paloma." Ines touched their arm. "Come see."

At the front of the schoolhouse, the old woman in white was speaking—not to the crowd, but to the chest itself, in San Marisolan, a language with maybe four hundred living speakers and now, apparently, one more learning it from recordings Ines made every week. The words were slow and formal. Paloma understood none of them and all of them. It was a welcome home. It was addressed to documents, and it meant every word.

Afterward the old woman came to Paloma directly, which surprised them, and looked up with eyes that had seen the erasure happen and outlived it.

"They tell me you are the one who flew it back," she said, through Ines.

"The river flew it back. I just kept it off the trees."

She considered this, then shook her head once, firmly, the way elders correct dates. "No. You carried it. That is not the same thing, and it is not a small thing." She pressed something into Paloma's hand—a woven band, plain, the kind used to tie bundles. "For your hands. So they remember what they carried."

Paloma wore it from that day on. They were wearing it now, a year later, flying south again with the afternoon light going gold on the water.

* * *

The mission station appeared out of the heat haze the way it always did—first the bell tower, then the roofs, then the long shadow of the chapel across the yard. Paloma brought the plane in low over the river and set it down on the strip without hurrying, and that was the difference, they thought, taxiing toward the mission buildings. That was the whole difference. A year ago every landing here would have been a calculation: how fast could they refuel, how soon could they be gone, what direction was trouble coming from. Now they shut the engine down and let it tick and cool, and walked across the yard at the speed of a person with nowhere else to be.

Sister Bernadette met them at the refectory door with flour on her hands.

"We heard the plane. I told Mother Superior it would be you—nobody else lands that badly in good weather."

"It was a crosswind."

"It was a perfect day." She wiped her hands and embraced them briefly, briskly, the way nuns embrace people they've fed. "She's in the chapel. Go. And you—" this to Ines, "—the archive list you sent arrived. We have questions about the duplicate sets."

"There are always questions about the duplicate sets," Ines said happily, and went off with her, already talking.

Paloma crossed the yard alone.

The chapel was cool and dim and smelled of wax and old wood, and Mother Superior was exactly where Sister Bernadette said, kneeling in the front pew, rising with the unhurried motion of a woman who had chosen her pace decades ago and never revisited it. She saw Paloma and did not seem surprised.

"You landed," she said.

"I landed."

"A year ago you would have circled twice and checked the tree line."

"A year ago I circled everything."

She studied them for a moment. "And now?"

Paloma thought about it honestly, because this woman had earned honesty—the donation she'd refused, the fugitives she'd sheltered, the morning she'd stood in her own doorway and told Meridian's fixer that some things were worth more than funding, and watched him understand that he'd lost.

"Now I land," Paloma said. "That's most of it. I just... land."

Mother Superior nodded slowly. "The river," she said, "does not run away from anything. It only goes forward. People mistake that for indifference." She touched Paloma's shoulder, briefly. "It was never indifference. It's just very sure of where it's going."

They stayed three days. Paloma fixed the mission's water pump, which had been broken since the trial, and delivered the medicine crate they'd carried north and back and north again, and on the second evening sat with the sisters at supper while Ines argued cheerfully about archival duplicates and someone laughed hard enough to spill soup. On the third morning they fueled up, and Mother Superior stood at the edge of the strip with the wind moving her habit, and said only: "Fly well. Come back slowly."

Not *come back soon*. *Come back slowly.* As if speed were the thing to watch for. As if she knew.

* * *

South of the mission the country opened out, and Paloma flew the way they hadn't flown in seven years—not watching mirrors, not reading weather for escape routes, not keeping one eye always on the fuel gauge as a countdown to the next place they couldn't stay. Just flying. The current below them ran north, carrying everything toward the sea, and Paloma rode the air above it with both hands loose on the yoke and the woven band on their wrist and Ines asleep in the right seat with a folder of photographs open on her lap.

Somewhere past the great bend, Ines woke and looked around, disoriented, then settled.

"Where are we?"

"Over the bend. Another hour to Toma's."

"Mm." She was quiet a while. Then: "Do you ever think about that night? The sandbar?"

"Every time I land on sand."

"Do you regret it? Telling me. About the charge."

Paloma thought about the sandbar night—the engine dead, dusk coming down, the two of them stranded with a chest full of other people's erased lives, trading truths because lies had gotten too heavy to carry. *I killed a man,* they'd said, out loud, for the first time in seven years, and the sky hadn't fallen and the river hadn't risen and Ines hadn't flinched. It had been the cheapest thing they'd ever bought.

"No," they said. "I regret the seven years before it."

"That's what I thought." She closed the folder. "Toma asked me last month if you'd changed. I told him I couldn't say—I'd only known you a year. But I said the plane lands differently now. Slower approaches. Longer rolls. Like someone who expects to be where they're landing."

Paloma laughed. "He'll say that's the new engine."

"He'll say that's the new engine," Ines agreed, "and then he'll know better, because he always knows better, and he'll charge you for telling you so."

Toma's mooring came into view at the bend, smoke from his cookfire standing straight up in the still air, his barge riding low with cargo, his dog already barking at the plane the way it barked at everything, including rain. Paloma swung wide, lined up on the water, and set the plane down smooth as a held breath, rolling long, taking their time, coming to rest exactly where they meant to be.

Toma stood on the deck with his arms crossed, grinning like a man about to overcharge an old friend.

"Well," he called across the water. "Look who stopped running."

"I was never running," Paloma called back, cutting the engine, letting the current take them the last few meters to the barge. "I was flying with the wind."

"Liar," said Toma, and threw them a line.

And Paloma caught it—one hand, easy, the woven band bright on their wrist—and tied off, and stepped up onto the deck of the barge into the smell of cargo and woodsmoke and the river going on forever in both directions, forward and back, carrying everything, holding nothing, certain of where it was going.

Ines came up beside them with the folder of photographs held against her chest, and together the three of them stood at the rail while the light went down over the water, and somewhere behind them, upriver, a village schoolhouse held its treaties in the sun, and ahead of them lay the capital, and the trial transcripts, and the slow work of a country remembering its own name.

Nobody hurried. There was no longer any reason to.

The current ran north. They rode it.
