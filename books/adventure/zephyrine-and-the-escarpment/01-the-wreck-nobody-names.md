## 1. The Wreck Nobody Names

The fog came off the water before dawn, the way it always did in Grayhaven, and Coral Voss stood at the rail of the Kestrel watching it eat the harbor whole. The town had no name on most charts—just a smudge of coastline between two better places, a gray tooth of breakwater, a cluster of roofs the color of wet slate. The locals called it Grayhaven because there was nothing else to call it. Coral called it home for as long as the job lasted, which was to say: not long.

He'd been in port nine days. That was already too many.

Below him, the harbor water lay flat and opaque as poured lead, holding the smell of diesel and kelp and old fish. Gulls argued over something dead on the pilings. Somewhere behind him, up the hill, a church bell counted six, its voice muffled by the fog like everything else in this town. Coral pulled his gloves on finger by finger and told himself the tightness in his chest was just the cold.

It wasn't the cold. He knew what the cold felt like. This was the other thing—the thing that had kept him awake half the night listening to the Kestrel's hull tick against the fenders, running through the dive plan again and again like a man checking a lock he'd already thrown.

The wreck sat four miles out, past the headland, in forty fathoms of gray-green water where the coastal shelf fell away toward the escarpment's drowned roots. The charts marked it as *obstruction, uncharted*. The harbor master, a broad silent man named Dree with hands like cured leather, had given Coral the coordinates on a scrap of paper and a price that made Coral's professional instincts itch like a healing scar. Three times what salvage was worth. Four times what his silence usually cost.

Dree hadn't asked what was down there. That was the first wrong note. In twenty years of this work, Coral had learned that men who paid well always wanted something for their money—a report, a look at the manifest, first pick of whatever came up on the line. Dree wanted nothing. He'd taken the paper back after Coral read it, burned it in the stove of his office with the same unhurried care he gave everything, and said only: *"Bring it up clean. Don't open anything."*

*Don't open anything.*

Coral finished checking his gear by feel in the pre-dawn dark—regulator, knife, lift bags, the slate and chalk he used for marking finds—and let himself down into the tender. The outboard caught on the third pull, coughing blue smoke into the fog, and he ran the four miles out by compass and dead reckoning, the Kestrel shrinking behind him until she was just a darker shape inside the dark. The sea was glass-calm, which he distrusted on principle. Calm water meant the weather was thinking about something.

At the coordinates he killed the engine and drifted, listening. Forty fathoms down, the wreck waited. Sonar would have found her in minutes; Coral didn't carry sonar. He carried instead a length of chain on a line, and he swung it out and let it pay down until the weight stopped, then swam the anchor down the chain into the green-black cold.

The water closed over him like a door.

* * *

Forty fathoms was deep enough that the light died by degrees—not all at once, but in layers, gold to green to a blue so dark it wasn't color anymore but memory. Coral equalized his ears and dropped along the anchor line, exhaling slow streams of silver that rose past him like escaping thoughts. At thirty fathoms the temperature fell away and the cold found the seams of his suit. At thirty-five, the wreck materialized out of the gloom the way wrecks do: not suddenly, but as a correction, the eye realizing that a particular darkness had edges.

She'd been a coastal freighter once, maybe sixty meters, steel-hulled, sitting upright on the shelf with a list to port. Her superstructure had collapsed into itself. Her holds gaped. Silt the consistency of ash lay over everything, softening her lines, making her look less like a ship than like the idea of one, something remembered rather than recovered. A trawl net hung from her bow in rotten curtains. Small fish moved through the wheelhouse windows like thoughts moving through an empty skull.

Coral hung in the water above her deck and felt the unease sharpen into something with a name.

He'd dived wrecks for two decades—war graves, storm losses, ships scuttled for insurance, ships sunk for reasons nobody wrote down. Every wreck had a texture, a mood, the way a room has a mood when you walk into it after an argument. Most wrecks felt abandoned. Some felt mournful. This one felt *curated*. The silt lay undisturbed in long clean drifts. Nothing had scavenged her. No lines trailed from her rails, no cut marks showed on her plating, no ghost of any previous diver's passage. She had been left alone, deliberately, completely, in water every fisherman in Grayhaven gave a wide berth.

Ships weren't left alone like that unless someone was paying for them to be alone.

Coral checked his gauge—plenty of bottom time if he worked fast—and dropped to the main cargo hold. The hatch covers were gone, removed cleanly, bolt-holes bright even under the silt. Inside, the hold stretched away into darkness, and his torch beam swept across rows of crates lashed to the frames: maybe forty of them, wooden, uniform, each about the size of a coffin, each wrapped in tarred canvas against the water.

He landed on the deck plating inside the hold and knelt in the silt, and the cloud he raised bloomed around him and settled, and he wiped the nearest crate clear with his glove.

The stencil came up gray-white through the grime. A serial number. A date—eight years old, give or take. And below both, stamped deep enough to survive decades underwater, a mark: a circle broken by a single vertical stroke, like an eye with a blade through it.

Coral went very still. Even his breathing slowed, the regulator hissing loud in the enclosed dark of the hold.

He knew that mark. You didn't forget a mark like that. Eight years ago, in another life, under another name, he'd stood in a navy transport's cargo bay at three in the morning and watched men load crates bearing exactly that stamp—crates going inland, up the escarpment roads, to a facility that appeared on no chart and answered to no ministry anyone would name. He'd asked one question too many about those crates. He'd been given one answer too honest to ignore. And he'd run, and kept running, port by port, year by year, changing his name the way other men changed shirts, until *Coral Voss* fit him like his own skin and he could almost believe the man he'd been had drowned somewhere behind him.

Now here was the mark again, forty fathoms down, eight hundred miles from anywhere he'd ever been, stenciled on crates in a wreck nobody would admit existed.

Coincidence was a word divers didn't use. The sea didn't deal in coincidence. The sea dealt in currents, and currents ran from somewhere to somewhere, and this current had run straight out of his worst memory and settled on the bottom under a gray town at the edge of the map.

His hands, steady as they'd ever been on a working dive, shook slightly as he rigged the first lift bag.

* * *

He worked fast after that, and he hated how fast he worked, because speed was fear wearing a workman's face. One crate at a time: sling it, inflate the bag, send it up the line to hang ten fathoms down where the tender could collect it. He took eleven crates before his gauge told him to stop—eleven coffins of tarred canvas rising through the green dark, each one swinging gently on its line like something hanged.

On the twelfth crate, the sling shifted.

It was wedged deeper in the hold than the others, jammed against a frame member, and when he hauled it free the canvas wrapping tore along one seam. Not much. A hand's width. Through the tear he saw not wood but metal—a case within the crate, dull gray alloy, seamless except for a seam, and set into the seam a seal: a disc of some composite material, stamped with the same broken-circle mark, and beneath the mark, in letters small as a fingernail, a word he couldn't quite read through the film of silt and growth.

He should have left it. Dree had said don't open anything, and Coral Voss had survived twenty years by being a man who followed instructions exactly as far as they suited him and no further—but this was different, and he knew it was different, because the shaking in his hands had spread to his chest, and the cold of the water had gotten inside his suit somehow, or maybe that was just the memory getting in, the cargo bay, the floodlights, the voice saying *you were never here, you saw nothing, and we will know if you speak of it.*

Coral put his glove against the torn seam and pressed, and the canvas parted another inch, and the silt film smeared, and the letters resolved:

*ESCARPMENT SURVEY — MATERIALS — DO NOT MANIFEST*

Survey materials. On a freighter that had gone down—or been put down—in water no fisherman worked, carrying government-stamped crates that were never meant to appear on any manifest, bound nowhere that appeared on any chart.

Above him, faintly, through forty fathoms of water, he heard it: an engine. Not his tender. His tender's outboard had a stutter in it, a missed beat on the high end that he knew the way he knew his own heartbeat. This engine was bigger, smoother, turning at low revolutions, patient as a shark.

Coral killed his torch and hung motionless in the black water beside the torn crate, breathing as shallowly as the regulator allowed, and listened. The sound crossed above him, east to west, unhurried. Then it faded, and faded, and was gone, swallowed by the fog that had never lifted all morning.

He stayed down another full minute, doing nothing, being nothing, a piece of the wreck among the wreck. Then he sent the twelfth crate up unfinished, its tear hidden by re-wrapping the canvas with practiced hands, and began his ascent.

* * *

The fog had thinned to a pale gauze by the time he surfaced. His tender rode at the pickup point, and beyond it, far off toward the headland, he caught—one instant, no more—the gray shape of a vessel moving away: low, angular, military lines, no lights, no markings visible at that distance. A patrol boat, or something shaped like one. It didn't come back. It didn't need to. Whatever it had been doing out here, it had already seen what it came to see.

Coral transferred the crates into the Kestrel's hold himself, tarpaulined them, lashed them down, and ran back into the harbor without speaking to his deckhand-less crew of one, which was himself. The fog closed behind him like a curtain drawn by careful hands.

Dree was waiting at the customs shed. He was always waiting; that was part of what he was. He watched Coral tie off, watched the tarpaulins come off, watched the twelve crates stacked on the quay, and his face did nothing at all, which was its usual accomplishment.

"Twelve," Dree said.

"Twelve I could reach. There's more down there."

"I know how many there are." Dree crouched, examined the crates without touching them, and paused—Coral saw it, a hitch no one else would have caught—at the re-wrapped seam on the last one. His eyes came up. For a moment, something moved behind them, deep and careful, like a fish under ice. "You opened one."

"Tore one. Wrapping split on the lift. I re-tarred it."

A long silence. A gull screamed somewhere out in the fog, and got no answer. Dree straightened, joints cracking like rigging in a swell, and reached inside his coat. The envelope he produced was fat, and it was cash, and it was more than the already-too-good price by a third.

"That's over the number we agreed," Coral said.

"The number changed." Dree held it out. "There won't be a second job. Take your boat and be gone inside the week, Voss. Anywhere you like. Just—" and here the old man's voice dropped, not to a whisper but to something quieter than a whisper, the register men use for things they're ashamed of, "—just be gone."

"What's in them?"

"You know I can't—"

"*What's in them?*" Coral didn't raise his voice. He'd learned long ago that quiet was heavier than shouting; it made men lean in, and leaning-in men heard themselves say things. "I've been lifting crates for you for six days, Dree. I've earned a question."

Dree looked at him for a long moment, and Coral watched the old man weigh something—weigh it visibly, the way a man weighs whether to throw a drowning stranger a line knowing the rope is tied to his own ankle. Whatever he decided, he decided against it. He pushed the envelope into Coral's chest hard enough to feel it through the suit.

"Escarpment Survey," Dree said. "That's all I'll give you, and it's more than I should. They bought my silence same as yours. Same as everyone's in this town." He turned away, then stopped, his back a wall of old wool and older regret. "Eight years ago a man dove a wreck near here and asked questions like yours. They found his boat adrift, empty, engine running. They never found him."

"And you still took the job? Still handed me the paper?"

Dree's shoulders moved—something between a shrug and a flinch. "They pay, and this town eats. You want the truth, Voss? Nobody names that wreck. Not because we don't know it's there. Because the ones who named it aren't around anymore to tell us what to call it."

He walked away down the quay, and the fog took him the way it took everything in Grayhaven: gradually, then completely.

Coral stood a long time with the envelope against his chest and the twelve crates at his back, feeling the money's weight and the day's wrongness settle into him together, twin burdens. Routine, he told himself. Just another job, just another port, just another reason to be gone inside the week. Take the money, burn nothing, ask nothing, run the Kestrel upcoast and forget the broken circle and the words *do not manifest* and the patrol boat's patient engine.

But his hands wouldn't stop remembering the feel of that torn canvas. And when he finally turned to look at the crates, stacked in the gray light like a jury waiting to deliberate, he found himself already reaching for the pry bar—because eight years ago he had run from that mark, and a man can only run from a thing so long before the thing starts following him, and this, he understood with a cold clarity that had nothing to do with the sea, this was the mark catching up.

He picked up the pry bar. Then he set it down again, slowly, and looked out at the fog-bound water instead, and made himself a promise he already knew he'd break: *one night. Sleep on it. Open nothing till morning.*

Behind him, unheard, the first crate's torn seam had begun, very quietly, to seep.
