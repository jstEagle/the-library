## 2. Salt and Silence

The Kestrel had a hole in her belly the size of a dinner plate, and Coral Voss lay on his back beneath her hull with salt water dripping into his eyes, thinking that this was as honest a conversation as he'd ever have with anything.

She'd taken a knock on the outer reef two nights back—his fault, mostly, for cutting the channel too fine in a running sea—and now she sat in the slings at Marlow's yard like a patient with her ribs cracked open. The damage wasn't bad. A sprung seam, three planks gone soft along the garboard, a scar of bright wood where the reef had scraped her clean of paint and years of lies. He ran his thumb along it. Under all that weathered gray she was still pale, still young, still the boat he'd bought in another port under another name from a man who'd asked no questions because Coral had paid in cash and not met his eyes.

That was the trick of it. Everything in his life could be repaired if you got underneath it soon enough. It was only the letting people look that ruined things.

He set the chisel against the first bad plank and began to work it free. The mallet's rhythm was good for him, the way breathing was good for him—one blow, one breath, one blow. Around him the yard woke up: gulls arguing over something dead on the pilings, the smell of tar and cut oakum, old Marlow somewhere behind him coughing his way through a pipe. The town was called Greywater, though nobody seemed to know why; the water was the same tired green-gray here as everywhere else on this coast, and the sky hung over it like a tarpaulin someone had forgotten to tie down.

"Plank's no good past the butt joint," Marlow said, materializing the way old men do, without any transition between absent and present. He squatted down beside the hull, joints cracking like green kindling, and peered up at the wound. "You'll want to sister it."

"I'll want to do it myself."

"You always do." Marlow spat thoughtfully into the sawdust. "That's why your seams weep and my boats don't."

"My seams weep because I ask them to hold more than they were built for." Coral knocked the plank loose and caught it before it fell. The wood came away soft and punky at the heart, rotten clear through where water had been getting in for years, working patiently, the way water does. He looked at it longer than he needed to. "Everything rots from the inside out," he said. "You just don't see it till you open her up."

Marlow gave him a look—the flat, unhurried look of a man who'd spent sixty years reading weather and men and found both equally unreliable. "You want breakfast or you want philosophy?"

"Breakfast."

* * *

The Salt House Inn sat where the harbor road bent toward the water, and its windows were fogged with steam and the smell of frying bread, which was how Marta Tull liked it. She was a broad woman with forearms like mooring line and a face that had decided long ago to be kind whether the world cooperated or not, and she had decided—somewhere in the four days since Coral had first tied up at her dock—that he was going to be fed properly if it killed them both.

"There he is," she announced when he came through the door, as if he were expected, as if he were *welcome*, and the words landed on him with a weight he resented. "Sit down. Eggs or the fish stew?"

"Whatever's left over."

"That's what I'm giving you anyway. Sit."

He sat, at the end of the bench nearest the door, back to the wall, the way he sat everywhere. The common room was half full of fishermen eating fast and talking slow, and a few of them nodded at him. He nodded back the exact amount required and no more. This was arithmetic he could do in his sleep: enough warmth to pass, not enough to owe.

Marta put a bowl in front of him—fish stew, thick with potatoes, a heel of bread balanced on the rim—and then, instead of leaving, she sat down across from him with her own cup of tea, which was not part of the arrangement.

"You dive that wreck again today?" she asked.

"I finished the job."

"The harbor master's been grinning about it for two days. Whatever you brought up off that thing, he's pleased as a man who's been paid twice." She sipped her tea, watching him over the rim. Her eyes were quick and brown and missed nothing, which made her dangerous in a way that no knife-carrying man in this town was. "Funny thing, though. Nobody knows what the wreck is. Been out there forty years, they say, and nobody's dived it, and nobody asks why. Then you come along, and within a week there's a navy patrol boat doing circles in the bay like a gull that's lost its nerve."

Coral kept his face where it was. "Boats circle. That's what they're for."

"Mm." Marta turned her cup slowly on the table. "My husband fished these waters thirty years. He used to say a patrol boat that circles twice isn't patrolling. It's counting."

The stew was good. He ate it steadily and let the silence stretch, because silences were the one currency he'd never run short of. Around them the room went on with its noise—spoons, laughter, someone complaining about the price of net twine—and Coral sat inside it the way he sat inside everything: present, and not there at all.

"You're a young man," Marta said at last, "and you eat like a man who expects the plate to be taken away. You sleep on your boat even though I've got rooms standing empty at rates I'd be ashamed to charge anybody. You've been here five days and I know your boat's name but not yours, and I make it my business to know names." She leaned forward. "I'm not asking you to tell me. I'm telling you that I noticed. There's a difference, and I find most men can't tell the difference anymore."

"There's nothing to tell."

"There's always something to tell. That's the whole trouble with people." She stood, gathered his empty bowl, and paused with her hand on his shoulder—a touch so brief and matter-of-fact that he couldn't flinch from it without making a scene, which was probably why she'd done it exactly that way. "Room's made up. Third floor, end of the hall, faces the water. If you change your mind about sleeping ashore, it'll be there. No questions. No ledger entry either, if that's what worries you."

"It doesn't worry me."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "Nothing worries you. That's the saddest thing I've heard all week, and I heard Old Piet describe his operation in detail."

She went back to her kitchen, and Coral sat alone at the end of the bench with the taste of stew and the strange, unwelcome ache of having been seen. It was like a splinter worked deep—too small to grip, too true to ignore. He paid for the meal by leaving coins under the bowl, more than it cost, because generosity was easier than gratitude, and went out into the gray morning to work on his boat.

* * *

The day went long and quiet. He steamed and bent new planks, drove them home, caulked the seams with oakum until his hands were black to the wrist, and paid them off with pitch while the yard smelled like a burning ship. Marlow drifted by twice to offer advice Coral didn't take, and stayed a third time without offering any, which was somehow worse. Toward late afternoon the light went bronze and low across the bay, and the work was good enough that Coral almost forgot to keep count of the exits.

Almost.

Because the navy patrol boat came around the headland at dusk, and this time it didn't circle.

It slowed.

Coral stood on the shingle with a pitch brush in his hand and watched it come down the bay—white superstructure, gun-gray hull, the number on her bow painted over with something newer and darker. She moved at maybe four knots, wallowing slightly, the way a ship moves when the man on her bridge has nowhere particular to be and all the time in the world to not be there. She came down the middle of the harbor mouth, close enough that he could see a figure at the rail with binoculars, and the binoculars did not sweep the shore generally, the way a bored lookout's would.

They stopped on the Kestrel. Held there. Moved on.

Then the boat swung wide, went back out past the headland, and was gone, and the bay closed over the place where she'd been like water over a dropped stone.

Marlow had come to stand beside him at some point. Neither of them spoke for a while.

"She circled yesterday too," Marlow said finally. "Twice. My boy counts ships the way other boys count birds. Says she's got a towed array—listening gear. Nothing to listen to in Greywater Bay except herring and gossip." He glanced sideways at Coral. "Unless somebody's brought something worth listening to."

"I haul crates for the harbor master. Crates don't talk."

"Crates stamped with a government mark don't need to talk. That's their whole charm." Marlow knocked out his pipe against his palm, unhurried. "Look, lad. I've been launching and hauling boats in this yard since before you were born, and I've learned one thing worth learning: when the navy starts noticing a small boat in a small harbor, the small boat's owner has about a week of good choices left, and then a lifetime of bad ones. I don't know what you're carrying and I don't want to. But that seam I told you to sister? Sister it. And maybe think about being somewhere else when the weather comes."

"What weather?"

"The kind that wears uniforms." Marlow shuffled off toward his shed, then stopped, half-turned. "Marta's room faces the water. Third floor. You'd see them coming." A pause. "She keeps it made up for you. Has since the second day. Don't tell her I mentioned it."

* * *

Night came down on Greywater the way it always did, without ceremony, the lamps coming on one by one along the quay like a string of dull beads. Coral worked by lantern light until his arms gave out, then sat on the Kestrel's deck with his back against the mast step and a mug of tea going cold in his hands, and let the loneliness come.

This was the part nobody understood about the life he'd chosen—or the life that had chosen him, he'd never been able to settle which. They imagined drifting was freedom. Wind in the rigging, every horizon an invitation, no debts and no names. And it was that, some days, in the first hour of a fair wind. But the rest of it was this: sitting alone on a cold deck in a strange harbor, knowing the name of nobody and known by nobody, keeping a mental map of every door and window and sightline because the habit of flight had grown into him like the rot grew into planks—from the inside, silently, until the whole structure depended on it.

Five days in Greywater and already Marta had a room made up for him. Already Marlow was calling him *lad* and offering advice he hadn't asked for. Already the town was trying to fit him into itself, gently, the way water fits into any shape offered to it, and he could feel himself wanting—God help him—to let it. To climb those stairs to the third floor and sleep eight hours in a bed that smelled of soap instead of bilge. To wake up and be greeted by name. To belong somewhere long enough to leave footprints.

And he couldn't. Because belonging was a debt, and debts were how they found you. Because the last time he'd belonged anywhere—a crew, a mess table, a navy transport with a sealed hold and orders he should never have read—he'd learned what happened to men who stayed. He'd seen it. He'd run from it, and he'd been running ever since, port by port, name by name, and the running had worked, and the working of it was the loneliest fact of his life.

Out in the dark, far off, an engine note changed.

He knew engines the way Marta knew faces. That was a heavy diesel under strain, throttling down, and there was nothing out there tonight but the navy. He set down the mug and moved to the rail without hurry, without light, and watched the headland.

The patrol boat came around again. Slower than before. Running dark except for a single white light at her masthead, she crept along the edge of the bay, and this time the binoculars weren't the only thing watching—there was a searchlight hooded at her bridge, not lit, but trained, ready, pointed at the anchorage like a finger kept politely behind a back.

Two circuits yesterday. One slow pass tonight, running dark.

Counting. Marta's husband had been right. Not patrolling—counting. Learning the boats at anchor, learning which lights burned, learning the shape of the harbor so that when the order came, it would come clean.

Coral stood in the dark and felt the old machinery wake in him: the tallying of fuel and water, the calculation of tide and moon, the cold inventory of what he could carry and what he'd have to leave. He'd done it so many times it barely hurt anymore. That was the worst part. It barely hurt at all.

But tonight, for the first time in years, something in him hesitated—not at leaving, but at how easily he could. Five days. He'd given Greywater five days, and already leaving felt less like escape and more like theft: stealing himself out of a place that had offered him eggs and a made-up bed and a woman's unembarrassed kindness, and giving nothing back.

*Run*, said the old voice, the reliable one. *You know how.*

He was still standing at the rail, tea cold, war half-won by the voice of habit, when he saw the lamp on the quay go out—then relight, lower, closer to the water. A figure stood under it with a canvas roll under one arm and the wind pulling at a coat that was built for cities, not harbors, and even at that distance, even in that light, Coral could tell two things about her.

She was looking directly at the Kestrel.

And she was waiting for him to come to her.
