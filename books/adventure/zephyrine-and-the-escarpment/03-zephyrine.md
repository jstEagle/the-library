## 3. Zephyrine

The woman was waiting at the end of the pier when Coral came down with the morning's first crate, and he knew before she spoke that she had been there a while. There is a particular stillness to people who wait in cold weather on purpose—not the fidgeting of the impatient, but the settled patience of someone who has decided the waiting is part of the work. She stood at the rail where the planking met the shore road, hands folded around a leather satchel worn soft at the corners, and she watched him work the way a gull watches a fisherman: without hurry, without apology, with the absolute confidence of something that intends to be fed.

He tried to ignore her. He was good at ignoring people; it was one of the few skills his life had left him with in abundance. He hauled the crate up the ramp on the hand trolley, its weight familiar under his palms, its government stamp turned inward against the boards where no casual eye would catch it. The harbor master's boy took it off him at the shed door without a word, as they always did, and slid the coin across—too much coin, always too much—and Coral pocketed it and turned back toward the Kestrel, and the woman was still there.

Not at the rail anymore. On the pier itself, walking toward him with the unhurried stride of someone who had already measured the distance between them and found it acceptable.

"Captain Voss," she said.

"No captain," he said. "Just Voss."

"Then just Voss." She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that he could see the salt wind had been at her for some time—chapped lips, wind-reddened cheeks, a coat that had been good once and had traveled far since. Her hair was dark, cut short and practical, escaping from under a wool cap in ways that suggested she'd stopped fighting it days ago. Her eyes were gray-green, the color of the sea over sandbar, and they did not move from his face when she spoke. "I need passage upcoast."

"Passenger boat runs Thursdays."

"I know. I didn't take it."

"Why not?"

"Because I need to get off where the passenger boat doesn't stop." She said it plainly, without drama, the way you'd report the tide. "There's a cove forty miles north. Old whaling station. The charts call it nothing. My father's charts call it Meridian Cove."

Coral set the empty trolley down. It was a small movement, deliberate, buying himself a moment the way a diver buys bottom time—one breath held, one calculation made. *Meridian Cove.* A name off no chart he'd ever been paid to read. A name out of a dead man's private papers, if the man was dead.

"Don't know it," he said.

"Yes, you do." Not accusatory. Almost gentle. "You've been diving the wreck off this harbor for nine days. The wreck nobody names. And my father's maps say there's a second wreck, farther upcoast, in water too deep for salvage men—but shallow enough, Captain Voss, for whatever it is you actually do."

The morning went very quiet around them. The gulls kept on with their arguments over the fish gutting tables, and the water slapped the pilings in its usual dull rhythm, and none of it touched the small cold space that had opened between Coral and this stranger. He looked at her properly now, and she let herself be looked at, which most people didn't, and what he saw was exhaustion held together by will—the kind of will that has nothing left to run on except itself.

"Who told you about the wreck?" he asked.

"The wreck, or the wreck nobody names?"

"Either."

"Nobody told me. My father drew the hydrography for this whole coast eleven years ago. Every shoal, every shelf, every anomaly the official charts smooth over." She tapped the satchel. "These are his working maps. The ones the survey office never got back. And three weeks ago my father walked into the escarpment country above this coast with five other people, and the government says they were caught in weather, and the government says there's nothing to find, and the government—" her voice caught, only slightly, a single dropped stitch quickly taken back up, "—the government has been very thorough about saying things."

Coral said nothing. The harbor master's shed door was open behind him, and through it he could see the boy stacking crates, and beyond the crates the gray water, and beyond the water the low smudge of the headland where, two mornings ago, a navy patrol boat had come around twice and gone away again. Twice. Patrol boats came around once, ordinarily. Once was routine. Twice was a question being asked.

"You have the wrong man," he said. "I dive wrecks. I strip brass and copper and anything else that pays. That's all."

"That's what I thought at first." She shifted the satchel to her other hand. "But then I sat in the inn last night, and I listened, because listening is what my father taught me instead of conversation. And the innkeeper's wife mentioned that a diver came into town at the start of the month, alone, quiet, keeps to himself, and the harbor master—who never pays anyone fairly in his life—has been paying this diver double rate for crates nobody's allowed to look inside. And I thought: what kind of wreck pays double for sealed cargo?" Her eyes came up to his. "Only the kind somebody wants recovered quietly. Only the kind with a mark on the crates that matches the mark on the correspondence my father sent home from his last survey, the correspondence that stopped arriving in March."

The wind came down the harbor then, straight off the open water, carrying the smell of kelp and diesel and cold iron, and Coral felt it move through him the way the deep cold moved through him at sixty feet—that pressure that finds the seams in everything, hulls and hearts alike, and presses until the seams speak.

"You should go back to the inn," he said. "And tomorrow you should take the Thursday boat like a sensible person, and forget whatever you think you saw in your father's maps."

"My father is not dead."

"I didn't say he was."

"You didn't have to. You said 'whatever you think you saw,' which is what people say when they've decided someone is chasing ghosts." She lifted her chin. "I'm not chasing ghosts, Captain. I'm chasing a route. Six people went into those mountains and six people did not come out, and their gear was found intact and their bodies were not found at all, and I intend to walk the route myself and find out why. All I'm asking from you is forty miles of coastline. You don't even have to anchor. Just put me ashore at the cove and sail away, and we're done, and you can go back to your crates and your double pay and your name that isn't a name."

*Your name that isn't a name.* Something moved behind his ribs at that, old and cold, a fish turning in dark water.

"I don't take passengers," he said.

"So I've heard. From the innkeeper. From the chandler. From the boy who mops the fish house." A ghost of something crossed her face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would live in a person who smiled more. "You've made quite a study of refusing people, Captain Voss. You must have had practice."

"I had reasons."

"I imagine you did." She looked past him, out at the Kestrel riding her mooring, the paint gone chalky, the rails salt-scabbed, a boat that had been maintained with competence and loved by no one. "She's a fine hull. Thirty-two feet? Sloop rig? You've re-caulked her starboard side recently—the seams are paler there. You do your own work." She turned back to him. "My father always said you could tell more about a person from how they maintain what they depend on than from anything they'd ever say. He'd have liked your boat."

"He'd have liked my money better. Everyone does."

"No," she said, simply, and the certainty in it unsettled him more than any accusation could have. "No, I don't think so."

She adjusted the strap of the satchel on her shoulder—a small, finished gesture, the gesture of someone concluding a transaction—and looked at him with those sandbar-colored eyes, and said:

"I'll be at the inn. Room at the top of the stairs. I can wait a long time, Captain. Waiting is mostly what I've done since March." She paused at the edge of the pier steps, and added, without turning fully around: "The patrol boat that circled the bay yesterday—it came around twice because it was photographing your boat. I watched it through the innkeeper's glass. They weren't checking fishing licenses."

Then she went, down the steps and along the shore road, her stride long and unhesitating, a woman who had already done all her hesitating somewhere else, in some other season, and had arrived here with none of it remaining.

*

He worked the rest of the day and told himself he wasn't thinking about her.

The work helped, the way it always did. There was a fitting on the Kestrel's forestay that wanted replacing, and he replaced it, and the simple arithmetic of pins and shackles and tension filled his hands and quieted the louder parts of his mind. He scrubbed her waterline. He bailed the bilge and ran the engine an hour to charge the batteries and listened to the steady knock of it, the heartbeat sound, the only companionable noise in his life. Toward late afternoon he rowed out and dove the wreck one more time, though the crate count was already full, because the harbor master had asked—hadn't asked, had *mentioned*, in that flat way of his—whether there might be another compartment aft, and Coral had said maybe, and had hated himself a little for saying it.

Underwater was the only place the world made sense to him. Forty feet down the wreck lay on her side in the green gloom, half-buried, her plates furred with weed, and he moved through her corridors with the ease of long habit, his torch beam sliding over bulkheads and doorframes, finding nothing new, finding the same silence that had greeted him for nine days. But today the silence had a different texture. Today he kept seeing, superimposed on the rusted steel, the image of a woman standing on a pier with a leather satchel, saying *your name that isn't a name.*

At dusk he climbed back aboard and sat in the cockpit with tea going cold in his hands, and the harbor lights came on one by one along the shore, and he allowed himself, finally, to think.

She knew about the double pay. She knew about the patrol boat's two passes. She knew the starboard seams were newly caulked, which meant she'd looked at his boat with a cartographer's eye, reading it the way her father must have read coastlines—every feature noted, every anomaly recorded, nothing smoothed over. And underneath all of it, underneath the competence and the directness, he had seen the thing she was carrying under the satchel and the maps and the plan: three weeks of not knowing whether her father was alive, and months before that of letters stopping, and the particular loneliness of being the only person willing to say aloud that the official story was a story.

He knew that loneliness. He had built a whole life inside it, the way a hermit crab builds a shell out of whatever the sea provides. Nine years of ports and boats and names used briefly and discarded. Nine years of being exactly interesting enough to hire and exactly forgettable enough to keep. He had thought, in his more honest moments—which he kept few and rationed like hardtack—that what he felt was freedom. Sitting in the cold cockpit watching the lit windows of the town, most of them gold and most of them holding people who would eat together tonight, he considered the possibility that what he had actually built was a very well-maintained prison, and that he was both its keeper and its only inmate.

And she had seen it. In ten minutes on a pier, a stranger had read him more accurately than anyone had managed in years. *You've made quite a study of refusing people.* It should have angered him. What it did instead was worse: it made him feel *seen*, and being seen, after nine years, was like light after long darkness—painful, disorienting, and impossible to pretend hadn't happened.

He slept badly, which was normal, and woke before dawn, which was also normal, and rowed ashore in the gray light to buy bread and paraffin, which gave him an excuse to walk past the inn.

He told himself it was on the way. It was not on the way.

Through the inn's front window, lamplit against the early dark, he saw her at a corner table with her father's maps spread out and weighted with teacups, and the innkeeper's wife leaning over her shoulder pointing at something, and the two of them talking with their heads close together, and Zephyrine Marchand nodding, taking notes in a small book, drawing the town's scattered knowledge into her own net the way her father must once have drawn soundings into his charts. She had been here one day and she was already woven in. Already belonging, in the provisional way travelers belong, and Coral stood outside in the cold looking through glass at warmth, and understood that he was doing exactly what he had done his entire adult life: standing outside windows, watching other people's light.

She looked up. Saw him. Held his eyes through the glass, without surprise, without triumph—as if she had known he would pass by, had charted his course the way she charted everything, and had simply waited for him to arrive at the point she'd marked.

He went in. He bought bread and paraffin and paid and did not look at her table, and she did not call out to him, and the restraint of it—the courtesy of it, the patience—was somehow more persuasive than any argument she'd made on the pier.

At the door he stopped.

"The cove you named," he said, not turning around. "Meridian Cove. There's no anchorage. Bottom's foul. Any skipper who put a boat in there would lose her."

"There's a shelf of clean sand on the eastern side," she said, from her table, conversational, as though they were resuming a discussion interrupted minutes ago rather than a day. "Twelve feet over it at low water. My father anchored there twice. He wrote it in the margin of the chart, in pencil, with a little anchor symbol. He annotated everything. It drove his colleagues mad." A pause. "It's page fourteen, if you'd like to check my work."

He checked her work. That night, alone in the cockpit with the lamp turned low, he unfolded the corner of a chart she'd left with the innkeeper addressed to him—no note, just the chart, which was its own kind of message—and found page fourteen, and found the penciled anchor symbol in the eastern bight of Meridian Cove, and beside it, in a small precise hand: *12 ft., sand, good holding, sheltered from N and NW. — E.M.*

Eleven years ago a man had knelt over this same paper with a pencil and recorded, for whoever came after, exactly where safety lay. Coral sat looking at the tiny symbol for a long time. He had spent nine years leaving no marks anywhere, erasing himself port by port, and here was a dead-or-alive stranger whose instinct was the opposite: to write it down, to make the hidden thing findable, to trust the future with the truth.

He did not sleep at all after that.

*

On the third morning she was on the pier again.

Not waiting at the rail this time—standing at the head of the ramp with her satchel and a seabag, dressed for travel, and beside her on the planks sat a second bag, canvas, heavy-looking, stenciled with faded letters. The innkeeper's wife stood a few steps behind her with a wrapped parcel, pressing it into her hands, saying something low and warm, and Zephyrine received it the way people receive blessings they aren't sure they deserve.

Coral came down the ramp because there was no other direction to go.

"I told you no," he said.

"You did."

"And I meant it."

"I know you did." She shouldered the satchel. "That's why I bought a ticket on the Thursday boat after all. It leaves at noon. I'll be on it. I'll get off at Harrow Point, the last scheduled stop, and from there I'll walk the coast path north, forty miles, alone, in November, carrying—" she patted the canvas bag, "—a fortnight's supplies and my father's maps, to a cove with no road out and no village within three days' walk, to look for a second wreck that I am not equipped to dive and cannot see underwater anyway."

The absurdity of it hung in the cold air between them. Behind her the innkeeper's wife had gone still, listening with the transparent lack of discretion of her profession, and further down the quay the fish boys had paused in their work, and Coral understood that this was a performance, deliberately staged, and that he was the audience, and that she had calculated—to the hour, to the mile, to the pound of supplies—exactly how much her own recklessness would weigh on him.

"You'd die out there," he said.

"Probably." She said it without flinching, and that was the worst part, that she'd already done the arithmetic and accepted the answer. "But my father walked into those mountains believing someone would come looking, and no one did, and I will not add myself to the list of people who chose comfort over him. If the price of looking is dying on a coast path in November, then that's the price. I've paid worse prices for less reason."

"What worse price?"

She looked at him then, and for a moment the composure cracked—not broke, but cracked, a seam opening under pressure, and what showed through was young and frightened and utterly unbending. "Every night since March," she said quietly, "I have lain awake composing letters to a man who may be dead, telling him I'm sorry I waited so long. That's the price. Everything else is just weather."

The gulls screamed. The water slapped. Somewhere up the street a door banged, and the ordinary morning went on around the two of them as though the world had not just tilted.

Coral looked at the canvas bag. At the satchel. At the woman standing in front of him offering him, in the guise of a fare, a chance to be the kind of man who did not stand outside windows.

"Thursday boat," he said. "Noon."

"Noon," she agreed.

He turned and walked down the ramp toward the Kestrel, and behind him he heard the innkeeper's wife say something to Zephyrine, and heard Zephyrine laugh—a short, surprised sound, as if laughter had become foreign currency she'd forgotten she carried—and the sound followed him all the way down the pier and lodged somewhere under his sternum and would not leave.

He cast off the Kestrel's lines and rowed out to her mooring and sat below with the chart spread open, and his eyes went, without his permission, to page fourteen, to the little penciled anchor, to the marginal handwriting of a missing man: *good holding, sheltered from N and NW.*

He was still sitting there when the harbor master's boy hailed him from the quay, cupping his hands, shouting across the water that there was a gentleman ashore asking after him—a navy gentleman, well-spoken, waiting at the harbor office, and would Captain Voss be coming in?

Coral looked up at the town. At the gray stone and the lit windows and the harbor office door, behind which, presumably, a well-spoken man was asking questions about him in the flat tone of someone who already knew the answers.

"Not yet," Coral called back. "Tell him I'm working."

The boy went. Coral sat in the cold cabin with the chart open on his knees, and outside, high and thin, the noon whistle sounded across the water—the Thursday boat, loading her passengers—and he thought of a woman walking up a gangway with a seabag and a satchel full of maps, getting off where the world ended, starting to walk north alone.

He thought: *she'll make three miles before dark, four if the wind holds fair.*

He thought: *she'll make it to the cove or she won't.*

He thought: *and if she doesn't, no one will ever find her, because no one else knows where to look.*

His hand was already on the chart, tracing the coastline north, measuring the miles, doing the arithmetic he had sworn an hour ago not to do. Twelve feet over clean sand. Good holding. Sheltered from the north and northwest.

From the north and northwest—from exactly the quarter the weather would come from tonight, according to the glass, which had been falling since dawn.

Coral closed the chart. Then he opened it again.
