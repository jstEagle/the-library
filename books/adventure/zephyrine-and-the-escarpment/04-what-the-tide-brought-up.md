## 4. What the Tide Brought Up

The storm came in off the water the way bad news always did—quiet at first, then all at once.

Coral felt it in his teeth before he saw it, an old ache from a jaw broken years ago in a place he didn't let himself name. He was standing on the Kestrel's deck with a mug of harbor coffee going cold in his hand when the wind swung around to the northeast and the gulls went up off the breakwater all at once, like a sheet shaken out. The barometer in the wheelhouse had been falling since dawn, ticking down in small stubborn increments, and by noon the sky over the escarpment had gone the color of wet slate, the mountains behind the town swallowed whole.

He should have run for shelter further upcoast. Any sensible diver would have. But the wreck sat in forty fathoms off Grayhaven Point, and Coral had learned long ago that storms did strange favors for men who stripped dead ships—lifted silt that had lain undisturbed for decades, rolled hulls over, opened doors that had been shut since before anyone living remembered why. The navy paid him to be sensible. He paid himself to be otherwise.

He spent the worst of it at anchor in the lee of the point, Kestrel bucking her lines, rain coming down so hard the world outside the wheelhouse glass was nothing but gray noise. He drank the coffee anyway, cold as it was, because wasting things was a habit from a life he'd left behind, and he listened to the storm try to tear the anchorage apart and thought, as he always did in weather like this, of nothing at all. That was the trick of it. You could outrun almost anything if you kept your mind as empty as your moorings.

Near dusk the wind backed off to a hard blow, and the swell came in long and rolling from the open sea, and Coral stood at the rail and looked at the water over the wreck site and felt something he couldn't account for. Not dread exactly. Recognition. The way you feel when someone says your name in a crowd—a stranger's voice, using words only one other person alive knew.

* * *

He dove at first light.

The storm had passed but the sea hadn't settled; the surface was a confusion of leftover chop, and the light going down was poor, green-gray and shifting. Coral went down the anchor line anyway, hand over hand into the cold, letting the pressure close over him like a hand. At thirty feet the chop smoothed out. At sixty the world turned blue-black and quiet, and the wreck appeared below him the way it always did—gradually, then all at once, a dark shape resolving out of the gloom like a thought surfacing.

She was a coastal freighter, maybe two hundred feet of rusted steel lying on her port side across the base of the escarpment's underwater shoulder, her superstructure collapsed, her holds full of sand and silence. The charts said she'd gone down forty years ago with a load of timber. The crates Coral had been pulling out of her said otherwise. He'd burned three of them already, in a drum behind the inn, watching government-stamped seals curl and blacken, telling himself it was just salvage, just another job, just money. The harbor master asked no questions and paid too well, and Coral had built a whole life on the principle that those two things together were none of his business.

But today the wreck was different.

The storm had done what storms do. A section of seabed on her seaward side had scoured out, and the old freighter had settled another few degrees, groaning down into the sand like an old man easing into a chair. And where she'd shifted, a compartment had opened—one that hadn't been there on any of his previous dives. A seam in the hull near the former engine room, warped open by the settling, gaped now like a wound. Inside, faintly visible in the beam of his lamp, were shapes that were not sandbags and not timber.

Coral hung in the water above the opening and made himself look.

It wasn't fear. He told himself it wasn't fear. It was professional caution, the same instinct that stopped a man from putting his hand into a hole in a reef before he'd seen what lived there. He circled the opening twice, playing his lamp along the warped plating, reading the metal the way he read everything—by touch, by weight, by what it refused to say. The compartment had been sealed deliberately. Welded, once. The welds had been cut and rewelded more than once, sloppy work, recent work, work done by men in a hurry who never expected the sea to audit their books.

He went in.

The space was small, coffin-tight, silt hanging in it like breath held for decades. His lamp found crates—more of them, smaller than the ones in the holds, packed tight and lashed down with webbing that had rotted to lace. And lashed on top of them, wrapped in oilcloth that had mostly done its job, a case. Metal, flat, about the size of a ship's logbook. Stenciled on the lid, still legible under a skin of corrosion: a mark he knew. The same mark on the crates. The same mark, though he'd spent nine years making sure he'd forget it, on the manifest clipboard of a navy transport out of a harbor whose name he had taken great pains to drink away.

His heart was going hard now, a fist knocking on a door he'd nailed shut. He worked the case free with hands that wanted to shake and wouldn't, tucked it under his arm, and backed out of the compartment without taking anything else. Above him the surface was a bright uncertain ceiling. Below him the wreck settled another inch with a sound he felt through his chest rather than heard, a long steel sigh, as if the ship had been waiting years for someone to come take this particular thing away.

* * *

He didn't open it aboard the Kestrel.

That was discipline, not superstition—or he told himself it was. Some things you opened in daylight, on land, with an eye on the road and a bag half-packed. He stowed the case in the bilge locker beneath a coil of chain, scrubbed the wreck-silt off in a bucket of seawater, and ran the Kestrel into the harbor with the swell following him home like a dog that had caught a scent.

The town took the storm's measure as he tied off. Shingles down along the front street, a fishing skiff driven onto the shingle beach and left there like something washed out of the sea's pocket, the harbor master's window lit yellow against the gray morning. Coral reported the dive in three sentences—the storm, the shift, nothing else worth lifting—and watched the harbor master's face do nothing at all. That was the arrangement. Nothing at all, both directions.

Then he climbed the hill to the inn, took the end room facing the back lot, and put the case on the table between the bed and the window.

Daylight came through the glass thin and colorless, the light of a coast that had never quite decided to commit to the sun. The lock on the case was a simple naval pattern, older issue, and Coral had spent a season of his misspent youth learning locks from a bosun who'd learned them in worse places. It gave after four minutes. He sat looking at it a while longer before he lifted the lid.

A logbook. Survey-issue, water-stained but sound, its spine cracked from use rather than age. And beneath it, in a waterproof sleeve, photographs.

He read the logbook first, because reading was easier than remembering. The handwriting was small and precise, an engineer's hand, entries dated across three weeks in early autumn. A survey team, six names listed inside the front cover. Barometric readings, bearing sightings, notes on rock strata and pass elevations—all of it referencing terrain Coral knew without ever having climbed it. The escarpment. The high country inland of every town on this coast, the range that hung behind the fog in fair weather and disappeared entirely in foul, the range no chart in the harbor office bothered to detail beyond a single inked line and the word *elevation unknown*.

And threaded through the surveyor's neat figures, growing more frequent toward the final pages, a second kind of entry. Shorter. Clipped. Written fast, in the margins, sideways, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the paper.

*Convoy sighted on valley road, 0400, unlit.*

*Aircraft overhead at altitude, circling grid reference, no markings logged.*

*Requested clarification from ministry office. No reply. Requesting again.*

*We are being observed from the ridge line. H. says we are imagining it. H. has not been up to the ridge line.*

The last entry was dated nineteen days ago. After that, nothing. The book simply stopped, mid-thought, the way a clock stops—not wound down, but stopped.

Coral set it down on the table with care, the way you set down something that might go off. Then he pulled the photographs from the sleeve, and the bottom dropped out of the morning.

They were aerials and ground shots mixed together, official prints, government stock. The first several showed a valley far inland, past the escarpment's shoulders—a green fold of country with a river through it and, set into the valley's throat, a facility. Low buildings, antenna masts, a fenced compound laid out with the deliberate geometry of a place designed to be seen from above by the right people and understood by nobody else. More prints showed the interior corridors, banks of equipment, rooms of filing cabinets photographed with an archival formality that made Coral's skin crawl. Not a military installation. Something quieter. Something that filed people.

He was reaching for the last photograph when he understood, from the weight of the sleeve, that it was thicker than the rest. Two prints stuck together. He separated them with his thumbnail.

The top one was the facility again, closer, a ground-level shot of a loading dock, men in plain clothes moving crates marked with the stencil he'd burned three times behind the inn. Ordinary. Deniable. The kind of photograph that existed so that some office somewhere could swear later that it had never been taken.

The second print had been face-down against it for years, and the emulsion had taken a ghost of its neighbor: faint reversed shadows, a mirror stain. Coral turned it over and the room went very far away.

It was a photograph of men on a dock. Navy transport, aft well deck, summer light, everyone squinting. A cargo manifest clipped to a board in the foreground, and beyond it the open sea, and beyond the sea nothing at all. Six or seven men in the frame, faces half-lost in the glare. Coral knew the ship. Coral knew the dock. Coral knew, with a certainty that arrived in his body before his mind could object, the exact hour the photograph had been taken, because he had been standing on that deck not ten minutes afterward when the thing happened that had sent him running for nine years—running port to port, diving wrecks nobody named, burning crates stamped with a mark he pretended not to recognize.

He looked at the faces. Glare-eaten, silvered, half-erased by the ghost of the print that had lain against it.

One of them was his own.

Younger. Thinner. Hair longer, the way he'd worn it in a life with a different name attached to it. But there was no mistaking the set of the shoulders, the angle of the head, the particular way he'd always stood slightly apart from whatever group he was standing in—as if already planning the leaving. Nine years of careful forgetting, of new harbors and invented histories, of a name worn smooth as sea glass from handling—and the sea itself had reached into a welded compartment forty fathoms down and handed him his own face.

The logbook's final pages were still open on the table. He looked at them again now with different eyes. *We are being observed from the ridge line.* The survey team, climbing toward a facility that filed people. The convoy on the valley road. The aircraft with no markings. And somewhere in the machinery of it, a connection he couldn't yet see but could feel closing around him like cold water—the same mark, the same navy, the same erasure that had taken his name and had now, apparently, taken six surveyors off a mountain and left their tents pitched and their meals half-eaten.

Someone had sealed this case in a wreck's belly and walked away believing the sea would keep the secret. Someone had been wrong.

Outside, the light was failing. Coral became aware, distantly, that he'd been sitting motionless for hours, that the coffee cup from the morning was still beside his elbow, that the inn below was filling with storm-day voices and the smell of stew. None of it touched him. He was forty fathoms down again, hanging in the green dark above an opened compartment, understanding at last what had shifted.

Not the wreck. Him. Nine years of ballast, moved.

He gathered the photographs, squared them, slid them back into the sleeve. His hands were steady now—steadier than they'd been all day, the eerie calm that came on him when a situation passed beyond ordinary trouble into the kind you survived by becoming very quiet inside. He wrapped the logbook with them, closed the case, and looked around the little room at the things that made up a life he'd assembled to be abandoned at ten minutes' notice. Diving gear. A seabag. Nothing else worth naming.

Through the wall, muffled by plaster, he heard the innkeeper's voice below, warm and unhurried, and another voice answering it—a woman's voice, low, with an accent that put hills in it. Asking questions. He caught only fragments through the floorboards: *the escarpment... my father... passage upcoast...*

Coral stood very still in the failing light with a dead man's logbook in his hands and his own young face folded in oilcloth against his chest, and listened to a stranger ask the town about the mountains.

The tide had brought something up. It had been bringing it up for nine years, patient as water, and tonight it had finally come ashore.

He blew out the lamp and began, quietly, to pack.
