## 5. The Vanished Survey

Zephyrine had learned to read men the way her father taught her to read contour lines: by what they did when no one was watching, by the small deviations from the expected grade. Coral Voss was a study in deviation. He walked like a man perpetually leaving a room, shoulders angled toward the door even when he sat. When she'd asked him for passage upcoast that morning, he had looked at her the way a man looks at a chart with an error in it—wanting to correct it, wanting it gone.

He had said no. She had expected him to say no. So she sat down on the bollard at the end of the pier and waited, because her father had also taught her this: *a route exists even when the map says it doesn't. You just have to be more stubborn than the paper.*

The harbor worked around her all afternoon. Fishermen hosing down their decks, gulls arguing over offal, the smell of diesel and brine and old rope. A boy sold her coffee from a thermos and wouldn't take her coin, watching her instead with the frank curiosity of children in small towns, who know every stranger within an hour of arriving. She let him look. Let them all look. Being seen was the one thing she could not afford to fear anymore; she had been unseen too long, walking through government offices with her father's folded maps inside her coat, asking questions of men whose eyes slid away like water off oilcloth.

*Weather,* they kept saying. *Bad weather on the escarpment. Early season. Terrible tragedy.*

Three weeks ago, Édouard Marchand had led a six-man survey team into the high country above this coast, contracted—she now knew—to map terrain no one had ever paid to have mapped before. Her father had been a careful man. A man who checked his instruments twice and his conclusions three times. A man who had once turned back a whole expedition rather than trust a barometer he suspected. And he had known those mountains since before Zephyrine could walk. He would not have been caught by weather. Not up there. Not with the passes he knew.

She unfolded the maps again as the light went long and copper over the water. They were copies—the originals were with the authorities, "for the investigation," though no investigation seemed to be occurring anywhere she could find—and she had redrawn them herself from memory and from the survey notes he'd left in her keeping, because a cartographer's daughter does not put her whole faith in any single document. His hand was in every line. She could feel him in the confident sweep of the ridge contours, in the tiny annotations in pencil: *scree unstable after rain*, *spring here even in drought*, *cairn — shelter 2 hrs*.

And there, in the northeast quadrant where the official charts showed only blank shading and a note reading *unsurveyed*, her father's ink ran confident and fine through a corridor no government map acknowledged. A pass. A route through the escarpment's spine that appeared on no published sheet, that her father had found twenty years ago as a young man and had never reported, because he understood—even then, even before whatever it was that had happened to make him take this contract—that some knowledge is safer held quietly.

She traced it with her fingertip. The line started at a decaying fishing village two days upcoast, climbed through pine forest into a hidden valley, crossed the range by stages—a first pass along a knife-edge ridge, then a high col near the summit snows—and descended into a country marked, in her father's hand, with a single word she had stared at until the letters lost meaning: *station*.

Not *facility*. Not *installation*. Just *station*, underlined twice, and beside it, smaller, almost apologetic: *ask V.*

*Ask V.*

She had spent three weeks turning that phrase over. She had gone through her father's correspondence, his old field notebooks, the guest registers of every inn between here and the capital. And in the margin of a notebook dated eleven years back, she had found a name: *Coral Voss — Kestrel — knows the water approach*. Written in her father's hand, in the same year that a navy transport had gone upcoast carrying cargo manifests her father had later tried, unsuccessfully, to see.

So she had come looking for Coral Voss. And Coral Voss had looked at her like a man looking at his own grave.

* * *

The light failed slowly, the way it did this far north, lingering. She bought bread and hard cheese from the innkeeper—a broad, kind woman named Marta who asked no questions but watched Zephyrine with shrewd warmth, and who told her, unprompted, that Voss kept a room above the chandlery but took his meals alone, that he'd been in town five weeks, that he was "one of the quiet ones, but decent under it." Marta also mentioned, in the tone of a woman discussing tides, that a navy patrol boat had circled the bay twice that morning, slow as a shark considering.

Zephyrine ate half the bread and saved the rest. She sat on the pier until full dark, because she had decided that if Coral Voss would not come to her, she would simply become part of the harbor, and harbors wear men down eventually. Her father used to say patience was the cartographer's first tool: you cannot map a mountain in a day, and you cannot map a man in an hour either.

It was nearly midnight when boots sounded on the planks behind her.

She didn't turn. "The answer's still no," Voss said, "if that's why you're sitting on my pier in the dark."

"It's not your pier."

"Everything out here is somebody's pier." He came around into her sightline, hands in his pockets, and stood looking down at her with an expression she was beginning to catalog: wariness layered over something tired. In the lamplight from the harbor office his face was all planes and shadow, a face built for weather. "You should get a room. Marta's is warm."

"So is the truth, I'm told. Harder to find, though."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "I don't know anything about your father."

"I haven't told you about my father yet."

The silence stretched. Somewhere out in the black water, a buoy bell clanked its slow, patient count. Zephyrine got to her feet, stiff from cold, and unrolled the map against the harbor office wall, weighting the corners with stones from the beach. She watched him try not to look. She watched him fail.

"This is the escarpment," she said, tapping the blank-shaded quadrant. "According to your government, nothing up there but rock and weather. This—" her finger followed the inked corridor, "—is what my father mapped. Twenty years ago. A route through the range that appears on no official chart. He kept it private. Until this spring, when someone offered him a contract to survey the high country officially. Six men. Three weeks ago they went up." She paused. "They haven't come down."

Voss's jaw tightened. "Avalanche season starts early up there."

"You've never seen the escarpment."

"I've seen enough mountains."

"Not this one." She stepped closer, close enough to see the salt dried in the seams of his jacket, the exhaustion banked behind his eyes like coals. "My father has climbed in weather that would kill a goat. He carried me over a pass on his shoulders when I was seven, in fog so thick we navigated by the sound of meltwater. Six experienced men do not simply vanish. Their camp was found intact—tents pitched, gear stowed, food out. No bodies. No tracks leading away. The authorities called it weather and closed the file in nine days." She let that sit. "Nine days, Mr. Voss. My father's funeral would have taken longer to arrange."

"They told you the camp was intact?"

"A shepherd reported it. Then the army sealed the valley and the shepherd stopped being available for questions." She watched his face carefully as she laid each piece down, the way her father had taught her to watch a needle swing toward north. "Here's what I think happened to that needle, Mr. Voss. I think you know exactly what kind of thing makes six men disappear without tracks. I think the word *station* in my father's handwriting means something to you. And I think that's why you won't meet my eyes when I say the word *navy*."

The buoy bell counted. The water slapped the pilings. Voss stood very still, the way deep-water things go still.

"My father wrote *ask V.*," Zephyrine said quietly. "Eleven years ago. Before whatever happened, happened. He trusted you—or he trusted what you saw. Either way, he thought you were worth finding. I've spent three weeks finding you. I can spend three more. But the snows are coming early this year—I've read the pressure charts myself—and when they close the passes, they'll close them until June. Whatever window my father had, it's narrowing by the day."

"He's dead," Voss said. Flat. Not cruel—precise, the way a man is precise when he's trying to cut something short before it grows roots. "You know that. Deep country like that, three weeks—"

"Then it costs you nothing to help me prove it." She heard her own voice crack on the word *prove* and hated it, and pressed on anyway, because grief that cannot find work becomes a weight, and she refused to carry it as a weight. "If he's dead, I need his body. If he's alive, I need to reach him. If he was taken—" she watched Voss flinch, a small involuntary motion, a telltale swinging hard off true north, "—then I need to know by whom, and why, and what my father found up there that was worth erasing six men over. Those are the only three possibilities, Mr. Voss. Weather isn't one of them. Weather doesn't fold tents and pack gear and leave no tracks."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, so quietly she almost missed it beneath the bell and the water: "What else did his notes say. About the station."

"Coordinates, mostly redacted. An approach from the coast. And a warning." She recited it from memory, having read it perhaps four hundred times: *'The valley is warmer than it should be. Steam vents, or something else. Do not descend without cover of dark.'*

Something moved behind Voss's eyes—recognition, old and grim, like a scar remembered in cold weather. He looked at the map on the wall, at the blank quadrant her father's ink defied, and Zephyrine saw him wrestling with himself, two currents meeting, and she understood suddenly that she was not persuading a stranger. She was persuading a man to stop running. That was slower work, and heavier, and it could not be done in a night.

"Passage upcoast," he said finally. "That's still all I'm offering. Two days' sail, and then you're on your own."

"That's all I'm asking tonight."

He studied her as if checking her figures against his own. Whatever he found, it made him exhale through his nose, almost a laugh, entirely without humor. "Marta will give you a room. Tell her I'm paying." He turned away, then stopped, half in shadow. "Marchand. Your father. Did he ever mention a transport? Eleven years ago. Navy hull, running dark up the coast."

Zephyrine's heart struck once, hard, against her ribs. "He tried to get its manifest," she said. "He was refused. He wrote in his notebook that night: *'Whatever they carry, it weighs more than cargo should.'*" She paused. "Why?"

Voss looked out at the black water for a long time. When he answered, his voice had gone somewhere she couldn't follow—not colder, exactly, but deeper, like soundings past the shelf edge.

"Because I was on it," he said. "And I've been trying to sink it ever since."

He walked away down the pier, and Zephyrine stood alone with her maps and the bell and the enormous indifferent dark of the sea, and felt the shape of her search change in her hands—no longer a daughter hunting a missing father, but something older and stranger: two vanished things, a man and a truth, converging on the same blank space in the same forbidden quadrant. Her father had drawn the route. Coral Voss had fled whatever lay at its end. And somewhere above the treeline, in a camp with pitched tents and no tracks, the two lines met.

She rolled the maps with steady fingers. Tomorrow they would sail. Tonight she allowed herself, briefly, sitting on the cold bollard with the bread untouched in her lap, to be only a grieving woman under a strange sky—before folding that woman away, carefully, like a chart no longer needed, and going in to claim the room Voss was paying for.

In the morning, the Kestrel's deck was wet with the first sleet of the season, and Coral Voss was already at the wheel, and he did not look surprised to see her aboard—which was how she knew that sometime in the night, without saying so, he had decided to stop being a ferryman and start being something else. He cast off the lines without a word. The harbor fell away behind them, gray and shrinking, and ahead the coast ran north into low bruised cloud, toward the foot of the escarpment, toward the last village before the blank space began.

Zephyrine stood at the rail with her father's maps against her chest and watched the mountains assemble themselves out of the murk, peak by peak, like a chart drawing itself.

*Hold on,* she thought, to the ink, to the man, to the stubbornness in both. *I'm coming. And I'm bringing the witness.*
