## 6. Navy Men at the Door

The fog came in off the water before dawn, the way it always did in Grayhaven, rolling up the harbor like something patient that had learned to wait. Coral Voss stood on the deck of the Kestrel with a mug of black coffee going cold in his hand and watched it swallow the masts of the fishing fleet one by one, until the whole harbor was a gray room with gray walls and he was alone in it.

He had not slept well. That was nothing new—sleep and Coral had been uneasy partners for six years now—but this was a different quality of sleeplessness. Not the old kind, the kind that came from dreams of green-lit water and a corridor flooding behind him. This was newer. Sharper. It had teeth.

The crates.

Three of them sat lashed under the tarp in his hold, and all night he had felt them there the way a man feels a stone in his boot—not painful, exactly, but present, insisting. Sealed steel, government gray, each stamped with the same mark: a circle quartered by a vertical line, struck through with a diagonal bar. He had burned two crates stamped with that mark once, four years ago, off a different coast, in a different life, and had told himself then that it meant nothing, that marks repeat, that governments are lazy with their iconography the way they are lazy with everything else.

But the harbor master had paid too well. And asked nothing. And those two facts together made a third fact that Coral could no longer ignore: someone wanted those crates moved quietly, and someone else—someone with the authority to make harbormasters generous—wanted to know where they were going.

He drank the cold coffee anyway. Ritual mattered. The hands needed their work even when the mind refused to cooperate.

The plan, such as it remained, was simple. Finish caulking the seam above the waterline by midday. Take on water and diesel. Be out of Grayhaven before the fog lifted tomorrow, and let the crates sit in his hold like a bad conscience all the way upcoast, where he would deliver them to a nameless address on a slip of paper and take his money and never think about any of it again, the way he had never thought about anything again for six years running.

It was a good plan. It had the elegant simplicity of all his plans, which was to say it depended entirely on the world leaving him alone.

The world, as it turned out, had other arrangements.

* * *

They came at nine, when the fog had thinned to gauze and the harbor had found its morning voice—gulls, halyards, the cough of an old diesel somewhere across the water. Coral heard the launch before he saw it, a low-throated engine running too steady for a fishing boat, and looked up from the seam he was scraping to see a navy cutter sliding through the last rags of mist with its paint fresh and its gunwales clean and its ensign snapping in a wind that hadn't reached the harbor yet.

Two boats lowered. Six men. Boarding party formation—he knew it the way he knew knots, without thinking, from a life he did not talk about.

Coral set down his scraper. Very deliberately, he wiped his hands on his trousers, and very deliberately, he did not look at the hold.

"Vessel Kestrel," called the lead officer as the first boat bumped against his hull. A lieutenant commander by his braid, young around the mouth and old around the eyes, the way career officers got when they'd spent their years doing paperwork about things that never happened. "Permission to come aboard."

"This is a civilian mooring," Coral said. "And a civilian boat."

"Permission to come aboard, Mr. Voss."

The name landed like a fishhook set deep. Not *Captain*. Not *sir*. Not the neutral *skipper* strangers used. *Mr. Voss*—the name he'd been using for three years, the name on his papers, bought in a port town two thousand miles south of here from a man who forged documents the way other men whittled. It was a good name. It had held.

"Mr. Voss is my father," Coral said. "I'm Corben. Corben Hale."

Something flickered in the lieutenant commander's eyes—not surprise, not quite amusement. Something more tired than either. "May we come aboard, Mr. Hale?"

Coral weighed it. There were perhaps forty seconds between the question and the moment refusal stopped being possible; he could feel them ticking past like bubbles rising. He could refuse. They would board anyway, and then it would be a scene, and scenes drew eyes, and eyes were the one thing he could not afford.

"Aboard," he said.

* * *

They came up over the rail in a practiced rhythm, four of them, leaving two in the boat. The lieutenant commander introduced himself as Rennick, offered a hand dry as paper, and made a small show of looking around the deck—the coiled lines, the dive gear drying on the rail, the scarred wheelhouse—with the unhurried attention of a man reading a room he already owned.

"Nice boat," Rennick said. "Old hull. Good bones. You do salvage?"

"When the work's there."

"We hear you've been doing some work in the bay." Rennick's gaze drifted, casual as weather, toward the tarp amidships. "Wreck off the point. Uncharted. Interesting diving."

"I dive where the fish aren't."

"Do you." Rennick smiled with his mouth only. "Mind if we look below?"

Coral's heart was beating hard and slow, a diver's heartbeat, the kind you trained into yourself so panic couldn't find a rhythm to ride. "You got a warrant, Commander?"

"I've got a naval requisition order covering all cargo of government origin recovered from restricted waters within fifty miles of this coast." Rennick produced a folded document without looking for it, as though it had been waiting in his pocket since before dawn. "Which I suspect covers whatever's under that tarp."

The fog was burning off outside. Sunlight was coming down the harbor in long pale shafts, and in one of them Coral could see the cutter holding station at the harbor mouth, blocking the channel, patient as a closed door.

"You can look," Coral said.

Rennick nodded to his men. Two of them went aft and peeled back the tarp with the efficiency of men who had done this many times and enjoyed it none and feared it some, and there were the crates, gray and sealed and stamped, sitting in the morning light like caged animals pretending to be furniture.

Rennick looked at them for a long moment. Then he looked at Coral.

"You opened any of these?"

"No."

"Good." He said it the way a man says *good dog*, and Coral felt something hot move up the back of his throat. "These are property of the Admiralty Survey Directorate. Recovered materials. They'll be coming with us."

"They're mine," Coral said. "Salvage rights. I have a contract."

"You have a contract with a man who had no right to offer one." Rennick's voice stayed pleasant, conversational, almost gentle, and that gentleness was worse than shouting. It was the voice of someone who had already read the ending of the conversation. "Harbor master Aldous is being dealt with. You're being dealt with differently, because you're not from here, and because—" he paused, and consulted a leather notebook he drew from his coat with two fingers, as if checking a detail he didn't actually need to check— "because you've been cooperative in the past."

"I've never worked for the navy."

"No," Rennick agreed. "Not under this name."

* * *

The harbor sounds went on around them—gulls, rigging, the far-off cough of that old diesel—as though the world hadn't just tilted. Coral stood very still. Below his feet, through the hull, he could feel the tide working, the Kestrel breathing against her lines, and he fixed on that rhythm the way he'd once fixed on a regulator's rasp in black water: one thing true, one thing steady, while everything else went strange.

"What name would that be?" he said.

Rennick closed the notebook. For a moment—and Coral would remember this moment for a long time afterward, would turn it over in the dark like a stone with a fossil in it—for a moment the officer's face did something unguarded. Not cruelty. Something closer to sympathy, or its bureaucratic cousin, pity. The look of a man who has put down animals before and gotten used to it.

"Coral," Rennick said. "Petty Officer First Class Coral Voss. Hydrographic survey detachment. Reported lost with all hands, transport *Meridian*, six years ago this autumn." He tucked the notebook away. "Except you weren't lost, were you. You washed up eighty miles downcoast with a story nobody believed and a discharge nobody could process, because dead men don't get discharged. So somebody upstairs decided to leave you dead. Convenient for everyone." His eyes went to the crates. "Convenient for a while."

The name went through Coral like cold water through a torn suit. Not the name itself—he'd said it to himself often enough in the early years, standing at rails in strange ports, testing whether it still fit—but hearing it spoken aloud, here, now, attached to a rank and a ship and a date, official, filed, *known*. Six years he had built his life on a single load-bearing assumption: that he had vanished cleanly, that the sea had swallowed the record along with the ship, that no one was looking.

They had been looking the whole time. Or worse—they had known, and waited, and let him run on a long leash, and today they had simply decided to shorten it.

"The *Meridian* went down in a storm," Coral said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later, and ashamed of the pride. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"The *Meridian* didn't go down in a storm." Rennick said it quietly, almost kindly, and that was the worst cut of all, because it wasn't a threat anymore—it was information. Freely given. Which meant they wanted him to know. Which meant knowing was part of whatever came next. "She was scuttled in eleven fathoms off a coastline that doesn't appear on any chart you ever surveyed, carrying cargo she was never scheduled to carry, to a facility that doesn't exist. You saw things that night, Mr. Voss. We know what you saw, because we know everything you saw. We've known where you were every winter since." He glanced at the crates again, and this time there was something almost apologetic in it. "You should understand: you were never hiding from us. You were hiding *for* us. A loose witness is safest when he thinks he's loose."

The words settled over the deck like the fog had settled over the harbor, totalizing, airless. Coral became aware of the four sailors standing among his lines and his gear, of the two in the boat below, of the cutter at the harbor mouth, of the whole quiet apparatus of it, and understood with a clarity that felt almost like relief how long the walls had been around him. He had thought he was free range. He had been a paddock.

"Take the crates," he said.

"We're going to."

"And then?"

Rennick studied him. Behind him, his men had begun rigging a lift sling over the hold, working in silence, quick and sure.

"And then you finish your repairs, you take on your water, and you sail out of Grayhaven and keep sailing, and you forget the escarpment exists. Restricted waters extend inland, Mr. Voss. All of it. The passes, the high country, the valley beyond. Civilians who enter are presumed lost to weather. It happens." He straightened his cap. "You seem like a man who understands weather."

* * *

They took the crates up over the side one by one, slung them down to the boat, and stowed them without ceremony. The whole operation took eleven minutes. Coral stood at his own rail and watched it happen and did nothing, and the doing-nothing was the hardest physical labor of his life, harder than any dive, his arms trembling with the effort of hanging at his sides.

At the rail, before stepping down, Rennick paused.

"One more thing," he said. "There was a woman asking after you yesterday. At the inn. Dark hair, maps, French name." He watched Coral's face with professional thoroughness. "Marchand. Ring any bells?"

The tide breathed against the hull. Somewhere across the harbor a hatch banged.

"No," Coral said.

"Mm." Rennick stepped onto the ladder. "Her father's team had an accident on the mountain three weeks ago. Terrible weather up there this time of year. If she comes asking you for passage—if she asks you to go anywhere near that escarpment—you'll remember our conversation." He smiled his mouth-only smile. "Dead men can't be arrested, Mr. Voss. But they can be *finished*. There's paperwork for it. There's paperwork for everything."

The launch pulled away. The cutter turned at the harbor mouth, slow and stately, and slid north into the brightening day, and the channel stood open again, empty, as though nothing had passed through it at all.

* * *

Coral did not move for a long time.

He stood at the rail with his hands gripping it, knuckles white, watching the water where the cutter had been, and inside him two currents ran against each other with a force that made his teeth ache. The first was the oldest current, the one that had carried him six years and a hundred ports: *run*. Slip out tonight, tonight, not tomorrow, dump the fuel drums if you have to, run south past the edge of the charts and find a new name and a new sea and never, never say the word *escarpment* again. That current was strong. It had kept him alive. It knew his name.

But underneath it, new, unwelcome, rising like something off a wreck site: *they knew*. They had known all along. Every port he'd chosen, every winter he'd survived, every careful anonymity—he had lived six years inside a cage and called it freedom, and the men who owned the cage had watched him pace it and found it convenient. The humiliation of that was a live coal. And coals, Coral knew—coals did one of two things. They went out. Or they started fires.

He thought of the *Meridian*, and made himself think of it, really, for the first time since the night itself: the green-lit corridor, the crates going over the side weighted, the coastline where no coastline should be, the lights inland—lights, plural, arranged in rows, in a valley that appeared on no chart he'd ever drawn or seen. He had told himself for six years that he'd imagined the lights. That shock and cold water had invented them.

They hadn't been invented. Rennick had all but handed him the coordinates.

And Marchand. *Dark hair, maps.* She had stood on this deck two days ago with her father's charts and looked at Coral as if she could see the watermark in him, the stamp beneath the surface, and he had sent her away and she had waited anyway, and now the navy knew her name too, and knew she'd asked after him, and had mentioned her casually, deliberately, the way you drop a lure into water you've already decided to fish.

They were using her to test him. Or they were going to use her the way they'd used the survey team. Either way, the leash had a hook in it now, and the hook had her name on it.

Coral looked down the length of his boat—at the empty space in the hold where the crates had been, at the dive gear on the rail, at the little brass compass his father had given him bolted by the wheel, pointing forever and uselessly north—and felt something shift in his chest that frightened him more than Rennick had. It was the feeling of a man whose boat has slipped her mooring in the night. The feeling of no longer being tied to the plan.

He went below, pulled the floor panel over the bilge, and took out the oilskin packet he kept there: his real papers, the ones from the forged man, and beneath them, folded twice, a chart he had drawn himself six years ago from memory and never once looked at since. The coastline that wasn't there. The valley with the lights.

He spread it on the chart table and smoothed it flat with both hands, and the creases cracked like small bones.

Outside, the fog was gone entirely now, and the sun lay hard and silver on the harbor, and up the hill at the inn, he knew, a woman with dark hair was waiting for an answer he had already, without meaning to, begun to give.
