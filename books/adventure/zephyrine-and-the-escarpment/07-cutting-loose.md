## 7. Cutting Loose

The harbor master's office smelled of pipe smoke and old ledger paper, and Coral stood in it for the last time counting money he would not collect.

"Salvage fee's due Friday," Pellam said, not looking up from his books. He was a narrow man built like a mooring post, all weathered grain and iron at the center, and he had asked Coral nothing in six weeks of business together. That had been the arrangement. That had been the whole beauty of it.

"I know."

"You're leaving before Friday." It wasn't a question. Pellam had watched boats come and go for forty years; he could read departure in the way a man stood, in where his eyes went when the window showed him the water.

"The Kestrel's patched. No reason to burn your berth space."

Pellam set down his pen. For a long moment the only sound was the tick of the harbor clock and, beneath it, the sea working against the stone quay like something patient chewing.

"There's a navy tender anchored past the point," Pellam said. "Been there two days. You'll have noticed."

Coral said nothing.

"They came to my office Tuesday asking after you. Your name, your boat, how long you'd been in port." Pellam finally looked up, and his eyes were flat and gray as slate in winter. "I told them I don't keep records on men who pay cash. Which is true. But they wrote down your hull number anyway, Voss, because they were standing on my quay looking right at it."

The word *Voss* sat in the room between them. Not his name—not anymore, not for seven years—but close enough to it that his skin tightened along the spine.

"Appreciate the warning," Coral managed.

"Don't appreciate it. Just take it." Pellam returned to his ledger. "And leave by the north channel. The tender sits on the south approach like a dog watching a door."

* * *

He walked back down through the town in the blue hour, when the lamps were lit but the sky hadn't given up its light entirely, and everything existed twice—once in flame and once in fading gold. Gulls argued over the fish sheds. Somewhere a woman was singing to a child, some tuneless local thing that rose and fell like the tide itself.

Six weeks he'd been here. Long enough to patch the Kestrel's planking, recaulk her seams, replace the fouled propeller shaft. Long enough that the innkeeper, Marta, had stopped asking when he'd be moving on and started leaving an extra roll on his plate without comment. Long enough that he'd begun, dangerously, to recognize faces—to expect them. The blacksmith's boy who nodded each morning. The old netmender who talked at him about herring runs whether or not Coral answered.

That was how it always went wrong. Not the navy finding him. The staying.

Except this time it wasn't only that.

She was waiting at the foot of the pier, exactly where she'd been every evening for four days, sitting on a bollard with her father's maps rolled in an oilcloth tube across her knees, watching the water as if it owed her something. Zephyrine Marchand. She didn't look up when he approached, which he understood was itself a kind of performance—she always knew precisely where he was.

"No," he said, before she could open her mouth.

"You haven't heard what I was going to say."

"You were going to say the wreck shifted again after the storm. You were going to say there's more in her than crates, and that the survey team that vanished up the escarpment was looking at the same government mark I've been pulling out of the hold. And then you were going to ask me again to take you upcoast." He crouched beside her, forearms on his knees, and looked out at the darkening bay rather than at her. "I've heard it. Four nights running."

"And four nights running you've said no while checking the southern horizon every time you think I'm not watching." She turned to face him then, and even in the half-light her eyes caught what remained of the day—gray-green, unblinking, a cartographer's eyes that measured everything they landed on. "You're not sleeping, Coral. You're deciding."

"I'm diving a wreck and minding my own business."

"You burned a crate stamp in your galley stove three nights ago. I saw the ash. Stenciled paper doesn't burn gray like that unless it's coated." A pause. "My father's field reports used the same stock."

The sea breathed against the pilings beneath them. Far out, past the breakwater, the navy tender rode at anchor with a single light burning, patient as a spider.

"Your father," Coral said slowly, "went into the mountains. Three weeks ago. With five other people. And the government told everyone it was weather."

"Yes."

"And you have his maps, and on those maps is a route that isn't on any official chart, and you want me to run you upcoast to the foot of it because no one else will go near the place since the survey team vanished." He shook his head. "I'm not a ferry, Zephyrine. I'm not anything to anybody. That's how I stay alive."

"That's how you stay *nothing*," she said, and the words landed harder than any blow he'd taken in years. "There's a difference."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He sat in the Kestrel's cramped cabin with the lamp turned low and the salvage fee—Pellam's envelope, heavy with notes—sitting on the chart table like a reproach, and he tried to do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for four days.

The photographs. He kept coming back to the photographs.

They'd been wrapped in oilskin inside the compartment the storm had cracked open, tucked behind the logbook of a man named E. Marchand—*her* father, though Coral hadn't known it then, hadn't made the connection until she'd said the surname and the world had tilted under him like a deck in a swell. Six men in cold-weather gear at the mouth of a valley. A concrete structure half-swallowed by the hillside, antenna arrays bristling from its roof like the spines of a sleeping animal. And in the corner of the third photograph, blurred but unmistakable, a younger man in a navy transport uniform, holding a clipboard, looking directly into the camera with the expression of someone who has just seen something he will spend the rest of his life outrunning.

Himself. Twenty-six years old and already gone.

He'd stood on that transport's deck nine years ago and watched crates marked with that same seal being loaded inland under guard, and he'd asked one question too many of one officer too senior, and within a month his service record had ceased to exist and so, officially, had he. He'd assumed it was routine erasure—the navy tidying up an inconvenient witness. He'd never imagined the thing he'd witnessed was still operating. Still growing. Still swallowing whole survey teams and handing their families a story about snow.

But now the navy tender sat in the bay. Now officers had boarded his boat and spoken his old name aloud like a key turning in a lock. Now a woman whose father had walked into that valley carrying a route no chart admitted to was asking Coral Voss to sail toward the one place in the world he'd spent nine years sailing away from.

Pellam's envelope. His anonymity. His life, such as it was—a life measured in harbors, in cash transactions, in innkeepers who learned not to ask. All of it balanced on the chart table against a rolled tube of oilcloth and a woman on a bollard who had looked at him four days ago and said, *You've seen it too, haven't you? Whatever's up there. It's written all over you.*

He blew out the lamp.

* * *

Moving at night through a harbor town required a different body than the one Coral used by day. By day he was deliberate, unhurried, a man with nowhere particular to be. At midnight he became what the navy had trained him to be before they erased him: quiet, economical, a shadow that moved along walls and counted the seconds between a sentry's footsteps.

The tender's searchlight swept the south channel every ninety seconds. He timed it from the darkness of the fish shed, Zephyrine beside him with her pack already shouldered, her father's maps strapped tight. She hadn't asked questions when he'd appeared at her lodgings window an hour ago and tapped the glass. She'd simply opened it, climbed through, and followed—which told him something about her that both steadied and unsettled him. This was a woman who had already decided, long before he had, and had simply been waiting for the world to catch up.

"North channel," he murmured. "We go along the seawall, low, until we're past the last lamp. Then it's thirty meters of open stone to the ladder down to the lower quay. The Kestrel's moored there."

"And the watchman?"

"Drinks. From eleven until roughly one. We have twenty minutes if his wife hasn't hidden the bottle again."

Zephyrine almost smiled. In the dark it was mostly a change in the shape of her breathing. "You've done this before."

"I've done everything before. That's the problem with me."

They moved. The seawall was wet and smelled of kelp and tar, and below it the harbor water slapped black and oily against the stones, full of reflected lamplight that broke and reformed like scattered coins. Coral counted the tender's sweep—one, two, three—and moved between the beams, Zephyrine's breath quick and controlled at his shoulder. She was frightened. He could hear it. But her feet found the holds he indicated without hesitation, and once, when a drunk lurched out of a doorway ahead of them, she pulled him into an alcove with a grip far stronger than her frame suggested and held him there, both of them pressed chest to chest in the dark, her heart hammering against his ribs, until the man staggered on singing.

For one absurd moment Coral forgot to be afraid at all.

Then the moment passed, and the ladder was there, and the lower quay, and the Kestrel riding at her lines like a bird pretending to sleep.

"Below," he whispered. "Forward compartment. There's a hatch under the spare canvas—if anyone boards, you're not aboard. Understood? You were never aboard. I'm a salvager running north ahead of a debt."

"Coral—"

"*Understood.*"

A pause. Then, quietly, with terrible gentleness: "Understood. Go start your engine before I lose respect for you."

* * *

The Kestrel's engine turned over on the third crank, coughed, caught, and settled into its familiar uneven heartbeat—and every sound in the world suddenly mattered. Coral stood at the helm with his hands on the wheel and listened to his own boat betray him: the rattle of the loose fitting he'd meant to fix, the slap of wake against the hull, the thin whistle of wind in the stays. Behind him, forward, silence where a woman was hiding. Ahead, the harbor mouth opening onto black water and the first suggestion of stars.

The tender's searchlight swept the south channel. Ninety seconds. He had the north passage to himself, a throat of dark water between the breakwater rocks, unlit, unwatched, exactly as Pellam had promised.

*Why*, he thought, hauling in the mooring lines with hands that knew the work too well to shake. *Why now. Why her. You could cast off alone tonight and be a hundred miles away by dawn and nobody in this world would ever connect Coral Voss to E. Marchand or a missing survey team or a listening station in the hills. You could keep the fee. Keep the name. Keep the nothing you've built.*

The bow line came free. The stern line. The Kestrel drifted a hand's width off the quay, and he put the wheel over and fed her throttle, and the engine note rose, and the black water began to slide.

Because, he thought, because for nine years running had felt like falling—gravity, not choice, the same motion over and over, port to port, always downhill, always away. Because a man can only fall for so long before he starts to wonder what it would feel like to move toward something instead. Because a woman had sat on a bollard for four evenings and looked through him like glass and said *that's how you stay nothing*, and she was right, God help him, she was right, and he was forty-one years old and tired of being a rumor in his own life.

Because somewhere up a mountain there was a journal with his name in it, and he wanted—needed—to read the ending in his own handwriting for once.

The breakwater slid past. The harbor light shrank to a smudge, then a spark, then nothing, and the open sea took the Kestrel into its long slow breathing, and Coral Voss stood at the wheel of his own boat heading toward the one coast he'd sworn never to see again, and discovered that terror and exhilaration, at sufficient speed, were indistinguishable.

The hatch to the forward compartment opened. Zephyrine climbed out into the cockpit, hair loose, salt wind immediately taking it, and stood beside him at the rail looking back at the town that had asked nothing of either of them.

"You could still turn around," she said.

"No," Coral said. "I couldn't. That's rather the point."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, the first he'd ever heard from her—and then went forward to stand watch, and the Kestrel ran north through the dark with the escarpment waiting somewhere beyond the curve of the world, a gray wall on the edge of every map, and on its flank, faint as breath on glass, a route that no official chart had ever dared to draw.

It was two hours later, with the coast a black seam astern and the first thin light graying the east, that Coral saw it: a wake on the southern horizon, straight and military and closing, the white feather of a tender pushing hard against the morning calm.

They had noticed. Of course they had noticed.

"Zephyrine," he called forward, hand steady on the wheel, eyes fixed on that approaching line of foam. "Get the charts out. We're going to need another way in."
