## 8. The Last Harbor

The village appeared out of the fog the way a memory does—half-believed at first, then suddenly, unmistakably real.

Zephyrine saw it from the bow of the Kestrel while Coral worked the wheel behind her: a scatter of gray roofs clinging to the shore like barnacles, a stone jetty reaching into the water with one arm broken off at the elbow, and above it all a single crooked chimney sending up smoke so thin it seemed ashamed to be seen. There was no lighthouse. There was no harbor bell. There was only the smell of fish and peat smoke and cold iron, carried out to them on a wind that had begun to taste of winter.

"Saint-Aubin-des-Écueils," she said, reading the name off her father's map before Coral could ask. "The Saint of Sunken Things."

Coral glanced at her. "You've been here."

"No." She kept her eyes on the shore. "But my father has. Eleven times, according to his logbooks. He always resupplied here before going inland." She tapped the map where a small hand-drawn anchor marked the jetty, and beside it, in her father's cramped, patient script: *Ask for Odile Ferrand. Tell her the heron still flies.* "He trusted someone here enough to write it down. My father didn't write down people he didn't trust."

The Kestrel slid through the water with the low, tired grace of an animal that has been running too long. They had come out of the harbor at Sainte-Marine two nights ago under a moonless sky, Coral's hands steady on the lines while the navy patrol boat's lights swept the breakwater behind them, and they had run north along the coast since, sleeping in shifts, speaking little. Zephyrine had expected to feel afraid the whole way. Instead she felt something stranger—a kind of terrible lightness, as though by cutting loose she had finally set down something heavy she'd forgotten she was carrying.

Now the jetty rose to meet them, its pilings furred with weed, and Coral brought the boat alongside with a gentleness that surprised her. He handled the Kestrel the way other men handled things they loved but would never say so aloud.

"Stay aboard," he said, coiling the line. "I'll see if anyone's even left."

"Someone's left," Zephyrine said. "There's smoke."

He looked at the thin thread rising from the chimney, then at her, and something in his face shifted—the wariness giving way, for just a moment, to something almost like relief. "Smoke could mean anything."

"It means breakfast," said a voice from the jetty, cracked as old rope. "And if you're who I think you are, it means I've been waiting three weeks for you to be stupid enough to come."

* * *

Odile Ferrand was small and brown and weathered down to essentials, like driftwood, with eyes the pale gray of the sea before a storm. She stood at the head of the jetty with a gaff hooked over her shoulder as though she'd been born holding it, and when Zephyrine climbed up beside Coral, the old woman looked at her for a long moment without blinking.

"You have his chin," Odile said at last. "Étienne Marchand's chin. Stubborn as a mussel stuck to a hull."

"You knew him?"

"I fed him eleven times over nine years." The gaff came down and pointed toward the village. "And now I feed his daughter. That's how it works in Saint-Aubin. Debts don't die, they just change pockets. Come. The kettle's on and the herring won't smoke themselves."

The village was dying—that much was plain within fifty steps. Half the houses stood empty, their windows dark or boarded, their doorways filling with sand blown up from the beach. Nets hung rotting on frames nobody tended. A dog followed them at a distance, ribs showing through its coat, watching with the patient hunger of an animal that had learned not to hope. But the house Odile led them to was alive: wood stacked to the eaves, herbs drying under the porch roof, a garden of hardy greens defying the season in raised beds built from old dory planks.

Inside, the room was warm and dim and smelled of peat smoke, tar, and thyme. Odile put them in chairs, put tea in their hands, and put bread and smoked fish on the table without asking whether they wanted any, because in her house wanting was assumed.

"Eat first," she said. "Talk after. Talking on an empty stomach is how lies get started."

So they ate. Zephyrine hadn't realized how hollow she was until the food hit her—the bread dense and dark, the fish rich with salt and alder smoke, the tea bitter enough to make her eyes sting. Across the table, Coral ate the way he did everything: efficiently, watchfully, as though eating were another task to be completed without being caught at it. But Zephyrine noticed that he took a second helping, and then a third, and that Odile pretended not to count.

Only when the plates were cleared did Odile sit back, fold her hands, and say, "Now. Tell me why the navy circled this coast twice yesterday, and why a girl with Étienne Marchand's chin comes up it in a stolen-looking boat with a man whose face says he's been running since before she was born."

* * *

Zephyrine told her most of it. Not everything—she held back the logbook, the photographs, the compartment in the wreck—but she gave Odile the shape of it: the survey team gone, the official story of bad weather, the maps, the flight from Sainte-Marine, the navy officers who had boarded the Kestrel and spoken Coral's old name like a sentence being passed.

Odile listened without interrupting, which Zephyrine learned was her way of measuring people. When it was done, the old woman got up, stirred the fire, and stood looking into it for a long time, her back to them both.

"Your father sat in that chair," she said finally, nodding at the one Coral occupied. "Eleven times. And every time, he asked me the same question: *has anything changed in the valley?* And every time, for nine years, I told him: nothing changes in the valley, Étienne. Nothing ever changes there. The valley doesn't let anything change." She turned around. "Last spring, I would have had a different answer."

Zephyrine's hands went still around her cup. "What happened last spring?"

"A man came. Not navy—not in uniform, anyway. A suit, a soft voice, papers with government seals on them. He bought the valley." Odile said it flatly, the way you'd report a tide or a death. "All of it. Every parcel from the treeline to the col. There were maybe forty people living up there—shepherds, a couple of families who'd farmed the same ground since before anyone wrote anything down. The man paid them triple what the land was worth and told them they had until the thaw to move. Most took the money. What else could they do? You can't fight paper with sheep."

"And those who wouldn't sell?"

Odile's jaw tightened. "The Brossard family wouldn't. Old Henri said his grandfather's bones were in that ground and he wasn't selling his grandfather twice. In June, Henri went down to the valley churchyard to tend the graves. He didn't come back. His wife went looking. She didn't come back either. The navy came through the village that week asking questions about trespassers in a restricted zone, and after that nobody asked about the Brossards anymore, because asking had become the kind of thing that got you visited."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the dog barked once at nothing and fell silent again, as if even it had decided caution was wiser than voice.

"They bought the whole valley," Zephyrine said slowly. "Before the survey team even went in."

"That's the part your father never knew," Odile said. "He planned his route years ago, when the passes were open country and a shepherd's bothy was a place you could sleep without permission. But the ground changed under his map, girl. Whatever he found up there—or whatever they thought he'd find—they'd already made sure no one else could walk in and see it." She looked at Coral. "And you. You knew about the valley already, didn't you? That's what the girl isn't saying, and it's what your face has been saying since you walked in."

Coral set down his cup. For a moment Zephyrine thought he would do what he always did—go smooth and closed, give the polite nothing he'd given innkeepers and harbor masters all along the coast. But something in Odile's flat gray stare seemed to make evasion impossible, the way you can't lie to a doctor about pain.

"I knew there was a facility," he said quietly. "I didn't know they'd emptied the land around it. That's new. That's—" He stopped, and Zephyrine watched him arrive at the arithmetic she'd already done. "That means they weren't surprised by the survey team. They were waiting for it."

"Or they invited it," Odile said, "and closed the door behind it."

The words settled over the room like frost. Zephyrine thought of her father's last letter, cheerful and vague, full of talk about *final corrections to the route* and *one more season and the map will be true*. She had read it a hundred times looking for a hidden warning and found none. Now she understood there had been none to find. Her father hadn't known he was walking into a trap. He had simply been walking into a place where traps had been laid years in advance, patiently, with government seals and triple payment, and the map he loved so much had been obsolete before he ever folded it into his pack.

"The passes," she said aloud. "Are they watched?"

"Some. The eastern routes, certainly—the ones a road could reach. The navy runs trucks up the logging cut twice a week; I hear them from the ridge when the wind's right." Odile came back to the table and sat, and for the first time her voice lost some of its flint. "But your father didn't take the eastern routes, did he. He took the old way. The herders' way. The one that isn't on any chart because the chartmakers never thought to ask a shepherd."

Zephyrine touched the map case at her side. "The hidden route."

"He showed me once, years ago, laughing about it. Said the best mapping secret in the world was a path everyone used and nobody wrote down." Odile's eyes went soft for a moment—an old grief moving behind them like a fish under ice. "It starts at the mouth of the Ravin Noir, past the fallen bridge. It's steep, and it's exposed above the treeline, and if the snows come early this year—and they will, the birds went south a month ahead of themselves—it will close like a fist. But it's not watched. Nobody watches a path that isn't supposed to exist."

"Then we leave tomorrow," Coral said.

"You leave tonight," Odile said.

Both Zephyrine and Coral looked at her.

"There's a patrol boat working the coast between here and the point—I counted it twice today, and I don't count wrong. Somebody told them you're moving north. Which means somebody in Sainte-Marine talked, which means the harbor master's silence was always rented, not owned." She was already up, moving through the room with sudden purpose, pulling a canvas pack from under the bed, filling it with the economy of long practice. "You eat, you rest till dark, and then I put you ashore at the Ravin Noir myself, in the dory, where no patrol boat looks because there's nothing there worth looking at. That's how your father liked it too. Invisible paths and invisible landings."

Coral was quiet for a moment. Then: "Why are you doing this? You know what happens to people who help. The Brossards—"

"The Brossards are dead because they stayed and fought paper with sheep," Odile said, not turning around. "I'm not staying and I'm not fighting. I'm feeding travelers and pointing them at a path, same as I've done for nine years, and if that makes me guilty of something, then half the coastline's guilty with me." She paused, her hands full of dried meat and hard biscuit, and her voice dropped. "Besides. Étienne Marchand sat in that chair and ate my bread eleven times, and I never once asked him what he was really mapping. I'm not going to carry that to my grave a twelfth time."

She thrust the pack at Coral, who took it. And then, unexpectedly, she crossed the room to Zephyrine, took her face in both rough hands, and studied her the way she might study a chart—looking for depth, for hazards, for the truth beneath the surface.

"He talked about you," Odile said. "Every visit. *My Zephyrine draws better coastlines than I do. My Zephyrine found an error in the hydrographic office charts when she was fourteen.* He was proud of you the way mountains are proud—quietly, and all the way down." Her thumbs brushed Zephyrine's cheekbones, rough as rope. "Bring him home. Or bring home the truth of him. Either one. But bring something back, girl. Don't let that valley keep everything."

Zephyrine couldn't speak. She nodded instead, and Odile released her, satisfied, and began wrapping smoked fish in oilcloth as though nothing had happened at all.

* * *

They went out with the last of the light.

The dory slipped along the base of the cliffs where the water ran black and glassy, Odile sculling with short, silent strokes, the Kestrel left swinging at her mooring behind the jetty like a promise they intended to keep. Above them the escarpment rose out of the dusk, vast and indifferent, its upper reaches already white. Zephyrine watched it grow against the darkening sky and felt the strange double weight she'd carried since the camp—grief and purpose fused into a single load, impossible to set down, impossible any longer to tell apart.

Her father was somewhere inside that mass of stone and snow. Alive, if the restacked cairn meant what she hoped it meant. Held, if the valley's new owners did what owners did. Either way, he was up there, and for the first time in three weeks the distance between them was measurable—in hours of climbing, in camps, in the length of a hidden path drawn in her own hand from her father's notes.

"There." Odil's whisper, and the gaff pointing. "Ravin Noir."

A seam of darkness split the cliff face, barely wider than the dory was long, exhaling the smell of wet pine and cold stone. Beyond its mouth, faint in the last gray light, Zephyrine could make out the shattered timbers of the fallen bridge her father had marked, and beyond that, climbing into shadow, the ghost of a path that existed on no chart but hers.

Odile backed water and let the dory kiss the shingle. "End of my map," she said softly. "Beginning of yours."

Coral stepped out first, boots crunching, and stood looking up into the ravine with the expression of a man reading his own sentence. Zephyrine gathered the map case and followed, and when she turned to thank the old woman, Odile pressed one last thing into her hand: a small tin lamp, its glass clouded with age.

"Your father left it on his eleventh visit," she said. "Said he'd collect it on the twelfth." Her voice roughened. "Go collect him."

Then the dory was pulling away, a darker shape on dark water, and the ravine swallowed them, and the climb began.

Above the treeline, unseen in the gathering night, snow was already falling on the high col—soft, silent, and early, like a door beginning, gently, to close.
