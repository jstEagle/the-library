## 9. Foot of the Escarpment

The valley that held the last harbor ended where the pines began, and the pines went up forever.

Coral stood at the treeline's edge with his pack straps biting into shoulders still salt-raw from three days of hauling the Kestrel through open water, and looked at what Marchand's map promised. The paper was soft as cloth from handling, its ink faded to the color of old bruises, and it showed a thread of a line climbing northeast through forest that no official chart bothered to name. No contour marks. No trailhead. Just the line, and beside it, in a cartographer's cramped hand: *the old drover's way — keep the water on your left until it forgets itself.*

"Until it forgets itself," Coral read aloud. "That's not navigation. That's poetry."

"It's my father." Zephyrine shouldered her pack without looking at him. She'd repacked it twice before dawn, checking and rechecking rope lengths, ration weights, the oilskin wrapping of the maps. "He never trusted a map that told you everything. He said a map should be a conversation with the land, not an order."

"And if the land doesn't feel like talking?"

"Then you listen harder."

She set off into the trees and he followed, because there was nowhere else to go. That was the truth of it now, unadorned: the Kestrel was tied up in a dead village's rotting harbor, the salvage fee abandoned on a navy officer's clipboard, and every port he'd ever outrun to had just become a place he could never return to. He had spent eleven years making himself light enough to vanish—no debts, no letters, no names—and in one night he'd picked up a woman, a map, and a war he didn't understand.

The forest took them gently at first. Old growth pine, the trunks so wide their shadows pooled like standing water, the floor cushioned with needles that swallowed their bootfalls whole. Light came down in long amber shafts, thick with pollen and the smell of resin heating in the morning sun. Somewhere above them a woodpecker worked at something dead, patient as a clockmaker. Coral found himself matching his breathing to the rhythm of the climb, four steps in, four steps out, the way he breathed off a regulator—deliberate, metered, hoarded. Diving taught you that. The air you carried was all the air there was.

By midmorning the gentleness was gone. The slope steepened until they climbed more with hands than feet, hauling on roots and ledges of blue-gray rock, and the pines narrowed to spindly things clinging like drowning men. Sweat soaked through his shirt and turned cold whenever the wind found him. His calves burned with a deep, mineral ache, different from the burn of swimming—the sea pushed back evenly, everywhere at once, but a mountain pushed back only where it touched you, and it touched you cruelly.

Zephyrine climbed like she'd been born on this grade. She moved in a low, economical crouch when the going got steep, tested each hold before trusting it, and never once stopped to check her direction. Twice she paused at forks where he saw nothing but two identical walls of trees, laid two fingers against the bark of one trunk or studied a stone half-buried in moss, and chose. Both times, minutes later, they crossed a stream exactly where the faded line said they would.

"How," he said, the second time, working to keep his voice level around his breathing.

She glanced back. Her cheeks were flushed dark with exertion, a smear of pine pitch along one jaw, and her eyes had the particular brightness of someone doing exactly what she was made for. "My father drew this route when I was fourteen. We walked it together, summer after summer. He made me lead the last three years." She turned and kept climbing. "He said if anything ever happened to him, the route would live in my feet even if the map burned."

*If anything ever happened to him.* She said it the way sailors say *if the boat goes down*—flat, rehearsed, a sentence carried so long it had worn smooth. Coral said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't either pity or a lie, and she'd have thrown both back at him.

They ate at noon beside the stream, which by then had grown opinionated, tumbling down over granite shelves in white ropes. The water was cold enough to hurt his teeth. Zephyrine spread the map on a flat rock and weighted its corners with stones, and Coral leaned over it, grateful for any excuse to stand still.

"We make the first pass tomorrow," she said. Her finger traced the line up through a notch between two peaks drawn with hard, confident strokes. "There's a ridge crossing—my father called it the knife, because that's what it looks like from below. Then we descend into a hanging valley and camp. Water, shelter from the wind, and—" her finger stopped, tapped twice, "—a cairn. He built one there. Every survey team used it as a waypoint."

"You think he might have—"

"I think someone restacked it." She said it quietly, and wouldn't look at him. "I know how it sounds. But stone doesn't restack itself, Coral. If we find that cairn standing tall and fresh-turned, then somebody came through. Recently. Somebody who knew what it meant."

Somebody alive. Somebody who might be a half-starved surveyor, or might be a navy tracker who knew the route too. Coral looked at the little penciled triangle on the map—a waypoint, a hope, a trap; there was no way to tell which until they were standing in it—and felt the old diver's calculus turn over in his chest. You didn't get to choose what was down there in the dark. You only got to choose whether you went down after it.

"We'll know tomorrow," he said.

"Yes." She began rolling the map, and her hands were very steady, and he understood suddenly that the steadiness cost her everything she had.

* * *

The afternoon broke him a little.

It started with scree—a long gray fan of broken stone fanning down from some collapse high above, the kind of slope where every step slid half a step back and the rock rang hollow underfoot like crockery. Zephyrine crossed it quickly, reading the stable lines the way she read streams, picking a zigzag path that looked random and wasn't. Coral followed her line exactly, and it still nearly killed him.

Halfway across, a slab the size of a door shifted under his left foot. Not dramatically—just a slow, grinding tilt, the mountain shrugging—and then the slope beneath him was moving, a hissing cascade of fist-sized stones pouring away downhill, taking his balance with it. He threw himself sideways onto his pack, arms out, spreading his weight the way you spread your weight on thin ice, and lay there with his heart slamming while the slide rattled down fifty meters below him and went quiet.

"Coral." Zephyrine's voice, tight. She'd frozen ten meters upslope. "Don't move. Tell me what's under you."

"Rock."

"What *kind.* Flat slabs or loose?"

He eased his head around. Beneath his ribs, a broad slab, seated solid. Around it, smaller stuff, locked in place by friction and luck. "Big slab under me. Rest looks seated."

"Then crawl. Don't stand. Crawl to me along the big stones—the ones with lichen. Lichen means they haven't moved in years."

He crawled. It was undignified and terrifying and it worked, and when he pulled himself up onto solid ground beside her his legs were shaking and his shirt was torn across the shoulder and blood had run warm down his arm from somewhere he couldn't identify yet.

She looked at the blood, then at his face. "You could have waited for me to pick your line."

"I was picking yours."

"That's not the same thing." But something in her voice had changed—not softer, exactly. Recalibrated. She dug a bandage from her pack and dressed the gash on his forearm with quick, impersonal competence, her fingers cool and sure, and while she worked she talked, low, the way you talk to keep a man's mind off a wound. "You dive, yes? Professionally?"

"Salvage."

"Deep?"

"Deep enough." He watched her tie off the bandage. "You learn to spend your air like money. Move slow, panic never, and always know where the surface is."

"And here?" She nodded uphill, at the whole gray weight of the escarpment. "Where's the surface?"

He considered it honestly. "Haven't found it yet."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. "My father would have liked you. He said the best navigators are the ones who've been lost somewhere worse."

They made the treeline proper by late afternoon—real treeline, where the pines gave out all at once into a world of dwarf birch and stone and sky, the air gone thin and clean and cold enough to taste like metal. Below them the forest they'd climbed through all day lay folded into the valley like dark green velvet, and beyond it, impossibly far and small, a glint of the sea. Coral stood looking at that glint for longer than he meant to. Eleven years he'd kept the sea close, kept it as his road and his cover and his whole geography. From up here it looked like what it probably had always been: a very large place to hide.

"Your arm will stiffen," Zephyrine said behind him. "We should camp while there's light to gather wood."

* * *

They camped in a hollow below the last trees, a wind-shadow of granite and dwarf pine with the stream running past close enough to hear. Coral built the fire small and shielded—old habit, though who would see smoke up here he couldn't say—and they ate hard bread and smoked fish from the last harbor, and the stars came out in numbers that seemed almost aggressive after a lifetime of coastal haze.

His arm throbbed. His legs felt poured full of wet sand. Every muscle he'd built for swimming had betrayed him by noon, and muscles he hadn't known he owned had filed their complaints since. And yet—

"You're stronger than you look," Zephyrine said, watching him feed a branch to the fire. "Most men would have quit at the scree."

"Most men are smarter than I look, too."

"That I believe." She wrapped her arms around her knees. Firelight moved on her face, and for a moment she looked very young and very tired, and then it passed and she was only steady again. "I keep thinking about the camp. Three weeks, and no bodies, no tracks. Gear intact. Meals half-eaten—that's the part I can't get past. You don't leave a meal unless you leave *fast*, or unless you're made to."

"Navy extraction teams don't leave tracks," Coral said, before he could stop himself.

Her head came around slowly. "You've seen one."

"I've *been* adjacent to one." He fed the fire another branch, buying seconds. "Years ago. A transport, a fog bank, men going over the side at night with gear that doesn't exist in any catalog. I asked one question too many and spent the next decade being paid too well by people who asked nothing." He looked at her across the flames. "You deserve to know that before we're higher than this. Whatever your father found on this mountain—it's the same thing that's been hunting me since before you ever heard my name."

The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope, the stream kept talking to itself in the dark.

Zephyrine didn't flinch, didn't pull away. She studied him for a long moment with those bright, exhausted, unflinching eyes, and then she nodded once, slowly, like a navigator confirming a fix. "Good," she said. "Now we're navigating by the same chart."

She rolled into her bedroll facing the fire, and within minutes her breathing slowed into sleep—trust offered that simply, that completely, after everything he'd just confessed. Coral sat up alone with the small fire and the enormous cold, keeping the first watch, and told himself the tightness in his chest was just the altitude.

Near dawn, half-dreaming against his pack, he heard it: a sound from the ridge above, faint but unmistakable on the frozen air. Stone clicking against stone. Rhythmic. Deliberate.

Like a cairn being built.
