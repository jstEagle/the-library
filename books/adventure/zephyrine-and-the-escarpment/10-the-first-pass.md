## 10. The First Pass

The map said the ridge was a day's climb. The mountain disagreed.

Zephyrine felt it first in the way the forest changed—pines thinning, then stooping, then giving up altogether somewhere below the cloud line, their last sentinels twisted into shapes like old men leaning into wind that never stopped. Above them the escarpment showed its true face: gray rock streaked with white, knife-edged against a sky the color of unpolished pewter.

She had been awake since before dawn, watching Coral sleep in the lee of a boulder, his breath frosting in slow rhythm, one hand curled near the axe he'd taken from the fishing village. Even asleep he looked like a man listening for something. She wondered if he ever truly slept at all, or only waited out the dark the way sailors waited out weather—patiently, warily, ready to run.

"Your father climbed this?" he asked when she woke him. He didn't look at her; he looked at the ridge, reading it the way she'd seen him read water.

"He climbed it twice," she said. "Once to survey it, once to come home." She unfolded Marchand's map on her knee, weighting the corners with stones. The route her father had drawn ran along the spine of the ridge—a line so fine and confident it looked almost casual, as though knife-edges were things a man strolled across on Sunday afternoons. "He said the trick is not to look down. Not because of fear. Because the eye lies up here. It makes the far side look closer than it is."

"And the second trick?"

"There isn't one. After that you just walk."

Coral studied the line a moment longer, then began repacking the rope without another word. That was the thing about him she was learning to trust: he did not waste breath on what could not be changed. He simply moved, economical as tide, and let the world argue with itself behind him.

They started up through the dwarf pine while the light was still the color of weak tea. The ground was soft with needles, then soft with moss, then no longer soft at all—rock rising through the skin of the earth like bone through flesh. Zephyrine counted her steps without meaning to, an old habit from surveying with her father: two hundred paces, check the bearing, two hundred more. He had taught her that navigation was mostly a conversation between stubbornness and arithmetic, and that the person who kept talking longest usually won.

The wind found them at the treeline.

It came off the escarpment in long, deliberate gusts, carrying the smell of stone and distance and something colder underneath—the smell of snow that had not yet fallen but was thinking about it. Zephyrine pulled her collar up and glanced west, where the clouds had begun to organize themselves into something with intent. They were not storm clouds yet. They were the preliminary sketch of storm clouds, drawn by a hand that had all winter to fill in the details.

"How long do we have?" Coral asked. He had noticed too. Of course he had. A man who read water for a living would read sky the same way.

"A day," she said. "Maybe less. My father's notes say the passes close early here. The navy bought the valley below us—" she tapped the map where the old friend's warning still lived in her memory, "—but nobody can buy the weather."

"No," Coral said quietly. "They only rent it."

* * *

The ridge began in earnest an hour later, and Zephyrine understood why her father had drawn the route so carefully and said so little about it.

It was not climbing, exactly. It was walking a spine. The rock rose on either side of them in shelves and fins, and between the shelves the ground fell away on both flanks at once—down into pine-dark valleys on the south, down into bare scree and shadow on the north. The path itself was rarely wider than a table, sometimes narrower than a plank, and the wind came across it sideways with the flat insistence of a hand pushing at your shoulder, testing, always testing, whether you meant to stay.

Zephyrine went first. She had argued for it at the base of the ridge—Coral had offered to lead, and she had refused, and for once he hadn't pressed. *You don't know my father's marks,* she'd said, which was true, and also not the whole truth. The other part was simpler: this was her mountain. Whatever else happened, whatever the navy had done, whatever Coral Voss was running from or toward, this ridge belonged to the man who had taught her to read it, and she would cross it the way he had taught her—with her own eyes open and her own feet deciding.

The marks were there. Small, patient, easy to miss unless you knew to look. A cairn no bigger than a fist at a bend where the path dipped. A blaze cut into a leaning slab of quartz, three fingers wide, already healing over with lichen—years old. A notch chiseled into a boulder where the route turned north, shallow enough that rain had softened it but deep enough that a thumb fit. Her father had made these marks the way he'd done everything: thoroughly, modestly, leaving the land looking almost untouched, as though the route had always been there and he'd merely remembered it.

"Here," she said, crouching at the quartz blaze. "He cut these himself. He used to say a good mark should be invisible to anyone who isn't looking for it."

Coral stood behind her, one hand on the rock, his body angled into the wind. "And visible to anyone who is?"

"Then it's a bad mark." She smiled despite herself. "He'd have liked you. You're very hard to see."

Something crossed Coral's face then—something quick and unreadable, gone before she could name it. He turned and looked back the way they'd come, down the long green fall of the southern slope, and Zephyrine realized she had said the wrong thing without knowing why. There were men, presumably, who were paid to be very good at seeing Coral Voss. She filed the thought away and stood.

The morning wore on. The ridge narrowed. At its worst it was barely a boot's width, with air on both sides and the wind coming through in pulses, like the mountain breathing. Zephyrine crossed those sections on her toes, arms out, eyes fixed on the rock three feet ahead—not down, never down, because her father had been right about the eye lying—and felt the whole gray world reduce itself to the next foothold, and the next, and the next.

Behind her, Coral moved like something born to it. She had watched him dive once, back in the harbor town, before she'd known anything about him except that he carried his past the way other men carried ballast. He moved underwater the way he moved here: without hurry, without wasted motion, as though gravity were a rumor he'd heard but never confirmed. Twice the ridge pinched to nothing and he flowed across it without appearing to decide to, and Zephyrine thought: *a man like that doesn't choose to be brave. He's just forgotten how to be anything else.*

At midday they reached a shelf broad enough to rest, sheltered from the worst of the wind by a fin of rock, and ate cold bread and the last of the village cheese. Below them the southern valley lay in folds of green and shadow, threaded with the silver thread of a river they'd followed for two days. Somewhere down there was the harbor, the Kestrel, the life Coral had abandoned in a single night. Somewhere beyond it, the coast, the sea, every port he'd ever slipped away from.

"Do you regret it?" she asked. She hadn't planned to. The altitude and the wind seemed to have loosened something in her.

Coral chewed slowly. "Regret what?"

"Cutting loose. The fee. The quiet life where nobody knows your name."

He looked out at the valley for a long time. When he answered, his voice was nearly lost under the wind, and she leaned closer to catch it.

"I regretted everything I ever left," he said. "That was the problem. So I stopped leaving things worth regretting." He brushed crumbs from his hands. "This is the first time I've left something and not looked back."

Zephyrine didn't answer. She didn't trust her voice to. Instead she folded the map, checked the bearing, and pointed north along the spine, where the ridge climbed one final time toward a saddle of pale rock between two dark towers of stone.

"The pass," she said. "An hour, maybe two. If we make it before the weather, we'll be down into the high valley by tomorrow night."

"And if we don't?"

"Then we learn what my father's notes say about wintering on a ridge." She met his eyes. "We won't like it."

* * *

The weather turned an hour before the pass.

It did not arrive dramatically. That was the treachery of mountains—it never announced itself; it simply accumulated. One moment the clouds to the west were a distant argument; the next, the light had gone flat and sourceless, the kind of light that erased shadows and depth and distance all at once, and the temperature dropped as though someone had opened a door at the top of the world. The wind swung around to the northwest and began carrying grit with it, fine as flour, stinging where it struck exposed skin.

Zephyrine pulled her hood forward and kept counting steps. Two hundred. Check the bearing. Two hundred more. But the bearings were getting harder to hold—the light gave nothing back now, and the rock ahead was turning white in patches, snow gathering in the wind-shadows of every ledge, thickening as they climbed. Her father's marks grew harder to find. The fist-sized cairns wore caps of white. The quartz blazes disappeared under rime.

"We're close," she said, and heard how thin her voice sounded. "There should be a marker at the saddle. A big one. He called it the doorpost—he built it the first season, said you couldn't miss it."

"You can't miss it," Coral repeated, without inflection.

"It's four feet tall. Stacked stones. You *can't* miss it."

But the world had shrunk to thirty feet of visibility and a wind that wanted them off the ridge, and Zephyrine felt the old fear rise—not fear of falling, which was clean and simple, but fear of the other kind, the formless kind, the kind that had been following her since the day the government letter arrived saying *weather conditions* and *regrettably* and *no survivors recovered*. The fear that the mountain had taken her father the same way it was now taking the trail: gradually, then completely, until there was nothing left to find.

She was scanning the whiteness ahead, hating herself for the doubt, when Coral's hand closed on her shoulder.

"Zeph."

She followed his gaze. Twenty feet ahead, half-buried in fresh snow, stood a stack of stones.

Four feet tall. Broad-based. Deliberate.

Her heart slammed once, hard, and then she was moving—scrambling the last stretch of ridge with no care for the drop on either side, falling to her knees at the cairn, brushing snow from it with both hands as though uncovering a face. The stones were dark granite, fitted with the particular patience she knew better than her own handwriting. Her father built cairns the way other men built arguments: each stone chosen, tested, settled, so the whole structure would stand through decades of thaw and freeze without shifting a finger's width.

Except.

Her hands stopped. Snow slid from the topmost stone and tumbled down the stack.

The capstone was wrong.

Not wrong in shape—in placement. It sat slightly proud of the course beneath it, tilted a degree off true, wedged rather than settled. And beneath it, in the gap the tilt had opened, a corner of something pale showed: not granite. Not stone at all.

"Coral," she whispered. "Look at the top."

He knelt beside her. Together they lifted the capstone free, and Zephyrine drew out what lay beneath it, her fingers numb and clumsy and shaking.

A tin box, small, dented, the paint worn to rust at the edges. Her father's brand of tin box—he'd carried a dozen of them, tobacco tins repurposed, holding everything from spare compass needles to the sugar he pretended he didn't hoard. This one was heavier than empty. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with cold, she found a folded square of paper and, beneath it, a small brass button, military, with an anchor stamped in its face.

Navy.

The paper was a survey field note, written in her father's hand, dated eleven days ago. Eleven days. Three weeks after the letter that said *no survivors recovered*. The characters were cramped, written fast, probably kneeling right here in weather much like this:

*If you are reading this, you came by the hidden route, which means either you are my daughter or you are smarter than the navy, and I have learned this year that the two categories overlap.*

*The team was taken, not lost. I will explain when I see you. I am going down into the valley tonight with the last of the food. Do not follow me down. Go over the col to the east while the weather holds, and wait at the shepherd's bothy—you remember it, the one with the turf roof, where I taught you to read stars. I will come to you there if I can. If I cannot—*

The writing broke off. Then resumed, smaller, harder, pressed deep into the paper:

*—if I cannot, burn the maps and forget my name. But I think you will not, because you are mine. One more thing. The man who escaped them years ago, the diver—they call him Voss. If he is with you, tell him they never stopped looking, and that what he saw aboard the transport is worth more than he knows. Tell him it is the reason I am still alive. They need witnesses. Dead men make poor catalogues.*

Zephyrine read it twice. The wind tore at the paper and she held it against her chest, inside her coat, feeling it crinkle against her heartbeat. Eleven days ago her father had knelt on this exact stone, in this exact cold, and stacked this exact capstone with hands that must have been shaking as badly as hers were now—and he had been alive. Alive eleven days ago, alive and walking down into the valley alone, toward the facility the navy denied and the maps recorded, and the cairn—

"The cairn," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on it. "Coral. The cairn was restacked. Look at the base courses—those are weathered, twenty years of freeze-thaw, but the capstone was wedged, not settled. Someone rebuilt the top recently. Someone who wanted it to stand out. Someone who wanted it *found*."

Coral was staring at the brass button in her palm. His face had gone the color of the rock.

"They passed here," he said. "Recently. Days. Maybe hours, before the snow covered their tracks." He looked up at the flat white sky, and Zephyrine saw something in his expression she had never seen in him before—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own name spoken in a language he'd tried to forget. "My God. They took the whole team through here. Over the pass. Toward the station."

"Or my father came back this way," Zephyrine said. "Alone. Restacking his own cairn so I'd know he'd made it this far."

"Either way." Coral stood, and the wind hit him full in the chest, and he leaned into it like a man leaning into a decision. "Either way, someone alive crossed this pass within the week. And they left word for us."

Zephyrine tucked the note inside her coat, next to the maps, next to her heartbeat. Hope arrived in her like warmth returning to frozen fingers—painful, almost unbearable, real. Her father had knelt here. Her father had written *I will come to you there if I can.* Her father had named Coral Voss in the same breath as his own daughter, as though the two of them had always been part of the same story, two threads of one knot the navy had tied years ago and never bothered to cut.

She looked east, where the ridge descended toward the col, and saw the first flakes coming down out of the flattened sky—fat, unhurried, almost gentle, the way snow begins when it intends to become serious.

"We keep moving," she said. "Now. While the trail's still findable. The col by dark, the bothy by tomorrow."

Coral picked up the pack. For a moment he paused, looking back down the ridge the way they'd come—at the vanished coast, the vanished harbor, the vanished man he had been a week ago—and then he turned east into the snow, and Zephyrine fell in beside him, and together they walked into the whitening world with a dead man's note warm against her chest and a living man's hope walking upright beside her.

Behind them, the cairn stood its watch in the rising snow, its restacked stones holding fast against the wind—waiting, as its builder had waited, for whoever came next.

And far below, down in the valley the navy had bought, a light blinked once in the failing afternoon, steady and green, like an eye opening.
