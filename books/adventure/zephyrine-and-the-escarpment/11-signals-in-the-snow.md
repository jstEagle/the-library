## 11. Signals in the Snow

The snow began an hour after they left the cairn, fine as sifted flour, and by midmorning it had thickened into something that swallowed sound. Coral felt the change in his ears first—a pressure, a hush, as though the whole mountainside had been submerged. He'd known that silence before, three fathoms down, when the world above stopped mattering and only the work remained. He did not like hearing it on land.

Zephyrine moved ahead of him with the certainty of someone following a line drawn in her own blood. She paused at intervals to check Marchand's map against the terrain, gloved fingers tracing contours, then pointing with her chin: *there, along the streambed, where the alders thin.* Twice she found markers he would have walked past—blazes cut high on trunks, deliberate, old, then newer cuts beside them. Her father's hand, and then another's.

"He came through here more than once," she said, when Coral asked. Her voice was flat in the way voices go flat when grief has been compressed for weeks. "These older marks are from years ago. The fresh ones—" She touched the pale wood. "Three weeks. Maybe less."

"Fresh enough that whoever made them might still be up here."

"That's what I'm counting on."

Coral said nothing to that. He counted instead what she was not counting: the cold, the light going gray and grainy behind the clouds, the way the snow was filling their backtrail faster than any pursuit could follow but also faster than any retreat could either. They were committing. Every step uphill was a step he couldn't take back, and some part of him—the part that had kept him alive through nine ports and eleven false names—kept whispering that this was madness, that the smart move was down and out and gone.

But the smart move had never once made him feel anything but hollow. He climbed.

* * *

They smelled the camp before they saw it.

Woodsmoke—cold woodsmoke, hours dead, but distinct beneath the snow-smell, threaded with something else. Paraffin. Tinned meat. And under all of it, faintly, a chemical sharpness that Coral knew from other lives: the bite of a solvent used to erase.

He put a hand on Zephyrine's arm and she stopped without question, which told him something about how far trust had already traveled between them. Ahead, through the pines, the trees opened onto a shelf of ground above a frozen stream—a good site, sheltered from the north wind, water near, sightlines long. Someone had chosen it the way he chose anchorage: deliberately, professionally.

Three tents stood pitched in a rough triangle. Snow sat on them in unbroken sheets.

Coral had spent enough winters aboard hulled-up boats to know what snow on canvas meant. It meant no one had brushed it off. No one had gone in or out. The tents hadn't been abandoned in haste or collapsed by wind; they had simply been left, the way you leave a place you expect to return to, or the way you leave a stage set after the play is over.

"No," Zephyrine said. Just the one word. She said it the way a diver says it, before the descent, to the water itself—an argument offered to something that will not listen.

She broke for the camp at a half-run and Coral let her go three steps before following, because there was no stopping her and because every instinct he owned was screaming the opposite direction.

The nearest tent's flap hung open. Inside, sleeping bags lay rolled and strapped, untouched. A lantern sat on a crate, its glass clean, its fuel gauge full. A rifle stood in the corner rack of the tent frame—racked, not dropped—with a dusting of snow that had blown in and settled undisturbed across the stock. Nobody leaves a rifle. Not in the mountains, not in winter, not ever. Unless they were made to leave it, or unless leaving it didn't matter anymore.

The second tent held the mess gear. That was where the horror sharpened into something with edges.

Four mess tins sat on the folding table. Three held food, congealed now under a skin of frost—stew, bread gone stale and hard, tea frozen in the cup. Half-eaten. Mid-meal. The spoons were still in two of the tins. A fourth man's ration, unopened, sat beside a pack with its straps still buckled.

"They were eating," Zephyrine said. Her voice had gone very quiet, very controlled, and Coral recognized the control for what it cost. "It was a meal. You don't stop a meal unless—"

"Unless someone tells you to." He was looking at the table edge, where four tin cups stood in a row, upright, arranged. Arranged things bothered him more than scattered things. Scattered meant panic. Panic meant violence, struggle, a fight. There was no fight here. This was tidiness, and tidiness in the wrong hands was worse than any blood.

* * *

He worked the camp the way he'd once worked wrecks: systematically, without touching what didn't need touching, reading the story written in placement and absence.

Boots by the tent doors—laced, paired, waiting. A drying line strung between two pines with socks and gloves still pegged to it, stiff as boards. An axe driven into a stump, blade buried in the cut, as if the man swinging it had simply ceased to be mid-swing. The survey instruments themselves sat in an open case: theodolite, levels, notebooks in oilcloth, all of it exactly where working men would leave tools they expected to pick up again after lunch.

And no tracks.

That was the thing that finally made the cold get into him properly. Around the camp, the snow lay smooth except for weathering—three weeks of it, drifted and refrozen, softened into a texture like dirty wool. But even accounting for weathering, there should have been traces. Paths beaten between tents. A latrine trench. Woodpile tracks, sled runners, the churned crescents of a camp lived in for days. Instead the snow recorded almost nothing: a single line of prints leading in from the east, filled and blurred, and nothing leading out. Nothing at all. Twenty-odd men—or however many the survey fielded—and the mountain kept no memory of them leaving.

Zephyrine stood at the center of it with her arms wrapped around herself, turning slowly, reading the same absence he was.

"There were eight," she said. "My father wrote that there were eight of them at the last resupply. Eight men and the mule train went up, and the telegram from the pass station confirmed they'd reached this shelf on the fourteenth. Then nothing." She looked at him. "Eight men don't walk off a mountain and leave their boots."

"No," Coral said. "They don't."

"Then where—"

"Look at the fire pit."

She turned. The stone-ringed hearth held ash, cold and crusted, and around its rim the snow had melted and refrozen in a wide, shallow lens. Coral crouched and studied the ash without touching it. Under the top layer, darker. Oily. He drew a glove through it carefully and brought up a smear that gleamed faintly, iridescent, and smelled of chemistry he'd hoped to die without remembering.

 accelerant," he said quietly. "Burned hot and fast, doused after. This wasn't a cook fire that died. It was closed down."

"You know that how?"

"I know it the way I know a lot of things I wish I didn't." He stood, brushing ash from his knee, and made himself say the rest plainly, because she deserved plainness and because saying it aloud made it real in a way he could act on rather than drown under. "There's a procedure. Navy, though they'd deny the word if you printed it. When an operation goes loud somewhere it was never supposed to exist, they don't leave evidence and they don't always leave bodies. They come in quiet, usually at night or in weather, because weather explains everything afterward. They separate the personnel, take the papers first, then the men. Fast, orderly, no shots if they can help it—shots make inquiries. The men are told it's extraction, temporary, security protocol. Most of them go willingly, because they're trained to obey and because refusing seems crazier than complying." He nodded at the table, the cups, the racked rifle. "That's why everything's neat. Fear looks messy. Orders look tidy."

Zephyrine had gone white, but her eyes stayed on him, fierce and dry. "You've seen this done."

"Once. From the outside." From below, actually—from the water, looking up at the running lights of a transport that officially had never carried what he watched it carry. But that confession belonged to a different hour, maybe a different chapter of whatever this was between them, and he wasn't ready to spend it yet. "I know the shape of it."

"Then you know where they'd be taken."

"I know where they wouldn't be taken," he said. "Down the south face, toward the coast. There'd be records of vehicles on the forest roads, witnesses in the valley towns. If they wanted this quiet—and they wanted it very quiet—they took them over the top. Inland." He looked north, where the ridge line climbed into cloud and the escarpment folded inland toward the high country no chart bothered to detail. "Toward whatever your father found that made eight careful men worth erasing."

Zephyrine crossed to the third tent, the command tent, and stood in its entrance a long moment before going in. Coral gave her that. Some doors you have to open yourself.

Inside, the map table still stood with its corners weighted by stones. The master survey map lay pinned under glass—Marchand's own work, Coral realized, the same drafting hand as the charts she carried, the same precise letters. Routes marked, elevations noted, and in the northeast quadrant, beyond the last contour anyone official had surveyed, a region left blank. Not unmapped-blank. Deliberately blank. A rectangle of empty paper inside a finished map, the kind of emptiness that takes effort.

"He saw it," Zephyrine whispered. "He saw whatever's in there, and he drew everything else around it so no one could pretend it wasn't there."

Coral lifted the glass carefully. Beneath the map's lower corner, weighted by a stone, lay a slip of paper, half slid free. He eased it out. A supply requisition, standard government form, stamped with the mark he knew better than his own name these days—the same mark burned into the crates he'd pulled from the wreck, the same mark he'd spent six years pretending not to recognize. And across it, in pencil, in a hurried hand quite unlike the map's patient lettering:

*They know we found it. R. says soldiers seen at the col. If anything happens, tell my daughter the route holds. Tell her the blank space is not empty.*

Coral read it twice. Then, because some things must be witnessed by more than one person, he handed it to Zephyrine without a word and watched her read her father's handwriting and understand, in the space of one breath, that he had known. He had known what was coming, and he had hidden a message in the least likely place, under the least likely stone, trusting that if anyone came looking it would be her.

"The blank space is not empty," she repeated. Her voice cracked on the second word and healed on the third. She folded the note with hands that trembled only slightly, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, next to the maps. "Then we go there."

"It's a facility," Coral said. "Whatever's in the blank space, it has guards, communications, and a procurement budget. Two people with a rifle and a grudge don't—"

"My father is in it. Or was. Or—"

"Or they moved him inland, alive, because dead men can't confirm what they found and living men who cooperate can be managed." He hated himself for the arithmetic even as he did it, but the arithmetic was mercy compared to hope without structure. "Which means there may be something left to save. Which also means we do this smart, or we join those cups on that table."

She stared at him, and he braced for fury—for the grief looking for somewhere to land—but what came out of her instead was colder and more frightening and more valuable.

"Teach me the shape of it," she said. "Everything you know about how they operate. All of it, tonight, while we dig out that stove. Because if they took eight men through the col, they used a route, and routes leave signs, and I am going to read every one of them."

* * *

They made camp at the edge of the clearing, in the treeline, where the shadows pooled deepest—not in the abandoned tents, because Coral trusted the tents about as far as he could throw a boat anchor. Whatever had happened here could happen again, and he wanted cover and a view of the approaches, not canvas walls and a door that flapped invitingly.

While Zephyrine dug the survey camp's stove out of the command tent—he insisted it come with them; a stove was warmth, and warmth was life at this altitude—he walked the perimeter one last time as the light failed.

At the eastern edge of the clearing, where the single line of inbound prints faded into drift, he found what he'd been half expecting and wholly dreading.

Boot prints. Military pattern, deep-treaded, larger than survey issue. Dozens of them, weathered nearly to nothing, but readable to a man who had spent years reading surfaces: the stride length of men moving in formation, the paired impressions of a loaded stretcher or a litter pole dragged between two carriers, the scuffed crescent where something heavy had been pivoted and set down. Moving west. Inland. Over the col.

He stood over them until his knees ached from crouching and the cold had climbed through every layer he owned. Somewhere in the blue dark, the wind changed pitch, and snow began again, soft and indifferent, already at work erasing the last proof.

In six years of running, Coral Voss had built a life out of not looking at things. He'd gotten very good at it. But standing there in the ruins of an ordinary meal, over the ghost-tracks of men who had been carried away from their own boots, he understood with total clarity that the thing he had been fleeing all this time was not behind him. It was ahead of him, up the mountain, in the blank space of a dead cartographer's map—and it had been waiting for him to stop running.

Behind him, the stove pipe began to draw, sending up its first thin thread of smoke. He turned back toward it, toward the small orange glow in the trees where Zephyrine was waiting with her father's note and her terrible new knowledge, and he thought: *they took them over the col, and the storm is coming, and we have perhaps two days.*

Two days, and the only proof that eight men had ever existed at all was a row of tin cups, standing neatly in the snow, waiting for someone to come home and drink.

He went to tell her.
