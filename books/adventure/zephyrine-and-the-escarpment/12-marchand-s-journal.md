## 12. Marchand's Journal

The stove was the only thing in the camp that had been cleaned.

Zephyrine noticed it the way she had been trained to notice things—her father's training, patient and sideways, the habit of looking at what *wasn't* wrong before deciding what was. The tents stood pitched against the prevailing wind, guy-lines taut, snow drifted evenly against their windward flanks. The mess table still held tin plates with fork tines pressed into frozen beans. A card game lay abandoned mid-hand, someone's queen of spades weighted under a stone against the wind that had never come to scatter it. Three weeks of weather should have buried all of it deeper than this. Should have torn at least one fly loose, toppled a tent pole, strewn the plates across the scree. The camp looked less abandoned than *paused*, a held breath, a room whose occupants had stepped out expecting to return before the kettle cooled.

And the stove: its iron door latched, its flue capped, ash swept from around its base in a neat semicircle as though someone had knelt there with a brush on the last afternoon of their lives.

"Coral." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. He stood ten feet away at the edge of the camp, where the survey's boundary stakes began, reading the ground the way he read water—with his whole body, head tilted, weight shifting. "The stove."

He crossed to her without hurrying. That was something she had learned about him in the days since the harbor, in the forest, on the ridge: Coral Voss did everything without hurrying, and yet nothing about him was slow. His hands, when they took hold of the stove's handle, moved like hands that had done this before—checked the flue first, then the latch, then pressed his palm flat against the iron as if listening for heat three weeks dead.

"It's cold," he said. "Been cold a long time."

"Open it anyway."

He glanced at her. In the flat white light off the snow his eyes were the color of deep water over slate, and for a moment—the moment before he lifted the latch—she saw something cross his face that she had seen once before, in the cabin of the Kestrel when she'd shown him her father's maps: recognition. Not of a place. Of a shape. The shape of trouble he already knew.

The stove door swung open on hinges someone had oiled within recent memory.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth and wedged behind a firebrick, was a bundle.

* * *

She knew the handwriting before she knew the object. That was the cruelest part—that her hands were shaking before her mind caught up, because her body recognized those small, slanted capitals the way it recognized the smell of cedar and pipe smoke and the particular creak of the third stair in a house that no longer existed. Élie Marchand wrote the way he drew coastlines: precisely, densely, every letter placed with intention, no flourish wasted. She had spent her childhood copying that hand for practice while other children copied poems.

The journal was bound in waxed canvas, its spine cracked from use, a pencil stub still tied to the cover by a loop of twine. Her father's field journals were legion—she'd grown up among forty of them, shelved by year—and this one matched them exactly, down to the grease-pencil grid number stenciled on the spine. Survey journals were supposed to be surrendered at expedition's end. Filed. Catalogued. This one had been hidden in a stove by a man who did not expect to surrender anything again.

Coral stood back while she unwrapped it. He had not touched the bundle; she registered that distantly, some part of her cataloguing his restraint, filing it beside the way he'd hung back at the treeline, the way he always seemed to be giving her room she hadn't asked for. The wind moved through the camp, worrying the tent fabric into low, conversational mutters.

She opened to the first page and the camp fell away.

*14 September. Dropped at the escarpment road-head at 0600. Team of six, thirty days' provision, orders to triangulate the eastern approaches. The navy liaison, Lt. Ferrand, was very clear that our charts are for internal use only. I asked him twice what we were charting for. Twice he smiled.*

Her father's voice came off the page whole. She could hear the dry note under the precision, the irony he thought he hid. She read standing, then kneeling, then sitting on an overturned crate with the journal braced on her knees, forgetting the cold entirely until Coral draped something over her shoulders—his coat, or a tarp; she didn't look up—and she kept reading because stopping would mean feeling, and feeling could wait until she knew.

*19 September. Found the old survey markers from '09, re-cut. Someone has been working these approaches recently and does not want it known. The bearing lines converge inland, past the col, into the valley the shepherds call empty.*

*24 September. Climbed to the shoulder below the high col. There is a wire strung through the pass—not telegraph, too fine, buried in places. It runs down-valley toward a structure my glass resolves as concrete. Concrete does not belong here. Nothing here has needed defending in four hundred years, and yet the wire is new.*

*30 September. Ferrand visited the camp unannounced. Asked, pleasantly, whether we had surveyed the valley itself. I said our commission ended at the escarpment. He said, also pleasantly, that commissions can be extended. I did not care for the way he said it. I do not care for any of this.*

Zephyrine became aware that Coral had moved. He was standing at the mouth of the largest tent now, and he had gone very still in a way she was beginning to understand meant danger—the stillness of a man watching a line go taut. She followed his gaze. Pinned inside the tent flap, curling slightly in the damp, was a sheet of paper: a printed circular, government letterhead, a list of names and descriptions.

Not a survey roster. A *watch list*. And near the bottom, in the same bureaucratic type as the rest:

**VOSS, C. — former contractor, maritime division. Status: escaped. Report sighting immediately. Do not approach.**

"You've seen this before," she said.

"I've been this," he said.

* * *

She read the last pages by the light sinking through the tent fabric, alone, because some pages a daughter reads alone. Outside, Coral's footsteps circled the camp in slow, methodical arcs—he was checking the tracks again, or the absence of them, hunting for what she already feared the journal would explain. The cold had gotten into her fingers. She flexed them and turned past the entries about wire, about concrete, about the night the team's radio received orders in a cipher her father identified as naval intelligence rather than survey command.

*11 October. We were told to descend to the valley. All six. No reason given beyond "verification of findings." I told the team what I suspected, which is more than I suspect and less than I know. Renard wanted to refuse. I told him refusal teaches them what we are worth. Better to be dull. Better to be boring men with boring instruments.*

*I am writing this at 0400 because I cannot sleep, and because if I am right, this book must outlive my discretion. There is a station in the valley. It is not a weather station, whatever the paint says. We passed its auxiliary gate today and I counted the antennas and I understood what the wire is for. It listens. Not to the sky—to the towns. To harbors, to registries, to ledgers. It collects people the way my trade collects soundings: one depth at a time, patiently, until the whole floor of a country is mapped. They keep files here on men who have done nothing but see something they were not supposed to see. They call the files assets. They call the men security interests. There is a wing of bunks behind the concrete, and lights behind painted glass, and I watched a man walk between two buildings at dusk and I swear to God he walked like a prisoner who has been told he is free.*

*If anyone finds this: the team will be taken tonight or tomorrow. They will tell our families weather. They will tell our employers equipment failure. Do not believe the mountain. The mountain is honest. It is the only party in this valley that is.*

Then, on the final written page, dated 12 October—the day the camp went cold—the hand changed. Tighter. Faster. Written, she realized, in the dark, by feel, the letters leaning like trees in wind.

*They came at dusk. Six of them, army boots under civilian coats, and they were polite, which is how I know it is finished. They want us to walk out "for debriefing." I have put the journal where only a careful person would look, and I pray the careful person is mine.*

*One thing more, and I write it because it may save the life of whoever reads this. Years ago there was another man. A diver, contracted to recover cargo from a transport that went down under circumstances the navy calls classified and I call criminal. He saw what was in those crates—or where they were going, which amounts to the same thing—and he ran, and they have hunted him port to port ever since, and they have never found him, and I confess I have hoped for years that he stayed lost. His file is here. It is the thickest file in the cabinet I was shown, thicker even than mine will be. They call him the witness who escaped.*

*His name is Coral Voss.*

The wind took the tent in both hands and shook it. Zephyrine sat with the journal open on her knees and heard her own breathing, ragged and shallow, and heard beneath it the memory of Coral's voice in the Kestrel's cabin saying *I don't go inland* with a flatness she had taken for temperament and understood now for scar tissue.

She had hired a runaway. She had climbed half an escarpment beside the only man in the country whose name appeared in her father's captors' files. Every step of her search and every mile of his flight, it turned out, had been drawn on the same chart by the same hand—hers reaching toward the valley, his fleeing from it, two lines that were always, secretly, one line, converging here in a frozen camp where six men had eaten their last meals and walked away upright.

He had known. Or he hadn't—but either way, the man outside her tent had carried this story inside himself for years, sealed tighter than her father's stove, and had let her buy passage on his boat and walk beside him into the mountains without saying a word.

* * *

She found him at the camp's edge, at the point where the snow gave out onto bare frost-killed grass and the escarpment fell away toward the valley. He was facing the descent, arms folded, watching the far haze where concrete might be. She came up beside him and set the journal in his hands without ceremony.

"Last page," she said. "Read it."

He read. She watched him instead of the page—watched the way his jaw worked once, the way his eyes stopped moving somewhere in the middle and didn't start again for a long time. When he finished he closed the journal gently, the way you close something belonging to the dead, and held it back out to her, and she did not take it.

"How long?" she asked.

"Zeph—"

"*How long.*"

He looked at the valley instead of at her, which was answer enough, but he gave her words besides, low and even, each one sounding like it had been stored in the dark a long time. "Eleven years. A transport went down off Cape Verren with government cargo aboard. My job was salvage. What I pulled up wasn't crates of instruments. It was records—files on people, thousands of them, civilians, fishermen, harbor masters, cartographers." His voice caught almost imperceptibly on the word *cartographers*, and she felt it land in her chest like a fishhook. "I reported what I'd found. Within a week my boat burned, my papers stopped existing, and a lieutenant with a pleasant smile explained to me that I had suffered a diving accident that killed everyone aboard except me, and that the kindest thing I could do for myself was agree. So I agreed. And then I ran, and I've been running since, and I told myself it was just how I lived."

"The crates," she said. "At the wreck. The ones you burned."

"Same mark. Same program." He finally turned to face her, and his eyes were not evasive—they were exhausted, stripped, a man standing in the wreckage of the one thing he'd built out of eleven years of silence. "Your father found the place I ran from. I've spent my whole life swimming away from it, and it's got a building in it now, and your father is inside it, and I'm—" He stopped. Started again. "I should have told you at the harbor."

"Yes," she said. "You should have."

The anger arrived then, late and clean, not the hot kind but the cold kind—the kind her father had taught her to use, the kind that points like a compass needle. Eleven years. While she grew up thinking her father charted coastlines because he loved them, he had been mapping the approaches to a prison, carrying that alone the way Coral carried his own knowledge alone, two men keeping the same secret from opposite ends of the country. And the man she'd chosen to trust—the one stranger in all the ports of that coast who might have understood—had stood in her father's shadow of a map and said nothing.

"You let me search," she said. "You let me drag you up this mountain, and you knew—"

"I didn't know it was the same place. Not for certain." He said it carefully, and she gave him that much, because it was true, and because she recognized in it her own habit of holding truth at arm's length until it became undeniable. "But I suspected. From your maps. From the bearing lines. And yes—I let you lead, because I wanted to believe it was just a missing survey team. Because if it was the program, then it was mine too, and I've spent eleven years making sure it never got to be mine again."

"And now?"

The wind gusted off the col above them, carrying the smell of ice and stone. Down-valley, the haze held its shape, patient as concrete.

"Now," he said slowly, "there's nowhere left to run to. Everything behind me was always just distance. This—" he nodded at the journal, at the camp, at the valley, "—this is the middle of the story. Your father wrote my name in it. I figure that makes me in it whether I stand still or run, so." He exhaled, long and visible in the cold. "So I'm done running. If he's alive down there, we get him out. And then somebody answers for all of it."

She searched his face for the lie, for the drift, for the smooth exit she'd have sworn was woven into him—and found none. What she found instead frightened her more than the valley: a man standing still, choosing it, and meaning it.

"Then we move now," she said. "Light's going, but the route holds moonlight along the shoulder. And Coral—if you lie to me again, even kindly, even to protect me, I will leave you on this mountain."

"Fair," he said. And then, quieter, glancing once more at the journal in her hands: "Your father was careful. Careful men survive places like that. We'll find him."

Above the col, the first stars came out hard and white, and far down in the dark of the valley, faint and steady as a pulse, a single light burned behind painted glass.
