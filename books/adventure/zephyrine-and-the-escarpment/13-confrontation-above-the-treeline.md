## 13. Confrontation Above the Treeline

The wind came up the valley like something herding them, and by late afternoon they had climbed out of the pines into a world of stone and light. The treeline ended not gradually but all at once, as if drawn, and above it the escarpment opened into a bowl of granite scoured bare by ten thousand winters. Snow patches lay in the hollows like spilled milk. The sky had gone the color of old pewter, low and heavy, promising weather that would not wait for them to be ready.

They made camp in the lee of an overhang where Marchand's map showed a dotted line and a small notation—*shelter, wind NW*—and Coral built a fire of scrub pine and dried lichen because Zephyrine's hands were shaking too badly to strike the flint. He told himself it was the cold that shook her. He knew better.

She had read the journal pages twice on the climb. Once at the survey camp, kneeling over the stove with the wind moaning through the tent walls. And once more, an hour ago, huddled against a boulder while he pretended to study the ridgeline through his binoculars. He had watched her lips move over the words. *Coral Voss. The witness who escaped.* Watched her close the journal with a care that frightened him more than anger would have, the way you close a door on a room where something has died.

Now she sat across the fire with her back against the stone, the journal closed on her knees, both hands flat upon its cover as if holding something down. The firelight moved on her face. She had not spoken since the camp.

Coral fed the fire another branch and listened to the wind comb the high stones. Above the treeline there was nowhere to hide—not from the navy trackers he was certain were behind them, and not from this.

"Say it," he said at last. "Whatever you've been carrying since the stove. Say it now, while we're warm enough to survive the answer."

Her eyes came up. In the firelight they were the gray-green of deep water over rock, and for the first time since the harbor he saw nothing in them that was soft toward him. "I've been carrying a question," she said. "It's very simple." She lifted the journal. "My father wrote your name. Not the name you go by—your *real* name. Étienne Vasseur. He wrote it in the last entry he made before someone took him off this mountain. So before I take one more step toward whatever is in that valley, you are going to tell me what my father meant when he called you *the witness who escaped.*"

The name landed in him like a hook finding flesh. He hadn't heard it spoken aloud in nine years. It sounded like a stranger's name, and also like the only true thing anyone had said to him all day.

"You went diving," she pressed, when he didn't answer. "You pulled crates out of a wreck nobody will name. You burned one. I watched you burn it from the quay—you thought I was looking at the boats. And then you let me aboard your ship knowing what my father's maps meant, knowing what was written in that journal—" She stopped. Breathed. Her voice dropped lower, which was worse than shouting. "Did you know? From the beginning. Did you know who I was and why I was really asking passage?"

"No."

"Don't lie to me."

"I'm not lying." He set down the branch he was holding. His hands were steady, which surprised him; everything below the steadiness was not. "I didn't know about the journal until yesterday. I didn't know your father's name meant anything until I read it in the logbook at the wreck—the survey logbook, the one the storm turned up. But Zephyrine—" He looked at her across the flames, and found that the words came easier than he'd feared, once he stopped trying to hold them underwater. "You're right that I knew things. I'll tell you all of it. But you should eat first. What I have to say takes longer than the warmth does."

"I don't want the warmth," she said. "I want the truth."

So he gave her the truth instead.

* * *

"It started with a transport," he said. "Nine years ago. The *Ardent Meridian*—you won't find her in any registry. Officially she never sailed."

He pulled his knees up, arms wrapped around them, the posture of a man making himself small around a story. The fire cracked and spat between them. Somewhere above, loose stone clattered down a gully, and they both went still until the sound died.

"I was twenty-three. Working deck crew on coastal freighters out of Tarnholt, running supplies up the fjords. Bad pay, worse company, and I was good at it, because I was good at not asking questions even then. One morning in October the union man tells me there's a charter. Double rate, no manifest, no questions. I took it because double rate was double rate and because I was twenty-three and stupid in the specific way that poverty makes you stupid—you think danger is just money arriving slowly."

"The *Meridian* was a navy hull dressed in civilian paint. I could tell from the rivet lines, from the way the superstructure was built to be stripped. Forty of us aboard, cargo crew, and cargo that wasn't cargo. Crated equipment, sealed drums, and then—" he paused, working the memory open like a rusted hatch, "—then people. Passengers, they called them. They rode aft, behind a canvas partition, and they didn't come out for meals. Meals went in. Trays came out empty."

Zephyrine had gone very still. The wind pushed at the fire, bending the flame toward her face.

"There was a woman among them," Coral said. "I saw her once, through a gap in the partition. Younger than me. She was writing—constantly, in a notebook, like her life depended on getting the words down. Maybe it did. And there was an officer, a lieutenant with a face like a folded map, who walked the deck at night and counted us. All of us. Crew and passengers alike, the same count, the same clipboard. That should have told me everything right there—that to him we were inventory."

"We steamed north for three days. Past the last buoy, past anything on any chart I'd ever seen. Then fog, and a coastline that matched no chart at all, and a valley." He nodded eastward, toward the dark mass of the escarpment's shoulder, beyond which the land fell away into country none of them would see until it tried to kill them. "That valley. We unloaded at a pier that isn't on any map, in water so black it swallowed the searchlights whole. And I saw the station. Just the edge of it—concrete, antennas, a building cut into the cliff face like it had grown there. And I saw the passengers go inside. All of them. Nobody came back out."

His voice had gone flat, the way divers' voices go flat when they describe the bottom: depth reported without feeling, because feeling would cost air.

"I asked one question. One. To the wrong person—a bosun who'd made the run before. I asked what the facility did. By morning I'd been reassigned to the engine room and my papers had been 'misplaced.' Which meant I couldn't leave the ship at port without being arrested as a stowaway, couldn't collect wages, couldn't complain to anyone official because officially the voyage had never happened. I was a man who didn't exist, held aboard a ship that didn't exist, bound for a valley that didn't exist."

"And the second run?" Zephyrine asked quietly. She had drawn her knees up too, mirroring him across the coals. "There was going to be a second run. There's always a second run."

"There was. And I decided I'd rather drown free than disappear catalogued." A short breath that was almost a laugh, with no laughter in it. "Third night out, during the coal transfer at Tarnholt Roads, I went over the side. No splash—I'd practiced. Swam to a fishing smack riding at anchor, hid under her netting until dawn, and stepped ashore in a town where nobody knew my face. I bought new papers from a man who dealt in such things, and Étienne Vasseur went into the water after me, and stayed there. Coral Voss walked up the beach instead."

He looked at her. "I ran because I saw what happens to people who get counted. Your father understood that. Read his own words again—he calls me *the witness who escaped*, not the witness who spoke. Because I never spoke. Nine years, Zephyrine. Nine years of small harbors and false names and diving wrecks nobody names, always moving before the questions got long. I told myself it was survival. Some nights I even believed it."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Zephyrine stared into it, and when she finally spoke, her voice came out scraped raw.

"My father spent eleven years mapping those mountains. Eleven years, and the government gave him a grant and a team and a smile, and sent him up here to vanish. And you—" her hands closed on the journal's cover, knuckles whitening, "—you *saw*. You saw the station, the transport, the people going in. You were the one living soul outside their fence who could have stood on a quay and said *this is happening*, and you chose the sea instead. You chose nine years of silence. While he was up here. While they were—" Her voice broke and she mastered it with visible effort, the way she mastered everything, and hated herself for the effort. "He is alive. I know he's alive, I feel it, and every day of your silence was a day he sat in one of those cells while the man who could have saved him dove for salvage money two hundred miles south."

"That's fair," Coral said.

"It's *not* fair, it's—" She stopped, thrown by his agreement, and fury regrouped around the wound. "Don't do that. Don't stand there agreeing with me like a man taking a whipping. Fight back or mean it."

"I mean it." He rose, stiff with cold, and crossed to sit beside her against the overhang's wall, near enough that she could have moved away and far enough that moving away would have been a choice. She didn't move. "Every word of it is true and I've known it was true for nine years and I ran anyway. You want me to defend it? I can't. I can only tell you what fear does to a man—it doesn't paralyze him, that's the lie. Fear *moves* him. It moves him fast and often and always sideways, and it lets him call the movement prudence right up until he looks back and sees the shape of the hole he's made." He exhaled. "Your father's disappearance is part of that hole. I helped dig it by never filling it in."

Silence, except the wind and the fire. Above them the first flakes had begun to come down, fine and dry, hissing where they drifted through the smoke.

"Why now?" she asked at last. "Why break the pattern? You could still run. The Kestrel's gone, but there are other ports. There are always other ports—you've proven that. Why hand yourself to my question instead of swimming?"

He considered the honest answer among the dishonest ones available, and gave the honest one.

"Because of the photograph." At the wreck, in the flooded compartment, among the survey team's effects, a print had surfaced showing a younger man in deck clothes standing at a transport's rail—and the younger man had been him. "Someone kept that photo. Someone in that station has been keeping a file on me for nine years, waiting. And when I saw my own face looking up at me out of the black water, I understood that running had never actually worked. They'd let me run. I wasn't escaped, Zephyrine. I was *stored*. Like the crates. Like the passengers. And a stored man gets taken out when they need him." He turned to look at her fully. "But that's not the real reason. The real reason is that you knocked on my cabin door carrying your father's maps and you looked at me like I was a man and not a transaction, and I discovered I'd rather be worth something to one person than safe from everyone. That's the truth. It's smaller than the conspiracy. It's the only part I'm proud of."

Zephyrine was quiet for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders and hair; she didn't brush it away. When she spoke, the fury was still there—he could hear it—but something else had arrived alongside it, wary and unarmored.

"I'm still angry," she said.

"I'd worry if you weren't."

"But I need you. Not the confession—the *man* who knows how they move, how they count, what the valley looks like from the water. And you need me, because without these maps you'll die on this ridge inside two days." She looked down at the journal, then at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and clear all at once. "So here is the truce, Coral Voss. We go down into that valley together and we bring my father out. And when it's done—when he's standing on solid ground—you and I settle accounts. Whatever that costs you, you'll pay it. Agreed?"

"Agreed," he said. "Gladly."

"And Coral." She hesitated, and for a moment the cartographer's daughter, the woman of precise lines and measured distances, looked almost young. "The woman on the transport. The one writing. If she's still in there—if any of them are still in there—then my father has been imprisoned beside witnesses for eleven years, and he has been *counting* too. He'll know things. Names. Dates. Everything your silence buried, he may have kept alive in a notebook of his own." Her hand found the journal cover again, resting on it gently now, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "Which means the story isn't what either of us thought. It's bigger. And it's still being written."

Coral looked east, where the snow was thickening along the shoulder of the escarpment, erasing the ridgelines one by one, drawing the white curtain down over the valley and everything hidden in it. Behind them, far below, he thought he saw—or imagined—a pinpoint of light where no light should be. A lantern. A signal. Or the first searchers' fire, climbing.

He put his hand over hers on the journal, and neither of them pulled away.

"We should sleep in shifts," he said. "Tomorrow we cross the col. And Zephyrine—we're not alone up here anymore. Something's following our track."

She followed his gaze down the darkening slope, and her fingers tightened beneath his.

"I know," she said. "I've heard it since the camp. Footsteps that stop when ours stop."
