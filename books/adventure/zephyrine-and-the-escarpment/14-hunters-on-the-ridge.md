## 14. Hunters on the Ridge

The fire was a mistake. Coral knew it before Zephyrine struck the first match.

They had come down off the knife-edge of the first pass with their hands split and their boots full of snowmelt, and the scree field below offered nothing—no trees, no overhang, no hollow deep enough to hide two people from the wind that came off the icefields in long, keening gusts. The cold up there did not bite so much as it *drilled*, working through wool and canvas and skin until it found the bone beneath and began to negotiate with it. By dusk Zephyrine's lips had gone the color of slate, and her fingers had stopped answering her when she tried to coil the rope.

"You'll lose them," Coral said, watching her fumble a third time.

"I'll lose them either way if we stop." She said it without drama, the way she said most things, as though she were reading coordinates off her father's maps rather than announcing her own destruction. "So we don't stop."

But they had to stop. Even she admitted it eventually, in the wordless way of sitting down heavily on a flat stone and not getting up. And a fire at eight thousand feet, in a bowl of white rock under a sky like scraped tin, was a beacon no tracking party could miss. Coral built it small—a fist of flame in a windbreak he stacked from stones, burning dry pine he'd carried up from the treeline in his pack for exactly this reason—and he told himself that small would be enough. That distance and dark would swallow it.

He told himself a lot of things those days. It was how a man kept moving.

Zephyrine slept first, curled against his side with the journal wrapped in oilcloth between them like a third traveler, and Coral sat with his back to the warmth and watched the ridgeline north of them, where the last light was draining out of the world in bands of iron and ash. His hands were steady. He noticed that, and was surprised by it. There had been a time—not so long ago, though it felt like another life, because it *was* another life, the one before Marchand's journal named him—when fear made his hands useless. Now the fear just sat in him, quiet and coiled, like ballast.

He almost missed it: a spark, far up the ridge. There and gone. Then again, lower, closer, moving laterally along the contour where a man would walk if he were following a fire's glow and didn't want his own seen.

Three sparks. Maybe four.

Coral put out the fire with snow, methodically, killing it by handfuls, and then he put his hand over Zephyrine's mouth and shook her awake.

Her eyes opened all the way at once—no groggy surfacing—and she read his face the way she read her father's maps. She didn't make a sound. She was already rolling the bedroll when he pointed up the ridge, and when she saw the sparks this time—one of them flared briefly, a man lighting a pipe or checking a compass—she went very still, and then very quick.

"They followed the cairn," she whispered. The freshly restacked cairn, three days behind them now. Someone alive had passed it recently; they'd let themselves believe it meant her father. It might have meant something else entirely. "How many?"

"Three. Maybe four. They're spread out. Sweep pattern."

"You know that how?"

"I know that how," Coral said, and didn't explain. He was pulling the rope off his shoulder, measuring lengths by eye, tying knots whose names he'd never learned in any book. Below them the scree field fell away toward a black seam—the glacier's edge, or a ravine; in the dark there was no telling which. Above, the hunters were coming down. They'd reach the old fire pit within the hour and find warm ash, and after that it would be tracks and moonlight.

There was moonlight. A thin crescent, but enough.

"Can we outrun them?" Zephyrine asked, shouldering her pack.

"No." He said it plainly, because lying to her had stopped being possible somewhere back on the knife-edge, when she'd talked him across thirty feet of rotten ice by voice alone while he stared down into blue nothing. "But we don't have to outrun them. We have to make them chase the wrong man."

* * *

The plan took ninety seconds to make and would take the rest of the night to survive.

Coral would go down and east, through the scree, loud and visible, kicking stone, showing himself on the skyline once if the moon allowed. They'd take him for the whole party—or at least follow him, because a running man is a story and trackers are creatures who need endings—and Zephyrine would go west and low, along the seam, quiet as weather, and circle back to the route her father's map showed threading the moraine. They'd meet at the split boulder marked on the survey sheet, dawn or after.

"And if they catch you?" she asked.

He was cinching the last strap. He looked at her—at this woman who had walked into his harbor asking passage and ended up walking into every locked room he owned—and he found that the honest answer was not the one he wanted to give.

"Then you keep going," he said. "You cross the col. You find your father. You put my name in whatever account gets written of this, and you spell it right."

"Coral—"

"They want me more than they want you. I'm the witness who escaped. You're a complication." He almost smiled. "Let them chase the important thing."

She grabbed his collar then, both fists, and pulled him close enough that he could feel the cold coming off her breath. "You come to the boulder," she said. "You hear me? Whatever you have to do, whoever is behind you—you come to the boulder. I will be there. I don't leave people on mountains."

Nobody had ever said anything like that to him. He carried it down the scree with him like a coal held in cupped hands.

* * *

Running scree at night is a kind of controlled falling. The slope is made of broken plate-rock the size of dinner plates and coffin lids, none of it agreeing with any other piece, and each stride is a negotiation you conclude half a second after committing to it. Coral ran it the way he'd once run deck plates in heavy seas—knees soft, weight low, letting the mountain argue and outvoting it anyway. Behind him he left noise deliberately: a dislodged slab clattering down fifty feet, the ring of his boot heel on a flat stone, once—just once—the pale flash of his face turned toward the moonlit sky, there and gone like something surfaced.

They took the bait. Of course they took the bait; they were professionals. He heard the whistle-signal pass along the ridge—two notes, low—and then the scrabble of boots above and behind, spreading out, converging on his noise the way water finds a drain. Good. Every man on that slope was looking at him, which meant none of them were looking west, where a cartographer's daughter moved through the dark without leaving so much as a breath behind her.

He gave them a show. He cut downslope hard, crossed a rib of solid rock where his tracks would vanish, picked them up again twenty yards on, doubled once through a field of boulders big as boats. He was buying miles and minutes and spending himself to do it. The cold air knifed his lungs. Somewhere below, the ground changed its mind about being ground—he heard it before he understood it, a hollow echo under the scree, drumming faintly with each footfall like a ship's hull sounding shallow water.

He should have slowed. He knew that even as his body refused, because the boots behind were close now—two men, maybe three, young and fast and angry at the altitude—and slowing meant stopping and stopping meant the story ended here.

The scree rang once more, loudly, directly beneath him.

Then it wasn't there.

* * *

Later—much later, in a bothy that smelled of old smoke and older grief, with his hands still shaking around a tin cup—he would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. What remained was fragments, filed without order: the lurch of the slope dropping away like a tide going out; the shriek of sliding stone all around him, riding down with him, a whole hillside deciding to move; the momentary, absurd clarity of stars overhead, wheeling, indifferent; and then the stop—not a landing, a *stop*, arrested mid-fall with a crack he felt in his teeth, his right leg plunged to the thigh into darkness, his chest pinned against a lip of ice.

A crevasse. The glacier's hidden mouth, masked by a bridge of scree that his weight had finally bankrupted. Most of the slope had poured past him and down into it; he could hear it still settling somewhere far below, a sound like the sea in a cave, going on and on. He was straddled across the gap on a shelf of surviving crust, one leg in the blue dark, and every time he breathed the shelf spoke—a dry, splintering whisper, a promise being drafted.

He didn't scream. Some old diver's discipline kept his mouth shut, some instinct from years of knowing that panic spends oxygen faster than effort ever could. Instead he did the arithmetic. His arms: free, hooked over the lip. Left leg: braced on the shelf. Right leg: buried, numb past the knee, possibly broken, definitely stuck. The shelf: dying. The crevasse below: bottomless as far as his ears could tell.

And the boots above: silent now. Listening. They'd heard the slide. Any second they'd come to the edge and look down, and whatever they saw at the bottom of that blue throat would end the chapter of Coral Voss forever, neatly, deniably, one more entry in a ledger of vanished men.

He tried to pull the leg. The mountain answered with a long tearing note, and the shelf dropped an inch, and the cold coming up out of the crevasse wrapped his buried leg like a hand.

"Stop."

The voice came from above and to the left—not from the hunters, from *beside* them, out of the dark west—and it was Zephyrine, and it was the calmest sound in the world.

"Don't move. Don't pull. Listen to my voice only."

He froze. The boots above shifted; a flashlight clicked on, its beam swinging, and her voice rose again, pitched now for different ears, hard as a thrown rock: "Over here! He went east—I've got his tracks!" And the light swung away, and boots went crashing off along the contour, chasing the fiction of him, and her voice dropped back to him alone, low and level, threading down through the dark like a line paid out to a diver.

"That's it. They're gone. Now: your right leg. Wiggle the toes."

He tried. Something answered—distant, bruised, but present. "Toes work," he breathed.

"Good. Ankle?"

"Yes. It's wedged, not broke. I think." The shelf whispered again beneath him.

"It's not going to hold while you fight it, so don't fight it. Take your weight on your arms. All of it. Let the leg hang slack—dead weight, Coral, like you're asleep." Her voice never wavered, never hurried, and he realized she was doing for him exactly what she'd done on the knife-edge: lending him her calm across a gap, rope made of nothing but sound. "Now push down with your left foot. Slow. One inch. Tell me when it holds."

It held. Then another inch. The shelf complained and held again. Sweat froze at his hairline. Below him the crevasse exhaled its cold, patient breath, and he understood with perfect clarity that the mountain did not care whether he lived, and that this woman ten feet above him cared so much it had weight, mass, grip—that her voice was the only thing keeping his death theoretical.

"Now ease the right knee forward. Toward me. Small. Smaller than that."

The leg came free all at once, the way a keel comes off sand, and for one sick heartbeat he swung over the void held by nothing but his own arms—and then his left boot found the ledge, and he hauled, and he rolled away from the lip onto solid rock and lay there gasping at the stars while the shelf behind him sighed, cracked, and let itself down into the dark in a long rumbling curtain.

Silence after. Just wind, and his own heart slamming, and then boots scraping carefully down, and Zephyrine kneeling beside him, her face a pale smudge, her hands finding his shoulders and gripping hard enough to hurt.

"You came back," he said stupidly.

"You were loud," she said. "You were very easy to track." Her voice cracked on the last word, just slightly, the calm finally costing her what it had cost, and she pressed her forehead against his, both of them breathing the same freezing air. "I told you. I don't leave people on mountains."

* * *

They reached the split boulder an hour before dawn, limping together, his right leg refusing stairs and her left arm holding him up without comment. The sky in the east had begun to consider the color of iron. Somewhere behind and above them, three navy trackers were still hunting a man made of noise and moonlight, following a trail that led nowhere at all.

Coral sat with his back against the boulder and watched Zephyrine check the map by touch, memorizing the next leg, and felt something rearrange itself in his chest—some load-bearing wall of the old life coming down. He had spent nine years making sure no one would come looking for him. She had spent one night proving she always would.

"We can't use fires anymore," she said quietly. "Not even small ones. They know our pattern now."

"No," he agreed. "From here we travel like the thing we're carrying." Like the journal, he meant—hidden, essential, worth everything. But as he said it, his eye caught the eastern horizon, where a light had appeared that was not the sun: a single white flare, far down-valley, rising from beyond the moraine and hanging in the frozen air before it fell.

Not a signal flare. A survey flare—Marchand's own design, Zephyrine's breath said it before her mouth did—"That's his. That's *his*, Coral, that's the pattern he taught me—"

Someone down in the valley was calling for help in a language only two people alive would recognize.

And someone else, Coral thought, standing painfully, testing his ruined leg, might be listening too.
