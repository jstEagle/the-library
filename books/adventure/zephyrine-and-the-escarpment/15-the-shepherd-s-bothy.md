## 15. The Shepherd's Bothy

The storm found them an hour after dark, and the bothy found them twenty minutes after that.

Zephyrine felt it before she saw it—a wrongness in the wind, a hollow note where there should have been the long whistle of open scree. She pulled Coral's sleeve and pointed into the white chaos. He squinted, shook his head. She pointed again, harder, and this time he saw it too: a break in the slope, a shadow with straight edges, which meant walls, which meant hands.

They came down to it half-crawling, the wind shoving them like a drunk uncle who wanted them gone. The door was a slab of driftwood on leather hinges, and it opened onto darkness that smelled of peat smoke gone cold years ago, of stone and old wool and something faintly sweet—dried heather stuffed into the cracks against the draft.

Coral struck a match twice before it caught. The light showed a single room, low-ceilinged, maybe four paces by five. A hearth of stacked slate. A table made from a cable spool. Two bunks built into the wall like ship's berths, and above them, pegs hung with nothing but cobwebs.

"Someone kept sheep up here once," Zephyrine said, her voice strange in the smallness of the room. "Before the valley closed."

"Someone kept more than sheep." Coral was crouched at the hearth, turning over a blackened kettle with two fingers. "This isn't rust. It's soot. Someone burned fires here recently enough that the rain hasn't washed it out of the stone."

She looked at him then—at the gash along his forearm where the scree had opened him during the chase, at the way he held his left arm close to his ribs, at the bruise blooming purple across his cheekbone where a rock had caught him in the dark. He had doubled back alone across the ridge for her, for Marchand, for people he'd known for less than a week, and he had nearly gone into a crevasse doing it, and when she'd shouted his name into the wind and heard nothing back she had understood, with a clarity that frightened her, exactly what her life would be if the mountain kept him.

"You're bleeding through," she said.

"It's fine."

"Coral."

He sighed—the sigh of a man surrendering a position—and sat on the edge of the lower bunk while she dug through their packs. Her father's medical kit had been built for survey work: gauze, a needle and gut thread, a brown glass bottle of iodine that smelled like a burning harbor. She heated water in the kettle over a fire Coral coaxed from splinters of the cable spool, and when the steam rose she cleaned the wound on his arm as gently as she could, which was not very, because the cut was deep and dirty and he flinched despite himself.

"Hold still."

"I'm holding still."

"You're vibrating."

"That's the cold."

"That's you being a bad patient." She threaded the needle. "My father used to say divers were the worst patients in the world. That you'd all rather bleed out quietly than admit a needle could hurt you."

"He wasn't wrong." Coral watched her hands work. Outside, the storm hammered the driftwood door like something demanding entry. "Your father stitched you up? When you were young?"

"Once. I fell off a sea wall chasing a gull. Six stitches, and he talked the whole time so I'd listen to him instead of my own arm. Told me about how coastlines are drawn—that they're arguments, not facts. That every map is a fight between what the land is and what someone wants it to be." She tied off the last stitch and bit the thread. "I thought it was just something fathers said. Now I think he was telling me the truth about everything."

Coral was quiet a moment. Then: "It doesn't hurt much. If you were wondering."

"Liar."

"A little."

She bandaged the arm, then his ribs, then pressed a cloth of warm water to the bruise on his face until he stopped wincing. And somewhere in the middle of it—in the lamplight and the storm-noise and the closeness of the little room—she became aware that she was touching him constantly, that her hands had run out of wounds and were finding excuses anyway, smoothing his collar, brushing ash from his shoulder, and that he was letting her, watching her with an expression she had no map for.

"There," she said, and didn't move away.

"Zephyrine."

"Yes."

"Thank you. For shouting my name. On the ridge." His voice was rough. "I heard it. Under everything. It's the reason I turned around instead of—" He stopped. "It's the reason."

She put her hand against his unbruised cheek. His skin was cold and stubbled and alive. "You're not allowed to die on this mountain," she said. "I've lost one man to this valley already. The arithmetic won't survive another."

* * *

Later, with the fire built up and their wet clothes steaming on lines strung across the room, Zephyrine went looking for firewood and found the history instead.

The bothy's back wall, behind the second bunk, was lined with old crates—sheep feed, probably, once. But wedged behind them, wrapped in oilcloth, was a tin box, and inside the tin box were papers.

Not official papers. Not maps. Letters, mostly—or things shaped like letters but never sent, written on scraps, on the backs of receipts, on pages torn from hymnals. Dozens of them. Some yellowed to the color of weak tea; others startlingly fresh.

She carried the box to the fire and knelt beside it, and Coral came to sit cross-legged opposite her, and together, without quite deciding to, they began to read.

The oldest was dated eleven years back. A woman's hand, careful and school-taught:

*I am writing this in case anyone ever comes. My brother Tomas worked at the station below—they called it a weather post, we all called it that, even those of us who knew better. In the spring they told us he transferred inland. But his boots were still under his bed. You do not transfer inland without your boots.*

*I have come up here three times now. I leave notes. Once I found one answered.*

The answers were there, folded among the questions, in different hands across different years. A whole correspondence conducted through a tin box in an abandoned bothy, between the families of the valley and—she turned a page and her breath went shallow—the disappeared themselves.

*Day 40. They moved us to the east block. There is a window. I can see the river bend. Tell Mama the river bend is how I know the season is turning.* — signed with a number, then, scratched out, a name: *Anselm R.*

*If you get this: the new ones don't cry anymore. That's the worst thing. Remember how to cry. It means you remember why.* — unsigned.

*There is a shepherd, Daan, who brings his flock too near the wire on purpose. He counts them loudly. He is counting messages. The guards think him simple. Tell him the third flock was perfect.* 

Zephyrine read until her eyes burned. Eleven years of it. Aunts and mothers and brothers-in-law climbing the escarpment in secret, leaving words in a tin box like offerings at a shrine, and sometimes—miraculously, terribly—receiving answers back. Proof of life passed hand to hand like contraband. Grief organized itself into a postal service.

"They never stopped," she said finally. Her voice cracked on the second word. "All these years. Everyone said the valley swallowed people and the valley kept its secrets, and the families just—accepted it. Mourned and moved on. But they didn't. They climbed up here. In winter. At night. For eleven years."

Coral had gone very still. He was holding a page close to the firelight, and she saw his jaw tighten as he read it.

"What is it?"

He handed it to her without a word.

The handwriting was hurried, the paper cheap, the ink blotted:

*To whoever finds this—I was crew on a navy transport, coastal run, autumn six years ago. What we carried was not cargo. I saw men loaded at the valley landing in the dark, sedated, tagged like freight. I reported it. Within a week my name was on a list and my boat was burned and I ran. If you are reading this, they have not stopped running their lists. Check the manifests. Check the manifests. Check the*

The sentence simply ended. Below it, in fresher ink, someone else had written: *We checked. We believe you. — the Thursday walkers.*

And beneath that, in a third hand, older and steadier: *The witness escaped. God keep him wherever he is.*

Zephyrine looked up. Coral was staring into the fire, and his face in the flame-light was the face of a man reading his own obituary written by strangers, and finding it kind.

"Six years ago," she said softly. "The transport. That was you."

"I wrote that page in a harbor tavern two towns south of here, the night before I burned my own boat and took the Kestrel's name off every register I could reach." He laughed, but there was nothing in it. "I thought I was shouting into the ocean. I thought nobody heard. And then I ran for six years believing I'd abandoned everyone in that valley, and the whole time—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "The whole time they were leaving me notes. Praying for me. *God keep him.*"

She moved around the fire and sat beside him, and he leaned into her, and she held him the way you hold something that has been carrying too much for too long and has finally set it down.

"You weren't a coward," she said into his hair. "You were a message that got out. And messages are only worth anything when they're delivered. So we deliver it."

* * *

Near midnight the storm eased to a mutter, and Coral slept—truly slept, for the first time since she'd known him, his breathing deep and even, his stitched arm cradled against his chest. Zephyrine sat by the fire with her father's journal open on her knees, though she wasn't reading it. She was thinking about the tin box.

She got up, took a blank page from her own notebook, and thought for a long time before writing.

Then she wrote:

*To the Thursday walkers, and to Anselm R., and to whoever keeps this box—we are two, coming down toward the station from the high col. We carry proof of what the facility is. One of us is the witness you prayed for; he is alive and he has come back. We will not stop. Leave word if you can. — Z.M., daughter of Édouard Marchand, who is inside and whom we mean to take home.*

She folded it into the tin, weighted the lid, and pushed the box back behind the crates where eleven years of hands had kept it hidden.

When she turned, Coral was awake, watching her from the bunk with firelight in his eyes.

"I felt you writing," he said. "Even asleep, I think I felt it."

"Go back to sleep. We climb at first light."

"Zephyrine." He sat up slowly, wincing. "Look at the far corner. Above the pegs."

She followed his gaze. She had noticed the marks earlier and taken them for tally scratches, shepherd's counting. But seen properly, in the full light of the rebuilt fire, they resolved into words, carved deep and re-carved over years, names layered over names like tide-lines on a harbor piling. She crossed the room and traced them with her fingers, reading aloud in a whisper:

*Tomas. Anselm. Brigid. Daan. Olev. Marta. Sef. Tomas again, home at last. Brigid again, still waiting.*

Dozens of names. A ledger carved in stone by people who refused to let the valley keep its dead-and-living in silence.

And at the bottom, in a space left deliberately blank, freshly cut within the last few weeks, in a hand she knew better than her own—

Her knees nearly gave.

*Coral Voss,* her father had carved. *The witness returns. Watch the east wire at moonrise.*

Below it, smaller, almost playful, in the manner of a man leaving a joke for his daughter to find:

*Zephyrine—if you are reading this, you took the eastern route. Your mother's stubbornness, thank God. We are fewer than they say and more afraid than we show. Come before the snows. Bring the witness. — Papa*

She stood there a long time, her fingers resting in the grooves of his letters, feeling the shape of his living hand in them. Behind her, Coral rose and came to stand at her shoulder, and read, and she heard his breath change.

"He knows you're coming," Coral said quietly. "He's been leaving messages for us. Here. In a bothy neither of us knew existed until tonight."

"The Thursday walkers," Zephyrine said. "He's one of them. Or they're his. Either way—" She turned, and the grief that had driven her up the mountain had transmuted into something harder and brighter, something that would hold an edge. "Either way, we're expected. And we're not alone. We were never alone."

Outside, the clouds were tearing apart, and through the rent she could see the moon climbing, silvering the eastern slopes—and there, faint but unmistakable, a light blinked twice in the black valley below. Paused. Blinked twice again.

"East wire," Coral said. "Moonrise." He was already reaching for his boots.
