## 16. The High Col

The storm came down the valley like a thing with intent.

Coral felt it before he saw it—a pressure behind his ears, the same deep-water hush he'd known a hundred fathoms down when the swell turned and every fish went silent at once. The pines below the treeline had stopped their whispering. Even the wind seemed to be holding its breath, gathering itself somewhere beyond the western peaks.

He stood at the mouth of the bothy with Zephyrine's binoculars raised to the col, that notch of gray sky between the black shoulders of the mountain where Marchand's map said they had to cross. Yesterday it had been a clean line of blue. Today it was filling with cloud, low and bruised and moving fast, the way weather moved in this country—without negotiation, without warning worth the name.

"Storm's early," Zephyrine said behind him. She'd been reading her father's journal again by lamplight, though Coral knew by now she could recite most of it with her eyes closed. "Papa wrote that it came three weeks ahead of schedule the year he surveyed the eastern face. He called it *the mountain's temper*. He thought it was funny." Her voice did something complicated on the word *funny*.

Coral lowered the binoculars. Snow was already dusting the high ridges, thin as flour sifted over slate. By morning it would be a foot deep on the col. By tomorrow night, two. And after that—the passes sealed until spring. He'd seen what a sealed range meant from the sea side: whole valleys gone dark for six months, no supply, no rescue, no way out except the long way east through country none of them had mapped.

"We go tonight," he said. "Now, if we can."

Zephyrine looked up at him. In the lamplight her face was sharp with exhaustion—they both were, four days out from the bothy's shelter, two days of hard climbing since the crevasse, since she'd talked him back from the edge of it with nothing but her voice—but under the exhaustion was that steadiness he'd come to rely on more than rope. She didn't ask whether he was sure. She never asked him that. She asked instead the question that mattered.

"Can you climb all night?"

He considered it honestly, because lying to her had stopped being possible somewhere around the first pass. His legs ached from hip to heel. His hands were still raw where the scree had flayed them during the chase. He was forty-one years old and had spent half his life underwater, where endurance meant something different than it did on rock—patience rather than power, the ability to stay calm while the body spent itself down to its last reserves. But he had those reserves. Divers always did. You learned to keep a sealed compartment at the bottom of yourself, an emergency ballast you never touched unless the sea demanded it.

"I can climb all night," he said. "Can you?"

"Yes." Then, quieter, with the honesty she gave him in return: "I don't know how much past dawn."

"That'll do." He began packing the lamp away. "Dawn is enough."

---

They left the bothy at dusk, climbing into the light that lingered longest on the heights—that strange copper hour when the snowfields burned orange and the world below had already gone to shadow. Zephyrine led. That was the order of things now, settled without discussion somewhere on the ridge above the survey camp: she read the mountain, he carried the weight. Her father's route threaded up through a couloir of broken stone, then onto a shoulder of snowpack that would take them to the base of the final wall. After that, the col itself—four hundred meters of steep ground, then the knife-edge traverse, then descent into whatever waited on the far side.

Coral carried everything: both packs lashed together across his shoulders, the rope coiled over one arm, the ice axe borrowed from the survey camp swinging at his hip. Zephyrine had protested the first day, and he'd told her the truth—"I've spent thirty years hauling salvage off the seabed; this is lighter"—and after that she'd stopped arguing and started navigating, which was a fair trade. She moved like her maps: precise, economical, never wasting a step. He watched her now, twenty feet above him on the snow slope, placing each boot with a care that wasn't timidity but arithmetic. Every step calculated. Every calculation correct.

The storm found them at full dark.

It didn't arrive so much as descend—one moment the sky held stars, the next the wind hit them broadside like a wave breaking over a deck, carrying snow that wasn't falling so much as flying, horizontal, stinging, erasing the world ten feet in every direction. Coral heard Zephyrine shout something and lost half of it to the wind. He clawed his way up to her and put his mouth against her hood.

"Say it again!"

She pulled her goggles up. Her face beneath was bloodless, lips cracked from the cold, eyes fierce. "The couloir ends! Papa marked a ledge—we have to cut left or we walk off the shoulder into the valley!" She pointed with her axe, and for a moment the snow thinned and he saw it: the pale seam of the ledge, no wider than a garden path, curving along the cliff face into the white roar.

"Then we cut left," Coral said.

"It's corniced. The snow hangs over the edge. If we're too far out—"

"Then you tell me exactly where too far is, and I'll walk the line." He took the rope off his shoulder and began tying in, fingers stupid with cold, working by feel the way he worked knots at depth. "You navigate. I'll carry us both. That's still the trade, isn't it?"

Something moved through her expression that the wind couldn't account for. She tied in opposite him, checked his knot twice—she always checked twice—and stepped out onto the ledge first, axe forward, probing the cornice with short, deliberate strokes.

The ledge took forty minutes that felt like forty years. The wind gusted in pulses, and between pulses the silence was worse, vast and ringing, the kind of silence that made Coral think of the deep holes in the seafloor where nothing lived and nothing echoed. Twice the cornice groaned under them, a sound like a whale dying, and twice Zephyrine stopped dead and waited it out with her hand flat against the rock, listening through her palm the way her father must have taught her, reading the mountain's bones. Coral followed in her exact prints. It occurred to him, absurdly, in the middle of that white darkness, that this was the closest he had ever come in his life to trusting another human being completely—not with money, not with secrets, but with gravity itself. With the simple fact of staying alive.

He had run from people for eleven years because staying near them meant they could be taken from him, or used against him, or made into witnesses of what he was. And here he was roped to a woman on a cornice above a mile of air in a night storm, and the strange thing, the thing he knew he would remember for the rest of his life however long it lasted, was that he wasn't afraid of losing her. He was afraid only of failing her. Those were not the same fear. He understood, finally, the difference between them.

---

Above the ledge the angle eased, and then, cruelly, steepened again—the final wall below the col, a ramp of ice-glazed rock that the map called *easy in summer*. Nothing about it was easy now. The wind drove snow into every crack and froze it there, so the holds were either iced over or buried, and they climbed by feel, by memory, by Zephyrine calling out her father's notes from a journal she'd memorized: *three meters right of the lone pine stump, a crack takes protection, then a slab, then easy ground.* There was no pine stump anymore—dead thirty years, probably—but she found the crack anyway, some inherited instinct guiding her hands, and Coral wedged the last of their pitons into it and climbed toward her voice.

That was the night's rhythm, he realized somewhere in the small hours: her voice, his arms. She called and he rose. Over and over. A tide of sound pulling him up out of the dark, and he responded the way he'd once responded to the pull of a lifting bag, trusting it absolutely, spending himself gladly.

At two in the morning, by the watch he kept wound inside his glove, they reached the col.

And it was there—on that saddle of wind-scoured snow between the two summits, with the storm screaming across it like something trying to scour them off the face of the earth—that Zephyrine fell.

He heard her go down more than saw it: a soft collapse, a cry bitten in half, the axe ringing on stone. He was beside her in three steps, unclipping the rope, dragging her out of the wind's main channel into the lee of a boulder fanged with ice. Her eyes were open but unfocused. Her lips had gone the color of the snow around them. When he pulled off her goggles, frost had formed in her eyelashes.

"Zeph." He shook her shoulder gently. "Zephyrine. Talk to me."

"'M fine." The words slurred. "Just—rest. Just need—" Her head lolled. She caught herself, fought her eyes open, and he saw her try to summon the navigator, the daughter of Marchand, the woman who had talked him out of a crevasse by voice alone, and he saw the summons fail. Whatever fuel she'd been running on—grief, purpose, the promise she'd made to find her father—it had finally burned down to the wick. The cold finished the rest. Hypothermia didn't ask permission. It simply collected what was owed.

Coral knelt in the snow and made the decision the way he'd once made decisions a hundred feet down, alone, with a bad regulator and a long way to surface: quickly, calmly, with total commitment and no review.

He couldn't leave her here. He wouldn't. The col was exposed, the storm was rising, and the temperature was falling through numbers that killed sleeping people in an hour. He couldn't build shelter—nothing to build with, nothing to burn. He couldn't wait for dawn, because dawn was four hours off and she might not have four hours. Which left one option, and the option was him.

He stripped the rope, lashed the two packs into a single load, and cinched them tight across his own shoulders. Then he got Zephyrine's arms up over his chest, crossed them, and tied her wrists together with the sling from his axe—gently, apologizing under his breath the whole time, knowing she'd hate it, knowing she'd understand. He zipped her jacket to his chin, wrapped the emergency bivvy sack around them both as far as it would reach, and stood up with her weight settling against his spine like a second heartbeat.

"Papa's map says—" she murmured against his neck, barely audible.

"I know what the map says." He set his boots on the descent side of the col and squinted into the maelstrom, where the eastern slopes dropped away into darkness. "You navigated us here. That was your job. Mine's getting us down." He tightened his grip on her wrists. "Rest now. That's an order from the crew of the Kestrel, and the Kestrel doesn't take arguments aboard."

He felt, rather than heard, the ghost of her laugh against his shoulder.

---

The descent took the rest of the night, and Coral would spend the rest of his life unable to describe it fully, even to himself.

There was no trail. There was barely any ground—only snow over scree over ice, shifting, treacherous, invisible in the flying white. He descended by the oldest method there is: facing inward, boot-heels first, feeling with each step before committing his weight, lowering himself down the mountain meter by meter like a man going down a ladder into black water. Zephyrine hung against his back, unconscious or nearly so, her breath fogging weakly against his collar—the one sign he checked every sixty seconds, the way he'd once checked a gauge, because as long as that breath kept coming everything else was negotiable.

His legs failed twice. Both times he caught himself on the axe and stood there in the roaring dark and let the pain pass through him like current, and both times he thought of the same thing, which surprised him: not the wreck, not the navy men saying his old name, not the facility waiting below. He thought of the harbor at home—of the innkeeper's coffee, of the smell of pitch and salt, of Zephyrine standing on the quay with her father's maps and refusing to leave. Of every port he had ever slipped out of before sunrise, and how easy it had been, and how empty every escape had tasted.

Running was easy. That was the whole obscene truth of it. Running required nothing but fear.

Staying—staying meant carrying a woman down a mountain in a storm because she would have done it for him. Staying meant walking into a valley full of men who wanted him erased, because a cartographer he'd never met had written his name in the last pages of a journal and called him *the witness who escaped*, and someone had to make the title true. Staying meant being known, and being known meant being losable, and he had spent eleven years making himself unlosable, which was another way of saying he had spent eleven years making himself into nothing.

No more. Here, in the white dark, with her heartbeat faint against his spine, Coral Voss chose the heavier life.

He climbed down through the storm until the storm ran out of mountain, and near dawn the wind suddenly dropped—as storms do, all at once, like a door closing—and he came out below the cloud layer into a silence so profound it rang in his ears. The eastern face of the range spread out beneath him, pink with first light, untouched snow falling away in long clean sweeps toward the tree line, and beyond the trees, far below in the valley floor, a smudge of smoke rose from a cluster of gray buildings.

The facility. The listening station. The place where they were keeping Marchand and the vanished team and God knew who else, catalogued like salvage, held as *security assets*.

Coral stood on the last of the snowfield with Zephyrine's weight warm against his back and looked at it for a long time. The buildings were small at this distance, toylike, almost innocent. He counted chimneys. He noted the generator shed, the antenna farm, the single road snaking south out of the valley—all the habits of a man who had spent his life casing wrecks, reading structures, finding the way in that nobody guarded because nobody imagined anyone would come.

Nobody imagined anyone would come. Not across the sealed passes. Not in the teeth of the mountain's temper.

Behind him, faintly, Zephyrine stirred. Her breath came stronger now, deeper, the color returning to her by degrees as the bivvy sack did its work. He should wake her soon. She'd want to see it—the valley, the station, proof that her father was down there breathing somewhere behind one of those gray walls. She'd earned the sight of it with every step of the climb.

But first Coral stood alone in the dawn for one more minute, and let himself feel the whole weight of what he'd carried over the col: not the packs, not the woman, but the decision. The one he'd made at the top of the world and could never unmake now, in either direction. The drifter was dead. He'd left him up there in the storm, frozen mid-stride on the knife-edge, and good riddance.

What remained was a man with a plan forming, cold and clear as the morning itself: the generator shed first. Power lines. And somewhere in that cluster of buildings, a cell with a window, and a cartographer alive behind it.

Zephyrine's voice came small and rough against his ear. "Is that it? Is that really it?"

"That's it," Coral said. "Your father's down there. And by tonight—" he shifted her weight higher, turning his eyes from the valley only long enough to press his frost-cracked lips to her temple, "—so are we."
