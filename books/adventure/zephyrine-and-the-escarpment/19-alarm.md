## 19. Alarm

The corridor smelled of ozone and old paper.

Coral moved through it the way he'd once moved through wrecks—slow, weightless, listening with his whole body. The listening station had corridors like a ship, he'd realized within the first hour of planning: same gauge of steel plating, same hum in the bulkheads, same institutional geometry designed by men who thought of buildings as vessels and people as cargo. He had spent eleven years learning to read ships by feel. This place gave itself away the same way. A seam in the floor where the concrete poured badly around 1949. A ventilation shaft breathing warm air from the generator level below. A lock, third door on the left, whose tumbler gave under the same tension-and-release his fingers knew from navy padlocks—the ones they issued at the depot in Tarnholt, the ones he'd picked open a hundred times on nights when the boredom got worse than the discipline.

Everything he had run from was here, laid out like tools on a bench.

He carried that thought down the corridor like a lamp.

Zephyrine was somewhere above him now, out on the scarp in the dark, working her way along the power line with wire cutters and her father's maps folded into her coat. He had made her rehearse it twice against the wind: cut, count to sixty, cut again. Not clean—never clean, because a clean failure told them exactly where to look, and a ragged one looked like weather, like accident, like the mountain itself objecting to their presence. Her father's lesson, passed down through paper. *Make your sabotage look like the land did it.*

Marchand was two doors ahead. Cell B-4, if Coral still trusted the schematic he'd memorized off the duty roster pinned inside the quartermaster's office—and he did trust it, because the navy never changed its habits, only its paperwork. The man who had once been Étienne Marchand, cartographer, who had walked into these mountains with five colleagues and come out of the world entirely, was behind a door with a brass number and a hasp lock older than Coral's career.

Coral put his ear to the steel.

No footsteps in the hall. Somewhere far below, the generators kept their patient diesel heartbeat, and over it, faintly, the sound he'd been hearing all night and refusing to name: wind rising off the eastern icefield, dragging the first taste of the storm across the col behind them. They had beaten the snow by half a day. Maybe less.

He worked the hasp. It surrendered with a click so small it vanished into the diesel hum, and the door swung inward on a room the size of a lifeboat cabin: bunk, desk, stool, one barred window looking out onto blackness and, far below, the scattered lights of the valley road. A man sat on the edge of the bunk with his hands folded, as though he had been expecting someone for three weeks and had used the time to decide exactly what he would say.

He was thinner than the photograph in the logbook. His beard had gone gray at the chin. But his eyes—Zephyrine had her mother's coloring and her father's eyes, and Coral knew them before the man even stood up.

"You're not the night orderly," Marchand said. His voice was roughened, like a rope dragged over stone, but steady.

"No."

"They usually come at two." Marchand glanced at the small clock bolted above the desk. "You're early. Or late. I've stopped being able to tell which."

"I'm here for Zephyrine," Coral said.

Something moved through the man's face then—not the collapse Coral might have expected from a man three weeks in a cell, but a kind of fierce recalibration, a cartographer replotting his position from a single bearing. "She came up the mountain," he said. "In October. She came up after me."

"She led me up. Every pass. Every route your charts didn't show anyone." Coral stepped back, held the door. "We don't have long. There are things you need to hear and we need to be moving before you hear them. Can you walk?"

"I can run," Marchand said, standing, "if you tell me which direction is away."

"Downhill and east. Toward the treeline. There's a bothy—" He caught himself. Explaining routes took words, and words took time, and every second the diesel hum went on uninterrupted was a second borrowed against a debt that always came due. "My partner cut the main line sixty seconds ago. When the lights go, we go. Stairwell at the end of this corridor, down two levels, out through the fuel bay. I know the layout because I helped build layouts like it."

Marchand was already pulling on the boots the station had issued him, lacing them with hands that shook only slightly. "You were Navy," he said. It wasn't a question. "The journal entry. I wrote it because I saw your file when they brought me here. They keep files on everyone, even their ghosts. Especially their ghosts."

"I know what I wrote—I know what you wrote."

"Do you?" For the first time something almost gentle entered the man's voice. "Then you know why my daughter climbed this mountain."

The lights died.

Not all at once—a stutter first, the corridor lamps browning down to embers, the diesel heartbeat faltering into a cough, and then darkness total and absolute, pressing in like deep water. Coral's night vision bloomed slowly, the way it did at forty fathoms, shapes resolving out of nothing: Marchand's pale face, the gray rectangle of the window, the corridor beyond.

"Now," Coral breathed. "Stay behind my left shoulder. Touch the wall. Count doors."

They moved. Marchand's hand on Coral's pack strap, light as a child's; Coral counting doorways by touch, by the draft of them, each unheated gap in the wall a breath of colder air against his cheek. Two levels down meant two flights, and the stairwell was an echo chamber waiting to happen, so they went down it heel-first, silent, the way divers descend a shot line.

At the bottom, the emergency circuit kicked in.

It came alive along the ceiling in strips of red, drowning the corridor in blood-colored light, and with it came the sound Coral had been dreading since the moment he'd planned this and known, somewhere honest and unsentimental beneath the plan, that it would end here: the alarm.

Not a siren. Something worse—a low, pulsing tone, felt in the teeth more than heard, rolling through the building in slow waves. And beneath it, muffled but unmistakable, boots. Many boots. The particular rhythm of men who have done drills until drill became reflex.

"They'll sweep toward the generator yard," Coral said. "That's protocol. Loss of primary power means sabotage, and sabotage means they secure the fuel and the vehicles first." He could hear his own voice going flat and procedural and was grateful for it. Procedure was a harbor. Procedure kept a man from thinking about what procedure was about to cost him. "Which means the yard is the worst place to be in ninety seconds and the best place to be right now."

"The yard," Marchand repeated.

"There's a gate on the east side. Chain-link, topped with wire, but the hinge pins are outside—it was built by contractors who assumed no one would ever be escaping *into* the valley." He almost smiled. Almost. "Come on."

* * *

The generator yard was a bowl of gravel and shadow between the station's rear wing and the cliff wall, lit now only by the red emergency strips bleeding through high windows and by one floodlight on the fuel bay gantry that ran on its own battery and had not yet died. Under that hard white cone, diesel tanks squatted like sleeping animals, and beside them, beautiful and terrible, two transport trucks with their canvas backs beaded with frost.

Coral had the hinge pin halfway out of the gate when the floodlight found them.

It snapped on without warning—one instant the gantry light alone, the next the whole yard washed in white, harsh as a flashbulb, erasing every shadow they'd counted on. Coral threw himself sideways behind the nearest tank, dragging Marchand down with him, and in the white blast he saw the yard for what it now was: a stage, and they were on it.

Figures at the corners. Four, six—he couldn't count past six with the light in his eyes. Rifles slung, not raised, which meant orders existed that hadn't yet been given to break them. And walking out of the glare, unhurried, a man in a greatcoat with silver at the temples whom Coral recognized the way you recognize a scar: Commander Aldous Vane, who had signed the transfer orders eleven years ago, who had stood on the dock at Tarnholt and watched a nineteen-year-old diver carry a secret ashore in his chest like shrapnel, who had said, *Disappearance is also a form of service, son,* and meant it as kindness and threat in the same breath.

"Voss," Vane said, into the pulsing red dark. His voice carried perfectly, the way voices do in stone bowls. "Eleven years, and you still walk like a sailor. Flat-footed on gravel. We had you flagged at every port from here to the equator, and you come home through my own front gate."

Coral pressed his back to the cold tank. Beside him, Marchand breathed fast and shallow. Above the yard, along the cliff-top path, the treeline was a black serration against the storm sky—two hundred meters of open ground, maybe less than that, maybe more, distance lying to him the way distance always lied when a man's heart was going like this.

He thought: *Zephyrine is up there.*

She should have been gone by now. That had been the design—her design, argued for at the bothy in a whisper while she stitched his split palm: *You go in, I take the line, we meet at the rocks below the switchback.* She'd have seen the floodlights. She'd have seen the yard bloom white as a diver's torch. And she would not have gone on to the rocks, because she was her father's daughter and she did not leave people in rooms she could reach.

So Coral did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the stairwell.

Two of them could not outrun rifles across open gravel. One might. An old man, thin, three weeks caged—but moving, willing, and invisible from the treeline if the light stayed on Coral. The navy wanted Coral Voss. Had spent eleven years wanting him, quietly, the way you want a loose page that proves the rest of the book is a forgery. Every rifle in that yard would track the man they'd been hunting since before some of the riflemen had enlisted.

And the gate hinge was already half free.

"Étienne." He said it low, barely a breath, lips hardly moving. "The pin. Pull it straight up, slow, on three. Then you run for the treeline, due east, don't stop, don't look back, there's a woman up there who will find you—dark coat, she looks like you. You follow the switchback down to the bothy. You remember the bothy?"

Marchand's eyes were enormous in the red light. "I'm not leaving—"

"You're leaving." Coral found the old man's wrist and gripped it, felt the bird-bone fragility of him, felt everything in himself recoil from what he was about to do and overrule it, the way a body overrules panic at depth. "Because she spent three weeks climbing a mountain for you, and I am not telling her I let them take you twice. On three."

"One," Vane's voice said pleasantly across the yard, "of us should explain to the other how you got in, Voss. My people are very curious. Curiosity makes them careless."

"Two."

"Voss—"

"*Three.*"

The pin came free with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the gate sagged, and Marchand went through the gap low and fast and shockingly quick for a man half-starved, a gray ghost skimming the gravel toward the dark serration of trees. Shouts. Boots. And Coral stepped out from behind the tank into the full white blaze of the floodlight, arms wide, hands empty, and planted his feet on the frozen ground like a man taking a sounding.

"I'm the one you want!" he called, and his voice cracked across the yard and came back to him off the cliff wall. "Coral Voss! Eleven years of paperwork, gentlemen—come collect!"

The rifles came up. Vane raised one gloved hand, unhurried, and the rifles held.

Above the yard, at the edge of vision, a shape reached the treeline and was absorbed into it. Gone. Safe. Coral let out a breath he hadn't known he'd been holding and discovered that his legs were shaking, and understood, with a strange floating clarity, that he had just done the one thing eleven years of running had trained him never to do: he had stopped moving.

"There he is," Vane said softly, walking closer, boots crunching. "There's the witness." He stopped an arm's length away, and behind him the red pulses rolled and rolled. "Do you know how much money it cost to lose you, Voss? How many men spent how many winters reading harbor ledgers in towns like this one? And you walk in through my gate." He tilted his head, studying Coral's face with something almost like affection. "Surrendering yourself. That's new. The file says you don't do that."

"The file's out of date," Coral said.

"So it seems." Vane turned, gestured. Two soldiers came forward, and Coral offered his wrists without being asked, watching over their shoulders as the east gate hung open on its freed hinge, as the storm's first flakes came down through the floodlight like static, as the treeline stayed dark and quiet and kept its secrets.

They cinched the cuffs. Vane leaned in close, and his breath smelled of coffee and cold air.

"Whatever you think you've won tonight," he murmured, "remember that the snow closes those passes by morning. Whatever's up there is mine now."

Coral smiled. It hurt his face, and he did it anyway.

"Commander," he said, "you should hope they make it down. Because the man who built this station's security taught me everything I know—and I copied every record in your vault before the lights ever went out."

Vane's expression did not change. But behind his eyes, for the first time in eleven years, Coral watched a man realize the ledger doesn't balance—and the floodlights blazed on, and the snow began to fall in earnest over the generator yard, and somewhere above them in the black pines, two figures were running east through the storm with everything that mattered.
