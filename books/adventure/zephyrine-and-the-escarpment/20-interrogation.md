## 20. Interrogation

They took his boots first.

Coral noticed that as a professional would—methodical, unhurried, the small indignities arranged in order of severity. Boots, then belt, then the knife he'd forgotten was still lashed inside his left cuff, which earned him a flat look from the young sailor who found it and a flicker of something like approval from the older one who didn't pretend to be surprised. Then the room: concrete, windowless, a steel table bolted to the floor with two chairs and a hanging lamp that buzzed at a frequency just below thought. The cold came up through the floor like water through a hull.

He sat for a long time alone. That was procedure too. He knew this building's grammar even if he'd never stood inside it—the way it spoke in silences first, then in kindnesses, then in threats, and only last in questions. He had spent eleven years learning to read rooms from the outside. Now he sat in the middle of one and found, to his own surprise, that his hands were steady.

The lamp hummed. Somewhere above him, muffled by concrete, an alarm had finally stopped ringing. He let himself think about Zephyrine—about her shape against the treeline, moving fast, low, Marchand limping between them—and made himself stop, because hope was a thing you rationed like air on a deep dive, and he wasn't out yet.

* * *

The door opened at what might have been midnight or dawn; there was no way to know, and he suspected the room had been designed around that fact.

The man who entered was not large and not small, not old and not young. He wore civilian clothes—a gray sweater over a collared shirt—which told Coral more than a uniform would have. Uniforms were for people who wanted you to know who they were. This man had spent his career being nobody, the same as Coral, except from the other side of the glass.

"You look well," the man said. "All things considered."

"I get that a lot," Coral said.

The man set a folder on the table but did not open it. He sat, folded his hands, and studied Coral the way Coral had once studied a wreck through six feet of green water—cataloguing, assessing, deciding where to cut.

"Do you know who I am?"

"No." Coral leaned back as far as the chair allowed. "But I've been running from you for eleven years, so I've built up a fairly detailed picture. You're the one who signed off on the Kestrel's false papers in Ternhaven. You're why the harbor master in Graywater paid double and asked nothing. You're why my name stopped existing in any registry after the *Meridian* incident." He paused. "You're also why my coffee always tasted like someone had gone through my galley before I got back to the boat. That one I never forgave."

Something moved at the corner of the man's mouth—not quite a smile. "Elias Wren," he said, offering nothing else.

"Captain? Commander? Just Wren?"

"Just Wren." He opened the folder at last and turned it around so Coral could see the photograph on top: the generator yard, floodlit, Coral kneeling with his hands laced behind his head, three sailors leveling rifles at him from angles that would have made the shot survivable only for the shooters. "You surrendered. That's the part I keep coming back to. Men who run for eleven years don't surrender. They die running, or they buy their way out, or they disappear so completely we spend two years chasing rumors of them in ports they've already left." He tapped the photo. "So either you're tired—and men do get tired—or you did what you did down there because you wanted exactly this. A chair. A table. Me."

Coral said nothing. Letting silence work was another thing he'd learned from the other side of it.

Wren waited him out, then turned to the second page. "Your friend cut our power line at the transformer housing on the north slope. Clean job. She knew where to find it, which means your cartographer's daughter has seen station schematics, which means—" he looked up "—her father showed her. Before we held him." A beat, deliberate. "Before we *secured* him. Forgive me. Old vocabulary."

"Does the vocabulary help?" Coral asked. "When you say it that way?"

Wren closed the folder. "No," he admitted. "It doesn't help. It helps me sleep, some nights, and poorly. But no." He rubbed his eyes, and for a moment he looked genuinely tired, a man carrying weight in places weight doesn't show. "Do you know what happens to this station now? Not the men—the *station*. If those records leave this valley, forty years of work ends. Programs end. Careers end. Some people go to prison, and some people go into the ground, and none of them will be the ones who deserved it most." He spread his hands. "I'm not asking you to pity us. I'm telling you how this actually works. Institutions don't burn cleanly. They burn sideways."

"Then here's how *this* actually works," Coral said, and heard his own voice come out level, diver-calm, the voice he used at sixty meters when panic meant death and calm was just a decision made every few seconds until it became a habit. "During the blackout—four minutes and eleven seconds, give or take, before your diesel backup spun up—I stood in your records office with a camera I took off the body of one of your own survey team members three weeks ago. Their gear was intact, Wren. Tents pitched, meals half-eaten. Your extraction crews are thorough, but they're not thorough enough to search a dead man's coat pocket twice."

Wren had gone very still.

"Every personnel file. Every asset designation. Every entry in your catalogue that turns a fisherman, a teacher, a missing survey team into 'security holdings.'" Coral kept his voice gentle, almost kind, and understood that this was the cruelest register available to him. "Four minutes is a long time when you know where the folders are. And I knew where the folders were, because eleven years ago I helped load crates stamped with that same government mark onto a navy transport, and I watched what happened to the hold when the order came down, and I have spent every night since trying to drink hard enough to forget the sounds." He leaned forward. "So yes. I wanted this chair. Because the copy exists now in a place you can't reach, and the only question left in this room isn't whether you believe me. It's what you'd rather explain to your superiors: that the witness escaped again, or that the witness escaped *and negotiated*."

* * *

For a long moment Wren simply looked at him, and Coral watched something complicated move behind the man's face—the expression of a chess player realizing the game ended several moves ago and he'd been playing out the ritual anyway.

"You understand," Wren said slowly, "that I could make you tell me where it is. This station has methods. We've used them. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise."

"You could." Coral nodded. "And I'd give you a location, because everyone does eventually—I'm not going to sit here and play the hero, Wren, I've read too many of your interrogation reports to flatter myself. But here's what you'd get: a cache that checks out just enough to buy me four days of pain, and a dead-man's arrangement that sends the full catalogue to three newspapers, two archivists, and Zephyrine Marchand the moment I miss a check-in. Which I would. You'd have broken me for a decoy." He smiled, thin and genuine. "I learned redundancy from you people. It seemed rude not to use it."

"The Marchand woman."

"Is halfway to the eastern pass with her father and five of your 'assets,' escorted by a guide who knows every route on that range better than your satellites do." He let that land. "You bought the valley last spring. You never bought the map."

Wren exhaled through his nose. When he spoke again, the performance had drained out of him, and what remained was worse and more interesting: a tired man doing arithmetic. "Say I believe you. Say the copy is real and unreachable. What is it you want? Amnesty? A new name? I can make you disappear so clean your own mother couldn't find you—that's literally my profession."

"That's the offer you gave me eleven years ago," Coral said quietly. "A new name, a new boat, a coast with no memory. And I took it. And I want you to hear this clearly, because I think it's the only honest thing anyone's said in this building in a decade: disappearing nearly killed me. Not the running. The *nothing*. Eleven years of harbors where nobody knew my face, and I told myself that was freedom, and it was just drowning in slow motion with good visibility." He straightened. "I don't want a new name. I want my old one back. All of it—the record unsealed, the *Meridian* incident declassified, my testimony on file with my actual signature under it. I want Marchand's team released and compensated. I want the catalogue published. And I want Zephyrine Marchand and her father left entirely, permanently, unforgivably alone."

"And in exchange, you hand over the original camera and the negatives."

"In exchange," Coral said, "I testify on the public record instead of the newspapers' front pages, and I give you one thing you actually need." He watched Wren's eyes narrow. "The names of the three officers above you who ordered the *Meridian* sealed. You've spent your career cleaning up after them, Wren. Don't tell me you haven't wondered what it would cost to point the fire upward for once."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere in the walls, pipes knocked and settled. Wren sat motionless for what felt like the length of a long dive, and Coral matched his breathing to the rhythm he used underwater—slow, economical, patient as pressure.

"You realize," Wren said at last, and his voice had changed—it was quieter, and younger somehow, stripped of the station's smooth institutional surface—"that if you're lying about any of this, I'll know within the hour, and everything I've offered becomes irrelevant."

"I know."

"And if you're telling the truth, I may be the last man in this valley who can protect you from what happens next. There are people above me who would rather you drowned quietly than testified loudly."

"I know that too." Coral held his gaze. "Eleven years ago, I chose drowning. Quietly, loudly—it was all the same water. I'm done choosing water, Wren. I'm choosing the shore. Whatever it costs."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Then he did something Coral hadn't expected: he reached into his jacket, produced a pen—a plain black pen, government issue, the kind that signs things away—and laid it on the table between them, next to the folder with its floodlit photograph of a kneeling man.

"There's a form," Wren said. "Witness protection intake. It's real, it's binding, and it requires a full written statement, in your own words, of everything you saw aboard the *Meridian*, dated and signed. Once it's filed, you're not an asset anymore. You're a source. Sources have rights. Thin ones—but rights, and more importantly, *records*, and records are the one thing in this world institutions fear more than newspapers." He slid a blank document from the folder and turned it to face Coral. "Fair warning. Filing this makes you a permanent enemy of the three names you mentioned. Protection ends when my authority does."

"Everything ends when authority does," Coral said. "That's rather the point."

He picked up the pen. It was lighter than he expected. Most weapons were.

He wrote his name at the top of the page—his true name, the one the registries had swallowed, the one he'd last signed in a different life on a different sea—and the letters came out steadier than his heartbeat. Above him, faint through the concrete, he heard the first sound in hours that belonged to the living world: wind rising off the eastern passes, the voice of weather moving in behind Zephyrine and her father, sealing the high country the way the plan required, closing the door on this valley and everything it had tried to bury.

Wren watched him write. When Coral finished the first line, the man spoke, softly, almost to himself.

"For what it's worth—the coffee. I apologize for the coffee."

Coral didn't look up. "You'll apologize for a great deal more," he said, and kept writing, line after line, the testimony flowing out of him like blood from a wound finally allowed to open—while outside, far above, the snow began to fall on the escarpment, thick and white and merciful, covering the passes behind the fleeing, and covering, too, the road along which the first copies of the truth were already traveling toward the light.
