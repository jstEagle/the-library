## 21. The Long Way Down

The snow began an hour after they left the col, and it did not fall so much as arrive—sideways, in long white strokes, as though the mountain itself were sweeping them clean off its face.

Zephyrine walked at the front of the line because her father's map said she should, though by then the map had become less a document than a voice in her head, a memory of his finger tracing the eastern route on the kitchen table in another life: *If you ever need to come down fast, little one, you go east where the water goes. Follow the water.* The water was somewhere beneath three feet of new snow now, but she could feel it—the grade of the slope leaning them gently downward, the way the wind came up from below instead of above, carrying a smell that was not quite pine and not quite stone but something older, thawed, patient.

Behind her came the survivors.

Seven of them, out of the eleven the station had held. Four had been moved deeper inland two weeks before the breakout—the guards had spoken of it carelessly, the way men speak of freight—and those four names were written now on a scrap of paper inside Zephyrine's coat, against her heart, where she intended to keep them until someone with the power to act read them aloud. The seven who walked behind her had been released from their cells by Coral's own hand: Marchand first, because Coral had insisted, because some debts are paid in order; then the others—Okonkwo, who had been the survey's geologist and whose hands still shook from whatever they had injected him with to make him pliable; the Vasseur twins, who had not spoken since the lights came on in the generator yard; Ilsa Brandt, who had been a radio operator once and walked like one still, chin up, listening to frequencies no one else could hear; and the two younger ones, Pell and Anselm, who had been hired only that spring to carry instruments and had carried nothing but their own terror ever since.

They did not look back at the station. That was the thing Zephyrine noticed most, in the first hour of descent—that not one of them looked back. She herself had looked back exactly once, from the shoulder of the col, and seen the facility for what it truly was in the storm light: a gray cluster of buildings crouched in the valley's throat like something swallowed, its floodlights already burning again, its antenna mast standing straight as a needle through the snowfall. Somewhere down there, Coral sat in a room under white light, trading his freedom for theirs.

She had made herself stop looking. It had felt like closing a door on her own hand.

*He knew what he was doing,* her father kept saying, or rather her memory of him kept saying it, walking beside her with his ghost's steady stride. *That man has spent ten years learning how to disappear. Let him teach them a lesson in it.*

But he hadn't disappeared. That was the whole point of what Coral had done, the thing she turned over and over as she broke trail through the deepening snow: for the first time in his life, he had stood in the open and let himself be seen. He had surrendered not because he was beaten but because surrender was the loudest thing he could say.

She walked faster, so the others wouldn't hear her breathing change.

* * *

By late afternoon the storm had buried everything—trail, tracks, the world. They roped together in pairs, Zephyrine's father insisting on being tied to her despite her protests that she needed to move freely to find the route. He had lost weight in captivity; the rope harness hung loose on his frame, and when he smiled at her argument his cheeks hollowed like a much older man's.

"Your mother used the same argument," he said. "In the Pyrenees, the winter we met." Then his face did something complicated, because her mother had been dead nine years and there was no good place for grief on a mountainside, so they both looked away politely and kept climbing down.

Ilsa Brandt came up beside Zephyrine when they stopped to rest against a granite wall. The woman's lips were blue-tinged but her eyes were clear, and she spoke quietly, in the tone of someone reporting conditions rather than asking favors.

"The girl says we're following water downhill. Is there a river at the bottom?"

"There's a river at the bottom," Zephyrine said. "And a track beside it, and a village two days past that with a telephone line that works half the year."

"Half the year," Ilsa repeated, almost smiling. "Then we'd best hope it's the right half." Her gaze went up the slope behind them, to where the col had vanished entirely into white. "Your diver. He said the records would surface within a week of his arrest. Said it like a man reading a train timetable." She paused. "Do you believe him?"

Zephyrine thought about Coral's face in the generator yard, lit orange by the floodlights, calm in a way that had frightened her more than any panic could have. *Copy the ledger, cut the wire, don't wait for me.* Three instructions, delivered like a diving briefing. He had spent his whole life preparing for the moment he'd have to vanish underwater and trust the surface to someone else.

"I believe he doesn't lie about the sea," she said. "And I think he's decided this mountain is the sea."

Ilsa studied her for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Zephyrine's face made her nod slowly and move back down the rope to check on the twins.

They descended until full dark, bivouacked in a hollow beneath an overhang that her father identified from the map with a shout of joy so sudden and so human that two of the freed survey members actually laughed—a cracked, disbelieving sound, the first laughter any of them had managed. They melted snow over a single small stove, shared a ration of biscuits and the last of the cheese from the shepherd's bothy, and lay pressed together for warmth like a litter of animals.

Zephyrine slept in fragments. In the gaps between sleep she listened to the storm working the upper mountain, and she found, to her own surprise, that she was praying—not to anything in particular, having never settled on a recipient, but in the general direction of the col, of the cell, of Coral. *Be slippery,* she prayed. *Be water. Be the thing they can't hold.*

It was, she realized somewhere in the black hours, the same prayer she had been saying about herself for three weeks. She wondered when she had stopped needing it for herself.

* * *

On the second day the storm broke as abruptly as it had come, and the world reappeared around them like a developing photograph: the forest rising out of the whiteness, dark and dripping; the sound of water, growing; and by midday, the river itself, swollen and brown and loud, running through a gorge whose walls steamed faintly in the returning sun.

They followed it down. The going was easier now and also stranger, because ease itself had become suspicious—as if the mountain were luring them toward some final cruelty. But the map held. Her father's map held. Twice Zephyrine located landmarks he had penciled years ago—a split boulder shaped like a molar, a waterfall threading down a rock face like spilled thread—and each time she felt him beside her more strongly than any ghost, felt the certainty of a man who had drawn escape routes for strangers all his life without ever expecting his daughter to walk one.

At the second landmark, Pell, the younger carrier, fell into step beside her. He had barely spoken since the breakout, and when he did speak now his voice came out rusty, as if borrowed.

"They asked us questions," he said, eyes fixed on the trail. "For weeks. Same questions every day. About the escarpment, the passes, who else knew the routes. And every night I'd lie awake thinking—I'll tell them everything, I'll tell them every pass I've ever heard of, just let me go home." His jaw worked. "But your father never told them anything. I watched him. Sixty-one days, and he gave them weather reports and jokes and nothing else. Nothing." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and young. "So I started giving nothing too. That's why I'm alive to be cold right now. Because of him. Because of both of you."

Zephyrine didn't trust her voice, so she put her hand briefly on his shoulder and kept walking. Ahead, her father pretended very hard to be absorbed in the map.

* * *

They reached the track on the third day, and the village of Sainte-Ombline on the fourth.

It was hardly a village anymore—forty roofs, half of them sagging, a church with a leaning spire, a café that was also the post office that was also, according to the painted sign, a garage. But it had a telephone line, and it had Madame Roux, the postmistress, who took one look at eleven gaunt, frostbitten people coming down the river path and did not ask a single question before unlocking the café, starting the stove, and setting water to boil with the efficiency of a woman who had lived through things that had taught her the order of operations.

What happened next happened in layers, the way thaw happens.

First: food, blankets, the twins wrapped and fed and finally, finally speaking, arguing about whose turn it was to sleep nearest the fire. Second: the doctor from two valleys over, summoned by telephone, arriving mud-spattered on a motorbike to splint Pell's wrist and listen to Okonkwo's heart with a frown. Third: Ilsa Brandt, seated at the telephone table with Madame Roux standing guard at the door, placing calls—to a newspaper editor in the capital whose name she had memorized in captivity and recited nightly like a rosary; to a magistrate; to a cousin in the postal ministry; to anyone, everyone, the whole slow web of people outside the valley who did not yet know what had been built above them.

Zephyrine stood in the doorway between the warmth of the room and the blue dusk outside, holding a cup of coffee she had forgotten to drink, and listened to Ilsa say, for perhaps the twentieth time, the sentence that would end everything:

"Yes. The documents are real. No—you cannot send soldiers up the western road; the navy holds it. Send the magistrate. Send journalists. Send photographers." A pause, and then, in a voice that cracked only slightly: "Send someone before they send their own."

Madame Roux touched Zephyrine's arm. "Your young man," she said. "The one they kept. There will be news?"

"He promised us a week," Zephyrine said.

"And you believe him?"

"I believe he's had ten years of practice at surviving rooms he wasn't meant to leave."

Madame Roux nodded as if this were a perfectly normal credential, and went back to her stove.

* * *

The news came on the sixth day, and it came the way weather comes to the coast—all at once, from everywhere.

A journalist arrived first, on foot, breathless, followed within hours by a photographer and a radio van that got stuck twice on the river track and had to be hauled out by villagers who charged nothing and asked everything. By evening the café was full of strangers with notebooks, and Ilsa Brandt stood at the center of it, transformed, her radio-operator spine perfectly straight, dictating dates, names, numbers—the four transferred prisoners, the ledger entries, the cataloguing system, the word *assets*—while the twins corroborated and Okonkwe drew the facility's layout on the back of a menu with a pencil stub.

Zephyrine's father sat by the window with his ruined hands folded, and when a reporter knelt beside him and asked gently how he had endured, he said only: "I had a map to finish," and the reporter wrote it down, and Zephyrine turned away to hide her face against the cold glass.

The radio broadcast went out at eight o'clock. Madame Roux had the only set, and the whole village crowded into the café's back room to hear it, a wall of bodies and held breath, while the announcer's voice crackled through static:

*"—documents smuggled from a classified installation in the high ranges have reached the capital tonight. The Ministry has confirmed the existence of the facility and announced, quote, 'a full independent inquiry.' Among the named witnesses is a salvage diver—"*

Zephyrine stopped hearing the rest. She was aware of the room going on around her—her father's hand finding hers, Ilsa's sharp clarifying voice cutting through the crowd—but mostly she was aware of a feeling rising in her chest like water finding its level, something that had been dammed for weeks breaking loose all at once.

Because the announcer had said it. Out loud, on the air, to the whole country: *a salvage diver*. Named. Witnessed. Real. Coral existed now in the official record, not as a fugitive or a file number or a man erased, but as a witness with a name and a story, and no government on earth could un-ring that bell. Whatever happened in the interrogation room—whatever they threatened, whatever they offered—they could not make him disappear again. The record existed. The copies existed. He had made sure of both, and now the whole machinery of silence was grinding against facts it could not digest.

"Zephyrine." Her father's voice, quiet, close to her ear. "Listen."

The announcer again: *"—the diver remains in custody at an undisclosed location. Authorities state he is cooperating fully. A spokesperson declined to comment on reports of a negotiated release—"*

Negotiated. The word went through her like warm water through ice. Not broken. Not silenced. Negotiating. Sitting across a table from the officer who had erased him, trading testimony for freedom, holding the ledger like a lantern they couldn't take from him.

He had told her once, on the Kestrel, in the gray light off the escarpment coast, what his plan had always been if they ever caught him: run, dive deep, change the name, start again. Ten years of that. Ten years of ports that asked nothing.

Now the whole country was asking about him, and for the first time it was safe to answer.

She laughed—one short, startled sound—and pressed her hand over her mouth, and her father laughed too, low and cracked, and around them the room was cheering, and Madame Roux was opening bottles of something, and outside the window the first stars were coming out over the eastern ridges, over the passes they had crossed, over the white and terrible country that had tried to swallow them and had instead, improbably, delivered them here.

Exhaustion hit her then, all at once, like a tide turning. She sat down heavily on the bench beside her father and leaned her head against his shoulder, and he put his arm around her the way he had when she was small, and she slept without meaning to, mid-celebration, mid-sentence, sleeping the way the rescued sleep—deeply, greedily, without one ear left listening for boots on the stairs.

When she woke near midnight the café was quiet, the strangers gone, embers ticking in the stove. A single lamp burned. On the table beside her, weighted under her father's compass, was a telegram that had come on the evening wire, addressed simply: *MARCHANT FAMILY, SAINTE-OMBLINE.*

Her father was watching her read it, his eyes bright.

The message was six words long, filed from a garrison town on the far side of the range, signed with a name she had heard Coral speak of only once, bitterly, in the dark of the bothy:

*RELEASE NEGOTIATED. HE IS COMING HOME.*

Below it, in different ink, added by whoever had transmitted it, a seventh word—an addition that made her father chuckle and shake his head, because only one man alive would bribe a telegraph clerk to append it:

*WHICH WAY TO YOUR COAST?*
