## 1. The Gate That Keeps Nothing

There are three rules to keeping a gate in Vellamoor, and I learned all of them from a man who was dead before I could walk.

Rule one: lock it every night, even if no one has come. *Especially* if no one has come. Rule two: never ask what you're locking out, or in. Rule three — and this is the one that keeps a keeper a keeper — do not hope.

I have kept the gate for eleven years, and I have broken rule three exactly once per year, which I consider excellent discipline.

Tonight the village was loud enough to hear all the way up at the gatehouse, which sits at the end of the Hollow Road where the valley narrows to a throat of grey stone. The twelfth moon-turning. Once a year Vellamoor's second moon — the pale one, the shy one, the one the almanacs politely pretend is a weather phenomenon — completes its slow circle overhead, and once a year the whole country throws a festival about it. Lanterns on the millrace. Honey-cakes shaped like crescents. Old Fenwick playing the fiddle badly enough that people dance anyway, because in Vellamoor we have learned to be grateful for effort.

I could hear them singing the Turnsong down through the trees. Everyone knows the Turnsong. It's four lines long and has been sung so many times that the words have gone smooth as river stones, meaning worn off, melody left. *Turn, turn, the pale moon turns, and what was shut stays shut till morning.* Something like that. Even I know it, and I know almost nothing about anything except hinges.

That's the joke of my position, if you like jokes. Keeper of a gate that keeps nothing. The gate hasn't opened in a hundred years. There are doors in the chapel older than its last use. There are grandmothers in Vellamoor who were born after it last opened and will die without seeing it move. And yet every dusk I walk the Hollow Road with my ring of iron keys — nine of them, each heavier than any key has a right to be — and I perform the ritual exactly as old Keeper Bram taught me to perform it, in the exact order, because the one time I tried to skip ahead to the third lock, the gate sulked. Yes. Sulked. The iron went cold under my hands and refused to take the key until I'd walked back to the first bolt and started over, properly, like a penitent.

You learn respect for a door like that.

The ritual goes thus: First the outer chain, wound thrice around the near post, sunwise. Then the bar, which is a single beam of black oak longer than I am tall and which I swear weighs less on festival nights, as though the gate itself is in a good mood. Then the seven small locks along the seam — not seven locks in the ordinary sense, but seven seams in the iron that only look like locks if you squint, and only open to keys if the keys are mine. Then, last, the great central lock, the one they call the Mouth, which has no keyhole that anyone else can find. Bram told me the Mouth opens to the keeper's hand alone. He also told me he'd never once felt it turn under his palm, in forty years of keeping, and that I shouldn't expect better.

He was right. Eleven years, and the Mouth has never turned. I press my hand flat against it each night, say the words Bram gave me — *keep what sleeps, sleep what keeps* — and feel absolutely nothing happen, in the way that nothing happens when you knock on the inside of your own skull.

Then I go home to the gatehouse, eat bread and honey-cake stolen from the festival by way of a basket someone always leaves on my step and never signs, and I do not hope.

* * *

Tonight I broke pattern slightly, which should have warned me. Festivals make me sentimental. Everyone else in Vellamoor belongs to somebody — a family, a guild, a choir, a scandal — and I belong to a door. So while the Turnsong drifted up the valley I sat on the gatehouse steps with my feet in the bracken and let myself want things. Small things. A seat at a table where my name was said warmly rather than officially. Someone who'd leave their signature on the honey-cakes instead of their pity.

And then, because rule three had already cracked, I did something worse. I talked to the gate.

"Don't suppose you're invited to the turning," I said. "Don't suppose anyone remembers you can hear."

The lanterns below looked like a spill of stars that had given up on the sky. Somewhere Fenwick murdered a high note. The second moon cleared the eastern ridge, pale as a coin rubbed blank, and threw just enough light for me to see my breath.

I got up, because sentiment doesn't lock gates, and did the ritual.

Chain, sunwise. Bar — lighter tonight, definitely lighter, I checked twice. Seams one through seven, keys turning in silence, the familiar nothing. And then I came to the Mouth, pressed my hand flat against the cold iron, and felt it hum.

Now. I want to be clear about what that hum was not. It was not wind; there was no wind. It was not the festival; sound doesn't travel uphill through stone like that, low and steady, like a held note from somewhere behind the metal. It was not my imagination, because I have a very disciplined imagination and it knows better than to hum.

It was a note. A single, patient note, deep under the iron, the kind of note a choir holds when it's waiting for the rest of the voices to find it.

I snatched my hand back. The hum stopped — or didn't stop, but went quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone realizes they've been overheard.

"Keep what sleeps," I said, too fast, "sleep what keeps," and pressed my hand back on, because duty is a habit stronger than fear. The iron was warm now. Warm as skin. Under my palm, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the note rose again — and this time I would have sworn, would stake my nine ridiculous keys on it, that it wasn't coming from the gate.

It was coming from beyond it. From outside, through a hundred years of shut iron, something was holding a tone. Waiting.

The Turnsong floated up the valley, four smooth worn lines sung by two hundred voices, and the note behind the gate slid toward it — matched it — harmonized with it. My whole body went cold. Because here is the thing about the Turnsong, the thing I've noticed for eleven years and never said aloud: it has a hole in it. A gap where a phrase should be, between the second line and the third, a silence everyone steps over without looking down. Every version I've ever heard has it. I used to think it was just an old song wearing thin.

But the voice behind the gate sang straight into that gap. Filled it. One clean phrase, in a language I don't speak and somehow understood anyway, and the hair on my arms stood up and the seventh seam-lock clicked — clicked — under its own power, rotating a half-turn toward open before I slammed the key home and held it there, heart slamming harder.

The gate went still. The moon rode on. Down in the village, nobody stopped singing.

I finished the ritual with shaking hands and then I did not go home. I sat on the steps until the lanterns burned down, telling myself reasonable things: old iron, cold night, festival wine at dinner. But reasonable things have never fit in that gap in the Turnsong either, and I knew, sitting there in the dark with my palms still tingling, that tomorrow I would walk down into Vellamoor and do something I have never done as keeper.

Ask questions.

Because a hundred-year silence does not hum. And a song with a hole in it is not a song that's finished.
