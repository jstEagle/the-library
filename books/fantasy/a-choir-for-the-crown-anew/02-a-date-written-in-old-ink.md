## 2. A Date Written in Old Ink

I want to be clear from the start that I did not go looking for prophecy. I went looking for a candle.

The chapel's lower archive had eaten my spare tallow the night before — mice, or damp, or one of the choir children who treated the undercroft as a private kingdom of echoes. Either way, my ritual at the gate required a light. The gate-keeper's manual, such as it was, said so: *Let the keeper carry flame to the door each turn, that the dark may know he comes in honor.* Nobody knew who had written the manual. Nobody had read past the third page in living memory. But I had read all of it, twice, because when your entire life is one door and one key and one walk down a hill, you take your literature where you can find it.

So: candle. Archive. Stairs that smelled like cold stone and older paper.

The archive beneath the Chapel of the Turning is organized according to no system any sane person would recognize. Maera, the archivist, claims this is deliberate — that memory, properly kept, should resist tidy hands. She has said this while standing atop a ladder made of three ladders, so I tend to believe her.

I found my candles eventually, in a crate marked *WAX (DO NOT)*, which Maera insisted was a complete instruction. And beside the crate, wedged where something had shifted it out over decades, was a book with a cover the color of dried blood and a spine that crackled when I touched it, like something waking up.

I should have put it back. Let the record show that Astrid Pemberton, keeper of the gate, dutiful as a fence post, reached instead.

* * *

The book was a ledger once — you could see the ghost-lines of columns beneath later writing. Someone had scraped a century of accounts off the vellum and written over them in ink gone brown as old wounds. The hand changed partway through. The first scribe wrote neat and small, like someone afraid of being heard. The second wrote large and slanting, like someone who had stopped caring who heard.

The first page said: *A Record of the Turns, Kept Against Forgetting.*

Which is a strange thing to write about Vellamoor, because Vellamoor does not forget anything. That's our whole character as a country. We remember every harvest, every grudge, every wedding for a hundred years back, because nothing leaves us and nothing arrives. Memory here doesn't fade; it just gets heavier, like furniture.

But the book disagreed with the whole country. The second scribe — and his entries grew shakier as they went, as though his hand were being erased along with everything else — had written:

*We are going out of the world slowly. First our name. Then our borders. Then, if we are not careful, our faces. I write this so that someone, someday, will know there was a country here, and that it was taken, and by whom, and what it cost.*

And then, further in, on a page that made my skin do something I didn't authorize:

*The thirteenth turn falls upon the last moonrise of the harvest month, in the year the keeper is twenty-and-six. On that night the valley must choose: keep its silence and dissolve, or sing itself back into the world. On that night the keeper must choose also. The door will open for him alone. It has been waiting for him since before his birth. His name is written in these margins, and I have prayed every day of my life that I am wrong about him.*

My name is Astrid Pemberton. I am twenty-six years old as of the spring. Tonight is the last moonrise of the harvest month. Tonight is the twelfth turning, which everyone in Vellamoor knows, because everyone in Vellamoor knows everything, always, forever, amen.

Except apparently not. Except apparently tonight was also something else, something written down a hundred years ago by a man whose handwriting got worse the closer he came to telling the truth.

I sat down on the floor of the archive with a candle stub burning between my knees and read the margins. Margins are where people tell the truth, I've found. The text is performance; the margin is the diary. And the margins of this book were dense with a smaller, tighter hand — annotations, corrections, arguments with himself. Dates cross-checked against almanacs. Moon-tables copied out and recopied. And names. Dozens of names, most crossed out, some circled, all written in a code of initials I couldn't crack until near the end, where the scribe gave up on subtlety entirely and wrote:

*A.P. — if it must be someone, let it be no one. Let it be the boy nobody sees. He will not be missed. God forgive me, but he will not even be noticed.*

I read that sentence four times.

Here is a thing about being invisible in a place where everyone remembers everything: you assume it's an accident. You assume the universe simply misplaced you, the way Maera misplaces candles, and that someday a cosmic archivist will find you behind a crate marked WAX (DO NOT) and file you correctly. You build your whole personality around being patient about it. Dutiful. Quiet. A fence post with opinions.

And then you find out it wasn't an accident. You find out somebody chose it. Somebody looked at the shape of the future and decided that the least noticeable person available would do, and wrote your initials in a margin next to the word *door*, and then died a hundred years before you were born, comfortably certain that you wouldn't mind.

I minded.

* * *

Maera found me there at dawn, sitting in the same position, surrounded by wax drippings like little white tombstones. She is eighty-some years old, built like a question mark, and she has looked at me exactly four times in my life with her actual attention, which is four more than anyone else has managed. This time she looked at me for a long moment, and then at the book, and something crossed her face that I had never seen there before.

It was fear.

"Where did you find this," she said, not asking.

"Behind the candles."

"Of course it was." She lowered herself onto the step beside me with the sound of a ship taking on water. "I've been looking for that book for forty years. It was never behind the candles. It was waiting."

"For whom?"

She didn't answer that. Instead she took the book from my hands — gently, the way you take something from someone standing on a ledge — and turned to the margin page. Her finger stopped under the initials. Under *A.P.*

"Read me the passage," she said. "About the thirteenth turn. Aloud."

I read it. My voice sounded wrong in the stone room, too loud, like the walls were listening harder than they should.

When I finished, Maera closed her eyes. "Tonight," she said. "Tonight is the date."

"The twelfth turning," I said. "Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what we've been told to know." She opened her eyes and they were wet and furious and ancient. "Boy, how many moons did the sky show last night?"

"Twelve. There are always twelve. We count them at every festival."

"Count again tonight. Count carefully." She gripped my wrist; her hand was stronger than her shipwreck suggested. "This book says there is a thirteenth. It says there always has been, hidden, and that it turns only when the valley decides whether to exist. And it says—" her finger tapped the page, tap, tap, tap, like a woodpecker going after my chest "—that you are the one who opens the door. The door you lock every night. The door that hasn't opened in a hundred years. It's been waiting for *you*."

"That's insane."

"Yes," she agreed cheerfully, in the tone of someone confirming that yes, the house is indeed on fire. "Completely. Now come along. The elders meet at noon, and you're going to stand in front of them holding this book, and they're going to call it heresy, and I'm going to enjoy watching their faces."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because," Maera said, hauling herself upright, "you've spent twenty-six years being nobody, and this morning you woke up being the most important somebody this country has seen in a hundred years, and I'd very much like to see whether that changes how you walk."

It did. I hated that it did.

* * *

The elders received us in the chapter room, five of them seated in a crescent like a jury that had rehearsed. Elder Coram in the center, gray-bearded, gray-robed, gray in the particular way of men who have decided the world should stop changing without consulting them first.

He listened to Maera's account with his eyes shut, which I learned later is how he listens to everything he intends to refuse.

"Heresy," he said, opening them. "Scrap-lore. A forged book by a frightened man a century dead."

"He dated tonight," I said. "Exactly. The harvest moonrise. Down to the year of the keeper's age."

"Coincidence."

"His initials are in the margin. Mine. A.P. He described me, Coram. 'The boy nobody sees.'"

Coram finally looked at me directly, and I understood in that instant why the village forgot to look at me: I recognized the expression. It wasn't indifference. It was effort. Looking at me cost him something, and he had simply decided, long ago, along with everyone else, to stop paying.

"There is no thirteenth moon," he said. "There is no door that opens. There is no prophecy. There is Vellamoor, whole and eternal, and there are troublemakers who want the young man frightened." He rose. "Burn it."

"You can't burn a book in the archive," Maera said mildly.

"Then lock it away where it was."

"It was never locked away. It was *waiting*. There's a difference, Coram, and the difference is currently standing right here holding it, and his name is in the margin."

For one heartbeat — I saw it, I'll swear to it — Coram's composure cracked, and underneath it was not anger but terror. Raw, wet-eyed terror, the kind you feel at three in the morning. Then he sealed it up again and said the words I would hear in my head for days after:

"The matter is closed. Keeper — return to your gate. Do your duty. Touch nothing else."

Duty. My oldest friend. My cage.

I carried the book out anyway. Maera walked beside me, and halfway across the chapel yard she said, quietly, without looking at me:

"They're afraid because the book is true. And they're more afraid of what else is true — that the elders' own grandparents helped make the bargain that put us here. Come to the undercroft tonight, after the festival. Before the moonrise. Bring the book." She paused. "And bring your voice, boy. However bad it is. Especially if it's bad."

"Why?"

"Because tonight," she said, "we're going to need a choir."

Behind us, above the chapel roof, the sky was already turning the bruised purple it gets before a harvest moon, and I counted the pale shapes gathering in it — one, two, three — and made myself stop counting, because I was suddenly, terribly afraid of what number I might reach.
