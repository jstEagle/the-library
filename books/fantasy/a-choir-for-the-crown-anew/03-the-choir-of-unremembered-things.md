## 3. The Choir of Unremembered Things

I have buried three husbands, one archive, and a great many lies, and I will tell you honestly: the hardest of these was the archive. Husbands, at least, stay buried.

The others came to my kitchen that evening the way outcasts always come—sideways, apologetic, as though they'd been invited by accident and were waiting for me to notice the mistake. Tobin arrived first, because thieves are punctual; it's the honest folk who dawdle. He sat on my good stool with his boots off—some half-remembered courtesy—and began, quietly, to steal the sound of my kettle. I watched him do it. He cupped his hand behind his ear and then pressed his palm flat against his chest, the way a man pockets a coin, and when the kettle finally screamed he flinched as if caught mid-larceny.

"Maera," he said, "your kettle sounds like grief."

"Everything in this valley does," I said. "That's rather the point."

Ilse came next. She did not knock; she never knocks, having spent eleven years knocking at doors that opened onto her own disgrace. She stood in my doorway with her moon-priest's collar torn out—the pale crescent of cloth gone from her throat like a tooth from a jaw—and she looked at my kitchen, at Tobin, at the two chairs I had set facing each other for no reason I was prepared to admit aloud, and she said, "You're gathering them again."

"I'm gathering *us*," I corrected her. "There's a difference, and the difference is you."

"The elders excommunicated me."

"The elders excommunicated a woman who told the truth. That's not an excommunication, Ilse. That's a citation."

She came in. Outcasts always do, in the end. It's the one door we all know how to open.

* * *

By full dark there were nine of us in my kitchen, which had not held nine people since my second husband's wake, and the room seemed to remember it, groaning in its joists. Let me name them, because naming is the whole of my trade and I am out of practice:

Tobin, who steals sounds and cannot explain why, only that empty air where music used to be feels to him like a wound in the world. Sela, whose voice could once crack ice on the millpond and who has not sung above a whisper since the elders fined her for singing her mother's name at the spring festival—a name, they said, that was not on any list. The brothers Fen and Odo, who lie so beautifully and so constantly that between them they have invented a Vellamoor more vivid than the real one, and who came tonight because, as Odo put it, "a lie needs somewhere true to stand." Old Wren, who remembers being a child in the first forgotten years and can no longer recall whether she ever had siblings or merely sang to fill the silence where they should have been. The twins, Ash and Ellery, who hum in perfect unison without meaning to and hate each other gently for it. And Ilse, disgraced priestess of a moon that still turns for us, thank whatever listens, even now.

And Astrid Pemberton, the gatekeeper, who stood last in my doorway with rain on his shoulders and the expression of a man who has been invited to his own surprise party and suspects the surprise is a funeral.

"Sit," I told him. "You're the guest of honor, though you don't know it yet, and honors go down easier seated."

He sat. He is a dutiful young man. Duty brought him here, though he'd have said curiosity, because duty is embarrassing to confess and curiosity flatters us all.

* * *

"Now," I said, and took down the book.

I had wrapped it in oilcloth against damp and disapproval, which in Vellamoor amount to much the same thing. The prophecy book. A century old, written in ink gone brown as old blood, found three days ago beneath the chapel floor by a mason who could not read and brought it to me because I am the only person in this valley who hoards words the way Tobin hoards sounds.

I laid it open on my table, and I felt the room lean in—not the people, mind you, but the room itself, the walls, the kettle, the dark beyond the windows. Some books are just books. This one had been waiting.

"Before your grandparents were born," I said, "Vellamoor had a name. Its true name. Not 'the valley,' not 'here,' not the embarrassed cough we use when traders ask where our wool comes from. A name. And we lost it—not mislaid it, not wore it out, but *sold* it, or had it taken, and the difference matters less than you'd think. When a country loses its name, the world loses the country. Maps forget. Roads bend away. Traders' eyes slide off us like rain off wax. We have been erased for a hundred years, and the erasure is not finished. It is *ongoing*. Every year the forgetting climbs another street. Every year someone else's mother becomes a woman nobody can picture."

Sela whispered something. It might have been her mother's name. Nobody fined her.

"This book," I went on, "was written by the last choir. Yes, there was a choir before us—there was always a choir, since the founding, since before the crown. Memory is heavy, children, and a country cannot carry its own past alone; it needs voices to share the weight. The choir sang the names into keeping. Streets, rivers, dead kings, living griefs—all of it held in song, passed mouth to mouth, because paper burns and stone crumbles but a song lives anywhere there are mouths. And when the bargain was struck—when our king gave away our name to save his skin and his throne—the choir did the last thing left to it. They wrote down what they knew was coming." I turned the page to the line I had read forty times until it read itself in my sleep. "*In the thirteenth turn of the thirteenth moon, the keeper must choose: keep the door shut and be forgotten with us, or open it and carry us out.*"

Silence. The good kind, the kind that means everyone is doing arithmetic.

"Tonight," said Ilse, "is the twelfth turning."

"Which makes tomorrow night the thirteenth," said Wren, "and none of us will remember why that mattered."

"Unless," I said, "we sing."

* * *

Here is what I did not say, what I kept folded away like the book itself: that I had known about the choir's purpose since I was a girl, that my grandmother taught me fragments over bread and made me swear to forget them, which is a grandmother's way of making sure you don't. That I had spent sixty years watching the forgetting climb our streets like ivy and telling myself the elders were right, that memory is dangerous, that a small safe nothing is better than a large remembered grief. I am old enough to know that cowardice wears duty's coat very well.

But Astrid had come to me two nights ago with the book's date in his hand and his face the color of paper, and he had asked me, in the voice of a man asking whether he exists, "Maera, is this me? Is this—" and he couldn't finish, and I had looked at the margins where some long-dead chorister had written *the keeper, the keeper, God forgive us, the keeper*, and I had lied to him. I said I didn't know.

I am done lying to children. Liars' privileges go to Fen and Odo; they've earned them.

So I said to my kitchen full of broken singers: "The door at the valley's edge has been locked for a hundred years. Everyone believes it keeps the outside out. It doesn't. It keeps the *name* in—or what's left of it. And it answers to one thing only: a sung memory, whole and true. The choir's work. Our work, if we're stupid enough and brave enough, which historically we have been both."

"And him?" said Tobin, nodding at Astrid. "Why's the gatekeeper here?"

"Because," I said, "he's the only soul in this valley the door has ever hummed for."

Astrid looked up sharply. "How did you—"

"You told me. Two nights ago. You said the gate hummed under your hand and you thought you'd imagined it." I let that sit. "Gatekeepers before you have tended that lock for a century and heard nothing but wind. Either the door has developed a taste for you specifically, Astrid Pemberton, or it has been waiting for you specifically, and I have lived long enough to distrust coincidence dressed as weather."

He was quiet a long moment. Then, in a voice smaller than I'd ever heard him use: "Waiting for me to do what?"

"To choose," said Ilse, from the shadows by the stove, and her voice had the old liturgical weight in it, the weight they tore her collar out over. "That's what the book says. The keeper must choose."

* * *

We did not rehearse that night. Rehearsal implies a performance ahead, and none of us yet believed in audiences. But Sela, emboldened by company or desperation, hummed four notes of the founding song—the one every child learns with the hole in it, the verse where the country's name should be and isn't, cut out a century ago like a cataract from an eye. Four notes, and the twins harmonized without meaning to, and Odo, incorrigible, invented a word to fill the gap, and Fen shushed him, and Wren wept without noise, which is a skill the old ones here perfect early.

And Astrid—

Astrid stood up. I thought he meant to leave. Dutiful men leave; it's their signature move. Instead he walked to the middle of my kitchen, into the middle of that ragged four-note hum, and stopped there, eyes closed, listening the way a deaf man reads lips—with his whole body.

"It knows me," he said. Not to us. To the song, maybe. To the hole in it. "The song. Maera, when you hummed it at the chapel, I thought—I've heard it before. Before tonight. Someone sang it to me. At the gate. When I was—" His hand rose to his sternum, the way Tobin presses stolen sounds against himself. "When I was very small. I've carried it my whole life and I never knew what it was missing until just now."

"What's it missing?" I asked, though I knew. Oh, I knew, and my heart was already breaking and mending in the same breath, the way hearts do when a story turns the corner toward truth.

"A name," Astrid said. "Mine."

The kettle, cold on the stove, ticked once, like a clock deciding.

Nine outcasts and a gatekeeper in a kitchen at the valley's edge, and outside, high and indifferent, the twelfth moon beginning its long turn toward morning—and somewhere in the dark between us and the hills, a door that had waited a hundred years for exactly this voice, exactly this song, exactly this boy left at a gate with a lullaby sewn into his silence.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow we find out what he is.

But tonight, for the first time in a hundred years, Vellamoor had a choir again—and its newest member had just discovered he was also its oldest song.
