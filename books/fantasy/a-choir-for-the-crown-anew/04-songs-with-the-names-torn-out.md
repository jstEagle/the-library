## 4. Songs With the Names Torn Out

I want it on record that I did not volunteer for this choir. I was *acquired*.

Maera found me in the tannery yard at dawn, which is where I keep my best things, and she didn't ask what I was doing there because Maera is a hundred years old and has stopped pretending not to know everything. She just said, "Tobin Quill. You steal sounds."

"I collect them," I said. "There's a difference. Stealing implies the owner wanted to keep them."

"Come sing."

"Absolutely not."

Which is how I ended up in the chapel undercroft three nights later, wedged between a disgraced moon-priest who smelled of cold ash and a girl named Sef who could hold a note so long I genuinely believed she'd die before she finished it.

The undercroft smelled like every secret Vellamoor ever kept: candle grease, old paper, damp stone, and underneath all of it something sweeter, like apples left too long in a cellar. Maera had us arranged in a rough half-circle around a table of songbooks so old their covers had gone soft as leather gloves worn through at the fingers.

"These are the founding songs," she said. "The ones your grandmothers' grandmothers sang when the valley was still *somewhere*."

"Still somewhere?" Sef asked.

"The valley was erased from the world, child. But before it was erased, it was written down." Maera opened the first book with the tenderness most people reserve for infants and explosives. "We are going to learn them. All of them. Before the thirteenth moon turns."

Ilse — the priest, the ash-smell, the woman whose name the elders had scraped off the temple doors with such enthusiasm they'd gouged the wood — snorted from her corner. "And if we don't?"

"Then we were never here at all," Maera said, "and neither was anything else."

I've heard a lot of threats in my line of work. Most of them involve broken fingers. This one involved nonexistence, and I'll be honest: it landed.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about singing in a group: you can't hide. Alone, I can hum in the dark and pretend my voice is a private matter. In a half-circle of six people, every crack and wobble is public record. My voice came out like a gate hinge that needed oiling, and Sef winced, and Ilse looked at me with an expression I can only describe as theological disappointment.

But then we got into it. And here's the other thing nobody tells you: the founding songs are *good*. They're built like rivers — they carry you whether you deserve it or not. There's one about the harvest that made me hungry just hearing it. There's one about a wedding where the bride ran off with the fiddler, and the whole room laughed even though none of us had ever heard the story before, because the song remembered it for us. That's the trick of these songs. They're not entertainment. They're storage.

We sang for two hours. Then we hit the third song, the one called "Vellamoor Rising," which by its title ought to have been the proudest thing in the book.

It wasn't. It was Swiss cheese.

There's a moment in every verse — the same spot, the fourth line — where the melody swells up like it's about to say the most important thing in the world, and then says nothing. A rest. A hole. The tune leans into the gap the way a person leans on a crutch, and moves on.

We tried it twice. Both times, the silence in the fourth line sat there like a guest nobody would look at.

"It's a rest," Sef said finally. "Songs have rests."

"Songs have rests," Maera agreed, "but not *hungry* rests. Sing it again. Listen to the shape of the quiet."

So I listened, because listening is my actual profession, whatever Maera calls it. And she was right. The rest wasn't empty. It was shaped. Every verse, the same shape — roughly four syllables long, rising at the start, falling at the end. Something had lived there once. Something with a name-shaped silhouette.

Someone had cut it out.

"Not a rest," I said. Everyone turned. I don't love being the one who talks; talking attracts attention, and attention is how you get caught. But the words were out. "A removal. Look — the ink's different around the gaps. Whoever wrote this copy went over the page after, smoothing it down. You don't smooth down a rest. Rests are supposed to be there."

"How would you know?" Ilse asked, but not unkindly. She had the voice of someone who'd asked a lot of hostile questions professionally and missed it.

"Because I know what erasure looks like," I said. "It's my whole trade."

* * *

Let me explain myself, since apparently we're doing honesty now.

Most thieves take objects. Rings, coins, the occasional decorative spoon. Fine work, honest work, but crowded. I specialized early in something rarer: sounds. It started as a survival skill — when you grow up unwanted in a valley where everyone knows everyone, you learn to memorize voices so you can tell who's coming for you before they arrive. Then I learned I could do more than recognize a sound. I could *keep* it. Hum it back exactly. The creak of the chapel door. The way the miller swore when his wheel jammed — a whole vocabulary, actually, the miller's swearing, I have hours of it. The bell that rang the night the twelfth moon first turned, and the sound the crowd made after, which was joy with a hairline crack running through it.

I hoard them. I know that's strange. But in a country that forgets, somebody has to remember, and I never trusted anyone else to do it properly.

So when Maera said *learn the songs*, some greedy little organ behind my ribs lit up like a festival. Because I had fragments. For years I'd been picking up scraps of old music the way other people pick up dropped coins — off drunk elders humming in the tavern, off a cracked phonograph-thing in the archive I definitely wasn't authorized to touch, off the wind coming down off the moor in autumn, which up there sings in a register nothing else does.

And some of those scraps fit the holes.

I hadn't known that until tonight. I'd been collecting puzzle pieces without knowing there was a picture. Now the picture was sitting on a table in front of me, full of gaps shaped exactly like things in my pockets.

I should mention: telling people what you have is how you lose it. That's Thief Rule One. Rule Two is *never sing in public*. I broke both rules within the hour.

* * *

"Play it again," I said. "The harvest song. Fourth line."

Maera's eyes narrowed. She's sharp for a hundred; probably sharper than she was at fifty, having spent the intervening decades sharpening herself on the elders' stupidity. But she sang it, and the choir followed, and when we reached the hungry rest I did the thing I've never done in front of another living soul.

I filled it.

Four syllables. I don't know what they mean — I stole them off a drunk shepherd eleven years ago, and he was crying while he hummed them, which is usually a good sign that a fragment matters. They rose at the start and fell at the end, and they slotted into the gap like a key into a lock, and the whole song *shuddered*, the way a house shudders when a door opens somewhere far away.

Nobody breathed.

"What was that?" Sef whispered.

"That," I said, and my voice cracked, which I'll deny forever, "was a piece of the name."

"The name of what?"

"The name of *us*." I dug into my coat — into the inside pocket, the real pocket, the one I keep against my skin — and pulled out the little tin where I keep my most valuable holdings. Not coins. A strip of birchbark with notes scratched on it. A dried reed cut to a specific length, which whistles one specific tone. A scrap of paper with a child's rhyme on it that no child in this valley currently knows. "I've been finding them for years. Fragments. Scraps of songs nobody remembers learning. I thought I was just — collecting. I didn't know they were pieces of the same thing."

"And now you do," Ilse said quietly.

"Now I do." I laid them out on the table one by one, and I want you to understand what that cost me. Each item was years of careful, solitary, paranoid work. Laying them down in front of five near-strangers felt like skinning myself. "Eleven fragments. Maybe more buried in my head that I haven't written down. The shepherd's four syllables. A phrase off the wind on the moor. Half a line from a lullaby I heard a woman singing at the north gate when I was nine — she stopped when she saw me, but I got most of it."

Maera touched the birchbark like it might bruise. "You've been keeping Vellamoor's name alive alone. In a tin."

"Don't make it noble," I said. "I'm a magpie. It's a disease."

"It's a *choir*," Maera said. "Or it will be. That's what a choir is, boy — a magpie flock with discipline."

Sef laughed, sudden and bright, and then Ilse smiled — actually smiled, which I suspect was as rare and dangerous an event in this valley as the thirteenth moon itself — and something happened in that damp stone room that I didn't have a word for yet. Later I'd call it trust. At the time it just felt like setting down a weight I'd forgotten I was carrying, and hating how good it felt, because weights are also armor.

We worked until the candles drowned in their own wax. Fragment by fragment, we tested my hoard against the songs' wounds. Some pieces fit. Some didn't — wrong era, maybe, or wrong song entirely, orphaned syllables from verses lost past recovery. But seven of the eleven slid home, and each time one did, the whole room flinched, like the songs themselves were surprised to be whole anywhere.

By the end, Sef was crying without noticing, and Ilse had gone very still and very pale, and Maera was looking at me the way you look at a door you'd assumed was painted on.

"There's a problem," I said, because there's always a problem, and better I find it than hope does.

"Name it."

"Seven fragments fill seven holes. But the big one — the name itself, the true name, the one every song is missing — I don't have it. I have edges of it. Echoes. The shepherd's crying syllables are *part* of it, I'm sure, but part isn't whole, and a half-name sung into a hungry rest—" I shook my head. "I think it either heals or it tears."

"Then we need the whole," Maera said.

"Where? Everything that remembers it is a hundred years dead, or a hundred years gone, or—"

"Or," said Ilse, in the voice of a woman about to commit her second heresy and enjoy it more than the first, "it's locked in the one place the elders guard harder than the prophecy books. Because the king's bargain — the one that erased us — is written down. And bargains have terms. And terms have names."

She looked at me. They all looked at me.

"You're the thief," Ilse said. "What do you know about stealing from a chapel?"

I looked at my tin, sitting open on the table among people who knew what was in it, and discovered that Rule One had already been broken beyond repair.

"Everything," I said. "That's the problem."
