## 5. What the Valley Forgets

The morning the baker forgot his own bread, I should have known the valley had begun to eat itself.

I mean that literally, or as literally as anything gets in Vellamoor. Old Sennit has baked in the same ovens for forty years—everyone knows this, including Sennit, who is proud of it in the way only a man with flour permanently worked into his knuckles can be proud of anything. But when I passed his shop on my way down from the gate, he was standing in the doorway holding a tray of loaves and looking at them like a stranger's laundry.

"Whose are these?" he asked me.

"Yours," I said.

He frowned at the loaves. The loaves, to their credit, looked fine. "I don't bake," he said, almost apologetically, and set them on the windowsill for whoever they might belong to.

By noon the windowsill was empty. By evening Sennit had swept his ovens clean and opened a shop selling, of all things, buttons, and seemed perfectly content about it, and no one in Vellamoor thought this strange at all, because that is the cruelest trick the forgetting plays: it doesn't feel like loss. It feels like Tuesday.

* * *

I went back up to the gate because the gate is where I go when the world misbehaves. It's a habit older than my understanding of it—I've been tending that door since I was small enough to fit inside the keeper's alcove without stooping, oiling hinges that never creak, sweeping a threshold no one crosses. A hundred years of nothing coming through, and still I keep it ready. Maera says that's what keepers are for: not the opening, but the readiness. She said it with a look I didn't understand at the time. I understand it better now.

The thirteenth moon hung over the western ridge, three nights from full, and it was wrong. I don't know how else to put it. Moons are supposed to be indifferent; this one watched. Its light came through the gate's iron lattice and lay across the threshold in long pale bars, and where the bars fell, the stone looked *hungry*—grain by grain, so slowly you'd swear you imagined it, the threshold was getting thinner.

I put my hand flat on the door, the way I do every night, and felt the hum again—that low note under the wood, like a held breath, like something on the other side pressing its ear to the door and waiting.

"You'll have to speak up," I told it. "I don't take messages."

The hum deepened. And then, for the first time in my life, the gate answered me in words—not words exactly, more like memory wearing words' clothing—and what it showed me was a street.

Not this street. A street I'd never seen, cobbled, loud, full of people whose faces I couldn't hold onto no matter how hard I looked, and above them a sign with letters that squirmed away from my eyes like fish. It lasted two heartbeats and then it was gone, and I was standing in my alcove with my hand on cold iron and my heart going like a drum in a parade.

That night I dreamed of the street again. In the dream I knew its name. I woke reaching for it and found nothing there, just the shape of where a name should be, smooth and empty as a pulled tooth.

* * *

It got worse the way floods get worse: gradually, then all at once.

The miller's lane shortened by half overnight. Not collapsed—*shortened*, as though the valley had decided it never needed all that road and quietly tidied it away. People walked from the mill to the chapel in forty steps instead of eighty and remarked on how convenient it was. Old Widow Cress stopped existing on Thursdays. Just Thursdays. The rest of the week she was there, sharp-tongued and alive, but every Thursday the village carried on around an absence shaped like her, and she never noticed, because on Friday she'd remember nothing missing.

And the faces. That was the part I couldn't shake. Faces started going soft at the edges, like wet paper. I'd greet someone I'd known my whole life and have to work for their name, hauling it up out of myself like a bucket from a deep well, and some days the well came up dry and I'd smile and say "friend" and hope it covered me.

Worst of all—the thing I haven't told anyone until now, the thing I'm telling you because soon there may be no one left to tell—it was happening to me.

My childhood is going. I noticed it first with the pear tree behind the keeper's cottage. I know I climbed it. There's a scar on my knee I've had since I was seven, and the scar is real, I can touch it, but the falling—out of the tree, the branch snapping, Maera binding the knee and scolding me between laughs—all of it has gone soft and gray, like a painting left out in rain. I remember *that I remembered it*. That's all. I remember the shape of the remembering.

Other things followed. My tenth winter: gone. The name of the woman who taught me letters: gone, though my handwriting still carries her slant. I sat up two nights ago making a list of everything I could still be sure of, and the list was short enough to memorize, which I did, which may have been the point:

The gate. The ritual. Maera's laugh. The smell of the archive. Tobin's terrible whistling. Ilse's hands, always moving. And one more thing, one thing I've been carrying cupped in both hands like an ember ever since I understood what the valley was doing:

Someone singing to me at the gate. Long ago. Before I could have memories, almost—a voice, low and warm, singing something I can't name in a language I shouldn't know, while I was very small and the gate was above us both like a sky. I don't know who sang. I don't know why I was there. But it is the oldest thing in me, older than Vellamoor, older than my duty, and every time the forgetting comes for another piece of my childhood, I press that ember harder and say *not this one*. 

*Not this one.*

* * *

On the second-to-last night before the full moon, I went down to the archive beneath the chapel, because if anyone knew what was happening to me, it would be Maera, and if Maera didn't know, the prophecy book might.

She was already there. Of course she was. She had the book open on her knees and a lamp turned low, and she looked up at me with an expression I'd never seen on her face before, and I have seen Maera's face do most things.

"You're losing them," she said. Not a question.

"Some."

"The keeper loses them first." She closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "Astrid. Sit down."

I sat. The archive smelled of dust and lamp oil and, underneath, faintly, of the pear tree I can no longer properly remember, and I hated the valley a little bit right then, which felt like heresy and also like honesty.

"The prophecy names tonight's moonrise and tomorrow's," Maera said. "The thirteenth turn. When the last moon goes full, whatever hasn't been sung back will be gone—not hidden, not sleeping. Gone. And the keeper—" she paused, and I wished she wouldn't, because pauses from Maera are load-bearing, "—the keeper must pass through before the final turn. Or stay, and be forgotten with everything else. The book is specific about it. Cruelly specific."

"I'm not leaving," I said, too fast, the way you say things you haven't decided yet. "This is my valley. These are my people. My whole life is here."

"Your whole life," Maera said softly, "is here. Yes. I know." She reached across and took my hand, and her grip was stronger than an old woman's has any right to be. "Astrid, listen to me. The valley isn't eating itself out of malice. It's starving. It was cut off from the world a century ago, and a country needs the world the way a fire needs air. We've been living on stored breath. And now—" she tapped the closed book, "—now the store is spent, and the valley is eating its own memories to stay standing, and it will start with the one thing it doesn't own."

"Which is?"

"You." She said it plainly, no drama in it, which somehow made it worse. "You were never Vellamoor's, child. You're the outside's. Left at the gate. The valley kept you because the gate did, but the valley doesn't know what you are, and when it gets hungry enough, it will forget you the way it forgot the miller's lane. Completely. Without malice. Without even noticing."

I laughed. I'm not proud of it; it came out cracked and wrong. "Left at the gate? Maera, I've tended that gate my whole life. Who leaves a baby at a locked door?"

"Someone who wanted it found," she said. "Or someone who wanted it kept safe. Or—" and here her voice did something careful, "—someone who meant to come back, and couldn't."

The ember in my hands flared. The voice, the song, the gate like a sky. Someone singing to me. Someone who—

I stood up too quickly and knocked the lamp, and the shadows swung around the archive like startled birds.

"The choir," I said. "Tomorrow night. Before the moon turns. We sing, we open the door, we—"

"We need the name," Maera said. "The true name of Vellamoor, torn out of every song a century ago. Without it, the gate opens onto nothing. With it—" she smiled, and it was a fierce, tired, hopeful thing, "—with it, we open onto everything."

"Tobin," I said. "Tobin steals sounds. If anyone's been hoarding pieces of a forbidden name—"

Maera's eyebrows went up, and I realized I'd just proposed trusting our hopes to a thief, and found, to my surprise, that I meant it completely.

Outside, through the archive's high slit of a window, the thirteenth moon was climbing, fat and pale and patient, and I could feel it watching me the way the gate watches, the way the whole valley now watches—hungry, and waiting to see whether I'll be remembered or eaten.

One night left. One song. One name, scattered in a thief's pockets and a choir's broken verses and, somewhere, maybe, in the oldest room of me—the voice at the gate, singing.

Hold on, I told the ember. Hold on. Tomorrow we find out who sang you.
