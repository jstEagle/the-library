## 6. The Moon-Priest's Heresy

I was defrocked for telling the truth at a funeral, which I have always considered an unfair place to be punished for honesty. Funerals are where truth goes to be buried anyway; I merely said it a little louder than the shovel.

The funeral was old Bramwen's, the bell-founder, and the elders wanted him sent off with the standard litany: *he lived, he kept the valley, he is gathered into Vellamoor's keeping.* But there was no name in that litany. There never is. *Vellamoor* — our valley's own sweet name — appears in exactly one prayer, and even there the word has been worn smooth as a river stone, rubbed of all its edges by a century of careful mouths. So I stood up over Bramwen's coffin and asked the question no priest is permitted to ask: *kept from what? Gathered into whose keeping? What is the name we are not saying?*

They took my stole, my keys to the moon-shrine, and my seat on the council. They let me keep my voice, which was either mercy or a catastrophic error of judgment, depending on how tonight goes.

Tonight, the choir meets in the tannery loft, because the tannery smells so powerfully of itself that no elder will come within a hundred paces of it. Maera arranged this. Maera arranges everything now, with the brisk efficiency of a woman who has decided she is too old to die politely.

* * *

I arrive early, because disgraced priests are always early — we have nowhere else to be, and a lifetime of vesting before dawn does not unlearn itself. The loft is low-beamed and amber-lit, hung with stretching frames that creak like ship rigging. Tobin is already there, cross-legged on a hide rack, turning something over in his fingers. A sound, I'd wager. He carries them like coins. The boy steals noises the way other thieves steal silverware — a hinge's squeak, a stranger's laugh, half a chord from a wedding forty years gone — and he keeps them somewhere I have stopped trying to understand.

"You're the heretic," he says, without looking up.

"I'm the priest," I say. "The heresy is extra."

He grins. Astrid Pemberton arrives next, gatekeeper's coat still dusted with road, looking like a man who has been handed a prophecy and hasn't yet found a shelf to put it on. Then the others filter in — Sela with her cracked singing voice, the Fenwick twins who can only harmonize when they're angry at each other, old Piotr who remembers every funeral hymn and nothing else anymore. The ragged choir of Vellamoor. We look, collectively, like a basket of misfit tools. Which is precisely what we are.

Maera stands beneath the great beam and does not open with pleasantries. She never does.

"Tonight you learn why the songs have holes in them," she says. "And I warn you: some of you will wish you hadn't come."

Sela laughs nervously. Nobody joins her.

"Sit," Maera says. "Ilse. You were there. Tell them what the shrine wouldn't keep."

I take a breath. One hundred years of silence, and they want me to spend it in a tannery loft. Very well. Truth has always had poor taste in venues.

* * *

"Before the Forgetting," I begin, "Vellamoor had a king."

Piotr frowns. "We had a *council*."

"We have a council *now*. Before, we had a crown. It sits in the chapel crypt under a cloth nobody is allowed to lift, and if you ask the sexton about it he develops a sudden interest in floor drainage. That crown belonged to King Aldous Vane, last of his line, and he did something a hundred years ago that every elder since has broken their back to bury."

I let the quiet stretch. In the shrine, I learned that silence is a door — people walk through it on their own.

"There was a war coming. Not our war — an outside war, one of those great grinding continental ones that eat countries like ours as appetizers. Aldous Vane looked at the maps and saw Vellamoor marked for burning. And so he went looking for a way out, and he found one. There are powers beyond the mountains that deal in names. Not land. Not gold. *Names.* A country's true name is its anchor in the world — say it, and the world knows where you are. Take it away, and the world... loses its grip."

Tobin has gone very still. Even his stolen sounds seem quieter.

"Aldous traded us," I say. "Our true name — the whole country's, every hill and hearth and child — sold to that outside power in exchange for being overlooked. Erased from the maps. Unfindable. Unrememberable. The war passed us by because the war could not find us. And neither could anything else. Neither can *we*, from inside. That is why memories fade here like breath on glass. That is why your grandmother's face slips from you. That is why the founding songs have holes torn in them where the name should be — the elders cut them out themselves, generation after generation, snipping away every thread that might lead back to what we gave away. They call it protection." My voice cracks on the word, and I let it. "It is amputation. Performed annually, by consent, on a sleeping patient."

Sela's hand is at her mouth. One of the Fenwick twins says, quietly, "My mother used to hum a name-tune. She stopped when the elders visited. I thought she'd forgotten it."

"She didn't forget," I say. "She was *corrected*."

Astrid speaks for the first time. His voice is careful, gatekeeper-careful, the voice of a man checking a lock. "And the thirteenth moon? The book said tonight is the turn. 'When the keeper must choose.'"

"The moons are the bargain's clock," I tell him. "Twelve turns a year, twelve times the outside power comes to collect its interest — a little more forgetting, a little more fading, a little more of Vellamoor slipping through the world's fingers. Twelve payments, and the debt renews. But the thirteenth turn — the turn that isn't supposed to exist, the moon the bargain doesn't account for — that is the flaw in the contract. The night the ledger can be rewritten. The prophecy book wasn't heresy, Astrid. It was *accounting*."

"And the keeper?" he asks. And I hate, briefly, that I must answer him honestly, because I watched his face when Maera read his name out of those margins, and I know what it cost him to be scripted before he was born.

"The keeper is whoever stands at the door when the thirteenth moon rises," I say. "That has always been you. Whether you knew it or not — whether any of us knew it or not — the gate has been waiting a hundred years for a keeper who could choose."

"Choose what?"

"To sing the name back," Maera says, from the beam-shadow. "Or to lock the door and let the valley finish forgetting. Those are the only two doors left, child. Everything else closed a century ago."

* * *

The loft erupts, as lofts do. The Fenwick twins begin arguing immediately — one insisting the elders must have known, the other insisting the elders *are* the bargain, which escalates with impressive speed into a shoving match conducted entirely in harmony. Sela is crying without noise, which is a skill the valley teaches you whether you ask for it or not. Piotr keeps murmuring fragments of funeral hymns, hunting through them for the hole, finding it, murmuring again.

I find Astrid standing apart, at the loft's small window, looking down at the village where the twelfth-moon lanterns are still being taken in.

"They'll have told you obedience is the valley's virtue," I say, coming to stand beside him.

"They've told me a great many things," he says. "Most of them instructions."

"And do you follow them?"

"I've followed them for thirty years." He turns the words over like stones. "Lock the gate. Tend the lamp. Ask nothing. I thought it was duty. Now I'm told it was a cage with a schedule."

"It was both," I say. "Most cages are. The bars feel like bones if you wear them long enough." I look at him — this man the margins named before he was born, this keeper who tends a door that keeps nothing. "Astrid. The elders will come for the book. They will come for Maera. They will come for that boy's hoard of stolen sounds, and for me most of all, because I have been saying the unsayable since Bramwen's funeral and they are tired of hearing it. When they come, you will be asked to choose. Obedience or the choir. The lock or the song. And I need you to understand — truly understand — that there is no neutral ground. Silence *is* the bargain. Every year we don't sing, we pay."

He is quiet a long moment. Below, a villager blows out a lantern, and a little more of the dark owns the street.

"When I was small," he says finally, "someone sang to me at the gate. I've never known who. It's the only memory I have that doesn't fade. Everything else goes soft and slips, but that one stays. Like it's pinned to something." He looks at me. "Is that the name? Is that what I'm holding?"

"I think," I say slowly, "that you may be holding more than the name. But that is Maera's revelation to give, not mine. I only do heresies."

He almost smiles. It is the first time I have seen his face do anything but dutiful.

Then the ladder creaks, and the loft goes silent as a held breath — because boots are climbing, several pairs, and the lantern-light that swings up through the floorboards is the hard official kind, the kind the elders carry when they intend to leave with something.

Elder Corvess rises into the loft first, gray-robed, flanked by two shrine-wardens, and his eyes go straight past all of us to Maera.

"The prophecy book," he says. "And everything else that was ever written in that archivist's hand. The council has voted. The thirteenth turn will not be sung. Vellamoor endures by *forgetting*, and it will go on enduring."

"Enduring," Maera repeats, tasting it. "Is that what we call it now. Fifty years ago we called it *shrinking*."

Corvess ignores her. His gaze sweeps the loft — Sela, the twins, Piotr, Tobin, me — cataloguing, sentencing. It lands last on Astrid, and softens into something almost gentle, which from Elder Corvess is the most frightening thing in the room.

"Keeper," he says. "You especially. The gate is yours to tend. Whatever these people tell you tonight — remember who fed you, who housed you, who made you keeper of the only door we have. Remember what you owe."

And Astrid Pemberton, thirty years obedient, opens his mouth — and I cannot tell, watching him, whether he is about to swear fealty or commit heresy, and neither, I suspect, can he.
