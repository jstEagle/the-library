## 7. Thirteen Names for a Door

We set out for the gate at dusk, which felt appropriate. If you're going to walk toward the end of the world as you know it, you may as well do it in good light—amber and bruise-purple, the thirteenth moon already a pale coin above the eastern ridge, fat with intent.

There were thirteen of us. Maera had counted twice, and when she announced the number, Ilse laughed in a way that made three people step back from her.

"Thirteen names," she said. "One for each turning. The old priests weren't subtle."

"The old priests were *thorough*," Maera corrected. "Subtlety is for people who expect to be forgiven."

I walked at the back, because that was my place in any procession and I had long since stopped minding. Ahead of me: Maera with her archive-dust shawl, Tobin jingling faintly with stolen sounds—I could hear a kettle-whistle and half a wedding bell in his coat pockets—and Ilse in her defrocked grey, walking like a woman who intended to argue with the moon personally.

And between us, the choir. The outcasts. The singers nobody had wanted: a fishwife who harmonized with her own echo, two brothers who'd been exiled from the harvest choir for improvising, a girl of maybe fifteen who could hold a note so long the village children called her the Well. They sang as we walked. Not the founding songs—we'd learned what those were worth, all holes and absences—but fragments. Tobin's hoard, doled out one scrap at a time like a miser paying a debt he'd secretly been dying to pay.

A syllable here. A vowel there. *Vel-* without its ending. *-lamoor* without its beginning. A sound like a river deciding to be a word.

The valley listened.

I don't mean the people. I mean the *valley*. As we passed the millrace, the water stuttered in its channel, keeping time. The hedges along the low road leaned after us like latecomers to a service. And once—only once—a street we crossed unmade itself behind us, cobblestones dissolving into soft grey nothing, and I understood that Vellamoor was not merely forgetting. It was being forgotten, and there is a difference, the way there is a difference between closing your eyes and having them closed for you.

"Keep singing," Maera said quietly, though no one had stopped. "The valley remembers through you or not at all."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hearing your country's name sung back together, piece by piece, over four miles of road: it's like watching someone reassemble a face you've loved from scattered photographs. You know it's right before it's finished. Your chest knows first.

By the time we crested the last hill and saw the gate below—the gate, my gate, my whole small kingdom of hinges and duty—the fragments had become something almost whole. Not the full name. Tobin shook his head at every request; whatever he was still holding back, he was holding it like a man holding the last plank of a sinking ship. But the shape of it. The weight of it. Enough that the air itself seemed to lean toward the door.

The gate stood where it had always stood, which is to say it stood nowhere, because a door to a country that isn't on any map is a difficult thing to locate even when you've tended it every night for eleven years. It rose out of the hillside like a tooth out of a gum: black oak banded in iron gone green, carved with thirteen moons in a ring, twelve of them worn smooth by weather and the thirteenth sharp as the day some long-dead carpenter cut it.

I had locked it ten thousand times. I had never once heard it make a sound.

It hummed.

Low, continuous, a note held under the earth like a singer breathing before the verse. The hair on my arms stood up. My hand, traitorously, rose toward the wood, and Maera caught my wrist—not hard, but absolutely.

"Not yet," she said. "You don't knock on a door until you know what's knocking back."

* * *

We arranged ourselves in the crescent before the gate, the way Ilse said the old moon-priests had arranged their congregations, backs to the valley, faces to the wood. The thirteenth moon cleared the ridge behind us and threw our shadows forward onto the door—thirteen shadows, long and thin, like notes written on dark paper.

Then we sang.

I should describe it properly, because I will want to remember this correctly later, and later may be a very strange country indeed. We sang the fragments in order, oldest first, the way Maera had reconstructed them from margins and memory and Tobin's criminal pockets. The fishwife began, her voice rough as net-hauling. The Well girl took the second line and held it past all decency. The brothers braided their parts together the way they'd been exiled for doing. Ilse sang like a woman throwing stones through windows. Maera sang like an index. Tobin sang like a man spending everything he'd ever stolen in a single afternoon.

And I sang too. Badly. I have the voice of a man apologizing. But the door didn't care about quality. The door cared about *names*.

With each fragment, the humming deepened, and the carvings woke. The twelve worn moons filled with a light like water held up to a lamp. And then the visions came.

They poured off the door the way heat comes off a forge—you didn't see them so much as stand inside them.

I saw Vellamoor whole. That's the only way to say it. I saw the capital that isn't on any map because there is no map: white towers with copper roofs gone green, a river running backward through the middle of town because the founders had asked it to and rivers then still took requests. I saw markets where they sold bottled birdsong and secondhand dreams, honestly priced. I saw a coronation, once, centuries back—a young king on a hill of ordinary people, and the crown passed hand to hand up the hillside because, Ilse had told us, the first kings of Vellamoor believed a crown should be carried by everyone before it settled on anyone.

I saw the war. Smoke on the southern horizon like a bruise spreading. I saw the king—older now, afraid now—signing something in a room with no windows, trading a name for a border, and I watched the ink dry and the towers dim and the river forget which way it ran.

I saw the erasure. Maps blanking. Letters arriving undeliverable. Grandmothers mid-sentence, losing the word for *grandchild*.

"Don't look away," Ilse said, and her voice cracked on it, the righteous anger finally showing the grief underneath. "This is the shame. Look at all of it. That's what remembering *is*."

I looked. I looked at all of it.

And then the door showed me the gate.

* * *

Not this gate. This gate, but younger—or rather, this gate and a night I did not remember, could not have remembered, except that now I did, fully, horribly, wonderfully, all at once.

Rain. A woman standing where I had stood ten thousand times, soaked through, holding a bundle against her chest. She was not Vellamoori—I knew it the way you know a stranger's accent. Her coat was wrong. Her buttons were wrong. Everything about her was *outside*, and she had come through the open door into a country that no longer existed, carrying—

She knelt. She set the bundle down on the threshold stone, inside the gate, on Vellamoor's side, and the bundle cried, and the cry was a baby's cry, and the baby was—

"I'm sorry," the woman said, to the baby, to the valley, to the door, to no one. "I can't cross back with him. It won't let me carry him both ways. He has to choose when he's grown. He has to—" and her voice broke, and she started to sing.

A lullaby. In a language I shouldn't have understood and understood completely, the way you understand your own heartbeat. A song about a door that opens for the one who carries it. About a child left on a threshold who becomes the threshold. About coming home by becoming the way home.

She kissed the baby's forehead. She stood. She walked backward through the door, eyes fixed on the bundle the entire way, singing until the last syllable, and the door closed, and the twelfth moon turned, and the valley forgot her by morning.

Everyone forgot her by morning.

Except the baby grew up and became the keeper, and kept the door shut every night for eleven years, and never once wondered why the ritual felt like tending a grave.

* * *

The vision released me and I was on my knees on the hillside grass, and someone was saying my name—Maera, probably, or Tobin—and the choir had stopped singing, and the thirteenth moon sat on the ridge like a witness.

"That's not possible," I said. My voice sounded borrowed. "I'm from here. I've always been from here. I'm the keeper. Keepers are born in—"

"In Vellamoor." Maera crouched beside me, and her old eyes were wet and fierce. "Say the rest of it, boy. Say what you just saw."

"She left me." The words came out flat, factual, unbearable. "Someone from outside. She couldn't carry me back through, so she—she left me *inside*, and the valley took me in, and it gave me a life, and it let me forget, because that's what Vellamoor does, it keeps things by forgetting them, and I was the thing it kept best, because—"

"Because you're not a memory of the valley," Ilse said. She had come around to face me, and for the first time since I'd known her, the disgraced priest looked almost gentle. Almost. "You're the opposite. You're the outside, made flesh, standing on the inside. You're the hinge, Astrid. The door doesn't open for a key. The door opens for *you*. You are the key. You've been sleeping on the ring of it your whole life."

The humming under the ground swelled, and the thirteenth carving on the gate lit last of all, brightest of all, and I felt it in my teeth, in my sternum, in the place where the lullaby lived now, permanently, the way scars live.

Tobin shuffled forward. He looked, for once, like a thief who had run out of pockets. He pulled something from inside his coat—not a bottle, not a bell, but a strip of cloth, embroidered, ancient, with the final syllable of the true name worked into it in thread gone gold with age.

"The last piece," he said. "Stole it off the king's own burial banner, years ago, when I still thought I stole for me." He held it out. His hand was shaking. "It was never mine. None of it was. It's yours, keeper. It's the valley's. Sing it through."

I took the cloth. The syllable hummed against my palm like a trapped moth.

Behind us, down in the valley, lights were moving. Lanterns, many lanterns, coming up the hill road in a slow deliberate line, and at their head, unmistakable even at this distance, the tall silhouettes of the elders, and the elders did not walk like people coming to sing.

Maera stood, and squared her shawl, and looked at the lanterns, and then at me, and smiled the way archives smile—patiently, knowing how every story ends.

"Well," she said. "They've come to stop us. So I suggest, dear boy, that you learn the rest of your lullaby *quickly*."
