## 8. The Elders' Silence

I have been old long enough to know that silence is never empty. It has a weight, a grain, a temperature. The silence of the chapel council chamber, when the six elders filed in and found us still sitting there with the prophecy book open between us, was the coldest I had ever felt in Vellamoor—and I once wintered through a season when the frost sang in the rafters.

They came for the book before they even greeted us.

Elder Corvane reached across the table with those long, careful hands of his—the hands of a man who had spent forty years counting tithes and could take a coin from your palm without your feeling it go—and closed the prophecy book on Maera's finger.

She did not flinch. She never flinches; it's one of the things I both admire and resent about her. She simply looked at him and said, "You'll want to be gentler than that. It's the only honest thing left in this valley."

"The book is heresy," Corvane said, "and now it is also confiscated."

"It was heresy yesterday too," said Tobin from the corner, where he sat turning one of his stolen sounds over in his cupped hands like a beetle he'd caught. "Funny how it only became *confiscated* once people started listening."

"Tobin." Maera's voice had that edge she saves for the choir when we're about to ruin a phrase by rushing it. He subsided, but not all the way. Tobin doesn't subside all the way into anything; it's why he's a thief and why, I suspect, he'll die either rich or hanged, possibly both.

There were six of them: Corvane, who spoke for the tithe-houses; Elder Sennet, who spoke for the fields and had a face like weathered fence-post; the twins Osla and Osric, who spoke as one because neither had ever had an original thought alone; Elder Bramwen, oldest of them, who had buried two husbands and a great deal of her own curiosity; and at the end, Elder Pell, young, nervous, promoted last spring, who kept looking at Astrid as if Astrid were a lit fuse.

And Astrid—there he was, our keeper, our gate-tender, our boy-shaped key, sitting very straight with his hands flat on the table as though the wood might bolt if he let go. He had not spoken since we'd been summoned. I watched him watch them, and I thought: *he looks like a man memorizing a room.* Which, given what the thirteenth moon was doing to the streets outside, was perhaps exactly what he was doing.

"You understand," Corvane began, settling into the chair at the head of the table as though it had always been his, "what you propose. You propose to sing open a door that has been shut for a hundred years. A door to the world that erased us. The world that—" he paused, savoring it, "—*forgot* us."

"They didn't forget us," Maera said quietly. "We were taken out of their memory. There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and the name is in this book."

"The outside will not come through that door bearing gifts," Sennet rumbled. "It will come through bearing soldiers, or merchants, or worse—*questions*. And what remains of Vellamoor cannot survive questions."

"What remains of Vellamoor," Maera said, "is a street that unmade itself on Tuesday. Is the miller's daughter, who looked at her own mother yesterday and asked who the stranger was. Is—" her voice caught, just slightly, and I saw her decide not to hide it, "—is my own sister, who no longer remembers there ever was a sister. The valley is eating itself, Elders. Your silence is not protecting anything. It is only making the meal slower."

Bramwen spoke then, for the first time, in a voice dry as seed husks. "Maera Ravensworth. You were a singer of the old choir when the choir was banned. You have spent fifty years humming treason under your breath. We tolerated it because humming harms no one." Her eyes went to the book under Corvane's hand. "This is no longer humming."

"No," Maera agreed. "Now it's singing."

* * *

They took the book at midday, formally, with a writ read aloud in the square so that everyone could hear the word *forbidden* attached to the final singing. Forbidden gatherings. Forbidden songs. Forbidden names—which is to say, the true name of Vellamoor itself, though none of them dared say what it was, which told you everything: they didn't know it either. They were guarding a door whose key they had swallowed generations ago and could no longer taste.

The village obeyed the way villages do—with a great show of obedience and a great deal of sideways looking. Doors closed early. Shutters angled. And yet I counted, walking home past the well, no fewer than eleven people humming fragments of the founding songs under their breath, badly, guiltily, like children licking stolen sugar. The songs were already loose. You cannot confiscate a melody once it has gotten into someone's back teeth.

But the elders were not fools, whatever else they were. They knew it too. So they did what frightened institutions always do: they went after the smallest, strangest, most deniable member of us.

They arrested Tobin at dusk.

I was there. I want that recorded somewhere, even if the somewhere is only my own stubborn head: I was there, at the corner of Thimble Lane, when four of the tithe-guard took him. He didn't run, which surprised me—he had running in him, quicksilver feet, a whole career of it. Instead he stood in the middle of the lane with his arms slightly away from his body, the way you do when you're showing you hold nothing, and he said, "Careful with the pockets. Some of what I'm carrying bites."

"Empty them," said the guard-captain.

And Tobin—foolish, brave, infuriating Tobin—opened his coat and let them see. All of it. The hoard of a lifetime: scraps of sound wrapped in waxed paper and knotted with string. A syllable of the valley's name bought off a dying woman in the fens. Half a note from a wedding song nobody living remembered. The creak of the old king's chair, traded to him by a madman for three eggs. And threaded through all of it, glowing faintly the way stolen things do when they want to go home, the fragments of the missing name.

The guard-captain went pale. Not at the magic—at the *audacity*. "Do you know what these are?"

"Yeah," Tobin said cheerfully. "That's rather the point of me."

They bound his hands in wool so he couldn't hum—that's a real thing, wool-wrapping, an old tithe-law trick, and watching it done to him made something in my chest tear along a seam I hadn't known was there. As they marched him past me he turned his head and grinned, that crooked grin, and said, "Tell Maera the high part's in my left boot. She'll know what to do with it."

Then he was gone down the lane between the guards, and the lamplight took him, and I stood in the cold learning exactly how heavy a found family weighs when it starts to break apart. It weighs more than a family you were born to. Nobody warns you of that. A family you make chooses you back, and so every piece of it that gets torn away takes some of your own choosing with it.

* * *

They scattered us next, though "scattered" is generous—it was more like shooing pigeons. Orders posted: Maera confined to her house. Ilse the moon-priest barred from the chapel steps. The choir forbidden to gather, sing, or "propagate memory," which is such an elder-ish phrase that I nearly laughed when I read it, and then didn't laugh at all, because Astrid read it over my shoulder and went white as the twelfth moon.

"They think this ends it," he said. His voice had changed in the last days—steadier, lower, like a bell that had learned its own weight. "Don't they understand? Tonight is the turn. Forbidding the song doesn't stop the moon."

"They don't forbid it because they think it will work," I said. "They forbid it because they're afraid it will."

He looked at me then, and I saw the thing in him that had been growing since the gate first hummed under his hand: not fear exactly, though there was plenty of that, but *readiness*. A man standing at the edge of a decision he has already half-made.

"Where are they keeping Tobin?" he asked.

"The tithe-house cellar. Wool-bound. Two guards."

"And the boot?"

I almost smiled. "I checked. Empty. But I heard him whistling as they took him away—three notes, over and over. He wasn't giving up the high part, Astrid. He was teaching it to the street."

Astrid closed his eyes, and I swear I could see him listening—not to me, to the valley, to whatever thin thread still connected him to the gate and the moon and the woman I had begun to suspect he was about to become. When he opened them again, he said the words that ended my confinement and my obedience in the same breath:

"Maera. Get the choir. Whoever's left. The back way to the gate, at moonrise. I'm going to get Tobin."

"You can't—"

"I'm the keeper," he said simply. "Opening doors is rather the point of me."

* * *

So here is what defiance looks like when you are seventy-one years old and your knees have opinions: it looks like slipping out your own back window while your appointed watcher dozes over his tea, and crossing wet gardens in the dark, and knocking softly on doors where frightened people live, and saying the only sermon I have left in me:

*"Moonrise. The gate. Bring your voice."*

Some doors stayed shut. I won't pretend otherwise—I saw the fear behind the cracks, and I understood it, and I grieved it. But other doors opened. Ilse came with her broken priest's collar hidden under a scarf, carrying the heresy she'd carried all her life like a coal she refused to let cool. Old Fen came, who hadn't sung since her husband forgot her face. The twins' younger cousin came, which will cause a family scandal lasting decades, and good. One by one, in twos and threes, avoiding the lanterns, the ragged remnant of the choir of unremembered things crept eastward through the darkening valley toward the gate that keeps nothing—or kept nothing, until a keeper touched it and it woke.

Behind us, the tithe-house cellar held a thief with wool-bound hands and a head full of our country's name. Above us, the thirteenth moon climbed, fat and wrong-colored, pulling the streets loose at their seams. Ahead of us, the gate waited in its hollow of hills, humming faintly, like a throat clearing before a very long song.

And beside me, falling into step out of the shadows where he'd been waiting—because of course he'd been waiting, because Astrid Pemberton had never once in his life been late for a duty—walked our keeper, our key, our boy from nowhere, with the lullaby already moving under his breath like water under ice.

"They took everything," I said to him. "The book, the boy, the square, the law."

"No," Astrid said. "They took the paper." He nodded ahead, toward the hills, toward the door, toward the last night of the country that had raised him without claiming him. "The song was never in the book."

The moon cleared the ridge.

We walked faster.
