## 9. The Thirteenth Turning

They took Tobin at dusk, and they took the book, and they took every voice in Vellamoor that might have answered mine — and then the elders went home to their suppers, satisfied that a country could be saved by confiscation.

I stood in Maera's kitchen while she wrapped a heel of bread and a stub of candle in cloth with hands that shook only a little. Ilse had gone to ground somewhere in the chapel undercroft. The choir was scattered to the four winds, which in Vellamoor is a short walk in any direction.

"You don't have to do this," Maera said. "That's a lie. You have to do this. But you should know you don't."

"That's very comforting."

"I'm an archivist, not a nursemaid." She pressed the bundle into my hands and held on a moment longer than the bread required. "Astrid Pemberton. Gatekeeper. Foundling. Door." She said each word like she was shelving it. "Whatever happens up there tonight, those words are true, and true things are heavier than people think. Carry them anyway."

The moon was already climbing when I left her house. Not full — not yet. It hung low over the western ridge like a coin someone had half-sunk into lead, and as I walked the streets toward the gate, I watched the valley eat itself one more time.

It had gotten worse since the elders' silence. Cobbles lifted out of the road ahead of me and settled behind me like water closing over a stone. A woman I'd known all my life — Brenna, who sold apples by the well — looked straight through me and then through herself, her face going smooth as an egg for three heartbeats before she remembered how to be a person. Children's chalk drawings faded off doorsteps before the chalk finished moving. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing, because the nothing was coming for its name too.

And my own head was no better. I reached for my mother — the woman from the vision, singing at the gate — and found her slipping, her melody intact but her face going soft as wet paper. I reached for my first winter in the keeper's cottage and got a smell of woodsmoke and nothing else. The valley was forgetting me in the order I'd learned it, newest first, peeling me back like an onion toward some small hard center.

At the center, I suspected, there was a lullaby.

* * *

The gate stood where it had always stood, which these days felt like a miracle rather than a fact. Iron and elder-wood, older than any record, humming faintly under my palm the way it had hummed that first night — the crack in the world that had started all of this, or ended it, depending on which verse you believed.

I set down Maera's bundle. I lit the candle stub. And I did what I have done every night for twenty years: I performed the locking ritual.

My hands knew it the way hands know anything — without me. Thumb to the left hinge, palm flat on the iron, the old words said backward so they close instead of open. *Vellamoor keeps. Vellamoor keeps. Let nothing remember us, and we will be safe.* Twenty years of saying it, and tonight, for the first time, I heard what it actually was.

A lullaby sung to a country by its jailer.

"No," I told the gate. "Not tonight."

The moon cleared the ridge.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because the songs will get it wrong — songs always do; they sand the terror off things until terror looks like courage. What actually happened was this: I stood alone in front of a door that hadn't opened in a hundred years, on a hilltop above a country that was forgetting itself street by street, holding one complete verse of one song, and I was afraid in a way that has no proper word. Not fear of dying. Fear of being *unhad*. Of the world closing over me like water over a stone, and no one — not even the valley itself — left to miss me.

Tobin would have stolen the sound of my shaking hands and sold it back to me later with a wink. Maera would have said something dry and true. Ilse would have prayed loudly and incorrectly. None of them were there. The elders had seen to that.

So it was just me and the verse.

It isn't much, as verses go. Four lines. The kind of thing a woman sings to a foundling child at a gate because she doesn't know his name and neither does he, and a song has to stand in where a name should go. I'd carried it my whole life without knowing I was carrying it — the way you carry a scar, or a key.

I sang it.

My voice cracked on the second line. I'm telling you this because it matters: the gate did not swing open at the sound of my beautiful singing, because I don't have beautiful singing. I have a keeper's voice, rough from twenty years of talking to iron, and it wavered, and I sang the verse again anyway, louder, and the third time I stopped performing it and just gave it away — gave it to the valley below, to the dark windows, to the unmaking streets.

*Sleep, the door is watching,
sleep, the moon is turning,
whatever you were called
before the calling, sleep.*

And the valley heard.

I felt it happen before I saw it — a shiver running downhill through Vellamoor like wind through wheat, except the wheat was people. In the village square, Brenna the apple-seller lifted her head. Old Hollis the cooper dropped his mallet. In the chapel, I'd swear on Maera's whole archive, the elders themselves sat up from their supper with the look of men hearing their own names spoken in a room they thought was empty.

Because that's what the lullaby was. That's what it had always been. The woman at the gate — my mother, whoever she was, whatever she was — hadn't just been soothing a child. She'd woven the valley's names into the melody, all thirteen of them, the ones torn out of the founding songs, hidden in plain tune where no censor would think to cut. A hundred years the elders hunted verses with holes in them, and the whole time the missing names slept in a lullaby, waiting for someone to sing it back.

Waiting, I suppose, for the door to sing it. For the outside made flesh. For me.

Down in the square, a voice joined mine. Thin, wavering, off-key — Brenna, singing the second line a half-beat behind, and as she sang it her face came back to her like color returning to a cheek. Then Hollis. Then a cluster of voices near the well. Then, unmistakably, from the direction of the chapel undercroft, Ilse — bellowing the melody with the confidence of a woman who has never once let correct pitch stand between her and righteousness.

Then more. Dozens. The dark windows lighting one by one, doors opening, people spilling into streets that were remembering how to be streets beneath their feet. They didn't know the words — most of them had never heard them — but the names inside the melody knew *them*, and a name, once heard, will not stay quiet. Voice by voice by voice, Vellamoor rose and chose, out loud, in public, together, to be remembered.

I stood at the gate with my candle guttering and my throat raw and I sang the verse a fourth time, and this time I wasn't singing alone. I was singing inside a sound like weather. Like a country clearing its throat after a century of silence.

The gate began to glow.

Not violently. That's the thing I'll want the songs to keep — it didn't burst or thunder or split. It warmed. The iron went gold along its seams, and the elder-wood grain brightened like coals breathed upon, and the humming under my hand resolved into something almost like words, almost like a question.

*Who remembers?*

"Everyone," I said. My voice was wrecked. It didn't matter. Behind me, down the hill, a whole nation answered in harmony. "Everyone does. That's the point. That was always the point."

The lock — the ritual, the backward words, the hundred years of *let nothing remember us* — came undone the way frost comes off a window: all at once, quietly, everywhere.

The door opened inward.

And standing in the opening, framed against a darkness that smelled of rain and roads and everything Vellamoor had traded away, was a figure I knew from a single glimpse a week ago and a lifetime of not-knowing before that.

She was older. Her hair was grey where the vision had shown it brown. But she was singing the lullaby's last line as she stepped through, and her voice fit mine the way a key fits the lock it was cut for.

"There you are," my mother said. "I've been singing to you for thirty years. I was starting to think you'd learned to sleep through anything."

Behind her, past her shoulder, the outside waited — and it was not empty. It was full of faces, and every one of them was looking at me.

Every one of them knew my name.
