## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The Hollow woke Bram the way it always did: with nothing.

No birdsong. No wind in the pines above the terraces. Not even the creak of the old Guildhall settling on its stones, because even wood and mortar seemed to have agreed, a century ago, to hold their breath. The valley kept its silence the way other places kept their bells—carefully, reverently, as though silence itself were something that could be stolen if you weren't watching.

Bram lay in her narrow bed beneath the eaves of the east wing and counted, as she did every morning, the sounds that were not there. Seven. There were always seven absences, if she was honest about it: birds, wind, water over the millrace, the far-off lowing of cattle, voices from the village road, her own heartbeat when she pressed too hard against the quiet, and—one she never told anyone about—a seventh sound, faint and rhythmic, like a drum played underwater, that had lived at the edge of her hearing for as long as she could remember. She had decided long ago it was her imagination. In the Hollow, everything unusual eventually resolved into imagination, because there was nothing else for it to be.

She rose before the grey light finished arriving, dressed in the dark wool of mourning that she had been wearing, in one form or another, for eleven months, and went down through the cold corridors to do what she did every morning: keep a dead guild alive by pretending it wasn't.

* * *

The Guildhall of the Songwrights had once held three hundred voices. Bram knew this because the founding ledger said so, in ink gone brown as old blood, and because the great vaulted ceiling of the singing chamber still bore the scorch-fans of three hundred throats' worth of resonance, patterns burned into the stone like frost-ferns made of fire. Now the hall held one voice, and it was hers, and she almost never used it where the songbooks could hear.

There were forty-one songbooks left. Bram knew this number the way other people knew their own age. She carried them down from the archive two at a time, wrapped in linen against the damp, and set them on the long oak table in the reading room to air, turning each page with a bone folder so her fingers' oils wouldn't touch the vellum. The pages were dense with notation no living person could read—not the five-line staves of common music, but spirals, ladders, interlocking rings of marks that climbed the margins and sometimes off the page entirely, as though the songs had outgrown their containers and had to be folded in like wings.

They were beautiful. They were useless. And every morning Bram aired them, dusted them, checked them for mold and silverfish and the slow brown rot of vellum, and every morning they gave her nothing back.

The elder had called that work *tending*. Elder Marenne—no relation to any queen; the name was common enough in the low country—had spent sixty years tending, and had taught Bram to tend, and had died in the autumn just past with her hands folded on a closed songbook and her last word half-formed, a syllable that might have been *stay* or might have been *sing*, and Bram would spend the rest of her life not knowing which.

"Good morning," Bram said to the books, because the elder had always said it, and because saying it aloud proved the silence hadn't won yet. Her voice fell flat in the reading room, swallowed instantly, the way all sound was swallowed in the Hollow. Even grief came out muffled here. Even crying felt like doing it through a wall.

She opened the first book. *The Laying of Foundations*, the label read, in the old hand. She turned to the middle, where a spiral of notation coiled across both pages like a snail shell seen from above, and she did what she had done every morning for six years, since she was fourteen and the elder had finally allowed her to touch the books at all: she hummed the first three notes of the restoration exercise, the only fragment of the Verse-tongue anyone in the Hollow still remembered, the little keyhole melody the elder called "the door-knock."

Three notes. Nothing happened. Nothing ever happened. In the Hollow, magic could not follow—that was the one law of the valley, older than the guild's decline, older than the elder's memory of why—and so the notes simply left her mouth and died, and the spiral of notation sat on its page like a fossil of a thing that had once flown.

But Bram sang them anyway. Every morning. Because the alternative was admitting that she was the last caretaker of a cathedral with no god, and she was twenty years old, and she did not know how to be that yet.

* * *

Midmorning she walked down to the village, which was less a village than an argument between a road and a handful of houses, to trade eggs for salt and hear whatever news the low-country peddlers had brought up the switchback trail. News came slowly to the Hollow and left quickly. Nobody stayed longer than they had to. Visitors always described the valley the same way afterward, according to the peddler Tam, who came twice a year and was the closest thing to a friend Bram had: quiet, they said. Strange-quiet. Quiet like a held breath. Their dogs wouldn't bark on the way down the trail, and their horses sweated, and nobody could say why.

"It's the stone," Tam told her, perched on his cart with his boots swinging, the way he'd been telling her since she was small. "Certain valleys eat sound. It's the shape of the walls, is all."

"It's not the shape," Bram said.

"No," Tam agreed comfortably, because he never argued with her about it, which was its own kind of kindness. "Reckon it isn't." He handed down the salt, then hesitated, and his weathered face did something complicated. "Heard about your elder. Passing of. I'm sorry, girl. She was a fixture. Like the hall itself."

"She was the hall," Bram said. "I'm just the sweepings."

"Now that's not—" Tam stopped, rubbed his jaw, tried again. "Your elder, she sent word down once, years back. Asked me to keep an ear open. For travelers asking after the valley. Anyone coming up the trail with questions." He shrugged. "Nobody ever did. But I kept the ear open, like she asked. Still will, if you want."

Bram went very still. "What kind of questions?"

"Oh—after the guild. After the songbooks. Whether anyone still sang up here." He smiled apologetically. "Foolish things. Nobody sings anymore anywhere, near as I can tell. Whole world's gone quiet, they say, outside the capitals. Bells and such, but no real singing." He clicked his tongue at the mule. "You want me to keep listening?"

"Yes," Bram said, before she understood why the word came out of her so fast, so raw. "Yes. Please."

Tam looked at her a moment longer than he needed to, and then nodded, and took the switchback down out of the valley, and the silence closed behind him like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

The afternoon she spent on the roof, because the roof was losing its argument with the winter and someone had to argue back. She worked alone, as she did everything now, prying up cracked slates and bedding new ones in the mortar the elder had taught her to mix—lime and ash and a spoonful of powdered horn, "for memory," the elder had said, and Bram had never asked what that meant, and now no one living knew, and the question sat in her chest like a splinter working toward her heart.

From the ridgepole she could see the whole of the Hollow laid out below her: the terraced fields going gold-grey with the season, the millrace running silent as poured glass, the forty-odd houses of the village with their smoke rising in perfectly straight columns because there was no wind to bend it. Wind never entered the valley. Rain fell straight down, polite as a curtain. Clouds gathered at the rim of the cliffs some mornings, piled and churning like a siege army, and then simply gave up and went around.

Her whole life was visible from up here, and it was small, and she loved it, and she hated it, and she could not have explained to anyone how both those things could be true at once.

The elder had promised her, once, that the silence was a mercy. *The world outside forgot us, child, and we forgot it right back, and that forgetting is the kindest spell anyone ever cast.* Bram had been nine. She had believed it the way children believe doors are for walking through. Now, kneeling on the cold slate with her palms bleeding pitch and her knees aching, she found herself wondering—for the thousandth time, and the thousandth time without answer—what exactly everyone had needed so badly to forget.

* * *

Dusk came early in the Hollow, sliding down the cliff walls like a lid closing. Bram made her rounds, as the elder had taught her: lamps trimmed, doors barred, the archive's inner seal checked—a heavy iron plate bolted across the oldest chamber's door, locked with a key the elder had worn on a cord around her neck for sixty years and which had gone into the ground with her, because her instructions had been explicit and her handwriting, in the letter Bram had found sealed among her papers, had been unusually firm: *Burn this unread, or keep it unopened until you are sure. You will know when you are sure. I am sorry either way.*

Bram had not burned it. She had not opened it either. It lived in the third drawer of the reading-room desk, under the spare quills, and every night she put her hand flat on the drawer and stood there while her heart hammered, and every night she took her hand away again.

Not tonight, she told herself, as she told herself every night. Tonight she banked the reading-room fire instead, and sat beside it with the newest of the forty-one songbooks—the youngest was two hundred years old—open on her knees, and let herself do the one thing she never did in daylight.

She grieved.

It came quietly, the way everything came in the Hollow. No sobbing; the valley didn't seem to permit it, or perhaps she had simply forgotten how. Just tears, hot and fast, and her hand pressed over her mouth, and the fire ticking, and the enormous patient silence of the Guildhall holding her the way a cup holds water—completely, and without warmth.

"I don't know what I'm doing," she said to the empty room, to the elder's ghost, to the forty-one books that would never answer. "You left me a legacy and I don't even know what it's *for*. I can keep the roof on. I can keep the rot out. I can't keep the *point*." Her voice cracked and flattened into the hush. "You were the last one who knew anything, and you're in the ground, and I'm here dusting your fossils, and I am so afraid that this is all I am. A keeper of nothing. A songwright who has never heard a song."

The fire ticked. The silence held. Somewhere in the walls, the house declined to settle.

And then—because grief makes the ears strange, or because the valley was listening, or because of nothing at all—Bram heard the seventh sound.

The drum-underwater. Except tonight it wasn't at the edge of her hearing. Tonight it was closer, and faster, and it had a shape to it, a rise and fall that her spine recognized before her mind did, because it was the rhythm of the door-knock, the three-note exercise, stretched and slowed and repeated, repeated, repeated, like something calling from very far away. Or climbing.

She sat up. The tears stopped as if cut.

The sound was coming from outside. From the gate, at the bottom of the long path, where no one had knocked in six years, where no one *could* knock, because the Hollow did not permit visitors and visitors did not permit themselves the Hollow—and because, she realized with a cold spreading through her chest, the gate bell had been silent since before the elder died, since before *she* had come to the hall, since—

Since forever. The bell rope hung rotted. She had checked it herself, a hundred times, tending.

The sound came again. Closer now. Not a bell. Not a drum.

A knock. Small fists, on old oak. And underneath it, threading up through the storm that had begun to fall straight and polite as a curtain against the windows, a voice—thin, wordless, tireless—humming three notes over and over.

The door-knock. Sung by someone who had never been taught it.

Bram stood, heart slamming, and reached for the lamp, and the drawer with the unopened letter caught the corner of her eye like a hook.

"You will know when you are sure," the elder had written.

Outside, in the dark and the rain, someone was knocking.
