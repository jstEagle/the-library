## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The storm came up the valley like something being chased.

Bram felt it before she saw it—a pressure behind her ears, a wrongness in the air that the Hollow wore like a second skin. For thirty years she had lived inside that stillness, and she had learned its moods the way a farmer learns his fields: which evenings hummed faintly with distant weather, which mornings lay flat as poured wax. This evening was neither. This evening the silence itself seemed to lean eastward, straining toward something on its way.

She was in the kitchen when it broke, rendering tallow into soap over a low fire because grief, she had found, could be boiled down into almost anything if you gave it enough hours. Elder Maren had been dead eleven days. The Guildhall had not yet learned to be quiet about it; every room still held her shape—the dent of her chair, the ring-stain on the ledger table where her tea cup had sat for forty years, the half-mended shawl Bram couldn't bring herself to finish or throw away. So Bram made soap. Soap required nothing of her but patience and burned fingers, and both were things she had in surplus.

The first thunder arrived strangely—muffled, as though the storm were shouting through a wall. That was the Hollow's doing. Sound entered the valley and went soft, went vague, went to sleep. Lightning lit the windows white and the thunder followed a heartbeat later like an apology. Rain came down in ropes.

And then, beneath all of it, a knock.

Not at the door. At the gate.

Bram stood very still, ladle dripping tallow onto stone flags that hadn't been scrubbed properly since before she was born. No one knocked at the Hollow's gate. That was rather the point of the Hollow. The valley sat three days' hard walk from the nearest road, down a track that didn't appear on maps because no one who traveled it remembered to draw it afterward. Pilgrims didn't come. Peddlers didn't come. In her whole life, Bram could count the visitors on one hand without opening her fist: a lost shepherd twenty years ago, a tax assessor who turned around at the ridge muttering that the place gave him a headache, and once—she was nine, and had never told anyone—a woman in a gray cloak who stood at the gate for a full hour in the rain, looking at the Guildhall windows, and left without speaking a word.

The knock came again. Three strikes, patient, spaced evenly, the way you'd knock if you had been knocking a long time and expected to keep knocking.

Bram set down the ladle. She wiped her hands on her apron, then unknotted the apron entirely and hung it on its peg, which was absurd—there was no one to see whether she looked like a guildmistress or a scullery girl—but her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking, so she let them have it.

The hall between kitchen and gate was long and dark and full of Maren's ghost. Bram walked it fast.

* * *

The gate was old ironwork, older than the guild, older than the kingdom that had swallowed the guild's name into its histories and spat out nothing. Beyond it, the courtyard was a lake of rain, and beyond that, the outer wall, and beyond *that*, the world—which was to say, darkness and water and wind trying its best to get in and failing, always failing, the way it had failed for a hundred years.

A child stood at the bars.

She was small—seven, perhaps eight, though it was hard to tell with the mud. She was caked in it, head to foot, hair plastered flat to her skull, and she was soaked past any meaning of the word, standing in the downpour as if rain were simply a thing that happened to other people. Her clothes had been fine once. Bram could see it even through the filth: good wool, dyed a deep river-blue, hemmed with silver thread that had mostly tarnished but caught the lightning now and then like fish scales. Nobody dressed a child like that to send her walking. Somebody had dressed her like that to send her somewhere important, and the somewhere had gone wrong.

"Hello," Bram said, and her voice came out strange in the storm-hush, too loud and too thin.

The child looked up. She had dark eyes, enormous, and they did not do what eyes did—they did not flinch from the light, did not dart to the exits, did not perform fear. They simply looked at Bram with an attention so complete it felt like being read aloud.

"You're going to die of cold," Bram said. "That's not a threat. It's just weather."

The child said nothing. Bram waited, counted five breaths, six. Then the child opened her mouth—and closed it again. Opened it. Her throat worked. Nothing came out. And Bram understood, with a lurch, that the girl wasn't refusing to speak. She was trying. Whatever sounds she knew, they weren't sounds this valley would let pass.

Guild law said outsiders did not enter the Hollow. It said it in the old charter, in ink gone brown as dried blood: *No stranger crosses the threshold; no song leaves the vault; the silence keeps what the silence keeps.* Maren had recited it to her a thousand times, usually while doing something kind—feeding a stray cat, mending a neighbor's fence—so that Bram grew up believing the law and the kindness were the same thing. The silence kept them safe. The silence kept everyone safe. Especially, though Maren never said so outright, whatever it was the silence kept *in*.

Bram's hand was already on the latch.

She noticed herself doing it the way you notice yourself falling—as a fact arriving slightly after the fact. She thought: *I should fetch the elder.* But there was no elder. There was only Bram, thirty years old, keeper of a guild of one, standing in a doorway with a dying fire behind her and a drowned child in front of her, and there was no one left in the world whose job it was to make this decision except her.

The latch clicked open under her hand.

* * *

Getting the child warm took an hour and most of Bram's dry wood. She stripped the ruined blue dress—it would need washing twice before it forgave her—and wrapped the girl in blankets by the kitchen hearth, rubbed her feet and hands until feeling came back into them, put broth in her when she could hold the cup and honey-water when she couldn't. The child endured all of it with that same unnerving attention, watching Bram's face throughout as though the nursing were a lesson being given and she intended to learn it.

It was only when the color came back into her cheeks that Bram noticed the humming.

At first she thought it was the kettle. Then the wind in the chimney. Then she thought, with a cold slide of dread, that she was finally losing her mind the way she'd always half-expected to, alone in a house built for forty voices, and that the madness had chosen music as its medium, which seemed fitting and cruel.

But the kettle was off the fire. The wind was outside. And the sound was coming from the child—low, barely voiced, more breath than tone, a melody rising and falling in slow phrases that didn't go anywhere and didn't need to. It wasn't a song the way songs are shaped, with beginnings and ends. It was more like weather. More like tide.

Bram sat down slowly on the bench across the hearth.

Because she knew it.

Not the tune—she had never heard the tune. But the *shape* of it, the grammar underneath it, she knew the way you know your mother's handwriting: not from reading it often, but from having it read to you, once, in a voice that is now ash. Maren had sung to her exactly four times in her life, always at midwinter, always the same fragments, always stopping abruptly as if the melody were a door she'd caught herself opening onto a room she'd sworn to keep shut. *Just bits of the old tongue,* Maren had said. *Lullabies, more or less. Verse-tongue. Before names, before kingdoms. Don't ask me what the words mean, because I'll tell you I don't know, and I'll be lying.*

The child was humming Verse-tongue. Purely, easily, the way children hum anything—without knowing it was impossible.

Bram's hands had gone cold despite the fire.

"No," she said aloud, to the child, to the room, to nobody. "That's not—that can't come in here. Nothing comes in here."

The child stopped humming. Looked at her. And began again.

* * *

Later—much later, when the storm had settled into a steady drumming and the child slept curled in Maren's old chair like a comma in the middle of a sentence—Bram climbed to the scriptorium.

She told herself it was to check the roof. The roof needed checking; it always needed checking; that was a true thing and she held onto it with both hands while her legs carried her up the stairs two at a time, heart hammering, toward the thing she actually wanted and was afraid to want.

The scriptorium occupied the hall's eastern wing, a long room of shelves and sloped desks and tall narrow windows that had not held glass in living memory. Forty workstations. Forty stools. One occupant, and she was the wrong size for the room, the way she had been the wrong size for it since she was six years old and Maren first set her down among the songbooks and said, *Someday this will be yours, little bird, and may the gods forgive us both.*

The songbooks.

There were ninety-one of them, arranged by age along the north wall, their covers of boards and dyed leather, their spines cracked, their pages soft as cloth. They were called songbooks because that was what the guild had called them, but they did not sing. Not anymore. Not for a hundred years—not since the Crown's purge, not since the memory-songs were carried off to the capital in crystal vials and the guild was broken like a spine. What remained were transcriptions, copies, the sheet music of a choir that no longer existed, written in notation half extinct. Bram had spent her life learning to read them and finding, page after page, decade after decade, that reading was all they would ever do. Ink on vellum. A recipe for bread in a world with no ovens. Magic did not cross the Hollow's boundary. Magic did not live here. The songbooks were dead things, beautiful and mute, and Bram tended them the way you tend graves.

She stood in the doorway, dripping storm-light, and looked at the north wall.

The child's melody was still in her head. It had lodged there like a splinter, turning over and over, and now, standing in the dark among the dead books, Bram realized she was humming it herself—had been humming it since the kitchen, maybe, since the gate, since God knew when—and the moment she heard her own voice carry the phrase fully, openly, into the room—

The third shelf began to glow.

Softly. Faintly. Like coals remembering fire. A wash of pale gold seeping out from between the boards of the songbooks, pulsing gently in time with the melody, brightening when the phrase rose, dimming when it fell. Dust motes rose off the spines and hung in the light, drifting upward instead of down, and the whole room filled with the smell of summer—actual summer, cut grass and warm stone and rain-washed air, in a windowless room in late autumn, in a valley where such a smell had not existed in a hundred years.

Bram stopped humming.

The glow guttered, held, faded. The dust settled. The room went back to being a room: cold, dark, smelling of vellum and mouse and time.

She stood there until her legs shook.

Then she crossed the floor, knelt at the third shelf, and pulled down the book that had glowed brightest—an old one, older than the purge, its cover stamped with the guild's sign: seven interlocking circles, the Choir for the Crown. Her hands were not steady. She opened it to the marker ribbon, to the page she had read a hundred times and understood none of, the page of dense pre-covenant notation that Maren had called *the root page* and refused to explain.

The notation sat there, inert, brown, silent.

But beside it, in a margin Bram had read a hundred times and never truly seen, someone had written a single line in a cramped, hurried hand. She had always assumed it was a scribal note, some dry instruction about parchment weights. Now, with the melody still ringing in her skull, she read it properly for the first time:

*A verse uncarried will find its own throat. Guard the gate. Guard the child. —M., in fear*

Bram sat down on the cold floor with the book in her lap and laughed—one short, cracked sound with no humor in it at all.

*M., in fear.* Maren. Who had died eleven days ago with her secrets intact and her hands folded, who had raised Bram inside a silence and called it safety, who had taught her the guild law against outsiders and repeated it like a prayer. *Guard the child.* Which child? The words were decades old. The ink had faded before Bram was born.

And yet.

Downstairs, in a chair that still held an old woman's shape, a mud-streaked stranger slept wearing clothes fit for a court, unable to speak a word of the common tongue, humming a language older than every kingdom, in a valley where magic had not drawn breath in a century—until tonight, until her, until the dead books woke at the sound of her voice like dogs hearing their master's step on the stair.

Bram closed the book. Held it against her chest. The leather was cold and smelled of dust and, faintly, impossibly, of summer.

"Who are you?" she asked the empty room, and meant the child, and meant the margin, and meant, in some small buried part of herself she was not ready to look at, the girl on the cold floor holding the answers to questions she hadn't known how to ask.

The room said nothing. Rooms here always said nothing. That was the whole point of the Hollow.

But upstairs in the scriptorium, on the third shelf, one songbook was still glowing—faintly, patiently, gold light bleeding between its boards like dawn under a door—and it did not stop until morning.
