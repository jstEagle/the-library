## 3. The Language Before Names

The child had been in the Guildhall for three days before Bram admitted she was not going to stop humming.

It wasn't an ordinary tune. Ordinary tunes wore themselves out; they circled a handful of notes and grew tiresome, the way the miller's daughter's lullabies did, the way even the hymns of the low-country chapels did when they drifted up the valley road on feast days. This melody never repeated. It climbed and folded back on itself like water finding its way downhill, always new, always inevitable, as though it had existed whole somewhere and the child was merely uncovering it note by note.

Bram heard it through the floorboards at night. She heard it while she mended the shutters the storm had cracked. She heard it in her dreams, which was the part that frightened her most, because nothing from outside the Hollow had entered her dreams since she was small enough to believe dreams were doors.

On the third morning she found the girl standing in the archive with both hands pressed flat against the glass of a songbook case, and the songbook inside—the *Canticle of Foundations*, ink faded to the color of weak tea, dead as every other book in this valley for a hundred years—was glowing.

Not brightly. Not dramatically. A faint amber warmth along the spine of the pages, like a coal remembered rather than seen. But glowing. In the Hollow. Where magic could not follow.

Bram set down the tray of bread and goat's milk she'd brought and did not move for a long moment.

The child turned. Her eyes were gray, unremarkable, mud-brown hair cut raggedly at the jaw as if someone had done it with a knife and no mirror. She looked perhaps eight years old. She had not spoken a single word since Bram had pulled her out of the rain at the gate—had not wept, had not whimpered, had not made any sound at all except that melody, which now, facing Bram, changed.

It rose. It opened. It became, unmistakably, a question.

"I don't understand you," Bram said quietly. "I want you to know that I would help you if I could, but I don't understand."

The girl tilted her head. Then she hummed again—different fragments now, stitched together, patient, the way one speaks slowly to a foreigner—and Bram felt something turn over in her chest, some long-seized mechanism groaning against rust.

Because she *did* understand. Not the words. The shape. The rising third that asked, the falling fifth that answered, the little cluster of dissonance that meant *I am afraid*—she knew these the way she knew the grammar of her own bones. She had spent twenty-nine years reading books that could not sing, tracing notation that meant nothing anymore, memorizing a language whose every speaker was dust. And here stood a child speaking it fluently, natively, carelessly, the way birds speak flight.

"You're singing Verse-tongue," Bram whispered. "That's impossible. That's—" She stopped herself. Nothing was impossible anymore. The impossible had walked through her gate three days ago with bleeding feet.

She crossed the archive, knelt, and opened the case herself. The glow brightened at her touch—or at the child's nearness, she couldn't tell which—and beneath the glass the old notation seemed to stir, the way leaves stir when wind is coming though the branches haven't moved yet.

"Say it again," Bram said. "Whatever you just said. Say it again."

The girl hummed. And Bram, guildless master of a dead art, last keeper of a silence she had never questioned until now, began—haltingly, shamefully, note by note—to answer.

* * *

*The rain has followed me for nine days.*

That is what Ilse knows of herself: the rain, and the walking, and the song. Everything else is fog. There was a place before the fog—a warm place, a loud place, hands and voices and a great turning wheel of light—but when she reaches for it the way she reaches for a doorhandle, her fingers close on nothing. The fog eats the memory. The fog eats everything except the song.

The song does not get eaten. The song gets *stronger*, the longer she walks, and she does not know if that is good. The song pulls her like a fish on a line, and the woman with the sad face and the strong hands is somewhere along that line too, though Ilse cannot say how she knows it. She simply knows things now. She knows the woman's name is Bram without being told. She knows the valley is wrong—not evil-wrong, but wrong the way a bone set badly is wrong, healed but crooked, aching when weather comes.

She tried to tell the woman this, the first night, by the fire. She sang *the valley is crooked, the valley aches,* and the woman went white as milk and gripped the table so hard her knuckles clicked.

So Ilse is careful now. She sings small things. She sings *bread good, fire good, your dog is old and kind.* The dog is named Turnip, which is a funny name, and when she sang his name back to him he barked once, astonished, and then put his head in her lap and stayed there all evening.

But the song keeps growing whether she is careful or not. At night it fills her chest like water filling a boat. Sometimes she opens her mouth to keep it in and it comes out anyway, spilling into the dark rooms of this crooked house, and the dead books light up one by one down their shelves like windows at dusk, and the woman stands in the doorway holding a candle she doesn't need, looking at Ilse the way people look at weather they can't stop.

Last night the song showed her something new. Not words. A picture, almost—a tower, and bells, and a thread of gold running from the tower across a map of the world, and the thread fraying, strand by strand, and where it frayed the world went gray behind it. Ilse woke with her own hands over her mouth and the taste of iron in her throat.

This morning she is in the archive again because the books are the only things that answer her. She puts her palms on the glass and sings *hello, hello, I am here, are you here,* and the book glows, faint and far away, like someone knocking from inside a locked room.

Then the woman comes in, and kneels, and opens the case, and says words Ilse doesn't know but understands anyway, the way she understands everything here—backwards, through the song. And then the woman does something that makes Ilse's chest hurt with a feeling she has no name for.

The woman tries to sing back.

She is terrible at it. Her pitch wobbles. She loses the mode twice and has to start over, and her voice is rough and unused, a door hinge in a house nobody visits. But she keeps trying. She frowns with her whole face, concentrating the way Turnip concentrates on a bone, and she gets the phrase eventually, more or less, and the book flares gold between them, and Ilse laughs.

It comes out of her as a laugh and leaves her as a chord. The whole archive rings with it. Dust leaps off the shelves in a fine bright cloud, and every dead songbook in the room glows at once, and the woman sits down hard on the floor, candle guttering, staring at her.

Ilse claps both hands over her mouth. *Sorry, sorry, sorry,* she sings through her fingers, mortified, and the apology comes out as a descending scale that makes the nearest shelf shiver.

"No," the woman says. Her voice is strange. "No, don't be sorry." She is still sitting on the floor of her own archive, in her own dead guildhall, in the middle of a miracle she has clearly been waiting for her entire life without letting herself hope for it. "Don't ever be sorry for that."

And then, slowly, carefully, like a person crossing ice, the woman sings a question: *Who are you?*

Ilse reaches for the answer and finds, at the bottom of the fog, at the very bottom, under the rain and the walking and the wheel of light—one word. One name-shaped thing, worn smooth as a river stone.

She sings it.

*Ilse.*

The woman repeats it, spoken aloud, tasting it. "Ilse." Then, in the Verse-tongue, halting but true, she asks the second question, the one Ilse has been dreading, the one the song has been circling for days like a bird over open water:

*What are you?*

Ilse doesn't know. That is the truth and she gives it honestly: a shrug translated into a minor arpeggio, *I don't know, I don't know.* But the song knows. The song rises in her without permission, and the oldest book on the highest shelf opens itself with a crack of splitting leather, and pages turn in a wind that isn't there, and light pours upward from the archive floor like water running a fountain in reverse.

And in that light, Ilse sees what the song sees. Herself, small, standing in a circle of robed figures. Hands on her head. A melody being poured into her the way you pour water into a jar—not teaching her the song, *making her* the song, folding verse after verse into her skin and blood and breath, until there was no Ilse underneath at all, only the vessel, only the—

The vision cuts off. The fog slams down. Ilse is on her knees and doesn't remember falling, and the woman is holding her, saying her name, her new name, over and over, spoken-voiced, human-voiced, and Ilse holds onto that voice like a rope thrown into deep water.

* * *

Bram held the child until the shaking stopped, and then she sat alone in the archive for a long time with the oldest text open on her knees.

The *First Codex*. Elder Maren—no, not Maren, that was another life, another ledger—Elder Sorrel had kept it chained shut in the deepest case, and Bram had never once been permitted to read it. *When you have need of it, you will know,* Sorrel used to say, and Bram had resented the answer for fifteen years, and now the chain lay broken on the floor, snapped by a gust of no wind at all, and she understood that "need" had finally arrived wearing muddy boots and a stolen melody.

The passage was in Verse-tongue, fully notated, and for the first time in her life Bram read it the way it was meant to be read—not as marks on a page but as music sounding in the skull, each phrase unfolding its meaning as it resolved. Translation and comprehension were the same act. She wept a little at that, absurdly, at the sheer rightness of it, and kept reading.

*...and when the covenant fails, it shall not fail all at once, as a rope snaps, but as a garment frays, thread by thread, season by season. And the final verse, which binds the binding, shall be taken out of the world and hidden where magic cannot reach, and it shall be kept by keepers who do not know what they keep, for knowing is the first theft...*

There was more. Diagrams. A notation she didn't recognize—notation for a *person*, chords written along an outline of a human body, verses marked at the heart, the throat, the blood. And at the bottom of the page, in a later hand, in ordinary common script, cramped and hurried:

*Sorrel — if she asks too soon, tell her nothing. If the song finds her, tell her the truth. God forgive us both either way. — E.*

Bram closed the codex. Her hands were steady, which surprised her. Inside, she felt like the valley felt: silent, and holding something enormous at bay.

The child—Ilse—was asleep in the kitchen chair where Bram had installed her, wrapped in Bram's own spare blanket, Turnip's head heavy on her feet. Even asleep, she hummed. Faintly. Endlessly. The melody had worked its way into the timbers of the house; Bram suspected it was in her own pulse now too.

A living fragment of the covenant. A fragment that was fraying—Bram had seen it, in the flashes of the vision, in the way the girl's songs came slower each morning, thinner, a well dropping level. Whatever Ilse was, she was coming apart, and the thing she was part of was coming apart with her, and the gray at the edges of the world that the old texts called *the Unmaking* was not a metaphor or a distant fear. It was a countdown.

Bram stood at the window and looked out at the Hollow: the terraced fields going gold with autumn, the millstream, the bell-less chapel, the soft green walls of the valley that had held her like a hand her whole life. Safe. Silent. Dead, she thought now, and the word landed differently than it ever had. Not peaceful-dead. Kept-dead. A vault. And she was not the keeper of a legacy.

She was the lock.

Ilse stirred. Opened her eyes. Found Bram's across the dim kitchen, and sang, softly, sleepily, a question Bram understood perfectly, because it was the same question Bram was asking herself:

*What do we do now?*

Bram looked at the sealed door at the end of the upstairs hall—the one door in the Guildhall that Elder Sorrel had forbidden her, on pain of guild law, ever to open. The door Bram had obeyed for fifteen years out of grief and habit and a cowardice she had dressed up as respect.

"We go and ask the dead," she said aloud, in plain common tongue, because some things should be said plainly. "Come morning, we ask the dead what they've done."

Behind the sealed door, in the silence of the Hollow, something that might have been a draft—or might have been a held breath, waiting fifteen years to be released—moved through the house, and was gone.
