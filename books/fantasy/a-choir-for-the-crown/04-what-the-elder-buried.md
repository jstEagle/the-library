## 4. What the Elder Buried

The elder's chamber had been sealed for nine days, and Bram had spent eight of them finding reasons not to open it.

She had swept the Guildhall twice, though it was already clean. She had oiled the hinges of the great doors until they swung without a whisper. She had re-inked the ledger, trimmed the candle stubs, mended a chair that did not need mending. All the small liturgies of grief, performed in the order she had learned from Maren Solace herself: work first, weeping later, and never let the weeping interrupt the work.

On the ninth day, Ilse found her standing in the corridor with her hand flat against the sealed door, not pushing.

The child had been with her a week now. A week of humming in a language that made the dead songbooks glow like coals under ash. A week of the girl following Bram through the halls like a small shadow, watching everything with those grey eyes that seemed older than the valley itself. Ilse did not speak—could not, it seemed—but she had a way of tilting her head at Bram when Bram hesitated, a way that said, plainly, *I see you.*

She tilted her head now.

"I'm going in," Bram said, as if the girl had asked. Her voice came out rough. "Elder Solace left it to me. The whole guild left it to me, such as it is. I ought to at least—" She stopped. Started again. "She sealed it herself. Three years ago. She told me it was storage. She told me a great many things that were storage."

Ilse reached up and pressed one small finger to the back of Bram's hand. Then she hummed—two notes, low and steady, the way one hums to a frightened animal.

"I'm not frightened," Bram said, and heard the lie land between them like a dropped plate.

She unhooked the key from her belt. It was not the original key; that had gone into the elder's grave, per guild custom, so that no living hand might open what a Songwright had closed. But Maren Solace had been a thorough woman, and thorough women left second keys, and Bram had found hers three days ago sewn into the lining of the elder's spare cloak, wrapped in oilcloth, with a note in the elder's cramped hand: *For Bram. When you are ready. Not before.*

*When you are ready.* As if readiness were a door one simply walked through.

Bram turned the key. The lock gave with a sound like a sigh.

* * *

The chamber smelled of paper and lamp-oil and, beneath it, something older—dust that had settled on dust, the smell of a room holding its breath. Bram lit the lamp on the desk inside, and the light climbed the walls and showed her a room that was not a storeroom at all.

It was a study. A scholar's study, dense and deliberate. Bookshelves packed to the ceiling, but not with the guild's songbooks—those were all catalogued downstairs, silent and dead. These were different: ledgers, correspondence, maps of the valley drawn and redrawn across decades, each version with the boundary marked in a different hand. A desk buried under paper. A cot in the corner with a single blanket, folded with military precision, and Bram felt her throat tighten at the thought of Maren Solace sleeping here, alone, night after night, while the rest of the Guildhall slept their quiet sleep of the almost-dead.

On the wall above the desk hung a frame. Bram expected a portrait. It was not a portrait.

It was a map of the world—or what passed for one in the guild's oldest atlases—two realms rendered as facing shores, and between them a thread of gold ink: the covenant, the Song that had once run between them like a bridge. Someone had drawn a second thread over it in black, frayed, unraveling at both ends. And at the center of the black thread, a small circle of red ink, and beside the circle, in the elder's hand:

*The Hollow. The vault. The last verse sleeps here. Keep it sleeping.*

Bram stood very still. The lamp flame leaned in a draft she could not feel.

*The last verse sleeps here.*

She read it again. And again. And the words did not rearrange themselves into sense.

Behind her, Ilse made a sound—a small, questioning note, the girl's language of pure melody, and Bram realized distantly that the child was humming the same two notes from the songbooks, the fragment that made them glow. The fragment that had no business existing inside the Hollow, where magic could not follow.

The fragment that had walked through the valley's gate in a storm.

Bram turned to the desk and began, with hands that wanted to shake and did not, to read.

* * *

The letters were bundled by year, tied in the elder's precise knots. Bram started with the oldest and worked forward, and the story assembled itself the way a song assembles—from fragments, from repetition, from the terrible logic of a refrain.

The first bundle was older than Bram by sixty years. The handwriting was not Maren Solace's; it was firmer, a younger woman's hand, though the same woman, Bram realized—Maren at forty, writing to a correspondent whose name appeared only as *W.*

*The Crown's men came to the Marches again. They took the Vessel of Lamentations from the Harrow chapel, and the Lullaby of the Ford, and old Tomas's voice with them—he sang when they came, fool that he was, and they bottled him like a spirit. The Guild is finished as an order, W. What they cannot steal they will kill for. So we will not fight them. We will do what the guild has always done when the song must be kept: we will hide it where it cannot be found. Not in a vault of stone. In a valley of silence. We will make a wound in the world and pour the last verse into it, and magic will not be able to follow us there, and neither will they.*

Bram read it twice. Then a third time, because her mind kept snagging on a single phrase and refusing to pass it.

*We will make a wound in the world.*

The Hollow was not a refuge. She had been taught—raised on it, raised *by* it—that the Hollow was the guild's sanctuary, the last quiet place, hallowed ground where the old magic could not reach and so could not corrupt. Every child's lesson, every evening blessing: *the silence keeps us.*

The silence had not kept them. The silence had been *built.*

She pulled the next bundle. The letters grew more urgent as the years turned. The valley's boundary was not natural—she read of rituals she had no names for, of a working performed by eleven Songwrights in a single night, of the cost of it. Two of the eleven had not survived the making of the Hollow. Their names were written in the margin, and beneath them: *The valley drinks deep. Let no one say this was free.*

And then, in a letter dated the year of Bram's own birth, the handwriting changed. Older now, shakier, but unmistakably Maren's.

*W. has died. I am the last of the eleven. The others are ash or gone silent. And so the keeping falls to me, and I find I am not equal to it, because the keeping was never meant to be done alone, and I have spent my whole life alone by design.*

*The verse is safe. It sleeps as it has slept. But I write this tonight with an old man's warning ringing in my ears—W. said the vault will not hold forever, that silence is a dam and not a wall, and dams fail from the weight behind them. I tell myself we have years yet. I tell myself many things.*

*The child is well. She grows. She asks why the songbooks do not sing, and I tell her they are resting, and she believes me, because she is four and believes me, and I do not know how much longer I can bear being believed.*

Bram set the letter down.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere in the Guildhall above, a beam settled with a groan, and Ilse looked up at the sound, and Bram looked at the child, and the child looked back at her with those grey eyes, and Bram thought: *she is four, and believes me.*

She was reading letters about herself. She had known that since the second page, in the way one knows a thing before one permits oneself to know it—the way you know a storm is coming from the smell of the air. But knowing it in the body was different from knowing it in the mind. Her whole life lay in these bundles, and she had never once appeared in it by name, and somehow that was worse. *The child. The girl. She. She. She.* As if even on paper, even in secret, the elder could not bring herself to write what Bram was.

Bram untied the next bundle with hands that were steady by main force.

* * *

The last decade of letters were all to the same dead correspondent. Maren Solace had gone on writing to W. long after W. could read them, and Bram understood, with a lurch of pity that hurt worse than anger, that these were not letters at all. They were prayers. They were the only confession available to a woman who had built a cage and locked herself in it with the person she loved most.

*W.—the girl is sixteen and restless. She stands at the boundary. I have seen her do it, though she thinks I have not. She stands there the way I once stood outside my mother's door, wanting in. What do I tell her? That the world outside will use her? That she is not a girl at all but a verse, and verses are not lived, they are sung? I tell her nothing. I teach her ledger-keeping and roof-mending and the proper care of songbooks that will never sing again, and I watch her grow up small, and I tell myself smallness is safety. I tell myself many things.*

*W.—she found the boundary stone today and asked me what lies beyond. I said the world. She asked what the world is like. And I, who have seen it, who remember bells and color and the sound of a hundred voices in one hall—I said, "Loud." As if loudness were a sin. She laughed at me. Thank the silence for her laughter. It is the only music this valley has left, and I have stolen even that, haven't I? I have stolen her whole life and called it keeping.*

*W.—I am dying. There. It is written, so it is true. I have perhaps a season. And I find that everything I meant to tell her is still unsaid, because there is no way to say it that does not break her. If I tell her what she is, she becomes a thing with a purpose, and no one should be a thing. If I tell her what the valley is, she will hate me, and worse, she will be right to. If I tell her what the Crown did, she will go out there, and they will take her, and everything the eleven built will have been for nothing.*

*So I will seal this room, and I will leave her the key in the cloak she never wears, and I will trust that grief makes her curious, because grief always made me curious. And if she is reading this, then I am ash, and I can say what I could never say while breathing:*

*Bram. If you are reading this, I am sorry. I loved you the way one loves a thing one has broken. I built the walls high because the wolves were real, and I forgot, somewhere in the building, that a child is not a lamb to be kept but a voice that must be used. You were never mine. You were never the guild's. You were only ever your own, and I am the one who made that the hardest thing for you to be.*

*The valley was a vault. You were the treasure. And I was the lock, and locks are not loved, Bram, they are only endured until someone needs the door.*

Bram read the last line until the lamp burned low, and then she read it again in the dark, from memory, because the words had gone somewhere behind her eyes and would not leave.

*Locks are not loved. They are only endured until someone needs the door.*

She did not know how long she sat there. The lamp died. Ilse did not light another; the child simply came and sat beside her in the dark, close enough that their shoulders touched, and hummed the two low notes, over and over, soft as breath. Not the fragment that made the books glow. Something else. Something that sounded, Bram thought dimly, like the shape of a hand on a shoulder, translated into sound.

Grief came at last, in the dark, as it always did—work first, weeping later. But it was not the grief she had prepared for, the clean grief of burying an elder. This was grief with a knife in it. She had mourned a woman who had, it turned out, never existed: not the keeper of a sanctuary, but the architect of a prison; not her teacher, but her jailer; not selfless, but *afraid*—afraid in the enormous, corrosive way that mistakes itself for love.

And yet.

And yet Bram had seen the fear in the letters too, and could not unsee it. The Crown had bottled old Tomas like a spirit. The Crown had stolen the guild's memory-songs, generation by generation, voice by voice. The wolves had been real. Maren Solace had built the cage out of terror and called it protection, and she had been *wrong*, and she had also been the only person in the world who had ever fought for Bram at all.

Both things were true. Both things could be true. Bram sat in the dark with the weight of both and found that she could not yet carry them, and did not try.

What she could do was think. Grief made her curious—the elder had known her that well, at least. And curiosity, working in the wreckage, began to assemble a shape.

The Hollow was a vault, and vaults are made for things that are wanted. The Crown had spent generations stripping the guild's songs from the world, and the one thing they had never found was the final verse—because the final verse was not in a songbook, or a crystal vial, or a chapel archive. It had been hidden in a valley of silence, in the one form no warden could catalog.

A form that hummed two notes in the dark and made dead books glow.

Bram went cold, and then colder.

*The last verse sleeps here.* Not *slept.* The elder's map, the elder's letters, the whole architecture of the valley—the vault had been built for the final verse, and the final verse had been Bram, and Bram had lived her whole life one room away from the truth of herself.

But then what was Ilse?

The child who had come through the storm, humming the language before names, making the songbooks burn faintly with light they had not held in a century. The child who could not speak, whose songs were older than any kingdom. The child who had arrived one week after the last keeper of the vault died, as if the valley itself had opened its hand and let something in.

Unless the valley had not let her in. Unless the silence was failing—unless the dam was breaking, exactly as old W. had warned, and the weight behind it was pressing through the cracks, and what pressed through had taken the shape of a small grey-eyed girl because that was the only shape a broken covenant knew how to wear.

Bram stood. Her legs were stiff; she had been sitting for hours. In the darkness she felt her way back to the desk, found flint and steel, and relit the lamp, and by its returning light she looked at the map on the wall one more time.

The black thread, fraying at both ends. The red circle at its center. *The Hollow. The vault. The last verse sleeps here.*

And beneath the map, pinned to the wall's corner where she had not noticed it before, a single loose page in the elder's hand, newer than the rest, the ink still dark. A list. Five items, numbered. The first four were instructions—where the second key was hidden, how to open the boundary, what to carry.

The fifth item was one line, underlined twice:

*If the silence begins to fail, do not wait. Take her and go. The world is loud, Bram, but it is also the only place left where the song can be finished.*

Bram took the page down. Her hands were steady now, entirely steady, the steadiness that comes after the storm has already broken over you.

Ilse watched her from the floor, head tilted.

"We're going," Bram said. "I don't know where yet. I don't know how. But the valley was built to keep a song from ever being finished, and I am done being kept."

The child rose. And then, in the lamplight, Ilse did something she had never done before: she reached out and touched the map—touched the black thread where it frayed—and hummed a phrase Bram had not heard from her before. Three notes, rising. And on the wall, impossibly, the old gold ink of the covenant's original thread seemed to brighten beneath the black, just for a moment, like an ember remembering it had been fire.

Then it faded. And Ilse swayed on her feet, and Bram caught her—caught her and felt, under the child's skin, a tremor like a plucked string losing its tension.

The girl was fraying. Whatever she was, whatever she carried, it was coming apart faster than the letters could explain.

Bram looked at the list in her hand. *How to open the boundary.*

She had spent her whole life on this side of the silence, being told the silence was salvation.

Tomorrow, she would teach herself to break it.
