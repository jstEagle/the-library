## 6. First Steps into the Loud World

The boundary of the Hollow did not look like a wall. That was the cruelest part of it, Bram thought, standing at the tree line with Ilse's small hand swallowed in her own. For a hundred years she had imagined a gate, a seam of light, some visible scar where the world's magic pressed against the valley's silence. Instead there was only a birch copse, a stream running shallow over grey stones, and the ordinary gray light of a morning that had already begun to rain.

"You'll feel it," she told Ilse, though the girl could not answer in words. "Like stepping out of a cellar into a storm."

Ilse only looked up at her with those too-old eyes and squeezed her fingers once. *Hurry,* the squeeze said. *Before the song frays further.*

Bram had spent three days preparing, and it had all been theater—packing bread that would go stale, a cloak she'd mended a dozen times, the oldest songbook wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. Preparation was a ritual for people who believed they would come back. She had stopped believing it somewhere around the moment she'd read the elder's letters a fourth time and finally stopped looking for a line that would tell her she could stay.

She stepped forward.

The world broke open.

It was not sound, exactly, though it arrived as sound. It was *everything*—color first, violent and wet, the greens of the grass beyond the birches so saturated they seemed to bleed, the rain not falling but *performing*, each drop catching light like a struck bell. Then the pressure: a hum beneath the ground, beneath her skin, in the marrow the Hollow had kept numb her whole life. Magic. Not the memory of magic, not the dry notation of it in dead songbooks, but the living thing, thick as weather, moving through the world in currents she could suddenly *see*—gold threads in the rain, violet pulses in the roots of trees, a great slow tide of it rolling east toward the horizon like light under skin.

Bram's knees buckled. She went down into the wet grass and the grass *sang* under her hands, a thin green note of alarm, and she snatched her palms back as if burned.

"Bram." Ilse crouched beside her, mud soaking through the hem of the dress Bram had given her, and did something Bram had never seen her do: she spoke. Not in the Verse-tongue. In a single cracked, rusted word of the common tongue, dredged up from wherever she kept the few she'd learned. "*Up.*"

Bram got up. It was the only word that mattered.

* * *

They walked east through a morning that would not stop assaulting her.

Every mile was a lesson in how much the Hollow had taken from her—and how much it had, without her knowing, been protecting her from. A hawk's cry hit her like a slap. The smell of a rotting log was a symphony. When they passed a farmstead with geese in the yard, the geese turned as one and *stared* at Ilse, necks curved, and Bram understood with a lurch that animals could feel what the girl was. What Bram, now that she had crossed, could faintly feel through her too: a resonance, a hum, the sense of being a struck bell that had not yet finished ringing.

By midday the farmland gave way to a road, and the road gave way to people.

Bram had seen travelers before—from a distance, from behind hedges, in the years before the elder's death when supply carts still came as far as the valley's mouth and left goods at a boundary stone. But she had never stood three feet from a stranger, close enough to see the pores of his skin, close enough to be *seen*. The first carter who passed them reined in his mule and looked her up and down with frank, unembarrassed curiosity, and Bram's whole body went hot. In the Hollow, no one looked at you, because there was no one. Here, eyes were everywhere, and every one of them was a small violence.

"Lost, mistress?" the carter called. "Road to Marrowford's behind you."

"We're fine," Bram said, and her voice came out strange—too loud, too raw, unpracticed in open air.

The carter shrugged and moved on, but his eyes lingered on Ilse a moment too long, and Bram pulled the girl's hood up and walked faster.

It was near dusk, with the road descending toward a river town whose chimneys smeared the sky, that Bram first heard the bells.

They were small bells, handbells, rung by two figures walking the road ahead of them in matching grey cloaks—wardens, though Bram didn't know the word yet, only that the sound they made was *wrong*. Not music. A probing, a questioning, a note repeated and repeated like a finger tapping on glass. And with every ring, the world around them flinched. A dog went silent mid-bark. A hedge's gold thread of magic dimmed. And Ilse—

Ilse made a sound Bram would hear in her dreams for the rest of her life. A thin, tearing note, as if a string inside the girl had been plucked too hard. She doubled over, clutching her chest, and the faint glow that had lived under her skin since the storm—the glow that meant her verse still held—flickered like a guttering lamp.

"What is it—" Bram dropped to her knees on the road. "Ilse, what—"

The girl's mouth moved. No sound. But the shape of it was unmistakable, and Bram felt the meaning land in her own chest like a stone: *They are looking for me. They have always been looking for me.*

The wardens had stopped. One of them turned.

"You there." The voice was bored, official, terrible. "Step away from the child. We're conducting a resonance survey by order of the Crown. Any unregistered resonance in your party must be presented for assessment."

Bram's mind, trained by a lifetime of quiet, did the only thing it knew how to do: it went still, and it counted. Two wardens. Twenty paces. One road, open on both sides, hedged and ditched. A child who could not run. A songbook against her heart that would hang her faster than any confession.

"I don't know what resonance is," she said, and was proud of how level it came out.

"No," the warden agreed, walking closer, bell swinging. "Nobody from around here does. That's rather the point of the survey." He raised the bell, and it rang again, and this time Bram felt it *in* her—a cold finger pressed to her sternum, a question asked of her blood—and Ilse screamed, a real scream this time, raw and wordless, and the hedgerow beside the road *bloomed*, every bud bursting open out of season in a riot of white, and the warden's bored expression fell off his face like a mask.

"Well," he said softly. "Well, well."

He reached for the whistle at his collar.

That was when the ditch exploded.

* * *

Later, Bram would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail. There was a man—she was nearly certain it was a man—rising out of the drainage ditch in a spray of mud and dead leaves, and there was a smell of pitch and smoke, and there was a sound like a struck anvil wrapped in thunder, and both wardens' bells cracked simultaneously, split down the middle, ringing a dying discordant groan as the men staggered back clutching their ears.

"Run," the man said. He did not look at them. He was looking at the wardens, and his grin was the widest, most unhinged thing Bram had ever seen on a human face. "Road's clear to the bridge. *Run*, songbird, unless you fancy a cell in the capital."

Bram ran. She hauled Ilse up and ran, down the road, over the bridge, into the tangle of lanes beyond, following the mud-flecked stranger through back ways and under a collapsed fence and through the reeking alley behind a tannery, until the wardens' shouts faded and the man stopped so suddenly she nearly collided with his back.

They stood in the shadow of a granary, all three of them breathing hard. Up close, in the last light, Bram could finally see him: lean, weathered, maybe thirty, with a face that looked assembled from spare parts and a pair of dark eyes that missed absolutely nothing. A scar split one eyebrow. His clothes had been fine once, and were now merely expensive-looking rags. He smelled of pitch, river water, and something faintly, oddly, of candle smoke.

"Now," he said, still catching his breath, "you're going to tell me what in the nine hells a Hollow-born is doing on the King's Road with a resonance-charge like her, and you're going to tell me quickly, because those wardens will have a full patrol out of Marrowford within the hour, and I have exactly one escape route and room on it for two."

"Who are you?" Bram demanded.

"Kestrel." He swept a mocking little bow. "Smuggler, salvage-man, occasional hero against my will. And you're bleeding."

She looked down. Her palms were scraped from the road, and—she hadn't noticed—there was a thin line of blood at her nose. From the bell's probing, she realized. It had *rung* her, the way the elder used to tap a cracked cup to hear if it would hold.

"Her name is Ilse," Bram said. "She's sick. She's—she's fraying. And I'm taking her to the capital, and I don't need your escape route, I need—" She stopped, because it was a lie and they both knew it. She had no route. She had no map. She had a songbook and a dying child and a lifetime of silence, and the first city she'd ever seen was glowing on the horizon like a hearth fire she was expected to walk into.

Kestrel studied her for a long moment. Then Ilse, swaying on her feet, looked up at him, and hummed—three notes, soft, the opening phrase of the melody she'd carried out of the storm.

Kestrel went very still.

"I know that song," he said quietly. All the mockery had drained out of his voice. "I heard it once. In a place I don't talk about." His jaw worked. "Where did a mute child get a Songwright's verse?"

"You know the Songwrights?" Bram whispered.

"I know what the Crown did to them." He said it flatly, the way men say things they've decided never to weep about. Then he rubbed his face with both hands and laughed, a short, bitter, exhausted sound. "Of course. Of course this is my life. Fine. Fine! The bridge at Marrowford, the barge, the river road. Three days to the capital, if the patrols stay stupid." He jabbed a finger at her. "But hear me, Hollow-born. I'm not a charity. I get you there, you owe me. And I collect."

"What could I possibly have that you'd want?"

Kestrel looked at her—really looked, past the mud and the fear and the scraped hands—and something in his face flickered, quick as a fish under ice, something that looked almost like grief.

"A song," he said. "One true song, sung just for me, whenever I ask for it. That's the price. That's the whole price." He was already walking, not waiting to see if they'd follow. "And if you're wise, songbird, you'll hope I never ask."

Ilse's small hand found Bram's again in the dark. Bram looked down at the girl, at the faint guttering light beneath her skin, at the road ahead running black toward the river and the bells and the crown that had stolen a guild's memory and a smuggler's voice and a child's song.

She had crossed the wall. There was no un-crossing it.

She followed him into the loud world.
