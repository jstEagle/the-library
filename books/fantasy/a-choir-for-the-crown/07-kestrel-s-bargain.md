## 7. Kestrel's Bargain

Kestrel Vane had stolen a great many things in his life—a bishop's ring, a warden's horse, three years of another man's identity—and he had never once felt bad about any of them. But watching the woman called Bram Winterbourne count out her coins on the smuggler's table, coin by coin, with the grim patience of someone paying for a funeral, made him feel something adjacent to shame. Not because she was poor. Because she was honest about it.

"Twelve coppers," she said. "That's what I have."

"That's what you have," Kestrel agreed, "and what I want is not copper." He leaned back in his chair until it groaned. The waystation at Marrow's Ford was empty except for them—the innkeeper had gone to bed an hour ago, or more likely was listening through the floorboards, which amounted to the same thing—and the fire had burned down to that sullen orange stage where everything in the room looked like it was remembering being warmer. "What I want is passage to the capital. Yours. And hers." He nodded at the child.

The child—Ilse—sat on the bench beside Bram with her knees drawn up and her eyes open, which was how she did everything: silently, watchfully, like a cat deciding whether the room deserved her. She'd been silent all evening. All day, actually, since they'd crossed into the Loud World and she'd begun to fade. Kestrel had noticed it the way you notice weather changing—not as an event but as a pressure behind the ears. The girl's color was wrong. Her hands were too still. Whatever she was, whatever strange cargo these two were carrying across the borderlands, it was leaking out of her by the hour.

Bram followed his glance to Ilse, then looked back at him. "You said twelve coppers would cover the Marches."

"I said twelve coppers would cover *a* route through the Marches. There are routes and there are routes. The cheap one goes past Thornhollow, where the wardens keep their resonance-sieves strung across the road like laundry lines. You'd be caught inside a day. Possibly less, given that your friend there hums in her sleep."

That landed. He watched it land—saw Bram's jaw tighten, saw her hand move without thinking to rest on the girl's shoulder. Protective. Instinctive. The kind of gesture that couldn't be faked, because people who faked it always performed it too large.

"And the expensive route?" Bram asked.

"The one I know. It adds four days and keeps us off every road the Crown watches. It costs more than twelve coppers because it costs me more than twelve coppers' worth of risk." He turned his cup on the table, watching the dregs spin. "I'm not a charity, mistress Winterbourne. I'm a businessman whose business happens to be other people's survival."

"You said last night you'd take us for the coppers."

"I said a lot of things last night while wardens were shooting at us. You'll find my character improves considerably when no one is firing crossbows."

Ilse made a sound. It wasn't a word—it was barely even a note—but both of them turned toward her anyway, and Kestrel realized with a small cold shock that he'd done it too, automatically, the way you turn toward a knock. The girl was looking at him. Not at Bram. At him, with those gray eyes that seemed older than her face, and she tilted her head slightly, the way a bird does, and hummed two notes.

Low ones. Descending.

Kestrel's cup stopped turning under his fingers.

He didn't know why those two notes went through him like a splinter finding old wood. They weren't anything. A child making noise. Except the room had changed temperature, or he had, and somewhere behind his sternum something that had been quiet for twenty years shifted its weight and listened.

"What was that?" he said, before he could stop himself.

"She does that," Bram said quietly. "She hears things. What people are, underneath." A pause. "I wouldn't take it personally."

"I'm not taking anything personally. I'm asking what the child just did to my chest."

Bram studied him for a long moment. He'd been studied before—he knew the taxonomy of looks: the appraising look of buyers, the flat look of wardens, the hungry look of people who wanted something from him. This was none of those. This was the look of someone reading a page and finding more text than expected.

"She heard you," Bram said finally. "Whatever you're carrying. She heard it."

"I'm not carrying anything."

"No," Bram agreed softly. "That's rather what she heard."

* * *

He should have walked away. That was the professional assessment, delivered later, in the dark, with the honesty that only came after midnight: he should have taken the twelve coppers, pointed them at the cheap road, and let the Crown have them. Two strangers and a fading child were not his problem. His problem was getting to the capital with his neck intact and his ledger balanced, and neither of those goals benefited from escorting a woman who looked at wardens like arithmetic problems and a girl who hummed at people's souls.

But he sat there instead, and the fire ticked, and outside the rain came down on Marrow's Ford like the sky was trying to scrub the world back to some earlier draft.

"Tell me what you're really doing," he said. "Not the pilgrim story. Not the 'we're visiting relatives in the capital' story. I've carried enough contraband to know contraband when it counts its coins twice."

Bram's mouth twitched. "I counted them once."

"You counted them once out loud and twice with your eyes. Tell me."

"Why do you want to know? So you can sell us?"

"If I wanted to sell you, I'd have done it at the border. Wardens pay thirty crowns for unregistered resonance, and the girl alone—" he gestured at Ilse, who was watching this exchange with the unblinking interest of someone following a story in a language she almost knew, "—the girl alone would fund my retirement. I'm still here. Draw your conclusions."

"Conclusions aren't trust."

"No," Kestrel said. "Trust is conclusions plus time, and we don't have time, so we'll have to settle for leverage. Which brings me back to my price."

"You already named it. Four days of risk."

"I named the route. I haven't named the price." He set the cup down. Here was the part where a smarter man would have kept his mouth shut, named a number, and slept well. But the two notes were still lodged in him, descending, and the rain was still falling, and there is a particular kind of loneliness that makes a man stupid in front of strangers precisely because they are strangers—because whatever he said tonight could be gone by morning, carried over the border into a country where nobody knew his name.

"One song," he said.

Bram blinked. "One... song."

"You're a Songwright. Don't look surprised—I've known since the ford, when you flinched at the warden's sieve-bell like it had stepped on your foot. Only guild-trained ears hear the wrongness in those bells. So. One song. Sung true, sung whole, sung for me and no one else. That's my price for the safe road."

"That's all?"

"That's all," Kestrel lied, because *that's all* was easier to say than *I lost something twenty years ago and I don't even know if I want it back or just want to remember what it weighed.*

Bram leaned forward. In the firelight her face had the quality of a map—lines everywhere, all of them meaning something. "Songs aren't nothing, master Vane. A true song changes things. That's rather the point of my entire order, or what's left of it. If you're asking me to sing something to manipulate a guard or open a lock—"

"I'm not asking for a lock-song." His voice came out lower than he intended. He cleared his throat. "I'm asking for a real one. Whatever you think a real one is."

"And what would I even sing? I don't know you."

"No," he agreed. "You don't."

* * *

They took the road at first light, because the Marches rewarded early starts and punished everything else. Kestrel led them west-northwest along the dry bed of a river that had died when the covenant began failing—everyone said so, though the Crown called it drought and posted rewards for anyone who suggested otherwise. The Withered Marches spread around them like a sentence someone had started and abandoned: fields the color of ash, hedgerows gone gray and brittle, a farmhouse with its door standing open and no one in it, ever again.

And the silence. That was the thing outsiders never understood about the Marches. It wasn't quiet the way night is quiet. It was quiet the way a missing tooth is quiet—you kept putting your tongue against the absence. No birdsong. When they flushed a hare from the gorse it ran in perfect soundlessness, and Kestrel saw Bram watch it go with an expression he was beginning to catalog as *her grief face*, of which she appeared to have several.

Ilse rode pillion behind Bram on the mule Kestrel had procured (procured being a flexible word), and she faded visibly as the day wore on. By noon her skin had gone the gray-white of paper held up to light. By mid-afternoon she was leaning against Bram's back, and her breathing had developed a faint whistle, like wind through a cracked window.

"She needs something," Bram said, reining in. "I don't know what. Song, maybe. Or—" her hands tightened on the reins, "—or the thing she is, repaired. And I can't repair it. I've tried. Every verse I know just slides off her."

"Then we make speed," Kestrel said. "Which we will, if you stop interrogating my route choices."

"I'm not interrogating. I'm asking why a smuggler knows a warden-free path through the Marches better than the wardens themselves."

"Because I used to walk it singing," Kestrel said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because Bram's head came around with the sharp attention of a woman who collected sentences the way he collected debts.

"Singing," she repeated.

"Forget I said it."

"Not possible. I'm a Songwright. Words are my trade." She urged the mule forward alongside him. "You sang. On this road. Alone?"

"It's a long road. A man fills it somehow."

"With what? You told me last night you can't carry a tune. You said it yourself—'don't ask me to hum, mistress, I'm tone-deaf as a fencepost.'"

"I remember what I said."

"So which is it? Tone-deaf, or singing?"

Kestrel walked five steps. Ten. The dead riverbed stretched ahead, gray and patient. Behind him he could hear Ilse's whistling breath, thin as thread.

"There was a choir," he said finally, because apparently the loneliness had won the argument while he wasn't looking. "In the capital. The Royal Chapel choir. Boys taken young, voices kept soft, trained to sing the Crown's music at court functions. Weddings. Coronations. Funerals." He kept his eyes on the road. "It's considered an honor. Your family gets stipends. You get fed, clothed, taught Latin and harmony. And when your voice breaks—" he shrugged with elaborate carelessness, "—you're released with a pension and a pat on the head."

"That's not how voice-breaking works," Bram said quietly. "You'd know that, wouldn't you. Yours didn't break. It was *taken*."

The word went through him like the two notes had, except these were his own words coming back, and somehow that was worse.

"The memory-songs," he said. "You know about them. Everyone in your guild knew about them, that's why your guild is a graveyard. The Crown doesn't keep songs in books, mistress. Books burn. Songs kept in *voices* last forever. Crystal vials, they say, down under the chapel, each one holding a captured singer. The royal music never falters because the same voices have been singing it for sixty years." He smiled, and was aware of how much effort the smile cost, and hated that he was aware. "I was eleven. They said I had the purest treble in a generation. My mother cried at the ceremony. With pride."

Behind him, the whistling breath hitched.

When he turned, Ilse had slid half off the mule, and Bram had caught her, and the girl was looking at Kestrel with those gray ancient eyes, and her mouth was moving—shaping words in that language older than kingdoms, the Verse-tongue, syllables that sounded like water deciding to be a river. And though Kestrel had never learned a word of it, he understood this fragment completely, the way you understand a blow:

*Hollow. You are hollow. I can see the shape of what was taken.*

"Don't," he said. His voice cracked on it, which hadn't happened since he was eleven, and he hated that too. "Don't look at me like that, child. I'm not your tragedy. I'm your guide. Guides get paid."

Ilse reached out—one small hand, gray-pale, trembling with whatever was eating her alive—and touched his wrist. Just that. Just a touch. And hummed.

Two notes. Descending. The same two notes from the waystation, and now, walking this road where he had once sung to fill the silence, he finally recognized them. Not a question. Not a judgment.

A lullaby. The opening phrase of a lullaby his mother had sung to him before the choir, before the chapel, before the hollowing—something in the old dialect of the river country, something he'd spent twenty years unable to recall and had never once stopped grieving.

"How," he said. His throat didn't work right. "How does she know that."

Bram's hand rested on the girl's hair. "I told you she hears what people are. What they've lost." A pause, and then, gentler than anything he'd heard from her yet: "She's not mocking you, Kestrel. She's telling you it's not gone. Somewhere under the crystal and the stone, it's not gone."

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a fallen boundary-stone, one of the old covenant markers, its carved runes worn to suggestions. Kestrel built a small fire because Ilse was shivering, and told himself the smallness was tactical. Fires drew wardens. Small fires died fast. Nothing to do with wanting the dark close around them.

Bram sat across the flames with the girl asleep against her shoulder, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The Marches held their breath around the little ring of light.

"My price," Kestrel said at last. "The song. Tomorrow, maybe. Or the next night. Whenever we're clear of the sieve-roads."

"I haven't agreed to your price."

"You fed my fire. You're riding my mule. You'll agree."

"What would I even sing to you?" Bram asked, and there was something in her voice he hadn't heard before—not suspicion, not calculation. Something almost careful. Almost kind. "You said a true song. Sung for you. But Kestrel—I don't know what you need. I barely know you."

"No," he said. "You don't." He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "That's rather the point. Everyone who knows me knows the version I built afterward. The smuggler, the rogue, the man who's never lost anything because he never keeps anything long enough to lose it. You'd sing to that man and it would slide off, same as your verses slide off the girl." He looked up, and found her eyes across the flames, steady and waiting. "Sing to the boy in the choir loft. Sing to what's under the crystal. I don't care what the song is. I care that it's true, and that it's mine, and that for four minutes somebody in this world sings *to* me instead of *at* me."

The fire popped. Ilse stirred against Bram's shoulder but didn't wake.

"That's a dangerous thing to ask a stranger," Bram said quietly.

"I know."

"It might not work. I might sing and you might feel nothing, and then you'll have led us four days through hell for nothing."

"I know that too."

"And if it works?" Her head tilted, mirroring the girl's gesture from the night before, and he wondered if she knew she'd caught it from Ilse, the way people catch each other's gestures, each other's silences, each other's wounds. "If it works, what then, Kestrel?"

He thought about lying. It was his native language; he was fluent. But the lullaby was still humming somewhere under his ribs, two descending notes, and the truth came out before he could dress it in anything.

"Then I suppose I'll owe you more than a road," he said. "And I've been owing people things my whole life. Might as well owe something worth the debt."

Bram held his gaze a moment longer. Then she adjusted the sleeping child against her shoulder and looked into the fire, and said, in a voice that was almost too low for him to catch:

"All right. One true song. But Kestrel—when I sing it, you'll want to look away. Everyone does. Don't. Promise me you won't look away."

Across the dead fields, far to the east, a bell tolled once—some distant town, some warden tower—and the sound crossed the Marches like a crack across ice. Kestrel found himself listening to it the way he'd once listened to everything: hungrily, helplessly, a boy in a loft waiting for the conductor's hand.

"Deal," he said.

He didn't sleep that night. Neither, he noticed, did she.
