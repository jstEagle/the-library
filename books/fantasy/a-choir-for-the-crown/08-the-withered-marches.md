## 8. The Withered Marches

The color left the world three days after we crossed the boundary.

I noticed it first in the grass. We had been walking through a country of ordinary greens—worn, dusty greens, the green of roadsides and hedgerows—but on the morning of the third day I looked down and saw that the blades beneath my boots had gone the color of old bone. Not dead. That was the terrible part. They were not withered or browned or frost-bitten. They were alive, and they had simply forgotten how to be anything but pale.

"It's like looking at a songbook with all the notes rubbed out," I said, mostly to myself.

Ilse heard me anyway. She always did. She crouched beside me in the road and pressed her small palm flat against the bleached grass, and her face did something complicated—the look of a child meeting a sick animal she already knows will die.

She hummed at it. Two notes, low and careful, the way she'd hummed at my silent songbooks the night she arrived at the gate. The grass did not turn green again. But for a heartbeat—a single held breath—it *shivered*, as if it remembered there had once been a word for what it was.

Then it went still, and Ilse pulled her hand back, and I saw that her fingers were trembling.

"That cost you something," I said.

She didn't answer. She never answered. But she tucked her hands into her sleeves the way an old woman tucks away a coin she cannot afford to spend, and she stood up, and we kept walking north into the Withered Marches.

* * *

Kestrel had told us what to expect, in his sideways, bargaining way, the night before we crossed: "Past the Marches you'll see things that'll make your valley look like a festival. Don't stop for the animals. Don't touch the wells. And whatever you hear—or don't hear—keep moving." He'd said it while sharpening a knife he never seemed to use, and then he'd named his price, and I'd paid it without understanding yet what I was paying for, and now here we were, three travelers in a country where even the wind sounded tired.

The Marches were not a wasteland. That was the second terrible thing. There were farms out there—low stone houses, fenced pastures, orchards of trees heavy with fruit that had gone white as chalk. There were people. We passed a woman driving a cart of pale turnips toward a market town, and she nodded to us the way farmers nod to strangers, weary and unsurprised. A child chased a hoop along a fence line, laughing silently, mouth open, no sound coming out at all—and neither the child nor the woman seemed to find this strange.

That was the part that made my skin crawl. Not the silence itself. The *accustomedness* of it.

"Kestrel," I said that evening, when we'd made camp in the lee of a drystone wall, "the people here. Do they know?"

He was feeding a fire with strips of bark, and he didn't look up. "Know what?"

"That they're living inside a wound."

His knife-hand paused—just a flicker, just long enough—and then resumed. "People live inside everything," he said. "Wounds, walls, marriages. You get used to the shape of it." He glanced at me then, one eye catching the firelight. "You lived in a valley that ate sound for a hundred years. Did *you* know?"

I had no answer for that, and he knew it, and he went back to his fire with the small, insufferable satisfaction of a man who has won an argument without raising his voice.

But later, when Kestrel had taken first watch and Ilse lay curled against my side under our single blanket, I lay awake listening to the silence of the Marches, and I understood that it was not the same silence as the Hollow's. The Hollow's silence had been deliberate—a vault door, a held breath, a mother's hand cupped over a candle flame. This silence was a *leak*. It was what happened when the covenant frayed thin enough that magic stopped reaching places at all, the way light stops reaching the far corners of a room when the candle burns low.

And somewhere in the dark, close enough to touch, the last living fragment of that covenant was breathing shallowly against my shoulder.

* * *

Ilse began to fade on the fifth day.

It started small. She stumbled on flat ground and blamed her boots with a scowl so fierce I almost laughed. Then she slept past dawn, which she had never done—not once, not even in the Guildhall, where she woke every morning before me like a bird trapped indoors. On the sixth day she stopped humming.

I didn't understand what that meant until I caught myself straining for it. For six days her melodies had been the background of our journey—the little rising phrase she sang at streams, the descending run she made at dusk, the sharp bright chirp she used, infuriatingly, whenever Kestrel lost an argument to her, which was often. Their absence was like a tooth missing from my own mouth. My tongue kept going to the gap.

"Ilse." I knelt in front of her at the midday rest, in the shadow of a whitened apple tree. "Look at me."

Her eyes were still hers—dark, quick, stubborn—but there was a glassiness behind them now, a film like breath on a window. When she focused on my face, I watched her *work* to do it, watched the effort arrive a half-second late, like a letter traveling a long way.

I took her hands. Her skin was cool. Not cold—cool, the way the Hollow's songbooks had been cool, the way things are cool when the warmth has been drawn out of them and carried elsewhere.

"You're fraying," I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. The word came from some deep shelf of guild learning I hadn't known I possessed—Elder Maren's voice in my memory, reading from the oldest text: *a verse uncarried begins to unravel; a verse unraveled cannot be rewoven by any hand but its own.* "The farther you go from—" From what? From the Hollow? From the covenant's spine, whatever buried line of it still ran under the world? "The weaker you get. Singing costs you double. Maybe triple."

She pulled one hand free of mine and pressed it flat against her own chest, over her heart, and then turned the gesture outward—pressed her palm toward me, toward the road, toward the whole pale country—and shook her head slowly. *Not me alone,* the gesture said, in her wordless grammar. *All of it.*

"Yes," I said. "All of it. I know."

And then, because she was a child and children should not have to be brave about the end of the world, I did the thing I had sworn I wouldn't do: I lied to her with my face. I smiled. I said, "Kestrel says the capital is nine days' walk if we push. We'll push."

Behind me, Kestrel said nothing at all, which from Kestrel was a shout.

* * *

We came upon the herd on the seventh day, and I finally understood what the Marches were trying to tell us.

They were sheep—or they had been sheep, once, in the way that the bleached grass was still grass. They grazed a hillside of colorless pasture behind a fence of grey wood, and they were healthy, thick-fleeced, placid. A shepherd boy sat on the fence watching them with the boneless boredom of all shepherd boys in all countries. Everything about the scene was ordinary except for one thing, and it took me a long moment to find it, because my ears kept refusing to report it:

The sheep made no sound.

No bleating. No rustle. When one of them jostled another at the trough there was only the soft percussion of bodies, like snow falling on snow. And the boy on the fence—when he whistled them along, he shaped the whistle with his lips, and nothing came out, and the sheep obeyed anyway, because they had never known a whistle that sounded like anything either.

"They're born that way," Kestrel said quietly. He had come to stand beside me, and for once there was no performance in him, no angle, no price. "Lambs in the Marches don't cry out. Calves don't low. Birds—" he gestured at a hedgerow where a pair of small pale birds hopped and preened in perfect silence, "—don't sing. Been that way since before I was born. Getting worse, though. My grandmother said the birds still made noise when she was a girl. Just badly. Like instruments out of tune." He spat into the dust. "Now they don't try."

I thought of Elder Maren's letters, hidden in her sealed chamber for thirty years, waiting for me with their patient, damning truth: *The Hollow was built to hide the final verse. The Crown stole the memory-songs to rule without the covenant. And the covenant, unsung, is dying—and everything downstream of it dies with it.*

I had read those words in the safety of the Guildhall and felt betrayal, fury, grief. Here, standing at a silent fence in a colorless country, I felt something harder and more useful.

I felt arithmetic.

Every day of our walking was a day of Ilse fading. Every mile of caution, every detour around a warden patrol, every night's rest bought with a fire that might be seen—that was a lamb born voiceless, a field gone grey, a child in some Marches village who would grow up never knowing her own laugh made sound. The Crown had stolen the songs a hundred years ago, and the realm had been bleeding out ever since, slowly enough that no one noticed the color leaving.

Slowly enough that everyone had gotten used to the shape of the wound.

"We can't take the safe road," I said.

Kestrel turned to look at me fully for the first time in days. "There is no safe road, songwright. There's the slow road and the fast road, and the fast road goes through Harrowgate, and Harrowgate has a warden garrison and resonance-scrying on both gates. With her—" he tipped his chin at Ilse, small and grey-lipped against the drystone wall, "—she might as well be carrying a lit lantern through a dark armory. They'd feel her a mile off."

"Then we don't go through Harrowgate."

"There *is* no other way through. That's why they built the garrison there."

"Then we go *over*." I heard my own voice come out flat and certain, and I barely recognized it. It was not the voice of the woman who had spent ten years dusting silent songbooks and calling it devotion. It was closer to Elder Maren's voice. It was closer, perhaps, to the voice of whoever had made me. "The old drovers' path. Over the fells, north of the town. Your smuggler friends must know it."

Something moved behind Kestrel's eyes—annoyance, yes, but underneath it something that might have been respect, arriving reluctantly, like a debt he hadn't planned to pay. "The Drover's Stair. It's not a path, it's a rumor with rocks on it. Sheep fall off it. Smugglers fall off it. In this season the top will be iced."

"Can you lead it?"

A long pause. The silent birds hopped in their hedge. Somewhere down the valley, a whistle-shaped nothing called sheep that had never learned to answer.

"Yeah," Kestrel said at last, and sighed like a man signing away his last quiet year. "I can lead it."

* * *

That night Ilse burned.

Not with fever, exactly—I put my hand to her forehead and found it no hotter than the rest of her, which frightened me more than fever would have. She burned the way the songbooks had glowed the night she arrived at the Guildhall gate: from inside, faintly, wrongly. Light moved under her skin in slow tides, following no vein I knew, tracing patterns that hurt to look at because they were almost, almost legible—half-formed staves, half-spoken words, the covenant's handwriting showing through her like watermarks through cheap paper.

She was unraveling. I could see it now. The verse she carried was coming loose at its edges, thread by thread, and each loose thread was a note the world would never hear again.

I sat up with her through the whole of the night, her head in my lap, my hand on her burning hair, and I let myself stop lying.

Nine days to the capital, Kestrel had said, if we pushed. But the Drover's Stair would cut it to six, maybe five, if the weather held and none of us fell. Five days. And Ilse had maybe more than that—but not many more. I was not a healer and not a seer, but I had grown up among the last keepers of a dying art, and I knew what a failing song looked like. It looked like this: a child glowing in the dark, spending herself note by note to hold together a world too wounded to notice.

Elder Maren had hidden me in the Hollow so that no one could use me or unmake me. She had traded a hundred years of the world's slow dying for one vessel's safety, and she had died before she could tell me whether she'd been right.

"Well," I said softly, to the sleeping child, to the elder's ghost, to the pale stars over the Withered Marches, "you hid the verse. But nobody hid the singer."

Because that was the thing I had finally understood, sitting in the dark with the covenant's last fragment burning in my lap: it had never only been about Ilse. She was a fragment—a shard, a spark, a single stanza torn loose. But the final verse needed a voice that could carry all of it, hold all of it, *be* all of it. The exiled elders had written a child out of pure song and hidden her away, and they had also, deliberately, clumsily, lovingly, written something else. Something they raised as a daughter of the Hollow and never told. Something with Bram Winterbourne's face.

I was not carrying the song.

I was the song. Ilse was only the proof, the key, the living fragment that had walked out of the storm to find me.

Above the drystone wall, the stars wheeled in their ancient silence. Below, in the whitened fields, voiceless lambs slept beside voiceless ewes. And somewhere north, past the frozen Stair and the warden-gates of Harrowgate, a queen sat in a capital of bells, humming stolen music she did not understand, over a vault full of voices that were not hers.

Five days.

I shifted Ilse gently onto the blanket, rose, and went to wake Kestrel for his watch. He opened his eyes before I touched him—he always did—and read my face in the firelight the way he read everything, quickly and without mercy.

"You've decided something," he said.

"Yes."

"It's a stupid something."

"Probably."

He grunted, rolled to his feet, and threw the last of the bark on the coals. "Tell me tomorrow," he said. "Tonight I'm going to pretend we're still taking the slow road, where people die of old age."

But as I lay back down beside Ilse and pulled the blanket over us both, I saw him pause at the edge of the firelight and look north, toward the black line of the fells and the rumor-with-rocks-on-it beyond, and I saw that he was already measuring the climb.

The Marches had shown me their arithmetic, and I had done the sums, and the answer was simple and merciless: the world was running out of color, and out of voice, and out of time—and the only cure for it was walking north at speed, straight into the teeth of the Crown.

Let the wardens feel her coming. Let them scry their lanterns.

We were done hiding in the dark.
