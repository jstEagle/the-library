## 9. The Choir of Ash

The village did not exist.

Bram understood this the way she understood the Hollow's silence—not with her eyes, which insisted on evidence, but with something older beneath her ribs. The Withered Marches had carried them three days through fields the color of old bone, past orchards that bore fruit with no scent and streams that ran without sound, and on the fourth morning Kestrel stopped at the lip of a shallow ravine and said, "There," pointing at nothing at all.

"There's nothing there," Bram said.

"There's nothing there," Kestrel agreed, grinning his crooked, guarded grin, "which is why nobody's ever found it. Look again. Not with your eyes. You're a Songwright's daughter, or whatever you are. Look with the part of you that hums."

Bram looked. At first the ravine held only ash-grey scrub and the pale suggestion of a track winding down between stones. Then, slowly, as if her attention were a match struck in a dark room, she began to see the seams. The way the shadows fell wrong. The way a thread of woodsmoke rose and unraveled a hand's width above the ground, as if something were editing it out of the world before it could be noticed. The way the silence here was not the Hollow's dead, pressed silence but a *chosen* one—a held breath.

"They've sung themselves invisible," she breathed.

"They've sung themselves *forgotten*," Kestrel corrected. "Different craft. Harder craft. Anyone can hide a house. Hiding a whole village from memory itself—" he shook his head. "The Songwrights who did this wanted to survive the purge, and they understood the only way to do it. Not to fight the Crown's silence. To *become* it."

Ilse tugged at Bram's sleeve. The child's face, usually so still, was lit with something between hunger and fear. She pointed down into the ravine and made a sound—not a word, not in any tongue Bram knew, but a rising phrase, three notes climbing toward a fourth that never arrived. A question. Bram had learned to read the girl's fragments the way she'd once read the dead songbooks, tracing meaning in what was withheld.

*Are they like me?*

"I don't know," Bram said softly, and took her hand. "But we're going to find out."

The path down was longer than it looked. That was the first trick of the place—distance that stretched like taffy, each step consuming more of the world than it should. Bram counted two hundred paces and knew she had walked a mile. The grey light thickened. The scrub gave way to actual green, the first true green Bram had seen since crossing the Hollow's boundary, and she nearly wept at the shock of it, the sheer insolent *color* of grass after days in the bleached Marches.

Then the village opened before them like a secret finally told.

It was small. Thirty houses, maybe forty, built of fieldstone and turf, huddled around a well and a long low hall with a slate roof. Goats grazed behind woven fences. Laundry hung on lines, grey shirts and grey dresses that might have been colorful once and had faded into the landscape on purpose, the way a soldier camouflages. People moved among the houses—men and women in plain wool, faces weathered, eyes downcast—and not one of them, Bram realized with a creeping horror, was *singing*. Not humming. Not whistling. Not even the unconscious half-melodies people carry like breath. The whole village moved in a silence so total it made the Hollow feel noisy by comparison.

"They've cut it out of themselves," Bram whispered.

"Voluntarily," Kestrel said. There was no admiration in his voice. There was something worse—recognition. "Every morning, probably. Some rite. Some discipline. You have to understand what they're doing, Bram. A memory-song doesn't live in a book. It lives in the singer. The Crown's wardens could burn every score in the realm, and as long as one old woman still carried the melody in her bones, the guild survived. So the guild did the only thing left. They amputated."

A dog barked. It was the first sound any living thing had made in their presence, and it cracked the morning open like an egg. Heads turned. Thirty faces, forty, all at once, and Bram felt the weight of their attention like a physical thing—like standing in a doorway when a room full of strangers stops talking.

An old woman came out of the long hall. She was small and straight-backed, with white hair braided in a crown and hands knotted like tree roots, and she walked toward them with the unhurried certainty of someone who had been expecting visitors for fifty years. When she was ten paces away she stopped, and her eyes—pale, almost colorless—went from Kestrel to Ilse to Bram, and stopped there, and stayed.

"Well," the old woman said. Her voice was rough, unpracticed, a door long unused. "You came back."

Bram's mouth went dry. "I'm sorry. I think you've mistaken me for—"

"I have never mistaken anyone for anything in eighty-one years," the woman said. "I was the guild's Keeper of Faces. Before." She said *before* the way other people said a dead child's name. "I memorized every member of the Choir of the Vale. Two hundred and six singers, their faces, their voices, their verses. I was the backup, girl. The archive made flesh, in case the books burned." She took another step closer, and Bram fought the urge to step back. "You have Maren's chin. And Elowen's brow. And that way of standing, weight on the left foot, always, like you're listening to the ground—that's Winterbourne posture, that is. That's *your* posture, girl, whoever raised you."

"My name is Bram Winterbourne," Bram said, and hated how young her voice sounded.

"Yes," the old woman said. "I know. I named you."

* * *

They were given a room in the long hall, and bread, and a thin soup of turnip that tasted like the color grey. Kestrel prowled the perimeter the way he always did, cataloguing exits, and Ilse sat pressed against Bram's side, watching the villagers with wide, unblinking eyes. And the old woman—Sera, she was called, though she said it wasn't her real name, her real name having been one of the first things she'd chosen to forget—sat across the table from Bram and told her the story Bram had spent her whole life not knowing.

"You were born in the spring of the Ash Year," Sera said. "The purge had already taken the western choir houses. The Crown's wardens had a new instrument—resonance nets, they called them, woven from the stolen memory-songs themselves. They could feel a trained voice from a mile away. The guild was dying, and dying fast, and the elders did what elders do. They made a terrible plan."

"They hid the final verse," Bram said. Her voice was flat. "In the Hollow. I know this part. The elder's letters—"

"You know the *shape* of it. You don't know the cost." Sera's root-knuckled hands lay flat on the table, and Bram noticed they were trembling, very slightly, the tremor of a woman holding something down. "The final verse couldn't be written down. A verse of that power, inked on paper, would burn through any page, any mind that tried to memorize it whole. It had to be *carried*. And the Crown's nets were getting closer every day. So the last three elders of the Choir of the Vale did the last working of their lives. They took the final verse out of their own memories—all three of them, cutting it from themselves like surgeons—and they wove it into something that could hold it. Something living. Something that could grow, and learn, and keep the verse safe inside itself without ever knowing it was there."

The soup went cold in Bram's stomach. "No."

"You were born in the guild's birthing room, girl, in the ordinary way, of an ordinary mother—a guild singer named Elowen who volunteered, who was honored, who wept for three days and then handed you over." Sera's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "But before you drew your second breath, three elders sang over you for nine hours. They sang the final verse into your bones. Into the marrow of you. You are not a woman who *knows* the covenant's last verse, Bram Winterbourne. You *are* it. Every part of you that isn't girl is verse. The elders made sure of it. Your fear of singing. Your flat, careful, guarded voice. The way the Hollow's silence felt like a blanket to you when it felt like a coffin to everyone else. All of it was craft. All of it was *design*."

The hall seemed to tilt. Bram gripped the table's edge. "The Hollow," she managed. "The silence. It wasn't to protect the guild's remnants. It was a *sheath*. For me."

"A vault," Sera said. "Built around a child. The elders argued about it for a season—whether any life should be spent as a lock, whether the covenant was worth a girl's whole unlived existence. In the end they decided the alternative was the Crown singing the final verse with stolen voices, and a covenant reforged in chains. They chose your cage over the world's. Whether they were right—" she spread her hands, "—that's not mine to say. I only kept the faces. And yours, child, I never forgot. I tried. We all tried. Forgetting was the discipline that kept us alive. But your face—" her colorless eyes glistened, "—your face was the last thing I let myself keep. I told myself it was operational. I told myself the guild would need to recognize its own vessel someday. Fifty years I've watched the road at the top of the ravine. Fifty years of grey mornings. And now here you are, with her chin and her brow and her listening stance, and I am an old fool who kept one memory too many, and I have never in my life been so glad to be a fool."

Ilse made a small sound. Bram looked down and found the child watching her with an expression of terrible, precise comprehension—the look of someone who had always known she was a fragment, recognizing another fragment, and grieving for her.

"I need a moment," Bram said, and her voice came out in pieces.

She walked out into the village grey. The goats watched her with flat yellow eyes. Somewhere a woman was beating a rug in perfect silence, the blows landing without echo, and Bram thought: *I have spent my whole life in a room someone built to keep me from hearing myself.* Every kindness the elder had shown her—the lessons, the patience, the slow years of tending dead songbooks—every one of it reorganized itself in her memory, and she could no longer tell which parts had been love and which had been maintenance. *You oil a lock. You feed a vault. You keep the vessel healthy.* Had the old man wept when he died because he was leaving her, or because he had finally run out of ways to keep her from asking questions?

*Was anything mine?* Even her name. *Bram.* She turned it over and found Sera's fingerprints on it, and the elders', and underneath them, nothing—a girl-shaped absence where a self should have been.

Except.

Except the girl who had taken in a mud-streaked child against guild law. Except the girl who had crossed the boundary with her heart hammering like a trapped bird. Except the girl standing in this grey village right now, furious and grieving and *still standing*. Whatever the elders had built, this—this stubborn, terrified, choosing creature—this had grown in the spaces they couldn't reach. The verse was in her bones. But the *Bram* was hers.

She became aware of Kestrel beside her. He didn't touch her. He never touched her. He just stood, shoulder a hand's width from hers, looking at the silent woman beating her silent rug, and said, "For what it's worth—I've spent twenty years wondering which parts of me were mine and which were carved out. You never get an answer. You just decide which parts you're keeping."

"That's not comforting."

"No," he agreed. "But it's true, and true's the best I've got."

* * *

That evening, the village broke its discipline.

Bram did not ask them to. She would have sworn, later, that she never asked at all. But word had traveled through the thirty grey houses the way word does in small places, and after the turnip supper, the villagers filed into the long hall and sat on benches facing her, and Sera stood at the front with her root-knuckled hands folded, and said:

"Fifty years we have kept the silence. Fifty years, and the covenant has rotted anyway. The Marches are grey. The beasts are born mute. The Crown's bells ring with dead men's voices. We cut out our art to survive, and survival has bought us nothing but more years of grey." Her voice was steadier now, used now, oiled by the day's talking. "This girl is the guild's last work. And I find, at the end of my life, that I would rather be hanged for one song than die safe and silent. So. For one hour. We remember."

And they sang.

It was not beautiful, at first. That was the thing no one told you—voices amputated for fifty years do not return cleanly. They cracked and wavered and went flat, old throats rusty with disuse, middle-aged voices that had never finished training, and the first phrases of the evening hymn—the guild's oldest song, the one about the two realms and the bridge between them—came out broken, like a wheel turning on a bent axle. Bram stood in the middle of it with her hands over her mouth, tears running freely, because it was the ugliest singing she had ever heard and it was the most beautiful thing in the world, because it was *chosen*, because forty people who had spent half a century sawing off their own wings were standing up on raw stumps and trying to fly.

And then it caught.

Somewhere in the third verse, the voices found each other. The cracks stopped mattering. The harmonies, half-remembered, reached for one another and held, and the hall filled with a sound that had weight and warmth and *color*, and Bram saw it—saw it with the part of her that hums—the sound rising off forty heads like heat-shimmer, gold and grey woven together, and Ilse was standing on a bench with her eyes closed and her small voice threaded into the alto line as if she'd been born to it, as if she *had* been born to it, and the dead songbook in Bram's satchel began to glow through the canvas, softly, like a banked coal remembering fire.

Sera found her after, in the doorway, watching the villagers disperse into the grey evening with their faces wet and their backs straighter.

"You felt it," the old woman said. It wasn't a question.

"Something moved," Bram said. "When they sang. Something in me. Like—" she searched, "—like a key turning near a lock it almost fits."

Sera's face changed. The joy of the evening drained out of it, and what remained was grim. "Then we have less time than I hoped, and I will tell you why. That feeling—the key turning—that is the verse in you *responding*. It responds to true singing. It has been responding your whole life, in small ways, in the Hollow where nothing could reach it. But out here, in the Loud World, with the covenant fraying and every stolen voice in the Crown's archive pulling at the same thread—" she took Bram's hand, and her grip was iron despite her age, "—the verse is waking up, child. And a verse that wakes begins to *sing itself*. The elders built you to be ready. But ready is not the same as willing. And if the Crown's wardens find you before you find the archive—if their resonance nets touch you and the verse answers *them*—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Bram looked down at her own hands, at the faint, impossible shimmer that had begun to gather in her fingertips when the hymn swelled, gold-grey light like dust motes in a sunbeam, and understood that the cage the elders built was not only around her.

It was a countdown.

Behind her, in the long hall, Ilse began to hum the rising three-note phrase again—*are they like me, are they like me*—and this time, for the first time, the fourth note came, and it was Bram's own voice that supplied it.
