## 10. Who Made Me

The fire in the Choir of Ash's longhouse burned low and blue, the way fires burn when they've been fed something other than wood.

Bram sat wrapped in a blanket that smelled of ash and tallow, her hands curled around a clay cup of broth she had not touched. Across the fire pit, the old woman—Sera Vane, they called her, though her true name had been sung away decades ago—watched her with an expression Bram could not name. Not pity. Not wonder. Something closer to recognition, the way one recognizes a face in a portrait one has studied all one's life without knowing who sat for it.

"You have her hands," Sera Vane said again. She had said it three times since they arrived, each time as if testing whether the words would hold their shape. "Elder Maren's hands. The way the fingers rest. The way she held a quill like it was a bird she was afraid to startle."

"I don't know any Elder Maren," Bram said. "I knew Elder Coth. He raised me."

"I know." Sera Vane's voice was gentle, and that gentleness frightened Bram more than cruelty would have. "He was there too."

* * *

The Hollow had always been quiet, but Bram had never thought of its quiet as strange until she was old enough to notice that Elder Coth flinched at sounds.

She was seven, maybe eight, sitting on the Guildhall steps shelling peas while he repaired the roof tiles above her. A crow had wandered into the valley—it happened rarely, once every few years—and when it cawed from the dead oak by the well, Coth dropped his hammer. It clattered down the slope, and Bram laughed at the sight of him scrambling after it, and then stopped laughing, because his face when he turned around was gray as old candle wax.

"It's only a bird," she said.

"There are no birds here," he said, and took the hammer inside, and did not come out again until dark.

She remembered other things now, things she had filed away as eccentricities of age. How he never let her past the boundary stones, not even to pick blackberries on the far slopes where they grew fat and dark. How he checked the seals on his study door every night, pressing his palm flat against the wood as though listening through it. How, when she was twelve and had begged him—*begged* him—to teach her even one line of the guild's songs, he had looked at her with such naked terror that she never asked again.

*"Not you," he'd said. "Never you. You must stay small, Bram. Small and silent and ordinary. Promise me."*

She had promised. She had spent twenty years keeping a promise she never understood, and she had called that keeping *duty*, and *devotion*, and *love*.

Now, watching Sera Vane across the blue fire, she understood it had been none of those things. It had been a lock.

* * *

"Tell me," Bram said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Tell me what I am."

Sera Vane did not answer at once. She rose, slow as a door on rusted hinges, and crossed to a chest against the far wall—a chest Bram recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water. It was a songbook chest, guild-made, iron-banded, sealed with wax stamped by the Choir of Ash's own sigil: a circle of open mouths, singing.

"We were forty-one when the Crown came," Sera Vane said, kneeling at the chest. "Forty-one Songwrights in this enclave alone. We sang the border-wards of the Marches, the rain-covenants, the sleep-songs for the dying. When the purge came, we had two choices. Fight, and be unmade voice by voice—the Crown's wardens were thorough; you'll see their work if you travel east—or forget." She lifted the lid. Inside lay no books, only rows of smooth river stones, each one scratched with a single mark. "We sang our art into these and buried them, and then we sang ourselves empty. A Songwright who cannot remember that she is a Songwright is safe. That was the theory."

"And it worked?"

"For thirty years." Sera Vane drew out one stone, held it to the light. Her hand trembled—not with age, Bram realized, but with longing. "Then your elder came. Coth. And another, a woman named Maren Winterbourne. They brought a bundle wrapped in a guild-cloak, and they asked us for the one thing we had sworn never to do again."

Bram's cup rattled against her knee. *Winterbourne.* Her name. Her name was not a name at all; it was a message, carried forward through a woman she had never met.

"What did they ask?"

"To help them make a child."

* * *

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, Ilse was laughing—the bright, wordless laugh she gave when Kestrel made faces at her, which he pretended to find beneath him and performed with increasing absurdity. Bram heard it and held onto it like a rail in a storm.

"They told us the covenant was failing," Sera Vane went on. "That the Crown's theft of the memory-songs had left it threadbare, and threadbare things tear. They told us the final verse—the verse that binds the whole song, the keystone—had to be kept somewhere the Crown could never reach. Not hidden. *Unreachable.* And there was only one place in either realm where magic could not follow." She looked up. "Your Hollow."

"The valley was built for me." Bram heard herself say it flatly, as though reporting weather. "It isn't a sanctuary. It's a vault. And I'm the thing in it."

"The valley was built *around* you," Sera Vane corrected softly. "There is a difference, though I grant it is a thin one."

"How?" Bram set the cup down because her hands had begun to shake. "How do you make a child out of a verse?"

Sera Vane's eyes went distant, and for a moment Bram saw what the woman had been before the forgetting: someone formidable, someone whose voice had once moved stone and season. "A memory-song holds a voice. A covenant holds a world. The final verse of the covenant was written to be sung by a living throat—but verses can be given flesh, if the singers are willing to spend everything they have. Maren and Coth and the others who survived the first purges—they gathered what remained of the guild's power. Every scrap. Every note we hadn't yet buried. And they wove it into a living form and set the final verse at the center of it, the way you'd set a seed in soil." Her gaze settled on Bram's face, and it was unbearable, being looked at like that—like a text, like a relic, like an answer. "You are not a girl who carries the verse, child. You *are* the verse. Everything else—the hands, the grief, the stubbornness—is the soil it grew in."

* * *

That night, unable to sleep, Bram walked out into the colorless fields beyond the exile camp and stood among the bleached wheat stalks that bent in a wind she couldn't feel.

*Everything else is the soil.*

She thought about her life as though reading a stranger's journal. The girl who liked the rain because it was the loudest thing in the Hollow. The girl who talked to the songbooks even though they never answered, who pressed her ear to their covers hoping to feel a heartbeat. The girl who had mourned Elder Coth not only for himself but because his death meant she would die too, someday, in that silence, having never once been loud.

Had any of it been hers? Or had Coth shaped her grief and her gentleness the way a gardener shapes a hedge—pruning here, forcing there—so that the vessel would remain intact? Had he loved her, or had he *maintained* her?

And the worst question, the one that circled back no matter how she drove it off: if she was made, then was her love for Ilse made too? Was her fear of the threshold a flaw in the crafting, or the truest thing in her?

She found she could not tell the difference anymore between the questions and the wind.

* * *

Ilse found her there near dawn. The child came barefoot through the pale wheat, humming her fragment-melody—the one that had made the dead songbooks glow—and when she reached Bram she did not speak, because she could not, but she pressed her small warm hand flat against Bram's sternum, right over the heart, and hummed something new. Three notes. Low, then high, then low again.

Bram had learned enough of the Verse-tongue by now to know what those notes meant. There was no word-for-word translation; the old language worked in movements, not meanings. But the movement of those three notes was unmistakable.

*You are still you. You are still you. You are still you.*

Or perhaps: *the same note played in different rooms is still the same note.*

Bram knelt in the wheat and pulled the child against her and cried the way she had never allowed herself to cry in the Hollow—loudly, badly, with her whole body—because the silence of the valley had taught her that grief was a thing to be tidied, and here, in the loud broken world, she discovered it was a thing to be *sung*.

Ilse held her and hummed, patient as tide, until the storm passed.

* * *

In the morning, Sera Vane led her to the enclave's buried cache. They dug in the grey earth behind the longhouse, and Sera Vane lifted out a stone unlike the others: larger, veined with silver, warm to the touch even after thirty years underground.

"This is yours," she said. "Maren left it. She said—if the child ever comes asking, give her this, and let her decide what she is."

Bram took it. The moment her skin met the stone, sound flooded her—not through her ears but through her bones, a voice like winter sunlight, a woman's voice, tired and fierce and achingly familiar in a way that made no sense because Bram had never heard it before:

*Bram. If you're hearing this, then Coth is gone, and the vault has opened, and I am sorry—I am so sorry—for every year of silence we bought with your life. We did not make you to be used. We made you so that no one else could ever be. Remember that, whatever they tell you. Remember that you were not our tool. You were our last act of hope, and hope is a thing that chooses. So choose, little verse. Choose what you'll be.*

The voice faded. The stone went cool in her hands.

Bram stood in the grey field with tears freezing on her cheeks, and felt something settle in her that was not peace, exactly, but was adjacent to it—the feeling of a question finally asked aloud.

She was made. That was true, and it would hurt for a long time, maybe forever. But Maren had said *choose*, and choosing, Bram realized, was the one thing no maker could do for their creation. Coth had built the cage. Maren had built the key. Neither of them could walk through the door.

Only she could.

Behind her, boots crunched on bleached wheat. Kestrel, arms crossed, face unreadable in the colorless dawn light, with Ilse perched on his shoulders like a small pale flag.

"Word from my contact in the east," he said. "Crown wardens crossed the Marches two days ago. They're following the child's resonance." His eyes flicked to the stone in Bram's hands. "And there's worse. The queen's chapel bells rang at midnight, all of them, unscheduled. My contact says they were ringing *your melody*. Badly. Like someone practicing."

Bram closed her fingers around the stone until the edges bit her palm.

Someone in the capital already knew the final verse. Someone was already trying to sing it.

"Then we leave now," she said. "Before they get it right."
