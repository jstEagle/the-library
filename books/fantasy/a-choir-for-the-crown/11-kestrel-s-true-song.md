## 11. Kestrel's True Song

The debt came due on the third night in the Withered Marches, and Kestrel had been counting down to it like a man counting steps to his own hanging.

They made camp in the ribs of a dead waystation—a tollhouse once, back when the border road meant something, before the color drained out of the fields and the trade dried up with everything else. The roof was gone. The walls stood hip-high and crooked, blackened beams jutting up like the fingers of something that had tried to claw its way out of the ground and failed. Kestrel had chosen it because it had one corner that still held warmth, and because from the broken doorway he could see the whole approach across the gray flats. Old habits. A boy who'd been taken once did not get taken twice.

Ilse slept first, curled against Bram's pack with her hands folded under her cheek like a small closed door. The girl hadn't spoken—not in any tongue Kestrel knew—but she'd stopped humming as much. Each day her songs came thinner, frayed at the edges, a tapestry coming undone thread by thread. He didn't need Bram's guild-learning to understand what that meant. Things that sang were dying out here, and the child was mostly song.

Bram sat across the fire from him, mending a strap on her boot with stubborn, precise little stitches, and Kestrel watched her the way he watched all dangerous things: openly, so no one could accuse him later of not having looked.

She wasn't beautiful in the way court women were beautiful—arranged, curated, lit. She was beautiful the way a blade is: honest about what it was for. Her hair was coming loose from its braid. There was ash on her sleeve. And when she looked up from her work and caught him staring, she didn't blush or look away. She just raised an eyebrow, waiting for him to explain himself.

He didn't. He never explained himself. That was half of how he'd survived.

"You're doing it again," she said.

"Doing what?"

"Deciding whether I'm worth the price you paid."

Kestrel turned his hands toward the fire. They were good hands, quick ones; they'd picked locks in four provinces and caught falling children and once, long ago, held sheet music. He kept them busy now, feeding a twist of dried moss into the flames.

"A bargain's a bargain," he said. "I don't renege. It's the only religion I've got."

"I know." She set down the boot strap. "That's why I'm going to pay tonight. Before we reach the capital. Before there are walls between us and wardens and whatever else is coming. If I wait until after—if I let you carry me through all of that on the promise—you'll have built it up into something I can't survive owing."

He laughed, and hated how thin it came out. "You think your voice is going to break me, Songwright? I've heard the cathedral choirs in Bellharbor. Two hundred voices in gilded stone. I've heard the queen's own cantors sing the Ascension Mass with gold leaf peeling off the rafters." All true. All beside the point, and he suspected she knew it. "What could you possibly give me that they couldn't?"

Bram looked at him for a long moment. The fire moved in her eyes.

"You already know," she said quietly. "Don't you. You've known since the boundary wall. Since you heard Ilse humming through the rain and went white as bone."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"You flinched," Bram said. "Every time. Every time she sings, you turn away like someone's shown you a scar. I've spent my whole life around people who lost things to silence, Kestrel. I know the shape of it."

The fire popped. Out past the dead walls, the Marches lay flat and colorless under a sky bruised purple at the edges, and somewhere far off a nightbird opened its beak and produced nothing at all—the covenant's failure made flesh, animals born without voices, fields without green. A land where everything had been sung out of.

Kestrel found that his mouth had gone dry.

"Fine," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Fine. Sing, then. One song. As agreed. But I'm telling you now, if this is some guild trick—if there's a hook in it—I'll walk out of this camp and take my chances with the wardens."

"There's no hook," Bram said. "There's only the truth, and it doesn't need one."

* * *

She stood to sing. He'd expected that; the Songwrights, according to every story he'd ever pried out of a drunk archivist, sang standing, feet bare or grounded, spine straight as a plumb line. But she didn't do any of the ritual things he'd imagined. She didn't close her eyes or raise her arms or invoke anything. She just stood in the ruined doorway with the fire behind her and the dead country in front of her, and she looked at him—only at him—and asked:

"What did you lose? Not the thing itself. The shape of it. Tell me that much, and the song will find the rest."

And Kestrel, who had lied to smugglers and wardens and priests and queens' men and his own reflection for twenty years, heard himself answer honestly, because apparently the debt cut both ways and he'd owed this longer than he'd owed her.

"I was a choirboy," he said.

The words came out of him like water from a cracked dam—slow at first, then all at once, and he couldn't have stopped them if he'd wanted to, and God help him, he didn't want to. He wanted to say them. He had never once wanted to say them, and now he could not stop.

"Not one of the pretty ones, either. The kind they keep in the back rows because the voice is true even if the face isn't. Crown Chapel of the Ascension, lower gallery, second cantor from the left. I could hold a note steadier than boys twice my size. Master Ellery said I had 'an instrument,' which is what they call it when they've started thinking of you as a thing instead of a person. I didn't mind. I liked being a thing that made beauty. It was better than being a nobody that swept floors."

Bram had gone very still. Even the fire seemed to quiet for her.

"When I was eleven," he said, "the king's grandfather needed singers for the new archive. That's the story, anyway—that's the story they told us. That the memory-songs required living resonance to bind them, that it was an honor, that our families would be provided for. My mother cried when they took me up the tower stairs. I thought she was proud." He laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, nothing anyone would mistake for laughter. "They put us in a circle under the chapel. There were thirty of us. We sang what they gave us to sing, and the crystal vials drank it out of us mid-phrase, and—" 

His hand rose to his throat without his permission. He forced it back down.

"You don't feel pain. That's the part no one tells you. You just feel the note leave and never come back, like a door closing in a house you're still standing inside. One by one. Boy after boy. Some of them screamed afterward—they still had their screams, screaming's not singing—and some of them just sat down on the floor and never really got up again. I walked out. I walked out on my own feet and collected the coin they gave my mother and I ate that winter." He looked at the fire because he could not look at Bram. "I've eaten on it every winter since. That's the part I've never told anyone. I *lived*. They paid for my voice and I took the money and lived, and I have been hollow in exactly the place where the music used to be for twenty years, and I have told myself every story a man can tell himself—that it was an honor, that it was necessary, that I would have died otherwise, that the realm needed the songs more than I needed to sing—and none of them fit. None of them fill it. There's just the hole, and the hole has the exact shape of a note I was holding when they took it."

Silence. The Marches pressed in around the ruin, vast and voiceless, and Kestrel realized his eyes were wet and did not care, or told himself he did not care, which amounted to the same performance with worse reviews.

"So," he said. "Sing me whatever you like, Songwright. Nothing's getting into that hole. I've checked."

* * *

Bram didn't stand in the doorway anymore. At some point she had crossed the camp, and now she knelt in front of him, close enough that he could see the fire reflected doubled in her eyes, and her expression was not pity. He would have forgiven pity eventually; people were weak that way. This was something else. Recognition. The look of one locked room regarding another.

"The vials," she said. "In the archive beneath the chapel. The elder's letters described them. Crystal, sealed with wax the color of old blood, each one holding a captured singer's voice." She paused. "Thirty boys."

"Fifty-one," Kestrel said. "Over three harvests. But yes."

"And you think the hole can't be filled." Her voice was low, careful, the way she spoke to Ilse. "Because you've carried it so long you've mistaken it for yourself."

"Don't." He leaned back. "Don't do the gentle thing. I'll walk."

"Then I'll do the true thing." She drew a breath, and he felt it in the air between them—the way he always felt it now, the faint pressure change, the sense of weather gathering, that followed her whenever the song came near. Magic didn't work on him anymore; whatever they'd left in him, they'd also hollowed out the channels it ran through. But he could feel the wind of it. He could feel it the way a man with no legs feels rain.

"This isn't a spell," Bram said. "I want you to understand that first. There's no craft in it. No guild technique. It's just a song my elder taught me when I was nine years old, and she told me it wasn't for performing. She said: *this one is only for giving.* I have never sung it for anyone. I've been waiting, without knowing it, for someone whose shape I recognized."

She began.

It was not a large song. That was the first shock of it. After his speech, after twenty years of wound dressed up as anecdote, he had braced for grandeur—for the cathedral thing, the two-hundred-voice thing, the thing that would try to drown the hole with volume. Instead she sang small. Four notes, then five, rising and settling like a bird deciding where to land. The melody repeated, and each time it repeated it gave something away—one note softer, one note offered rather than held—and Kestrel understood with a lurch that this was a song shaped like handing something over. Like opening your fist.

And it slid into the hole.

Not filling it—he'd been right, nothing filled it, the hole was architectural, load-bearing, part of the structure of him now. But the song went *into* it, the way light goes into a well without touching bottom, and for the first time in twenty years Kestrel could see the shape of the emptiness from the inside. Could see that it had edges. Could see that an edge is the beginning of a container, and a container is the beginning of a vessel, and a vessel—

"—can be filled again," he said aloud, without meaning to, his voice cracking apart on the word.

Bram sang on. Her eyes were open and fixed on his, and tears ran down her face without changing her expression at all, the way rain runs down stone, and he understood suddenly that she was not singing *at* him. She was singing *with* him. The melody had a space in it—a rest, a deliberate absence, a held breath where a second line was meant to enter—and the space was shaped exactly, exactly, like a stolen voice. Like the note he'd been holding when the vial drank it. Like the door in the house.

The song ended the way tides go out: not stopping, just withdrawing, leaving the sand changed.

Kestrel discovered he was on his knees. He didn't remember moving. The fire had burned low and Bram was still in front of him, close enough to touch, and Ilse was awake—he saw that with distant horror, the child sitting up with her blanket pooled around her, watching them with those old, old eyes—and none of it mattered. None of it reached him. What reached him was the rest in the song, still ringing in the hole, still shaped like him.

"My voice might still be in there," he whispered. "In the vault. Under the chapel. Fifty-one vials, and mine could be one of them."

"It could be."

"If I take it back—" He stopped. He'd thought about this, in the dark stretches of the road, more than he'd admitted even to himself. "There's a cost. There's always a cost with guild-work. What does it cost, Bram? To take a voice out of a vial after twenty years?"

She didn't flinch from the question, which was its own cruelty. "I don't know. The elder's letters didn't say. Memory-songs are woven from the moment they captured—the voice carries the memories of the singing. Rejoining them after so long..." She shook her head slowly. "It might mean taking back everything the song remembers. Everything from that day in the circle. Or it might mean—" she hesitated, hating the words, hating herself for saying them— "it might mean losing what's come since. The vial holds what it holds. Twenty years of your life happened outside it, Kestrel. Outside the song."

The road unspooled behind him. Three weeks of it. The boundary wall and the rain and Ilse's thinning hymns. Bram's stubborn stitches by the fire. The way she'd said *there's only the truth, and it doesn't need one.* Fifty-one vials in a vault beneath a chapel, and somewhere among them, maybe, the missing piece of him—and the price of reaching for it might be the first weeks of his life that had ever felt like they belonged to a whole man.

"That's a filthy bargain," he said hoarsely.

"Yes," Bram agreed softly. "Most true ones are."

He looked at her. The fire had nearly died; the last coals threw her face into relief and shadow, and she was watching him with that blade-honest attention, and Kestrel understood that she had known—from the beginning, from the tollhouse, maybe from the boundary wall—exactly what she was offering and exactly what it would cost, and she had sung it anyway, because someone had once taught her that certain songs were only for giving.

"Twenty years," he said, "and the best part of my life fits in three weeks. Isn't that obscene."

"It's human," she said. "Obscene would be pretending otherwise."

He laughed—really laughed, the sound rusty and enormous in the ruined tollhouse—and Ilse smiled her small closed-door smile, and Kestrel reached out and took Bram's hand, rough palm to rough palm, and neither of them made anything sentimental of it. It was simply two hollowed places acknowledging the same architecture.

"All right," he said. "New terms. I get you to the capital. I get you into the vault. And if my vial's down there, I decide at the threshold whether to reach for it—with both of you standing witness, so I can't lie to myself about which life I'm choosing." He squeezed her hand once and let go. "Deal?"

"Deal."

He should have slept then. The watch was his, the road tomorrow was long and warden-haunted, and a smart man banked the fire and shut his eyes. Instead Kestrel sat with his back against the dead wall while Bram tucked the blanket around the sleeping child, and he hummed.

Badly. Barely. Four notes, wavering, tuneless as a hinge.

But the hole had edges now, and something in him was learning their measure, and far to the east—past the colorless flats, past the last hills of the Withered Marches—the bells of Bellharbor were ringing midnight over the capital, fifty-one voices deep, and one of them, he swore to God, rang in a key he almost remembered.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, and Kestrel led them east along the old toll road, walking faster than he needed to, because the Marches were behind them by midday and the capital's smoke was a smudge on the horizon by dusk, and because a man who has finally heard the shape of his own emptiness walks quickly toward the place where it was made.

He did not yet know that the vault had a keeper. He did not yet know that the queen herself walked those corridors at night, listening at the sealed doors. And he could not have known—as the three of them crested the last ridge and the City of Bells opened below them, golden in the dying light, every tower humming with stolen song—that the first thing to greet them on the wind from the west was a melody.

Her melody. Bram's melody. The final verse of the covenant, rising ragged and wrong from the royal chapel, sung badly by someone who had no right to it.

Kestrel heard Bram stop breathing beside him, and he took her hand without asking, and they went down together into the loud world.
