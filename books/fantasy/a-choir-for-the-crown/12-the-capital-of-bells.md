## 12. The Capital of Bells

The city announced itself long before we saw it.

We had climbed the last ridge at dawn, Kestrel ahead on the switchback trail with his collar up and his hood low, Ilse riding his shoulders with her arms hooked under his chin like a child who had decided he was furniture. I came last, as I always did now—partly to watch the road behind us, partly because my legs had learned the Withered Marches' pace and refused to hurry out of spite.

Then the sound reached us.

Not a bell. Not yet. A hum, deep and layered, rising through the soles of my boots before it ever touched my ears, as if the mountain itself had been strung like an instrument and someone enormous had drawn a bow across its spine.

Ilse felt it first. She went rigid against Kestrel's back, her small hands tightening in his collar, and she made the sound she made when words failed her—a thin, urgent note, two tones climbing, the Verse-tongue's shorthand for *danger* or *hunger* or *come quickly*, depending on the context the rest of the melody supplied. There was no rest of the melody. Just that one warning, repeated.

"I know," I said. "I hear it too."

Kestrel stopped and turned, and even through the road grime and the gray exhaustion of three weeks' travel, his face had the look of a man arriving at a debt collector's door. "That's not how it used to be," he said quietly. "When I was a boy, you heard the bells *ring*. Proper ringing, on feast days, and the rest of the time they kept their peace. This—" He gestured at the horizon, at the sound pouring over us like weather. "This is them never stopping."

"They're singing," I said. And then, because I could no longer pretend otherwise: "They're singing guild-songs."

I had known, in the abstract way one knows a wound is mortal. I had grown up on the elder's stories—the purge, the theft, the memory-songs carried off to the capital in iron-bound chests while the Hollow sealed its gates. But knowing a thing and feeling it move through your own bones are different countries entirely. The hum in the mountain was *Avelet's Cradle-Song*, the lullaby every Songwright learned first, the one Elder Marenne used to hum over her mending. It had been bent, stretched, forced into a register it was never written for, but underneath the distortion it was unmistakable—my guild's music, wearing a stranger's voice, played on an instrument made of stolen throats.

Ilse slid down from Kestrel's shoulders and stood facing the city with her fists at her sides, and for the first time since the valley she looked less like a child than like a soldier reviewing a battlefield.

* * *

Vellamere wore its bells like a crown wears jewels: everywhere, excessive, impossible to ignore.

From the ridge we had seen only smoke and spires. Up close, the city resolved into something older and stranger—a sprawl of pale stone built up the flanks of three hills, terraced and towered, and on the summit of each hill stood a bell tower, and between those three great towers stood a dozen lesser ones, and threaded among the streets like veins were smaller belfries still, some no wider than a chimney, hanging single bells over doorways and market squares and bridges. Every one of them was moving. Not swinging—*vibrating*, humming in their frames, so that the whole skyline shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers above a fire.

And every one of them was singing stolen music.

I counted four guild melodies in the first hour alone. There was the processional they'd warped into a marching anthem outside the east gate; there was the *Lament for the Drowned Choir* chiming flat and broken from a bridge belfry; there was, God help me, the *Verse of Opening*—which had once unlocked the covenant's gentlest workings—now ground down into a rhythm that made shop shutters rattle and dogs whine and press themselves flat against walls. The songs hadn't just been taken. They'd been *used*. Bent to purposes their composers never dreamed of, hammered into tools, until what remained of them was shape without soul, a skeleton of melody walked around on strings like a puppet.

"It's loud," Ilse said.

Kestrel nearly dropped the bundle he was carrying. Three weeks of silence, of her speaking only in fragments and song, and her first full sentence in the common tongue landed on him in the middle of a crowded street outside the Pilgrim's Gate.

She said it again, frowning, testing the words like loose teeth. "It is very loud here. Everything is loud." She looked up at me, and her eyes—those old, old eyes in that young face—were frightened. "The songs are screaming, Bram. They don't want to be doing this."

"Keep your voice down," Kestrel hissed, already moving us toward the shadow of a wine merchant's awning, his body angled instinctively between Ilse and the street. He'd done that a hundred times since the Marches; I'd stopped remarking on it, but I noticed it still, the way you notice a heartbeat. "Especially here. Especially *that* voice. Half the wardens in this city can't find their own boots, but the resonance-hunters—" He touched his own throat, briefly, unconsciously. "They listen for exactly what she sounds like."

"What does she sound like?" I asked, though I knew.

He gave me a look over his shoulder. "Like the thing they're all humming."

* * *

We entered Vellamere as pilgrims, because pilgrims were the one traffic the gate-wardens waved through without questions. Kestrel had produced three faded gray cloaks from some smuggler's cache in the Marches, along with wooden medallions stamped with the sunburst of the Church of the Unbroken Bell—an institution I gathered had flourished precisely because the real bells had gone mad, offering quiet contemplation to citizens whose heads never stopped ringing.

"Pilgrimage of Quiet Thanks," Kestrel coached us in a murmur as we joined the line at the gate. "You've come to pray at the Chapel of Stillness in the palace quarter. You're mother and daughter." A nod at me, then a flicker of something apologetic at Ilse. "And the little one is mute. Blessed by the Bell, they'll call it. Some sects think muteness is holy. Wear it like a badge, girl. Stare at people. Monks love that."

"I am not your daughter," I said.

"You are today." He pulled his hood lower. "In Vellamere, everyone is someone's cousin until the paperwork says otherwise. Play the part."

The warden at the gate was young and bored and had the glazed eyes of a man who had heard the city's hum so long it had become silence to him. He glanced at our cloaks, our medallions, Ilse's solemn stare—and waved us through with two fingers.

Walking into Vellamere was like walking underwater, if the water were made of sound. The pressure of it built as we descended from the gate into the streets—layer upon layer of bent melody, harmonics colliding, the great hill-towers droning their foundations while the little belfries scattered discordant grace-notes across the whole. Ordinary citizens moved through it without looking up. A fruit seller argued cheerfully over the price of plums beneath a bell tolling a funeral dirge that had once been a wedding song. Children skipped rope to the rhythm of the anthem. No one flinched. That was somehow worse than fear would have been. Fear, at least, would have meant they remembered what the music was supposed to be.

Ilse pressed close to my side, her hand finding mine, small and hot. Through our linked fingers I felt her trembling—not with fear, I realized, but with recognition. Every stolen song we passed, she knew. Her body knew it the way my body knew my own name. Each distorted melody scraped across her like a hand dragged the wrong way through hair, and she bore it, jaw tight, because she understood better than any of us that these songs were her kin, imprisoned and put to work.

At the mouth of the Chantry Street, where the palace quarter began its climb, a procession crossed our path.

Wardens—but not the bored gate kind. These moved in files of six, armored in gray lacquer, faces masked, and where a crest should have been each breastplate held instead a small brass bell, soldered flat, silent. Between the files walked a prisoner. A woman, middle-aged, barefoot, her wrists bound with cord that hummed faintly—resonance-cord, I realized, tuned rope, the kind that sang when magic passed near it. Her mouth was bound with a strip of the same material.

She was humming anyway. Through the gag, through the bindings, through whatever pain they had already worked on her, she was humming, and the tune was *Marenne's Vigil*, the evening prayer of the Songwrights' Guild, and the resonance-cord around her wrists had begun to glow.

The masked warden nearest her raised one gloved hand, and the woman's humming cut off mid-note as if her throat had been closed by a hand. She swayed. The file marched on. The crowd flowed around the procession like water around a stone, eyes carefully elsewhere.

Beside me, Kestrel had gone very still, and very white.

"Unregistered resonance," he said, so quietly I barely caught it. "That's what they call them. Singers who won't sing for the Crown." His jaw worked. "There used to be hundreds in this city. Choir schools, street cantors, temple voices. Now if you can carry a tune in the shower, you register with the Office of Bells or you learn to be silent." He laughed, short and terrible. "Or you leave. Or you end up barefoot on Chantry Street."

"You knew," I said. "Before. You knew what they do here."

"I told you I was born here." He started walking again, fast, pulling us into the pilgrim current flowing uphill. "I told you everything that mattered. Come on. Chapel of Stillness opens to pilgrims at noon. We want to be inside the palace quarter before the afternoon shift change, when the wardens count noses twice and get both counts wrong."

* * *

The Chapel of Stillness sat at the shoulder of the central hill, wedged between a treasury annex and the barracks of the Bellguard, its whitewashed walls deliberately, ostentatiously plain—the one building in the palace quarter with no bell, no chime, no hum. Inside, it was cool and dim and smelled of candle wax and old stone, and the moment the doors closed behind us, the city's roar dropped away to a distant pulse, like hearing the sea through a wall.

Pilgrims knelt in scattered rows. We took places near the back, beside a pillar, and I knelt on cold stone and tried to pray to gods I didn't believe in while my heart hammered out a rhythm of its own.

Through the chapel's eastern window, half-hidden behind an altar screen, I could see the royal chapel of the palace proper—its gilded spire, its single enormous bell, dark bronze, motionless in its frame. Motionless, and yet. As the afternoon light shifted, I began to feel it. A pull. Faint, at the edge of perception, like a word on the tip of the tongue.

The great bell wasn't ringing. It was *listening*.

"Bram." Ilse tugged my sleeve. She had gone rigid again, head tilted, and her lips moved silently, shaping syllables of the Verse-tongue. "Bram. Listen. Listen with your *inside* ears."

I closed my eyes. Let go of the chapel, the stone, the fear. Reached for the sound beneath the sound, the way Marenne had taught me—or tried to teach me, in a valley where there was nothing to listen to but wind.

And there it was.

Underneath all the stolen clamor of the city, beneath the droning towers and the discordant belfries, there was another thread. Thin. Pure. Rising from the direction of the royal chapel like smoke from a hidden fire.

A melody. Slowly unfolding, phrase by phrase, sung by a single voice—trained, disciplined, and utterly wrong for it, forcing notes that wanted to soar into a cramped and careful box.

I knew the melody.

Of course I knew it. I had known it before I knew my own name, which, I had learned in the Choir of Ash, was closer to the truth than I'd ever guessed. It was the melody that lived in me the way marrow lives in bone. The final verse of the covenant—the verse the elders had folded into flesh and smuggled into the Hollow, the verse I had spent twenty-three years not knowing I was.

Someone was singing my verse. In the royal chapel. Badly.

The world narrowed to that thread of sound. I was aware distantly of Ilse's hand gripping mine hard enough to hurt, aware of Kestrel saying something—my name? a warning?—aware of the kneeling pilgrims and the candles and the vast indifferent weight of the palace above us. None of it mattered. There was only the voice, mangling my music, dragging my verse through registers it was never shaped for, flattening its turns, missing its heart entirely, the way a man who has read a map can walk a country but cannot know it.

Whoever it was had the notes. Someone had transcribed the final verse—impossible, unless the elders' hiding had failed somewhere, unless a fragment had escaped the vault, unless—

Unless they'd had a piece of me all along.

The violation was physical. It crawled up my spine like ice water. My song, my self, handled by strangers. Sung by hirelings. Bent toward purposes I couldn't begin to guess.

And beneath the violation, colder and quieter, something else settled into place. Clarity, terrible and clean, falling over my mind like frost forming on glass.

They didn't have the whole verse. That much was obvious from the botched phrasing—they had the outline, the skeleton, and no matter how many choirs they fed it to, it wouldn't take, because a verse made flesh cannot be sung from paper. They had been trying. For years, maybe generations, the Crown had stood in that chapel night after night, throwing stolen scraps at the covenant's lock and wondering why the door wouldn't open.

They needed me.

The realization didn't frighten me. That was the strangest part. Standing in a borrowed cloak in an enemy's chapel, I felt the fear drain out of me and leave behind something harder. For twenty-three years I had been nobody—the keeper of embers, the last of nothing, a woman inherited by a legacy too dead to bury her. And all that time, in this city, men in masks had been hunting unregistered resonance and binding singers' wrists, because somewhere beneath the gilded spire, the most powerful throne in the world was missing exactly one thing.

Me.

The voice in the chapel faltered. Stopped. Began again, from the top, patient as a clock.

"Bram." Kestrel's whisper, right at my ear, urgent now. "Your face. Whatever you're hearing, un-hear it. There's a deacon coming down the aisle, and he's looking at us, and you're glowing."

I opened my eyes. He was right. Along my forearms, beneath the gray sleeves, light was moving—not blazing, not yet, but a faint gold shimmer, the same luminescence I'd seen on Ilse's skin in the storm the night she arrived, and the kneeling pilgrims nearest us had begun, very slowly, to turn.

The deacon had stopped walking. His hand was drifting toward the rope that rang the chapel's small alarm bell.

Ilse stood up. Deliberately, calmly, in front of the whole congregation, she opened her mouth and sang—one clear note of the Verse-tongue, pitched soft and low, a phrase I recognized from the oldest pages of the guild's silent books: *sleep, sleep, the shepherd watches.*

Every candle in the Chapel of Stillness guttered at once. The pilgrims slumped gently where they knelt, breathing slow, faces slack with sudden peace. The deacon sat down on the altar steps with a sigh of profound contentment and folded his hands.

Ilse looked at me over her shoulder, ancient eyes bright with adrenaline and fury.

"Run now?" she said.

"Yes," I said. "*Now.*"

We ran.
