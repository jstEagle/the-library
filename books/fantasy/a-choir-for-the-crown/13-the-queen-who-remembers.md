## 13. The Queen Who Remembers

Queen Maren had not slept in the royal bed in eleven years.

She slept instead in the old nursery wing, in a narrow room that had once belonged to a governess, with a window that faced the chapel and its bell tower. From there she could hear the bells at every hour—the deep bronze voice of the Coronation Bell, the silver chatter of the Vespers set, the small cracked bell they called the Orphan that rang only when someone in the palace died. She had grown up believing the bells were beautiful. She had spent forty years learning what they actually were: stolen voices, stretched on wheels of brass, made to sing songs that were never theirs.

Tonight they were singing wrong again. She could hear it even through the glass—a thin sourness under the midnight peal, like a choir with one singer dying quietly in the back row. The wardens called it weathering. The bell-masters called it settling of the metal. Maren knew better. The songs inside the bells were fraying, because songs kept prisoner frayed, the way any captive thing frays. Her grandfather had never understood that. He had thought magic was a treasury, something to be seized and stored and spent by the Crown alone, and he had built his vault beneath the chapel and filled it with crystal vials, each one holding a voice taken from a Songwright, each one a little sun of sound that lit the realm's roads and mended its fields and kept the borders sealed against whatever waited beyond them.

And now the little suns were going out, one by one, and the realm was rotting in the dark, and Maren was the third queen to sit on the throne her grandfather had bought with other people's throats.

She stood at the window in her nightgown, an old woman of sixty-one years with her hair down and unbraided, watching the chapel spire cut a black notch out of the stars. On the table behind her lay the night's reports. Withered fields in the Marches, whole counties gone grey as ash. A village in the east where nine children had been born this spring without voices—not mute from injury or illness, but simply without, as though the sound had been skimmed off them before it could settle into flesh. And from the warden-captains, the report that had brought her to the window in the first place:

*Two travelers entered the pilgrim quarter at dusk. A woman past thirty, travel-worn, carrying a child who does not speak. The woman was heard humming at the well by the Tanner's Gate. Three witnesses wept without knowing why. The resonance signature is consistent with Guild work. Possibly pre-Guild.*

*The woman hummed a fragment of the First Verse.*

Maren had read the line four times. Then she had dismissed her ladies, dismissed her guards, dismissed everyone, and stood here in the dark with her hands pressed flat against the cold glass, listening to the bells sing their slow lie over the sleeping city.

Because she knew the First Verse. Not all of it—no living soul knew all of it—but she knew four bars of it, the way you know a lullaby your mother sang before she died. Her grandmother had been a Songwright before the Crown took her. That was the family secret beneath all the family secrets: the woman whose portrait hung in the Long Gallery, serene and jeweled, had once sung the covenant lines that knitted the two realms together like sinew through meat, and Maren's grandfather had married her for her bloodline and then, when he built his vault, had taken her voice along with all the others. He'd told the court she had died of fever. There was no fever. There was a vial.

Maren had found the vial when she was nineteen. She had found it in the deepest row of the archive, in a niche apart from the others, labeled not with a name but with a date—the date of her grandmother's supposed death—and when she had uncorked it, just a crack, just to see, a woman's voice had come out of it like breath from a parted mouth, and it had been humming four bars of a song, over and over, patient as water, and young Maren had stood in the dark between the crystal shelves and wept until she could not stand.

She had corked it again. She had told no one. And she had spent the forty years since telling herself that someday—someday, when the moment was right, when the court was stable, when the wardens could be trusted—she would open the vault and give everything back.

She was still telling herself that. The moment had never come. That was the thing about being queen: you could command ten thousand men, and yet you could not command yourself to do the one thing that mattered, because the doing of it would break everything, and breaking things was the one power a crown did not truly hold.

But the woman by the Tanner's Gate had hummed the First Verse, and the witnesses had wept, and somewhere in Maren's chest an old locked door had swung open on rusted hinges.

*If I do not go to her,* Maren thought, *she will be taken. The wardens will find her before morning. They will put her in irons lined with lead-salt and bring her to me in chains, and I will have to decide, again, as my father decided, as my grandfather decided, whether to spend her.*

She dressed herself. It took longer than it once did; her fingers had gone stiff at the joints, and the lacings defeated her twice before she conquered them. She took no guard, no lamp-bearer, only a hooded traveling cloak the color of wet slate and the small knife she had carried since her coronation, more habit than intention. At the door of the nursery room she paused and looked back at the table, at the reports lying there with their neat warden-script, and she thought about her grandmother's vial, patient in the dark, humming its four bars to no one.

"Not tonight," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the dead, to herself. "Not this time."

* * *

The pilgrim quarter after midnight smelled of tallow and wet wool and the river's mud. Maren had walked these streets before, in her younger years, when grief drove her out of the palace like a fever drives a body from a burning house—always hooded, always alone, always returning before dawn with straw in her hair and something in her chest slightly less clenched than when she left. The city had a different face at night. By day it was hers, obedient, gilded, ringing. By night it was itself: cramped and breathing and full of people she had taxed.

The lodging-house by the Tanner's Gate was called the Drowned Bell, which struck her as either coincidence or omen; she chose not to examine which. Its lower room was nearly empty at this hour—one drunk asleep in his cups, a landlord banking the fire—and the stairs creaked a map of the building as she climbed. The warden report had named the room. Third floor, corner, paid in copper, taken without argument.

She stood outside the door a long moment, hand raised, heart behaving badly for a woman who had faced down two wars and one assassination attempt. Through the wood she could hear—nothing. No snoring, no stirring. Just a silence so complete it had texture, and beneath the silence, so faint she might have imagined it, a hum.

Four bars. Over and over. Patient as water.

Her grandmother's tune. Her grandmother's exact tune.

Maren knocked.

The silence snapped shut. She heard movement then—quick, light, the scrape of a chair, the child's breath catching. When the door opened a hand's width, a woman's eye appeared in the gap, brown and wary and exhausted, ringed with the dust of many miles.

"I'm not buying anything," the woman said, "and we haven't anything worth selling."

"Neither am I selling," Maren said. "May I come in, Bram Winterbourne?"

The eye flinched at the name. Behind it, deeper in the room, a small shape sat up in the narrow bed—a child, seven or eight perhaps, with mud still dried in her hair and eyes too old for her face, eyes that fixed on Maren with an attention that felt less like a child's curiosity and more like a reading. As though the girl could hear something underneath Maren's skin.

"You know my name," the woman said. "Then you know I have no reason to open this door wider."

"I know your name because my wardens are competent and my archive is thorough," Maren said quietly. "I know also that three warden patrols have orders to sweep this quarter before dawn. I know that if they find you, they will not knock. And I know—" her voice caught, and she hated it, an old woman's voice cracking in a pilgrim's lodging-house— "I know that song you're humming. I've known it since I was nineteen years old."

Silence. Then the door opened fully.

* * *

The room held almost nothing: two beds, one stool, a washbasin, a candle burned to a thumb's width. The woman—Bram Winterbourne, though the ledger-name meant nothing, Maren suspected, compared to whatever name the Hollow had given her—stood with her back against the wall, one hand resting on the child's shoulder, and studied Maren with the flat assessing gaze of someone who has learned that authority is a kind of weather and must be survived accordingly.

"You're the queen," Bram said at last. It wasn't a question. "Without your guards."

"Guards would make this a seizure. This is not a seizure." Maren lowered her hood. She watched the recognition move across the other woman's face—the coin-portrait face, older now, grayer, but unmistakable—and saw something complicated follow it: fear, certainly, but also a flicker of something harder to name. Pity, maybe. Or kinship.

"Why are you here?" Bram asked.

"Because in a few hours my wardens will find you anyway, and I wanted you to hear the truth from me before they gave you their version of it." Maren sank onto the stool—her knees were past pretending—and folded her hands, and found, to her surprise, that the speech she had composed on the walk across the city dissolved entirely, leaving only the raw thing underneath.

"The songs you're looking for are real," she said. "They're in my vault. My grandfather took them. He took them from your guild, from singers who trusted the Crown's protection, and he sealed them beneath the chapel and called it the Royal Reserve of Sacred Harmony, and every history book in this kingdom calls it a wise measure against chaos. I grew up on those books. I believed them. I want you to understand that—I believed them right up until the night I opened a vial I should not have opened, and heard my own grandmother's voice coming out of it, singing four bars of the verse you were humming at the well today."

The child made a sound. Small, wordless, but not nothing—a note, low and resonant, like a finger run around a bowl's rim. Both women looked at her. The girl was staring at Maren with an expression of terrible recognition, and her small hand rose halfway toward the queen and stopped there, trembling, as though reaching for something familiar across a great distance.

"She hears it in you," Bram said softly. "The song. Ilse hears songs in people. It's how she speaks."

"Does she." Maren's throat had gone tight. "What does she hear in me?"

Bram tilted her head, listening to the child's silence the way another woman might listen to speech. "Grief," she said finally. "Old grief. And—" a pause, and the queen watched her hesitate, watch her decide to say it honestly— "a cage. She says you're in a cage. She says it politely, in her way."

Maren laughed. It came out broken, half sob, and she let it, because there was no dignity left to protect, not here, not tonight, kneeling metaphorically before a fugitive and a mute child in a rented room above a tannery.

"Yes," she said. "A cage. You've seen the outside of it; I've seen the inside. Do you know what it is to inherit a theft? Not a crime—those can be answered, punished, ended. A theft that became the foundation of everything. If I return the songs, the bells fall silent. The roads lose their binding. The border-seals fail. Half my council will call it the apocalypse and the other half will call it treason, and both halves will be right in part. But if I don't return them—" she spread her hands, and they shook, and she let them— "then look. Look at the Marches. Grey fields. Voiceless children. The covenant your guild sang was the ligament between the realms, and my grandfather cut it and wore it as a belt, and now the limb is dying below the cut, and I am the third monarch to sit here holding the knife and calling it a scepter."

Outside, far off, the Orphan bell rang once—someone in the poor districts had died in the night—and the sound passed through the room like a cold draft. Ilse shivered. Bram pulled the blanket around her shoulders without taking her eyes off the queen.

"So you've come to surrender it," Bram said. "The vault. The songs."

"I've come to beg," Maren said.

The words dropped into the silence and lay there. Bram went very still. Even the child seemed to lean in.

"I am sixty-one years old," the queen said. "I have been crowned for twenty-nine of those years, and in twenty-nine years I have signed every warrant, funded every warden patrol, presided over every harvest festival where the bells sang stolen voices over stolen grain, and told myself each year that next year—next year I would open the vault. Next year I would find the courage. Next year the realm would be calm enough, the council settled enough, my grip secure enough." She shook her head slowly. "There is no enough. There is no next year. There is only this year, and this year the Marches are going grey, and children are being born without voices, and tonight a woman hummed the First Verse at a public well and three strangers wept—which means the song knows it's being sought, which means the covenant itself is reaching for whatever can mend it. The realm is running out of time faster than I am running out of excuses. So I am here, past midnight, in my dead governess's cloak, begging a fugitive Songwright to forgive the Crown that destroyed her order—and to let me help her steal it all back."

Bram stared at her. For a long moment the only sound was the candle flame eating its wick.

"You realize," Bram said at last, quietly, "that I have no reason to trust you. Every story I was ever told about the Crown ends with a Songwright in a vial."

"I know."

"And you realize that if this is a trap—if this is some test of loyalty, some theater staged for my benefit—it's an elaborate one, and I'll want to know why the queen herself would bother."

"It is not theater." Maren reached into her cloak and drew out the thing she had carried through the sleeping streets pressed against her ribs like a second heart: a key. Old iron, long-shanked, worn smooth at the grip. She set it on the washbasin's rim where the candlelight could find it. "That opens the archive stair beneath the chapel. Only three keys exist. One is mine. One hangs around the neck of the Lord Warden, who would sooner die than use it. And one—" her voice steadied, deliberately— "was my grandmother's. The warden records list it as lost. It was not lost. It was hidden, by her, in the one place my grandfather never searched: inside the lining of a christening gown, in the nursery where I sleep tonight. She hid it for whoever came after her. It seems she was waiting for me."

Ilse made her small resonant sound again, closer to a chord this time. Bram looked down at the child, and the child looked up at the queen, and something passed between them that Maren could not follow but could feel, the way one feels thunder through a floor.

"If I take your key," Bram said slowly, "and we're caught in your vault, the sentence won't stop at me. It will reach you. Treason, abdication, worse. Your grandfather's system devours its own queens—that's rather the point of it."

"I have spent twenty-nine years being devoured politely, from the inside," Maren said. "I find I no longer mind the prospect of being devoured honestly." She rose, knees protesting, and stood in the candlelight with her hood pushed back and her gray hair loose, no longer a queen in any way that mattered, only an old woman at the end of her excuses. "Take the key. Take my help. But hear my price, because I do have one, and it is not gold."

Bram waited.

"When you reforge what my grandfather broke—and I believe you intend to, I believe it's why you crossed the world to reach this city—do not restore the old arrangement. Do not sing the covenant back the way it was, with the realms bound and the guild bound and the power gathered into whichever hands hold the song. That arrangement is what made the theft possible. Build something new. Something no single crown can carry off and lock in a cellar." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile, tired and fierce. "My grandmother hid a key in a christening gown because she believed someone after her might choose better than her husband did. Prove her right. That is my price. That is my beggary."

For a heartbeat the room was utterly still—and then Ilse slid off the bed, crossed the floorboards barefoot, and took the queen's stiff hand in both of her small ones, and hummed.

It was not the four bars this time. It was something new—something Maren had never heard, and yet her bones recognized it, the way a bone remembers rain. The candle flame stood up straight. Somewhere below, in the Drowned Bell's common room, the banked fire leapt in its grate. And Queen Maren, third of her name, keeper of the vault of stolen voices, felt something inside her chest that had been clenched for forty years loosen—not all the way, not healed, but loosened, like a fist deciding, just barely, to open.

When the child stepped back, Bram was watching Maren with an expression that had thawed several degrees.

"She says you remember," Bram translated softly. "That's her language. Remembering." She picked the key up from the basin's rim and closed her fist around it. "We move tomorrow night. The archive changes its watch rotation at moonset, and Kestrel has a way in through the bell-foundry yard. If you're truly with us, Your Majesty, then be at the chapel door when the last bell stops ringing."

Maren inclined her head—the old gesture, automatic—and turned to go. But at the threshold she paused, hand on the latch, and spoke without turning around.

"There is one more thing you should know before you descend into that place. In the deepest row, in a niche set apart from the others, there is a vial marked with a date instead of a name. What's inside it has been humming four bars of the First Verse for sixty years, waiting." Now she turned, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "Whatever else happens down there—whatever we find, whatever we lose—promise me that voice goes free. Promise me, and we are allies until the end of this."

Bram met her gaze across the candlelit room, the key gleaming in her fist, the strange child standing silent between them like a held breath.

"On my life," Bram said. "Which may be the same thing."

Outside, the sky had begun, very faintly, to gray. Somewhere across the city a warden's whistle sounded—once, sharp, then answered by a second, nearer—and Maren pulled her hood up over her gray hair and slipped down the stairs of the Drowned Bell like any other old woman going home before dawn, while behind her, in the third-floor corner room, a Songwright blew out the candle and began, very quietly, to pack.
