## 14. The Vault of Stolen Voices

* * *

The chapel above them sang.

Bram felt it through the soles of her boots before they ever reached the door—a vibration in the stone, patient and enormous, like a heart beating under the floor of the world. The royal chapel of Vael Amarth was a white spire visible from every quarter of the capital, and every hour on the hour its bells rang, and every note that fell from those bells was stolen.

She had known it intellectually since Kestrel first told her. Knowing it was different from standing beneath it, feeling her own bones hum with someone else's grief.

"Stay close," Kestrel murmured. He wore the gray robe of a mendicant pilgrim, hood up, and he had not smiled once since they'd entered the palace quarter. "The crypt stairs are behind the reliquary screen. Left of the altar. The wardens change shift at the third bell."

"And you know this how?"

"I was a choirboy here." His voice was flat as slate. "I know where everything is. That's the problem."

Ilse pressed against Bram's side, small and warm and frightened. The child's songs had gone quiet in the capital—too much noise, too much resonance pressing in from all directions—and without them she seemed diminished, an ordinary mute girl lost in an extraordinary place. But her hand found Bram's and gripped it, and that was its own kind of song.

They moved when the third bell began. The sound covered their footsteps—Bram flinched at every toll, feeling each note rake across her skin like a physical thing—and then they were past the altar, past the gilded screen, down a narrow stair that smelled of cold stone and older secrets.

The stair went deeper than any crypt should. Bram counted forty steps, then sixty, then lost count. The air changed. Warmer, somehow, though there was no fire anywhere. And the humming grew—not the bells now but something beneath them, a chord held so long it had become silence, the way a word repeated becomes nonsense.

At the bottom stood a door of black iron worked with the sigil of the Crown: a bell inside a crown inside a circle, the old guild's broken wheel hammered flat at its center.

Kestrel produced a key. Of course he did. "Choirboys clean the archive," he said. "Nobody asks why a child would need to sweep a vault full of dead voices. Nobody wants to think about it at all."

He turned the key. The door opened without a sound, which was somehow worse than groaning hinges.

And Bram looked into the Vault of Stolen Voices.

It was round, carved from living rock, and its walls rose out of sight into darkness. Thousands of niches lined those walls in spiraling rows, floor to unseen ceiling, and in each niche rested a single crystal vial no larger than a fist. Light came from nowhere—from the vials themselves, each one holding a faint glow of a different color: gold and blue and green and violet, some bright, some guttering, some dark as smoked glass.

Thousands of them. Thousands upon thousands.

Bram's breath left her in a rush. She had grown up on the songbooks' descriptions—the memory-songs, the covenant sung into vessels of glass—but description was nothing against this. Each vial was a voice. Each voice had been a person. The guild had never taken voices by force; theirs had been an art of consent, of singers who gave their songs willingly to be remembered. This was not that. This was harvest.

"They're singing," Ilse said suddenly—her first words in days, hoarse, awed. Not aloud. Into Bram's mind, the way the Verse-tongue traveled between them. *"All of them. Quietly. All the time."*

"I know," Bram whispered back. "I hear them too."

Because she did. Standing in the vault was like standing inside a shell and hearing the ocean—except the ocean was made of people. Fragments of melody drifted through the air, half-heard, overlapping: a lullaby in a minor key, a work song with a rhythm like oars, a hymn that climbed and climbed and never quite arrived. And threaded through them all, unmistakable once she knew to listen for it, ran the covenant itself—shredded, scattered, distributed across hundreds of vials like a poem cut into confetti.

"The memory-songs," she breathed. "They're all here. Every verse."

"Not every vault holds every song," Kestrel said quietly. "There are three archives in the capital. This one holds the oldest holdings. The covenant material." He paused, and his jaw tightened. "And the choir collection."

"The what?"

"Come see." His voice cracked on the second word, and he pretended it hadn't.

He led her along the outer spiral, past niche after niche, and stopped at a section marked by a silver band inlaid in the stone. ROYAL CHOIR — FOUNDATION THROUGH PRESENT, read the engraving. The vials here glowed brighter than the others, warmer, more alive. As if they'd been filled recently. As if the people they'd come from might still be walking around somewhere, minus the part of them that mattered most.

Bram read the little brass labels beneath each vial. Names. Dates. Ages.

*Tomas Wren, age nine.* *Lira Sable, age eleven.* *Ansel Fairweather, age ten.*

Children. Row upon row of vials labeled with the ages of children, going back a century, and near the bottom of the list, in handwriting she recognized from his ledger-scrawl because she'd watched him mark their supplies with it—

*Kestrel Vane, age nine.*

She looked up at him. He wasn't looking at the vials. He was looking at the wall beyond them, at nothing, at the middle distance where a person goes when looking directly at something would cost too much.

"It's here," he said. "My voice is here."

* * *

Kestrel had imagined this moment for twenty years, and every imagining had been wrong.

In the tavern imaginings, he kicked the vial off its shelf and ground it under his heel, and the Crown wept. In the honest imaginings—the ones that came at three in the morning on cold roads—he knelt before it like a relic and begged. In none of them did he feel what he actually felt now, standing six feet from a fist-sized piece of glass containing the last true sound he'd ever made:

Nothing. A great ringing nothing, like the inside of a struck bell after the tone fades.

"There it is," he heard himself say, conversationally, as if pointing out a landmark on a road. "Nine years old. They took it the week before the purge reached our chapter. Grandfather King wanted the children's voices especially—pure tones, untrained, easy to bind. I remember thinking it was an honor. I remember *thanking* him."

Bram's hand found his arm. Her grip was careful, the way you hold a bird.

"What happened after?" she asked softly.

"After? Nothing happened after. That's the whole trick of it." He heard his own voice go distant, reciting. "You don't scream. You can't—you've got nothing to scream with. You just... continue. You learn to speak with the leftover parts of your throat, the parts that make words but not music. You spend the rest of your life sounding like everyone else and being nothing like anyone else. Every song you ever loved becomes a wound. Every choir loft you pass makes your teeth ache." He shrugged, a small ugly motion. "You get very good at locks and lies and roads, because roads don't sing."

"You could take it back." Bram was studying the niche, the mechanism of it. "Right now. We could—"

"No."

The word came out harder than he meant. He softened it. "Not yet. We do the job first. The memory-songs, the covenant verses—we get what we came for, we get out, and then—" He didn't finish, because he didn't know how to finish. Then what? Take back a nine-year-old's voice into a thirty-year-old man's ruined throat and discover whether there was anything left of him worth the having?

He'd told himself the story on the road south: that reclaiming the voice would make him whole again, that the hollow would fill. But standing here, he understood something he hadn't let himself understand before. Twenty years of hollowness had shaped him. Whatever poured into that shape would have to be someone new. The boy who'd sung in this chapel was dead; the vial proved it. What lived in front of it was a stranger who'd learned to love the sound of one particular Songwright humming over the campfire, badly, off-key, and who would trade every voice in this vault for—

He shut that thought away. Locked it. Later.

"We should move," he said. "Fourth bell's coming. Wardens do a sweep before—"

Light bloomed at the top of the stair.

Torchlight, yellow and swinging, and with it boots—many boots, descending in disciplined rhythm. Kestrel's blood went to ice water. The sweep was early. Or they'd been followed. It didn't matter which.

"*Hide,*" he mouthed, and pulled Bram and Ilse into the shadowed gap between two spirals of niches, deep in the curve of the wall where the torchlight couldn't follow. There was barely room. Bram's back pressed against cold stone; Ilse folded herself small against her chest; Kestral wedged in front of them both, a shield of gray wool and bad intentions.

The patrol came down. Six wardens, by the sound—armored, unhurried, bored. Their torches swept the vault entrance.

"Archive check," said a voice with the flat authority of long routine. "Quarterly resonance audit. Fan out. Report any vial below threshold glow."

Below threshold glow. A dying voice. A voice fading because its singer, somewhere in the world above, was dying—or had simply been used too hard, drained year after year to power the bells, the wards, the palace lights. Kestrel realized, with a lurch, that the Crown didn't preserve these voices. It *spent* them.

The wardens moved along the spirals, checking niches with instruments that clicked and whined. And the vault, disturbed, woke up.

The whispers started soft. Bram felt them first—a pressure against her mind like fingers testing a seal. Then Ilse stiffened in her arms. Then the whispering became audible even to Kestrel, who had no gift for such things, who had only absence where the gift should be—and it reached him anyway, the way cold reaches through a coat.

The voices in the vials had noticed the visitors.

*Who's there,* whispered a woman's voice from a niche above Bram's head. *Who's there, who's there, it's been so long, is it choir day, is it my turn—*

*Mama?* said another, small and piping, from somewhere near the floor. *Mama, the dark got in again.*

*I remember a garden,* murmured a third, slow and dreaming. *I remember a garden and I cannot remember whose garden or why I am glass.*

Bram pressed her hand hard against her own mouth. Tears slid hot between her fingers. Beside her, Ilse was trembling—not with fear, Bram realized, but with recognition. The child's wide eyes were fixed on the niches, and her lips shaped silent answers to the whispers, greeting each voice like a relative at a door. Some of these fragments were Verse-tongue. Old ones. Covenant-singers, harvested a century ago and kept singing ever since, alone in the dark, keeping the realm's magic running on nothing but their captivity.

*We're sorry,* Ilse sang back to them, soundless, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face. *We're sorry. We came to let you rest.*

A warden's boot scraped three feet from their hiding place.

Kestrel held very still. He counted the man's breaths—one, two, three—and in the silence between them he heard the vial beside his shoulder begin, very faintly, to glow. To *sing*. A child's voice, thin and clear, rising through the vault like smoke:

*"The wheel turns, the crown is worn, the song is kept, the singer's gone—"*

It was the choir training hymn. It was *his*. The vial next to his shoulder—no, he saw now with a horror that stopped his heart—it was his own niche, his own label, his own captured nine-year-old self, waking up because its body had finally come home after twenty years, and singing to him the way a dog sings to a whistle only it can hear.

*Come back,* sang the small voice that had been his. *Come back. It's cold in here. Come back.*

The warden paused. Turned his torch toward the sound.

Kestrel didn't think. He stepped out of the shadows into the torchlight, hands raised, pilgrim's robe hanging loose, face arranged into the simpleton's smile that had gotten him out of a hundred tighter corners than this.

"Begging your pardon, honored sirs," he said, in the flattened accent of the river districts. "Got separated from me pilgrimage group. Terrible sense of direction. They'll be wondering where I've got to."

The warden stared at him. Behind Kestrel, invisible in the dark, Bram pressed Ilse's head gently against her shoulder, and the child's song faded to silence, and the vial dimmed, sulking.

"Pilgrims don't come down here," the warden said.

"Don't they? First pilgrimage." Kestrel scratched his ear, the picture of baffled harmlessness. "Fella in front of me went left instead of right. Whole crowd's probably at the reliquary by now, wondering who's nicked their candles."

Something flickered across the warden's face—the particular boredom of a man deciding whether a problem is worth becoming. Kestrel had built his life on that flicker. He let it stretch. One second. Two.

"Get out," the warden said finally. "And if I catch you down here again, you'll join the collection. Understood?"

"Understood, honored sir. Thank you, honored sir."

Kestrel bowed his way backward toward the stair—and Bram and Ilse slipped from the shadows behind him, heads down, two more anonymous pilgrims herded toward the light. The wardens watched them go with the incurious eyes of men guarding a library no one was allowed to read.

But at the top of the stair, pausing while the warden unbarred the door, Bram caught Kestrel's eye. Her face was wet. Her voice, when it came, was the barest thread of sound, pitched for him alone:

"It sang to you. Your vial. It knew you."

Kestrel looked back down into the vault—at the thousands of tiny lights burning in the dark, each one a throat, each one waiting—and felt the hollow in his chest open like a door.

"Yeah," he said. "It did."

Behind them, as the iron door swung shut, the whole vault exhaled—one vast, soft chord, thousands of voices rising together for a single instant before the stone swallowed it—and Ilse grabbed both their hands and *sang*, urgent and terrified, straight into their bones:

*"The fourth bell is lying. Someone has changed the song. The bells above us are turning to face the palace. Run."*

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, the chapel bells began to ring—and the melody was wrong, dissonant, an alarm dressed as a hymn.

They ran.
