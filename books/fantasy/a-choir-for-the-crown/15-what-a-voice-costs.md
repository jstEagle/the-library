## 15. What a Voice Costs

The vials hung in their thousands, and every one of them was whispering.

Kestrel had heard whispers before—smugglers in the dark, lovers through walls, the confessions of men about to die—but nothing like this. This was a sound that came from inside his own skull, as if the archive beneath the royal chapel had reached into him and rearranged something. Each crystal vessel held a voice, and each voice held a life, and together they made a low tide of murmuring that never quite resolved into words. He thought of rain on a roof. He thought of a crowd praying.

He thought of Bram's hand on his sleeve, three fingers only, because she had learned that pressing all five made him flinch.

"Kestrel." Her whisper came from somewhere behind the shelving to his left. "The patrol's gone past. We have minutes, not hours."

He knew. He'd been counting the warden's footsteps the way he counted everything—out of habit, out of hunger for anything with rhythm. But he didn't move. Because two rows back, on the third shelf from the floor, in a niche marked with a small brass plate that read *CHOIR, ROYAL — MISCELLANEOUS*, there was a vial that was not whispering at all.

It was humming.

*His* tune. The one he'd caught himself whistling—no. Not whistling. He hadn't whistled in eleven years; you needed breath shaped by a throat that remembered how. The one he'd been *humming* since the Withered Marches, low and tuneless, without knowing where it came from. The one Ilse had once turned toward him with those ancient, unchildlike eyes and hummed back, note for note, like an answer to a question he hadn't asked.

Kestrel crossed the aisle. His boots made no sound—he'd oiled them out of habit too—and still he felt loud, felt enormous, felt like a man walking into a room where everyone knew his name except him.

The vial was no bigger than a thumb. The crystal was old work, guild-work, with a seam of silver running around its neck like a collar. Inside, something moved. Not liquid. Light, maybe. Or the memory of light. It pulsed gently, patiently, the way a heart beats when it has decided to wait forever.

There was no name on the brass plate. Just a number: *XLVII*.

Forty-seven. He'd been the forty-seventh boy they'd taken. He knew this the way you know your own birthday—not because anyone told you recently, but because it is stitched into you somewhere below memory.

Behind him, soft as moth-wing, Bram said his name again. And then she saw what he was looking at, and stopped talking altogether.

* * *

Here is what Kestrel remembered of the day they took his voice, which is to say here is what he had spent eleven years refusing to remember:

He was nine years old. The choir master called him out of the line by name—*Kestrel Vane*—and the other boys went silent in that particular way boys go silent when it is someone else's turn. There was a room under the chapel with warm stone floors, warmer than stone should be, and a woman in grey who smelled of candle smoke and said, very kindly, *it won't hurt if you don't fight it.*

He fought it.

It hurt.

Afterward there was a long white silence, and then years of it, and somewhere in those years he learned that a boy with no voice could still be useful. He learned locks. He learned rooftops. He learned the hundred small languages of the hand and the eye and the coin passed palm to palm. He learned that people talked freely in front of a mute man, the way they talked freely in front of furniture, and so he became furniture, and then he became a ghost, and then he became Kestrel—which was not a name so much as a shape he cut in the world, sharp-winged and quick and gone before you could look at him straight.

And always, underneath, the hum. A tune with no source. A wound that sang.

He had told himself a hundred stories about that tune. That it was his mother's lullaby. That it was something he'd heard in a tavern. That it meant nothing. The truth was simpler and crueller: it was the echo of his own voice, rattling around inside a crystal in a vault beneath the chapel, calling to the hollow place it had been carved out of.

Now he stood in front of it, and the hollow place stood up inside him and howled.

* * *

Bram touched his arm. Three fingers.

"Ilse found the ledger," she whispered. Her eyes were wet, but her voice was steady—the guild-voice, the Songwright voice, the one that could hold a room or a collapsing covenant or, apparently, a thief on the edge of breaking. "Every vial is catalogued. Voice, name, date taken. Forty-seven entries under the royal choir alone." She paused. "There's a restoration rite. The queen's archivists use it to... refresh the songs. It works both ways. A voice can be poured back."

Kestrel looked at her.

He had spent eleven years being eloquent in everything but speech. He could say *I love you* with a raised eyebrow and *I'm afraid* with a shrug and *stay* by simply not leaving. But now none of his languages would come. Now there was only the vial, pulsing, patient, and the question underneath it like a stone under a floorboard.

*If I take it back, what does it cost?*

Because he'd heard the whispers. In the archive, everything whispered, and some of what it whispered was knowledge. The voices in the crystals were not just voices—they were vessels, and vessels hold more than sound. They held the shape of the life poured into them. The guild had built them to carry memory-songs: whole histories, whole selves, decanted and stoppered. His voice had been in this vault for eleven years. Eleven years of road. Eleven years of Bram.

Ilse appeared beside them, silent as ever, and did the thing she did—laid her small hand flat against the shelf, against the wood, listening with her palms the way she listened with everything. Then she looked up at Kestrel, and her face did something complicated and old, and she drew one finger slowly across her throat, and then across her eyes, and then pressed both hands over her heart.

*What is given away cannot be kept. What is kept cannot be given.*

He didn't need the translation. Bram gave it anyway, barely above breath: "She says the voice remembers what it lived. Everything it lived. If it comes back to you..." She swallowed. "It comes back carrying eleven years it never saw. And it can't hold both. The rite requires emptying the vessel first. Pouring out what it's stored."

"What's it stored?" he signed, fast, angry, hands cutting the air.

Bram understood him—she'd learned his hands faster than anyone—and her answer came out cracked down the middle. "You. Since the Marches. Every mile. Every—" She stopped. Started again. "Kestrel, it's been drinking you in since the day we met. Every word you never said. It knows the road. If you pour it back in, the road goes."

* * *

The patrol came back early.

They heard it as a change in the whispering overhead—a thousand captured voices hushing at once, the way birds go quiet before a hawk. Boots on chapel stone. Lantern-light sliding down the stairwell in a yellow blade.

Kestrel moved without deciding to move. He swept the vial off its shelf, and the moment his skin touched the crystal the humming climbed into his bones, and he nearly wept, right there, nearly went down on his knees in a Crown archive because a piece of glass knew his name. Bram pulled him behind the shelving. Ilse folded herself small between them, and the three of them crouched in the dark among the stolen dead while lanterns swung past the ends of the aisles.

In the dark, with death walking ten feet away, Kestrel opened his hand and looked at what he held.

Two futures, balanced on his palm.

One: the rite, tonight, later, whenever Bram could manage it. His voice returned. His mother's kitchen, his father's laugh, the choir master's kind grey woman—all of it flooding home. And then a blank where the last year should be. Bram a stranger. Ilse a stranger. The road erased, the firelit nights erased, the moment on the wall at Duskwatch when she'd fallen asleep against his shoulder and he'd sat unmoving until dawn so as not to wake her—that gone. He would open his mouth and sing, and not know why the woman beside him was crying.

Two: the vial, corked, carried, kept. Half-made forever. A man who communicated in eyebrows and coin and the pressure of three fingers on a sleeve. Who would never say her name aloud. Who would stand beside her when the covenant broke over the world and have nothing to offer it but his body and his hands.

The lanterns passed. The boots receded. Somewhere above, a bell tolled the hour, and the whole archive shivered with borrowed resonance, and Kestrel understood suddenly, completely, with the terrible clarity that only comes in mortal danger, that the bells were singing *his* song badly, along with four hundred others, and that this was what the Crown had done with his voice: used it as fuel. Made him furniture for a kingdom.

He looked at Bram.

She was watching him with that expression he had catalogued over a year of roads—the one where her jaw set slightly left of center, the one that meant she had already guessed his answer and was trying to decide whether to argue.

He took her hand. Pressed it flat, palm down, over the vial. Then he lifted his other hand and pointed—at her, at Ilse, at the dark stairwell leading up and out, at all of it, everything, the whole loud doomed beautiful road—and shook his head.

*Not yet. Maybe not ever. This instead.*

"You don't have to choose now," Bram whispered. "We can take it with us. You can decide at the Guildhall, after—"

He tapped the vial once, firmly, and slid it inside his coat, over his heart, where it settled against his ribs like a second heartbeat. Then he signed, slow enough that even Ilse could follow:

*I already chose. On the road. I just didn't know what I was choosing.*

Bram made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed her forehead to his shoulder, and for a moment the three of them stood in the vault of stolen voices, and Kestrel thought: *let them keep my voice. Let them keep the whole rotten kingdom. I have the only thing in this building worth stealing, and I stole it fair.*

Then Ilse grabbed his sleeve and pointed upward, eyes wide, and the whispering changed again—and this time it wasn't hushing.

This time it was rising. A thousand voices climbing toward a single note, and beneath their feet the crystal shelves beginning, very softly, to ring.

Above them, muffled through chapel stone, came the sound Kestrel had dreaded since the capital first appeared on the horizon: the alarm-bells of the palace quarter, waking one tower at a time, and a voice shouting orders in the cold formal cadence of the Crown wardens.

Someone had told them the queen was a traitor.

And the vault of stolen voices had begun, at last, to sing.
