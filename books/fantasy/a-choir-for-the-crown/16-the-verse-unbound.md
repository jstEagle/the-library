## 16. The Verse Unbound

The queen's key was small and cold in Bram's palm, no larger than a sparrow's wishbone, and it hummed.

Not loudly. Not the way the bells above the capital hummed, that vast stolen chorus that pressed against the skull like weather. This was a private sound, almost shy—a single thread of resonance that Bram felt more than heard, traveling up through her wrist bones and settling somewhere behind her sternum, where her heartbeat had always seemed slightly out of step with everyone else's.

*Because it was*, she thought. *Because I am.*

"You'll have until the third bell after midnight," Queen Maren had said, pressing the key into her hand with fingers that trembled only once before going still. The queen's chamber had smelled of candlewax and old paper and something underneath both—faintly, unmistakably—of the Hollow. Of home. "My grandfather's archive answers to blood-oath and iron, but not to me. It never has. The wardens change at the second bell. You will have one hour, perhaps less if the loyalists are restless tonight, and they are always restless on nights when the bells sing flat."

"They sang flat tonight," Kestrel had murmured from his place by the door, arms crossed, watchful as ever.

"Then you know what waits for you." Maren had looked at Bram then with an expression Bram was still trying to name—not pity, not quite guilt, but the particular grief of a woman who had spent forty years inheriting a theft she could not return and could not stop apologizing for. "Bram Winterbourne. Whatever you find down there—and whatever it asks of you—remember that my grandfather took these songs because he believed a realm could be ruled by one voice. He was wrong. But he was thorough. Thoroughness is its own kind of cruelty."

Now, past midnight, standing before an unmarked door beneath the royal chapel, Bram understood what the queen had meant.

The corridor down had been built to humble. Each stair was cut from black stone that drank their lantern light and gave nothing back, and each landing bore a carved inscription in Verse-tongue—the old language, the language before names, the language Ilse spoke in her sleep. Bram read them as they descended, translating haltingly, and the words grew worse with every flight:

*HERE LIES WHAT WAS TAKEN SO THAT IT COULD NOT BE GIVEN.*

*HERE SINGS WHAT WAS SILENCED SO THAT ONE MIGHT SPEAK ALONE.*

*TURN BACK, VESSEL. THE VAULT KNOWS YOU.*

Kestrel stopped at the last inscription. His hand found her shoulder—light, brief, the way he touched things he was afraid of breaking. "It knows her," he said quietly. "That's not a warning meant for us."

"It's a warning meant for me," Bram said. "Which means it's exactly where I need to be."

Ilse said nothing. She hadn't spoken since the chapel, not in common tongue, not even in fragments of song. She had simply taken Bram's left hand and refused to let go, her small fingers laced tight, her gray eyes fixed forward with a stubbornness that Bram had learned over these long weeks to recognize as love wearing armor.

The key fit the lock the way a verse fits its melody. Bram felt the tumblers give one by one, each with a soft chime, seven notes descending—and then the door swung inward onto darkness that was not empty.

The Vault of Stolen Voices breathed around them.

Thousands of crystal vials lined the walls in honeycomb rows, rising from floor to vaulted ceiling, and each one held a faint, shifting light: blue-white, gold, green, the deep violet of dusk. They were not still. They moved like things dreaming. And beneath the silence came the whispering—that terrible, tender murmur Bram had heard once before while hiding among the shelves, the sound of captured voices speaking to themselves across decades, singing fragments to keep from forgetting themselves entirely.

"Oh," Kestrel breathed. His voice cracked in the middle of the word. He stood very still, staring up at the nearest wall of vials, and Bram saw him counting them without meaning to, the way a starving man counts bread. Somewhere in this honeycomb of light was a boy's voice, taken in a choir room forty years ago. Somewhere here was everything he had refused to take back on the road, and everything he'd secretly grieved ever since.

"Not yet," Bram said gently. "We came for the memory-songs. One thing at a time."

"I know." He swallowed. "I know. Lead on."

They were in the heart of the archive now, the central aisle where the oldest vials were kept in cases of lead and glass, and there—exactly where the queen's map had promised—stood a case marked with the seal of the Songwrights' Guild: the interlocking circles, the eight-pointed star, worn nearly smooth by time and deliberate neglect.

Inside lay eleven vials.

Eleven. Bram counted twice, three times, her hands shaking against the glass. Eleven memory-songs, when the guild's registers had listed thirty-one. Twenty songs simply gone—melted down, perhaps, or drained into the bells overhead, or shattered so completely that even their whispers had been scattered. What remained glowed with a tired amber light, and through the crystal she could see shapes moving: not voices, but *structures*. Whole compositions folded into themselves like sleeping birds, wings of harmony tucked close, each one a piece of the covenant that had held two realms in balance for a thousand years.

"The Dawn-Counting," she whispered, reading the labels etched in guild script. "The Binding of Rivers. The Lullaby That Holds the Border. Oh—oh, *there*—" Her breath caught. "The Root Chord. Elder Marenne's own composition. I used to dust its case in the Guildhall and wonder what it sounded like."

She lifted the case's lid with the queen's key, and the moment the seal broke, all eleven vials began to sing.

Softly. So softly it might have been imagination—but no. The whispering throughout the vault shifted, turned, *listened*. Thousands of captured voices went quiet at once, and in that enormous hush the eleven memory-songs raised their voices together for the first time in six generations, harmonies unfolding like dawn over water, and Bram felt the covenant's shape whole again for one impossible moment: two realms, one song, a border that was not a wall but a chord.

Then the vision passed, and the vials dimmed to embers, and Bram was on her knees on cold stone with tears running freely down her face and no memory of falling.

"Bram." Kestrel crouched beside her. "What did you see?"

"What it's supposed to be." She laughed, wet and unsteady. "What it *was*. Kestrel, it's beautiful. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever—"

She stopped.

Because the eleventh vial—the smallest, set apart from the others in its own recess at the bottom of the case—had begun to glow differently. Not amber. Silver. And it was not singing.

It was calling.

The sound threaded straight through her chest and found the off-beat of her heart and matched it, note for note, and Bram knew this melody the way she knew her own hands. She had hummed it as a child without knowing where it came from. She had heard it in dreams. She had heard it echoing, badly sung, from the royal chapel towers the day they arrived.

Her verse. The final verse. Kept here all along, in a vial the size of a thumb, waiting for her the way a seed waits for rain.

"Don't," Ilse said.

Bram turned. The girl had released her hand for the first time since the chapel, and she stood with her fists clenched at her sides, trembling, her face white as the crystal around her.

"Don't touch it," Ilse said again, and her voice—her rare, precious, hard-won voice, the common tongue she had learned syllable by syllable on the road—cracked on the second word. "Bram. *Don't.*"

"It's mine, little bird." Bram reached for the vial. "It's been mine since before I was born. It's why I exist."

Ilse caught her wrist. For such a small child she had astonishing strength, or perhaps it was only desperation, which looks the same. Her eyes were huge and wet and furious. "I dreamed it," she signed one-handed, because speech was failing her, her other hand still gripping Bram's arm. "Last night. In the palace. I dreamed the song finished and you weren't—you weren't *anywhere*. There was music and the valley was singing and the sky was singing and I called your name into all of it and the song just—it just *kept going*, Bram, it didn't need you anymore, it had *used* you—"

"Ilse—"

"That's what the fine print says!" The words burst out of her, ragged. "I can't read their letters but I don't need to, I *am* the old language, I feel it, I've always felt it, and when you touched the case I felt the truth come up through your skin like frost. The vessel sings the final verse and the vessel *becomes* the verse. That's the price. That's always been the price. One voice, given wholly, forever. That's how the first covenant was made and that's how they mean to remake it and you *know*, you've known since the choir of ash, you knew when the old woman looked at you—"

"Ilse." Bram knelt, bringing herself level with the girl, and took both her small hands. They were ice-cold. "Look at me."

Ilse looked. And Bram saw the truth she had been dreading for weeks reflected there: that this child, mute by the world's cruelty, half-unraveled by the covenant's collapse, had crossed a kingdom clinging to one person. That Bram was not her rescuer. Bram was her *home*, the way the Hollow had been Bram's—sheltering, beloved, and never truly hers to keep.

"If I don't sing it," Bram said quietly, "the covenant dies. The Marches go colorless. The animals stay voiceless. The realms drift apart until the magic rots out of the world entirely, and children like you are born into silence forever, and no one remembers there was ever another way to live. I'm the last verse, little bird. If I refuse, there is no song. There's just the quiet, spreading, like the Hollow spread. Like a wound that never closes."

"Then find another way!" Ilse's signs were sharp enough to cut. "You told me—on the road, in the Marches, when I was sick and scared—you told me the guild's first law. *A song belongs to everyone who carries it.* You said it. You made me say it back. Was that a lie?"

"No."

"Then it doesn't belong to you alone! A verse isn't a cage, Bram, it's a *part*—and parts can be carried by more than one throat—" Her voice gave out. She stood there breathing hard, fists balled, tears cutting clean lines through the dust of the vault on her face, and finished in the old language, in Verse-tongue, in the tongue before names:

*Two throats hold a chord. Three hold a bridge. No one must carry the roof-beam alone.*

Bram stared at her.

Because that wasn't in any text she had ever studied. That wasn't in the elder's buried letters or the guild registers or the exiles' fragmented memories. That was new. That was *hers*—somehow, impossibly, this child who was a living fragment of the broken covenant had just composed a line of theology in a dead language, and the vault itself seemed to lean in to listen.

Behind them, softly, one of the eleven memory-songs changed its tune.

Kestrel heard it too. "Did that vial just—"

"It answered her." Bram rose slowly, her mind racing ahead of her fear for the first time in days. "Ilse. Say it again."

*Two throats hold a chord,* Ilse repeated, wary now, watching the vials. *Three hold a bridge.*

And the amber lights pulsed—gently, tentatively, the way a fire breathes when someone feeds it—each pulse falling between Ilse's words like footsteps finding a rhythm.

"The covenant was made by many," Bram whispered. "The histories say the first binding took a hundred singers. A *choir*. It was only later—only when the Crown stole the songs and the guild dwindled—that the reforging rite was reduced to one voice, one sacrifice, because one singer was all anyone had left." Her heart was pounding now, a wild, dangerous hope. "But we don't have one singer. We have—"

She stopped. Looked at Kestrel. Looked at the vials.

He had gone very pale. "No," he said. "Whatever you're thinking—I can't sing, Bram. That's rather the entire tragedy of my life. You were there for the firelight. You know what's missing."

"Your voice is in a vial ten feet from where you're standing."

"And taking it back costs me—" His jaw tightened. "It costs me the road. It costs me *you*. I told you. I chose already. Don't ask me to unchoose."

"I'm not asking you to take it back." Bram stepped toward him, and her voice dropped low, urgent, the way it did when a song was building toward its turn. "I'm asking whether a voice has to be *whole* to be given. Your vial holds your boyhood voice, yes—but you've spent forty years learning to speak anyway. To laugh anyway. To fight and bargain and care anyway. What if the verse doesn't need the voice the Crown stole? What if it needs the voice you *made* out of what was left?"

Kestrel stared at her. Something moved behind his eyes—hope and terror wrestling for the same ground.

"And Ilse," Bram continued, turning, "is a fragment of the covenant itself. Living Verse-tongue. She doesn't need to be taught the song. She *is* part of it." She looked down at the girl, whose face had shifted from fury to something fragile and stunned. "Three voices. Three broken voices, little bird—mine that was made instead of born, yours that the world tried to silence, his that was stolen and rebuilt. Maybe that's not a flaw in the plan. Maybe that's the whole point. Maybe a covenant reforged by the unbroken would only teach the realms that perfection binds. But a covenant reforged by the wounded—"

"—teaches them that wounds can carry weight," Kestrel finished hoarsely.

The vials flared. All eleven at once, amber light washing the vault like sunrise, and the whispering of the thousand captive voices rose—not in alarm, Bram realized, but in something like *acclamation*, a rustling tide of the dead and silenced saying *yes, yes, we heard, we remember, that is how it was in the beginning.*

And then, from far above, through leagues of stone and the roots of the palace, came a sound that killed the moment like a blade through a song.

A bell. Ringing wrong. Ringing *flat*—and then a second bell answering it, and a third, and beneath them all, faint but unmistakable, the shrill coordinated whistle-calls of Crown wardens mustering in the night.

Kestrel moved first, sweeping the eleven vials into the padded satchel with hands that shook only after they finished. "Loyalists found the empty case," he said grimly. "Or the queen's maids talked. Doesn't matter. We have minutes, maybe less."

Ilse grabbed Bram's hand again—and pressed something into it. A scrap of parchment, torn from the hymnal in her coat, covered in her careful, angular script. Bram looked down and read:

*A verse isn't a cage. Parts can be carried by more than one throat. Promise me you'll try the third way before you try the last one.*

"Promise me," Ilse said aloud, fierce and small and terrified.

Above them, the bells began to scream.

Bram closed her fist around the note, around the queen's key, around the whole impossible fragile hope of it. "I promise," she said. "Now run."
