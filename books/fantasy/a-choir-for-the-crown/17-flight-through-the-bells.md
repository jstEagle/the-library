## 17. Flight Through the Bells

The first warning was a bell that rang wrong.

Kestrel heard it from the archive's antechamber, where he'd been keeping watch with one ear cocked toward the stairwell and the other on Bram's voice, which drifted up from the vault below in low, careful phrases—the sound of a woman counting her own heartbeat. Then the Chapel of Bells began its noon toll, and somewhere in the middle of it, one tower's note bent sideways, like a knife slipped between ribs.

He'd grown up in this city. He knew what bells were supposed to say. This one said *traitor*.

"Down here," he called softly, already moving to the vault door. "We've got company."

Bram came up the ladder two rungs at a time, Ilse clinging to her back like a burr, a satchel of crystal vials wrapped in wool against the girl's chest. The vials whispered. They always whispered—faint voices seeping through the glass, singers who had been poured into storage generations ago—and Kestrel had learned, over three days in this palace, to stop flinching at them. Mostly.

"How many?" Bram asked.

"I don't know yet. But someone just told every warden in the capital that their queen is a traitor." He pulled the wool tighter around the vials, checked the satchel strap twice. "That kind of news travels faster than fire."

Ilse slid down from Bram's back and tugged his sleeve. Her hands moved quick and urgent—not the Verse-tongue, not quite, but the shorthand they'd built together on the road, half gesture, half hum. *Which way?*

Kestrel looked at the map he'd drawn in his head over years of thieving: the archive sat beneath the royal chapel; the chapel connected to the cloister; the cloister opened onto the Bellwrights' Stair, which climbed the outside of the Great Tower itself. Every route but one ended in a courtyard full of halberds.

"The up way," he said. "It's always the up way."

---

Queen Maren stood at her study window and watched her own soldiers cross the inner ward in column formation, which was answer enough to the question she'd been asking herself all morning: *how long do I have?*

She had known about Warden-Commander Aldous Vey for eleven years. She had promoted him twice. A man like Vey did not betray suddenly; he betrayed patiently, the way rust worked, and today the rust had finished its work because she had finally given him something worth seizing—a Songwright in her vaults, stolen songs leaving her palace in a pilgrim's satchel.

Let him have the palace, then. Let him have the throne room, the banners, the whole gilded machine her grandfather had built on theft. What Maren wanted was already gone, carried out by a mud-born woman with a voice like a drawn blade and a mute child who sang without sound.

She took the secret passage behind her tapestries—the one her grandmother had shown her, back when passages were still secrets—and made for the chapel.

If she was going to be a traitor, she intended to be an honest one.

---

They almost made the cloister.

Almost. The word would haunt Kestrel later, because they had been *close*—close enough to see the gray daylight through the cloister arches when the wardens came down the opposite corridor in a wall of steel and lamplight, and the lamplight caught them, and a voice barked *"There!"* with the flat satisfaction of a man who has been right all along.

"Stair," Kestrel said, and shoved Bram sideways into the narrow doorway of the Bellwrights' Stair, and followed, and heard the first crossbow bolt bury itself in the wood where his head had been.

Then they were climbing.

The stair was a spiral bolted to the tower's outer wall, iron and old stone, open to the sky on the left side and sheer drop on the right, and Kestrel took the steps three at a time because below them boots were taking them two at a time, and above them—

Above them, the bells woke up.

Not the noon toll. Not any human hand. The bells themselves began to move, all twelve towers of the capital at once, and the sound they made was not music. It was what happened when you stole a hundred voices and forced them into bronze and never let them rest: a scream with harmony in it, dissonance stacked on dissonance, a chord that had been waiting a century to break.

Bram stopped dead on the stairs, one hand clapped to her ear, and Kestrel saw blood on her fingers when it came away.

"It's the songs," she shouted over the din. "The memory-songs—they're fighting the bells! Everything the Crown stole, everything in these towers—it knows we've got the vials!"

"Can it help us?"

"NO!"

Below, the lead warden reached the stair's base and raised a whistle to his lips, and before he could blow it, the nearest bell swung hard and screamed directly down at him—a cone of pure wrongness that knocked him off his feet and sent his men stumbling backward, hands over ears. One dropped his crossbow. It fell forty feet into the cloister garden and stuck in the soil, quivering.

Kestrel allowed himself half a second of unholy delight. "I take it back," he yelled. "Can it keep doing that?"

"It's not aiming! It's just—everything is waking up angry!" Bram hauled Ilse past her, up, always up. The girl's face was white but her lips were moving, silent, steady—she was *humming*, Kestrel realized, humming some thread of counter-melody that kept her walking while the world came apart around them. The child who couldn't speak was the only one of them not deafened.

They burst onto the tower gallery at the top, wind hitting them like a wall, the city spread out below—and there, across sixty feet of empty air, the bridge of ropes and planks that the old bellwrights had strung between the Great Tower and its twin, for maintenance no one had done in fifty years.

"That's not a bridge," Bram said. "That's a dare."

"It's a shortcut." Kestrel tested the first rope. It held, groaning. "Everything I love is a dare."

Behind them, boots on the stairs. Ahead, the screaming bells. And below—Kestrel risked one glance and wished he hadn't—the courtyard filling with soldiers, and cutting through them, tall and gray-cloaked and moving like weather, Warden-Commander Vey himself, looking up.

Vey didn't shout. He simply raised one hand, and every warden in the yard stopped, and pointed upward, and waited.

"Go," Kestrel said. "Bridge. Now. I'll cut the ropes after."

"You'll fall."

"I've fallen off worse things than a tower, Winterbourne."

"That's not comforting!"

"It wasn't meant to be!" He pushed her toward the gap. "Move!"

---

Maren came out onto the chapel roof into a hurricane of noise and understood, in one cold flash, why her grandfather had needed the Hollow.

Because this was what magic was when it wasn't caged. Not the tame glow of court thaumaturges, not the obedient shimmer of the Crown's rituals—this was a hundred stolen voices tearing at their bronze prisons, and the sky itself leaning in to watch, and her realm's entire history of theft suddenly audible, a debt come due with interest, ringing from every tower.

She had spent thirty years pretending the rot was manageable. It had never been manageable. It had only ever been *quiet*.

She saw them halfway across the rope bridge—a woman, a man, a child, small as figures on a coin—and she saw the wardens reaching the top of the stairs behind them, and she did the arithmetic of her remaining power and found it nearly spent.

But not quite.

Maren walked to the chapel's great bell—the Coronation Bell, largest in the realm, cast from melted-down guild silver—and laid both her bare hands against the cold bronze, and spoke the words her grandmother had taught her and she had sworn never to use:

*"I am the thief's heir. I release what was taken."*

The Coronation Bell went silent.

And in that silence, every other bell in the capital faltered—confused, suddenly masterless—because for one hundred years the great bell had been the metronome of stolen song, the spine of the Crown's whole resonant architecture, and its silence was a wound in the pattern.

Sixty feet of rope bridge. Sixty feet bought with a queen's last authority.

Maren closed her eyes and held on.

---

Halfway across the bridge, the wind changed.

Bram felt it before she understood it—the bells' screaming shifting key, coiling, finding a shape—and then the dissonance condensed into something with intent, a standing wave of hostile resonance rolling along the bridge cables like a wave along a whip. The planks began to sing, high and shivering, and then to snap, one after another ahead of them, *pop pop pop*, stitching a line of failure straight toward Ilse.

"No—" Bram lunged, caught the girl around the waist, and threw them both flat against the tower wall as the last plank between them and the far side dissolved into fraying rope.

Silence, except wind. Except bells, distant now, keening. Except Ilse's breath against her collarbone, fast and frightened.

Bram looked at the gap. Eight feet of nothing, and beyond it the twin tower's ledge, and beyond *that* the rooftops and freedom, and behind her Kestrel shouting something the wind stole, and below, far below, Vey's gray cloak turning as he gave orders she couldn't hear but could imagine perfectly: *wait. They're trapped. Wait.*

Her arms burned. The satchel of vials dug into her shoulder blade, whispering, whispering—dozens of dead singers murmuring against her spine like a congregation deciding whether to pray.

And Ilse, pressed against her chest, looked up. And hummed.

One note. Clear, small, absolutely certain. The same fragment the child had hummed at the Guildhall gate a lifetime ago, the melody that had made dead books glow—the first verse, the seed verse, the one Bram had spent months learning to hear and still couldn't sing right.

Except now, in the open air, above the loud world, with the covenant's broken machinery howling around them—now Bram heard it *whole*. Heard where it went next. Heard the interval she'd been getting wrong, the one that needed not strength but *letting go*, the way a bell lets go of its strike, the way a valley lets go of its silence.

The bridge was gone. But the air between the towers was full of song, if you listened.

"Hold on to me," Bram said, though she didn't know yet what she meant by it.

She planted her feet on the ledge. She filled her lungs with the screaming wind. And instead of jumping, she *sang*—the first true note she had ever sung outside the Hollow, raw and untrained and hers, aimed not at the gap but at the standing wave itself, at the hostile resonance rolling along the cables.

The note hit the wave and the wave *bent*.

For one impossible moment, the dissonance parted like a curtain, and in the gap where it parted there was something solid enough to bear weight—or close enough, or maybe it was only faith shaped like a bridge—and Bram stepped out onto it with Ilse in her arms, onto nothing, onto song, and the nothing held.

Three steps. Four. Behind her she heard Kestrel swear with tremendous feeling and then follow, his boots landing where hers had landed, trusting her note with his life, and the wave screamed and snapped back the instant his foot left it, and then they were all three collapsing onto the twin tower's ledge, alive, gasping, ringing like struck metal.

Bram lay on her back on cold stone, laughing or crying or both, and above her the bells of the capital slowly, slowly, began to quiet—not tamed, not silenced, but *heard*, perhaps, for the first time in a century, and therefore less furious.

Ilse crawled onto her chest and pressed her small ear over Bram's heart, listening to it pound, and smiled the wide, gap-toothed smile that meant *good* in whatever language she dreamed in.

Kestrel rolled over, spat wind-blown hair out of his mouth, and stared at her.

"What," he said hoarsely, "was that."

"I don't know." And it was even mostly true. Because she did know—she'd known since the Choir of Ash, since the letters, since the vault—she knew exactly what she was and what that step had cost and what it would someday cost entirely, and she filed the knowing away in the place where she kept everything she wasn't ready to give them. "A third-way sort of thing. Hold the thought."

He laughed, shaky, and pulled them both upright. "Rooftops," he said. "South quarter. There's a smuggler's gate in the river wall that opens on my mark, and a boatman who owes me more than he'll admit. We can be out of the city by dark."

"And the queen?"

Kestrel hesitated. On the wind, faintly, from the direction of the chapel roof, came a sound none of them could name at first—a single held tone, thin and clear and utterly alone. Someone down there was singing into the silence the great bell had left. Someone was buying them time with the only coin she had left.

Bram stood very still, listening, and her face did something complicated.

"She's covering us," Bram said quietly. "She's holding the wardens with her own voice. Kestrel—we can't leave her."

"We can't *reach* her. That's the point of what she's doing!" He grabbed Bram's arm. "Winterbourne. Listen to me. If we go back, everything she's spending herself for is wasted. The songs get out of the city tonight or they don't get out at all. That was the bargain. That was always the bargain."

"But—"

"*That was always the bargain.*"

Below them, the lone voice wavered. Held. Wavered again.

And then, threading up through the wind from the chapel roof, the voice found a new note—one Bram recognized with a jolt that went through her like lightning, because it was the counter-melody. The answering phrase. The part of the verse that was never meant to be sung alone, the part that had been waiting a hundred years for a partner, and the queen was singing it now, badly, bravely, *at* them—an offering, or an instruction, or a plea.

Ilse's head snapped up. Her eyes went wide and wet, and her hands flew through the urgent grammar she used when words failed her: *She knows it. She knows the answer. Take me to her.*

Bram looked at the child. At Kestrel. At the rooftops running south toward the river and freedom and the long road home.

Then she looked east, toward the voice, and made the choice that would follow her for the rest of her life.

"Change of plans," she said.
