## 21. The New Choir

### Kestrel

The scaffolding went up in the first week of spring, and by the second week the children had already claimed it.

Kestrel watched from the Guildhall steps as three of them swarmed the eastern gallery like starlings, their bare feet hammering the planks, a stolen hammer passing hand to hand. One of them—a warden's daughter, of all people, a girl named Petra with her father's square jaw and none of his fear—slipped, caught herself on a rope, and laughed. The sound carried across the whole valley. It carried the way everything carried now, since the silence broke: loud, unguarded, bouncing off the mountainsides and coming back changed.

A year ago, that laugh would have died an inch from her lips. A year ago, the Hollow would have swallowed it whole and called that safety.

He still couldn't speak. He had chosen not to, in the end—had stood in the vault beneath the royal chapel with his own voice glowing in its crystal vial like a trapped wasp, and had set it down and walked away. Some nights he wondered if he'd been a fool. Most nights, standing here, listening to a valley full of noise where there had been nothing but held breath for a hundred years, he knew he hadn't.

"You're brooding," said Bram, coming up beside him with two mugs of cider. She handed him one without looking, the way she did everything now—easily, as though ease were a language she'd finally been permitted to learn. "I can tell because you're doing the thing with your eyebrows."

He tapped her arm twice—their shorthand, *yes*—and drank.

Below them, the Guildhall's great doors stood open for the first time in living memory. Not propped open, not broken open, but *opened*, on oiled hinges, by choice. Inside, the hall that had once housed forty silent songbooks now rang with the chaos of a school being born. Exiles from the Choir of Ash sat elbow to elbow with former wardens who still flinched at raised voices. Ilse moved among them all like a small queen, fourteen now, taller, her mud-brown hair braided with ribbon, correcting pronunciation in a language older than any kingdom with the infinite patience of someone who remembered when it had been the only language there was.

"She's better at this than I am," Bram said.

"She's better at most things than most people."

Bram snorted into her cider. "Don't let her hear you say that. Her head won't fit through the door."

But she was smiling, and Kestrel found himself doing the thing he did more often these days: simply looking at her. Part girl, part song—that was what the reforging had made her, or perhaps what it had revealed. When she spoke, the air around her words shimmered faintly, like heat off summer stone. When she sang—and she sang constantly now, walking, working, washing dishes—the valley answered. Grass leaned toward her. Bells in distant towns, miles away, hummed themselves half-awake.

She had nearly dissolved into light on the Guildhall floor eleven months ago. He had joined his voiceless throat to hers anyway, pushing breath and shape and *meaning* without sound, and Ilse had screamed the third part in raw Verse-tongue, and somehow—because three voices carried what one could not—the covenant had reforged without unmaking her.

The valley had paid the price instead. The Hollow's silence was gone forever. Magic poured in like floodwater, and the old protective numbness was replaced by a thousand small sensations Kestrel was still learning to name: the itch of ley-lines underfoot, the taste of weather before it arrived, the way the dead songbooks on their shelves now glowed softly all night, like coals refusing to die.

Grief and gift, tangled past separating. That was the whole story of this place now.

Bram bumped his shoulder. "Queen's party was sighted at the pass an hour ago," she said. "Maren herself, riding escort. Old woman, bad knee, refuses to be carried." Her voice softened. "She asked if we'd sing the welcome. The *old* welcome. The one from before her grandfather."

Kestrel looked out at the valley—at the terraces being replanted, the color seeping back into fields that had spent generations gray, the animals born with voices again, bleating and barking and generally making nuisances of themselves—and felt something in his chest that had no name except maybe *hope*, which he'd always considered a con man's word.

He tapped her arm twice.

*Yes.*

### Maren

The queen had signed four hundred documents in her reign, and had hated every one of them until today.

This one lay on a table carried up the mountain by six sweating soldiers, weighted at the corners with river stones, because Bram Winterbourne had refused to sign anything inside a palace ever again—"paper remembers where it's written," she'd said, with the particular stubbornness of someone who had learned history the hard way—and so the accord would be executed here, in the open air, on the Guildhall steps, before witnesses who were neither courtiers nor clerks.

Maren read it one last time. Her hands shook. They had shaken since the bells screamed; the physicians called it age. She knew better. Some tremors are the body remembering what the mind tries to bury.

*The Accord of the Hollow,* it began, in her own clerk's careful hand. *Let no crown hold song alone. Let no valley hold silence alone. Let the stewardship of magic be shared among singers, wardens, exiles, and thrones alike, and let each check the others, and let none forget the cost.*

Her grandfather had forgotten the cost. Her grandfather had thought a covenant was a leash and songs were treasure, and he had locked both in a vault beneath his chapel and called it security, and the realm had rotted for three generations while he counted his hoard. Maren had spent her first fifty years maintaining his theft and her last ten trying to undo it, and she had learned, late and painfully, that you cannot undo a wrong by decree. You can only open the door and hope someone walks through it willing to build something better.

They had walked through. This ragged, scarred, improbable company—novelist-turned-songwright, mute smuggler, verse-child, choir of ash, even a handful of her own wardens who had laid down their resonance-cuffs and picked up tuning forks.

"Your Majesty." Bram approached, and the air shivered pleasantly around her. Behind her came the others: Kestrel, sharp-eyed and silent; Ilse, solemn in her good dress; old Sefa of the Choir of Ash, leaning on a staff carved with notes; Warden-Captain Dree, formerly Maren's own, now wearing guild gray with no visible shame about it.

"Master Winterbourne." Maren inclined her head—an old habit, and also a new courtesy, because titles mattered less than they used to and therefore mattered more, given freely rather than taken. "You'll forgive me if I don't make a speech. I've made too many speeches over things I didn't believe in. I'd like this one to be short and true."

"Short and true," Bram agreed. "A novel concept for a monarch."

Someone laughed—Dree, Maren thought, though it might have been the child. Maren found she didn't mind. Laughter aimed at queens was a luxury she was learning to afford.

She took up the pen. It was only ink on vellum. It was the hardest thing she had ever signed, harder than her coronation oath, because this document bound her heirs and her realm to limits—willingly, publicly, permanently. Kings do not sign their own leashes. But her grandfather had never understood that a covenant is not a cage. It is a promise sung between equals, and it holds only as long as everyone keeps singing.

She wrote her name. The ink gleamed wet and black in the mountain sunlight.

Then Bram sang—not a grand thing, just seven notes, the opening phrase of the old welcome—and the valley took it up. The exiles' cracked voices. The wardens' trained ones. Ilse's clear thread of Verse-tongue running underneath like a root. Even Kestrel, mouth closed, eyes shut, moving his hands in shapes that somehow *were* music, conducting a choir only he could see.

And the accord, lying on its table between the river stones, began very faintly to glow.

Maren watched the light sink into the vellum, into the signatures, into the memory of everyone present, and understood that this document would never be lost, never be forged, never be quietly amended in some chancellor's back room. It was written in the oldest archive there was.

It was written in everyone who heard it.

"Well," she said, when the last note faded and her eyes were wet and she saw no reason to pretend otherwise. "I suppose we'd best get to work."

### Ilse

Ilse had a class of nine students, and they were the strangest choir ever assembled, and she loved them with her whole fractious heart.

There was Petra, the warden's daughter, who had perfect pitch and terrible patience. There was old Tam of the Choir of Ash, sixty years old and learning his first note, weeping every single time he managed one, which slowed lessons considerably. There were twins from the Marches whose voices had come in late and strange, harmonizing with things that weren't there. There was a former Crown warden named Hask who had once hunted people like Ilse and now sat in the front row taking notes with the diligence of a man doing penance, which, Ilse supposed, he was.

Today's lesson was names.

"In the Verse-tongue," Ilse said, pacing the length of the hall the way Bram paced it, "you do not *say* your name. You *sing* it. Your name is the smallest song that is still you." She stopped. "Bram says that. She stole it from the elder texts. Everything wise anyone says here gets stolen from somewhere, so steal freely."

Laughter. Even Tam laughed, wet-eyed.

"Now. Close your eyes. Find the note that feels like—" she searched for the common-tongue words, still sometimes reaching for the old grammar first, "—like *coming home*. Not the prettiest note. The truest one. When you find it, hold it. Don't show it to anyone yet. It's yours first."

She walked among them while they searched. She could hear the almost-notes, the false starts, the little sounds people made when they were frightened of their own voices. Hask kept producing polished, correct tones that meant nothing—warden training, all surface. She put a hand on his shoulder.

"Not from your head," she whispered. "From wherever you keep the thing you're sorry for."

His breath caught. And then, quietly, terribly, beautifully, he found it—one rough, unlovely, absolutely honest note, and the nearest songbook on the shelf flickered like a candle in wind.

"There," Ilse said. "That's the beginning of your name. Guard it."

After class, she stayed behind in the hall, as she often did, and climbed to the gallery where the dead songbooks lived. They weren't dead anymore. They glowed day and night, and lately they had begun doing something new: filling in. Pages that had been blank for a century were slowly growing text, faint as breath on glass, as though the covenant's reforging had given the guild's stolen memory somewhere to return to. Not everything. The Crown's vault still held vials upon vials of captured voices, and the work of freeing them would take years, decades, lifetimes. Queen Maren had pledged the archive's dismantling; Warden-Captain Dree had volunteered to oversee it; and every freed voice, when it came home to the valley, added another line to another book.

Restoration as collective work. Bram's phrase. Ilse had decided it was a good one.

She heard footsteps on the stairs and turned, expecting Bram.

It wasn't Bram. It was a stranger—a young woman in travel-stained clothes, standing in the doorway with a satchel and an expression Ilse recognized from the mirror of her own memory: the look of someone who has crossed a long distance carrying something fragile and is terrified to set it down.

"I'm looking for the Songwrights," the stranger said. Her voice cracked oddly on the words, doubled, as though two speakers shared one throat. "They told me in the capital—I was told there was a school now. In the Hollow." She swallowed. "I think there's a song stuck in me. It isn't mine. I don't know how to give it back."

Ilse looked at her for a long moment. Then she smiled, and reached for the ledger.

### Kestrel

Evening found him on the steps again, because the steps had become his post, his porch, his piece of claimed ground—the first ground he'd ever claimed that nobody had to pay him to stand on.

Bram came out and sat beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his. Above them, the Guildhall windows glowed with lamplight and lesson-light. Somewhere inside, Ilse was drilling the twins on scales; somewhere else, Tam was weeping over a successful middle C; somewhere else, Hask was practicing his one true note over and over, guarding it like a coal.

"They signed," Kestrel said—or rather, he shaped, tapping the rhythm against her arm, because after a year she read his hands the way scholars read scripture. *The accord. It's done.*

"It's done." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "Do you know what my elder used to say? That our job was to keep the embers. Tend the last fire, wait for the dark to pass." She huffed a laugh. "She was wrong, bless her. Our job was never to keep the fire. It was to teach everyone else to carry sparks."

He tapped: *You'd know. You're mostly spark yourself.*

She pinched him. He grinned.

Below, the last students straggled out into the blue dusk, and the valley settled into its evening noise—frogs, wind, a dog barking at nothing, the far-off hum of the boundary stones where the old wall of silence had been, now worn smooth and warm like a threshold instead of a barrier. Travelers came through it daily now. Pilgrims, refugees, the curious, the gifted, the hunted. The school took them all. The ledger grew fat.

That was the thing Kestrel understood best, in his wordless way: healing was not a moment. It was not a climax, not a bell rung once. It was chores. It was showing up tomorrow and the day after. It was a mute man holding a door open and a queen signing her name with shaking hands and a child teaching grown men to find the note that felt like home.

He had lost his voice and gained a life. On balance, on most days, he'd make the trade again.

Bram stirred. "One more thing before bed," she said, and there was something in her tone—half mischief, half ceremony. "Come inside. Both of us. There's a page waiting."

He followed her in, up the stairs, to the long table where the guild ledger lay open. It was the same ledger her elder had tended, the same one Bram had finally signed her own name in months ago—*Bram Winterbourne, Songwright, author*—after a lifetime of believing she was only a vessel.

Tonight there was a fresh page. And on it, in careful chalk (so it could be argued with, Bram insisted; ink was for things already decided), someone had written:

*Founding Masters of the New Choir:*

*Bram Winterbourne.*
*Ilse, Speaker of the First Tongue.*
*Kestrel —*

The line after his name hung empty. Waiting.

He stared at it. His hands, so fluent on her arm, suddenly had nothing to say. What was he, in this story? Not a songwright. Not a singer. A thief, a guide, a hollow man who had chosen the road over his own voice and never once regretted it and could not explain why in any language but touch.

Bram watched him, patient. "It doesn't have to be tonight," she said gently.

But it did, a little, and he knew it, and she knew he knew it. So he picked up the chalk, and he thought about the road—the Withered Marches and the bell towers and the vault full of whispering dead, the firelight where she'd sung his true song to him, the thousand small mornings since—and he wrote, in a smuggler's blunt hand, the only title he'd ever wanted:

*Kestrel — Keeper of the Door.*

Bram read it, and laughed, and kissed his cheek, and the songbooks on their shelves glowed a little brighter, as if the guild itself had nodded.

Outside, the valley sang itself to sleep: frogs and wind and distant bells and the soft, ceaseless murmur of a world remembering its own music.

And down in the entry hall, by lamplight, Ilse was still talking with the stranger whose voice held two speakers—listening, really listening, with her head tilted, the way she did when the old tongue stirred in someone unaware.

"Stay the night," Ilse was saying. "And then tell me, slowly, everything about the song that isn't yours. Because I think—" she glanced toward the stairs, toward the two silhouettes descending, and smiled, "—I think it's going to want finding."
