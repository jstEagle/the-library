## 22. Bram Winterbourne, Sung and Singing

The morning came up gold over the Hollow, and for the first time in a hundred years, the light arrived with sound.

Bram stood on the Guildhall steps with her hands folded behind her back, listening to it. Wind moved through the birches at the valley's rim and carried their leaves' whisper all the way down to the door—no wall of silence swallowing it halfway, no dead air pressing on the ears like water. The wind simply traveled, the way wind was meant to. Somewhere upslope a stream chattered over stones that had been mute her whole life. A bird she couldn't name sang three notes, paused as if embarrassed by its own boldness, then sang them again.

Ilse sat two steps below her, cross-legged, a slate across her knees and chalk dust on her chin. She had grown half a head since the night she'd knocked at the gate, mud-streaked and humming a language older than kingdoms. She still didn't speak the common tongue—not properly, not yet—but she no longer needed to reach for song alone to be understood. She had words now, rough-hewn ones, and she deployed them like a mason who'd just discovered mortar.

"Again," Ilse said. "From the turn."

"Greedy," Bram said fondly.

"Yes." No hesitation. Ilse had learned *yes* early and wielded it often.

Bram laughed, and heard her own laugh travel down the valley instead of dying at her lips, and something in her chest turned over gently, like a key in an old lock.

* * *

The Guildhall had never looked like this. Not in her lifetime, and—if the elder's buried letters were true—not in anyone's lifetime since the purge. Bram had spent the winter expecting the flood of outside magic to rot the old timbers or crack the foundation stones, the way a drowned thing swells when it finally breathes air. Instead the opposite had happened. The hall drank the world in and straightened. Beams that had sagged for decades lifted by degrees, so slowly you'd swear you saw nothing until one morning you noticed the roofline ran true against the sky. Moss retreated from the north wall. The great hearth drew without smoking.

And the songbooks—the silent songbooks she had tended like grave-markers for twenty years—had woken.

Not all at once. That was the miracle of it, and also the discipline of it: they woke the way the valley woke, page by page, note by note, each recovered memory-song from the Crown's vault finding its way home through Bram's voice and settling into the vellum like a bird returning to a nest it remembered better than its keeper did. Some mornings she opened the cabinet and found a book glowing faintly, humming under her fingertips, and she would sit down right there on the cold floor with her tea going cold beside her and read until her eyes burned.

The guild ledger lay open on the lectern inside the doors. It was the first thing she saw every morning and the last every night: two hundred years of names, and then the long blankness where names should have been. Her predecessor's hand stopped mid-entry the week before Bram's birth. Below it, nothing. For twenty years she had looked at that nothing and felt it looking back.

Today she had brought ink.

* * *

The school had started small, because everything true started small. Kestrel had joked about that once, in his dry way, sitting on these same steps with his hands laced over one knee: "Every kingdom I've ever robbed started as three people agreeing they deserved more." He hadn't been joking, really. He never quite joked. His humor was a door left ajar so you could choose to look through it.

Three people. That was how it began. An old exile from the Choir of Ash named Maren-of-the-Marshes—no relation to the queen, though she enjoyed telling people otherwise—who had forgotten her art on purpose and was learning, painfully, joyfully, to remember. A former Crown warden named Aldous Vey who had laid down his resonance-cuffs on the Guildhall steps the day after the reforging and asked, in a voice cracked with shame, whether there was work here for a man who had spent thirty years silencing singers. And Ilse.

Now there were nineteen. They came from the Withered Marches with their voiceless herds and colorless fields; they came from the capital, where the bells no longer screamed but rang, honestly rung, by human hands pulling human ropes; they came from the Choir of Ash, one gray-haired survivor at a time, blinking in the doorway like people stepping out of a cellar into noon. Even the queen sent letters—long, careful, self-lacerating letters that Bram read aloud to the students because Maren-of-the-Marshes said royalty should be studied like weather: useful, powerful, and not to be trusted out of sight. But the queen's latest letter had contained something new. A draft accord. *On the Shared Stewardship of Resonance,* it was titled, in her grandfather's stolen script, and beneath the formal language ran a plainer current: *I am told your valley is where we learned to sing together. Teach us again. I will sign whatever you write.*

Bram had not answered yet. She wanted to answer well.

* * *

"Now," Bram said, and crouched on the step beside Ilse so their shoulders touched. "The turn. Why does it turn?"

Ilse frowned at the slate, where she'd copied the opening phrase of the morning's lesson in her careful, angular notation—a notation she was inventing herself, because the Verse-tongue had never been written down and someone had to be the first. "Because it is sad?"

"It's sad. But why does the melody *turn* there? What is it doing?"

Ilse chewed the chalk. Then her face changed—that transformation Bram had watched a hundred times now, the child's whole body becoming a listening instrument—and she hummed the phrase low, feeling for it rather than thinking. When she reached the turn she didn't stop. She let the note bend, fall away from where it was expected, and land somewhere else entirely.

"Oh," Ilse said. "It turns because it is *looking* for something."

"That's it." Bram felt the familiar warmth spread through her ribs, the one that had replaced the old hollow ache. "That's exactly it. Every covenant song turns, Ilse. Every single one. Because a covenant isn't a promise you make alone. It's a promise that goes looking for the other side. The melody reaches out and says—are you there? Will you meet me? And the second voice answers the turn, and that answering is the whole art. That's what the old guild forgot, maybe. Or what the Crown made them forget. They thought the song was the notes." She tapped the slate. "The song is the reaching."

Ilse looked at her for a long moment with those too-old eyes, the eyes of a child made from a broken covenant, a living fragment of a promise that had gone unanswered for a century. Then she set down the chalk, took a breath, and sang the phrase complete—turn and all—and this time Bram answered it.

Two voices, meeting in the middle of a morning.

Down in the yard, the other students had gone quiet to listen. Even the stream seemed to hush. When the phrase resolved, Ilse grinned—gap-toothed, entirely a child for one unguarded instant—and Bram's throat tightened with a joy so plain it was almost embarrassing, the kind of joy she had spent her whole guarded life in the Hollow believing she wasn't allowed.

"Your first full song," Bram said. "Do you know that? Beginning to end. You've sung fragments since the day you arrived. Today you sang a whole thing."

"I know." Ilse picked the chalk back up, suddenly businesslike. "I want to write it down. Before I forget how it felt."

"You won't forget."

"The Marches forgot," Ilse said simply. "The Choir of Ash forgot on purpose. The queen's grandfather made everyone forget." She smoothed the slate on her knee. "Forgetting is very strong. Writing is stronger. Write it down."

Bram stared at her. Out of the mouths of babes and living verses. "Yes," she said. "Write it down. All of it. Everything we teach you—you write it, and you keep the copies somewhere we haven't thought of yet, somewhere no crown can ever find. Promise me."

Ilse spat in her palm, an alarming gesture she must have learned from Kestrel, and held it out. Bram laughed and shook it.

* * *

She found Kestrel in the kitchen garden, which was a generous name for the tangle of turned earth behind the hall where he spent his mornings doing violence to weeds with more concentration than he'd ever given lockpicking. He worked in silence, of course. He would always work in silence. But he'd taken to humming lately—or trying to. What came out of him was less melody than intention, a shape of music with no sound in it, and somehow, impossibly, you could follow along. The students had begun to harmonize with his silence. Bram suspected that within a year the guild would have invented an entire tradition around it, and that Kestrel would pretend to be annoyed and secretly build the tradition himself.

He looked up when her shadow fell across the bean rows. His face did the thing it always did now—the small rearrangement, the softening he probably believed only happened on one side of his mouth but actually happened everywhere.

"Bram Winterbourne," he said. He could say her name perfectly. It was, she thought, his favorite word in any language, including the ones he'd lost.

"Kestrel of Nowhere-in-Particular."

"That's *Kestrel of the Road*," he signed—hands moving in the quick grammar they'd built together over the winter, a sign-language braided from smuggler's cant and Songwright gesture. *Of the road.* Though he hadn't left the valley since they'd returned. Though last month he'd quietly moved his belongings from the smuggler's loft above the kitchen into the small room off the library, the one with the good window, the one nobody had questioned him taking.

"Of the road," she agreed, "who is currently losing a war with chickweed."

"They're organized." He dusted his hands and rose, and for a moment they just stood there in the sun with the dirt smell rising between them, and Bram thought about all the sideways ways love had come to them—through bargains and debts and firelight confessions, through a man choosing the road over his own stolen voice, through a woman learning that she'd been made, not born, and deciding the making didn't get to own her. Nothing about them had ever been direct. Perhaps that was fine. Rivers weren't direct either, and they got where they were going.

"The ledger," she said.

His eyebrows went up. "Today?"

"Today."

He wiped his palms thoroughly on his thighs, which she understood completely. Some things you wanted clean hands for. Then he offered his arm, absurdly formal, courtly as a man who'd once pickpocketed a duke at his own wedding, and they walked together around the hall to the front steps, where the future was waiting with its ink bottle open.

* * *

The ledger lay open on the lectern, and the students had gathered without being asked—they always knew, somehow, the way people know when a thing is about to become a story. Maren-of-the-Marshes stood at the back with her hands folded, wearing the expression of a woman watching a debt get repaid across generations. Aldous Vey stood beside her, hat in his hands. Ilse had abandoned her slate entirely and pressed close to the lectern, chalk dust and all.

Bram dipped the pen.

Her hand hovered over the blank space below her predecessor's final entry—that long nothing she had tended like a wound. Twenty years she had believed that blankness was hers, that it was the shape of her failure, proof she'd inherited a legacy with nothing left to give. Then she'd learned the truth: the blankness wasn't hers at all. It had been carved deliberately, a vault's empty shelf, a hiding place waiting for its hidden thing. The elder who raised her had kept her name out of this book the way you keep a treasure out of a thief's inventory. Bram Winterbourne had been invisible on purpose, protected by erasure, loved in the shape of absence.

Well. The vault was open now. The covenant was reforged—not by one dissolving voice, as the old law demanded, but by three refusing voices, a girl and a smuggler and a child who would not let her sing alone. The old law was dead. The blank line belonged to whoever had the courage to fill it.

She wrote slowly, in her best hand, the hand the elder had drilled into her over twenty years of copied-out exercises:

*Bram Winterbourne. Entered this day not as vessel, not as verse, but as author. Let it be known that the covenant is not a thing we keep—it is a thing we make, and remake, and hand on. Let every name below this one be written in the same spirit.*

She hesitated. Then, beneath it, smaller:

*First among the new choir: Ilse, who knocked.*

Ilse made a sound that was half gasp and half song, and grabbed Bram's sleeve with both hands.

"My name is in the book," she whispered—in common tongue, her hardest-won sentence yet, assembled word by word over a whole winter. "My name is *in the book*."

"Where all the realm can read it," Bram said. "You wrote the first song of the new guild, little verse. I only held the pen."

Kestrel leaned in to look, and Bram watched him read the entry twice, his lips moving silently around the words—he still did that, tracing language like a man relearning a country—and when he reached *author* he went very still. Then he stepped back, pressed his fist to his heart, and gave her the smuggler's salute, the one that meant *debt acknowledged, freely owed, gladly kept.*

"There's room beneath yours," Bram said to the gathered students, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "That's what a ledger is. That's what a guild is. Not a vault. Not a cage. A chorus, written down. Whoever wants a line, come and take it."

They came. Of course they came. Maren-of-the-Marshes first, weeping openly and pretending she wasn't, writing her true name for the first time in sixty years. Then Aldous Vey, whose pen shook and whose line came out straight anyway. Then the others, one by one, the steps filling with the scratch of pens and the murmur of names being spoken aloud—names spoken aloud, in the Hollow, carrying all the way to the birches, and no wall anywhere to stop them.

* * *

Evening found Bram alone on the steps, the ledger closed and locked in the cabinet with the waking songbooks, the school quiet behind her. The valley lay violet and gold in the last light. Far off toward the western rim, where the invisible wall had stood for a hundred years, she could see travelers on the new road—actual travelers, coming down into the Hollow with packs and lanterns, drawn by rumors of a place where songs were taught freely and no voice was ever confiscated. Tomorrow some of them would knock. Tomorrow the work would continue, which was the point of work.

She thought about the queen's unsigned accord, waiting in her desk. She thought about the Withered Marches, where the fields were already flushing faintly green at their edges, color returning like blood to a numb limb. She thought about the elder who had built a cage out of love, and forgave her again, the way you forgive the dead: imperfectly, repeatedly, with gratitude underneath.

Mostly she thought about the girl who had knocked at dusk in a storm, humming a language older than names, and about the turn in every covenant song—the melody reaching out into the dark, asking *are you there, will you meet me*—and how the answer, it turned out, had never been a single voice at all. It had been a choir, waiting a hundred years for someone brave enough to begin.

Bram Winterbourne sat on the steps of her guildhall, part girl and part song, visible to the whole wide world, and she was not afraid.

Down the valley road, past the birches and the singing stream, a lantern bobbed. A rider, coming fast. Behind the rider, faint but unmistakable on the evening wind, came a sound Bram knew in her bones before her mind caught up to it: bells. Many bells, ringing in a pattern she had never heard—but which Ilse, bursting out the hall door with her slate still in hand, clearly had.

"That's the gathering-call," the child breathed. "Someone is calling a choir. Someone is calling *all* the choirs."

The lantern light grew nearer, and Bram rose to meet it.
