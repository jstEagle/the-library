## 1. Salt on the Smuggler's Road

The salt came first, as it always did. Before dawn, before the mules were loaded, before the first gray light touched the peaks of Thornfell, Astrid Eastwick walked the line of the caravan and cast a handful of coarse white grains into the frost-rimed mud.

It looked like superstition. That was the point of it. The crew watched him do it every morning — old Hesk, gap-toothed Brannic, the twins Pell and Poll with their matching scarves — and they had long ago folded it into the litany of Astrid's oddities. *The quiet one salts the road.* They thought it was luck-work, some hedge-blessing his grandmother taught him, a charm against ill weather and worse soldiers. They teased him for it, gently, because he was young and useful and never rose to the bait.

They did not know what the salt was for.

Astrid crouched at the head of the pass where the smuggler's road bent around a shoulder of black granite, and he pressed his bare palm flat against the earth, and he listened.

Not with his ears. With the thing beneath his skin that he had spent eleven years learning to keep very, very still.

The mountains spoke in ember-light. Every living thing carried it — trees, stone, water, the slow green patience of roots under snow — but it was faint here, high up where nothing grew tall, like banked coals under ash. What Astrid heard through his palm was not sound but heat: the great cold river of the pass itself, flowing down from the saddle three miles north; the small warm smudges of his crew behind him, six souls and nine mules; and — there, exactly where he'd feared it would be — a knot of iron-cold brightness below the ridge to the east. Soldiers. A checkpoint, newly pitched, its watchfire burning with that particular greedy color that meant crown-issue oil.

He lifted his hand. The knowledge settled into him the way all his knowing did: wordless, certain, and utterly useless if spoken aloud.

"Trouble?" Hesk asked, coming up beside him with a lead-rope over his shoulder. The old man's breath steamed in the dark.

"Ahead," Astrid said, which was true and safe. "Below the east ridge. I heard mule-shoes on rock when I scouted at first light." A lie braided into a truth; he was good at the weaving by now. Eleven years of practice made fine cloth.

Hesk spat thoughtfully. "Crown men?"

"Crown men."

"Then we take the Widow's Stair." Hesk turned and bellowed down the column, voice cracking like a whip across the frozen morning. "Widow's Stair, you lazy sons of donkeys! Up and over!"

Astrid stayed crouched a moment longer, letting the last threads of the land's warmth drain out of his fingers, and felt the familiar hollow ache open behind his ribs. It always came after he used the gift — a sense of having swallowed something cold that sat in his chest and slowly melted, taking a little of him with the melt. He had asked Maren about it once, years ago. She had gone very quiet and told him that magic drawn from the deep veins cost more than magic drawn from herbs and prayers, and that a boy who kept reaching for the deep veins would one day find the well of himself running shallower than he liked.

*You are not a kettle, Astrid,* she had said. *You cannot be poured out and filled again.*

He stood, brushed the salt dust from his knee, and became once more what the crew needed him to be: the quiet one, nineteen years old, good with mules and maps and weather-sense, no gift at all.

The Widow's Stair earned its name twice over. It climbed the western face of the pass in a series of switchbacks so narrow that two mules could not walk abreast, and the drop on the outward side fell away into a gorge full of mist and the distant complaint of water under ice. The contraband — salt-pork, iron nails, and forty pounds of unlicensed dreamroot bundled in sacking — rode in double-pannier loads that shifted and swayed with every step. By midmorning the sun had crested the eastern peaks and begun, grudgingly, to warm the black stone, and the crew had stopped talking altogether, saving their breath for the climb.

Astrid walked point, and in the silence he let himself feel the ember-light again, just barely, the way a man might let a candle flame lick one finger. It was the only indulgence he permitted. Up here, far from hearths and wards, the land's fire ran thin and clean, and touching it was like hearing a song he half-remembered from somewhere he could not name. He could feel the mountain's bones glowing far below, ancient and patient, and threaded through them — faint as memory, persistent as grief — something else. Something older. A warmth that did not come from stone or root at all, but seemed to come from *elsewhere*, leaking into the world through cracks too small to see.

Maren had called those leaks the Emberlands breathing. He had been seven. He had believed her the way children believe their mothers know the shape of the sky.

Now he only knew that when he felt that other-warmth, his chest hurt, and he never let himself wonder why.

"Eastwick." Pell dropped back beside him, breathing hard, scarf dark with sweat at the hairline. "Brannic says you've been off your feed. You look like a man chewing on a wasp."

"I'm fine."

"You're never anything but fine. That's what worries him." Pell grinned. His brother Poll grinned the same grin ten yards back, and between them they had once made a wager over whether Astrid smiled at all. Poll had won a copper piece proving he didn't. "There's a girl in Millbrook Cross asks after you, you know. Rosamund, the cooper's daughter. Says you've got honest eyes."

"Honest eyes," Astrid repeated flatly.

"That's what she said."

"They're the most dishonest part of me."

Pell laughed, took the joke for a joke, and clattered back down the line. Astrid watched him go and felt the cold thing in his chest shift. *Honest eyes.* If Rosamund the cooper's daughter knew what lived behind his eyes — what he saw when he let himself truly see, the burning lattice of the world, the way every living soul glowed like a coal wrapped in flesh — she would run. They would all run. Or worse, they would look at him the way the villagers of Harrow Fen had looked at the tanner's boy, four winters back, before the witch-finders came for him and the whole village stood in the square and *watched*, and nobody — 

He put the thought away. He was practiced at putting thoughts away. He had a whole cellar of them, locked and dark, and he visited each one rarely, and never for long.

* * *

They made the top of the Widow's Stair by late afternoon and camped in the shelter of an overhang whose lip hung like a petrified wave. Hesk set watches. Brannic built a small fire, shielded by stones, and they ate salt-pork and hard bread and drank snowmelt tea, and the talk turned, as it always did on this road, to the news from below.

"They hanged the Vintner in Lowmarsh," Brannic said. "Caught him with a wagonload of fae-glass. Actual fae-glass, mind, not the knockoff stuff. They say the Regent's paying fifty crowns a shard now, burn it right there on the magistrate's step."

"Fae-glass isn't contraband," Pell protested. "It's trash. Half-dead stuff. My cousin bought a piece at market and it wouldn't even glow."

"It glows enough for the crown to want it burned, doesn't it?" Hesk said quietly. He was turning his tin cup in his hands, watching the tea inside. "My brother-in-law's in the city guard. He says the Gift-Hunt's going formal come spring. Proclamations in every crossroads town. Registration rolls. All bearers of the old blood to declare themselves or be taken." He glanced up, and for a moment his eyes found Astrid's, mild and unreadable. "Old blood. Whatever that means these days."

"Means people who can do things," Poll said, with the cheerful cruelty of a man discussing weather. "Hedge-witches, cunning-men. My gran could stop milk from souring. If she were alive now she'd have to stand in a queue and sign her name for it, or swing for it."

"And would she sign?"

Poll shrugged. "She'd sign. Only a fool burns for a party trick."

Nobody answered that. The fire crackled. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the naked crags with a sound like slow breathing, and Astrid sat very still and felt the ember-light of five men flicker around him — Brannic's steady orange, the twins' quick twin-flames, Hesk's banked and thoughtful red — and understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife laid flat against his skin, that he was sitting inside a circle of men who would have to decide, someday soon, whether he belonged in a queue or on a rope.

And that Hesk, watching him over the rim of his cup, might already be wondering.

*He can't know,* Astrid told himself. *There's nothing to know. I'm the quiet one. I salt the road.*

But the cold thing in his chest grew a little heavier, and he slept badly, and dreamed of a kitchen that smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke, and a woman's voice saying *you cannot be poured out and filled again,* and woke with frost on his eyelashes and the taste of ash in his mouth.

* * *

The trouble came on the third day, on the long descent toward Millbrook Cross, and it came not from the checkpoint Astrid had smelled at the week's start but from a second one — new, mobile, pitched overnight in a defile where the road narrowed between walls of wet shale. Astrid tasted it a mile out: the greasy crown-fire, the iron-and-oath brightness of armed men, and beneath it something that made the hair on his arms rise. Dogs. Two of them, maybe three, and dogs were a problem no amount of route-planning could solve, because dogs did not read roads. Dogs read *people*.

He called the halt early, citing a lame mule, and got the crew into the rocks while he went forward alone to look. He didn't need to look. He already knew: eight soldiers, a sergeant with a badge that carried its own cold authority like a lantern, a portable barrier of sharpened stakes across the road, and the dogs staked near the fire, big brindle things with the flat patient hunger of animals bred for one purpose.

Witch-hounds. Not the crown's ordinary trackers — the special kind, the kind the Gift-Hunt used, bred on something Maren had never named but had once described, obliquely, with a face like closed shutters. They could smell the old blood the way sharks smelled a cut in water.

Astrid crouched behind a boulder and did arithmetic. Eight men, three dogs, one narrow road. The caravan could turn back — lose three days, lose the delivery window, maybe lose the contract. Or they could try the defile and trust that eight soldiers cared more about dreamroot than about a raggedy salt-train, and pray the dogs didn't catch the scent of the one thing in the world that mattered less than cargo and more than life.

Because here was the truth he could not say aloud, the truth that sat in his chest like swallowed winter: the dogs would not smell the dreamroot. They would smell *him*. And when they smelled him, they would not be gentle, and neither would the sergeant, and neither would the rope.

He went back and told Hesk the size of it. The old man chewed his mustache for a long time.

"Eight men," he said. "We're eleven, counting the twins' knives twice."

"Soldiers with a charter," Astrid said. "They kill us legal, and the next train turns back for a season, and the road dies. We go around. North fork adds two days but there's no picket on it — I'd stake my share on it."

"We'll lose the Millbrook window."

"We'll lose our necks otherwise."

Hesk looked at him a moment too long. Around them the crew waited, stamping against the cold, and Astrid felt their attention settle on him with the weight of a verdict pending. *Why does the quiet one know so much about where the soldiers aren't? Why is he always the one to scout, to smell trouble, to salt the road?*

"North fork," Hesk said at last, and the moment broke. But as the crew turned, Brannic muttered something to Poll, and Poll laughed, and though Astrid couldn't hear the words he could guess their shape, and the cold thing in his chest grew another degree colder.

They lost the light in the north fork and made a cold camp, no fire, pressed into a fold of the hill. Astrid took first watch because he always volunteered for first watch — it was the only hour of any day when he could let the gift breathe, spread his senses up and down the valley like a man stretching after a long confinement, feeling for the cold knots of soldiers and the bright thread of the road ahead.

That was how he felt them coming. Not soldiers. The dogs.

Two of them, running loose ahead of a patrol that had circled wide, and they had caught something on the wind — not the caravan, not yet, but the ghost-trace of the trail they'd left that morning, and dogs that found a ghost followed it to the body. They were a half-mile out and closing, and behind them, torches bobbing along the ridgeline, the patrol.

Astrid stood in the freezing dark and understood that he had perhaps ten minutes and exactly one option, and the option was a thing he had sworn never to do, a thing Maren had bound him with words never to do, a thing that had a price written on it in letters he had read only once and never forgotten.

Memory-charms were not illusions. Illusions faded. Memory-charms *burned* — and they burned fuel, and the only fuel a hedge-charm ever had was the charmer.

*You will spend yourself,* Maren had said. *Piece by piece. A charm takes a memory to make a memory. Never forget that, my love. The fire has to eat something.*

He had eleven years of carefully buried self to spend, and no time to choose carefully.

He knelt in the snow where the trail bent, laid out the salt — his own salt, the road-salt, the one honest-seeming tool he had — and began to work. It was fast and ugly, hedge-magic at its crudest: a woven suggestion laid across the trail-scent, a false memory seeded into whatever mind crossed it. *Nothing here. Cold trail. The caravan passed days ago, heading south.* Simple. Crude. Enough for dogs, enough for the men holding their leads.

The fire reached up through his hands and asked for its price.

He fed it a memory almost at random, snatching blindly in the cellar of himself, and felt it torn out root and stem — and only as the hole opened did he see what he had given. Not a shame. Not a fear. Something small and golden: an afternoon, years ago, on a riverbank with the crew, before the twins had stopped trusting him, when Poll had taught him to skip stones and he'd gotten three skips on his first throw and Poll had slapped his shoulder and laughed and said *natural, Eastwick, you're a natural* —

Gone. Not faded, not dimmed. Gone, a tooth pulled from the jaw of his life, and he could feel the smooth edge of the socket where it had been, and he knew that tomorrow he would look at Poll and feel nothing but the shape of the absence, a laugh he couldn't place, a warmth with no story attached.

The dogs reached the bend and stopped. Astrid lay flat in the snow and watched them circle, noses down, brindle bodies stiff — and then, as the woven lie took hold, he watched them lose interest. One sniffed twice more and sat. The other simply turned, trotting back the way it came, and the torches on the ridge wavered, halted, and began to withdraw.

He stayed flat until the cold soaked through him completely. Then he got up, shook the snow from his coat, and walked back to the fold of the hill where his crew slept, and sat down among them, and kept the rest of the watch alone.

* * *

Near dawn, Poll woke, stamped his feet, and settled beside him to share the last of the watch. For a while neither spoke. The eastern sky turned the color of a healing bruise.

"Pell says you saved us yesterday," Poll said finally. "North fork. Says the dogs would've had us."

"The dogs weren't after us."

"Doesn't matter. Hesk thinks so too. He's talking about cutting you in past the usual share." Poll knocked his shoulder companionably against Astrid's. "Natural, Eastwick. You're a natural."

The words landed in Astrid like a coin dropped down a well. He heard them, heard the echo of a memory he no longer owned saying them once before, and he could not find where the echo came from, and the smooth socket in his heart ached like weather coming.

"Thanks," he said.

They watched the sun rise over Thornfell, and below them, far off, the roofs of Millbrook Cross began to show through the valley haze — smoke rising straight and thin from a hundred chimneys, a town asleep and innocent, and somewhere in it a royal crier with a scroll in his hand, though Astrid could not have known that yet. He only knew that the road went down, and that he was carrying less than he'd started with, and that the fire inside him had eaten again and would, someday, unless he found another way, eat everything.

He took out the salt bag and weighed it in his hand.

Then he poured the first handful for the morning, and led them down.
