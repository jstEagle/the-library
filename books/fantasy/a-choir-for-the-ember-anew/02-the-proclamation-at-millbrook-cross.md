## 2. The Proclamation at Millbrook Cross

The rain had followed Astrid down from the high passes for three days, patient as a debt-collector, and by the time he reached Millbrook Cross it had settled into a fine grey drizzle that made the whole valley look like something painted and left out in the weather. He came in on the drover's track with his hood up and his collar turned against the damp, leading a mule loaded with salted eel and iron nails — nothing that would raise an eyebrow, nothing worth remembering him for. That was the trick of it. You did not pass through a place unseen; you passed through it *unremarked*, the way water passes through cloth.

Millbrook was not his home. He had no home, if he was honest about the thing, and honesty came to him rarely enough that when it did arrive he treated it like a stranger at the door. But he had wintered twice in this valley in the years since Maren's cottage burned behind him, and he knew its rhythms the way you know a song heard through a wall. The mill wheel turning brown water. The smell of woodsmoke and wet wool. Old Hesta's geese shouting at the sky over nothing. It was the kind of village that would forget your face before you reached the far hedge, which was precisely why he liked it.

He should have kept moving. The plan — there was always a plan, folded up small and carried next to his ribs like a second knife — was to cross the ford below the mill before dark and be three valleys gone by morning. But the square at Millbrook Cross had gathered a crowd, and crowds on a grey day meant either a hanging or news, and Millbrook was too small for hangings.

Astrid told himself he stopped because of the mule, who had thrown a shoe two miles back and favored her near foreleg on the cobbles. He told himself a great many things. He was good at telling himself things; it was the first skill Maren had ever taught him, though she hadn't known she was teaching it.

* * *

The crier stood on the mounting-block outside the Wheelwright's Rest, a narrow man in crown-blue with a voice like slate splitting, flanked by two soldiers who looked bored in the way of men paid by the day rather than the deed. A herald's sash hung across the crier's chest, and pinned to it, catching what little light the day allowed, was a badge Astrid's stomach recognized before his mind caught up: a black circle ringed in white flame. The sigil of the Office of Gifts. He had seen it once before, stamped on a letter Maren burned without reading aloud.

"By order of the Regent's court," the crier called, and the crowd hushed the way a field hushes before hail. "And by the authority vested in the Commission for the Registry of Inherited Gifts: let it be proclaimed throughout the vale, the fells, and all lands of the Thornfell march."

He unrolled a parchment with ceremony that felt rehearsed down to the crease.

"Whereas it is found that certain persons carry in their blood the old covenant-gifts, being remnants of the ancient pact with the Emberlands; and whereas such gifts, ungoverned, are a peril to crown and commons alike; let every bearer of the old blood present themselves to the registry within forty days of this proclamation, to be counted, recorded, and granted license under law." He paused, wetted his lips. "Those who fail to register shall be deemed concealers, and taken."

*Taken.* The word went through the crowd like a fishhook through a net, snagging everyone a little.

An old shepherd near the front spat into the mud. "Counted," he said loudly, "like sheep."

The soldier to the crier's left shifted his weight. Nobody answered the old man, but nobody laughed either. That was the difference between a village like Millbrook and a market town — here the joke stayed in the throat where it was safer.

Astrid stood at the edge of the square with his hand flat against the mule's warm neck, feeling her heartbeat come slow and even under his palm, and made himself listen to every word. This was discipline. Panic wanted him to turn around now, walk back up the drover's track, and never learn what was said. But a smuggler who ran from every checkpoint died on the first road that forked, because he'd never learned which fork held the hunters. So he listened, and catalogued, and hated how easily his mind worked at this — sorting threat from noise, filing away the number of soldiers (two visible, likely four more billeted at the Rest), noting which faces in the crowd went pale and which merely went still.

Forty days. Registration. *License under law.*

Maren had used other words for it, once, sitting by the fire with her hands wrapped around a cup she never drank from. *They will call it counting, little ember. Counting is always the first word. The last word is burning, and between them lies a long gray country full of lists.* He had been fourteen and thought she dramatized. He had been fourteen and thought most of what she said was theater — the herbs, the warnings, the way she'd stand some nights at the window staring east toward hills that weren't there anymore, toward a land only she could see the edges of.

He missed her so sharply just then that it felt physical, a hook set behind his sternum. Three years since he'd fled her hearth, and grief still ambushed him at the stupidest moments. A proclamation. Wet wool. The smell of rosemary off someone's kitchen garden.

"—and let it be further known," the crier was saying, "that the Commission pays honest bounty for information regarding concealed bearers. Ten crowns silver for a true naming. Fifty for the taking of a registered bearer who flees his license."

Ten crowns. Half the square did arithmetic they'd deny doing. Astrid saw it move through their faces like wind through barley — not greed, mostly, not in Millbrook, but the terrible neutral arithmetic of the poor, the same look they gave a sick ewe or a flooded field. *What is it worth. What does it cost me to know this.*

He should go. He should be going. The mule shifted, and he murmured nonsense at her, and did not move.

* * *

That was when he saw Wren.

She was eleven, maybe twelve — the daughter of the Tanner family who held the croft at the valley's mouth, though everyone called her Wren for the way she talked, quick and constant, a bright chatter that filled any room she entered. Astrid had sold her father eel-hooks the previous autumn and she'd talked at him the entire transaction, asking questions with the relentless sincerity of children: Was it true the high passes had wolves? Had he ever seen the Emberlands' lights, the ones in the old songs? Her mother had shushed her — *enough, Wren, leave the man be* — and Astrid had said, surprising himself, "They say on clear nights, in the deep fells, you can see fires that burn blue. But you'd have to walk farther than anyone walks." And Wren had looked at him with such naked delight that he'd felt, for one absurd moment, like a person instead of a disguise.

Now she stood near the front of the crowd, on tiptoe, craning to see the crier, her hair plastered dark with drizzle. She wasn't afraid. She was *interested*. Of course she was — she was a child, and proclamations were theater, and ten crowns was a story-quantity of money.

Then one of the soldiers stepped forward and took her arm.

It happened fast and strangely gently, the way these things were done now, done well, done with practice. The soldier bent and spoke to her, low, and Wren's face went through confusion into something else, and then her mother was screaming through the crowd — actually screaming, a sound Astrid had never heard from that quiet, capable woman — and the tanner himself shoved forward and was met by the second soldier's leveled spear, not thrusting, just *present*, the way you show a dog the stick so it understands.

"She's a child," the tanner was saying, over and over, in the reasonable tone of a man bargaining with weather. "Whatever you think — she's twelve years old, she's my daughter—"

"The girl showed signs at the spring muster," the first soldier said, still holding Wren's arm, courteous as a dancing master. "Lights in the palms during the fever, witnessed by the muster-master. The registry requires her. There'll be assessment. Perhaps schooling, if the gift proves small. Perhaps license." His tone made clear what *perhaps* covered.

"I don't understand," Wren said. She said it loudly, in her carrying chattering voice, and then again quietly, to no one, and the second saying was worse than the first. "I didn't do anything. Mam, I didn't *do* anything—"

Her mother had gotten past the spear somehow — mothers did — and was clawing at the soldier's grip, and for a moment the whole square teetered on the edge of something ugly, forty people deciding all at once what kind of world they lived in. Astrid felt the moment balance. He felt it the way he could feel heat through a wall, or the wrongness in a load that would shift on a switchback — that other sense, the buried one, the ember-sense, waking up along his nerves like a struck match smelling itself.

He could feel Wren. That was the obscene part. Even across the square, through the drizzle and the crowd, he could feel the small warm flicker of her — new-lit, unshaped, a candleflame in a drafty church, guttering and stubborn. The old blood. Covenant-blood. Whatever had shown in her palms during the fever was real, and rare, and hers, and it marked her the way his marked him, and the crown had come with a ledger to collect her.

*Do something,* said a voice in him that sounded young and sounded like Maren.

*Do what?* answered the voice he'd built over three years, the survivor's voice, level and cold and tired. *Burn a charm on a soldier? Two soldiers, four more inside, a valley with one road out? Reveal yourself to save one child and doom every bearer in the march, because they'll know then what they're hunting, they'll have proof it hides among them—*

*You burned a memory-charm on Tam Coyle's boy not a month ago,* said the Maren-voice. *You can spend yourself for strangers on the mountain road but not here, in daylight, in front of everyone? What are you saving yourself for, Astrid Eastwick? For what?*

*I don't know,* he admitted, to the fire and the rain and no one. *To keep living. Isn't that enough?*

*Is it?*

The soldier turned with Wren, walking her toward the Rest, where the billeted men would be, where a cart waited — Astrid saw it now, canvas-topped, standing in the yard, and understood the whole choreography of the morning. They had not come for the proclamation. The proclamation was the excuse. They had come for Wren, and read their decree to a captive audience while they did it, so that everyone in the vale would learn exactly what *register* meant.

Wren twisted once and looked back at the crowd. Looking, Astrid realized with a horror that arrived slowly and then all at once, for a face. Any face. Someone who would step out of the crowd and make it stop, because surely someone would, because the world she'd lived in for twelve years was a world where someone would.

Forty people looked at the mud, the eaves, their own hands. Hesta's geese shouted at the sky.

Astrid looked at Wren. He let her see him looking — some animal honesty in it, the least he could give — and her eyes found his and held for half a heartbeat, recognition dawning, the eel-hook man, the man who'd promised her blue fires in the deep fells, and something in her face *asked* him, asked without words, asked the question children shouldn't have to know existed—

He lowered his eyes.

When he raised them the yard door had swallowed her. The screaming had stopped. Her mother knelt in the mud of the square, rocking, and the tanner stood beside her with his hat crushed in both fists, and the crier was already rolling up his parchment, already moving to the next village, already forgetting.

* * *

Astrid did not remember leaving the square, which frightened him more than anything else that happened that day. He remembered fragments: the mule's bridle in his fist, the ford's brown water, the mill wheel turning its endless patient circles. He remembered standing on the far bank longer than he should have, letting the drizzle soak through to the skin, because the cold gave him something true to feel that wasn't shame.

Shame was a country he knew well. He had emigrated there three years ago and never quite taken out citizenship — always the sojourner, the man passing through. But this was different from the ordinary background hum of it. This had a name attached, and a face, and a small carrying voice that said *I didn't do anything.*

Because here was the truth he'd been circling for three years, the truth he could dodge on any given morning but not today: hiding was not free. Every lie cost. He'd learned that on the smuggler's roads — every memory-charm burned took a coin of himself with it, some small warmth, some color; he'd caught himself lately unable to recall the exact sound of Maren's laugh, only that he had once known it, and he suspected the charms had eaten it, spent it like toll-money. Concealment was a fire that fed on its keeper. And now the concealment had a price tag nailed to the church door for the whole realm to read: *ten crowns silver for a true naming.* His neighbors — and they were not his neighbors, that was the point of never staying, but they were somebody's neighbors, everywhere, in every vale — his fellow travelers in hiding would be weighed and priced by the people around them, and most of those people were decent, and decency had never once stopped a hungry family from doing arithmetic.

He climbed out of the valley as the light failed, taking the shepherd's track north and east, away from the ford he'd planned, away from the low country entirely. The plan revised itself in his head with the smooth ease of long practice: deeper into the Thornfells, past the salt-roads, past the last summer pastures, into the high white country where even smugglers didn't winter and witch-hunters' sniffer-hounds lost scent in the scree. Alone. Alone had always been the shape of safety. Alone was the shape he'd cut himself into, carefully, over years, until it fit through every narrow place.

But tonight, climbing through the wet dark with the mule's bell chiming soft ahead of him, the shape fit badly. Tonight he kept seeing a girl on tiptoe in a crowd, delighted by the world, looking for a face that would help her, finding his — and watching his eyes go down.

*Forty days,* he thought. Forty days until registration closed, and after that the taking began in earnest, house to house, vale to vale, with the ledger and the black-and-flame sigil and men who said *perhaps* in voices like dancing masters. Forty days, and somewhere out in the fells and vales there were others like Wren — like him — unregistered, uncounted, each one alone, each one certain that alone was the shape of safety, each one wrong.

He stopped at the top of the pass where the shepherd's track split, one branch dropping toward the salt-road and the world he knew, the other climbing toward the white country and the world that wanted him dead or silent. Rain ticked against his hood. Below, far behind, Millbrook's few lamps glimmered like coals raked over.

He thought of Maren at her window, staring east at a land only she could see, and heard her voice with terrible clarity for the first time in months: *You cannot hide forever, little ember. Fire doesn't hide. It only waits for air.*

"Then I'll find higher ground than the smoke can climb," he said aloud, and hated how young his voice sounded, and took the upward track into the dark.

Behind him, in the vale, the forty days had already begun to run.
