## 3. What the Apprentice Left Behind

The fire in Maren's cottage never went out.

Astrid learned that the first night he arrived, half-frozen and eleven years old, delivered to her door by a peddler who had found him shivering in a ditch outside Thornfell with his mother's ring burned into the palm of his hand. The peddler had wanted nothing for his trouble except a hot meal. Maren gave him one, then stood in the doorway watching him go until the road swallowed him, and when she turned back to the boy dripping on her hearthstones she said only, "Well. You're late."

"Late for what?" Astrid had asked, teeth chattering.

"For everything," she said, and set a bowl of barley stew in front of him, and that was how it began.

Now he was nineteen, and the fire still hadn't gone out. He knew — because he had asked once, years ago, expecting some clever hedge-trick — that it wasn't magic keeping it alive. It was discipline. Maren banked it every night before bed, raked the coals together like a woman tucking children under blankets, fed it a single log at dawn. Eighteen years of small attentions. When he'd asked why she bothered, when any village girl could strike flint to steel, she had looked at him with those pale eyes that seemed always to be looking slightly past him, at something standing just over his shoulder.

"A fire you keep is not the same as a fire you make," she said. "Anyone can make one. Keeping one means you've decided something deserves to go on living." She stirred the pot. "You'll understand eventually what I've decided about."

He hadn't understood. He had thought it was just an old woman's sentiment, the kind of thing said over soup by people whose lives had shrunk to the size of their kitchens. He had thought a great many foolish things in those years, and the shame of them came back to him now, riding through the dark of the smugglers' roads with a memory-charm's ash still bitter on his tongue, as though the past had been waiting all this time for exactly this moment to collect its debt.

Because tonight, for the first time since he fled, Astrid let himself remember properly. Not the edited version he kept folded up in the pocket of his mind — the tidy story of a restless apprentice outgrowing a small teacher — but all of it, uncut, the way the ember-light showed him truths whether he wanted them or not.

* * *

Maren's cottage sat where two valleys met above the village of Ashcombe, close enough to walk down for market, far enough that no one did unless they needed something only a hedge-witch could give. She delivered babies and set bones. She read the weather in the behavior of crows. She brewed fever-tonics and love-charms of such gentle uselessness that they worked mostly by making the drinker believe in them, which, she liked to say, was how most charms worked anyway.

And twice a year, on the turning of the seasons, she walked alone up the old shepherd track behind her house to a place she never named, carrying a lantern she never lit, and came back the next morning smelling faintly of woodsmoke and something older than woodsmoke — something like the air after lightning.

Astrid followed her once. He was fourteen, full of the invincibility of that age, and he told himself it was concern rather than curiosity, and believed himself for almost the whole climb.

She stopped before he ever saw her stop — simply spoke to the hillside ahead of her without turning around. "If you're going to follow someone, lad, learn to breathe quieter. You sound like a bellows with a grudge."

He came out from behind the gorse, ears burning. "I only wanted—"

"I know what you wanted." She sighed, and there was no anger in it, only a tiredness so deep it seemed geological. "Come on, then. Better you see it from me than from nothing."

What he saw, when they crested the last shoulder of the track, stopped the breath in his chest.

A stone. A single standing stone, taller than three men, black as cooled iron, rising from a bowl of moorland grass that should not have been green in the dying days of autumn. And in the stone — not carved upon it but *in* it, the way light lives in a lamp behind shuttered glass — glowed a seam of gold. It pulsed. Slowly. He understood, in the wordless way one understands a heartbeat or a held breath, that he was looking at something alive.

"The covenant-stone," Maren said quietly. "One of nine, once. This is the only one left burning."

"Burning?"

"That's what the light is. Ember-work. The old pact made flesh and stone." She walked toward it, and as she moved the seam brightened, warming like a face turning toward a familiar voice. "Before your grandmother's grandmother, the Emberlands and our world were one country with a border drawn through it. The fae kept the wild magic; we kept the fields and the fires and the dead. The covenant bound the two halves together — their light feeding our hearths, our memory feeding their songs. Nine stones, nine bearers, one promise." She laid her palm flat against the rock, and the gold beneath it flared gently, and Astrid felt the warmth of it across twenty feet of open air, like standing near an oven door. "Then the Emberlands began to fade. No one knows fully why. The bearers scattered or died or forgot what they were, and the stones went dark one by one. All but this."

"Why does it still burn?"

Maren took her hand away. For a long moment she didn't answer, and the wind moved the grass around them in slow silver waves.

"Because someone keeps it," she said finally. "Someone has kept it, generation after generation, without title or thanks, the way I keep my hearth. A fire you decide deserves to go on living." She turned and looked at him straight on, and for the first time in four years he saw that her pale eyes were not vague at all. They were the sharpest things he had ever been looked at by. "It's time you told me what you see when you look at it, Astrid Eastwick. Truly. Don't lie to me. Lying to me wastes both our evenings, and I've a stew on."

He opened his mouth to say he saw a glowing rock. What came out instead, pulled out of him like a splinter, was the truth:

"It's singing. There's a — it isn't a song, exactly, it's more like... heat. Like standing in a doorway between a warm room and a cold one, and I can feel both at once, and I can feel where the door is thin. And there are threads. Gold threads going out from the stone, over the hills, and most of them are cut, frayed ends, but a few—" His voice cracked. He hadn't spoken of it aloud since his mother died, and saying it felt like opening a wound he'd convinced himself was a scar. "A few still burn."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the sun to move.

"Ember-sight," Maren said at last, very softly. "So it's true. So it came down whole."

"What do you mean, it came down—"

"Your mother," she said, "was the keeper's daughter. And you are the keeper's heir, whether either of us likes it or not."

* * *

After that evening, everything changed and nothing changed, which Astrid would later decide was the cruelest way for anything to change.

Maren did not suddenly begin treating him differently in the daylight hours. She still sent him to market with a list and scolded him for haggling badly. She still made him grind valerian until his arms ached. But now there were other lessons too, fitted in around the ordinary ones like dark threads through plain cloth. She taught him the names of the nine stones, or the ones anyone remembered. She taught him the shape of the covenant — not its history, which filled books nobody wrote anymore, but its logic: reciprocity, balance, the understanding that neither realm could survive as a closed fist. She taught him which of the gold threads still burned, and where they led, and what it meant that they were growing thinner each year, the way a river thins approaching its mouth.

And she taught him fear.

Not deliberately. That was the terrible part. She taught him fear simply by being afraid herself, and being bad at hiding it. When riders passed on the valley road below — crown riders, tax men, the occasional gray-cloaked figure whose badge bore a closed eye over a flame — she would go quiet in a particular way, her hands stilling in whatever work they held. Once, when a proclamation was nailed to the Ashcombe post announcing the crown's new registry of "persons of unsanctioned gift," she read it twice and then burned it in her hearth, which was illegal, and watched it burn with an expression Astrid had never seen on her face before.

"You could register," he offered carefully. "Say you're a licensed midwife. Half the villages would swear to it."

"And say what of you?" She didn't turn from the fire. "A boy of nineteen with a gift the registry has no name for? They have categories, Astrid. Hedge-craft. Wild-tongue. Second-sight. They'll write you down in one of their little boxes and file you under one of their little laws, and none of their boxes will hold what you are, and when they discover that — and they will discover it, because boxes that don't fit get examined —" She finally looked at him. "Do you know what happened to the last keeper's line?"

"No."

"Neither does anyone else. That's what happened to them."

He laughed, because the alternative was not laughing, and she did not laugh with him.

"They're not hunting yet," she said. "But hunts begin as lists. Lists begin as registries. Registries begin as proclamations nailed to posts in market squares, asking nicely." She poked the fire harder than it deserved. "I have kept you hidden for eight years by making you small. Small lessons, small magics, small life. And it has worked, and I am glad it has worked, and I hate it. Because you were meant to stand in front of that stone and be seen, and I have spent eight years teaching you how to vanish."

"Maybe vanishing is enough," he said. "Maybe the stone doesn't need—"

"It needs what it needs." Her voice gentled. "That's the trouble with destinies, child. They aren't arguments. You can't out-talk them. You can only accept them or spend your whole life listening to them knock."

* * *

The knocking got louder the winter he turned twenty.

It began with dreams. He dreamed of the stone, always the same dream: the seam of gold flickering like a guttering candle, and somewhere far off along the frayed threads a sound like a door closing in an empty house, one after another, doors closing, and each closing made the stone dimmer, and each dimming woke him with his heart slamming and his hands — his hands were the worst part — his hands glowing faintly at the fingertips, gold light leaking out of him like warmth from a cracked window, and he would have to press them against the cold floorboards until the light died.

Maren knew. Of course she knew; the cottage was small and her hearing was better than her age admitted. One morning after a particularly bad night she set his porridge down and said, without preamble:

"The stone is calling its heir. It does that, when a keeper dies without passing the keeping on. Your mother was meant to take it up. She was meant to bring you to it when you came of age." A pause. "She ran out of time. So the stone has waited, and waited, and now it has begun to call the next in the blood, because a covenant-stone cannot choose. It can only ask what already belongs to it."

"I don't want it." He heard his own voice come out flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "I never chose any of this. My mother didn't tell me. You didn't tell me for eight years. And now I'm supposed to just — kneel down in front of a rock and hand over my whole life?"

"Yes," Maren said. "That is precisely what's asked. And no one — least of all me — will pretend it isn't a great deal to hand over."

"There must be another way."

"There is always another way. Most of them are worse." She sat down across from him, folding her hands on the table, and when she spoke again her voice had the careful evenness of someone walking on ice they know is thin. "Let me tell you what acceptance looks like, so you can refuse it honestly instead of refusing it blind. It looks like this: you go up the shepherd track at the solstice. You lay your hands on the stone. You don't surrender your gift — the sight stays yours, always yours — but you bind it. You weave it into the covenant, thread by thread, until your seeing and the stone's burning are one motion. You become the hinge. The Emberlands' light passes through you into the world, and the world's memory passes back, and as long as one keeper stands, the last stone burns, and as long as the last stone burns, the border holds, and the blight that is coming for both realms finds the door barred."

"The blight."

"You've seen it. Don't tell me you haven't. The rivers running silver in the high fells. The bees forgetting their way home. The fae lights going out along the Thornfell ridges, one each winter, fewer each spring." She leaned forward. "Both realms are sickening, Astrid. The Emberlands are fading and our world is drying up behind them, because the two were watered from the same root and the root is failing. The covenant was the tending of that root. The crown has forgotten. The court plays at witch-hunts and registries while the garden dies. Someone has to tend it. The stone says that someone is you."

"And if I say no?"

"Then the stone dims, in its own time, and goes dark like the other eight, and the threads go dark, and whatever comes next comes for everyone equally." She said it without drama, without pleading, which somehow made it worse. "I won't lie to you. I have thought for eight years about how to say this so you'd stay, and I have concluded there is nothing I can say. Duty that's argued into place isn't duty. So I'll say only this, and then never raise it again unless you do: freedom bought by walking away from the only door that bars the flood is not freedom. It's just a longer road to drowning, with better scenery."

* * *

He stayed six more weeks.

He told himself he was thinking it over. He told himself he owed Maren a fair hearing, owed his mother's memory consideration, owed the stone — absurdly, infuriatingly — courtesy. He ground valerian. He went to market. He slept badly and dreamed of closing doors and woke with gold leaking from his fingers, and each morning the leak came a little easier, as though his skin were learning the shape of a gate.

And underneath all of it, honest as a stone in a boot, sat the thing he would not look at directly: he was afraid.

Not of the burden. That was what he told himself — that his refusal was principled, that no one should inherit a destiny unchosen, that Maren herself had said destiny wasn't an argument and he was entitled to decline the terms. All of that was even true. But it was not the truth entire. The truth entire was that he had spent twelve years learning to be small, and smallness fit him like a coat broken in by wearing, and the thought of standing in the open on a moor with his hands full of light, seen — truly seen — by whatever saw such things, made something in his chest shrink and whimper like a kicked dog.

He was afraid of being known. That was the whole of it. Twelve years of concealment had not been survival practice; they had been construction of a self built entirely of averted eyes, and he did not know who he would be if anyone ever actually looked.

So he left at night, the way cowards and smugglers leave, taking only what fit in a satchel and telling himself it was freedom.

He left a note. He hated himself for the note even as he wrote it — three lines on the back of a seed packet, *gone to seek work over the pass, don't wait up, thank you for everything* — because he knew Maren would read it and know it for exactly what it was, and he knew she would let him go, because she had promised never to raise it again unless he did, and he had not raised it. He had run instead. Fear dressed in the language of choice, walking out the door with a satchel and calling itself free.

At the top of the shepherd track he stopped. Just for a moment. Below him the cottage windows glowed, and beyond them, invisible in the dark, the covenant-stone stood in its bowl of moorland with its seam of dying gold, and he felt it — felt it notice him leaving, the way a hearth notices a draft under the door. Not angry. Not pleading. Just attentive, patient, the attention of something that had waited eighty years and could wait eighty more, though it might not have eighty more to wait with.

*I don't want this,* he said silently, to the stone, to Maren, to his mother's grave in the Ashcombe kirkyard. *I never chose it.*

And something answered — not in words, never in words, in warmth only, faint and far off, like a fire glimpsed through a window of a house you are walking away from:

*You were chosen before you could choose. The choosing left is only this: what you do about it.*

He walked on. The pass took him south, into the Thornfells, into the smugglers' roads, into eight years of contraband and false names and banked fires in wayside inns that he tended with unnecessary devotion and never examined why. He got good at vanishing. He got excellent at it. He became, in time, a man so thoroughly constructed of averted eyes that he nearly succeeded in fooling the one person whose gaze mattered.

Himself.

* * *

Now, on the smugglers' road with the ash of a burned memory-charm souring his mouth, Astrid finally let himself finish the memory he had spent eight years cutting short.

Because he had gone back. Once. Three winters ago, in the early days of his smuggling runs, when Ashcombe lay conveniently near a new route. He had told himself he was only passing through. He climbed the shepherd track at dusk meaning only to look, and the cottage was there, and the fire was lit in the window, and he had gotten as far as the gate.

Through the window he saw Maren at her table, older, much older, her hair gone white as frost, bent over a letter she was reading by candlelight. Even from the gate he could see the seal on it. Gray wax. A closed eye over a flame.

She read it a long time. Then she put it down, pressed both hands flat against the table the way she did when steadying herself against news, and looked — slowly, deliberately, with those sharp pale eyes that had never once missed him — directly at the window. Directly at the darkness where he stood.

She couldn't have seen him. It was impossible. It was completely impossible, and she looked at him anyway, for a long, level moment, and then she rose and banked her fire with the same small devotions as always, and blew out her candle, and left the window dark, and Astrid stood at the gate of the woman who had saved him and taught him and asked nothing of him but the one thing he was too frightened to give, until the cold drove him back down the track, and he never returned, and told himself that was a choice too.

He had told himself many things. He had been, he understood now, trudging northward through the dark toward Hollowmere and the hunters and the bargain he could feel waiting for him like weather, a very gifted liar — but only to one audience. Everyone else he'd deceived easily. Deceiving yourself, it turned out, required talent, and he'd had it, and spent it lavishly, for eight years.

The ember-sight stirred in him as he walked, unbidden, threading gold through the black landscape: the faint far pulse of the covenant-stone behind him, still burning, still waiting, thinner than it had been three winters ago. Thinner than it had been last month.

Something was hastening. Something in the pattern of the fading had changed, the way a sickness changes when it turns from chronic to mortal. Far off to the east, past the passes, past the borders of the map, he could feel doors closing faster now — one after another after another — and beneath the closing, faint but unmistakable, carried along the old gold threads like a voice down a wire:

a summons. Not Maren's. Older. The stone itself, or whatever spoke through stones, calling home what belonged to it.

Astrid Eastwick, runaway, liar, keeper's heir, stood on the smugglers' road under the indifferent stars and understood, with a clarity eight years of flight had purchased at ruinous price, that he had never left at all. Every road he'd walked had been a circle drawn around that moorland bowl. Every false name had been a variation on one true one. And the knocking Maren had warned him of had never stopped — it had only grown quiet enough, all these years, to be mistaken for silence.

Ahead of him, down the pass, torchlight flickered where it should not be: a checkpoint, a hunt, the crown's gray cloaks moving through the night like a closing hand.

Behind him, far away, a fire he had refused to keep went on burning without him, and called him by the only name he had never dared use.

He tightened his grip on his satchel and walked toward the torchlight — toward Hollowmere, toward Drevane, toward the bargain that would put a leash on him and aim him, like a hound, at the very people he had spent eight years pretending he did not belong to.

Fear dressed as freedom had carried him this far. He wondered, with a bitterness that tasted almost like hope, what freedom dressed as fear would ask of him next.
