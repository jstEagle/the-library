## 4. Hunted Through the White Pass

The snow began at dusk, soft as an apology, and by midnight it was trying to kill him.

Astrid had crossed the White Pass twice before in his smuggling years, and both times he'd sworn never again. It was a liar's road — a shelf of stone barely wide enough for a mule, carved into the mountain's throat where the wind came down from the high snowfields like something starving. In summer it was merely treacherous. In the first hard snow of the season, it was a grave with a scenic view.

He climbed anyway, because the low roads had teeth in them now.

He had heard the crier's voice in his sleep for three nights running. *All bearers of the old blood shall present themselves for registry, by order of the Crown.* He had watched a girl with flour on her hands dragged through Millbrook Cross by men in grey-and-scarlet, and he had stood among the crowd with his hands in his pockets like everyone else, like a stone, like a coward, and done nothing. The shame of it had followed him up out of the valley, up the goat trails and the deadfalls, up into the passes where even the crown's writ went thin.

But the hounds did not care how high he climbed.

He heard them first as a wrongness in the wind — a rhythm under the white noise, a *tup-tup-tup* of many feet in snow, and threaded through it, faint as a hair pulled taut, a smell that only he could smell. That was the cruelty of his gift. Ember-sight was not sight at all, not really. It was a way of *tasting* the world's fire, and every living thing burned with its own flavor. Most people tasted of hearthsmoke and tallow, small warm flames guttering in flesh. The hounds behind him tasted of something else — of iron and wet ash, of fire that had been *taught* to hate.

They were not hunting his body. They were hunting his ember.

He stopped on the shelf of the pass and looked back, and the snow swallowed the sound of his breathing, and far below — a hundred yards, maybe less in the dark — he saw the lights. Not torches. Lanterns shuttered to slits, moving with the disciplined patience of men who had done this many times and never once lost. And moving with them, low to the ground, shapes like dogs but *wrong*, too long in the spine, too quiet, their heads swinging in that slow pendulum arc that meant they had the scent and were simply walking it home.

*They've had it since the valley,* he realized. *Since Millbrook. Maybe since the road.* The charm he'd burned on Tam Vessel's memory had hidden his own trail, but it had not hidden the *heat* of what he was. A man could mask his footprints. He could not mask the fact that he burned brighter than he should.

Astrid turned and ran.

The pass narrowed ahead into a place the mule-drivers called the Needle — a slot of rock where the shelf pinched to the width of a man's shoulders and the drop on his left fell away into blackness that the snow made bottomless. If he could reach it, he might buy an hour, might find a drift to burrow into, might —

His boot found ice where his eyes had promised stone.

The world went sideways. He hit the shelf hard, shoulder-first, felt something in the joint speak a hot word of warning, and slid. His fingers scrabbled for the rock and found only powder over glass. The drop yawned. He slid a foot. Two. His hip ground over a buried stone and he seized it with both hands, and there he hung, half over the void, snow filling his collar, his breath coming in white rags.

Below, the lantern-slits stopped.

He heard a man's voice, low and unhurried, carrying up the pass with terrible clarity in the still air. "He's stopped. Cornered himself against the Needle." A pause. "Light the lamps. Let him see what's come for him."

The shuttered lanterns opened.

Three beams of white light lanced up the pass and found him, and in that light Astrid saw the hunters properly for the first time. Five of them, grey-and-scarlet, faces shadowed under oilskin hoods. And at their feet, straining at chains that were not chain but *cord* — braided cord, silver-grey, humming faintly in his ember-sight like a struck wire — the hounds. They were leaner than he'd thought from a distance, and their eyes were wrong for dogs: too pale, too knowing, the eyes of creatures that had been made from something else. Their mouths were open, and what came out of them was not barking but a kind of low, eager whine, the sound of a kettle just before it sings.

They could taste him from there. He could feel them tasting.

"Eastwick," the lead hunter called, and Astrid's blood went cold, because that was his *name*, his true name, the one he'd buried under three false ones and a smuggler's grin. "Astrid Eastwick. Apprentice of Maren of the Thornfell hedges. The Crown knows what you are." The voice was almost gentle. Almost kind. "There's no shame in it. Come down. There's a wagon. There's a fire. There are men whose whole work is understanding gifts like yours, and no one will hurt you who cooperates."

*No one will hurt you.* He thought of the girl with flour on her hands, of the wagon she'd been put into, of the fact that no one in Millbrook Cross had ever seen her again. He thought of Maren's voice, three years gone: *They will be kind to you, little ember. Kindness is the cheapest tool they carry.*

"I'm just a mule-driver," Astrid called back, and his voice cracked on the lie, and he hated himself for the crack.

The lead hunter sighed, a small white ghost of breath. "The hounds say otherwise." He made a small motion with two fingers. "Loose them."

The silver-grey cords fell away, and the hounds came up the pass like water running uphill.

Astrid did the only thing left. He stopped trying to climb and let himself fall — rolled off the shelf's inner edge into the shallow scoop of rock behind it, dragged his hands through the snow, and *pulled*. Not at the snow. At himself. At the small stubborn flame that lived behind his breastbone and had never once, in twenty-two years, been allowed to show its face in daylight.

He banked it. Choked it. Wrapped it in cold and silence and the memory of Maren's hearth gone dark, and held it down the way a man holds a screaming child under water.

The hounds reached the shelf and stopped.

He felt their confusion like a physical thing, a pressure against his held breath. They cast back and forth along the shelf, noses down, and their pale eyes went from the drop to the dark slot of the Needle to the empty snow, and the taste of him — he could feel it fading even as he held it, his own fire going grey and small and *buried*, so deep that even he could barely find it —

But they were made for this. He watched, through the slits of his own squeezed-shut eyes, as the lead hound went still. Raised its head. Turned, slowly, toward the scoop of rock where he lay.

And that was when the tinker's cart came around the bend.

* * *

He would spend the rest of his life trying to remember it properly, and he never would. Terror does that — it takes the sharpest moments and files them down to splinters. But some things stayed whole.

He stayed whole to the sound of a bell. A small, cheap, tin-pot peddler's bell, jingling, absurd, merrily wrong in the white death-quiet of the pass. And then a voice, high and cracked and cheerful, the voice of a woman who had talked to mules and innkeepers and weather for forty years and lost all respect for silence:

"Well, and good evening to you all, and aren't we a lot of serious faces for such a fine snowy night!"

Astrid risked one eye open.

A cart stood on the shelf below the hunters, where no cart had any right to be — a narrow two-wheeled thing hung with pots and kettles and bundles of tinware, drawn by a mule so shaggy it looked less groomed than *weathered*, like a thing the mountain had grown. And standing at the cart's rail, reins in hand, was a woman. Tall. Thin as a fence post. Wrapped in a patchwork coat that had been patched so many times the original cloth was a rumor, and a broad-brimmed hat pulled down over eyes that the lantern-light somehow never quite reached.

"Tinker," the lead hunter said flatly. "This pass is closed by order of the Crown. Turn your cart around."

"Closed! Is it closed?" The tinker looked up at the sky, at the snow, at the lanterns, with an air of enormous interest. "Funny. The mountain didn't mention it to me. And the mountain and I, we're old friends." She leaned forward on the rail, and something in her posture changed — a small thing, a tilt of the weight, a stillness — and Astrid, who had spent his life reading bodies the way other men read letters, felt the hair rise on his arms. "Now. I've a delivery for the shrine at Coldspur, and it's perishable, and you're standing in my road."

"Your road," the hunter repeated.

"The mountain's road," the tinker agreed pleasantly. "I just pay the tolls."

One of the hunters — the second, a heavy man with a crossbow slung at his back — stepped toward the cart, and the hounds turned their pale heads toward her, and Astrid felt their attention slide off him at last, and knew he had perhaps ten heartbeats to crawl into the Needle and vanish, and found that he could not move. He could not stop watching her. Because now that he looked — now that he *tasted* — the tinker was wrong. Wrong the way the hounds were wrong, but inverted, mirrored. Where they burned with taught hate, she burned with —

He had no word for it. His gift had no word for it. It was like looking at the sun through a keyhole. It was like the memory of a fire he'd never sat beside. It was old. It was *older than the crown*, older than the road, older than the names of things, and it was wrapped up in a patchwork coat and a peddler's bell, and it was looking at the hunters with an expression of vast, patient, ancient amusement.

"Last chance to be somewhere else," the tinker said.

"Take her," the lead hunter said. "Quietly."

The heavy man reached for the cart rail, and the crossbow man raised his weapon, and the hounds lunged —

The bell rang.

Not the tin-pot jingle. The *bell*. The sound went through the pass like a crack through ice, like the first note of something enormous waking, and Astrid felt it in his teeth, in the held flame behind his breastbone, in the mountain itself. The snow on the shelf shivered. The lanterns guttered. And in the space of that single ringing note, the tinker was no longer standing at the cart.

She was among them.

Astrid's ember-sight, straining wide, caught what his eyes could not: a blur of silver-grey motion, a shape that unfolded like a heron striking, like a willow in wind, like something that had never in its life learned to move the way human bodies move and had only ever pretended. The heavy man's own knife was in his throat before his hand finished reaching. The crossbow man got his weapon half-raised, and the tinker's staff — when had she picked up a staff? it was a tin-peddler's pole, it was a spear of white wood that sang the same note as the bell — took him through the chest and lifted him and *pinned him to the pass wall*, and the sound he made was very small.

The hounds hit her from both sides.

She turned inside them like water. One hand — bare now, and the hand was wrong, too long in the fingers, the skin faintly luminous as moonlit frost — closed on the first hound's muzzle, and there was a sound like a snapped branch, and the hound went limp. The second got its teeth into her coat, into her shoulder, and she made no sound at all, but the fire of her *flared* — Astrid felt it like noon at midnight, like standing too near an open forge — and the hound released with a yelp and tried to flee, and she let it. It ran down the pass, howling that kettle-whine, and the howling faded and faded and did not stop until it was beyond hearing.

Then it was quiet. The snow fell. The lanterns lay in the trampled snow, still burning, lighting the scene with terrible honesty: two hunters dead, one pinned and dying, three more fled down the mountain with their cords and their courage left behind, and the tinker standing among them breathing hard, one hand pressed to her shoulder, her hat knocked askew.

And under the hat, where the lantern-light finally touched her eyes, they were silver. Not grey. *Silver*, like the covenant-cord on the hounds, like the light on deep water, like the ember-sight's oldest memory of what fire had been before men taught it to burn things.

Astrid, still wedged in the rock scoop with his flame choked down to a coal, made a sound. He didn't mean to. It escaped him — a small, broken, wondering noise, the noise a man makes when the story he was told as a child turns out to be standing in front of him, bleeding.

The tinker's head came up.

She looked straight at the scoop of rock where he lay. Straight through the snow, through the dark, through the buried coal of his hidden fire, as if it were a lantern he was holding.

"Well," she said. Her voice had changed. The cracked peddler's chirp was gone, and what remained was low and cool and very, very old, with an accent like wind through a place he'd never been. "There's a smell I haven't smelled in three hundred years."

She came toward him. She did not climb the shelf; she *arrived* at the top of it, the way snow arrives, the way dusk arrives, and Astrid pressed himself back against the rock and found there was nowhere in the world to press to.

"Please," he said. His voice was a ruin. "I'm not — I'm nobody. I'm a mule-driver. I don't have anything worth—"

"Boy." She crouched in front of him, and this close, in the lantern-light, he saw that the tinker disguise was a coat and a hat and a voice and nothing more; underneath it she was all wrong angles and silver eyes and a face that was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, which is to say without any intention of being kind. She was still bleeding from the shoulder. The blood, where it dripped, glowed faintly, and the snow where it fell did not melt but *brightened*. "You are holding your own fire so far down your throat that it's killing you. I can see it strangling. Let it up. Slowly. Now."

"I can't. They'll — the hounds—"

"The hounds are dead or running, and the men are gone, and the mountain has already forgotten them." Her silver eyes narrowed. "But you. You I will not forget. *Let it up*, little ember. I have crossed half a world and worse than half a world, and I did not climb into this frozen throat of stone to watch the last of the old blood smother himself out of fear."

The last of the old blood.

Astrid's hands were shaking. The cold had gotten into everything; he could no longer feel his feet. And he was so tired — tired in a way that three years of running had built up like silt, like debt, and here was a voice that knew what he was, that had named it without flinching, that had killed for him within a minute of meeting him, and asked nothing yet but that he *breathe*.

He let the fire up.

It rose through him like dawn through a window — slow, then all at once — and he gasped as it came, because he had held it down so long and so hard that releasing it was pain, was weeping, was the first full breath after drowning. And in the moment it rose, the whole pass *lit* for him. Not with light. With sight. The snow was a sea of small cold stars. The mountain's bones ran with veins of deep red heat. The dead hunters were already going grey and cold, their flames guttered to nothing. And the woman before him — the woman was a bonfire the size of a cathedral, silver-white, so bright that looking at her directly was like looking at the memory of the sun, and woven all through her fire, in patterns too intricate and too old to read, were threads of a color he had no name for, a color that his gift recognized the way an orphan recognizes a lullaby.

The Emberlands. It had to be. Proof, living and bleeding in the snow, that the Emberlands still reached into the world.

"By the stone," he whispered. "You're *real*."

Something moved across her face — too fast to name, gone before it landed. She stood, and pulled her hat down, and the peddler's chirp came back into her voice like a mask going on, though her eyes stayed silver and stayed on him.

"Real as the cold, and considerably more useful. Can you walk?"

"I — yes. I think. My shoulder—"

"Your shoulder is bruised and your feet are frozen and your name, I think, is Astrid Eastwick, and every grey-and-scarlet man between here and the capital will know it by morning." She held out her hand — the too-long, frost-luminous hand — and the tin-peddler's bell on her cart, far below, rang once, softly, as if in agreement. "So. You can stay on this shelf and freeze, or you can come with me, and I will tell you what the hounds were made from, and what the crown is hunting, and why a woman who has been dead for three hundred years is selling tin pots in the Thornfell mountains."

Astrid looked at her hand. At the snow. At the long black drop he had nearly fallen into, which suddenly seemed like the least of the abysses available to him.

He took her hand. Her grip was cool as riverbed stone, and strong as roots.

"Sylwen," she said, as she drew him up. "The name under the hat. Remember it. You're the first human in a very long time to earn it."

Below them, far down the pass, the lantern of the last fleeing hunter vanished into the dark. And somewhere beneath the snow, beneath the mountain, beneath the world, Astrid felt — faint as a heartbeat, faint as a memory — something answer the bell.
