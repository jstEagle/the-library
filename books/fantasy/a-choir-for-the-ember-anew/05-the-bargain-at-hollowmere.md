## 5. The Bargain at Hollowmere

They did not bind his hands until the valley opened below them.

Astrid had expected ropes from the first moment — had braced for them through three days of forced march down out of the White Pass, wrists already rehearsing the ache of cord. Instead the witch-hunters let him walk free between two riders, as though he were a guest rather than a prize, and it was this courtesy that frightened him more than any chain would have. Chains meant they feared him running. Courtesy meant they believed he had nowhere left to go.

The valley was called Hollowmere, though there was no mere in it that Astrid could see — only a long grey lake of morning fog pooled between the shoulders of the mountains, and rising out of it, a fortress that looked less built than grown. Black stone, older than the kingdom's banners, its towers crowned with iron cages that held no birds. Smoke rose from a single chimney in a thin, obedient line. Somewhere below the fog, water moved; he could hear it, a sound like whispering that never quite resolved into words.

His ember-sight stirred unbidden, the way it always did near old places, and he crushed it down before it could show him anything. Not here. Not with them watching. He had learned to fold the gift small, to make himself a grey and ordinary thing, but the land beneath Hollowmere *hummed*, low and patient, like a struck bell still deciding whether to ring.

"Your hands," said the rider on his left.

He offered them. The man — weathered, incurious, the kind of soldier who had stopped seeing what he arrested years ago — bound his wrists with soft leather rather than rope, and Astrid understood that even this was a message. *We know what you are. We are not afraid.*

They took his pack first, of course. They had taken it at the moment of capture, back in the pass, when Sylwen's tinker's cart had rattled away into the snow and the hunters' hounds had gone suddenly, mysteriously silent, and Astrid had stood alone in the white with nowhere left to be but caught. In the pack: flint, dried fish, Maren's old knife with the antler handle, and a bundle of memory-charms wrapped in waxed linen that he had not yet decided whether to burn or keep. He thought of them now the way a man thinks of a door closing behind him.

The gates of Hollowmere did not creak. They swung open on greased hinges, silently, which somehow made them worse.

* * *

Inside, the fortress was warm.

That was the second wrongness. He had imagined witch-hunters' holds as cold places — stone and frost and the smell of ash, pyres forever smoldering in the yard. But Hollowmere's halls glowed with lamplight, and the air smelled of bread and beeswax and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath, something like the air after lightning. Servants moved through the corridors with their eyes lowered. No one looked at Astrid except to look *through* him.

They brought him to a room with a fire already lit and sat him in a chair beside it, and for a quarter of an hour no one came at all. He tested the leather at his wrists once, twice, and then stopped, because the knots did not tighten when he pulled — they simply *were*, as if the binding had been made by someone who understood exactly how much struggle a prisoner permits himself before dignity requires him to stop.

When the door finally opened, it opened without hurry.

Lord-Captain Drevane was not what Astrid had prepared himself for. He had imagined a brute, or a fanatic — some scarred zealot with a voice like a whipcrack, all coals and scripture. Instead a tall, spare man entered in a plain grey coat with silver at the collar, carrying a covered dish, of all things, and a decanter of wine. His hair was iron-grey and neatly cut. His face was the kind that juries trust and widows thank. He might have been a magistrate, a scholar, a kindly uncle arrived late to a family supper.

Only his eyes gave him away. They were pale, almost colorless, and they moved over Astrid the way an assayer's thumb moves over ore — weighing, testing, already knowing the answer.

"Master Eastwick," Drevane said, setting the dish on the table between them. "Or is it? We found three names for you in the toll-records along the Thornfell roads. Astrid Voss. Astrid Kell. One entry, charmingly, only says 'the quiet one.' I've settled on Eastwick because it has an honest ring to it. Forgive me if I'm wrong."

"You're not," Astrid heard himself say. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "It's Eastwick."

"Good. I dislike lying to men whose cooperation I need." Drevane lifted the cover from the dish: roast fowl, dark bread, stewed pears. He poured two cups of wine and pushed one across the table, within reach of Astrid's bound hands, and then sat down opposite him with the unhurried ease of a man settling in by his own hearth. "Eat. You've been three days on army rations, and whatever else happens tonight, there's no virtue in conducting this conversation with a starving man."

Astrid did not move. "Is it drugged?"

Drevane smiled — a real smile, brief and almost warm. "If I wanted you unconscious, Master Eastwick, I'd have had you clubbed in the pass. The wine is from the Regent's own cellars. It would offend me deeply if you thought I'd waste it on sedatives."

Slowly, awkwardly, Astrid leaned forward and drank from the cup without lifting it from the table. The wine was very good. That offended him too.

For a while Drevane ate, and spoke of nothing — the road, the weather turning early this year, the state of the passes. He asked about Thornfell as a traveler asks, not an interrogator. And Astrid, who had spent six years reading men across campfires so he could price their silences, found himself unable to read this one at all. There was nothing to read. Drevane's courtesy was not a mask over cruelty; it was simply the shape his cruelty took, the way a blade's polish is part of the blade.

At last the Lord-Captain set down his fork, folded his hands, and said, pleasantly:

"Tell me about the woman in the pass."

Astrid kept his face still. "What woman?"

"The tinker." Drevane turned his cup a half-circle on the wood. "Grey coat, red scarf, mended everything she owned twice over. My hounds are bred off bloodlines older than my commission — they can find a hedge-talent buried under six feet of snow and a lie. In the White Pass they went mad for your scent, closed to within fifty yards, and then they lay down in the snow and *slept*. Both of them. When we reached them they were healthy, docile, and utterly indifferent to you. Something took their noses away, Master Eastwick, and put them back again an hour later. Hedge-craft can't do that. Your kind charms memories and muddles tracks. It doesn't rewrite a hound's whole nature." He looked up, mild as milk. "So either you have been concealing the true depth of your gift, which I would respect, or someone else was in that pass. Someone whose scent my dogs were never bred to follow."

The fire popped. Down in the foundations of the fortress, Astrid felt the humming again, fainter here, muffled by stone.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"No," Drevane agreed. "You wouldn't. But I'm going to tell you what I think, because I find that honesty saves time, and time is the one thing I am short of." He rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the fog-choked valley. When he spoke again, his voice had not changed at all — same warmth, same reasonableness — and that was the most frightening thing yet.

"There is a fire going out in this world, Master Eastwick. Most people can't see it. They see bad harvests, sick herds, rivers running strange colors, and they blame the weather, or the crown, or God's inscrutable mercy. But I have spent nineteen years hunting the old blood, and I will tell you what nineteen years taught me: every gift in this kingdom is a coal from one fire. The hedge-witch's charm, the seer's dream, your — whatever it is you do. All of it drawn from the same hearth. And that hearth is dying. Has been dying since before you were born. Slowly enough that no one panics. Quickly enough that I can measure it year over year, the way a physician measures a fever."

He turned back. The lamplight made his pale eyes look almost silver.

"My commission is to register and contain bearers of the old blood. That is what the proclamations say, and proclamations are true the way maps are true — accurate, and missing everything that matters. What I am actually doing, Master Eastwick, is assembling a census of embers. Because the crown has reason to believe that within twenty years — perhaps ten — the blight reaches the heartland. Not just the mountain valleys. The wheat fields. The capital wells. Every child born in the lowlands coming into the world grey, the way some mountain children already do." He let that sit. "You've seen it up here. Don't pretend you haven't. Grey-eyed babies. Trees that leaf and never fruit."

Astrid had seen it. He thought of a village two winters back where the well-water had tasted of ash, and of Maren, in the last year, going out to her garden every morning to stand over bean rows that came up pale as bone. He said nothing, and Drevane nodded as if he had said everything.

"So. Here is the bargain, and I'll make it only once, because I respect you too much to haggle." Drevane returned to his chair but did not sit; he rested both hands on its back and looked down at Astrid with the expression of a man offering a ladder out of a well. "Every bearer I take is questioned, registered, and — depending on what they are — detained or released under bond. Some few are executed, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise; there are gifts that cannot safely walk loose. But the great majority live, Master Eastwick. They live in comfort, in fact, better than they lived as outlaws. And the hunt goes faster, and kinder, and fewer of them burn, if I have someone who can *find* them before my hounds and my hard men do. Someone who knows the smuggler's roads, knows how the gifted hide, knows the difference between a danger and a frightened farm girl with a charm against warts."

He paused.

"You watched one such farm girl taken at Millbrook Cross three weeks ago. Don't look surprised — the crier's route is recorded, and a girl matching the description of Sera Ilves was registered as seized that day. Her father hanged himself the following week. I mention it not to wound you, but because you and I both know you stood in that crowd and did nothing, and you have been doing arithmetic about it ever since. So consider this your opportunity to change the sum."

Astrid's hands had gone cold inside the leather. "And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow morning you go on the pyre with the others, and I write in my ledger: *one ember-sight, minor talent, burned uncooperative*, and I spend another eighteen months finding the next one, and in those eighteen months people die who would not have died if you had helped me." Drevane's voice did not rise. It never rose. "I want to be clear that I am not threatening you. Threats are for men who enjoy this work. I am simply describing consequences, the way I'd describe the weather to a farmer deciding when to sow. The pyre is real. It is also, in your case, a waste — of you, and of everyone you might save. I have read the reports from the pass. Whatever you did to my hounds, it wasn't malice. You could have killed my riders instead. You didn't. That tells me you're a man who counts costs." A thin smile. "So count them."

Silence stretched. The fire settled. Somewhere far above, iron creaked in the wind — the empty cages, swinging.

"And the woman," Astrid said quietly. "The tinker. If I agree — does the hunt turn toward her?"

Drevane considered him for a long moment, and Astrid saw, with a lurch, that the question had pleased him — that it confirmed some private assessment, filed away like a specimen.

"The hunt follows evidence," Drevane said. "Give me good evidence, and it goes where the evidence leads. Give me poor evidence—" he spread his hands, gracious, terrible— "and I must rely on my own methods. Which are slower, and louder, and involve burning things until the truth falls out. I would prefer your evidence."

It was not a promise. Astrid understood that with perfect clarity. It was a leash described in the language of partnership, and the worst of it — the thing that made his stomach turn even as his shoulders loosened — was that it was *enough*. Three days ago he had lain in the snow waiting for the hounds to find their noses and the crossbows to find their hearts, and he had wanted, more than freedom, more than his secret, simply to keep existing. Drevane was offering him existence, priced fairly, payable in other people.

He thought of Maren. *They will offer you a chair by the fire, little ember,* she had told him once, in one of her fits of sight that she pretended were jokes. *And the chair will be warm, and the fire will be lit with something you loved.* He had laughed then. He was not laughing now.

"If I do this," Astrid said, each word placed like a foot on river ice, "the bearers I find come in alive. No pyres unless they've earned it by your own law — actually earned it, not convenience. And the ones who are only hiding, only frightened — they get your bond and release, in writing, before I lead anyone anywhere."

"In writing," Drevane repeated, and something flickered behind the pale eyes — amusement, perhaps, or appreciation. "You negotiate like a merchant's son."

"I negotiated like a smuggler. It's cheaper."

"Yes," Drevane said softly. "It usually is." He came around the table, drew a key from his coat, and unlocked the leather cuffs himself, sliding them free with a gentleness that was worse than roughness. Then he stood close, so that only Astrid could hear, and his voice lost none of its warmth and gained, underneath, the sound of deep water.

"One more thing, Master Eastwick, so that we begin honestly. Everything I have told you is true — the blight, the dying hearth, the census of embers. But it is not the whole of why the crown wants its bearers gathered, and you are clever enough to have guessed there is more. In time, if you serve well, you will learn the rest, and you may find it changes your mind about our arrangement. I tell you this now because I would rather you discover it at my side than against me." He straightened, and the warmth returned fully, seamlessly, the magistrate's face once more. "We ride at dawn. There's a bearer in the river country — an old man who ferries the Greywater, and has done for sixty years without aging a day. The locals call him Corvin and think him blessed. Eat. Sleep. Tomorrow, we hunt."

He paused at the door, hand on the latch, and looked back.

"For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, which is nothing — I'm glad it was you the pass gave us. Gifts like yours shouldn't die in the snow." A beat. "They should be *used*."

The door closed behind him. The lock did not click; there was, Astrid realized, no lock at all. None was needed. The whole fortress was the lock, and the bargain was the chain, and it was already fastened around him, comfortable as the leather had been, invisible as courtesy.

Astrid sat a long while by the fire. He ate the pears, because his body betrayed him and ate them, and they were sweet, and that was the worst thing of all. Then he lay down on the narrow bed and stared at the ceiling beams and did the arithmetic Drevane had named — Sera Ilves dragged through the mud at Millbrook, her father swaying from a beam, and now an old ferryman sixty miles west who did not age, whose name was already written in the Lord-Captain's neat, reasonable hand.

*I can warn him,* Astrid thought, in the dark, in the space between one breath and the next. *I can lead them wrong. I can misread the signs, foul the trail, lose the hounds. I've spent six years learning how to hide things from men exactly like him.*

And beneath that thought, quieter, colder, the memory of Drevane's parting words turned over like a stone in a streambed: *you may find it changes your mind about our arrangement.* The crown wanted its bearers gathered for reasons beyond the blight. Reasons the courteous man had chosen to dangle in front of him like bait on a hook.

Down in the roots of Hollowmere, the old stone hummed its patient note, and for one unguarded instant Astrid's sight slipped its leash — and he saw the fortress as it truly was: a cage built over something buried, corridors laid out in patterns that were not architecture at all but script, vast and deliberate, written in a language of circles and thorns. The crown had not built Hollowmere. The crown had *occupied* it. And whatever slept beneath the fog, beneath the lake that was not a lake, had been bound here a very long time — and was beginning, ember by stolen ember, to wake.

Astrid closed his eyes and made himself small and grey and ordinary, and began, in earnest, to plan his first treachery.
