## 6. Ember-Sight

They gave him three days to recover from the road before they put him to work.

Three days in a tent that was finer than any room he had ever slept in — oiled canvas, a cot with a wool blanket that smelled of cedar, a basin of water brought warm each morning by a soldier who would not meet his eyes. Three days of good food he could not taste and sleep that came thin and shallow, the way water comes through a cracked roof: enough to survive on, never enough to drown out what waited.

On the fourth morning, Lord-Captain Drevane's aide fetched him at first light and walked him up the ridge behind the camp without a word of explanation.

Astrid had expected a pyre. He had expected chains, a cage, the long ride south to whatever black place the crown kept for people like him. Instead there was this: a hilltop above the river valley, frost silvering the grass, and Drevane standing alone with his hands clasped behind his back like a man admiring his own orchard.

"Master Eastwick." The captain did not turn. "You slept well?"

"Well enough."

"Liar," Drevane said pleasantly. "But I appreciate the effort. Come. Stand here."

He indicated a flat stone at the crest of the ridge, worn smooth as if generations of feet had polished it. Astrid approached it slowly, and when he was close he understood why it was smooth. It was old. Older than roads. There were marks cut into its face beneath the moss, spirals and lines that made his eyes ache if he tried to follow them, and something underneath the stone — under the ground itself — breathed.

*Ember,* whispered the thing in his blood that Maren had spent eleven years teaching him to starve. *Ember, ember.*

"You know what this is," Drevane said. It was not quite a question.

"A waystone." Astrid kept his voice level. "Or what's left of one."

"The last of nine along this valley, by my count. The others are dead." Drevane finally turned, and his grey eyes held no cruelty in them, which somehow made everything worse. "That is why you are here, Master Eastwick. Not to burn. To *look*. You have a gift for seeing fire where other men see nothing. I am told your kind can read these stones the way a clerk reads a ledger."

"Who told you that?"

Drevane smiled faintly. "The same archives that told me how many of your kind remain. Fewer every year. Which is precisely why you are worth more alive." He gestured at the stone. "Look. Tell me what you see."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will have you flogged until you change your mind, and then you will look anyway, and we will both have wasted a morning." He said it the way another man might comment on the weather. "I do not enjoy cruelty, Master Eastwick. I find it inefficient. But I will use whatever tool the task requires. You are simply the least cruel of the tools available to me."

Astrid looked at the stone. The wind moved through the valley below, bending the brown grass, carrying the smell of woodsmoke from the camp and, beneath it, something else — a smell like a doused fire, like wet ash, that had been following him for weeks now and which he had told himself was only the season.

*If I refuse,* he thought, *he hurts me, and then I do it anyway. And Sylwen is out there somewhere. If I'm dead or broken, no one warns anyone.*

It was a good excuse. He was getting very good at excuses. That, too, was a thing Maren had warned him about.

"All right," he said. "But not here, with your soldiers watching. They'll see my face do things."

"There are no soldiers. I sent them down when I heard you coming." A small courtesy, offered like a knife laid flat on a table. "And I promise you, whatever your face does, I have seen worse."

Astrid closed his eyes.

Eleven years of hiding the gift had taught him every trick of suppression — the breathing, the clenching behind the sternum, the way you buried the ember-sense under layers of deliberate numbness until the world went flat and gray and safe. Now, for the first time since Maren's cottage, he did the opposite. He unclenched. He let the breath fall all the way down into his belly, and he opened the door he had nailed shut.

The world caught fire.

Not the ordinary fire of sun on frost and camp-smoke below. This was the *other* fire, the one only his bloodline saw: the slow golden burning that lived in living things and old places, the warmth that hedge-witches coaxed into charms and covenant-stones had once poured across whole kingdoms. Astrid gasped. He had forgotten — truly forgotten, the way you forget a color after years of blindness — how much of it there had once been. Maren's stories spoke of valleys that blazed like furnaces, of rivers running bright with borrowed light, of the Emberlands' glow visible from the high passes on midsummer nights.

What he saw now was a hearth at the end of its night.

The waystone beneath his feet still held a core of light, but it guttered. That was the only word for it. The gold within it flickered the way a candle flickers in a draft, flaring and sinking, flaring and sinking, and each time it sank it took longer to rise again. Around it, the land itself was dim. The trees showed as faint brown embers, barely warm. The river was a thread of dying silver-gold, and even the fish in it — he could see them now, small sparks moving under the current — burned low and unsteady.

And beyond the river, far off toward the east where the mountains folded down into the deep forests that no map drew correctly, there was a darkness.

Not absence. Astrid knew absence; he had grown up half-blind to this sight, seeing the world through smoked glass of his own making. This was different. This darkness had weight and appetite. It lay over the eastern lands like a hand pressed over a flame, and where it lay thickest, the little fires of the world were going out. He could see the boundary of it, a ragged front creeping westward, patient as winter, and everywhere it passed it left ground that did not merely lack light but *drank* it.

"Oh," he said aloud. His voice sounded very small on the empty ridge. "Oh, no."

"You see it," Drevane said quietly. "Good. Describe it."

"It's real." Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "The blight. Whatever the court calls it — it's not a rumor. It's not a story to scare children. It's *there*." He pointed east, his hand unsteady. "There's a — a hunger, moving out of the deep country. It eats the fire out of things. The old fires, the magic. And it's spreading."

Something moved behind Drevane's eyes — satisfaction? relief? confirmation of a long-held suspicion? — and then smoothed away so fast Astrid doubted he had seen it at all.

"How fast?"

"I don't know. Seasons? Years?" Astrid shook his head, still staring. "But it's not stopping. It doesn't look like anything stops it."

"No," Drevane agreed softly. "Nothing has stopped it yet."

Astrid turned back to the valley, and now that he had opened the door he could not fully close it, the sight poured through him whether he willed it or not. He saw the camp below as a scatter of human sparks, each soldier a small warm flame — and saw, with a lurch, how much dimmer they were than they should be. Maren had shown him a village once, early on, before the fear set in, letting him look at the harvest-festival crowds from her hilltop. The people had blazed. Every child had been a bonfire. Even then, twelve years ago, she had frowned and said, *Dimmer than my mother described. Dimmer than they should be.*

Now he understood what she had been measuring against. Now he could see the whole arithmetic of decline stretched out beneath him: a realm running out of fire, and a darkness coming to finish the job.

And threaded through all of it, faint as veins in marble, the old covenant-roads. The waystones. He could see them now, all nine along the valley, most dark as cold iron, two still holding their guttering cores — this one beneath him, and one more far downstream near a ford. Greywater, some instinct supplied, though he had no way of knowing the name. The stones were meant to be fed, he realized. Meant to be tended, sung to, renewed. A covenant was not a wall built once; it was a fire kept alight by keepers. And the keepers were gone, or scattered, or dragged to registers and pyres by men like the ones sleeping in the tents below.

The land wasn't just dying of the blight. It was dying of neglect. Of abandonment. Of a bargain broken and never mended.

*And they're hunting the last people who could mend it.* The thought arrived cold and complete. *They're hunting them to register them, to cage them, to burn them — while the fire goes out under everyone's feet.*

Unless. Unless Drevane was telling the truth about wanting bearers found for another reason. Astrid glanced sideways at the captain, trying to read him the way he read flames, and found nothing. Drevane's inner fire was banked and disciplined, a lantern shuttered to a slit. Whatever burned in that man, he showed none of it to a gift like Astrid's. That, more than anything, frightened him. Everyone else in the world, Astrid could see. Not Drevane.

"Enough," Drevane said, and it was not unkind. "Close it. You've gone white as milk."

Astrid slammed the door shut, and the world went flat and gray, and he discovered he missed the light terribly, even the dying light, even knowing what it meant. That frightened him too. The gift was a thirst, and every drink made the next one harder to refuse. Maren had known that. Had she hidden him from the gift, or hidden the gift from him?

"Sit," Drevane said, nodding to the smooth stone, and produced a flask from his coat. Astrid waved it off. The captain took a swallow himself, unhurried, watching the valley.

"What you saw," Drevane said, "the court has seen too. In fragments. In rumors. In the price of grain and the sickness in the southern herds and the wells going bitter in the border provinces." He recapped the flask. "The Regent's council has argued for six years about what the darkness is and where it comes from. Some say the fae broke their old bargains and the land is punishing us. Some say the fae are dying and dragging the world's warmth down with them. A few — the ones nobody listens to — say both realms are dying together, because the old covenant that bound them was the keel that kept both ships upright, and someone let it rot."

"And what does the council listen to?"

Drevane looked at him then, and for a moment — a single unguarded moment — the captain looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the campaign.

"The council listens to whoever promises salvation cheapest," he said. "As councils always have." Then the shutter came down again, and he was Lord-Captain Drevane once more, courteous and reasonable and utterly unreadable. "Your task remains as I described it. There are names in the old registries — bearers of the old blood, descendants of the covenant-keepers. Scattered. Hidden. Your gift will find what my hunters cannot. You will walk ahead of my company and tell me where the embers hide in the ash. In exchange, you eat, you sleep under canvas instead of under a rope, and when the work is done, we will discuss your future like civilized men."

"And if the bearers don't want to be found?"

"Then that is between them and me, and need not trouble your conscience." Drevane started down the ridge, then paused. "One more thing, Master Eastwick. What you saw today — the darkness. You will describe it to no one but me. Not to my officers, not in your sleep, not in a letter home. Panic spreads faster than blight, and panic kills more surely. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Astrid said, and hated how easily the obedience came.

* * *

They moved south along the river over the following week, and Astrid learned the shape of his new slavery.

It was not what he had imagined. No chains, no guards gripping his arms — just two witch-hunters walking always within earshot, and the whole armed weight of the company behind him, and the knowledge that a word from Drevane would turn all of it into fire and rope. His cage was made of arithmetic, and he was inside it.

Each morning they came to a waystone or an old covenant-site — a ring of standing marks drowned in a millpond, a hollowed hill with spiral-cut doors long sealed, a bridge whose stones bore carvings worn nearly smooth. Each time, Drevane would clear the ground of soldiers, and each time, Astrid would open the door in himself and look, and report, and close the door again as slowly as he dared.

The reports were the same everywhere. Guttering. Failing. Dark. The stones were candles burning down in a house where no one lived. And the darkness in the east crept a little further west each day; by the fifth morning he could see its edge from the ridge tops, a stain on the world like frost made of hunger, and the little fires nearest it burned frantic and strange, flaring like things screaming.

He began, without meaning to, to catalogue what he saw beyond his reports. The two living stones — his ridge-top waystone and the one near Greywater Ford — pulsed in rhythm, he noticed. Slowly, faintly, but in rhythm, like two hearts keeping time across the miles. When one flared, the other answered a breath later. They were talking to each other. Calling. The way survivors call across rubble.

*Someone should answer them,* he thought, and then made himself stop thinking it, because thoughts like that were the kind Drevane would eventually read off his face.

On the sixth night it rained, and the company made cold camp under the pines above a bend in the river, and Astrid lay awake listening to the water and hating himself with a thoroughness that was becoming familiar. He had used his gift for smugglers to move contraband. Now he used it to hunt the last keepers of a broken world, and both times he had told himself the same lie: *I have no choice.* Maren's voice, in memory, dry as autumn leaves: *There is always a choice, love. There is only ever the choice, and then the story you tell afterward.*

Near midnight he gave up on sleep, slipped out past the dozing pickets — smuggling skills, at least, were still his own — and climbed the riverbank alone to be somewhere no one could watch him.

He stood on the shingle in the rain and, for no reason he could defend, opened the door again.

The river blazed up in his sight, silver-gold and guttering, and the rain fell through it in threads of dark, and the hills on either side were banked coals under wet ash. It was beautiful. That was the unbearable part. Even dying, the world's old fire was beautiful, the way a sunset is beautiful, the way a person is beautiful when you love them and they are leaving. Astrid stood in the rain and let himself grieve for it, openly, for the first time — for the blazing valleys Maren remembered and he had never seen, for the keepers scattered and burned, for the neighbor's daughter at Millbrook Cross whose face he still saw when he closed his eyes, for the boy he had been who ran from a destiny because it had asked him to be brave.

Movement, upstream. At the tree line.

He turned his head without closing the sight — a mistake, or maybe the opposite of one — and saw her.

She stood among the pines a hundred yards off, and in the ember-sight she was impossible to mistake for anything human. Where men were candles, she was a torch. No — more than a torch. She burned in colors Astrid had no names for, layered flames, gold over green over a white that hurt, and around her the air itself shimmered with borrowed radiance, moths of light drifting off her like sparks from a struck flint. She was veiled — deliberately, tightly veiled, wrapped in a disguise that dampened her down to tinker-sized dullness — but the veil leaked, and to a gift like his it leaked like dawn through shutters.

Sylwen. Watching him. Watching the camp.

For a long moment neither moved. Rain fell between them. Behind him the company slept, and somewhere in it two witch-hunters who should have been awake were not, and he understood distantly that she had done that — that she could do that — and that she was trusting him, right now, in the rain, with the single most dangerous secret on either side of the mountains.

*Run,* he thought at her fiercely. *They'll find you. Run.*

She tilted her head. And though she made no sound, though the distance and the rain swallowed everything, he felt — not heard, *felt*, the way you feel a chord struck on a string touched lightly to your own ribs — something pass from her to him along the guttering thread of the river's fire.

Not words. An image, almost: a stone, sundered, and many small lights gathering around it like singers gathering around a broken altar. A choir. She was showing him a choir.

Then she stepped back among the pines, and her light folded away like a fan closing, and she was gone, and Astrid stood alone on the shingle with the rain coming down and his heart slamming against his ribs.

They marched at dawn. Drevane rode beside him out of the camp, hands loose on the reins, and said, conversationally:

"One of my sentries swears he saw a light in the trees last night. A lantern, he thought. Tinker traffic, probably — the ford attracts them." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "You were out of your tent, Master Eastwick. See anything?"

Astrid looked east, at the darkness creeping patient across the world, and at the two living stones calling to each other across the miles, unanswered.

"No," he said. "Nothing at all."

And Drevane smiled, and said nothing, and rode on.
