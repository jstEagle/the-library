## 7. The First Bearer

The river came to Astrid before the road did.

He heard it first as a change in the hounds' voices — a thinning of their certainty, the way water always confused scent. The witch-hunters rode behind him in a loose column, six of them plus Drevane's lieutenant, a gray-eyed woman named Serah who never spoke except to give orders he was already following. Ahead, through the bare black teeth of the alders, the Vell ran wide and slow, carrying the last of the autumn leaves south like small burning ships.

"There," Serah said, pointing with her gauntlet. "The ferry crossing. Old man lives alone on the far bank. Hound marked ember-trace on him three days past."

Astrid nodded and let his gift open — carefully, the way a man cracks a door in winter to keep the warmth from fleeing all at once. Ember-sight was not like ordinary seeing. It did not show him faces or walls. It showed him what the world remembered: old fires, old vows, old magic soaked into stone and wood and bone like wine into bread. Most of the land had gone dim since the blight began, the colors leached from everything living, as though someone had banked the world's fire down to embers and forgotten to feed it. But here and there, faint as breath on cold glass, something still glowed.

The crossing glowed.

Faintly — a thread of amber wound through the ferryman's landing, through the rope strung across the current, through the man himself, wherever he stood on the far shore. Not the wild silver-green of fae light. Something older and warmer. Covenant-light. Astrid had seen it only twice since Drevane put him on a horse and told him to hunt his own kind, and each time it had felt like looking at his own face in someone else's memory.

"He's there," Astrid said. "By the boathouse."

"Can you tell how strong?"

"Old," Astrid said truthfully. "The trace is worn thin. Like a coin rubbed smooth." He paused, weighing the lie against the truth and finding they could share a body for one more mile. "But it's covenant-blood. It doesn't fade clean. You'd need to burn the whole riverbank to be sure."

Serah looked at him a moment too long. Her eyes were the color of river ice, and they did not miss things, and Astrid felt again the particular chill of being useful and expendable in the same breath.

"Drevane wants them alive," she said at last. "All of them. He says they sing better in choirs than in ash." She turned her horse toward the ford. "Cross here. You first — if the old man has wards, I want them spent on you, not my men."

*They sing better in choirs.* Astrid held the words inside himself as his horse picked its way into the shallows, and something in his chest that had been dying quietly since Millbrook Cross turned over and woke.

He had been telling himself a story these two weeks. That he was surviving. That survival was not a choice but a fact, the way weather was a fact, and no one could blame a man for weather. That when the moment came he would find some clever smuggler's trick, some half-magic sleight, and slip the leash without anyone bleeding for it.

But the hounds were behind him, and an old man with worn-smooth fire in his blood was ahead, and the story would not hold its shape any longer.

The ferryman stood on the far bank with a boathook in his hands, watching them come. He was broad as a door, white-bearded, wearing a coat the color of wet slate. He did not run. That was the thing Astrid noticed first, and the thing that decided everything after: Corvin — he would learn the name within the hour — stood his ground the way men do who have already made their peace and are merely curious about the terms.

Astrid dismounted in the shallows and walked the last few yards, boots filling with river-cold, and let his sight go fully open for the first time since Hollowmere.

And the old man *saw him see.*

It happened in a single heartbeat: the thread of amber in Corvin's chest flared bright, bright enough to gild the water around his knees, and the old man's grip shifted on the boathook, and his eyes went from the hunters to Astrid and back with the terrible quick arithmetic of a hunted creature reading another hunter's face.

"You're not one of theirs," Corvin said. His voice was gravel over deep water. "You've got the look. The old look. Who was your mother's mother, boy?"

"Serah," Astrid said loudly, over his shoulder, "the trace is strongest at the boathouse. He'll have a cache under the floor — covenant-stones, maybe records. Take the men around the eastern side. Slowly. If he spooks he'll torch the crossing; I can smell the ward-oil from here."

It was a good lie. It had weight and texture and just enough truth — there *was* old ward-work on the crossing; he could feel it humming in the pilings like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing. Serah hesitated, then wheeled her horse east along the bank, the column following, hooves sucking in the mud.

That left perhaps four minutes. Maybe five.

Astrid turned to the ferryman and found he had no speech prepared, because every honest version of it sounded like madness.

"I'm going to tell you how to live," he said instead. "And then I'm going to tell you why, and you're going to think I'm either a fool or a traitor, and both of us are going to hope we're right."

Corvin lowered the boathook an inch. "Start with the part about living."

"The hunters will circle the boathouse. When they find nothing, they'll come back for you. So don't be here. There's an old salt-road upriver — the one the toll-men abandoned when the pass flooded, eleven years back. It runs under the cliffs. Your ferry rope crosses it at the third bend; I know because I've moved cargo through worse places than that with less warning than this. Go north tonight. Don't use your gift, don't light a fire, don't sing anything the old people used to sing at crossings. In three days, go to the ruined chapel above Greywater Ford. Wait there."

"Why?" said Corvin.

"Because someone will come who isn't wearing a crown's colors," Astrid said, "and because I am done pretending I don't know which side of this I'm standing on."

The words left him and did not come back, and Astrid understood, standing knee-deep in the Vell with the hunters' shadows lengthening along the eastern bank, that something irreversible had just occurred. Not the warning itself — warnings could be denied, explained, drowned in the river if it came to that. What was irreversible was the feeling. It rose through him like the first hot pull of a fire catching in green wood, terrifying and sweet, and he knew he would spend the rest of his life either feeding it or smothering it, and he already knew which one he lacked the courage to do.

Corvin studied him for a long moment. Then the old man did something Astrid had not expected: he pressed his fist briefly against his own sternum, where the amber thread burned, and inclined his head — a small, formal motion, older than any kingdom Astrid knew.

"Third bend," Corvin said. "I know that road better than the river knows its own bed. And boy—" the old eyes were shrewd and kind at once, "—whoever taught you to hide that light of yours taught you well. But hiding's not the same as putting it out. Remember that, when the time comes you have to choose."

Then he was moving, unhurried, into the boathouse, and by the time Serah's riders rounded the eastern bend there was nothing at the crossing but a moored ferry, a strung rope, and a tracker standing in the shallows looking at the place where an old man had been.

"The cache is empty," Astrid called. "Or there never was one. The trace is residual — years old. He may not even be a bearer anymore. Just blood-memory."

Serah stared at him across the water, and the river-ice of her eyes did its arithmetic too, and found, for now, nothing worth reporting.

* * *

Sylwen watched the crossing from the ridge above the alder-stand, and what she saw made her hands tremble in a way that two hundred years of exile had not managed.

She had followed the hunt for nine days. She had told herself it was reconnaissance — that a tinker with a mule cart learned more from roadsides than a spy ever learned from courts — but she knew the shape of lying to oneself; she had written the book on it. She had followed because of the boy. Because on the night in White Pass, when she had put her knives into two witch-hunters' throats and vanished into the snow, she had looked back once and seen the apprentice standing among the bodies with his gift blazing out of him unhidden — a pillar of ember-light in the dark, gold shot through with red, the exact color of the covenant-fire she had watched gutter and die across the whole width of the Emberlands.

No bearer alive burned like that. She had told herself it was a trick of snowlight.

She had followed for nine days to stop telling herself that.

Now she crouched in the bracken and watched the boy lie to armed men with a straight face and a steady voice, and send an old ferryman away along a dead road to safety, and stand afterward in the cold water like a man who has jumped from a cliff and is surprised to find himself still falling.

"Well," she murmured to the mule, who flicked an ear. "There goes my quiet retirement."

She waited until dusk, until the hunters made camp on the near bank and the boy was sent — as she had predicted, because Drevane's methods were thorough and predictable as tides — to walk the perimeter alone. Then she simply was there, stepping out of the alder-shadow ten feet from him, and she had the rare pleasure of watching a gifted man yelp.

"You," Astrid said.

"Me." Sylwen pushed back her hood. In the failing light she kept the tinker's face, the wind-chapped human mask she wore the way other women wore shawls, but she let one thing show through: her eyes, which went silver at the edges when she stopped pretending, like moonlight caught in deep water. "You sent the old man north."

"I sent him to Greywater Ford. In three days."

"Yes. I heard. I also read your little trail-signs on the salt-road markers — very neat work, chalked in smuggler's cipher, invisible to anyone who hasn't run contraband through Thornfell." Her mouth curved. "You warned him twice."

"Once wasn't certain enough."

"No." She studied him, and he endured it, arms crossed against the cold, refusing to look away. "Do you know what you've done? Truly? Not the danger — I mean the shape of it."

"I broke my leash."

"You *cut* it," Sylwen said. "There's a difference. A broken leash can be mended. A cut one means Drevane will start checking your work against other sources. It means the next bearer you're pointed at gets a second hunter, walking a different road, in case you lie again. Everything you do now costs double." She tilted her head. "And you did it anyway. For a man whose name you didn't know."

"Did you?" Astrid asked. "Know his name?"

"Corvin of the Vell. Third-generation bearer. His grandmother signed the Lamentation Rolls." Sylwen's voice changed subtly as she said it — the human roughness smoothing out, something older underneath, like a riverbed showing through shallow water. "Sit down, apprentice. You look like a heron standing on one leg. We have until the watch turns, and I have a history to give you, and then I'm going to ask you for something enormous, and I'd rather you weren't swaying when you answer."

* * *

They sat against the bole of the oldest alder, where its roots braided down into the riverbank, and Sylwen drew in the dirt with a stick, and Astrid listened the way he had once listened to Maren — wholly, hungrily, storing every word somewhere deeper than memory.

"Before your kingdoms had names," she began, "there was a war. Of course there was a war; there is always a war, and yours and mine fought it over the same reason all wars are fought — hunger. Your ancestors' land was thin soil and harder winters. Our land was the Emberlands: warm, bright, fed by the First Fire that burns under all growing things. Your people came with iron and numbers. Mine met them with glamour and grief. Neither side could win. Both sides were starving."

The stick traced two shapes facing each other across a line.

"So twelve souls stepped between the armies. Six of ours, six of yours. They built the Covenant-Stone on the border-march — you've felt its ruins even if you don't know them; every old crossing, every boundary oak, every bridge-toll feels wrong to a gifted man because the Stone's echo used to make those thresholds holy. And they swore the Vow of the Choir: that fire would cross the line freely, in both directions. Our warmth for your harvests. Your songs and children and mortal brevity — which burn so *bright*, Astrid, you cannot imagine — for our long patience. Neither realm would starve while the choir sang."

"And the bearers?"

"Twelve lines. One for each voice of the original choir. Human families who carried a spark of the Emberlands' fire in their blood, so that even if the fae withdrew — and we did withdraw, we always withdraw — the covenant would have anchors on your side of the line. Living stones." She looked up. "The ferryman is the eleventh line. There was a twelfth."

"What happened to the twelfth?"

Sylwen was quiet for a moment. Somewhere downstream a heron cried, once, and the dark took it.

"I did," she said. "Not by blood. By failure. I was appointed warden of the choir — the youngest ever named, because I was reckless and charming and the Elderlight mistook those things for promise. And I broke Emberlands law: I loved a human. Openly. It was forbidden because love makes wardens partial, and partial wardens let the covenant fray." Her stick snapped a root-tip clean off the dirt-map. "So they exiled me. Stripped my name from the rolls, bound me to your side of the line, and set a new warden in my place. And the new warden was young too, and proud, and when the court factions began whispering that the covenant was outdated — that the Emberlands should guard its fire rather than share it — she did not fight hard enough. The borders closed. The bearers scattered or hid or forgot. The Stone cracked along its heart-line forty years ago, and the First Fire has been leaking out of the world ever since."

"That's the blight," Astrid said quietly. "It's not natural."

"It is entirely natural. That is the horror of it. The world is starving because the covenant broke, exactly as it was always meant to starve if the singing stopped. Nature is simply doing what nature does." She finally looked at him directly, and the silver in her eyes was steady and bleak. "But your crown has decided that if the world must starve, it will starve *last*. There is a faction at court — Drevane serves it knowingly or unknowingly, I have not yet determined which, which frightens me more than anything else I've said tonight. They are building something. An engine. To draw what remains of the First Fire out of the Emberlands and pour it into your realm alone. It needs covenant-stones to work — the anchor-points of the twelve lines. Which is why Drevane does not want bearers burned. Burned bearers are wasted stones."

Astrid sat with that. Around them the night gathered, and the river talked to itself in the dark, and he thought of Maren saying, *your gift marks you, child, it marks you as one of the keeping-lines,* and of himself at nineteen, stuffing the knowledge into a pack beside his spare shirt and running for the mountains.

"Greywater Ford," he said. "The ruined chapel. Why there?"

"Because it stands on the site of the sixth anchor. Because Corvin will be safe in its walls — the old wards still hold there, thin but holding. And because I intend to gather the choir, Astrid Eastwick." She said his surname deliberately, watching it land. "Yes, I know who trained you. Maren's disappearance was noted, even in exile. All the twelfth-line lore points to a hedge-witch in Thornfell who took in a boy with ember-sight and then vanished the same season he fled. I have been looking for her heir for a long time."

"I'm not a bearer. My sight is hedge-craft. Scrying, warding, small fires. Nothing anchored to any line."

"Your sight is *ember-sight*," Sylwen said. "There is exactly one kind of eye that sees covenant-fire, boy, and it belongs to the keeping-line. Whether your blood remembers it or not, whether Maren told you or spared you — the Stone will know. The Stone knows everything we've spent our lives forgetting." She reached out and closed her hand gently around his wrist, over the pulse, and her fingers were cooler than a human's and steadier than a mountain. "I am asking you to help me reforge it. Find the bearers before Drevane does. Mislead him, warn them, gather them. Sing with us, if it comes to singing. It will cost you everything you have spent your whole life protecting — your secrecy, your safety, possibly your skin. I know that. I ask anyway."

Astrid looked at her hand on his wrist. He thought of the neighbor's daughter at Millbrook Cross, dragged past him with her wrists tied, her ember-light guttering with fear, and of himself standing in the crowd doing arithmetic about survival. He thought of the memory-charm he had burned on Tam's cousin — no. On a fellow smuggler, weeks ago, a face he could no longer quite recall because the charm had taken the memory of taking the memory. Pieces of himself, spent on staying hidden.

"I've been running since I was nineteen," he said. "From a destiny, from a name, from whatever Maren saw in me that scared her enough to warn me and not explain. Every road I took was supposed to lead away from this." He laughed, short and unamused, breath smoking in the cold. "And here I am, sitting on a riverbank with an exiled fae warden, volunteering."

"That's usually how prophecy works. Badly, and late."

"It's not prophecy. It's arithmetic." He turned his wrist and gripped her hand back, brief and hard, the smuggler's handshake refashioned into something else — an oath with no ceremony and no witnesses but the alders and the dark. "I'll get you your choir, Sylwen. Starting with a ferryman who should reach the chapel in three days. After that—" he glanced upstream, toward the campfires, where Serah's silhouette moved against the flames, and felt the full weight of the cut leash settle onto his shoulders, exhilarating and cold, "—after that we do it faster than they can hunt. Because starting tomorrow morning, Lord-Captain Drevane is going to begin wondering why his tracker keeps finding empty houses."

Sylwen smiled, and for one unguarded instant the tinker's weathered face was not a disguise at all but a lantern hung over something vast and ancient and, despite everything, hopeful.

"Then we had better teach you to lie beautifully," she said, "because tomorrow morning, apprentice, you're going to have to lie to the best liar in the capital's service — and the second-best will be watching your eyes."

Far off, a hound bayed at the rising moon, and the hunt rolled over in its sleep, and did not yet know it had been robbed.
