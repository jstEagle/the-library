## 8. Drevane's Patience

The report from Greywater Ford lay on Lord-Captain Drevane's desk like a fish left too long in the sun. He had read it four times, and each reading smelled worse.

*No bearer found at the ferry crossing. Subject Eastwick reports the trail cold; suggests the old man may have moved upriver to his sister's holding at Marlow Bend. Recommends a three-day detour to confirm.*

Three days. A detour. A *sister*, conjured out of river fog.

Drevane set the page down and looked out the window of the field headquarters at Hollowmere, where autumn was peeling the birches bare and the sentries' breath hung in white rags. Somewhere below, in the paddock, the sniffer-hounds were being fed — meat and tallow and, twice a week, a scrap of charred covenant-stone that made them keen toward any ember-touched thing within a league. Good dogs. Honest animals. They had never once lied to him.

People lied to him constantly. It was practically their vocation. He had simply never expected it from this one.

He turned the boy over in his mind the way he turned an uncut stone: Astrid Eastwick, runaway apprentice, hedge-trained, self-concealing, captured half-frozen in White Pass with frost in his lashes and terror worn so thin you could see straight through it into something else. Something harder. He had wept when the chains went on — Drevane had watched him do it, had noted the genuineness of it, the ugly animal honesty of a young man who believed he was about to die. And then he had accepted the bargain with a relief so naked it was almost touching.

Almost. But there had been a moment — one moment, in that first interview, when Drevane had described what the hunt sought, *the last living bearers of the old blood*, and the boy's eyes had flicked. Not wide with fear. Flicked. Sideways, downward, the way a card-player's eyes move when he checks a hand he already knows by heart.

Drevane had filed it away, as he filed everything, in the great cold archive behind his breastbone where he kept every fact he had ever learned about every person he had ever needed to use.

The archive was whispering now.

He rang the small silver bell on his desk, and Sergeant Vess came in with her helmet under her arm and snowmelt dripping off her cloak. Vess was the only officer in the hunt whose reports he never checked against other sources, because she had never once embellished, exaggerated, or softened anything in eleven years of service. Her face had the settled grimness of a woman who had stopped expecting the world to improve.

"You wanted the ferryman's file, my lord."

"Give me your own impression first." He gestured her to the chair across from him, which she took with the wary stiffness of someone unaccustomed to being asked for opinions. "Corvin. The old ferryman at Greywater Ford."

Vess frowned. "Kept himself clean for forty years, far as anyone knew. Paid his taxes. Ferried soldiers without complaint through two border skirmishes. If he's bearer-blood, he buried it deep enough to drown a diviner."

"And yet our hounds pulled hard toward the ford on the last pass through."

"They did, sir. Then they lost it in the rain."

"In the rain," Drevane repeated softly. "Hounds that can hold a scent across a snowfield, across running water, through a burning village — lost it in the rain." He let the silence sit. "What do you make of Eastwick?"

Vess considered. She was not quick with words but she was honest with them. "He's frightened. He's clever. He wants to live." A pause. "And I've seen men frightened and clever before, my lord. They're the dangerous ones."

"Yes." Drevane smiled, and saw Vess's shoulders tighten slightly; she had never entirely made her peace with his smile. "They are exactly the ones I like best. Dismissed — no. Wait. One more thing. When we ride on Marlow Bend, I want you riding at Eastwick's flank. Not guarding him. *Watching* him. Watch his hands. Watch where his eyes go before he speaks. Bring me what you see, even if it seems like nothing."

"Especially if it seems like nothing," Vess said, deadpan, and he inclined his head, pleased. That was why he tolerated no one else in his confidence. She understood that nothing was where all the important things lived.

She left, and Drevane returned to the window, and to the letter that sat beneath the fishy report like a knife under a napkin.

It was from the capital. From the Undercroft, specifically — the sealed offices beneath the Regent's wing where the crown's true arithmetic was done. He broke the wax again and read it a fifth time, though he knew every line by heart, because knowing a thing by heart and feeling its weight were different disciplines, and he forced himself to feel it each morning like a man taking medicine.

*The blight models have been revised upward. The Emberlands' decay is accelerating beyond projection. Absent intervention, the human realms will see crop failure within six years, and the fae territories will be uninhabitable within ten — after which whatever magic remains will gutter out entirely, everywhere, for everyone.*

*The Council of the Regent has therefore authorized final phase preparations. The harvesting engine proceeds. The covenant-stones recovered at Ashmoor and Dunhollow are adequate for the frame. We require the living stones — the bearers themselves — to serve as regulators. All seven, my lord captain, or the engine fails. Partial harvest is no harvest.*

*Drevane. You have been given a gift in this apprentice. Ember-sight of his clarity appears perhaps once in three generations. He can find what our dogs and diviners cannot. Do not waste him on pyres. Do not waste him on kindnesses. Find us the seven.*

*And understand: the covenant must not merely be broken. It must be seen to die. There are factions at court — sentimental factions — who still speak of renewal, of negotiation, of the old treaties. When the engine turns and the Emberlands run dry, those voices must be able to say nothing except that it was already over. That the fire went out on its own. That the crown merely gathered what the heavens abandoned.*

*Let no survivor carry a story. Let no story survive the survivors.*

He folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, and slid it into the iron box beneath the floorboard, where it clinked faintly against the others.

Seven bearers. The number had a neatness to it that he distrusted on principle. Seven stones for the engine, seven voices for some ancient choir, seven names on the Undercroft's list — and somewhere in the world, presumably, seven people going about their lives unaware that they were components. Corvin the ferryman might be one. The blind seer the diviners kept sniffing traces of near the Thornfell waystations might be another. The disgraced court mage Tam, exiled to the salt marshes for falsifying auguries, might be a third, though Drevane held private doubts; a man who falsified auguries was either a fraud or a prophet pretending to be a fraud, and both kinds were trouble.

And the seventh — or the first, depending on how you read the old genealogies — might be sleeping thirty feet below him in the wagons, chained by the ankle to a smuggler's former partner-in-crime who had volunteered for the duty with suspicious eagerness.

Drevane poured himself a measure of plum brandy, more out of ritual than thirst, and let himself think plainly for once, without the gauze of strategy.

He did not believe the boy had found nothing at Greywater Ford. He believed the boy had found something very much indeed, and had chosen to spend a lie rather than spend the truth. Which meant the boy had already chosen sides — quietly, privately, in some interior country Drevane could not map — and had done so without any of the tells that betrayal usually announced itself with. No nervousness. No over-performance of loyalty. Just a smooth, plausible, *three-day detour to Marlow Bend*, delivered with the flat competence of a man who had spent years guiding contraband past checkpoints and had learned that the best lie is the boring one.

That was what troubled him. Not the treachery itself — treachery was weather; you planned around it. What troubled him was the quality of it. This was not the treachery of a coward, which breaks under pressure and confesses everything. It was not the treachery of an idealist, which burns hot and announces itself. It was the treachery of a smuggler. Patient. Economical. Designed to give up as little as possible while appearing to cooperate fully.

Which meant the boy would keep feeding them just enough truth to stay trusted, and enough falsehood to keep his people ahead of the hunt. Which meant, if Drevane simply followed the leash, he would arrive at every location three days late, holding nothing but the boy's plausible explanations and a growing inventory of empty crossings.

So he would not follow the leash. Not closely. Not yet.

He would slacken it.

The thought arrived with its usual cold pleasure. Let the boy lead. Let him warn his bearers, herd them, gather them — because that was what he was doing, obviously, unmistakably, once you stopped flinching from the obvious: he was not hiding the bearers from the hunt, he was *collecting* them. A smuggler did not scatter his cargo. A smuggler consolidated. Somewhere out in those hills, in some hidden waystation or hollow hill or smuggler's den with a hearth older than the crown, the remnants of the old covenant were assembling around his runaway apprentice like sparks around a struck flint.

And Drevane did not need to catch them one at a time, in the wet and the dark, spending months and losing them to the terrain. He needed only to be patient. To let the boy do the gathering. To watch, and wait, and mark every false trail for what it truly was — a signpost pointing at the true destination. Every place the boy claimed the trail went cold was a place worth circling back to. Every detour was an admission. The boy thought he was spending lies to buy safety, but each lie was also a confession of where the treasure was *not*, and the shape of all those absences, mapped together, would draw the outline of the one place that mattered.

When the choir was assembled — all seven, or however many of the seven still breathed — then the leash would come tight all at once, and there would be no more three-day detours, and no more sisters at Marlow Bend, and the Undercroft would get its regulators, and the engine would turn, and the fire would go out "on its own," and the crown would drink deep while the sentimentalists at court wrung their hands over a death no one could be blamed for.

It was good work. Necessary work. Drevane had made his peace with necessity long ago, in a burned border town whose name he no longer permitted himself to remember, back when he was young enough to believe that ruthlessness was a temporary condition and peace a permanent one. The blight was real — that was the thing his critics at court never grasped, the thing he almost admired the Undercroft for understanding. The land *was* dying. The magic *was* failing. He had stood in fields where the wheat came up silver-stalked and hollow, had watched a river run clear and dead as glass, had held a fae lantern that dimmed year over year like a man dying slowly in the next room. Whatever the council's motives — ambition, fear, the ordinary venal hunger of courts — the arithmetic underneath was honest. Two realms were starving, and only one of them could be saved, and Drevane had never in his life been able to save everyone, and so he had learned to save whom he could and account the rest as cost.

The boy would not understand that. The boy would see chains where Drevane saw scaffolding, murder where Drevane saw triage. That was fine. Understanding was not required of the instruments, only of the hand that used them.

But.

He swirled the brandy and did not drink it.

*But.* There was a splinter in his certainty, and he worked at it the way he worked at everything: methodically, without flinching. The splinter was Sylwen.

The tinker. The gray-eyed wanderer who had appeared out of nowhere at the edge of the hunt's perimeter three times in two months, always at the periphery, always selling pots or mending kettles, always gone by dawn. His diviners had brushed her mind twice and reported nothing — reported *smoothness*, which was itself the loudest possible report, because no human mind was smooth. And the hounds would not go near her wagon. Would not. Three of them had cowered and one had bitten its handler, and Drevane had put the matter in his archive and turned it over nightly ever since.

Fae. Of course she was fae; the signs were a primer for children. But which fae, and why here, and why shadowing *his* apprentice rather than fleeing the hunt like every other ember-touched creature in the Thornfells? Unless she was not shadowing the boy. Unless she was guarding him. Or guiding him. Or — and here the splinter went deeper — unless the exile and the apprentice had found each other long before Drevane's net closed, and the whole apparatus of the hunt was being read, mapped, and led by the nose by two creatures who had decided the crown's leash ran both ways.

If that were true, then his patience was not a trap. It was an invitation. He would be letting his quarry assemble not because he was clever but because they wished him to believe he was clever, right up until the moment the choir stood complete and sang something that no engine could regulate.

Drevane finished the brandy at last, one slow swallow, and set the cup down without a sound.

Very well. He had planned for a treacherous instrument; he would plan also for a treacherous instrument with a keeper. He drew fresh paper toward him and began, in his small precise hand, a list that no one else would ever see:

*Marlow Bend — verify personally. If the sister exists, the boy is honest and I have misjudged him. If she does not exist, the boy has begun lying, and I will know which direction his lies point.*

*Double the diviners at the waystations. Quietly. If the tinker visits them, I want her followed — not taken. Taken, she warns the others. Followed, she leads.*

*Send word to the Undercroft: request the genealogy folio on the Eastwick line. Maren of the Thornfell hedge, deceased these nine years. If the old woman's records name an heir, I want to know what the heir was bred for.*

*And — *

He paused, pen hovering, and looked out at the dark coming down over the paddocks, where the hounds had gone quiet all at once, the way animals go quiet when something passes that they cannot name. Far off, at the tree line, a single light moved where no road ran — small, green-gold, patient as a star that had wandered too close to the earth. It hung there for the space of three heartbeats. Then it was gone.

Drevane wrote the last line.

*Watch the boy's dreams. Ember-sight does not sleep. Whatever he sees at night, he will act on by day — and so shall I.*

He sanded the page, folded it, and gave it to Vess with orders to distribute before moonrise. Then he sat alone in the lamplight with the cold archive open behind his breastbone, doing the arithmetic he did every night: seven stones, one engine, two realms, one apprentice.

Somewhere out in the black hills, the boy was asleep, or pretending to sleep, dreaming in colors Drevane could not see. Let him dream. Let him gather. Let him believe, a little longer, that his leash was slack because his master was blind.

Patience, Drevane had learned, was not the absence of action. It was action, held underwater, drowning quietly, waiting for the moment it could surface with its hands full.

Outside, the light at the tree line did not return. But the hounds did not bark again all night, and in the morning, when the column formed up for Marlow Bend, Astrid Eastwick mounted his horse with his face carefully arranged into tired obedience — and Drevane, watching him from the porch, smiled his smile, and touched two fingers to the brim of his hat, and said, pleasantly, for all the camp to hear:

"A fine day for finding family, Master Eastwick. Let's hope your sister is home."
