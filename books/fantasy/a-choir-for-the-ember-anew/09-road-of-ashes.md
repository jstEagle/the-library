## 9. Road of Ashes

The smoke reached them before the village did.

It hung in the pines like a grey shroud, thick enough that Sylwen pulled her tinker's hood tighter and said nothing, which from her was a kind of speech. Astrid reined the mule to a stop where the smuggler's path met the king's road and stood a long moment with his head tipped back, reading the wind the way he'd once read it for contraband — weight of it, sweetness of it. Woodsmoke was ordinary. Thatch smoke was ordinary.

This was neither. This was peat and wool and hair, and beneath it all something sweeter and fouler that he had smelled only twice before: once outside Maren's cottage the night he ran, and once in a dream he'd never told anyone about.

"Quench-stone," Sylwen said quietly. "They used quench-stone on the fires."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know what it means. They wanted nothing left growing here. Not even memory." She looked at him sideways, her eyes catching the flat light through the hood's shadow. "Your captain's work?"

*Not my captain.* He didn't say it. He'd stopped saying it three days ago, somewhere past the second river ford, when the lie had worn so thin it had begun to chafe against his teeth every time he spoke. "Drevane doesn't burn villages," he said instead. "He doesn't need to. He takes people alive."

"That's worse," Sylwen said, "isn't it?"

Astrid didn't answer. He clicked his tongue and the mule plodded forward, and the road of ash began.

* * *

Emberfell had been a village of perhaps forty souls, strung along a stream in a fold of the hills where the Thornfells softened into farmland. Astrid knew its name because Corvin knew its name; the old ferryman had kept a ledger in his head of every bearer left in the western reaches, and Emberfell had held one — a woman named Sera Voss, midwife and herb-wife, third daughter of a third daughter, the kind of bloodline that sang quiet and never suspected it was singing at all.

She had been taken alive. That much was written plainly in what remained.

The houses were not simply burned; they were *opened*, their walls collapsed outward as though something had peeled them apart to look inside. The well was choked with ash. The little shrine at the crossroads — a smooth stone worn glossy by generations of touching, older than any god anyone prayed to now — had been shattered with hammers, its fragments scattered into the stream so the water ran over them without reverence. Quench-stone dust lay in drifts against the doorsteps, dull grey and dead, drinking whatever warmth the ground still tried to give.

And everywhere, chalked on doors and gateposts and the flattened dirt of the square: the crown's mark, the circle bisected by the hunting spear, and beneath each mark a single word in a clerk's careful hand.

*Registered.*

"They came with lists," Astrid said. His voice sounded strange in the silence, too loud, like a man talking in a church. "They knocked. They read names off a roll. They took her out of her own house in daylight."

"You sound surprised."

"I am surprised." He crouched by the doorstep of the largest house, where the door itself had been carried off — no, not carried off, he saw now, but loaded onto a cart, the hinges unbolted rather than broken, because Drevane's men did not waste good iron. "I keep thinking I understand what they are. And then I find they're worse in a way I hadn't imagined yet."

Sylwen moved through the ruin the way she moved through everything, silent as spilled water, but her silence had changed quality since morning. When she reached the shattered shrine she knelt and lifted one fragment from the streambed, turning it in her fingers. For a moment — just a moment — her disguise thinned, the way heat shimmers above a fire, and Astrid saw the light beneath her skin, silver-green and grieving.

"This stone was covenant-work," she said. "Older than me. Villages like this one kept it fed with small devotions for nine hundred years without knowing why their crops came true or their fevers broke. It was a root, Astrid. A taproot of the old bargain." She set it down in the mud with terrible gentleness. "They didn't burn this village to take one woman. They burned it to kill a root."

"And the woman?"

Sylwen looked up at him, and there was pity in her face, which was somehow more frightening than anger would have been. "You already know. You've known since you smelled the smoke. Ask your ember-sight, if you want it confirmed. Or don't." She rose. "Some knowledge is just grief wearing a scholar's robe."

But Astrid was already closing his eyes, because he had learned in the weeks since Hollowmere that refusing the gift hurt worse than using it — that it pressed against the inside of his skull like a hand against a locked door, and the door always opened in the end.

He let it open.

The world went dark and then went *lit*. Ash glowed faintly, the way coals glow hours after a fire, holding shape without heat. The stream was a thread of dying silver. The pine forest breathed slow green embers along every ridge. And in the center of the village square, stamped into the trampled snow-mud where a cart had stood, was a scorch of violet-white so bright it hurt him even through the sight — a bloom of power wrenched out of a living person, splashed across the ground like spilled wine.

Sera Voss had fought. Whatever they'd used to bind her, she had fought, and her gift had torn loose in one last flare, and they had taken her anyway, alive, because alive was worth more.

Astrid came back to himself on his knees in the ash, gasping, Sylwen's hand hard on his shoulder.

"Well?" she asked.

"She's alive. They took her alive." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and found his fingers shaking. "There'll be others. There are always others. Where there's a bearer, there's—" He stopped.

Because he had seen something else in the sight. Something small. Faint as a spark trapped under a banked fire, tucked into the shadow of the largest house's collapsed root-cellar, where the quench-stone dust lay thickest — and yet burning. Burning stubbornly, the way a candle burns in a wind that should have killed it.

Something alive. Something young.

* * *

The root-cellar door was a slab of oak half-buried under a fallen beam, and it took both of them and the mule's rope to shift it. Cold air breathed up out of the dark, smelling of earth and turnips and old straw, and beneath that, thin and wavering, the sound of breathing that was trying very hard not to be heard.

Astrid went down the ladder rungs slowly, hands first, the way Maren had taught him to enter any den where something frightened might be waiting. *"Go slow, boy. Fear bites fastest at the threshold."*

At the bottom, behind a barricade of shelving and sacks that someone small had dragged across the corner, two eyes caught the grey light from above.

"Don't," said a voice. A girl's voice, maybe eight years old, cracked with thirst and smoke. "I've got a knife. I'll do it. My gran said if they come back I'm to—"

"It's all right," Astrid said. He kept his voice low and level, the voice he'd used on spooked horses and green recruits and, once, on a customs officer with a pistol. "We're not them. We're not coming back, either. Nobody's coming back here, ever again. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Silence. Then, smaller: "Gran said that too. Before she went up to see."

Astrid closed his eyes. *Before she went up to see.* The girl had been hidden in the cellar while her grandmother walked out into the square to meet the witch-hunters, because that was what you did when they came with lists — you went out, so they wouldn't tear your house apart looking, so the ones you loved could stay hidden. Sera Voss had gone up to see, and she had never come down.

"What's your name?" he asked.

A long pause. "Nell."

"Nell. My name's Astrid. The woman up top is called Sylwen. We've got a mule, some food, and a road nobody uses anymore. Will you come with us?"

Another pause, longer. Then the barricade shifted, board by board, and a small figure crawled out of the dark: a girl so smudged with ash she might have been carved from it, clutching a paring knife in a fist white-knuckled around the handle. Her eyes were enormous in her dirty face. On her wrist, half-hidden by a ragged sleeve, Astrid saw a birthmark — a curling whorl like a fingerprint, like a flame seen through smoked glass.

His stomach dropped.

*Ninth daughter of a ninth line,* Corvin's voice said in his memory, unbidden. *That's how the blood runs truest, lad. It skips and it sleeps and then it wakes all at once, and everyone wonders where the fire came from.*

Sera Voss had been taken alive. But the blood hadn't gone with her.

"All right, Nell," Astrid said, and his voice did not shake, because he had spent his whole life making sure it wouldn't. "Here's how we're going to do this."

* * *

They traveled the way he had once moved contraband, and the irony of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He knew these roads — not the king's roads, but the real ones, the veins beneath the skin of the mountains: shepherd cuts and drover paths, dry streambeds that ran under bramble, the hollow lane behind the Torbeck quarry where the rock overhung so low you led your beast by hand. He knew which farmsteads sold bread without questions and which ones sold questions instead. He knew the timing of the checkpoint at Miller's Arch down to the quarter hour, and the blind spot in the patrol route along the high moor, and the abandoned kiln at Colder's Rise where a man could sleep a whole day with a fire lit and no one the wiser, because the chimney drew so strangely the smoke went *down*.

For six years he had used all of it to move untaxed brandy and unregistered iron and, once, a chest of stolen covenant-fragments whose buyer he'd never dared ask about. Now he used it to move a child who fell asleep against the mule's flank and woke screaming from dreams she wouldn't describe, and Sylwen, who watched him work with an expression he couldn't read and finally named, on the third night, at the kiln:

"You're enjoying this."

Astrid nearly dropped the stew pot. "I am *not*—"

"Not the danger. Not the fear." Sylwen sat with her knees drawn up, firelight moving on her face, and Nell slept in the curve of the old kiln wall behind her, breath whistling faintly. "The *purpose*. I've watched you for weeks, tinker-Astrid, tracker-Astrid, the crown's leashed dog. You walked like a man carrying something heavy uphill. Today you walk like a man who knows why he's walking." She tilted her head. "It suits you. It frightens you how much it suits you."

He crouched by the fire and stirred the pot longer than it needed stirring. "When I was running cargo," he said at last, "there was a rule. Never carry anything you can't afford to lose. Because the roads take tolls, eventually, one way or another. Everything you move, you risk." He looked at the sleeping girl. "I can't afford to lose her, Sylwen. Which means I've broken my own rule, which means—" He laughed, short and bitter. "Which means I finally care about something more than myself, and it turns out that's the most dangerous cargo there is."

"No," Sylwen said softly. "It turns out that's the only cargo that was ever worth carrying. The rest was just practice."

The fire popped. Down in the valley, miles away, a light moved along the king's road — a lantern, or a witch-hunter's cold-lamp, patient as a spider. Astrid watched it pass and thought about Sera Voss walking out into the square so the house behind her could stay standing, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like pain, that she had been doing exactly this. Carrying the one thing worth carrying. Paying the toll herself.

* * *

On the fifth day, in the high country where the pines gave way to grey stone and the wind sang in the passes, Nell started to hum.

She didn't know she was doing it. She was sitting sideways on the mule, watching a hawk, her heels drumming idly, and the tune came out of her the way breath comes out — artless, unguarded, old. Old beyond her years, old beyond her grandmother, old beyond the village that no longer existed. Three notes, then five, then a falling phrase that made the hairs rise on Astrid's arms, because he had heard that phrase before: in the waystation below Hollowmere, in the voices of Corvin and Ivetta and Tam finding a harmony none of them had been taught.

The dormant ember-light had stirred to it. So did the air around the mule now — faintly, barely, a shimmer like heat-haze in cold air, a whisper of silver-green that wrapped the three of them and the beast and then faded, leaving the hawk wheeling and the wind singing and Nell oblivious, humming her inheritance into the empty sky.

Sylwen had gone absolutely still.

"Astrid," she said, and her voice was not quite her own. "Do you hear it?"

"I hear it."

"The choir is missing a voice. It has been missing a voice since the stone broke." She turned in the saddle to look at him, and her hood had fallen back without his noticing, and for once she made no move to hide what she was — the fae light in her eyes, bright and urgent and afraid. "The stone will refuse us. It refused us all, each of us alone. But a choir needs a harmony, and harmony needs *parts*, and that child is carrying a part that hasn't been sung in nine hundred years." She gripped his arm. "If Drevane's lists reach the western reaches — if anyone with a roll of names finds out what sleeps in that girl's blood—"

"Nobody's finding out," Astrid said. "Not while I'm breathing."

"You can't promise that. You of all people know you can't promise that."

"I know." He looked ahead, where the pass narrowed between walls of naked stone, and the old road — the smuggler's road, his road, the road that had taught him everything he knew about hiding things — climbed toward a saddle of sky. "But I also know a waystation, three days north, where a ferryman and a seer and a fool are waiting. And I know every cut and cover and blind path between here and there. And I know—" He paused, and found the truth sitting in him solid as bedrock, heavier than any contraband he'd ever carried, and lighter too. "I know that I'm done running cargo for other people's profit. From here on, everything I smuggle, I smuggle home."

Sylwen studied him a long moment. Then she smiled — a real smile, rare as sun on the high moor — and pulled her hood up, and said only, "Then lead, smuggler. And pray your roads stay secret."

They rode north as the light failed. And behind them, far down in the valley, a second light appeared on the king's road, following the first — two lanterns moving together now, unhurried, patient, keeping pace with the hills.

As if something knew exactly where they were going.
