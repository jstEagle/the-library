## 10. The Choir Gathers

### Corvin

The river had taught Corvin patience, but it had never taught him this: the patience of waiting to be useful.

He sat on the doorstep of the old toll-house with his hands wrapped around a clay mug of nettle tea gone cold, listening to the mountain say what it always said at dusk — wind through the pines like breath drawn through teeth, water talking to itself in the gorge below, stone settling into stone. Forty years he had ferried souls across the Greywater and learned to read the river's moods the way other men read faces. Now he was learning to read silence.

They were coming tonight. The girl had said so — Sylwen, though she had given him no name at first, only a tinker's cart and a warning wrapped in riddles. *Three more voices, ferryman. When they come, do not be afraid of what the dark does.*

He was afraid anyway. Not of the dark. Of the singing.

It had started three nights ago, after Astrid Eastwick had stood in his ferry-house with rain streaming off his cloak and asked him, in a voice that shook worse than an old man's, *Do you remember the words your grandmother sang when the lamps were lit?* And Corvin, fool that he was, had sung them. Four lines of nonsense his gran used to hum over the coals, words in no tongue he knew, and the moment they left his mouth the hearth-fire had leaned toward him like a dog hearing its master's whistle.

He hadn't slept properly since. Every time he closed his eyes he could feel something under his breastbone, banked and patient, like a coal that had waited thirty years for someone to remember it existed.

Boots on the switchback now. Two sets, one light and quick, one dragging slightly on the left side. He set down the mug and stood, knees cracking, and made himself look toward the tree line without flinching.

Astrid came out of the shadows first, leading a mule whose load smelled of dried fish and lamp oil — smuggler's cargo, or smuggler's disguise; with that boy it was hard to tell which was which anymore. Behind him came a woman Corvin did not know, gray-haired and straight-backed, walking with a hand on Astrid's elbow as much for companionship as guidance. Her eyes were the color of milk gone faintly blue, and they did not track anything. They looked at a point somewhere past the world.

"You found her," Corvin said.

"She found us," Astrid said. "Mostly she found the road and we tried not to get in her way." There was a new thing in his voice these days, Corvin had noticed — a steadiness laid over the fear like ice over running water. "Corvin, this is Ivetta. Ivetta, this is the man who pulled me out of the Greywater when I was nine and never told anyone."

"I remember," Ivetta said. Her voice was dry and warm, like paper kept near a hearth. She turned her blind face toward Corvin with unsettling precision. "You fished a drowning boy out of the current and told his mother he'd fallen from the ferry rail. You lied well, ferryman. It was a kindness."

"He was half-dead and babbling about lights in the water." Corvin shrugged, uncomfortable. "What was I going to say? That the boy glowed?"

"Yes," said Ivetta. "That is exactly what you should have said. But then, we are all of us late with our truths." She smiled, and it took the sting out of it. "May I come inside? My knees have opinions about mountain air."

* * *

### Ivetta

The toll-house smelled of river-stone and pipe smoke and, beneath it all, of something Ivetta had not smelled in forty years: ember-ash. Not burned things. Burned *possibility*. It clung to the boy Astrid like smoke to wool, and it clung, fainter but unmistakably, to the old ferryman, and — she reached inward, to the small banked heat behind her own eyes — to herself.

Three candles. Three wicks. One of them nearly guttered, one burning crooked and bright, and one — hers — that had been hidden under a bushel so long she sometimes forgot it was fire at all.

She let Astrid guide her to a chair by the hearth and folded her hands in her lap and listened to the room arrange itself around her: Corvin banking the fire with the fussiness of a man who had tended passengers for decades, Astrid seeing to the mule because it was easier than seeing to himself, the wind worrying the shutters.

"Tell me what you see," she said to Astrid, when the room had settled. "When you look at us. Truly look. No hiding — hiding is how we got here, all three of us, alone in the dark with our little secrets."

A long silence. Then, reluctantly, the boy spoke, and Ivetta heard him choose honesty the way a man chooses to step off a ledge.

"Corvin is..." A pause, the sound of a man groping for words for something no one had ever given him words for. "There's a light in him. Low, like a banked fire, like embers under ash. It's been there so long it's grown into his bones. And yours—" His voice caught. "Yours is different. Yours is like... starlight through a cracked door. Old. Cold-burning. There's a crack in it, though. Something broke it a long time ago, and it never healed right."

"And yours?" Ivetta asked gently.

Another silence, longer. When he answered, it was barely above the wind.

"Mine's too big. It doesn't fit. It's like standing inside a bell when someone strikes it — everything in me rings, and I can't make it stop, and I've spent my whole life trying to be quiet enough that nobody hears it." A bitter little laugh. "I ran to the mountains to hide a bonfire behind my back. Stupid."

"No," Ivetta said. "Necessary. There is a difference, boy, and the world will try very hard to convince you otherwise." She turned her face toward the fire, letting its warmth lie on her cheeks like a hand. "I saw you before you came. Did Sylwen tell you that? Thirty years ago I saw a child standing between two doors, holding them both shut with his small hands, and I wept, because I understood that someday those hands would have to open."

"That's cheerful," Corvin muttered.

"Truth usually is," said Ivetta. "Now. Ferryman. Your grandmother's song. Sing it."

* * *

### Corvin

Corvin had not sung in front of anyone since his wife died, and even then it had only been at the funeral, four bars of a mourning hymn before his voice failed him. Now two strangers and a half-strange boy sat watching him — one of them watching with eyes that couldn't see and somehow saw everything — and waited for him to produce four lines of nonsense from a childhood sixty years drowned.

But the strange thing — the thing he would carry to his grave — was that the moment Ivetta asked, the words rose in him like water finding a hole. As if they had been waiting under his tongue all along, patient as the river.

He cleared his throat. Sang.

*"Wake, wake, the wick and the willing heart,*
*the door stands dark where the door stood apart,*
*one voice is nothing, and two are few,*
*but three make a bridge that the dark walks through."*

The fire moved.

Not leapt — *moved*, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice, the flames leaning as one toward him, and the coals beneath flushed from orange to a deep gold Corvin had only ever seen once before, the night his grandmother died, when the hearth had flared unbidden and the hedge-witch Maren — young then, sharp-eyed, visiting the ferry-house — had gone white as whey and told him, *never sing that again, Corvin, not until I tell you, and I may never tell you.*

She had never told him. And he had kept silent for fifty years, and here he was, and the fire was looking at him.

"What," he said, when he could speak, "was that."

"The first verse," Ivetta said softly. "The verse I was taught, and Tam after me, and every bearer back to the founding. The covenant-song. It isn't magic, ferryman. It's memory. The land remembering what it agreed to." She drew a breath. "My turn."

Her voice was nothing like Corvin's — thin and dry, a reed against his river-stone — but when she sang the second verse the candle flames stood up straight as soldiers, and a smell of summer filled the room, hay and warm dust, impossible in the teeth of autumn.

*"Wake, wake, the ward and the watched-for door,*
*the hand that was given is given once more,*
*what fae gave freely let man keep free,*
*two fires, one hearth, on either sea."*

And then the room went quiet, and everyone looked at Astrid.

* * *

### Astrid

Astrid had spent his whole life being the one who hid. Hiding was a craft, like smuggling — you learned the routes, the blind spots, the ways to fold yourself small. But sitting in that firelit room with two old people who had just sung the air into honey, he understood that his hiding was over. Had been over since Hollowmere, maybe. Since White Pass. Since Maren had first looked at him across a kettle of herbs and gone still as a hare.

"I don't know any verses," he said. "Maren never taught me the song. She taught me shielding, and concealment, and how to burn a memory out of a man's head without killing him. She taught me everything except the thing I was for."

"Perhaps," Ivetta said, "she was waiting for you to be ready. Or perhaps she hoped you'd never need to be." Her blind eyes found him. "Sing anyway. Whatever you have. The song doesn't care about verses, boy. It cares about truth."

So Astrid closed his eyes, and stopped holding the bell still, and let it ring.

He didn't sing words, not at first. He let out the sound that lived under his breastbone, the one he'd been smothering since he was six years old — and it came out low and strange, less a melody than a resonance, the note a struck bell keeps after the striking. And the room answered.

The hearth roared up gold-white. The candles bloomed. And along the walls, out of the mortar and the old river-stones themselves, threads of light woke — thin as spider-silk, ember-orange and silver both, rising like heat-shimmer, weaving between the three of them in slow arcs. Where Corvin's deep note crossed Ivetta's dry reed-tone, the threads brightened. Where Astrid's resonance passed beneath them both, the whole web shivered and *held*, the way a bridge holds weight.

And for one moment — one breath — Astrid felt them. Not as light. As *voices*. Distant, vast, glad beyond bearing: a chorus rolling in from somewhere east of the world, from a country of living flame, and it was singing back.

Then his control slipped — one heartbeat of raw gift, unshielded — and the flare went through the roof-thatch and out into the night sky like a shout, a pillar of soft gold visible, he would realize later, for ten miles in every direction.

The song died. The light sank back into the stones. The three of them sat blinking in ordinary firelight, hearts hammering.

"Well," said Corvin faintly. "I expect they heard that in the capital."

* * *

### Sylwen

Sylwen had felt it from the ridge, two miles south, and it had brought her to her knees in the bracken.

Not the light — though the light had been beautiful, a single golden exhalation against the dark flank of the mountain, and some starving animal part of her had wanted to run toward it the way the dying run toward warmth. What had brought her down was the *voice underneath*. For eight years she had heard the Emberlands the way a deaf man remembers music: imperfectly, painfully, from memory. Tonight, for the space of three heartbeats, she had *heard* it again. Felt the far country lean toward the mortal world like a lover toward a lit window. Felt her own faded blood flush briefly, unbearably, with home.

Three voices. Only three. Enough to open a crack, not enough to hold a door. But the covenant had never required perfection. It required willingness.

She rose, brushed the bracken from her cloak, and did what she had done every night for eight years: she checked her backtrail, read the wind, and listened for hounds.

The wind carried woodsmoke, pine, river-stone. And something else. Far off, down-valley, iron and horse and the particular sour musk of sniffer-hounds — many of them, moving with the unhurried confidence of hunters who already knew where the quarry slept.

Drevane. Or whatever the crown sent in Drevane's name. They had taken the bait of the beacon, or they had never needed one. Either way, they were coming, and they would be here in two days, perhaps three if the ford ran high.

Sylwen stood a moment longer, looking at the toll-house window below, where three small figures moved behind the glass — an old man pouring tea, an old woman with her face turned to the fire, a young man who could not stop glowing if he tried. Fragile. Ridiculous. The last hope of two worlds, gathered in a smuggler's toll-house drinking nettle tea.

She smiled despite herself, and turned down the slope toward them, and began to plan.

Behind her, far to the south, a hunting-horn sounded once across the dark — clear, patient, and horribly close.

And in the toll-house, Ivetta lifted her head as if she'd heard it too, and said quietly, to no one and everyone:

"We have less time than we thought. Someone must ride for the fourth voice — the mage. Before the hunters find him first."
