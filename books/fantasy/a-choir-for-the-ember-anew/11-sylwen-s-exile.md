## 11. Sylwen's Exile

The fire had burned down to its red heart by the time the others slept.

Corvin snored in his bedroll like a man who had spent fifty years arguing with rivers and won most of them. Ivetta lay curled on her side, her breathing slow and even, her blind eyes turned toward the dark as though it were simply another kind of light she had grown tired of watching. Even Tam, who had spent three days pretending he wasn't afraid, had finally surrendered to exhaustion, his long fingers still curled around a scrap of covenant-stone he carried like a talisman. And the child — Wren, whom Astrid had pulled half-frozen from the ashes of Harrow's Bend — slept pressed against Astrid's side with the absolute trust of someone too young to know that trust could be a debt.

Sylwen sat apart, at the edge of the firelight, where she always sat.

It was habit older than the mountains. In the Emberlands she had kept to the borders of every gathering, the watcher at the rim, the one whose eyes went out past the torches while others looked within. Exile had not changed that. It had only given her more darkness to sit in.

She was mending a strap on her tinker's pack when she felt him wake.

Astrid did not move — he never did, at first; he had learned that much from her, or perhaps they had both learned it from the same hard teachers — but his breathing changed, and the ember-light behind his eyes flickered open. She knew without looking that he would be seeing the world the way only he could: the faint gold threads running through stone and water and sleeping flesh, the land's magic guttering like a lamp low on oil. He saw it everywhere now. It haunted him. She had watched it haunt him since Hollowmere.

"You're staring," she said quietly, not looking up from her work.

"I'm thinking."

"About the same thing you've been thinking about for three days."

He was silent for a moment. "You felt it too. At the waystation. When the three of them sang."

"I feel everything." She set down the strap. "That is my curse and my craft."

The harmonizing had been small — barely a whisper of what the covenant once was — but Sylwen had heard it with ears no human possessed, and it had frightened her more than any witch-hunter's hound. Three voices, finding each other after two hundred years of silence, and the dormant ember-light stirring in answer like embers breathed upon. Like something waking. Like something *remembering*.

"They're ready," Astrid said. "Or close to it. Corvin's voice has settled. Ivetta—"

"Ivetta hears the song even when she isn't singing it. I know." Sylwen glanced at him then, and found him watching her with that particular attention of his, the look of a boy who had spent his whole life reading faces because he could not afford to be read. "And Tam is terrified, which means he's honest. For now."

"And you?" he asked. "Are you ready?"

The question landed somewhere beneath her ribs, in the place where she kept the things she did not say. She looked into the fire instead, into the coals shifting like slow fish, and felt the old guilt rise in her throat the way it always did — not sharply, not all at once, but gradually, like water rising in a locked room.

"No," she said.

* * *

They walked out beyond the camp together, because some confessions cannot be made under other people's sleeping breaths.

The waystation sat in a fold of the Thornfells where the smuggler's roads braided together like roots, an old drover's shelter with a roof of split shingles gone soft with moss. Beyond it the mountain rose black against the stars, and below, far below, a river ran silver in the starlight — too silver, Sylwen thought. The blight was reaching even here, leaching the warmth out of the water, and every day the silver crept a little further upstream. She had watched rivers die before. She had watched worse things die.

Astrid followed her up the goat-track behind the shelter without asking where they were going. That was another thing about him — he asked fewer questions than anyone she had ever known, and the questions he did ask were always the ones that mattered. She suspected it was because he had spent so many years hiding that he understood, better than most, the cost of being asked anything at all.

She stopped at a shelf of granite overlooking the valley, where the wind came clean off the snowfields, and stood there for a long moment saying nothing.

"You don't have to tell me," Astrid said at last. "Whatever it is."

"Yes, I do." She wrapped her arms around herself, though the cold did not touch her the way it touched humans. "Because you've been carrying your guilt alone since Millbrook Cross, and I have watched you do it, and I know exactly how heavy it gets. And because tomorrow we walk toward Greywater Ford, and Drevane's patience is not infinite, and if we are all to die in the next few weeks I will not carry this into death unspoken."

"That's cheerful."

"I am fae. Cheerfulness is a human invention." But there was no edge in it, and he smiled faintly, and the smile made the confession both easier and harder, because it reminded her what she stood to lose by speaking.

She turned to face him fully. In the starlight her glamour thinned — she let it thin, deliberately, the way one opens a door — and she watched him see her: the slight wrongness of her proportions resolving into something older and stranger, the silver undertone of her skin, the eyes like deep water over drowned stars. He had seen glimpses before. He had never seen her *choose* to be seen.

"My name is not Sylwen the tinker," she said. "That is a costume. My name is Sylwen of the House of Evening Reeds, third daughter of the Court of the Long Twilight, and two hundred and eleven years ago I broke the oldest law of my people."

The wind moved through the valley below, and somewhere down in the dark a nightbird called, once, and fell silent.

"The Emberlands are governed by the Stillness," she said. "Not stillness as you understand it — not emptiness. It is a discipline. A keeping of boundaries. The fae do not cross into the human realm, do not love what lies across the border, do not bind themselves to mortal lives that end like mayflies. The law exists because the last time the fae loved humans — truly loved them, gave themselves to them — the Sundering came, and the Emberlands burned for a century, and the covenant itself was forged from that grief. To love across the border is to invite the Sundering again. Every faeling child learns it before they learn their own name."

"But you loved someone."

"A human man." The words came out of her like splinters. "His name was Edric. He was a warden on the border marches — one of the old wardens, back when the crown still honored the covenant and posted watchers to keep the peace rather than hunt its bearers. He patrolled the river that divides the realms, and I was assigned to watch *him*, in the way my people watch — from the far bank, unseen, cataloguing the movements of mortals for the Court's records. For six years I watched him. Six years of seasons passing over him like weather. And I told myself I was only observing. I told myself that curiosity was not the same as longing. I was very good at lying to myself. I come from a long line of liars."

Astrid said nothing. He had gone very still, the stillness of someone who recognizes the shape of a story because he is living a version of it.

"In the seventh year there was a flood," she went on, quieter now. "The border river rose in the night, and Edric went into it after a drowning child, and the river took him too. And I—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, two hundred years and it could still catch. "I crossed. I broke the Stillness with my own hands and pulled him out of the water, and he woke on the far bank with my face above his, and neither of us ever recovered from it. You cannot be seen by someone you were never meant to meet. It changes the shape of your soul. We met in secret for eleven years. Eleven years, Astrid. I taught him songs of my people. He taught me the names of every bird on the marches. I thought — I allowed myself to think — that love was larger than law. That the Stillness was made for lesser hearts than mine."

"What happened?"

"The Court found out." She laughed, softly, without humor. "Of course they did. We are not subtle creatures, whatever our legends claim. My eldest sister turned me in herself — she had always been dutiful where I was wild, and she believed, sincerely, that she was saving me. Perhaps she was. The punishment for crossing is exile. Not death — death would be simpler, kinder, cleaner. Exile: cast across the border into the human realm, glamour-bound, forbidden to return, forbidden to speak to the Court, forbidden to *go home*, forever. And because I had been a warden of the border myself, because I had held one of the highest offices of the Stillness—" her hands closed slowly into fists at her sides— "they stripped me of my post. They gave my duties to another. And the stretch of border I had guarded for three hundred years — the stretch where the great covenant-stone anchored the boundary between the realms — was left *unguarded*."

The wind gusted, sharp with snow-smell, and Sylwen let it move through her hair and did not fight it.

"For a time, nothing happened. Edric and I hid in the marches. He grew old — that is the cruelty of loving humans, Astrid, they grow old while you are still learning the shape of their laughter — and I stayed beside him anyway, because I am apparently incapable of learning anything. And in his old age he told me about the covenant-stone. How the wardens' order had thinned. How the crown's interest had drifted elsewhere. How the stone stood season after season with no one to tend it, and how he had petitioned the crown three times to restore the watch, and been refused three times, because the court had begun to whisper that the Emberlands' magic might be more useful *harvested* than *honored*. He died believing the stone would hold. He died believing someone would come."

"And no one came."

"And no one came." Her voice was flat now, scraped clean. "Twenty years after his death, the faction that now serves your Regent sent men to the stone. Not soldiers — scholars, at first, with instruments. Then soldiers with instruments. The stone was sundered — cracked along its heartline with tools of iron and salt, the only things that can wound covenant-work — and the moment it broke, the boundary between the realms began to fray. The Emberlands started to dim. The magic that fed both worlds began to leak away through the wound. And the blight you have seen in the rivers, the silvering, the dying fires — none of that is natural decay, Astrid. It is a *wound*. A wound I failed to prevent, because I was on the wrong side of the border, holding a dying man's hand, when I should have been standing my post."

Astrid was quiet for a long time. Below them the river ran on, silver and silent, carrying its stolen light toward the sea.

"So the guilt you carry," he said finally, "isn't just that you loved someone."

"It's that my love was the crack in the wall. Everything else poured through it." She turned to look at him, and let him see all of it — the two hundred years of it, the exile, the empty post, the dead man, the sundered stone. "I have spent two centuries telling myself I was punished enough. Exile is a long punishment, Astrid. It gives you nothing but time, and time gives you nothing but memory. But watching the Emberlands die — watching the lights go out one by one, watching my sister's messages stop coming, watching the Court of the Long Twilight grow dim as a guttering lamp — I understand now that my punishment was never the exile. My punishment is *this*. Being alive to see what my absence cost."

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke. The stars wheeled slowly overhead, indifferent and ancient, and the cold deepened around them the way cold does in high places after midnight.

Then Astrid sat down on the granite shelf, deliberately, folding his legs beneath him, and patted the stone beside him.

"Sit," he said. "Your legs have been holding you up like a soldier at inspection for an hour. It's exhausting to watch."

"I don't get exhausted."

"Liar. I've seen you sleep. You drool."

"I do *not*—"

"You do. Little silver drool. Very dignified. The fae courts must tremble."

Despite everything — despite the confession still raw in her chest, despite the dying river below, despite the war walking toward them — Sylwen felt her mouth twitch. She sat. The stone was cold through her clothes, and Astrid's shoulder was warm near hers, not touching, but near, the way a fire is near without burning.

"You want to know the worst part," she said, after a while.

"There's a worst part? The dead lover and the shattered covenant weren't enough?"

"The worst part is that I did it again." She stared out at the dark valley. "Here. Now. With all of you. I told myself I was guarding this choir because it was my duty — penance, reparation, the warden returning to her post at last. And that was true, in the beginning. But somewhere on the road, without noticing, I stopped guarding you and started *loving* you. All of you. Corvin's terrible jokes. Ivetta's terrifying serenity. Wren's grip on Astrid's sleeve. Your stubbornness, your guilt, your ridiculous self-sacrifice — you fling yourself between danger and other people the way other men fling themselves at wine. And I know how this story goes, Astrid. I have *lived* how this story goes. Love is the crack in the wall. Love is how everything breaks."

She felt him turn to look at her.

"And yet," he said quietly.

"And yet." She laughed, and it came out broken. "I keep doing it. Knowing the cost. Knowing that everything I have ever loved has either died, or been taken, or been used as a blade against me. And I cannot seem to stop. Two hundred years of discipline, and one ragged choir of fugitives undoes it all."

"Maybe that's not the flaw you think it is."

"You sound like Edric."

"He sounds like he was smart."

"He was smart and he died holding my hand, and his last word was my name, and I have not spoken it aloud in two hundred years because saying it feels like reopening a wound that never healed." She pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her sternum, as if pressing something back inside. "I am telling you this so you understand what you are traveling with. Not a tinker. Not a guide. A criminal. An exile. The reason the covenant fell. If the Court of the Long Twilight learned I was helping reforge the covenant, they would not thank me — they would call it presumption, a twice-damned traitor presuming to mend what her treason broke. And if Drevane takes me, he will not need instruments to learn what I am. One drop of my blood in a covenant-stone's presence and every hunter in the realm will smell fae for a hundred miles."

"Then we won't let Drevane take you."

"Says the man who already bargained himself into custody once."

"That was different. That was strategy."

"That was you being you." She shook her head, but she was almost smiling. "You cannot keep buying everyone's safety with yourself, Astrid. There is not enough of you. Eventually the ledger comes due."

"Eventually," he agreed. "But not tomorrow."

"No. Not tomorrow." She looked at him sideways. In the starlight his face was young and old at once, carved by worry, lit by something underneath that refused to gutter no matter how the world tried to smother it. "Tomorrow we walk to Greywater Ford, and Ivetta says the ford remembers us, whatever that means, and Tam keeps jumping at shadows, and Drevane's patience runs thinner by the hour. Tomorrow the road gets harder. But tonight—" she exhaled, long and slow, letting two hundred years leave her in pieces she could not take back— "tonight I have said the unsayable, and the sky did not fall, and you did not flinch, and I find that I do not know what to do with that."

"You could start by not sitting out in the cold all night brooding. Wren worries. She pretends she doesn't, but she counts your footsteps when you leave the fire."

"She *counts*—"

"Every one. She told me. She says you walk too quiet and it makes her nervous, so she counts until you come back."

Sylwen stared at him. Something moved through her chest that was not quite pain and not quite warmth, but some new alloy of the two, forged in a fire she had not known was lit. "Two hundred years," she said, "and a child defeats me in a single sentence."

"We're very effective. It's the guilt. It binds us."

And she laughed — really laughed, helpless and startled, the sound ringing off the granite and rolling down into the valley where the silvered river ran — and Astrid grinned at her, tired and crooked, and for one suspended moment the night felt less like a countdown and more like a gift.

Then the moment passed, as moments do. The cold returned. The river ran on, silver and wounded. Somewhere far to the east, in the capital beneath the mountain, lanterns were being lit in rooms where men measured patience in days.

Sylwen rose, and offered Astrid her hand, and pulled him up, and they stood a moment facing the valley together, shoulder to shoulder, two exiles looking down at the world that had broken them and the road that might yet mend them.

"Thank you," she said quietly. "For listening. For not flinching."

"Don't thank me yet." His voice had gone serious again, and when she glanced over, he was frowning eastward, toward the dark line of the hills beyond the ford. "Sylwen — the ember-light. Look at the river."

She followed his gaze. And her breath caught, because the river's silver was *moving* — not flowing with the current but against it, crawling upstream in slow luminous veins, like frost growing in reverse, like something searching. The blight had never moved like that. The blight crept. This *reached*.

"That's not the blight," she whispered. "That's directed. Someone is pulling the magic out of the land — right now, tonight — and following it upstream, following it *toward*—"

"Toward us," Astrid finished. "Toward the waystation."

Far below, at the edge of the firelight, a hound began to bay.
