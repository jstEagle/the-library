## 12. The Trap at Greywater Ford

The ford announced itself long before they reached it — a low murmur in the ground, a change in the smell of the air, the way the pines thinned and let the sky lean close. Astrid reined his mule to a stop on the last rise above Greywater and looked down at the water sliding silver-bright over its stones, and felt the ember-sight stir behind his eyes like a cat waking.

He had learned, these weeks on the road with Drevane's company, to read what the sight showed him the way he'd once learned to read weather off the Thornfell peaks. The land's magic guttered everywhere now, thin as tallow-end light, but it pooled where old things lingered: standing stones, river-shrines, the deep roots of mountains. And it clung to people. To bearers.

Ivetta was somewhere ahead of him, past the ford, in the hollow country beyond — he could feel her like a coal wrapped in wool, banked but burning. The blind seer had been harder to find than Corvin had been, and harder still to convince; she had seen him coming, she said, in a dream three winters old, and had not liked the dream. But she had come. The choir was gathering, note by note, and every note they gathered made the song more real and made the noose around all of them draw tighter.

He knew that now. He had known it since the burned village, since the child.

Pell was asleep against his back in the sling, her small weight warm through two layers of wool, her breath slow and even against his neck. They had argued about it — Sylwen arguing in that quiet, flint-edged way of hers, saying the girl should go east with Corvin's cousin to the coast, anywhere but trailing a hunted choir through crown country. But Pell had gripped Astrid's sleeve with both hands and said, in the flat, unchildlike voice of children who have watched their village burn, "I'm not being left again," and something in Astrid had folded. He understood being left. He had done it himself, once, to a woman who loved him, and he had spent years telling himself it had been freedom.

So Pell rode with him, and Sylwen ranged ahead as a tinker with a cart of dented pots, and Tam kept to the rear with Ivetta's donkey, complaining about his boots in a voice pitched to carry reassurance rather than grievance. It was almost a family. That was the danger of it. Families were things Drevane could count.

Astrid nudged the mule down toward the water and tried to name the wrongness he felt, the way Maren had taught him to name things so they lost their power over him. *Name it and it is only a thing,* she used to say, her hands smelling of ash and rosemary. *Unname it and it is a monster.* The wrongness was this: the ford was too easy. No toll-men, no checkpoint, no crown-wardens on the bridge though the war-road ran within a day's ride. Greywater Ford sat on the seam between two baronies, a place where smugglers crossed and wardens pretended not to see, and yet today it lay open as a held door.

*They're letting us through,* he thought, and then corrected himself with the cold clarity the road had taught him. *They're letting me think they're letting us through.*

Behind his eyes, the ember-sight flickered. He closed them briefly, let the second sight slide over the landscape like oil over water.

The magic of the place told him nothing. That was the answer, and it was worse than any answer. A ford this old should have hummed with residue — centuries of crossings, blessings scratched into the bridge-stones, the faint grey ghost-light of ordinary human hope. Instead the sight slid off it, smooth and blank, like glass painted to look like water.

Warded. Scrubbed. Prepared.

"Pell." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Pell, wake up."

She stirred against his back. "Are we there?"

"We're turning around."

* * *

They were already turning when the horns sounded.

Not hunting-horns — he knew those, high and hungry, from the White Pass. These were deeper, ceremonial, the kind blown at executions. Three notes, spaced wide, rolling up the valley from both directions at once, and Astrid understood with a lurching certainty that the trap had never been the ford at all. The ford was the anvil. The horns were the hammer falling.

Riders came out of the treeline on the far bank first — a dozen, then two dozen, black-liveried, torches lit against the grey afternoon as though night were a thing they carried with them. And on the near side, behind them, closing the road they'd just ridden, more. Astrid wheeled the mule, heart slamming, and saw the banner unfurl above the rearguard riders: the crowned flame of the Regent's hunt, stitched in gold thread that seemed, even at this distance, to drink the light.

Drevane rode at their head.

The Lord-Captain did not spur forward or shout orders. He simply came on at a walk, unhurried as a man arriving at his own estate, and the terrible courtesy of that pace told Astrid everything. There was nowhere to run because running had been priced into the plan. There was nowhere to hide because the hiding places had been swept before dawn. Drevane had let them gather. Had let *him* gather them. All those weeks of small rebellions — the misread trails, the warnings slipped to Corvin, the choir assembling note by note in the hidden waystation — and Drevane had watched it happen the way a falconer watches a hawk fly wide, knowing the jesses were still on.

Except the jesses weren't on Astrid anymore. They were on everyone he loved.

"Sylwen," he breathed, scanning the treeline where her cart had gone. Nothing moved. Either she'd slipped away — good, good, be gone, be safe — or she'd been taken, and he couldn't know which, and the not-knowing was a knife he'd have to carry.

Tam burst from the alders downstream, leading Ivetta's donkey at a stumbling run, the old seer swaying in the saddle with her white eyes wide and fixed on nothing and everything. "They came out of the ground," Tam gasped, hauling up beside Astrid's mule. "Astrid, they were *under* us, warded pits along the whole road, we walked over them like fools—"

"Ivetta." Astrid caught the donkey's bridle. The old woman's lips were moving. He leaned close.

"Two songs," she whispered. Her hand found his wrist with shocking strength. "Only two songs left, ember-boy. The third is a scream. Choose which one you'll sing."

Then the riders were around them, and it was too late for choosing anything.

* * *

They dismounted them gently. That was the worst of it — the gentleness. Hands that took Astrid's arms were careful as a surgeon's. Someone lifted Pell down from the mule-sling and set her on her feet and did not hurt her, and somehow the not-hurting was more frightening than any blow, because it meant she was worth more whole.

Drevane walked his horse the last fifty yards and dismounted, and stood before Astrid in the shallows' edge with the water talking softly behind him, and for a moment the Lord-Captain simply looked at him. He had a face built for patience — long, lined, grey-eyed, the face of a man who had learned that cruelty hurried loses money. When he spoke, his voice was mild as a tutor's.

"You've cost me a great deal of trouble, Master Eastwick. Or should I say—" a fractional pause, delicate as a blade turned flat, "—Master *Nystrom*. No. Forgive me. Whatever name you prefer. Names are such slippery things among your kind."

Astrid said nothing. His hands were bound in front of him, soft cord, considerate cord, and beneath the cord his fingers found each other and pressed, and he thought: *Maren, I am sorry, I am sorry, I should have listened.*

"The ferryman," Drevane went on, conversationally, strolling a half-step along the waterline. "Corvin of the Lower Reaches. You warned him. You thought I didn't see. I let you save him, Astrid, because a man who saves one bearer will lead me to the rest — that was my error of arithmetic, and I own it. I assumed you'd keep saving them one at a time, forever, like bailing a boat with a thimble." He stopped. Turned. The grey eyes were kind and bottomless. "But then you began to *gather* them. Corvin. The seer. Vessik's disgraced mage. Do you know what that told me? It told me you weren't running anymore. Running men scatter. Only men with a purpose concentrate."

"You could have taken us at the waystation," Astrid said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Why here?"

"Because at the waystation I would have had four bearers and a mystery." Drevane smiled thinly. "Here, I have four bearers — and you. And you, Astrid, are the sum the others add up to. Kill the bearers and the covenant stays broken, yes, that serves. But the Regent's engineers need to understand the weave before they can unpick it properly, and every bearer we take alive teaches us another thread." He glanced at Ivetta, at Tam, with the mild interest of a man appraising livestock. "And then there is the girl."

Pell made a small sound. Astrid's whole body turned toward her without his permission.

"She's not a bearer," he said quickly. Too quickly. "She's nothing. A orphan from Ashvale village. She's—"

"Ashvale." Drevane nodded slowly. "Where the bearer was taken alive and the house burned around her family. Yes. I have reports." He crouched slightly, bringing his grey eyes level with the child's, and his voice softened into something that would have been tender in any other mouth. "You've been brave, little one. Braver than most soldiers I've known. And you're going to keep being brave, aren't you? Because your friend Astrid is going to make a bargain with me, and bargains require good faith from both sides."

"Don't talk to her," Astrid said. The words came out of him low and shaking. "Don't you *look* at her."

"There it is." Drevane straightened, and something almost like respect crossed his face. "There's the leash. I wondered where you kept it. Not ambition, not fear for yourself — you'd have burned cheerfully enough, I think, if it bought the others time. No. It's her. It's always someone small, isn't it? The ones who carry the world do it on behalf of someone who can barely carry a satchel."

He gestured, and a soldier brought forward a brazier on chains, coals glowing sullen orange in the grey light. Another soldier uncoiled a length of iron rod from a saddle-pack, and Astrid's stomach turned over, because he knew what branding-irons looked like, he'd seen the marks on registered gift-bearers in Millbrook, the neat crown-flame seared into the collarbone.

"The bargain," Drevane said, "is simple, and I'll only make it once. You come back to me willingly. You wear the chain, you track for the hunt, you tell me what I ask about the nature of your gift — and in exchange, the girl goes free. Unharmed, unmarked, delivered to a foundling-house in Millbrook with coin enough to eat. The seer and the mage come with us as well, alive, as bearers in custody — which, whatever you think of me, is a better fate than the pyre they'd get from Inquisitor Hale's people. And in exchange for all this—" he spread his hands, reasonable, magnanimous, monstrous, "—you give me one true thing. One. Tell me where the rest of the choir is gathering, or where the covenant-stone lies, or the name of whoever has been helping you — anyone, anything. One truth, freely given, and everyone here walks away breathing."

The river murmured. The torches crackled. Somewhere in the treeline a crow called twice and fell silent, as if it too were waiting.

Astrid looked at Pell. She was watching him with those too-old eyes, and she shook her head, very slightly, very fast. *Don't*, she was saying without words. *Don't do it for me.*

And behind her, Ivetta's blind gaze had found him across twenty feet of trampled grass, and the old woman's voice rose thin and clear over the water, singing — two notes, only two, the beginning of some song Astrid didn't know, and the ember-sight flared behind his eyes so hard he nearly staggered. Because he could *see* the notes. They hung in the air like sparks off a grindstone, and where they fell, the dead grey blankness of the warded ford flickered — just for a heartbeat — with the old light underneath. The real light. The light the wards were strangling.

*Ivetta sees the trap,* he realized. *She's known since the dream. Two songs left. The third is a scream.*

If he gave Drevane the stone's location, the harvesting engine would swallow it within the month. If he refused, Pell burned or worse, and Ivetta and Tam went to Hale's cells, and everything they'd built died here in the shallows. Every road led through fire. There was no path that didn't cost.

So he chose the only road that cost *him* alone.

"One truth," Astrid said.

Drevane's eyebrows rose a fraction. "That was quick."

"You asked for good faith. Here's mine." Astrid stepped forward until the soldiers' hands tightened on his arms, until he stood close enough to see the fine seams of age around the Lord-Captain's grey eyes. He kept his voice level, and he poured every lie he had ever told — every memory-charm, every misread trail, every year of concealment — into keeping it level. "The covenant-stone lies in the Emberlands themselves. Past the Veil. No army of yours reaches it, no engine of yours touches it, because it doesn't stand in our world at all. That's the truth. That's the one true thing I'll give you."

It was, technically, true. It was also useless, and they both knew it, and Drevane studied him for a long moment with the expression of a man reading a ledger and finding the columns balanced *too* neatly.

"Hm," the Lord-Captain said at last. "Yes. I believe you believe that. Which is its own kind of information." He turned away, and his voice shifted into command as easily as a man changes coats. "Bind the bearers. The girl comes with us — she'll be ransomed against the tracker's continued cooperation. Gag the seer; I've no interest in more singing today. We camp at the ford tonight and move for the capital at dawn."

"No!" Astrid pulled against the soldiers, cords biting his wrists. "You said she goes free! You gave your word—"

"I said she'd go free *in exchange for one true thing*," Drevane replied without turning, "and the thing you gave me was worthless, however sincerely packaged. I'm adjusting the price, Master Eastwick. Consider it a lesson in markets." He paused, then added, almost gently, "You may ride beside her cart, if it helps. I'm not without mercy. I'm merely not governed by it."

They marched them across the ford as the light failed. Astrid walked with his bound hands and his head high and his heart going to pieces quietly inside his chest, and he counted what he had saved — Ivetta alive, Tam alive, Pell alive, Sylwen's fate unknown but unwitnessed, the stone's true location still locked behind his teeth — and what he had lost, which was everything else: the road, the freedom, the fragile almost-family, the self he'd been building out of rebellion and purpose. He had bought their lives with his own chains, and the purchase price was still climbing, and he could feel, with terrible clarity, that Drevane had gotten exactly what he wanted regardless of what Astrid had said.

Because the Lord-Captain hadn't needed the stone's location. He'd needed Astrid back in custody, willing, leashed by love — and here he was, walking into the capital of his own accord, carrying the last secret of the covenant in his skull like a coal in cupped hands.

As they made camp, Astrid knelt beside Pell while a soldier pretended not to listen. She pressed her forehead against his bound hands.

"You shouldn't have," she whispered.

"I know," he said.

"They're going to use me against you forever now."

"I know that too." He touched her hair, clumsy with tied wrists. "Listen to me, Pell. Whatever happens — whatever they show you, whatever they threaten — remember the waystation. Remember the song Ivetta started at the ford. Two notes. Hold onto those two notes, and no matter what they do to me, don't give them a third."

Her too-old eyes searched his face. "What's the third?"

Astrid looked up, past the fires and the pickets, to the north — to the capital crouched under the mountain like a spider under a shelf, its furnaces already glowing faintly red against the darkening sky, hungry, expectant, waiting for the last piece of the covenant to be delivered to its door.

"The scream," he said. "And I'm going to make sure it's theirs, not ours."

Far off in the dark, beyond the picket lines, something moved in the treeline — a flicker of green-gold, there and gone, like a lamp carried past a window. Astrid's breath caught. The ember-sight flared one last time before exhaustion dragged it under, and in that flare he saw her: Sylwen, whole, unbound, watching from the shadows with her fae eyes burning like twin stars — and beside her, half-hidden in the gloom, broad-shouldered and river-grey, leaning on a ferryman's pole.

Corvin.

They hadn't been taken. They'd escaped. And they were following the column north — into the capital, into the spider's web, into the dark beneath the mountain — to do something reckless and impossible and utterly like them.

Astrid closed his eyes and, for the first time since the horns had sounded, smiled.

*Come on, then,* he thought, as the chains were checked and the fires burned low. *Follow me in. Let's see how the song ends.*
