## 13. Chains and Cages

The cells beneath the Mountain were not dark. That was the first cruelty Astrid came to understand, and he understood it within the first hour.

Darkness would have been a mercy. Darkness was the smuggler's oldest friend — the black of the Thornfell passes where contraband moved like water and a boy with a secret gift could breathe. He had spent six years learning to love the dark, to read it, to make it his cloak and his accomplice. The witch-cells offered no such fellowship. Everburning lamps of pale witchfire burned in iron sconces along every corridor, and they never guttered, never dimmed, never slept. They were fed on something that was not oil. Astrid did not let himself wonder what.

His cell was eight paces by five, measured in the shuffling steps his ankle-chains allowed. Stone floor, stone walls, a stone bench worn smooth in the middle as if generations of prisoners had sat exactly there, wearing their own grooves into the mountain's bones. A slop bucket behind a low screen. A slot in the door at the height of a kneeling man's face. Nothing else. Nothing soft. Nothing that remembered being alive.

They had taken his boots. They had taken the memory-charm husks in his coat lining, the pinch of salt, the twist of red thread Maren had tied around his wrist when he was nine years old and frightened of storms — *so you can find your way back to me, little ember.* They had even taken his belt with its false buckle, and Astrid had lain awake the first night mourning that particular loss more than any other, because the buckle had been the last thing he'd made himself, and everything he made himself got taken away.

What they left him was the light. Always the light.

He learned the rhythm of the place the way he'd once learned the rhythms of the passes: when the wardens' boots changed shift, which corridors echoed and which swallowed sound, when food came through the slot (twice daily, gray bread and something that claimed to be soup), and when the lamps nearest his door flickered — just slightly, just for a heartbeat — whenever he dreamed of fire.

That last one, he told no one. There was no one to tell. But he noticed it the way he noticed everything now: with the flat, exhausted attention of a man cataloguing what remained of himself.

Three days. Or perhaps four. Time moved strangely under everburning light, with no dusk to mark it, no dawn, only the changing of boots beyond the door and the slow accumulation of hunger. On the third day — or the fourth — the boots stopped outside his cell, and a voice like dry paper said, "Master Eastwick. We should talk."

* * *

Inquisitor Hale was not what the word *inquisitor* promised. Astrid had imagined Drevane's world full of such men: broad, scarred, loud with conviction. Hale was narrow and gray and precise, a man assembled entirely of angles, with small bright eyes set deep in a face that seemed embarrassed by them. He wore plain wool without insignia. He carried a single leather case, and he set it down on the cell's stone bench as carefully as a physician laying out instruments.

Two wardens unlocked Astrid's chains from the wall-ring and re-fastened them to a ring bolted before the bench, leaving him enough slack to sit on the floor facing it. It was, Astrid noted distantly, almost courteous. Almost a conversation between equals. He had been in enough smugglers' negotiations to know courtesy when it was being used as a tool.

"Thank you for receiving me," Hale said, and sat.

"You chained me to the floor."

"A formality. I find formalities put everyone at ease." He opened the case. Inside lay neither thumbscrews nor irons but paper, ink, a folded cloth, and — Astrid's breath caught despite himself — a candle. A real candle, tallow, ordinary, unlit. "You have not eaten well, I think. You leave the meat."

"I leave the meat because I don't know what's in it."

"Sensible." Hale made a small note. Astrid could not see the words; the angle was wrong; he suspected the angle was always going to be wrong. "Lord-Captain Drevane believes you know where the remaining bearers are gathered. I am not Lord-Captain Drevane. I am not interested in Corvin the ferryman, or the seer Ivetta, or Tam Voss, whose fall from court grace was, I assure you, extensively documented years before you ever met him."

Astrid kept his face still. Somewhere behind it, though, his heart had begun to move faster, because this was worse. This was so much worse than questions about locations. Locations had answers you could refuse.

"What are you interested in?"

"The gift." Hale said it the way another man might say *the weather*. "Ember-sight. You understand, Master Eastwick, that you are the first registered bearer we have held who possesses it intact and cooperative. Most of the old blood carry only echoes. A trick of warmth. A knack for kindling. Yours is—" he unfolded the cloth, revealing slender silver instruments Astrit refused to name, "—architectural. Foundational. The covenant was built by sight like yours. Which raises a question that has occupied my office for some years."

He picked up the candle and turned it in his gray fingers.

"When you look at the land with that sight of yours — when you saw the magic guttering, as the reports say you described it — what is it you actually *see*?"

"I see fire," Astrid said. "Old fire. Fading."

"Mm. And fire, as every child knows, can be *fed*." Hale set the candle down between them, upright, patient. "There it is. Do you know why I brought a candle into a witch-cell, Master Eastwick? Because I want to watch you look at it. Not burn it. Look. And I want you to tell me, precisely, in whatever words you have, what a candle looks like to a man who sees the way you see."

"And if I refuse?"

Hale smiled. It was a small smile, exact as a ledger-line. "Then we continue these conversations until you don't. I have time, Master Eastwick. The Mountain has time. Blight does not hurry men like me; it makes us patient. Now." He struck a match against the bench-edge. The flame stood up small and yellow and ordinary, and Astrid felt his gift rise in him unbidden, the way a dog lifts its head at a whistle.

He tried to hold it back. He had spent six years holding it back, and he was good at it, better than anyone alive. But he was starved and sleep-starved and chained, and the ember-sight had never once in his life obeyed him when it mattered most.

The candle opened to him.

It was not fire the way eyes saw fire. It was a thread — a thin gold filament rising out of nothing, burning itself upward and outward, spending itself, and below the flame, impossibly, Astrid saw the wick's whole history: the field where the flax grew, the river that turned the retting-mill, the hands that had twisted the cord, all of it still present in the burning, all of it *remembered* by the flame as it consumed its own past. And around the flame, faint as breath on cold glass, the shape of something vast — the Emberlands pressing close against the skin of the world, drawn toward any open flame the way moths are drawn, or the drowning toward air.

And beneath all of it, underneath everything, he felt again the wrongness he had felt on the road. The hollow note. The land's great fire guttering not naturally, not the way fires fade when fuel fails, but the way fires fade when someone is *drawing off the fuel.*

He must have shown something in his face, because Hale leaned forward, very slightly, and made another note.

"Fascinating," the Inquisitor murmured. "Continue."

"No."

"Master Eastwick—"

"I'll describe the candle," Astrid said, and heard his own voice come out cracked and stubborn, "because you asked about the candle and the candle didn't chain me to anything. But I'm done giving you the rest for free. You want the architecture of my sight? You want to know what feeds it and what starves it and what it means? Then bring me something worth trading for. Bring me news of a girl called Wren. Red hair. Nine years old. Taken from a village your people burned."

Hale regarded him for a long moment. The candle flame bent in a draft from somewhere deep in the Mountain, and Astrid watched the Inquisitor watch him, and understood suddenly that this, too, was data. Everything was. His refusal was a datum. His crack in his voice was a datum. Hale was not hunting bearers; Hale was hunting *the gift itself*, mapping it the way a cartographer maps a coastline he intends to own.

"You are in no position to trade," Hale said at last, mildly.

"Everyone's in a position to trade. That's what trading is."

Another note. Then Hale closed the case over the instruments, pinched out the candle between finger and thumb without flinching — and Astrid saw the tiny golden thread of it curl and vanish back into the wick, unspent, *saved*, and filed that away too, in the growing hoard of things he knew that Hale did not know he knew — and rose.

"We will speak again," Hale said. "Eat the meat. You'll want your strength. What comes next requires it."

* * *

What came next required it. Astrid learned that too.

Hale returned — always alone, always with the case, always with some small ordinary object to be looked at: a key, a sprig of rowan, a coin, a lock of hair wrapped in linen that Astrid refused to touch and refused to explain why he refused to touch. Each session, the questions drew closer to the bone. Not *where are the bearers* but *what do the bearers feel when they harmonize*. Not *where is the covenant-stone* but *what would happen if the stone were reforged — describe the mechanics, if you can, the flow, the exchange*. Once, softly, terribly: *if a bearer surrendered his gift entirely, what would remain of him?*

Astrid gave ground inch by inch, trading truth for truth, hoarding the girl's name like a coin he could not spend. Wren. He asked after her each session. Each session, Hale wrote something down and said nothing, and the nothing said *she is alive, or she is useful, or she is leverage, and we have not yet decided which.*

Between sessions, there was the light, and the chains, and the slow erosion of days. Astrid began talking to himself just to keep language in his mouth. He recited smuggling-routes under his breath, the Thornfell passes, the salt-roads, the ferry-landings — Corvin's ferry, Corvin's river, Corvin's laugh like wet gravel. He recited Maren's hedge-cantations until he feared the walls were learning them, then stopped, then started again, because the alternative was silence, and silence in the everburning light was its own kind of interrogation.

It was on a day he could not number — the meat untouched again, his ribs sharp under his fingers, the witchfire steady and pitiless — that he noticed the warden.

Not the boot-shift warden, whose tread he knew. This one paused outside his slot longer than duty required. When the food came through, it came with something extra pressed flat against the tray's underside, and Astrid nearly missed it: a heel of real bread, dark and dense, smuggler's bread, road-bread, the kind baked in the Thornfell foothills. Someone from home. Someone who knew the roads.

The warden was already moving on, but Astrid caught the profile through the slot: young, heavy-browed, a faded burn scar along one jaw. He said nothing. Neither did she — for it was a woman, Astrid realized, despite the cut of the uniform — but as she passed the lamp-sconce beside his door, her gauntlet brushed the iron frame.

And the witchfire flickered.

Not the way it flickered for his dreams. It *stuttered* — a deliberate stutter, long-short-long-short, three beats and a pause — and Astrid's skin went cold all over, because he knew that rhythm. Every smuggler on the Thornfell roads knew that rhythm. Three and a gap. *Watch. Listen. Wait.*

Someone had marked him. Someone inside the Mountain was signaling in smuggler's code, and his heart slammed against his ribs so hard the chains rattled.

Over the next span of days — he had stopped counting, but it was many — the signals resolved into a grammar. The bread kept coming. The lamp-code grew bolder: three-and-gap meant *wait*, two quick stutters meant *not tonight*, and once, unmistakably, a long pattern that spelled out, in old road-tap, a word every smuggler used for a trusted stranger: *friend?*

The night Astrid answered — rapping his chain against the wall-ring, three-and-gap, then, after a heartbeat, *yes* — he heard, very faintly, through the stone, a breath that might have been a laugh.

Her name, when it finally came, came scratched into the underside of the next bread-heel in letters too small for any lamp-lit inspection to catch: *BRYN.*

And beneath the name, a question: *WHAT DO YOU NEED?*

Astrid sat with the bread crumb in his palm for a long time, in the merciless light, and thought about what he needed. Escape — yes, obviously, escape, but escape alone was worthless while Wren rotted somewhere in these depths and Ivetta sat in a cell like this one and Sylwen faded in the far Emberlands and an engine of stolen covenant-stones drank the life of two worlds beneath the Regent's feet. Escape was a smuggler's answer. He had been a smuggler. He was something else now, or becoming something else, and the something else needed more than a door.

He needed to be a message.

He closed his hand around the crumb. And then, slowly, deliberately, he did the thing he had sworn since childhood never to do in a cage: he opened his gift.

Just a seam. Just enough. He let the ember-sight rise through him like heat through a chimney, and he reached — gently, the way Maren had taught him to reach toward a dying hearth-coal to coax it back to life — toward the witchfire lamp beside his door.

Witchfire was not fire. He had known that from the first hour. Witchfire was *bound* fire, enslaved flame, a burning thing made to serve, and now, up close, with his sight laid open against it, Astrid could finally see what it truly was: a thread of gold, thin and struggling, wound tight in chains of gray binding-script, burning against its will.

Just like him.

*I know,* he told it, in the wordless way his gift spoke. *I know. Me too.*

And he unpicked the lowest strand of its binding — one thread, one small mercy, the least unchaining imaginable — and the witchfire flared, once, gold instead of pale, a heartbeat of true flame inside the prison-light, and in that heartbeat Astrid poured everything he needed carried: *Sylwen. Greywater. The choir lives. Wren — find Wren. Tell them Astrid endures.*

Then he sealed the binding again, neat as a smuggler's knot, and slumped back against the stone, shaking, drained, half-blind with afterimages of gold.

Through the slot, faintly, came Bryn's voice for the first time — low, rough, awed:

"What in the Thornfell's name was that?"

"That," Astrid whispered, smiling at the ceiling with cracked lips, "was mail."

The lamp settled back to its pale obedience. But somewhere in the veins of the Mountain, Astrid knew, a message was traveling — riding the bound fires from lamp to lamp, corridor to corridor, down toward whatever darkness held the girl with red hair, and out, perhaps, beyond, toward rivers and ferries and a woman with wings folded into a tinker's coat.

Even caged, he was not powerless. Even caged, he was *heard*.

He ate the bread. All of it, crust and crumb, and for the first time since the chains went on, he slept — really slept — and dreamed not of fire but of water, a brown river, a gravel-voiced ferryman poling toward him out of the mist, and standing at the bow, arms crossed, exasperated and alive, a tinker with storm-grey eyes saying, *took you long enough, apprentice.*

He woke to boots. Many boots. And a voice like dry paper, outside his door, saying:

"Good morning, Master Eastwick. I believe you have news for me."

* * *

Hale entered without the case.

That was how Astrid knew, before a word was spoken, that everything had changed. The case was Hale's ritual, Hale's armor, Hale's theater of the harmless scholar — and it was gone. In its place the Inquisitor carried only a folded sheet of paper, and his small bright eyes had a new stillness in them, the stillness of a man who has found a discrepancy in a ledger and cannot yet decide how large it is.

"Sit," Hale said, though Astrid was already sitting, chained as ever to the ring before the empty bench. Hale remained standing. That was new also.

"Last night," Hale said, "at approximately the fourth bell, every bound-flame lamp in the eastern cells burned true-gold for the space of one heartbeat. One heartbeat, Master Eastwick, across forty lamps, in perfect synchrony. My colleagues believe it was a fault in the binding-works." He unfolded the paper. "I do not believe in faults. I believe in causes. And I believe the cause was you."

Astrid said nothing. His pulse was a drum in his throat, but his face, six years of practice, held.

"Here is what interests me," Hale continued, crouching suddenly to bring his bright eyes level with Astrid's, and his voice dropped to almost a confessional murmur. "Not that you did it. That is merely power, and power I understand. What interests me is *how*. To unbind even one strand of a witchfire binding without breaking it — that requires sight of the binding itself. Sight of the *scripture of enslavement*, if you will forgive the term. Which means your gift does not merely perceive fire, Master Eastwick. It perceives *chains*." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "All kinds."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, the Mountain hummed with its vast indifferent machinery, engines and edicts and the slow grinding appetite of the crown.

"So here is my new question," Hale said softly, "and I confess I ask it with genuine scholarly delight. If you can see chains—" his eyes flicked, just once, deliberately, to the ring on the floor, the manacles on Astrid's wrists, the whole architecture of the cell, "—what do you see when you look at *me*?"

And Astrid, who had spent his whole life hiding his sight from everyone including himself, looked — openly, for the first time, letting the ember-vision rise like dawn through his exhaustion — and saw.

Inquisitor Hale stood wrapped in chains. Great linked lengths of them, gray and dense as gravestone, winding from his shoulders down around his chest and arms and throat, runed and orderly and *old* — older than his office, older than his name, binding-script woven so deep into the fabric of him that Astrid understood in one lurching glance that Hale had been bound since boyhood, bound to something above him, something vaster, the same gray script that wreathed the Mountain itself, the same script that ran down through the floors beneath, converging, all of it, on the harvesting engine and the stolen stones and the Regent's throne —

— and threaded through those gray chains, fine as spider silk, almost invisible, almost lost, a single strand of *gold*.

One golden thread, running straight through the heart of Inquisitor Hale, humming faintly, waiting.

An ember-thread. A covenant-thread. Hidden so deep even Hale himself could not know it was there.

Astrid looked up into the Inquisitor's bright, patient, chained eyes, and smiled with split lips, and said:

"I see a man who doesn't know what he's carrying."

For the first time since the cells, Inquisitor Hale had nothing to write down.
