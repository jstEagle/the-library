## 14. The Court Beneath the Mountain

They took him down before dawn, when even the witch-cells grew quiet and the torches in their iron cages burned low and blue.

Astrid had learned to measure time by those torches. Three days since Greywater Ford, three days since he had spoken his own name into Inquisitor Hale's careful silence like a man paying out rope from his own gallows. Three days of cold stone, thin gruel, and the jailer Wren's soft footsteps passing his door at hours no honest man kept. He had begun, in the manner of prisoners everywhere, to make a country of small things: the drip of water at the cell's far corner, the rhythm of boots overhead, the particular way Hale's voice carried through the walls — patient, unhurried, a blade being sharpened on a strap.

So when the locks began turning — all of them, one after another, down the length of the corridor like a row of falling dominoes — Astrid knew it was not an ordinary hour.

Two guards entered without speaking. A third carried shackles, but not the burning-iron kind they used on registered bearers. These were plain steel, almost courteous, and that courtesy frightened him more than cruelty would have. They chained his wrists before him, wrapped a hood of grey wool over his head, and marched him into the dark.

He counted turns. It was an old smuggler's habit, and it kept the panic at arm's length: left, then a long descent, right, another descent steeper than the first, the air growing colder and tasting of deep stone and old water. The mountain under the capital was hollowed like a honeycomb; every child in Vaelmoor knew the stories of the Underhalls where the first kings were crowned. No story had ever mentioned how far down the crowning went.

The air changed. Cold gave way to warmth, and warmth to something stranger — a dry, mineral heat, like standing near a forge that breathed. And beneath it, faint as a memory of a memory, Astrid felt a hum.

His gift stirred before he could stop it.

Even hooded, even half-starved and chained, ember-sight woke in him the way a sleeper wakes to smoke. Through the wool he saw nothing with his eyes, but behind them bloomed light: threads of it, gold and green and fading rose, running through the rock like veins through a leaf. Covenant-stone. He knew its signature now, had spent weeks learning it on the road — the warm grain of it, older than any kingdom, singing at a pitch only he could hear.

There should not have been covenant-stone beneath the capital. The old roads ran north and west, through Thornfell, along the river-countries. Whatever this was, it had been *brought* here.

*Stolen*, said a voice in him that sounded uncomfortably like Sylwen.

The hood came off.

* * *

The chamber they stood in was vast beyond reason, a cavern the kings of old must have carved when they still feared the sky. Its ceiling vanished into darkness hung with chains and scaffolds, and its floor — Astrid's breath caught — its floor was a map. Not drawn: built. Rivers of silver inlay, mountains of pale stone raised to knee-height, forests rendered in lapis and malachite, the whole of the twin realms spread beneath his feet in miniature, so perfect he could find the bend of the Emberwash where Corvin poled his ferry, the notch of White Pass where Sylwen had come out of the snow like a knife made of kindness.

And across the map, from the borders of the Emberlands inward, a blight had been rendered too — veins of black glass creeping through the fae territories, thinning as they crossed into human lands, reaching toward the capital itself like frost toward a window.

At the heart of the chamber, where the map showed the Sundered Stone's ancient site, engineers had raised a scaffold around something enormous. Astrid could not see what it held yet; canvas shrouded it. But the hum came from there, and the light behind his eyes gathered there, dozens of covenant-stones' worth, bound together in a lattice of iron and gold wire, drinking.

Men swarmed over the scaffold like ants over a wounded beast. He heard hammers, the song of wire being drawn, a foreman's voice calling measurements in the flat cadence of men who have done a thing ten thousand times and will do it ten thousand more.

"You'll want to close your mouth, apprentice. It's unbecoming."

Hale stood at his shoulder. The Inquisitor wore court dress tonight instead of the road-grey, and looked wrong in it, like a heron in a stable. His hands were folded behind his back, his face arranged in its usual expression of mild, scholarly interest — the expression of a man reading a book he has already finished.

"Where are we?" Astrid asked.

"The Foundry," Hale said, as if naming a chapel. "Or the Court Beneath the Mountain, if you prefer the old name. Their majesties of the first dynasty held audience here, back when kings still remembered what the covenant cost." He smiled thinly. "We remember now. We've simply revised the accounting."

Beyond the map, on a dais of black basalt, chairs were being set. Servants moved among them with candles. A court was assembling above ground somewhere, descending in silk and furs to witness whatever the shrouded thing would become.

"The Regent will receive you shortly," Hale went on. "Until then, look. You have a gift for looking, I'm told. Consider this an education."

"I'd rather consider it evidence," Astrid said. "For whatever trial you're building."

"There is no trial, Master Eastwick. Trials are for the guilty." Hale turned his mild gaze on the scaffold. "You are a tool of state now, and tools are maintained, not judged. Though I confess—" he lowered his voice, conspiratorial, almost gentle— "I did enjoy our conversations. You lie beautifully. Most bearers lie badly; guilt leaks out of them like light under a door. But you—" he tapped Astrid's chained wrist once, lightly, "—you seal your doors. It's why Drevane chose you, I think. And it's why I know you lied about Greywater Ford."

Astrid kept his face still. Somewhere behind his ribs, his heart was trying to climb out.

"The seer," Hale continued, watching him. "Ivetta. You gave us a river-crossing that doesn't exist and a hermit who died nine years ago. Very good work. Drevane believes you broke under pressure. I believe you're the best liar I've ever interrogated, which is a compliment, and also means the others are still free." He sighed, genuinely regretful. "Which is why you're here tonight. Not to be persuaded. To be *shown*. The Regent finds that witnesses convert faster than arguments."

"Shown what?"

"What we're building. What we're saving." Hale gestured, and a servant brought wine; Astrid refused it with a shake of his head, and Hale drank his own with the serenity of a man toasting nothing at all. "You've seen the blight, haven't you? On your little tour of the countryside. Dead orchards. Silvered rivers. Fae lights going out like snuffed candles. You believe it's natural. A dying of the light. The Emberlands failing because the covenant failed, the world winding down like a neglected clock."

"It is dying," Astrid said quietly. "I've *seen* it."

"With your gift, yes. Which sees truth but not causes." Hale set down his cup. "Come. Walk with me. The Regent dislikes being kept waiting, but she dislikes ignorance in her instruments more."

* * *

They walked the edge of the great map, Hale's boots clicking along the silver rivers, and as they walked, the Inquisitor lectured in the tone of a tutor leading a slow student through proofs.

"The blight is real," he said. "That much your eyes told you truly. The Emberlands *are* failing — have been for two centuries, since the last bearer stopped tending the stones. Magic is not infinite, Master Eastwick. It is a crop. It requires husbandry. The covenant was husbandry: bearers feeding the land, the land feeding both realms, the border porous and generous. Then the covenant broke, and the fields went fallow."

"And so you harvest," Astrid said. "From the stones you stole."

"Recovered," Hale corrected mildly. "Salvaged from neglect. Do you know what happens to a fallow field if no one claims it? Weeds claim it. Rot claims it. Or a neighbor with sense claims it, plows it, and feeds people." He nodded toward the scaffold. "The engine will draw the residual magic of the Emberlands — the deep reserves, the ones the fae hoard out of sentiment — and redistribute it. Into the crown's lands. Into the crown's *people*. Crops that grow through drought. Children who don't die of winter-fever. Is that ambition, apprentice? Or is that mercy?"

"It's theft."

"It's *arithmetic*," Hale said, and for the first time something sharp moved behind his mildness. "One realm hoards magic it can no longer use while the other starves. The arithmetic favors us. The Regent merely intends to balance the books."

They had come around the map's curve, and now Astrid saw the blight-veins up close, the black glass inlaid among the lapis forests — and stopped walking.

Because the veins were wrong.

Ember-sight is not only sight; it is *taste*, *grain*, the feel of a thing's making. He had felt true blight on the road, in the burned village, in the silvering streams: a sickness with a texture like ash and grief, slow, organic, sorrowful. What lay inlaid in the map before him had that texture — and under it, threaded through it like wire through wax, another signature entirely. Human workmanship. Recent. Deliberate.

He looked up at Hale. "This isn't a record. This is a plan."

Hale's smile was the smile of a teacher whose student has finally arrived. "There it is. Yes. I wondered how long your sight would take to read it." He stepped closer, voice dropping. "The blight is real, Master Eastwick. It is also *encouraged*. Seeded, in certain places, along certain lines. A dying Emberlands justifies the hunt. A justified hunt recovers the stones. The recovered stones build the engine. The engine saves the realm — or enough of it to matter. Fear is simply the plow that breaks the ground first." He spread his hands. "You wanted a villain. There isn't one. There's a ledger."

For a moment Astrid could not speak. The fury came up through him slowly, the way floodwater comes into a cellar — silent, total, rising past his knees before he notices he is drowning.

Not fate. Not the world winding down. Not some sorrowful natural death he might grieve and forgive. *Ambition.* Men had seeded the blight the way farmers seed a field, had let villages burn and children be dragged from their mothers at Millbrook Cross, had hunted Sylwen's people to the edge of extinction — all to justify a theft dressed as salvation. All those dead, and the ledger balanced neatly above their graves.

"You're smiling," Hale observed. "I'd expected weeping. Or rage. Rage is common."

"I'm not smiling."

"No. Forgive me. It's difficult to read you." Hale studied him a moment, then inclined his head toward the dais. "Ah. She's here."

* * *

The Regent descended into the Foundry like winter coming into a room.

She was not old — that surprised him. Silver at her temples, yes, and lines around her mouth that spoke of long decisions rather than long years, but she moved with the brisk vitality of a woman in her forties who has never once doubted herself. She wore black and white, no crown, only a circlet of plain iron, and she crossed the map-floor without looking down at it, as though the realms beneath her feet were beneath notice.

Her name was Regent Maerlin. She had ruled for eleven years since the king's death, governing in the name of a boy heir no one had seen in public since his ninth birthday. She paused before Astrid, and looked at him the way Hale read books: already finished.

"So," she said. "The tracker."

"Your Majesty."

"Don't grovel. It wastes time." She turned to the scaffold. "Do you know what you're looking at?"

"A machine to kill the Emberlands."

"A machine to *save* mine." She said it without heat, the way one states the weather. "Your fae friend — Sylven, Silwen, whatever she calls herself this decade — will tell you the reserves belong to her people. Her people are dying anyway. Magic unused is magic wasted; magic wasted is murder of everyone who might have lived by it. I intend to spend the inheritance before the house burns down. If that makes me a thief, then every granary-master in history is a thief, and I'll wear the title comfortably."

"And the blight you planted?" Astrid heard his own voice go flat and cold, and did not recognize it. "The villages. The hunt. The girl at Millbrook Cross they dragged away in front of her mother. Was that arithmetic too?"

Something flickered across the Regent's face — not guilt. Recognition, perhaps, of an inconvenience. "Collateral," she said. "Regrettable. Necessary. You of all people should understand necessary losses, Eastwick. You burned a memory-charm on your own crewmate to save your skin. Did you weep for him?"

The words went in like a hook behind the breastbone. Astrid let them. He had carried Tamrin's empty eyes for years; one more weight would not drown him, and he would not give her the flinch she was fishing for.

"No," he said. "But I never called it mercy."

For the first time, the Regent looked at him fully — a long, measuring look, the look of a woman reassessing the quality of a horse. "Hale says you're the best liar he's met. I begin to think he undersold you. You're not lying now, though. Now you're furious. Good. Fury is useful. Fury can be aimed." She gestured, and the engineers began hauling the shrouds from the scaffold. "Look at what your fury could serve."

The canvas fell.

The engine was beautiful, and that was the worst of it. A great wheel of interlocking rings, gold and iron, each ring set with covenant-stones — dozens of them, scores, whole constellations of stolen dawn-light caged in wire — and at its center a socket, empty, waiting, sized for one stone larger than all the rest. Astrid's sight flared involuntarily, and he gasped despite himself: the lattice *sang*, a vast discordant chord, all the voices of the choir forced into one grinding note.

"That socket," Hale murmured beside him, watching his face with scholarly delight, "is for the keystone. The master-stone of the Sundered Stone itself. Without it, the engine draws trickles. With it—" he spread his hands— "it drinks rivers."

"We don't have the keystone," the Regent said. "It shattered with the covenant. But shards survive. Fragments. Held by bearers, hidden in relics, buried in graves. Your choir, Master Eastwick. Every bearer you've been tracking carries a piece of it — in blood, in bone, in the little charms they don't know they carry." She smiled, and it was almost kind. "You've been more useful than you know. Drevane's leash was long, but you reeled in every fish yourself."

The cellar-flood of fury crested and broke over his head, and Astrid let it fill him, let it burn away the last of his fear like frost before sun. They thought he was broken. They thought he was theirs. Fine. Let them think it. He had been hiding things from dangerous people since he was twelve years old, and he had never yet been caught by anyone who mattered.

He bowed his head, the picture of a beaten instrument. "What would you have me do?"

"Rest," the Regent said, satisfied. "Eat. In three days the court rides north to the Sundered Stone to witness the first harvesting. Drevane will want his tracker on the road — the bearers always run to the old site when the end comes; sentiment is predictable. You'll point, we'll take, and then, Eastwick, both realms eat." She turned away, dismissing him, already speaking to Hale about wire gauges and tribute schedules.

As the guards took his arms, Astrid looked back once at the engine — at the empty socket waiting at its heart like a hungry mouth — and let his ember-sight drink the lattice's discordant chord one final time, memorizing its pitch, its grain, its every sour harmonic.

Every lock has a key. Every cage has a seam. And every song, however mighty, can be sung wrong.

Three days. A court on the road. A choir scattered but alive — Corvin on his river, Ivetta in whatever dark place hid her, Sylwen somewhere between worlds, already looking for him. He had gotten messages out of worse places than this. Wren owed him nothing, but Wren had soft footsteps and a jailer's keys, and hope, Astrid had learned on the smuggler's roads, travels fastest in small hands.

They led him up out of the Foundry, and the hum of the stolen stones faded below him like a tide going out.

He walked into the dark with his chains loose at his wrists and his fury banked like a coal in his chest, and began, silently, to count the turns.
