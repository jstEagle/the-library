## 15. Tam's Betrayal

The letter came on a Tuesday, folded into the morning bread the way Tam had once folded poison into a rival's wine — cleanly, without ceremony, as if it belonged there.

He knew before he broke the seal. He knew by the wax: black, unmarked, pressed with a plain signet that no house in the capital would dare claim. He knew by the weight of it, too light for its size, the way a knife is lighter than the hand expects. And he knew most of all because for three days he had been waiting for it, sleeping badly in his chair with his boots on, and men who wait for bad news always recognize it when it arrives.

Tam set down the bread. His hands were steady. That was the worst part — they were always steady now, the tremor that had plagued him since Vellin Court stripped his robes gone these two years, replaced by a stillness he did not trust.

*Master Tam of the Ember Choir,* the letter began, which was a lie twice over, since there was no choir yet and he was master of nothing but debts. *Your sister Lirra resides at our convenience in the Lantern District. Your nephews attend their lessons punctually. It would grieve us to interrupt such promising educations.*

There was more. There was always more. A sum of money that would have bought his silence outright two years ago, when he still had a name worth buying. An address where reports might be left. And at the bottom, in a different ink, a single line that was not a threat so much as an accounting:

*The Regent requires only what you already know. Refusal requires what you do not.*

Tam read it four times. Then he fed it to the stove and watched the black wax bubble and go soft, and he sat in the cold gray light of his rented room above a chandler's shop, and he thought about Lirra's laugh, which was loud and undignified and had gotten her thrown out of two respectable houses before she was twenty. He thought about Benat, seven years old, who had once asked him whether mages could make it snow inside, and Tam had said yes, easily, and made it snow inside, and been scolded by his mother for wasting good magic on nonsense. He thought about little Oskar, who was afraid of dogs and thunder and being left alone, in roughly that order.

Then he thought about Ivetta's blind eyes turning toward him across the waystation fire, seeing him anyway, seeing everything, and how she had said, in that voice like dry leaves settling: *You carry something heavy, Tam of the Court. Set it down before it sets you down.*

He had almost told them, that night. Almost. The words had been in his mouth like a stone he'd been carrying under his tongue for weeks — *they know where I am, they know what I was, they will come asking* — and then Corvin had begun one of his endless river stories, and Sylwen had laughed, actually laughed, a sound like ice cracking off a spring branch, and Astrid had looked around the fire at all of them with an expression Tam recognized from mirrors: the look of a man who has spent his whole life alone and cannot quite believe he has stopped.

And Tam had swallowed the stone. Had smiled. Had added a verse to the song they were learning, the covenant-song, the one that made the ember-light rise off their skin like heat-shimmer, and had gone to sleep warm among people who trusted him.

That had been six days ago. The letter had taken three of them to find him.

* * *

He told himself he would not answer. For two days he told himself this, pacing the chandler's narrow stairs, buying candles he didn't need from the shop below just to have an excuse to be seen doing nothing. The chandler's wife, a broad woman named Hess who missed nothing, watched him wear a groove in her floorboards and finally said, "Whatever it is, mage, it'll still be there tomorrow. Sit down. Eat."

He ate. It helped, the way a plank helps a drowning man — briefly, and only until the next wave.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not leave him alone, that followed him into sleep and stood waiting when he woke: the choir was five people and a child against the crown of Vellin. Against Drevane, who had hunted bearers across three provinces without losing a man he couldn't afford. Against whatever engine they were building beneath the mountain, the one Astrid had seen in chains and described in a voice gone flat and careful, the way men describe wounds. If the crown wanted the Emberlands' last magic, it would take it. Not might. Would. The blight was real — Tam had felt it himself, the land's deep fires guttering, the old ley-roads going cold beneath his feet like veins closing — and frightened kingdoms did not negotiate with what they feared.

So the choice, presented honestly, was never whether the choir would fall. It was whether Lirra's boys would grow up, and whether they would grow up knowing why.

That was the lie he built, brick by patient brick, in those two days of pacing. That it was arithmetic. That love was simply a weight on one side of the scale, and truth a weight on the other, and any honest merchant would tell you which way the beam must tip. He was good at building lies; he had been Vellin Court's finest construct-mage before a scandal he still could not speak of had unmade him, and a lie was only a construct made of words.

The flaw in it — he saw this even then, some cold witness in him watching the whole edifice go up and marking every crack — the flaw was that the choir was not a weight. They were people who had sung to him. Who had saved him a place by the fire without being asked. Who had given the child Nessa the first full night's sleep she'd had since her village burned, and given Tam, God help him, the first purpose he'd had since the Court cast him out.

But Lirra had held his hair back when he drank himself sick at nineteen. Lirra had pawned her wedding bracelet to pay his fines. Lirra had written him every month for two years, letters addressed to *my brother, wherever you're hiding*, because she had never once believed the charges against him, and she was the only one.

Any honest merchant, Tam thought, staring at the ceiling through the third sleepless night. Any honest merchant knows exactly which way the beam tips.

* * *

He went on the fourth day. Of course he went on the fourth day; the drama of resistance was for men with more time than he had. He dressed in his shabbiest coat, the one that made him look like a failed scholar rather than a disgraced mage, and he walked to the Lantern District through streets gone strange with early winter, and he told himself at every corner that he could still turn back, and at every corner he did not turn back.

The address was a counting-house, gray and respectable, with brass fittings polished by generations of clerks. A man met him in a back room that smelled of ledger-paper and lamp oil. The man was nobody — middle-aged, forgettable, the kind of face Drevane's service bred on purpose — and he had a folder open on the table before Tam even sat down.

"Master Tam." The nobody smiled without warmth. "You received our letter."

"I received your letter."

"And you've come to discuss terms."

"I've come," Tam said, and heard his own voice go thin and courtly, the old Vellin accent rising like a scar, "to ask you a question first. If I give you nothing — if I walk out that door — what happens to my family?"

The nobody considered him with the patience of a man who had answered this question many times and enjoyed none of those answers less than this one. "Nothing," he said. "Immediately. Nothing happens immediately. That is rather the point of us, Master Tam. Nothing happens immediately, and then, after a while, everything does. A fire in a tenement. A fever that takes both boys in the same week, poor things. Your sister's husband loses his post and then his footing on a wet stair. Grief is very hard on widows." He turned the folder around. Inside were pages of neat script — Tam's history, his debts, the scandal, every name he'd ever drunk with, every door he'd ever knocked on, including, he saw with a lurch, the waystation's general region, circled in red. They had narrowed it to a valley. They needed only the valley's throat. "Or," the nobody continued, "you give us what you know, and your family continues its unremarkable life, and you continue yours, and everyone is spared a great deal of unpleasantness. We don't even require you to do anything further. One report. One location. After that, frankly, we won't need you — the Captain-General has his methods, once he has a starting point."

"And the bearers?" Tam asked. He hated how small his voice came out. "What happens to them?"

"What happens to anyone who stands in front of the crown." The nobody closed the folder gently, like a man tucking in a child. "Master Tam, I'll offer you a kindness, since you asked one of me. Don't make yourself important. Important people get interrogated. Give us the location, go home, drink yourself quiet, and live. In a year the whole matter will be settled and no one will remember your name, which I understand" — and here the smile again, worse than any threat — "is something you've wanted for a long time."

Tam looked at the circled valley. He thought: *I can give them a false location.* He thought: *I can warn them tonight, send word by ember-light, and give Drevane an empty hillside.* He thought these things, and he watched himself think them, and he understood with perfect clarity that he was constructing his alibi in advance, laying the bricks of a story in which he was secretly loyal, secretly clever, secretly good — and that the story required him to first commit the act itself, and that no man in the history of the world had ever committed a small treason intending to remain honest, and that the road to the pyre was paved with exactly this kind of arithmetic.

"Corvin the ferryman," Tam heard himself say. "Ivetta the seer. The girl Nessa, a child, eight years old. And the others." His mouth kept moving. Some distant part of him observed it with horror, the way one watches one's own hand slip on a cliff-face. "The Hollowmere waystation. Under the ruined mill, east fork of the Greywater. There's a cellar door behind the ivy. I can draw it."

"You can draw it," the nobody agreed, and slid paper and pen across the table.

Tam drew it. His hand, damn it, was perfectly steady. He drew the mill, the fork, the ivy, the door, the root-cellar where Nessa slept, the bolt-hole Corvin had dug in case of fire. He drew everything, because anything less would look like what it was, and they would know, and then Lirra's boys would learn what happened to families of men who tried to be clever.

When it was done, the nobody studied the drawing for a long moment, then looked up. "The fae woman," he said. "Sylwen. Describe her true form."

"I've never seen it."

"Describe what you have seen."

And Tam, who had seen her once at dusk on the ridge, silver-limbed and terrible, her shadow cast by no sun — Tam, whose whole life had been built on the precise management of what he revealed — Tam said: "She looks like a tinker. Ragged coat, mended cart. You'd walk right past her."

It was the last thing he ever gave them freely, and it was a lie, and it was worthless, and he knew all three.

* * *

He did not go home. He walked instead, for hours, through the graying afternoon, past the Lantern District where his nephews were at their lessons, past the river where Corvin had once ferried him as a boy for a copper and a joke, up onto the high road where the wind came off the Thornfells smelling of snow and, faintly, impossibly, of woodsmoke and honey — the smell of the Emberlands, the smell the covenant-song left on the air.

He stood on the high road and took out the ember-light charm Ivetta had pressed on him weeks ago, a little knot of woven straw that glowed when he sang to it, meant for sending word in emergencies. *Emergencies*, she had said, folding his fingers over it. *Not excuses. There's a difference, and you know it.*

He could send warning. Even now. Even with the drawing in Drevane's hands, riders moved slower than light, and the choir could scatter, could take the deep roads Astrid knew, could vanish into the mountains the way smugglers and hedge-witches had always vanished. He could save them. He could save them and hang, because the moment the waystation proved empty, the nobody's promise would curdle, and Lirra would learn what her brother had cost her, and it would not be a fever, it would be faster and worse.

Or he could stay silent, and let them be taken in their sleep, and live.

Tam stood on the high road until dark, holding the little straw knot, singing to it and stopping, singing and stopping, and the ember-light rose and fell in his fist like a small dying thing, like a heart deciding.

In the end — and this was the truth he would carry into whatever came after, the truth that drowned him more thoroughly than any river — in the end he did neither. He put the charm away unlit. He walked back down into the city. He bought a bottle of the cheap white wine Lirra liked and drank half of it in an alley and poured the rest into the gutter, because he could not even manage the honesty of getting properly drunk.

They would come for the waystation within days. He knew Drevane's methods; he had served beside the man's predecessors at Court; there would be no warning, no parley, no chance for a message to outrun the hounds. Whatever Tam did now, the die was cast, the drawing delivered, the door drawn in ink. All that remained was to learn what kind of man he had chosen to be when the bill arrived.

He went back to the chandler's and climbed the narrow stairs and sat in the dark, and for the first time in two years he prayed — not to any god he could name, but to the ember itself, to the old fire under the world, to whatever in the fading magic of two realms still listened.

*I didn't mean it,* he said to the dark. *I love them. God help me, I love all of them, and I sold them for two boys who won't remember my face.*

The dark said nothing. The candle he hadn't lit stayed unlit. But far to the east, beyond the mountains, beyond the borders of the world, the Emberlands flickered — and somewhere in the capital, in a gray counting-house, a bell rang once, and riders began to saddle in the night.

Tam sat awake until dawn, listening for hooves, hearing only his own heart, and dawning on him slowly, terribly, was the knowledge of the one thing left that he could still do — the one payment left in him, the only coin he had not yet spent.

He reached for his coat, and his traveling boots, and the last of his stolen Court magic, and he went out into the freezing dark to spend it.
