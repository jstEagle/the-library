## 16. Ride of the Tinker

The capital of Vaelmoor had a smell that clung to everything — coal smoke and tallow and the sour iron of the witch-cells beneath the Regent's mountain. Sylwen had worn her tinker's shape for three days now, hunched and gray, a stooped woman with chapped hands and a cart of pots and pans she did not need, and even so the city's stench seemed to find the fae in her, the way water finds a crack in stone.

She hated it here. She hated the walls most of all — stone stacked on stone until the sky was a ribbon overhead, as if the humans who built them believed they could wall out everything they feared. They could not wall out what was coming. She had seen it in the silvering of the river at dawn, in the way the gulls wheeled inland and would not return to the water. The Emberlands were dying by inches, and somewhere beneath this mountain, men were building the machine that would kill them altogether.

And Astrid Eastwick was in a cell under those stones.

She had told herself, on the long road from Greywater Ford, that this was strategy. That the choir needed its keystone whether he knew he was one or not. That Ivetta's vision — *the boy is not a bearer, girl, he is the hearth itself* — made his rescue a matter of necessity, not feeling.

She had told herself many things in three hundred years. She had learned to recognize the ones that were lies.

* * *

The jailer's name was Brannoc Hale's opposite in every way that mattered: broad where the Inquisitor was narrow, kind where Hale was precise, and afraid in the particular way of good men doing bad work. Sylwen had found him the second night, sitting alone in the shrine of the Ninefold Hearth with his helmet in his hands, praying to gods whose names the crown had struck from the calendar years ago.

She had not used glamour on him. She had simply sat down beside him, in her gray tinker's body, and said: "You prayed over the child they took from the ash village. I saw you do it."

He had gone very still.

"They say the gift-bearers are blight-bringers," he said finally. "That burning them saves us."

"And do you believe it?"

"I believe," Brannoc said, "that I have carried more children down those stairs than I have carried up." He looked at her then, really looked, and something in her disguised face must have answered him, because his voice dropped to almost nothing. "Who are you?"

"A friend of the man in the deep cell. The one Hale questions." She let a sliver of truth show through, just enough to catch the light. "The one who bargained himself into chains so that a child and an old woman could go free."

Brannoc closed his eyes. When he opened them, the decision was already made; she could see it settle over him like frost. "The cells flood at spring thaw," he said. "There are channels under the mountain, old ones, from when the fortress was a mine. They run down to the river level. Nobody uses them. Nobody remembers them."

"Show me."

* * *

Corvin came up the river on the third night, poling a flat barge he had bought with coins from a purse that had once belonged to a tax collector, and if the ferryman felt any remorse about the exchange he did not show it. He wore a fishmonger's apron over his old bones and smelled convincingly of eel, and when Sylwen met him at the rotting pilings below the tannery district he gripped her arm hard enough to bruise.

"You look terrible," he said.

"Thank you."

"No — I mean you look *thin*. Faded. Like a coat left out in winter." His eyes, milky-rimmed with age but still sharp underneath, searched her face. "How long since you've been home, girl? Truly home?"

"Home is a word for other people, Corvin."

"It wasn't always." He said it gently, and it landed like a hook behind her ribs, because he knew — the whole choir knew by now — what her exile had cost, what her love for a human man three centuries dead had broken open. She had confessed it to Astrid by firelight on the road, and she had thought that confession was the hardest thing she would ever say aloud.

She had been wrong. Tonight she intended to say something harder.

"The tide turns at moonrise," Corvin said, mercifully practical again. "I can hold the barge at the outflow channel for perhaps a quarter hour before the harbor watch wonders why an eel-boat is loitering under the fortress wall. After that we are either gone or we are fish food." He paused. "You're certain about the boy?"

"He is the keystone."

"That's not what I asked."

Sylwen looked out at the black water, at the fortress rising above it like a clenched fist, torchlight crawling along its battlements. Somewhere inside, in a cell carved from the mountain's own bone, a young man who had spent his whole life hiding was enduring interrogation because he had chosen chains over letting his friends burn.

"Yes," she said. "I'm certain."

* * *

Brannoc met her at the postern gate at the hour of the owl, dressed in plain watchman's black instead of his colors, carrying a ring of keys and a face like a man walking to his own execution and finding, to his surprise, that his legs held.

"The wardens change at the fourth bell," he murmured as they moved through the service corridors, past cisterns and coal stores, deeper into the cold. "Hale keeps irregular hours — he'll be in his chambers above until dawn, writing his reports. But the deep cell has its own keeper, and he drinks. He's drunk now. I've seen to it."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else for three days." His jaw worked. "My daughter has the hedge-sense. Small things — knowing when bread's done, when rain's coming, when her brother's lying. If the proclamation comes to our village, they'll take her. Register or be taken, the crier says. There's no registering for people like us. You understand?"

"I understand."

They descended. The stairs went down farther than any building should go, the air growing wet and mineral, until the torches gave out and Brannoc lit a shuttered lantern and the light showed walls slick with seepage and doors of black iron set at intervals like teeth.

At the last door, Brannoc stopped. Through the grate, in the lantern's spill, Sylwen saw him.

Astrid sat against the far wall with his knees drawn up, and even in the ruin of him — the split lip, the wrists raw from irons, the hollows under his eyes — there was something in his posture that had not been there on the road, something settled and unafraid, and Sylwen understood with a lurch that whatever Hale had wanted from him, he had not gotten it. The boy had starved the Inquisitor of the only thing worth taking: the nature of his gift. He had kept the ember hidden even here, even starving, even bleeding.

*Stubborn,* she thought, and the word came out warm. *Stubborn as a banked fire.*

Brannoc fitted the key. The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and Astrid's head came up, and for one heartbeat his eyes were pure animal terror — and then they found her, found the gray tinker-woman shape, and terror became confusion became something else entirely.

"Sylwen?" His voice was rusted. "Your face—"

"Is a lie I'm done wearing." She pulled the glamour off like a cloak thrown back.

It was not a transformation the way humans imagined transformations, all flash and spectacle. It was simpler than that and far stranger: she simply stopped pretending to be small. Her spine lengthened, her shoulders widened, the gray fell away from her skin like ash from a live coal, and what remained was what she had been before exile — tall and strange and luminous, hair like dark water, eyes the green-gold of light through leaves, the faint ember-glow beneath her skin that no human blood ever carried. In the lantern light of the dungeon she must have looked like something out of the oldest stories, the ones the crown had burned along with the bearers.

Astrid stared. Even beaten, even chained, he stared the way people stare at the first fire of autumn.

"You're—" he began.

"Loud, if we don't move." She broke the shackles at his wrists with a twist of fae strength that made Brannoc flinch, and hauled him upright. "Can you walk?"

"I've been walking in circles in that cell for six days. I could walk to the Emberlands."

"Don't tempt me." She caught his arm over her shoulder. He weighed less than he should have. Fury rose in her, slow and banked, at what they had done to him in six days, and she pushed it down where it would keep. "Jailer. The channels."

"This way. Quietly."

* * *

The old mine-channels were a nightmare of low stone and standing water, and Astrid's legs shook so badly by the second turning that Sylwen half-carried him, his breath ragged against her neck, and she found she did not mind at all. Behind them, Brannoc moved with the sureness of a man who had grown up in these tunnels — a miner's son, he'd said, before the crown bought the mountain — pausing at each junction to listen.

At the third junction, the listening saved them. Boots above. Voices. A bell beginning to ring somewhere far off, thin and wrong.

"They know," Brannoc breathed. "The cell keeper woke. Or—" his face went gray, "—or Hale checks on him at night. Sometimes he does. Sometimes he sits outside the door for hours."

"Hale," Sylwen said, "is going to be very disappointed."

They ran. Or stumbled, rather — Astrid's weight against her side, water sloshing at their ankles, the bell tolling faster now, joined by another, and another, the fortress waking above them like a hive struck with a stick. Light bloomed ahead: the outflow grate, moon-silvered, the river beyond it running high and black and cold.

Corvin's barge slid out of the shadow of the wall exactly as the quarter hour died, the old ferryman poling with a strength that belied his years, and Sylwen got Astrid through the grate with her shoulders screaming and Brannoc shoving from behind, and then they were tumbling onto wet planks and Corvin was pushing off and the current took them.

Shouts from the wall. A crossbow bolt slapped the water a hand's breadth from the hull. Then another. Then torches streaming along the battlements like a lit fuse, and the harbor bells joining the fortress bells, and somewhere a horn sounding the alarm-note that meant fugitives on the water.

"Down!" Corvin roared. "All of you flat!"

They flattened. The bolts went over. And then the barge swept around the breakwater's arm and into the open river, and the fortress fell behind, and the night opened up ahead of them — wide and dark and smelling of rain coming, of living water, of the world beyond the walls.

Sylwen lifted her head. Astrid was beside her, soaked and shaking and grinning — actually grinning, a wild, disbelieving grin that transformed his battered face into something young.

"We're alive," he said.

"Barely. Don't celebrate yet."

"I've been in a cell for six days, Sylwen. I'm celebrating." He laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through and became something else, and she put her hand on the back of his neck and let him press his forehead against her shoulder, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the river carried them south through the dark.

Behind them, the capital began to burn.

It was not their doing — she needed to believe that, and mostly did. It was panic, probably, watch-fires mislaid, a granary alight near the docks where someone had knocked a lantern in the chaos of the alarm. But fire in a wooden city feeds itself, and soon the glow on the horizon was orange and climbing, and the smoke rolled out over the water like a black banner, and the bells kept ringing, ringing, ringing, and Astrid watched it with an expression she could not read.

"All those people," he said quietly.

"Most of them will be fine. Cities burn often and survive it. It's what cities do." She hesitated. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing. Everything." He turned to look at her — at her true face, lit by the distant fire-glow, and she watched him take it in without fear, without flinching, and something in her chest that had been clenched for three hundred years loosened by a single degree. "I keep thinking about the first time I saw you. White Pass. The snow. I thought you were a tinker. Just a tinker." A breath of a laugh. "I was so busy being terrified of the hunters that I didn't even wonder why a tinker was walking alone in a blizzard."

"I wondered why a smuggler was walking alone in a blizzard," she said. "We were well matched."

"Sylwen." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "In the cell. When Hale asked me where the others were — Ivetta, the child, all of them — I told him nothing. Six days, and he got nothing. Not because I'm brave. Because I kept thinking — if I give them up, I'll never see—" He stopped. Started again. "I couldn't stop thinking about a fire, and someone sitting across from me, telling me about her exile. About loving someone she wasn't allowed to love. And I thought, if that's the thing I get to come back to, then I can endure anything Hale does to me. And that's not courage. That's just—"

He stopped again, and this time he did not start over, because Sylwen kissed him.

It was not graceful. They were both soaked, and the barge pitched, and Corvin made a pointed noise about watching the pole. But it was a declaration, and declarations do not need grace. When she drew back, the ember-light was rising faintly under Astrid's skin, answering hers, two small glows leaning toward each other in the dark like flames in a draft.

"There," she said, and her voice was not quite steady. "Now you've said it. So have I. No more unsaid things between us — we haven't time for them, and I've wasted three centuries on unsaid things already."

"Sylwen—"

"Save it. You can say it properly when we're not being shot at." She nodded past his shoulder, toward the stern, where Corvin stood rigid at the pole.

The old ferryman was staring upstream. Lanterns — dozens of them — were coming down the river road on the eastern bank, a procession of light moving fast, and above them, on the ridge, something larger: a glow that was not fire but engine-light, cold and white and wrong, the color of stolen covenant-stones working.

"They're not chasing us," Corvin said. His voice had gone thin. "Look where they're going, girl. Look where they're *all* going."

Sylwen followed his gaze, and her heart turned to ice water.

South. Every light, every lantern, every soldier — south, toward the ruined covenant-site. Toward the sundered stone. Toward Ivetta and Tam and the child and the fragile, half-woken choir.

Toward the harvesting engine, which was already glowing on the ridge like a second, terrible moon.

"They're not hunting us anymore," she said. "They don't need us. Drevane has what he wants." She turned to Astrid, and took his hand, and felt his pulse hammering against her palm. "They're marching on the stone. We have until dawn — maybe less. Corvin, how fast can this barge go?"

The old ferryman spat over the side, set his pole, and grinned like a man half his age.

"Faster than anything the crown ever built," he said. "Hold on."
