## 17. The Broken Covenant-Stone

They came down into the Vale of Alderhollow at dusk, when the light went long and amber and made even ruin look like memory.

Astrid rode last, as he always did, with the child—Wren, though she had not yet given him leave to use the name—wrapped in his cloak behind Sylwen's saddle. The girl had not spoken since Greywater Ford. She ate what was put in her hands and slept pressed against whichever body was warmest, and her eyes had the flat shine of a pond in winter: still on top, and who knew how deep beneath. Astrid did not push. He knew something of what it was to keep yourself folded small inside your own skin.

The vale opened before them like a cupped hand. Alders gone silver with age stood along a stream that ran thin and clear over pale stones, and above them, on a shelf of granite where the hills gathered close, the covenant-site waited. He felt it before he saw it—the way you feel a hearth room from a cold street, a warmth in the air that has no business being there. His ember-sight, guttering these past weeks like a candle in a draft, flared once, involuntary, and he gasped.

"What is it?" Sylwen reined in beside him. In her tinker's guise she was all sharp angles and road dust, copper hair bound under a scarf, but her eyes—her eyes were always the same, green as deep water over embers.

"It's *warm*," he said. "The whole hill. Like standing outside a forge."

"That is because it remembers being one." She said it quietly, and there was something in her voice he had never heard before—not the wry distance she wore like armor, not the guilt that lived underneath it, but something older and more careful. Reverence, maybe. Or grief so old it had polished itself smooth. "Every fae child learns this place's name before they learn their own. Cael Aenach, the Singing Seat. Where the two worlds shook hands."

They left the horses among the alders and climbed on foot. Corvin went first, leaning on his river-staff, breathing hard on the steep places; Ivetta came behind him with Tam's hand at her elbow, her blind face tilted up as if she were reading the sky by some script only she could see. Wren would not let go of Astrid's sleeve, so he climbed with her fingers knotted in the wool, and it occurred to him, absurdly, that this was the first time in his life he had climbed toward something instead of away from it.

The stone stood at the top of the shelf, and it was broken.

He had imagined it whole without ever meaning to—a pillar, perhaps, or a menhir such as dotted the high pastures back home. What rose from the granite shelf instead was a sundered thing: a great spire of veined grey rock, taller than three men, split cleanly from crown to root as if by a single stroke of unimaginable force. The two halves leaned apart, a hand's width of empty air between them, and through that gap the evening sky showed in a narrow bright seam, like a door left ajar in a dark house.

And through the seam, faint as breath on glass, shimmered ember-light.

"Oh," Ivetta said softly. She had stopped three paces out, her head bowed, her hands working at each other. "Oh, it's *still trying*."

Astrid stepped closer, and his sight opened whether he willed it or no.

He had seen the land's magic guttering for weeks now—the silvering rivers, the hedgerows going grey at the root, the way birdsong thinned year over year until the mornings felt underpopulated. But here, at the broken heart of everything, he saw what the guttering meant. The ember-light bled upward through the seam in the stone in a slow, threadbare ribbon, and where it passed over the granite it left traces, ghost-warmths, the fossil glow of ten thousand years of traffic between worlds. He could see the shapes of what had been: processions of light descending the hill, human voices rising to meet them, a weaving so dense and constant it had soaked into the very rock. And threaded through all of it, holding the pattern's shape the way a loom holds cloth, a song. Not sound. Something deeper than sound. A harmony with parts for every kind of voice—human and fae and the stone itself, which had been the deepest part of all.

The song was nearly silent now. But in the gap between the sundered halves, the last few threads still trembled, still reached for each other across the break, and could not touch.

"The Blight didn't do this," he heard himself say. His voice came out strange, hushed, as if he were in a sickroom. "This was struck. Deliberately."

"A hundred years ago," Sylwen said. She had come to stand at the base of the western half, one palm flat against the stone, and her tinker's disguise had begun to slip—he could see it fraying at the edges of her, the air around her hands bending with heat-shimmer, her shadow on the rock too tall and too fine for any mortal frame. "In the last days of the old court, when fear of humans curdled into law. There were those who believed the covenant made us weak. That closing the door would make us pure again." Her jaw tightened. "They were wrong about what closing the door would cost. But they broke it anyway, and the ones who understood the price were exiled or worse, and I—" She stopped. Swallowed whatever the next word was.

"You weren't there," Astrid finished gently. "You'd already been cast out."

"I was already nothing," Sylwen said, "which amounts to the same absence." She looked up at the seam of light, and for a moment her composure cracked clean through, and Astrid saw the exile entire: a woman locked outside a burning house, watching the windows. "But the stone can be reforged. That much is true, whatever else is ash. My grandmother sang its making. It wants only—" She hesitated.

"Only what?" Tam asked. He had hung back the whole climb, and he hung back now, arms crossed tight against the cold, looking less like a court mage than like a man standing at the edge of a debt he could not pay.

"Sacrifice," Sylwen said. "The stone is not mortar and masonry. It is an agreement given flesh. To reforge it, a bearer must give their gift into it wholly—the whole of it, root and branch, never to be called again. Their power becomes the binding. Their song becomes the stone's song." She turned, and her eyes found Astrid's, and held. "That is the price written in my grandmother's verses. One voice, surrendered entirely, to hold the door open forever."

* * *

They built no fire. There was no need; the stone gave warmth like a banked hearth, and they sat in its glow on the granite shelf while the sky went from amber to violet to a blue so deep it was nearly black, and the first stars pricked through the seam in the stone as if the heavens themselves were leaning in to watch.

Corvin offered first.

He did not make a speech. He simply got up, the old ferryman, joints cracking, and walked to the gap between the halves, and laid both hands on the stone, and said, in the plain voice he used for naming fares: "I've carried folk across water fifty years. Carried 'em over the boundary too, them that needed crossing quiet. If my gift's good for anything, it's good for doors." He closed his eyes. "Take it, then. All of it."

For a moment nothing happened. Then the ember-light in the seam brightened, swirled, gathered toward him—and Astrid's sight flared wide, and he saw what the stone saw.

He saw Corvin's gift laid open like a hand unclenching: the ferryman's knack, the old blood in him that let him read currents no eye could find, that had let his fathers pole their barges between the worlds when the crossings were legal and common. It was a modest gift, homely, worn smooth with use—but it was *his*, grown into the grain of him like rings into oak, and when the stone reached for it, the reaching caught. Snagged. The light shuddered around Corvin's offering the way water shudders around a half-sunk log, probing, testing, and then withdrew, ebbing back into the seam, dimmer than before.

Corvin staggered. Tam caught his arm. "It refused me," the old man said, bewildered, hurt, as if the stone had slapped his hand away from a cradle. "Why would it refuse? I offered *all* of it."

"Because it isn't enough," Ivetta said.

Her voice was very quiet, and it silenced the shelf. She rose without help, feeling her way forward with her white staff, and stood where Corvin had stood, and lifted her blind face to the seam.

"I have seen this night," she said. "I have seen it for thirty years, in fragments, in fever-dreams, and I hoped I read it wrong. I did not read it wrong." She set her palms against the stone. "I give my sight. All of it. Every vision, every veil, every terrible mercy it has ever shown me. Take it and be whole."

Again the light gathered. Again Astrid saw the opening of a soul—Ivetta's second sight unfolding like a moth from a chrysalis, vast and sorrowful, winged with everything she had ever foreseen—and again the stone probed it, tested it, and recoiled. The ember-light flickered and sank. Somewhere below them in the vale, the stream seemed to run a little quieter.

"No," Ivetta whispered. Her composure, which had held through capture and threat and the loss of everything, bent now. "No, please. I have *carried* it long enough. Take it."

"It refuses her too," Tam said. He had gone grey. "Why? Sylwen, why would it refuse?"

Sylwen did not answer at once. She was staring at the seam, and her hands had curled into fists at her sides, and when she finally spoke her voice was rough. "Because sacrifice alone is not enough. My grandmother's verses say the stone takes a gift only when the gift completes the song. It is not asking who will *pay*. It is asking who will *fit*." She turned to look at them all, and her face in the ember-light was terrible and young. "It is a lock, and we are keys thrown at it in the dark. Corvin's key turns halfway. Ivetta's turns halfway. The shape is wrong. The shape has been wrong for a hundred years, because the covenant was made for voices that no longer exist, and the world has changed key since."

"Then the song is dead," Corvin said heavily. He sat back down on the granite, suddenly old beyond his years, and stared at his own hands. "All this road. The girl taken at the ford. Ivetta in chains, Astrid in chains, Tam's family hunted. All of it, for a lock with no key."

"Not dead," Ivetta said. Still kneeling at the stone, she had begun to laugh—softly, hopelessly, the laugh of a woman who has just understood a joke told at her expense across decades. "Not dead. *Unfinished.* Oh, I am a fool. I saw a choir in my visions and I thought it meant us, the bearers, the ones I could name. Four voices, five. But a choir is not four voices. A choir is however many the song requires." She turned her blind face toward Astrid, and though she could not see him, he felt seen more thoroughly than he had ever been in his life. "There is a voice missing. A voice none of us can sing, because none of us are shaped like it. The stone knows its own keystone, child. It has been waiting for him since before he was born."

The cold that went through Astrid then had nothing to do with the mountain night.

*Keystone.* Maren's word, spoken once, in the last conversation they ever had, on the worst night of his life. *Your sight isn't a talent, boy, it's a hinge. You're not a bearer. Bearers carry the covenant. You* are *it.* He had called it madness. He had called it a cage dressed in glory. He had packed his bundle in the dark and slipped out the window while she slept, and he had spent six years telling himself the flight was freedom.

Tam was looking at him too now. So was Sylwen. Even Wren, wrapped in the cloak against Sylwen's side, had lifted her head, her winter-pond eyes reflecting the ember-light in the seam.

"Astrid," Sylwen said carefully. "What aren't you telling us?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. The truth sat in his chest like a coal swallowed whole, burning through everything soft it touched. If he spoke it, there was no taking it back. If he spoke it, then the flight, the hiding, the burned memory-charm, the neighbor's daughter dragged away at Millbrook Cross while he stood silent—all of it resolved into a single shape, and the shape was cowardice, and he would have to live inside that knowledge for whatever remained of his life.

And if he did not speak it—if he kept his secret one day longer, one hour longer—the stone would go on refusing, and Drevane's engine would go on turning, and the Emberlands would go on dying, and Sylwen with them. He watched her in the failing light, this woman who had crossed a hundred years of exile to stand here, and he understood that he had already decided. He had probably decided the moment he saw the seam of light and felt the warmth of the singing seat. Everything since had been the long fall of a man stepping off a ledge.

"My teacher was a hedge-witch named Maren," he said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "She raised me after my mother died. And the last thing she ever told me, the night I ran, was that my ember-sight wasn't a gift at all. It was a birthright. That I wasn't born to *carry* the covenant." He looked up at the sundered stone, at the trembling threads of light reaching for each other across the break, and finished the sentence he had been running from for six years.

"I was born to *be* it."

The seam flared.

It was not dramatic, at first—it was like a fire fed one dry twig. The ember-light in the gap brightened, steadied, and turned, all of it together, toward him, the way flowers turn toward sun. The warmth of the shelf gathered around him, entered him, recognized him, and Astrid felt his own gift answer from its deepest roots, rising through him like water finding its level, and the stone *sang*—one low, vast note, felt in the teeth and the breastbone rather than heard—and every hair on his body stood on end.

Then, from the darkness below the shelf, from the alder-hollows where they had left the horses, a horn sounded.

Long. Cold. Deliberately unhurried.

Sylwen was on her feet before the echo died, her disguise burning away from her like morning mist, her true form blazing through—taller, luminous, crowned in a corona of dim gold. "Riders," she said. "Many. Coming up the valley road."

"They followed us," Tam breathed. "How—"

"It doesn't matter." Astrid's heart was hammering, but strangely, beneath the fear, he felt calm—clearer than he had felt since Millbrook Cross. The stone's note still hummed in his bones, and it had told him something in that single tone, something simple and enormous: *there is a shape that fits, and it is yours, and it will cost everything.* He turned to the others. "Drevane doesn't need to catch us. He only needs to keep us here until his engine's ready. Which means we have until dawn—maybe less—to learn what Maren tried to teach me six years ago."

Ivetta's blind face turned up to him, radiant with dread and certainty in equal measure. "And what is that, child?"

"How to stop running," Astrid said, "and start singing."
