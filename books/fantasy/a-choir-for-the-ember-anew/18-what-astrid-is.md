## 18. What Astrid Is

The waystation smelled of pine smoke and old stone, and for the first time since the White Pass, Astrid slept without dreaming of fire.

He woke to grey light leaking through the shutter slats and the sound of Ivetta's voice — not speaking to anyone, but speaking anyway, the way she did when a vision moved through her like weather. She sat by the hearth with her hands open in her lap, palms up, as though catching something that fell from the air only she could see.

"You're awake," she said, without turning her head. "Good. It's been circling you all night like a moth round a lamp. I'd rather it land while the fire's still warm."

Astrid pushed himself up on his bedroll. His ribs still ached from Greywater Ford, and the memory of chains at his wrists lived in his skin even now, hours after Sylwen had broken them. Across the room, Corvin was mending a net that had no business being in the mountains, and Tam hunched over a scrap of parchment, pretending to study warding diagrams he already knew by heart. Sylwen was gone — out walking the perimeter, or standing somewhere in the trees listening to whatever the Emberlands whispered to its exiles. The choir, minus one voice.

"What's been circling me?" he asked.

Ivetta turned. Her eyes were white as river ice and had been since birth, but when she looked at him he always felt *seen* in a way that made his body want to find a door.

"Sit down, ember-boy. And don't argue. Arguing makes it worse."

He sat. He had learned, over the weeks of their strange fellowship, that Ivetta's visions were not commands but tides — you could stand against them, but you would get wet either way.

She reached out and took his hands. Her grip was stronger than it looked, calloused from decades of rope-work before blindness took her sight and left her something else instead. Her thumbs pressed into his palms, into the lines there, and Astrid felt a faint warmth kindle under her touch — the old familiar heat of his gift stirring toward another bearer like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"Oh," Ivetta said softly. "Oh, you poor fool. You've been carrying this your whole life and calling it a burden."

"Ivetta—"

"Hush." Her fingers tightened. "I'm going to show you what I saw last night, and then you're going to be angry, and then you're going to grieve, and then — if the gods are kind and Drevane is slow — you're going to understand. Not necessarily in that order."

* * *

It came into him the way her visions always came: not as pictures but as *knowing*, knowledge arriving whole, the way you know the shape of your own mouth.

He saw Maren's cottage — not as he remembered it, warm and crooked and smelling of rosemary and tallow, but as it had been twenty-six years ago, before he was born. He saw Maren younger than he had ever known her, dark-haired, fierce, kneeling in the snow outside a covenant-stone half its proper size, its light guttering like a candle in wind. He saw her weep over it. He saw her make a bargain with someone whose face was folded in shadow — a figure tall and slight, with light beneath the skin, and though the vision would not give Astrid the face, he knew the posture of those shoulders. He had watched them across a hundred campfires. Sylwen.

No. Not quite Sylwen. Older. A sister, perhaps. Or Sylwen herself, long ago, before exile wore her down to human proportions.

The knowing poured through him faster now, relentless:

*Maren was a bearer. One of the last. When the covenant-stone began to fail — when the first cracks spread through it like frost through glass — the Emberlands reached into the human world for a keystone. Not a bearer. Bearers sing the covenant; they keep it alive the way singers keep a song alive. But a keystone is different. A keystone is *part of the arch*. The stone needed someone born of both lineages, woven from both worlds, so that the reforged covenant could never again belong to one realm alone.*

*Maren could not be that person. So she went to the Emberlands' edge, and she asked, and the fae — desperate, dying, generous in the terrible way of the drowning — answered.*

*Astrid Eastwick was not an accident. He was an answer.*

The vision released him and he came back to himself gasping, on his knees somehow, the hearth-smoke sharp in his throat. Ivetta held his hands still, steady as mooring posts.

"No," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "No, that's — Maren found me. She told me that. She said my mother left me on her step during the hard winter, that she never knew who—"

"She lied to you," Ivetta said, gentle and merciless. "Or she told you the truth badly, which amounts to the same thing when you're young and frightened. Think, Astrid. Think about what she actually said, all those nights by her fire, when you weren't listening because you thought she was just an old woman talking."

And he did think, and the memories rearranged themselves around the new knowledge like bones setting.

Maren saying: *Your blood runs older than the crown, child, older than the borders.* He had taken it for hedge-witch poetry. Maren saying: *One day you'll have to choose what you are, and it will cost you everything either way, so choose with your eyes open.* He had thought she meant the choice between hiding and burning. Maren, the night he fled, gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise, saying: *If you run from this, you're not running to freedom. You're running in a circle. The road bends back, Astrid. It always bends back.*

"The thing I ran from," he said slowly, tasting each word like a coin he suspected was false, "was never a destiny. It was a *description*. She wasn't asking me to do anything. She was trying to tell me what I already was."

"Yes."

"And I called it a cage and I climbed out the window."

"Yes," Ivetta said again, softer. "You were nineteen and you wanted your life to be yours. No one blames you. Well — Maren might have, briefly, before she died wondering if she'd driven you away. But no one who matters."

Astrid laughed, or something near it — a cracked, airless sound. He sat back against the cold wall of the waystation and looked at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. They looked the same. Knuckles scarred from rope-burn, nails bitten, the faint old burn-mark on the left palm where he'd grabbed a kettle too young. Smuggler's hands. Apprentice's hands. Nothing about them said *keystone*, nothing about them said *born of two worlds*.

Except.

He let his ember-sight rise — carefully, the way he'd learned to do it under Drevane's eye, a candle-flame lifted behind his ribs. The world bloomed into its second layer: Corvin a deep brown-gold, river-clay and lamp-oil; Tam a flickering uncertain violet; Ivetta a white so dense it was nearly silver, a frozen star. And himself—

He had never dared look at himself directly. Sight was for other things; looking at your own light was vanity, Maren had said, and worse, it invited things to look back.

He looked now.

Where every bearer he had ever seen burned in a single color, Astrid burned in two, braided. Human gold wound through with fae-silver, thread over thread, and where the threads crossed they made something neither color alone could make — a warm, living amber, the exact color of embers banked for morning. The color the covenant-stone had burned in Corvin's story. The color of the old woodcuts in Maren's books, the ones she'd kept wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a chest, the ones she'd told him were relics of a dead age.

"It's true," he whispered. "Gods help me, it's true."

Corvin had stopped pretending to mend his net. Tam had set down his parchment. Neither spoke. There are moments when silence is the only respectful language, and they knew this one.

* * *

Sylwen returned at midday and read the room in a single glance — the way they were arranged around Astrid, the way he sat apart, the rawness in his face.

"You showed him," she said to Ivetta. Not accusatory. Weary.

"He was going to learn eventually. Better from a friend than from the stone itself, when it refuses us all tomorrow and he understands why."

"Understands why—" Astrid stood. "What do you mean, refuses?"

Ivetta sighed. Sylwen crossed the room and lowered herself onto the bench opposite him, folding her long frame down with that careful economy she used when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed.

"When we reach the sundered stone," Sylwen said, "the choir will try to reforge it. That is the whole of our plan, such as it is. Four bearers singing the old harmony, offering what we have. And it will not work, Astrid. I have known it would not work since the night Ivetta first sang with us and I felt what was missing."

"Then the whole plan is—"

"A decoy," Tam said quietly, from his corner. "A beautiful, doomed decoy. Sing loud enough to draw Drevane's army, buy time, die well." He gave a thin smile. "I've written worse endings."

"No." Astrid's heart was hammering now, anger rising through the grief like fire through green wood. "No, you don't get to decide that my sacrifice is decorative. If the stone needs a keystone—"

"It doesn't need *a* keystone," Ivetta said. "It needs *the* keystone. And here is the cruelty of it, ember-boy, so listen closely. The stone will refuse every one of us. Not because our gifts are small — mine has carried three generations of seeing, Corvin's has held a river's memory for sixty years — but because gifts are not enough. The covenant broke because it was built to serve two realms and guarded by one. It needs a foundation that belongs wholly to neither and wholly to both. It needs someone who *is* the bridge, not someone who carries one."

"And that's me." His voice came out flat. "That's the great secret. Maren's secret. The thing worth running from."

"That's you," Sylwen said.

"Why didn't you tell me?" He rounded on her, and the ember-light flared behind his ribs, and he saw her flinch — actually flinch, the fae exile who had killed two witch-hunters without blinking. "You knew. All this time, on the road, at the fires — you knew what I was and you let me believe I was just another bearer, just another voice in the choir. Why?"

"Because I was a coward," Sylwen said, "and because I loved you, and because those two things refused to be separated."

The words landed in the room like dropped glass.

"I was there," she went on, and her voice stayed level the way a rope stays level over a gorge — tension, nothing else. "At the bargain. I was there when your mother — when Maren knelt in the snow and asked the Emberlands for a miracle it had no right to grant. My sister Vaelith made that bargain; I stood witness; I argued *against* it, because I believed then that no good comes of weaving mortal lives into immortal architecture. I was wrong, and my wrongness is part of why the covenant fell the way it did — unguarded, unloved, unclaimed. So when I found you in the White Pass, half-frozen, hunted, hiding a gift you clearly hated — I recognized you. Of course I recognized you. And I said nothing, because telling you what you are felt like chaining you to the very destiny you'd fled. I told myself you deserved to arrive at it freely."

"And instead I arrived at it chained to Drevane's bargain, with a child's death on my conscience and an army marching on us."

"Yes," Sylwen said simply. "My cowardice bought exactly that. I won't dress it up."

Astrid turned away. He walked to the window slit and put his hand against the cold stone and breathed, and outside the mountains went on being mountains, indifferent and ancient, and somewhere beyond them the Emberlands were dimming like coals under ash, and somewhere south of them an army was moving, and none of it cared about the small catastrophe happening inside one man's chest.

Because that's what it was. Not revelation. Catastrophe. Every story he had told himself about himself was ash. The runaway apprentice — a boy fleeing a truth, not a fate. The hidden gift — not a curse to conceal but a cornerstone to deliver. Even his flight from Maren's cottage, the one act he had always counted as his own, the one clean rebellion of his life — even that bent back on itself, because Maren had known he would run, had perhaps *counted* on it, had known that a keystone forced into place crumbles and a keystone that chooses—

*Choose with your eyes open,* she had said.

"Oh," he said aloud, very quietly. "Oh, you clever, cruel old woman. You let me run because you knew I'd have to come back on my own feet or not at all."

* * *

Evening came. Tam cooked — he was a bad cook and insisted on doing it anyway, some penance of his own devising — and they ate in a silence that was not uncomfortable, exactly, but was full, like a held breath. Afterward Corvin took out a battered wooden flute and played three notes of the old harmony and stopped, as he always did, because four voices were needed and three was just longing.

Astrid sat apart, turning it all over, the way he'd once turned smuggled goods over by lantern-light, checking for cracks, for telltales, for the mark that revealed what a thing truly was.

Here was what he found, when he checked honestly:

He had spent his whole life believing his gift made him a target. The hunt had proven it. But the hunt was the *small* truth. The large truth was stranger and heavier: his gift made him necessary. Not wanted — necessary, the way a keystone is necessary, the way a bridge is necessary, and nobody asks a bridge whether it finds its work fulfilling.

And yet.

He thought of the burned village, and the girl, Wren, asleep now in the loft above with Corvin's coat for a blanket. He thought of her hand trusting his on the old roads, of how smuggling skills turned to sanctuary had felt like the first honest day's work of his life. He thought of Sylwen's face when she'd said *because I loved you* and hadn't dressed it up. He thought of the amber light braided through his veins, two colors making a third that neither world contained alone.

Maren had told him once, in one of her moods of terrible gentleness: *The difference between a burden and a gift is rarely the thing itself, child. It's whether you carry it alone.*

He had run because he thought the destiny was solitary — one marked boy, one pyre or one pedestal, alone either way. But it had never been solitary. That was the whole design. A keystone is nothing without the arch. An arch is nothing without the keystone. The covenant had failed not because it lacked power but because it lacked *belonging* — and he, bastard of two lineages, orphan of two worlds, smuggler of two roads, was belonging made flesh.

"I'll do it," he said.

They all looked up. Corvin's flute went silent against his knee.

"Not because it's my destiny. I've had enough of destinies — they're just other people's plans wearing holy clothes." He stood, and let the ember-sight rise openly for the first time among them, the braid of gold and silver lighting the room like a hearth waking. "But because I choose it. Because Wren gets to grow up in a world with rivers that remember and lights that don't go out. Because Sylwen goes home. Because Tam's ending hasn't been written yet. Because Maren lied to me for twenty years and loved me for twenty years and those are the same act, and I finally understand that."

Ivetta smiled — a rare thing, like sun through ice.

"There's one condition," Astrid said.

Sylwen raised an eyebrow. "Keystones don't usually negotiate."

"This one does. When we reach the stone — when I give it my gift — I want it understood that I'm not surrendering anything. I'm *spending* it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and if the stone wants my ember-sight it can damn well take it as an offering or not at all."

For a moment no one spoke. Then Corvin laughed, a great rolling river-stone of a laugh, and said, "Aye, that sounds like Maren's boy, right enough."

Outside, far to the south, the sky flickered — once, twice — a wrongness in the stars that every one of them saw and none of them named. The harvesting engine, waking. Somewhere out there, Drevane's patience had finally run out, and torchlight was climbing the mountain roads like a tide of orange ants.

Sylwen rose, and her eyes had begun to glow faintly, the exile's disguise thinning as the end approached.

"Rest," she said. "Tomorrow we walk to the Sundered Stone. And Astrid—" she paused at the door, silhouetted against the failing stars, "—tomorrow, the whole of both worlds learns what you are. Are you ready to be seen?"

Astrid looked at his hands, at the amber light resting in them like a bird that had finally come home.

"No," he said. "Let's go anyway."
