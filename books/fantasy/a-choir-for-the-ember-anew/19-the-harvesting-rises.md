## 19. The Harvesting Rises

The engine woke at dawn.

Lord-Captain Drevane stood on the observation gallery of the Deepworks, hands clasped behind his back, and watched the thing breathe. It filled the great cavern beneath the mountain like a heart of black iron and stolen light — covenant-stones torn from their resting places across three kingdoms and mortared into a lattice the size of a cathedral, each one wrapped in silver wire and rune-chains, all of it pulsing now with a slow, greedy rhythm. Draw. Hold. Draw again.

Around him the artificers of the Regent's faction moved through the gantries in their ash-grey robes, checking gauges, whispering calculations, avoiding his eyes. They had worked for eleven years to reach this morning. Most of them could not look at what they had built.

"Increase the draw," said Inquisitor Hale, from her place at the master console. Her voice was dry as old parchment, satisfied as a ledger balanced. "Phase two. Full saturation."

Drevane did not answer immediately. He was watching the first of the covenant-stones — the one they had pulled from the ford at Greywater, the one that had sung when the chains went around it. It had sung for six days before it fell silent. Now its light poured out of it in a thin silver stream, feeding into the engine's throat, and where the stream touched the lattice the whole structure brightened like a furnace door swinging open.

"Captain?" Hale prompted.

"I heard you." He turned. "Do you understand what we are about to do, Inquisitor?"

She smiled without warmth. "Save the realm."

"The realm," Drevane repeated. He looked back down at the engine. Somewhere beneath his ribs, in the place where he kept the things he did not say aloud, something stirred uneasily. He had burned villages. He had hanged bearers of the old blood from their own doorposts when they would not register. He had done it all with a clear conscience, because the alternative — the blight creeping up from the south, the wheat coming up grey, the wells failing one by one — demanded hard men doing hard arithmetic. Every life spent against the Emberlands' hoard was an investment. He had believed that for eleven years.

But he had not expected the stones to sing.

"Full saturation," he said at last. "Proceed."

Hale pulled the master lever.

The sound that rose through the Deepworks was not mechanical. It was a chord — vast, low, wrong — as if every bell in the world had been struck at once and then bent out of true. The cavern lights flickered. Dust sifted from the ceiling. And along the walls, carved into the living rock by masons who had never known why they were told to carve those particular spirals, the old glyphs began to glow: faintly at first, then brighter, then with a hunger that made the nearest artificers stumble back and cross themselves.

Above them, through a mile of stone, the mountain began to hum.

* * *

Astrid felt it first in his teeth.

He was standing on the ridge above the sundered stone with Sylwen beside him, watching the dawn come grey over the Thornfells, when the world changed key. There was no other way to describe it. For twenty-three years the land had had a music underneath it — faint, mostly inaudible, but always there, the way a house has a sound of itself that you only notice when it stops. His ember-sight had lived in that music the way a fish lives in water.

Now the music was being drained.

"Oh," he said. The word came out of him small and helpless. "Oh, no."

"Astrid?" Sylwen's hand found his arm. "What is it? What do you see?"

He didn't have words for it yet, so he let the sight speak. He opened himself to it the way Maren had taught him, breathing slow, letting his vision go soft — and the mountainside lit up around him like a bonfire seen through smoked glass. Every root and river and buried vein of ember-light in the land showed to him as threads of gold and green, woven through the earth like the warp of some unimaginable loom.

And the threads were moving. All of them. Streaming southward, streaming toward the capital, streaming toward the black chord he could feel hammering under the horizon like a second heartbeat imposed upon the world's. Where the threads passed, the gold dimmed. Where the gold dimmed, frost crawled.

"It's started," he whispered. "They've lit the engine. Sylwen — I can see it pulling. Everything's pulling toward it. The rivers, the deep roots, the—" He stopped. Turned. Looked at her properly for the first time since dawn.

Sylwen was fading.

It was subtle — her skin had always held a light of its own, the pale green-gold of willow leaves backlit by sun, and now that light was guttering. The fine tracery of patterns along her jaw and throat, patterns that shifted when she laughed, had gone still and dull. She caught him looking and lifted her chin in the old defiant way, but her hand trembled against his sleeve.

"How long," he said, "have you been feeling this?"

"Since midnight." She tried for a smile and nearly managed it. "It is nothing. A chill."

"Sylwen."

"Do not say my name like a funeral bell, Astrid Eastwick." But her voice cracked on the last word, and she turned away from him, toward the broken covenant-stone in the valley below — the great sundered mass of it, split down its heart like a felled tree, its fractured faces dark for the first time in a thousand years. "The engine draws from the Emberlands first. We are closest to the fire, so we burn first. When the fae light is gone, it will begin on yours. The blight will come faster than any of them dreamed, because they are hastening it themselves, the fools, the proud blind fools—"

Her knees buckled.

He caught her before she hit the ground. She weighed almost nothing — less than she should, less than she had a week ago — and when he eased her down against a boulder her eyes were wide and frightened, and for once she did not hide it.

"The choir," she managed. "Get the choir singing. Even unsundered, even imperfect — song slows the drain, it tangles the threads, it buys us hours. Perhaps a day. Go, Astrid. Go."

He ran.

* * *

In the waystation beneath the hill, Ivetta was already awake.

The blind seer sat with her back to the hearthstone, hands flat on the warm rock, her clouded eyes aimed at nothing and everything. Corvin crouched beside her, his ferryman's hands useless around a cup of tea he'd forgotten to drink. Tam paced. He had been pacing since midnight, when he'd felt the engine's first pull through the covenant-stone shard he kept in his pocket, and now he stopped mid-stride as Astrid came down the ladder three steps at a time.

"We know," Ivetta said, before he could speak. "Child, we've known since the small hours. Sit down. You look like a man about to set fire to himself."

"The engine's live. Sylwen's fading. Ivetta, she can barely stand—"

"Then we sing." The old woman's voice was iron. "Corvin, your verse. Tam, the counterline. Astrid, you hold the drone — your gift can carry what our voices cannot. We are not reforging anything today. We are keeping the patient alive until the surgery. Sing."

And they sang.

It should not have worked. Four voices in a dugout under a hill, two of them old, one of them half-broken with guilt, one of them running on no sleep — against an engine built by the finest artificers of a desperate court. But the choir had never been about power. That was what Maren had tried to tell him, years ago, in a kitchen that smelled of bread and herbs, and what he had been too young and too afraid to hear: the covenant was not a spell. It was a relationship. It was two peoples agreeing, in harmony, to share one fire.

Their voices found each other the way they had in the first gathering weeks ago — Corvin's low rolling bass like a river under ice, Tam's trained tenor threading above it, Ivetta's cracked alto cutting through both like a blade of morning light. Astrid closed his eyes and let his gift rise, and instead of sight he gave sound: a sustained note below hearing, drawn up out of the ember-threads still clinging to the hillside, a drone like the land itself humming its own name.

The hearthstone flared. Frost retreated from the doorway. Far above, he felt the drain stutter — the silver streams thickening, slowing, tangling in the harmonics like wool caught on brambles.

"It's working," Tam breathed between phrases. "It's actually—"

Then the engine adjusted.

The chord beneath the horizon bent, shifted, found their frequency — and pushed. Astrid felt it like a hand closing over his mouth. The drone tore. The hearthstone dimmed. Blood ran warm from Ivetta's nose, and Corvin caught her as she swayed.

"Hold!" Tam shouted, hauling the melody back up. "Hold the line, hold—"

They held. Barely. It cost everything they had, and Astrid understood, with cold clarity, what Ivetta's metaphor meant: they were not winning. They were buying hours. Maybe a day. Maybe less.

When the song finally gentled into something sustainable — a quieter weaving, a holding pattern — he knelt by Ivetta and wiped the blood from her chin with his sleeve.

"How long?"

She reached up and found his face with fingers gone thin as winter twigs. "Long enough," she said, "if you run fast enough."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean the army marched at first light, child. Twelve hundred pikes and the witch-hunters' van and Hale's artificers with their portable siphons. They are coming here, to this stone, because the last bearer-light in the north is gathered on this hill like moths around a lamp." Her ruined eyes seemed, for a moment, to fix on him with terrible precision. "You have until they crest the pass. One day, if the song holds. Less if it breaks."

"And the reforging? The stone refused everyone. Each offer, refused—"

"I know what the stone refused." Something moved behind her blindness — sorrow, or certainty, or both. "I have seen it, Astrid. I have seen what the stone is waiting for. But that truth is not mine to give, and today there is no time for gentle revelations. Today there is only this: the army comes, the engine feeds, and somewhere on that road a man named Drevane is riding at the head of it, believing he is saving the world." Her grip tightened on his wrist. "Go to Sylwen. Stay with her while she fades. Whatever happens next — whatever the stone asks — do not run from it. Not this time."

His blood went cold. "Ivetta, what does the stone ask?"

But she had already turned back to the hearthstone, already begun the low humming that meant she had withdrawn into whatever country the blind travel, and would say no more.

* * *

Drevane rode at the head of the column and did not enjoy the view.

The Thornfell passes were beautiful in the way of all places designed for ambush — narrow, high, hung with pine shadow — and twelve hundred men moved through them slowly, kicking loose scree, breath steaming. Behind him the baggage train carried the siphon-lances and the portable containment rings; ahead, his outriders reported the covenant-site unguarded, or seemingly so, which in his experience meant guarded by something worth the seeming.

He had wanted to bring the apprentice. Hale had argued for leaving Astrid caged in the Deepworks — too much risk, the boy had already misled them once at the ferry crossing, and everyone knew it, whatever polite fiction they maintained. But Drevane had overridden her, and then, in the end, agreed with her, and the contradiction sat in him uncomfortably. The truth was simpler and worse: he had not wanted Astrid to see this. Not the army, not the siphons, not the moment the last free bearers were taken and the last wild magic in the world was fed into the machine. Some part of him — the part that had once been a boy in a fishing village before the blight took the fish and his father's boat and two of his brothers — that part had not wanted the boy to watch.

Because the boy had looked at him, in the cells at Hollowmere, with something that was not hatred. It had been worse. It had been recognition. As if he'd seen exactly what Drevane was and what he'd paid to become it.

"My lord." An outrider reined in beside him, saluting. "Scouts report movement at the site. Small numbers. Four, perhaps five individuals. And—" the man hesitated "—lights, sir. On the broken stone. Faint, but they're there. After everything the engine's drawn, there are still lights."

Of course there are, Drevane thought. That is rather the entire point of them.

"Form the line at the mouth of the pass," he said. "No one fires without my word. If the bearers surrender, they are taken alive and gently. Am I understood? Gently. I will hang the man who puts a mark on a covenant-bearer."

The outrider blinked. "Sir — with respect, the orders from the Regent's office specify—"

"I know what the orders specify." Drevane's voice did not rise, but the temperature of the conversation dropped ten degrees. "I wrote the original drafts of those orders. The revision stands. Gently."

The rider saluted again and galloped off. Hale, riding at Drevane's left in her ash-grey traveling cloak, regarded him with her ledger-balanced smile.

"You're going soft, Captain."

"I'm going efficient, Inquisitor. Dead bearers sing no songs, and we have learned — painfully — that songs matter." He nodded toward the southern sky, where a smudge of wrongness sat on the horizon that no weather explained: the visible shimmer of the drain, the land's light bleeding away toward the capital. "We take the site intact because the stone may yet be needed. The engine eats the wild light. But the Regent's own artificers admit the harvested power degrades. What we take is less than what was there." He paused. "Unless the covenant itself can be made to work for us rather than against us. Which requires bearers. Living ones."

Hale studied him for a long moment. "And if the boy is up there? With them?"

"Then," Drevane said quietly, "we take him too. Gently."

He spurred forward, and the column followed, twelve hundred strong, up toward the pass and the broken stone and the last lights of a dying world — while behind them, far to the south, the rivers ran silver and the fae lights went out one by one, like windows darkening down a long street at the end of an age.

* * *

Sylwen lay on the hillside above the stone and watched the sun set through herself.

It was the strangest sensation of her long strange life: the light of it passed through her hands as though they were made of autumn air. She could still feel the ground, still feel Astrid sitting close beside her with her fingers gripped in his — but distantly, as though both were happening to someone she remembered fondly.

"Do not make that face," she said. "The one you're making. Like a boy whose dog has run off."

"You're dying."

"I am fading. It is not the same thing. Dying has an end; fading merely... continues." She laughed, and the laugh came out thin as wind through reeds. "My mother warned me, you know. When I chose exile. She said: the covenant is our root, little branch, and a branch cut from its root lives on borrowed sap. I thought she meant it as poetry. Mothers usually mean it as weather forecast."

Astrid's grip tightened. Above them, from the waystation, the choir's holding-song drifted down — quieter now, rationed, a candle burned in halves. Against the last light, the sundered stone stood like a tombstone for a god, and Sylwen thought: a thousand years I kept faith with a broken promise, and this is where the account comes due.

"There is something I never told you," she said. "About the night the covenant broke. About why the exile was truly given."

"Sylwen—"

"Hear me. I may not have another dusk to say it." She turned her head, and even faded, even translucent, her eyes held their old green fire. "They tell you I broke the law by loving a human, and that is true, but it is not the crime. The crime was this: when the Ember Council came to seal the borders — when they voted to let the covenant rot, to withdraw, to save the Emberlands by abandoning yours — I was the warden of the gate. My post. My watch. And I was not at my post, Astrid. I was across the border, in a woodcutter's hut, holding a human man's hand while he died of fever. The border sealed at moonrise. I was due at my stone at moonset. And in that one night between, the last anchor was left unguarded, and the enemies of the covenant struck it, and it broke, and by the time I returned there was nothing left to ward." Her voice frayed. "Your blight. Your hungry years. The hunt, the pyres, Maren's warnings, your whole life in hiding — the taproot of all of it runs through the night I was absent. I am not a victim of the covenant's breaking, Astrid. I am its cause."

The words fell into the silence like stones into a well.

Astrid was quiet for a long time. Then: "Maren told me something once. The night I ran. I've never told anyone either." He looked down at their joined hands — his solid, hers barely there. "She said I wasn't just a bearer. She said the word 'keystone.' And I ran because I thought she meant I'd be used up. Sacrificed. That the covenant would spend me like a coin." His voice shook. "I think I'm done running, Sylwen. I think I've been done since Greywater Ford. So whatever the stone asks when the army comes — whatever Ivetta saw — I need you to believe me: it won't be sacrifice. I finally understand that much. It'll be an offering. There's a difference. She tried to teach me the difference for ten years and I was too stupid and too scared to learn it."

Sylwen looked up at him — at this runaway apprentice, this smuggler of contraband and sanctuary, this boy born of both lineages whether he yet knew the full shape of it or not — and she felt the borrowed sap in her veins stir one final time.

"Then listen well," she whispered, "because the drums have started."

And they had. Down in the valley, carried on the evening wind from the mouth of the pass: the beat of twelve hundred marching feet, and beneath it, growing louder, rising through the earth itself, the black chord of the engine bending toward the broken stone — hungry, patient, certain —

— and on the stone's sundered face, for the first time in a thousand years, a single thread of gold light kindled, and waited.
