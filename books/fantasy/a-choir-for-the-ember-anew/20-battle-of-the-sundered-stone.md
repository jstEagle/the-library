## 20. Battle of the Sundered Stone

The dawn came up grey and wrong, the light itself thin as watered milk, and Astrid knew before he opened his eyes that the engine had been lit.

He felt it in his teeth. He felt it in the old scar on his palm where Maren's needle had once drawn blood for a lesson he'd slept through — a low, grinding pull, like a hook set behind his ribs and hauled taut by hands he could not see. The ember-sight, even caged as it was inside him now, even half-drowned in his own fear, turned toward the pain the way a compass turns north.

*There,* it said. *That is where they are killing the world.*

He stood at the lip of the hollow above the sundered stone, breath steaming, and looked down into the ruin that had been a holy place when the world was younger. The covenant-stone lay in its ring of broken satellites like a felled giant, split down its heart, the two halves leaning apart with a gap between them wide enough to drive a cart through. Frost crawled over everything. Not the clean frost of winter but a grey creeping blight-rime, eating the lichen, dulling the granite, drinking what little warmth the dawn offered. Beyond the ruin, down the long valley, the river ran silver — not bright silver but dead mercury-silver, the color of coin pulled from a miser's fist, and where it touched the banks nothing grew.

And beyond *that*, filling the valley mouth like a tide of iron filings, came the crown's army.

Astrid counted banners out of habit, the smuggler's arithmetic of threat: three hundred pikes at least, two companies of crossbow, the witch-hunters' black-and-white at the center around something vast and wagon-borne under oilcloth. The harvesting engine. Even shrouded, he could see the stolen covenant-stones set into its frame, could feel them *screaming* — faintly, horribly, the way a man screams through clenched teeth. They had been part of the ring here once. Part of the song.

"They're early," said Ivetta beside him. The blind seer's face was tilted to the sky she could not see, her milky eyes full of reflected dawn. "Or we are late. It amounts to the same thing." She had braided her hair with rowan and iron-free silver thread, old woman's magic, hedge-magic, the small stubborn craft of people who had never stopped believing the Emberlands were worth singing to. "Can you feel them listening?"

"Who?"

"The fae. On the far side of the veil." Her hand found his wrist, grip surprisingly strong. "When the covenant lived, every bearer's death was heard there. Every birth too. A whole country holding its breath for us." She smiled, and it was a terrible, beautiful smile. "They are holding their breath now, child. Don't make them exhale."

Below, in the shadow of the great stone's western half, Corvin was checking the ferry-chains he'd spent three days rigging across the ruined approach — old river-craft rope-work repurposed, snare-lines and trip-weights, a smuggler's welcome laid out for soldiers. He moved slowly this morning. Astrid had watched him move slowly for a week now and told himself it was age. This morning he could no longer lie to himself about it: the old ferryman was dying by inches, some deep-water cold in his lungs that no fire would cure, and he knew it, and he had chosen to spend his last coins here.

"You could be in the trees," Astrid said, dropping down beside him. "Ivetta can sing the warding without you. We only need your voice at the stone."

Corvin grinned through his grey beard. "Boy, I've poled a ferry through flood-ice on the Greywater. You think I'll sit in a tree while the last song of my life gets sung without me?" He tested a knot, grunted approval. "Besides. Someone has to teach these proud bastards what a toll road costs."

* * *

Sylwen saw the army from the ridge, and for the first time since her exile began, she did not hide what she was.

She stood in the open wind with her glamour folded away like a put-away garment, and the morning saw her: tall past human measure, skin like birch and moonstone, hair the color of banked coals lifting in a wind that touched no one else, eyes the green-gold of the deep Emberlands where the oldest lights still burned. Along the ridgeline, the few remaining defenders — smugglers who owed Astrid, hedge-folk who owed Ivetta, one disaffected sergeant with eleven deserters who had decided the Regent's blight was a lie worth dying against — went quiet, then bowed their heads, not in worship but in recognition. *So it is true,* their faces said. *It was all true.*

She let them look. Then she looked south, along the valley, to where the engine waited under its oilcloth, and her heart did what fae hearts do, which is break slowly and beautifully and without any hope of mending cheap.

*Astrid is down there.* Her human, her impossible, her guilt-made-flesh and forgiveness-made-man. He would go to the stone. Of course he would. He had run from his destiny across half a kingdom and it had caught him anyway, the way all true things catch runners eventually, and now he would walk into an army to give away the only thing he had ever owned outright.

She loved him for it. She hated him for it. Both were useless this morning, so she set them down like heavy packs and picked up her blades instead.

"Tinker," said the sergeant, because that was the name he knew.

"Sergeant." Her voice carried the resonance of a struck bell; men flinched and steadied. "When they come — and they will come in waves, because Drevane does not waste lives but he will spend yours gladly — you hold the treeline until the singing starts. When the singing starts, you fall back to the stone and you *sing*. Badly. Loudly. It doesn't matter if you know the words. The covenant was never about knowing the words." She showed her teeth, which were not entirely a human shape. "It was about meaning them."

* * *

Drevane watched the ruin through his far-glass and felt, for the first time in the campaign, something adjacent to respect.

The smuggler had done more with forty bodies than most captains did with four hundred. Chains across the approaches. Charms woven into the bracken — he could see their heat-shimmer from here, hedge-work, amateurish, but layered with a patience that spoke of real gift. And at the center of it all, the broken stone, and somewhere near it, the boy himself, standing in the open like a man waiting for a coach he knows is coming.

Lord-Captain Drevane had never wanted this war. That was the truth he kept locked in the strongbox behind his breastbone, the one he opened only at night: he had wanted to be an engineer, once, before the succession wars took his family's fortune and gave him instead to the crown. He understood machines. He understood that the machine behind him — the harvesting engine, the great theft dressed as salvation — was a machine built by frightened men to eat the future, and that machines which eat the future always, *always*, turn on their builders.

But the Regent had his family's names on a list, and the blight was coming regardless, and a man served the realm he had, not the realm he wished for.

"Signal the advance," he said. "Pikes first. Let them spend their tricks on steel. The hunters go in when the singing starts — kill the singers, spare the boy. The Regent wants him breathing."

"And the fae woman?" asked Inquisitor Hale, whose voice always sounded like a key turning in a lock.

Drevane lowered the glass. Down in the valley, faint as a candle seen through fog, he could see her now — the exile, unveiled, burning like a green star on the ridgeline. Something in his chest, some drowned and buried thing, stirred and said: *that is what we are killing.*

"Hale," he said quietly, "if the day comes when you must choose between your orders and your soul, I hope for your sake someone taught you which is which." He raised his arm. "Advance."

* * *

They came in waves, exactly as Sylwen had promised, and the treeline ate them.

Astrid fought the way he'd smuggled — sideways, low, using ground nobody else wanted. He had no soldier's discipline, only a lifetime of reading terrain at speed, and he used it: pulling hunters into the chain-lines, dropping trip-weights, shouting warnings in a smuggler's cant that half the defenders understood and all the enemy didn't. Around him the hedge-charms bloomed and burst, little suns of confusion and terror, and through it all he kept moving downhill, keep moving, *always* downhill, toward the stone, because the plan was simple and terrible:

The choir sings. The stone wakes. Astrid gives it everything.

He was fifty yards out when the first arrow took Sylwen through the shoulder, and the sound she made unmade something in him forever.

* * *

Tam felt the engine wake beneath his hands and understood, finally, completely, what he had become.

They had put him at the control-frame because he was useful — the disgraced court mage, the traitor twice over, the man who sold the choir for his family's lives and had learned yesterday, from Hale's own lips, smiling, that the family had been dead for a month. Killed quietly. Filed away. The leverage discarded once the betrayal was banked.

He had vomited. He had wept. And then he had walked back to the engine with his face arranged, because a dead man's grief is the one weapon no one thinks to disarm, and Tam had spent thirty years at court learning exactly how much a man can hide behind an arranged face.

Inside the engine, the stolen covenant-stones sang their agony into the focusing lattice, and the lattice drank the Emberlands through the wound in the world, and Tam's hands moved on the controls with a courtier's smooth competence while his heart recited one word over and over, like a prayer, like a penance: *Astrid, Astrid, Astrid.*

The boy had forgiven him. That was the unbearable part. At the waystation, after the confession, after Ivetta had turned her blind eyes away and Corvin had spat, Astrid had sat down next to him and said, "You did it for love. So did we all. Get up. We've a song to learn."

So Tam fed the lattice a false harmonic — small, deniable, a fraction of a percent of drag — and bought the choir minutes. And began, very quietly, methodically, with the neat precision of a man tidying his affairs, to overload the focusing array.

* * *

The singing started, and the world remembered.

Ivetta's voice rose first, cracked and ancient and absolutely certain, and the words were older than the kingdom, older than the blight, older than the quarrel between realms: the covenant-song, the bridge-song, the song that says *you are not alone over there, and neither are we over here.* Corvin joined, rough as river gravel. The deserters joined badly and loudly, exactly as instructed. And the sundered stone — frost-rimed, blight-eaten, split to its heart — *shivered.*

Light came up out of the gap between the halves like sap rising. Faint. Guttering. But rising.

"It's working," Astrid gasped, running the last stretch, arrows hissing past him, Sylwen limping at his side with her blood smoking green-gold where it fell. "Ivetta, it's—"

"I feel it," she said, and her face was radiant and wrecked. "Now, child. Now, while it listens. Give it—"

The hunters hit the choir like a hammer.

They came out of the smoke in their black-and-white, six of them with witch-irons and blessed chains, and they went for Ivetta first because Drevane's orders were precise and Drevane's orders were good. Astrid turned, ember-light flaring off his hands for the first time in front of enemies, blazing, magnificent, three seconds too late—

—and Corvin stepped into the space between.

The old ferryman took the first witch-iron spike meant for Ivetta full in the chest, and did not fall, and did not stop singing. He took the second one reaching for the hunter's throat with bare hands, an old man's hands, poler's hands, and he dragged the man down with him as his legs finally failed, and the last thing he did in this world was grin up at Astrid with blood on his teeth and say, clear as a bell over the battle:

"Toll's paid, boy. *Cross over.*"

Then the light in him went out, and the song faltered, and Astrid screamed.

* * *

At the engine, Tam closed his eyes and finished his arithmetic.

The array was past saving. He had made sure of that — heat bleeding into the lattice housings, harmonics tearing at each other, the stolen stones inside screaming louder now, frenzied, as if they knew. In ninety seconds the focusing chamber would rupture and take the engine's heart with it, and the harvest would end not in triumph but in a crater of dead magic and dead men.

In sixty seconds, if he stayed at the frame, holding the containment runes open with his own craft so the rupture vented upward and away from the valley — away from the stone, away from the choir, away from Astrid.

Hale was shouting at him. Soldiers were running. None of it mattered. Tam thought of his family, dead a month, and found to his astonishment that his grief had changed shape somewhere along the road: it was no longer a wound. It was a compass. It pointed home, and home was a waystation fire where a boy had said *get up, we've a song to learn.*

"Well," Tam said aloud, to no one, in the accent of his university years, long buried. "Every victory paid for."

He held the runes open. The engine went up around him in a pillar of white fire that cast shadows on the clouds, and the harvesting stopped, and the stolen stones' screaming cut off mid-note like a throat finally allowed to rest.

* * *

Astrid reached the stone through a world gone white and ringing.

Sylwen was down on one knee, shoulder ruined, still singing through her teeth. Ivetta knelt in Corvin's blood and did not stop, could not stop, her voice the only thread holding the waking light to the world. The gap between the stone-halves glowed like a forge-door. It was waiting. It had been waiting for him his entire life.

He put his hands into the gap, into the light, and gave it everything — and the light refused him.

Not enough. Not *him* enough. The stone needed a keystone, and he was not yet what the keystone required, and through the roaring and the dying and the singing, one thought rose in Astrid Eastwick with perfect, terrible clarity:

*I don't know how. Someone has to show me what I am.*

Behind him, out of the smoke where the engine's pyre still stood, came footsteps. Unhurried. Unarmored. Lord-Captain Drevane walked through his own shattered army with his sword sheathed and his far-glass left lying in the mud, and stopped at the edge of the light, and looked at the guttering covenant-stone, and at the boy with his hands full of refusing fire.

"The Regent sent me to bring you back alive," Drevane said. His voice was strange — stripped, almost young. "I find I have no further use for the Regent's instructions." He drew his sword — and drove it point-down into the earth between them, a captain's gesture, a surrender older than crowns. "Tell me what you need, tracker. I commanded the hunt. I know every rite the old records hold. And I think—" his gaze went to the silvered river, to the failing lights on the far side of the veil, to Sylwen burning dim on her knee, "—I think I have been serving the wrong realm all along."

The stone pulsed. The light reached for Astrid again, hungry, incomplete.

And in the space between the halves, where no light had been, something new kindled — a small, patient flame, shaped like a memory of a kitchen, smelling of bread and herbs, wearing the voice of a dead hedge-witch who had once told a frightened boy the truth he ran from:

*"Come and be what you are, love. I'll show you the rest."*
