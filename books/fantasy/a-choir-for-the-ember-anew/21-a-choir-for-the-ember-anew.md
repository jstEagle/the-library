## 21. A Choir for the Ember Anew

The sundered stone stood at the heart of the ruin like a broken tooth in an old jaw.

Astrid had seen it whole only once before — in Maren's scrying bowl, years ago, when he was young enough to believe the world was larger than his fear of it. In the vision it had been a pillar of white granite veined with gold, singing a note too low to hear and too deep to forget. Now it was two leaning halves, each taller than three men, their fractured faces blackened as though lightning had lived inside them for a century. Frost crawled up from the ground around its base in patterns that were not frost at all: they were words, dead letters of a covenant-language no living tongue spoke, frozen mid-sentence on the stone's skin like breath caught in a window.

Behind him, the choir waited.

Corvin was gone. That truth sat in Astrid's chest like a swallowed coal. The old ferryman had held the eastern rise against Drevane's crossbows for eleven minutes — eleven minutes that had bought Astrid the open ground between the army's line and the stone — and when they pulled him back behind the ruined wall, there was more river in him than blood. He had died with his hand in Ivetta's, humming the first bar of the covenant-song, and the ember-light had risen off him like steam off morning water, drifting up and up until it thinned into nothing.

*Eleven minutes,* Astrid thought. *He gave us eleven minutes, and now we have to make them worth forever.*

Tam lay where Sylwen had dragged him, half beneath the shadow of the western half of the stone, his robes scorched to ribbons, his hands still curled around the empty air where the harvesting engine had been. Shattering it had cost him everything — the backlash had burned through him like fire through dry grass — but he was breathing, shallow and stubborn, and his eyes found Astrid's across the broken ground with something that was not quite apology and not quite peace. *Go,* his cracked lips shaped. *Finish it.*

Ivetta knelt at the base of the stone, her blind face tilted upward, her white hair loose in the wind that came down from the pass. She was singing. She had been singing since Corvin fell, low and wordless, and Sylwen had joined her, and now the two voices wove together in a pattern older than either of them — the seer's voice dark and rooted, the fae's voice bright and aching, threading through each other like roots through water.

It wasn't enough. Astrid could see that with the sight that had defined every hour of his life since Maren first put his hands over a candle flame and told him to look deeper.

Because he could still see. Even now, even with the Emberlands dying behind the veil, even with the blight silvering the rivers and the harvest-engine's death throes still shuddering through the ground — he could see what remained. And what remained was almost nothing. The land's magic guttered like a candle in a doorway, a thin gold shimmer lying over the fields beyond the ruin, full of holes, full of dark places where the light had simply stopped. Where it touched the standing stones along the ridge, it clung and flickered. Where it touched the army — Drevane's ranks drawn up in disciplined silence at the edge of the battlefield, the second engine's scaffolding looming behind them like a gallows — it recoiled.

And where it touched him, it gathered.

That was the thing Ivetta's last vision had shown him, the secret Maren had carried to her grave rather than press upon a frightened boy: he was not a bearer. Bearers held the covenant's song in fragments, one voice among many, each necessary and none sufficient. He was the keystone. Born of both lineages — Maren's hedge-craft braided with a fae inheritance she had never named aloud — his ember-sight was not a window onto the covenant. It was the covenant, folded small into a single runaway apprentice who had spent ten years running from exactly this.

The wind shifted. It carried the smell of ash and cold iron from the army's line, and beneath it, fainter, the green-and-honey scent of the Emberlands bleeding through the thinning veil. Both worlds, starving. Both worlds, waiting on him.

Sylwen broke off from the song and came to him, her boots crunching over frost-letters, her tinker's disguise long since burned away. In her true form she was taller than he remembered, her skin the grey-white of birch bark, her eyes twin coals banked low — and dimming. He could see the fading in her the way he saw everything now: the harvest had taken its tithe of her already, and what remained of her light was rationed out in careful, dwindling measures. She had perhaps a season left. Perhaps less.

"You don't have to say anything clever," she said. "I know you. You'll want to."

"I was going to tell you your hair looks terrible in battle-wind."

"There he is." But her voice caught, and her hand came up and rested against his jaw, cool as riverstone. "Astrid. When you do this — if it works — you will not be what you were. I need you to understand that. Not a wielder. Not a bearer. The sight goes *into* the land. All of it. You'll be—" She stopped. Fae did not lie, and she would not soften this into one. "You'll be ordinary. In the way that matters most, you'll be like everyone else."

"Good," Astrid said.

She blinked. Coals flickered.

"Maren told me once," he said, "that a gift you spend on yourself is just a hoard, and hoards rot. I didn't listen. I ran instead, because spending it on everyone else felt like dying." He looked past her, at the sundered stone, at the two halves leaning toward each other across a gap of dead air like lovers who had forgotten how to close the distance. "Turns out I was right about the dying part. Just wrong about which kind."

"Astrid—"

"The stone refused all of you." He said it gently, because it needed saying and because gentleness was the last craft he had left. "Corvin offered. Ivetta offered. Tam, half-dead, offered. You offered, and the stone wouldn't take you, because sacrifice isn't the same as surrender. A sacrifice is taken from you. A surrender is given." He turned his hand over and opened it, palm up, the way Maren had taught him to offer a flame. "I'm not sacrificing my gift. I'm giving it home."

For a moment Sylwen said nothing. Then she leaned forward and pressed her forehead to his, and her voice came so low it was nearly part of the wind. "When the borders open — when this works — come find me. Giftless. Ordinary. Come find me anyway."

"That was always the plan," Astrid said, and discovered, with a strange quiet wonder, that it was true. It had been the plan since a snowbound pass, since a tinker's cart, since a fire built small and shared. "Sing me in."

She stepped back. She lifted her voice, and Ivetta's rose to meet it, and Tam — coughing, wrecked, grinning through the blood on his teeth — dragged himself upright against the stone's blackened flank and added his ruined baritone to the chord. Three voices. A broken choir. It should not have been enough.

But Astrid walked toward the stone, and the land itself began to remember the rest.

* * *

He felt the army before he heard it — a pressure at his back, a wrongness in the ember-light, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere far away. He did not turn. There was no time to turn, and besides, he knew who it would be. Some part of him had known since Hollowmere that it would end here, with Lord-Captain Drevane's measured footsteps crossing the frost-letters, stopping a dozen paces behind him.

"Apprentice Eastwick." The voice was courteous as ever, reasonable as ever, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it. "You are aware that I have four hundred crossbows and you have a rock."

"I'm aware you have a problem," Astrid said, still facing the stone. "Your engine is dead. Your Regent's justification is ash — Tam's records are already riding south with the jailer's boy, and by tomorrow every court scribe will know the blight was manufactured. You have no hunt, Captain. What you have is a choice."

"I have orders."

"You have a realm that's starving." Now Astrid turned. Drevane stood in the wind with his helm under his arm, grey at the temples, unarmored but for a breastplate, his sword sheathed — sheathed, Astrid noted, and wondered if that was confidence or exhaustion. Behind him the ranks waited in perfect discipline, and behind *them*, the second engine's scaffold creaked like a ship in a storm, gutted and useless. "So do I. So does every fae behind the veil, and every farmer on this side of it whose wells are going bitter. The blight doesn't care whose fault it is. It only cares whether the covenant stands." He spread his empty hands. "I'm about to find out whether it can."

Drevane studied him for a long moment. Astrid had been studied by this man before — in the tent at Hollowmere, across an interrogation table, through the bars of a cage — and he had learned to read those pale eyes the way he read ember-light. What he saw in them now was not cruelty. It was arithmetic. Drevane was calculating, with the same precision he brought to supply lines and troop movements, whether the crown's survival lay in a harvested Emberlands or a reforged covenant — and whether the boy in front of him was a weapon to be seized or a wellspring to be protected.

"If you fail," Drevane said quietly, "both realms die, and I will have stood aside while it happened."

"And if you kill me," Astrid said, "they die faster, and you'll have done it yourself. Those are the numbers, Captain. I've seen you do harder sums."

Something moved at the corner of Drevane's mouth. It might have been the ghost of a smile; it might have been pain. "Maren Eastwick's apprentice," he said. "She wrote to the court once, you know. Years ago. Offering to register you. The letter was intercepted by the same faction that built the engines." He paused. "You were never hunted, boy. You were *harvested*. From the beginning. They just hadn't caught up to you yet."

The words landed like a blade sliding between ribs — not because they surprised him, but because they didn't. All those years of hiding, all those roads, all that loneliness, and the trap had been laid before he'd ever taken his first step down it. He let the grief move through him and out, the way Maren had taught him to let a flame move through cupped hands without burning.

"Then this," Astrid said, "is me choosing what they never intended me to choose."

He turned back to the stone.

The choir was singing, and the land was listening. He could feel it now — feel *everything* — the thin gold shimmer gathering along the ridge, the standing stones waking one by one like sentries remembering their posts, the frost-letters at his feet softening, thawing, the dead language stirring as though someone had spoken its name after a hundred years of silence. Ivetta's voice. Sylwen's voice. Tam's ragged, glorious noise. And underneath, rising from the soil itself, from the rivers and the roots and the bones of the mountains: a fourth voice, vast and slow, the voice of the world between worlds, remembering how to sing.

Astrid climbed onto the fallen half of the stone. Its surface was warm beneath his boots — the first warmth it had held in a century — and the fracture line between the two halves glimmered faintly, a seam of gold in the black, like a wound deciding whether to heal.

He thought of Maren, and the night he fled, and the door he'd left open behind him. He thought of the memory-charm he'd burned on a fellow smuggler, and the piece of himself that had gone with it. He thought of Millbrook Cross, and the neighbor's daughter, and the shame that had finally become a road. He thought of a child asleep against his shoulder on the old smuggler's roads, carried toward sanctuary instead of profit. Every lie, every concealment, every year of being small on purpose — he gathered all of it, the whole crooked architecture of his hiding, and he set it down.

Then he reached for his gift.

Not the way he had always reached for it — grasping, guarding, keeping it banked and hidden like contraband under a false floor. He reached for it the way you reach for a bird you intend to open your hands beneath. *Here,* he said, in the place where words don't go. *This was never mine. Take it home.*

And he surrendered.

* * *

There was no pain. That was the first impossible thing.

There was light — not the guttering candle-shimmer he had known all his life but a dawn, a true dawn breaking outward from the seam in the stone, gold flooding along the fracture line like sap rising in a felled tree that has decided, against all reason, to live. The two halves groaned. The whole ruin trembled. Somewhere behind him Sylwen's voice cracked and steadied and rose higher, and Ivetta laughed — a wild, ringing sound Astrid had never heard from her — and the frost-letters at the stone's base melted all at once into water that ran glittering downhill toward the river, carrying the covenant's first words in a hundred years back into the world.

The gap between the halves closed.

It did not close quickly, and it did not close gently — stone screamed against stone, the ground bucked, the army's ranks staggered — but it closed, the way a book closes, the way a hand closes, inevitable and complete, and when the two halves met there was no seam at all. Only a pillar of white granite veined with gold, singing a note too low to hear and too deep to forget.

And Astrid's gift went into it.

He felt it leave him the way you feel a fever break — the constant pressure behind his eyes lifting, the ember-sight pouring out of him in a long golden exhalation, and with it went everything the sight had ever shown him, everything it had ever cost him, every secret and every scar. For one suspended moment he was entirely empty, a man standing on a warm stone in the wind, ordinary as bread.

Then the covenant took him in.

Not his power — his *sight*. Woven into the land itself, threaded through root and river and standing stone, his way of seeing became the land's way of seeing, and the land woke. Astrid felt it happen from the inside: the gold shimmer thickening, filling its holes, racing along the ridgelines and down into the valleys; the rivers shedding their silver like snakeskin and running brown and living again; the veil between the worlds growing translucent, then permeable, then — with a sound like a held breath released — *open*. Far away, in the deep places of the Emberlands, lights that had failed for generations kindled one by one, a constellation relighting itself. The blight did not retreat so much as dissolve, revealed at last as the absence it had always been, and where it had been, green came up through the ash of the battlefield like a tide.

He heard Drevane's army go silent. Four hundred crossbows, and not one string drawn. Men were kneeling — some of them, the ones from the blighted border villages, kneeling with their helmets off, watching their own dead fields flower. Astrid understood, in the last fading edge of what he had been, exactly what Drevane was seeing: not a weapon to be seized, not a source to be harvested, but the crown's salvation standing in plain view, asking nothing, threatening nothing, feeding everything. Slaying it would be slaying the only future the realm had left.

"Stand down," Drevane's voice carried across the ruin, steady as ever, but rougher than Astrid had ever heard it. "All companies. Stand down." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "Gods forgive us. It was never ours to take."

Astrid slid down from the stone.

His legs did not hold him well. Sylwen caught him — solid, real, *bright*, blazing now with restored light, her exile's dimness burned away — and lowered him to the thawing ground, and Ivetta's hands found his face, and Tam collapsed laughing beside them like a puppet with cut strings.

"Well?" Astrid managed. His voice sounded strange to him. Thin. Human. "Did it work?"

Ivetta's blind eyes were streaming. "You're gone," she said softly. "The sight. It's — oh, child, it's everywhere. You're in the rivers. You're in the stones. You're in the light coming through the veil." She pressed her forehead to his. "You didn't lose your gift. You became it."

Sylwen held his hand and said nothing at all, which from a fae was the loudest vow there was.

Astrid lay back against the warm stone and watched the sky, which was doing something sky had never done in his lifetime — rippling, faintly, like the surface of a pond, with shapes moving behind it that might have been towers or trees or ships or songs. The borders were open. Both realms, fed. Both futures, possible.

He was nobody now. No gift, no sight, no secret, no destiny. An ordinary man on a battlefield turning green.

And for the first time in his life, Astrid Eastwick was not hiding from anything at all.

He was still lying there, laughing quietly at the enormous lightness of it, when a rider came down from the northern pass at a gallop — a courier in no livery he recognized, waving a sealed letter overhead and shouting a word that rolled across the renewed fields and into the opening veil and the hearts of everyone who heard it:

*"Envoy! They're asking for the envoy!"*
