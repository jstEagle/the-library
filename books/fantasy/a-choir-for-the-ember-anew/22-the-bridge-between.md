## 22. The Bridge Between

The first frost of the season came to Thornfell on a morning so still that the smoke from Millbrook's chimneys rose in unbroken columns, like prayers no one had dared speak aloud for years.

Astrid Eastwick stood at the edge of the village green and watched them rise. He had not stood here since the day of the Proclamation — since the crier's brass voice and the neighbor's daughter dragged away in chains while he did nothing. The girl's name had been Wren Halloway. She was seventeen then. She was home now, and she was the one who had taught him where the new border-stones stood.

*You could have run,* she'd told him once, when he'd come back through the pass with Sylwen's hand in his and the whole broken machinery of the hunt collapsing behind them like a rotted fence. *When they read the Proclamation. You could have gone deeper into the mountains and no one would ever have found you.*

*I know,* he'd said.

And that was all he'd ever needed to say about it.

He turned up his collar against the cold and walked the length of the green, past the well, past the shrine where someone had left a bowl of late apples and a twist of ribbon in the old fae fashion — a fashion that was no longer old, no longer forbidden, just *fashion*, the way things become when they stop being dangerous. Children were chasing each other around the maypole, shrieking, and one of them — a boy of six or seven — stopped mid-chase to stare at Astrid with open curiosity, because children always knew, somehow, what adults only suspected.

"You're the bridge man," the boy said.

"I'm a man who walks between places," Astrid said. "That's all."

"My gran says you used to be able to see fires inside people."

Astrid smiled. It didn't hurt the way it once would have. "Your gran tells stories."

"She says lots of people used to have fires. Before the blight. She says yours was the biggest."

"Your gran," Astrid said gravely, "is a menace." And the boy laughed and ran off after his friends, and Astrid stood a moment longer in the cold gold light, feeling the place where his gift had been the way one feels an old scar in winter: present, familiar, no longer an wound.

He had thought, in the first weeks after the stone, that the silence inside him would drive him mad. For eleven years his ember-sight had been the constant weather of his life — the flicker of hedge-magic in Maren's hearth, the banked coals of smugglers' hearts, the terrible guttering of the land itself as the blight spread. To lose it had been like going deaf in a world of music. He had lain awake in Sylwen's camp listening to ordinary darkness and wept without shame, because grief for a thing freely given is still grief.

But the silence had not stayed silent. That was the secret no one had expected, least of all him. When his gift poured into the covenant-stone and the stone wove it into the roots of both realms, it had not been spent. It had been *translated*. He could no longer see the fire in a person's heart — but he could feel the warmth of it, faintly, the way a man who has lived beside a forge all his life can walk into any room and know whether a fire has recently burned there. He felt it now, all across Millbrook green: dozens of small warmths, human and stubborn and alive, and beneath them something vaster and stranger, a slow green-gold pulse rising out of the ground itself, out of the old roads, out of the borders that were no longer walls.

The Emberlands breathed again. And their breath moved through everything.

He walked on, out of the village, onto the smuggler's road he knew better than his own heartbeat.

* * *

Sylwen found him at the third waystation, which surprised neither of them; she had always been able to find him, or perhaps she simply refused to be lost.

She came up the trail in her traveling shape — the tinker's shape, brown coat and patched satchel, the disguise she wore now by habit rather than necessity, though she claimed she'd grown fond of it. Only her eyes betrayed her when she let them: eyes like light through amber, ancient and amused.

"You're walking too fast," she said. "For a man with no destination."

"I have a destination. I always have a destination. I'm an envoy. Envoys are famous for destinations."

"You're an envoy who hates meetings." She dropped onto the bench beside him, close enough that her shoulder pressed his, and produced from her satchel two apples and a knife, and began to peel one in a single unbroken spiral, the way fae did everything — beautifully, and slightly showing off. "The delegation from the Emberlands court arrived at Hollowmere yesterday. They're asking for you specifically. There's a dispute over the ford at Greywater."

"The ford I was captured at."

"The very same. The humans want to rebuild the bridge. The river-fae want the crossing kept wild. Both sides remember blood on those stones." She handed him half the apple. "They think you're the only soul alive whom both realms will listen to without reaching for weapons."

"They're wrong. Corvin would've—"

"Corvin is dead," Sylwen said, gently, the way she always said it, as if laying the words down carefully so they wouldn't bruise. "And the dead are honored precisely because they cannot be argued with. You can be. That's your value." She bumped her knee against his. "Also you're stubborn, and you lie beautifully when it's merciful, and you once walked a child through a blizzard on half a loaf of bread and never told anyone how afraid you were. The courts have heard the story. Stories are currency now, Astrid. You're rich."

He looked at the apple in his hand. Red skin, white flesh, grown in an orchard near the border where the soil had been silvered and dead two years ago and now ran sweet and dark. Everything was like that now. Everything bore the mark of what had nearly ended.

"Do you ever miss it?" he asked. "Exile?"

She considered this with the seriousness she gave all questions, which was one of the thousand things he loved about her and would never, apparently, name aloud, because some things survived better unnamed, like seeds under snow.

"No," she said at last. "I miss my mother. I miss the deep forests where the light sings instead of shines. But exile made me a tinker, and being a tinker made me the one fae in a hundred years who learned every smuggler's road in Thornfell, and learning those roads put me in a snowbound pass on exactly the right night to kill two witch-hunters and save a foolish apprentice." She smiled, and let her eyes go amber, and looked at him fully. "I broke the law and lost my home and gained everything. I don't recommend it as a strategy. But I don't regret it either."

"And me? Do you regret—" He stopped. Started again. "The stone took my gift. Some nights I wonder if I gave away too much. If there should have been another way."

"There wasn't. Ivetta saw it. The stone refused us all — Corvin's strength, my years, Tam's craft. It wanted a keystone born of both lineages, and it wanted him willing." Her voice softened. "You were willing. That's the whole miracle, Astrid. Not the magic. The willingness."

Below the waystation, far down the valley, the new road wound toward the open border — the border that was no longer a line of stakes and wardens but a slow mingling, market-days where fae lanterns hung beside human stalls, hedge-witches teaching saplings to grow straight, fae masons teaching humans to build with living wood. A cart crawled along it, and even from this distance Astrid could feel its cargo: two small warmths, huddled together. Refugees, or traders, or simply people moving between worlds because moving between worlds was possible now.

"Greywater ford," he said. "When do they need me?"

"Yesterday, ideally. Tomorrow, acceptably." She finished her apple spiral and tucked it into her satchel — she kept every peel, dried them for tea, a habit he'd long stopped questioning. "We can sleep here and walk at dawn. Or—" and here her voice did something sly and warm, "—we can take the old smuggler's cut above the falls. You know, the one you showed me in the spring. The one with the view."

"The one where you pushed me into the pool."

"The one where you *fell* into the pool, entirely unprompted, in front of witnesses."

"There were no witnesses. That's why you pushed me."

She laughed — the real laugh, the one that came from somewhere older than her tinker-shape — and leaned her head against his shoulder, and for a long time neither of them spoke, and the mountain held them the way it had always held its secrets: patiently, and without judgment.

* * *

Wren Halloway's cottage sat at the crook of the millstream, and it smelled, as it always did, of rosemary and woodsmoke and the particular mineral scent of fresh-cut stone.

Astrid ducked under the lintel — the doorframe was carved now with the covenant sigil, the interlocking flame-and-branch, and Wren touched it every time she passed through, a superstition she'd invented herself and defended fiercely. Inside, the workbench ran the length of the wall, littered with chisels and wax rubbings and half-finished border-stones, each one a small twin of the great sundered-and-reforged stone at the heart of the mountains.

"You're late," Wren said, without looking up. She was nineteen now, and carried herself like a woman of fifty, which was what surviving a cage at seventeen did to a person. Her hands were sure. Her teacher's hands had been sure too, before the pyre.

"The roads were bad."

"The roads are always bad. Sit down. Taste this." She thrust a cup at him — hedge-tea, bitter and restorative — and then finally looked up, and her sternness cracked into the grin she saved for him alone. "All right. Out with it. How bad is Greywater?"

"Bad enough that both courts sent for me on the same day, which means both courts are frightened, which means it might actually get solved."

"Mm." She went back to her carving. He watched her work a moment — the sure strokes, the flame taking shape in the granite — and felt the old ache and the old gratitude sitting side by side in his chest like old friends.

"You know," she said, chips flying, "when they dragged me off that wagon, I hated you. Did I ever tell you that? I saw you in the crowd. I saw you *not doing anything*. And I hated you for three days in that cell, and then Hale came, and I was so busy being terrified that I forgot to hate anyone." The chisel paused. "And then months later, word comes up the mountain that the bridge man burned his own magic into a rock to save everyone, and I thought — oh. Oh, he wasn't doing nothing. He was waiting. He was carrying it. All that time he was carrying it alone."

"Wren—"

"Shut up, I'm not done." She blew stone dust off the sigil. "I'm telling you this because you never let anyone tell you. You think the guilt is the truest thing about you. It isn't. The truest thing is that you came back. Everyone runs. You came *back*." She set down the chisel and fixed him with a look that had ended arguments with guildmasters twice her age. "So when you stand at that ford tomorrow and both sides shout at you, remember: you've already done the impossible thing. This is just carpentry."

Astrid laughed, surprising himself. "Carpentry."

"Bridges, isn't it? That's the whole job description." She wiped her hands on her apron and nodded toward the corner of the cottage, where a small figure sat cross-legged on the floor, so quiet Astrid had almost missed her.

The child — Lark, though no one remembered giving her the name, so presumably she'd chosen it herself, as she chose most things — was eight now, and solemn as a magpie, and she was holding one of Wren's practice-stones in both hands and staring at it with an intensity that Astrid recognized the way one recognizes one's own face in old portraits.

"She's got a spark," Wren said quietly. "Small. Hedge-craft, maybe less. First new gift since the reforging. I'm teaching her the way Maren taught you — slowly, kindly, with the door left open."

Lark looked up at him. Her eyes were the flat gray of the mountain in winter, and utterly unafraid.

"Did it hurt?" she asked. "Giving it away?"

Astrid crouched to her level. Outside, the millstream murmured over stones that had once been silvered with blight and now ran clear.

"It hurt," he said honestly. "But it hurt the way letting go of a rope hurts, when you've been hanging on so long you forgot you could put your feet on the ground."

She considered this with the gravity of a high judge. Then she nodded once, satisfied, and went back to her stone, and Astrid rose, and Wren pressed a wrapped parcel of bread and cheese into his hands — envoys, apparently, still traveled on smuggled food — and he stepped out into the afternoon with the taste of bitter tea and the sound of a chisel ringing behind him like a small bright bell.

* * *

He walked the last stretch of the old smuggler's road alone, as dusk came down.

This was the road he'd run along with contraband brandy and stolen charms, the road where he'd burned a memory-charm on a friend rather than be seen, the road where every mile had cost him a piece of himself and he'd called the spending *freedom*. The pines stood the same. The wind smelled the same. But the road itself had changed — or he had, which amounted to the same thing — and now when he laid his palm against the mossy stone at the pass summit, he felt what he always felt: the deep slow pulse of the covenant, running through the land like sap through a tree, his own gift beating at the heart of it, everywhere and nowhere, no longer his and therefore more his than anything had ever been.

Two figures waited at the summit cairn, silhouetted against the last red light.

One was tall and slight, wearing a tinker's coat that shimmered, at certain angles, into something like armor. The other was broad and gray-bearded and leaning on a riverman's pole, though he needed it less each month, and he grinned when he saw Astrid coming like a man greeting a son.

"You're late," said Corvin's ghost — no, not his ghost; his echo, the imprint the reforged covenant kept of every bearer who had fed it, walking the old roads on feast-days and moments of need, warm as memory and twice as stubborn. "Ferry won't wait forever, lad."

"Ferries wait," Astrid said. "That's the entire point of ferries."

"Not mine." The echo clapped his shoulder, and the touch was cool and real as riverwater, and then he tipped his cap to Sylwen and was gone into the dusk the way echoes go — not vanished, just elsewhere, the way a song is elsewhere between verses.

Sylwen took Astrid's hand. Below them, the valley filled with evening: hearth-smoke rising straight and unafraid, lanterns kindling in windows, and far off at the border a string of fae lights lifting into the air like a procession of stars come down to learn the roads.

"Greywater tomorrow," she said.

"Greywater tomorrow."

"And after that?"

"After that," Astrid said, "somewhere else. And after that, somewhere else again. That's the whole job description."

She laughed, and drew him on down the mountain, and behind them the covenant-stone pulsed once in the dark like a heart that had remembered its rhythm — steady, patient, and warm, keeping faith with every road that led between.
