## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

The ravens told me about the snow before the sky did.

*White coming down the throat of the pass,* said the one perched on the lead wagon's canvas, tilting its head at me with that air of professional detachment ravens affect, as if weather were merely a rumor they'd heard and couldn't be bothered to confirm. *Two days early. The mountain is in a mood.*

"The mountain is always in a mood," I murmured, keeping my voice under the creak of the wagon wheels. "It's a mountain."

*I'm just saying,* it said, ruffling its feathers. *Don't blame me when you're digging sledges out of drifts and wishing you'd listened.*

Nobody else on the caravan heard any of this. To them I was Astrid Eriksen, pathfinder for Halvard Voss's smuggling concern, a woman who read the passes like a farmer reads clouds—by instinct, by squinting, by some uncanny luck that had never once been explained. Twenty-two years of practice had taught me how to make the gift look like guesswork. You pause at a fork, frown, pick the left trail. You never explain. Explanation is where people start asking questions, and questions are where the pyre starts being built.

My mother explained. Once. To the wrong person.

I pushed that thought down where it lived, in the cellar of me, behind a door I'd painted over so many times the wood was more paint than grain.

"Pathfinder." Halvard's voice rolled up from the rear wagon like a rockslide with opinions. He was a big man gone soft in the middle but not in the hands, and he was currently wearing the expression he reserved for ledgers and bad news. "Ravens are acting strange."

"They're birds," I said. "Strange is their whole occupation."

"Flocking low. Circling the third wagon." He squinted up at the gray throat of Thornfell above us. "You see weather coming I don't?"

Snow, I thought. Two days early. *The mountain is in a mood.* But saying *the raven told me* would cost me everything, so instead I did what I always did: I performed the ritual of looking like I was only guessing.

I stopped, turned my face into the wind, let it tug my hood. Frowned at the peaks the way a woman frowns who is thinking about weather and nothing else. Then: "Squall by nightfall, maybe. We should push for Hollowmere bend and camp under the overhang."

Hollowmere bend was also where the wolves were, but that was a separate conversation, and one I intended to have privately.

Halvard grunted—the sound of a man filing my guess under *lucky again*—and bellowed orders down the line. Six wagons, fourteen crew, cargo that officially didn't exist moving from a town that barely did toward buyers whose names Halvard would deny knowing under torture, and had, pleasantly, twice. Smuggling through Thornfell was less a crime than a regional economy; the king's roads were taxed to ruin and the king's soldiers were mostly rumors, so everyone from priests to granaries moved goods the crooked way, and men like Halvard kept the crooked way passable.

We made good time for four hours. Which, in my experience, is exactly how long the world allows before it collects a toll.

* * *

The toll came due an hour shy of Hollowmere, at a place the old maps called the Widow's Teeth—a stretch where the pass narrows between two faces of granite like a jaw mid-sentence. I'd come through it forty times. I knew its moods. And I knew, from three separate conversations with three different animals that morning, that something underneath it had shifted.

The marmots had gone quiet first. Marmots are the gossip network of the high country, and silence from a marmot colony is like silence from a tavern—it means either sleep or trouble. Then a chough had flown past us going the wrong direction, downhill, fast, without its usual commentary. Choughs always have commentary. And finally, half a mile out, I'd felt it: a wrongness in the stone, a held breath, the way a room feels when someone has died in it recently.

"Stop the line!" I shouted, and the wagons ground to a halt in a spray of grit. "Nobody crosses the Teeth. Nobody."

Halvard jogged forward, scowling. "What now?"

"Listen." I held up a hand.

He listened. Wind. Creak of leather. A mule complaining about philosophy somewhere in the middle of the line.

"I don't hear anything."

"That's the problem." I dismounted and walked ahead of the lead wagon, alone, into the jaw of the pass. Behind me I heard him mutter something about women and mountains and gods, in the tone of a man who trusts both less than he trusts his own ledger.

Up close, the wrongness was worse. It wasn't sound. It was the absence of the small sounds that should be there—the tick of settling scree, the dry click of stones warming in the thin sun. The mountain was holding its breath, and I knew what it was holding its breath for.

I put my palm flat against the granite.

This is the part I can't explain to anyone, because there's no explaining it. The gift isn't hearing words from beasts—that part people could almost understand, could file under madness or witchcraft or trickery. But it's more than that. Stone doesn't speak in words. Stone speaks in pressure, in patience, in the long slow memory of weight. When I touch rock I feel what it feels, and what this rock felt was *about to let go.*

Fifty yards above me, a shelf the size of a barn floor had cracked loose from its moorings sometime in the last season, hanging by roots and habit. The vibration of our wagons—six of them, heavy-laden, grinding over frozen ground—was the last straw it was waiting for someone to set on its back.

I could feel it the way you feel a sneeze building in someone else's face. Any moment now. Any weight, anywhere along the Teeth, and the whole jaw would snap shut.

I walked back to the line. Halvard watched me come with his arms crossed.

"Well?"

"We go around. South rim, the goat track. It adds half a day."

"Half a *day?*" His voice went up an octave, which for Halvard is a scream. "Do you know what the ice on the south rim does to wheel grease? Do you know what a half day costs us at the border? Pathfinder, I've crossed the Teeth forty times with you and—"

"Halvard." I kept my voice flat. "Move the wagons."

He stared at me. Something flickered across his face—not belief, not quite, but the shape of a man deciding whether to bet his whole operation on a woman's frown. Fourteen crew watching. Six wagons loaded with his year's profit.

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor and it is cruel, one of the mules chose that exact moment to bolt.

It happened fast, the way disasters do—one second the line was standing, arguing, alive with human stubbornness, and the next a young mule named Turnip decided he'd had enough of the cold and the waiting and the general indignity of existence, reared, snapped his lead rope, and plunged forward past me into the gap of the Teeth with a wagonload of salted fish rattling behind him.

"No—" I said, uselessly, to a mule.

Turnip made it thirty yards. Then his hind hoof came down on a slab of shale with a crack like a knuckle popping, and the mountain exhaled.

I have heard avalanches. I've heard ice calve off glaciers with a sound like the world clearing its throat. But nothing prepared me for the sound of the Widow's Teeth closing—a deep, grinding groan from the bones of the pass, rising to a roar as the shelf let go and came down in a white-and-gray flood of stone and snow-dust that swallowed Turnip's wagon whole and slammed a wall of rubble across the pass behind him, cutting our road in two.

When the dust settled, the pass was shut. A rampart of broken granite twenty feet high lay where the road had been. And somewhere on the far side of it, alone, was a mule, half a wagon of fish, and—God help me—I could hear him screaming. Not in words. In mule. But screaming.

*Trapped. Leg caught. Cold. TRAPPED. TRAPPED—*

Every head turned to me. Halvard's face had gone the color of old tallow. "The boy," someone said—no, the mule, but grief makes people stupid—"Tomas went after Turnip, he was leading him—" and then the murmur ran down the line like fire through dry grass: Tomas. Nineteen years old. Tomas, who sang off-key and owed everyone money and was the closest thing this company had to a mascot, had run after his mule and was now on the far side of twenty feet of fresh avalanche.

Silence. Fourteen people looking at a wall of stone, and then at each other, and then—at me. Because I was the pathfinder. Because I was the one who knew things.

Because I was the one who had told them to stop, and been ignored, and now a boy was on the other side of a mountain's temper.

"We dig," Halvard said hoarsely. "Shovels. All of it. If we start now—"

"It took seconds to fall," I said. "It'll take a day to dig. Tomas doesn't have a day, and neither does Turnip, if the leg's broken." My heart was slamming against my ribs. I could hear the mule's terror ringing against the stone, bouncing back to me, and beneath it, fainter, human sounds—coughing, cursing, a boy's voice cracking on a prayer. Alive. Both of them, alive. For now.

"There's another way," I said.

Every eye fixed on me. And I understood, in that moment, the true cruelty of gifts: they're only useful at exactly the moments when using them destroys you.

"There's a chimney," I said. "A crack in the east face, behind that spur. It goes up and over the slide—comes down on the other side. Narrow, but a man could squeeze through with a rope. I've used it before."

That much was even true. What I didn't say was how I knew the chimney was stable, or how I knew the exact angle to descend on the far side, or how I knew that the rockfall had left a pocket of air where Turnip stood, because the stone itself had told me—all of it, freely, in the voiceless language I'd spent my life pretending I didn't understand.

"Show us," Halvard said.

So I showed them. And here is where I made my choice, the one I'll carry until they burn me or bury me:

I climbed. I found the crack, wedged myself in, and climbed—up through freezing dark, breath rasping, heart hammering, while behind me I heard Halvard organizing ropes and shovels and the particular grim competence of smugglers doing something dangerous for free. And when I reached the top of the chimney and looked down at the far side, at the pocket of rubble where Turnip stood pinned and shrieking and Tomas crouched beside him with blood on his forehead—

I didn't hesitate. That's the thing I keep returning to, in the nights since. I didn't hesitate, because hesitation kills boys and mules, and because twenty-two years of hiding had already taught me that the gift doesn't care about my caution. It rises in me like water finding a crack.

I pressed both palms to the stone above the slide and I *spoke.*

Not aloud. Not in words anyone human could parse. But I spoke, with everything I had, into the bones of the mountain: *Hold. Hold, old one. Hold your teeth. Let me borrow your strength for one hour and I will owe you a debt no one has ever paid a mountain.*

And the mountain—this is the part I still can't believe, though I saw it with my own eyes—the mountain *listened.*

The trembling in the rubble went still. The groan of shifting stone settled into silence. Below me, the slope that should have continued sliding, should have buried a boy and a mule under fifty more tons of rock, simply... held. Like a dog told to stay.

"By all the saints," breathed Tomas below, staring up at me, at my hands glowing—or not glowing, that's the thing, they weren't glowing, but he swore afterward they were—"she's talking to it. She's talking to the *mountain.*"

I dropped down the far side, freed Turnip's leg with a lever bar and a prayer to no one in particular, got the mule up, got Tomas up, got them both moving toward the chimney while the mountain held its breath around us. And it worked. Of course it worked. We came through the crack one at a time, boy and beast and pathfinder last, and by the time we reached the wagons the sun was setting and fourteen smugglers were staring at me the way men stare at things they can't fit into their understanding of the world.

Halvard opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"You stopped the slide," he said finally.

"I asked nicely."

"Astrid." He stepped closer, lowering his voice, which was somehow worse than shouting. "I've known you six years. You've saved my cargo eleven times, my neck twice, and my marriage once, God knows how. Whatever you are—" he glanced at the pass, at the held-still mountain, back at me—"whatever that was... you're still you. Right?" 

Something in my chest cracked, very quietly.

"Right," I said.

He nodded slowly, accepting it, or choosing to accept it, which may be the same thing. "Good. Get the wagons turned around. We camp at Hollowmere tonight. And Astrid—" a pause, and then, gruffly, almost gently: "Thank you."

* * *

We made camp under the great overhang at Hollowmere bend as the first flakes came down, fat and unhurried, the storm arriving precisely when the ravens said it would. Fires bloomed along the wagon line. Someone started singing—Tomas, naturally, off-key, with a new verse about the pathfinder who argued with mountains, and the crew laughing, and the laughter having that slightly too-bright edge it gets when men laugh at the thing that frightened them.

I sat apart, as I always sit apart. Not far. Just far enough.

That's the loneliness nobody warns you about, when you're built like me. It isn't the burning—they burned my mother, and yes, that fear lives in me like a splinter against the heart—but it's this: sitting ten feet from warmth and song and people who'd die for you today because you saved them, and knowing that every single one of them would look at you differently tomorrow. That the saving and the damning are the same act. That the only way to be loved by these people is to be a smaller version of yourself, forever, and that the smaller version gets tired, gets so *tired*, of carrying everything alone.

I was tired. Sitting there in the firelight with the snow coming down and Tomas's terrible singing washing over me, I was so tired I could have wept, and probably would have, if a wolf hadn't chosen that moment to sit down beside me.

She arrived without sound, gray as the dusk, yellow-eyed, ancient in the way that wolves who survive twelve winters are ancient. None of the crew noticed her. They never do; the gift works both ways, cloaking her presence among the oblivious as surely as it cloaks mine. She sat, and she leaned her warm weight against my side, and she said, in the low rolling voice of the high country packs:

*You gave the mountain your word. Mountains collect.*

"I know," I said softly.

*A debt to stone is a strange coin,* she went on. *But that's not why I came. Word runs faster than snow, little speaker. There are men asking questions in the valley towns. Men in white coats. Asking after a woman who talks to beasts.*

My blood went cold, colder than the snow.

"How many?" I whispered.

*Enough,* she said. *And they have dogs. Dogs talk, little speaker. Dogs tell everything.*

Across the fire, Tomas hit a note that curdled milk at forty paces, and the crew roared, and the warmth of them washed over me like something from another life. The wolf leaned harder against my side, patient, warm, waiting.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow I'd have to decide whether to run, whether to warn them, whether to become the thing they feared or stay the thing they trusted—and I couldn't do either, because both meant losing everything.

But that was tomorrow's toll.

Tonight there was only the snow, and the fire, and the wolf's heartbeat steady against my ribs, and the long dark road ahead.
