## 2. A Date, Written Down

I want it on the record, before anyone accuses me of romance, that I came to Thornfell for a book.

Not a woman. Not an adventure. Not the crisp mountain air or the charming local custom of charging travelers a toll for breathing near the pass. A book. The Codex of Ash, specifically—a prophetic text sealed away a hundred years ago by an archbishop who, according to every fragment I could find, considered it too dangerous to read and too holy to burn, which is the scholarly equivalent of locking your diary in a vault and then telling everyone at dinner parties how scandalous it is.

I had spent four years chasing fragments. A marginal note in a monastery ledger: *the Ash Codex speaks true and speaks soon.* A heretic's confession, extracted under unpleasant circumstances, mentioning "the book that writes itself." A letter from a dead archivist to a deader bishop, describing pages that were never blank and never still. Most reasonable scholars had concluded the Codex was a legend, the way most reasonable people conclude most interesting things are legends. I had been told, more than once, that I was not a reasonable person. I chose to take it as a compliment.

So: the border town of Greyhollow, last stop before the Thornfell passes, smelling of woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular desperation of a place that exists solely to separate travelers from their coin before the mountains finish the job. I arrived at dusk with a satchel of notes, a forged letter of introduction, and eleven coins to my name, which my mother would have called reckless and my father would have called Tuesday.

The tavern was called the Drowned Crow, which struck me as an odd name for an establishment in a landlocked mountain town, until I went inside and understood that everything in Greyhollow was slightly drowned—voices, lamplight, the ambitions of the clientele. Smugglers, mostly. I could tell by the way they looked at my boots. Scholars' boots are a distinct species: too clean, too many buckles, worn by men who walk to libraries rather than across them.

I ordered stew I did not fully trust and beer I trusted even less, and I asked the barkeep, with the studied casualness of a man who has rehearsed this in a mirror, whether he knew anything about old books in the area.

He looked at me the way one looks at a dog that has just asked for a loan.

"Books," he said.

"Old ones. Sealed ones, possibly. Church-sealed."

"Church stuff's up the hill. They don't let folk read anything."

"What if I told you I was authorized?"

"By who?"

"By people above your hill."

He considered this. "You've got the look of a man authorized by nobody," he said, and poured me another beer I hadn't asked for, which in Greyhollow I suspect counts as hospitality.

I was composing my rebuttal—which would have been devastating—when the door opened and the cold came in wearing a leather coat.

She was tall in the way of people who are used to being looked up at, with grey eyes and wind-chapped cheeks and the particular stillness of someone who has spent a great deal of time being quiet on purpose. Her hair was the color of the ash the Codex was named for, which I did not notice at the time because I am not a poet, but which I am recording now because the alternative is admitting what I actually noticed, which was that she moved like the mountains owed her money and she'd come to collect.

She took the stool beside mine because it was the only one, and I, being a scholar, being an idiot, being twenty-four years old and armed with nothing but a dead father's conversational habits, said:

"Is the stew here safe?"

She looked at me. "Define safe."

"Safe in the sense of—edible. Without consequence."

"Consequence for who?"

"For me, ideally."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile. A scout sent ahead of a smile, deciding whether the terrain was worth it. "The stew's fine," she said. "The beer's a crime. The company varies."

"I've been warned about the company already."

"By the barkeep?"

"By the barkeep."

"He's right. Best eat fast." She signaled for her own beer with two fingers, and the barkeep brought it without being asked, which told me she was known here, and known the way regulars are known in places where regulars don't give names.

I should have eaten my stew in silence. I want that understood. I had a mission. I had four years of fragments in my satchel. I had a forged letter and eleven coins and a plan that involved asking careful questions in careful corners until someone pointed me at a careful door.

Instead I heard myself say: "Do you come through the passes often? The Thornfell passes, I mean. Professionally."

"Is that a scholar's way of asking what I do?"

"How did you know I was a scholar?"

She glanced down at my boots. "You dress like a library."

I looked at my boots. They had, in my defense, been clean that morning. "I'm looking for a book," I admitted, because apparently my mouth had voted to abandon my mission without informing the rest of me. "A specific one. Sealed a century ago. The Codex of Ash."

And here is the thing I would think about later, at length, at three in the morning, at every three in the morning thereafter: she went still. Not surprised. Still. The difference matters. Surprise is a flinch; stillness is a decision. She set her beer down with the care of someone setting down something that might go off, and she said, in a voice with nothing in it:

"Never heard of it."

"You haven't."

"No."

"It's just—you went still."

"I go still when strangers ask me about forbidden books in taverns full of smugglers. It's called survival." She picked the beer back up. "Ask me about the weather."

"The weather."

"Everyone talks about the weather. It's the only safe subject left."

So we talked about the weather. Or rather, I talked about the weather, badly, while she drank her beer and watched me with an expression I would spend the rest of this story learning to read, and which at the time I catalogued—wrongly—as *amused tolerance*. There was a rockslide on the western pass, she said, when I ran out of weather. Closed it for a week. She'd been on the mountain when it came down.

"Were you hurt?"

"No."

"You're certain? You can say if—"

"I said no." And then, softer, in a tone that was almost an apology for the hardness before it: "The mountain and I have an understanding."

"That's an unusual way to put it."

"That's the only way to put it."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind us a smuggler laughed at something that was probably a war crime. And she looked at me—really looked, the way you look at a puzzle you suspect has pieces missing—and said, "You're going into the passes anyway. Even with the slide."

"I have to. What I'm looking for is on the other side."

"Nothing's on the other side but more mountain."

"Everything's on the other side. That's the trouble with mountains."

The scout at the corner of her mouth came back, and this time it brought friends. "You're either very brave or very stupid."

"The two are frequently mistaken. My father said the difference is whether you live."

"Your father sounds like he died stupid."

"He did, actually. Fell off a roof. He was retrieving a manuscript." I shrugged. "The manuscript survived."

She laughed. It was short and rough, like it didn't get out much, and I remember thinking—this is the part I'll want on the record later, when the question of what was mine and what was written becomes a matter of some urgency—I remember thinking: *I would like to hear that again.*

"Tomorrow," she said, standing, dropping coins beside her untouched stew. "If you're still alive. Meet me at the well in the market square, midmorning. I know the passes. I know which trails the slide didn't eat. And I know," she added, pulling her coat on, "who else is asking about sealed books in this town, which is a question you should have asked me first."

"Who else is asking?"

"Men in white coats who don't drink and don't blink." She paused at the door, cold air rushing in around her like an honor guard. "Midmorning. Don't be late. I won't wait."

"What's your name?" I called.

She considered it, the way she seemed to consider everything—like the answer might cost something. "Astrid," she said, and the door closed behind her, and the tavern got colder in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

Astrid. I turned the name over like a coin of uncertain mint. I finished the criminal beer. I paid my tab down to four coins and told myself the mission was proceeding exactly as planned, and I did not believe myself for a second, because the plan had not included a grey-eyed guide who talked about the mountain like an old friend and laughed like it hurt her.

I had a date. That was the word my father would have used, with the eyebrow that accompanied it. I had a date, and I had a book to find, and I had perhaps six hours of darkness in which to find it, because I had a strong suspicion that men who don't blink were asking about sealed books for reasons less scholarly than mine.

I went out into the cold to commit burglary.

* * *

The chapel-house on the hill had been a chapel once, before the Church of the Binding Flame consolidated its holdings and turned it into an archive, which is what the Church calls a place where books go to be forgotten on purpose. I had spent two days establishing my cover: a visiting archivist's assistant, papers forged with a skill I will not detail here except to note that my father's roof-fall left me an inheritance of talents he never knew he'd taught me.

The seal on the archive's lower vault was a century old and the lock was older, and neither had been oiled since the archbishop who sealed it died smug. I will say this for the Church: they are magnificent at hiding things and terrible at keeping them. The lock surrendered in forty minutes. The dark beyond it smelled of dust and tallow and time.

I had a candle stub and a hand lantern and a heart going like a drummer who'd been told the song was ending. The vault was small—three shelves, one desk, one chest. The chest was iron, banded, locked again, and stenciled with words in Old Liturgical that I translated by lantern-light with my hands not entirely steady:

*HEREIN: THAT WHICH SPEAKS TRUE AND SPEAKS SOON. LET NO MAN READ WHO WISHES TO REMAIN A MAN.*

Which is, I will note for the record, a truly terrible thing to stencil on a box you don't want opened. It is practically an invitation. Whoever wrote it understood scholars exactly as well as I understand locks.

The inner lock took twenty minutes. The lid came up with a sound like a held breath released.

The Codex of Ash was not what the fragments promised. The fragments promised a tome, a grimoire, a monster of vellum and iron. What lay in the chest was modest—calfskin, hand-sized, unadorned except for a scorch mark across the cover in the shape of a wing. It weighed almost nothing. It was warm.

That was the first wrong thing. The vault was cold enough to see my breath. The book was warm as a sleeping bird.

I opened it on the desk, and the pages were not blank, and they were not still.

The hand that filled them was old—quill and iron-gall ink, a scribe's hand, careful and cramped—but the ink was not dry. I know ink. I have spent my life with ink. This ink moved. Not visibly, not the way water moves, but the way something alive moves when you watch it from the corner of your eye: a settling, a breathing, a sense that if you looked away and looked back, the words would be in slightly different places.

The first pages were history. The founding of the Ember-keep. The crowning of kings. A list, growing shorter down the page, of dragon-fires: *Vaelmora, extinguished. Skarnhal, extinguished. The Ember of Thornfell, dimming.* Each entry in the same hand, each dated in years that hadn't happened yet when the book was sealed.

I sat down on a crate that was not clean and did not care, and I read.

The prophecies were not the thundering kind. No beasts with seven heads, no seas of blood. They were small. Terribly, specifically small. *A grey-eyed child will refuse her gift and be burned for it anyway*—dated forty years past, true, and my stomach turned over, because I had heard that story, everyone in the borderlands had heard that story, the beast-speaker's wife, the pyre in the market square. *A smuggler with a soft heart will choose a road with no profit in it.* *A priest will be exiled for loving the wrong part of the flame.* Each one dated. Each one, as far as I could check against history, exact.

And then the dates turned forward. And then the dates turned to this year. And then, on a page near the end, the date was yesterday.

*The scholar comes to Greyhollow with forged papers and eleven coins. He asks the barkeep about sealed books and is refused. He is not discouraged. He is never discouraged. This will be the death of him, or the saving of everything, or both.*

I read it twice. My eleven coins felt suddenly very loud in my pocket, like they were testifying against me.

I turned the page.

*The same night, he meets her.*

The ink was still wet. I mean that literally—I touched the corner of a letter and my fingertip came away faintly grey, and I wiped it on my coat and told myself it was residue, oxidation, anything, everything except what it was.

*He asks if the stew is safe. She says: define safe. He is charmed and does not know the word for what he is feeling, which is fortunate, because the word is doom, and he would only look it up.*

I stopped breathing.

I read it again. *Define safe.* My hands were shaking. I told myself: coincidence. Common phrase. People say *define* things all the time. Scholars say it constantly. She could have—

*He asks if she comes through the passes often, professionally. She tells him the mountain and she have an understanding. He thinks: I would like to hear that laugh again.*

I stood up so fast the crate went over behind me and the crash echoed up the vault stair like a bell tolling for someone. I didn't check the stair. I didn't check anything. I stood over the book with my lantern held high and I read the rest with my heart slamming against my ribs like something trying to get out—or in, I genuinely could not have told you which.

*They agree to meet at the well, midmorning. She will not tell him yet that the men in white coats are hunting a beast-speaker through the border towns. He will not tell her yet that the book has already written their deaths. Both silences are mercies. Both will run out.*

*The mountain road, the Ember, the guttering light, the—*

The page ended there. Not finished. *Not finished yet.* The last line simply stopped, mid-clause, the way a sentence stops when the hand writing it is waiting for something to happen first.

Waiting for me to live it.

I closed the book. The scorch-wing on the cover seemed, in the lantern light, to be beating. I picked it up and it was warmer than before, warm as a held hand, and I stood alone in a century of dust with the only book in the world that had written down my evening before I'd lived it—including, I noted with a kind of hysterical clarity, the exact number of coins in my pocket, which I counted twice, eleven both times, and laughed, once, a sound with no humor in it whatsoever.

Wonder, I had always believed, was the best feeling a scholar could have. I had built my whole life on it. I had chased it across four years and two countries and one regrettable incident involving a monastery roof.

I had been wrong. Wonder curdles. Wonder is milk left out in the sun, and I was standing in the sun now, holding the cup, and there was no putting it back.

Because here was the arithmetic, and it would not balance any other way: if the book had written my meeting with Astrid—word for word, coin for coin, *define safe* for *define safe*—then the meeting was not chance. And if the meeting was not chance, then the grey-eyed woman at the well tomorrow was not a woman I had met, but a woman I had been *assigned*. And if the book wrote forward—if it wrote deaths, if it wrote *the death of him, or the saving of everything, or both*—then somewhere in those warm, wet, waiting pages was an account of how Wren Calloway ended, and it was already written, and the ink was drying toward it even as I stood there.

I should have put the book back. I should have relocked the locks, gone home, taken up a quiet life cataloguing boring things in safe places.

I am never discouraged. The book knew that about me before I did.

I wrapped the Codex of Ash in my spare shirt, put it inside my coat against my chest where I could feel it breathing, took one last look at the vault I had just made myself a felon in, and went out into the cold to keep a date that had been made for me a hundred years before I was born.

Midmorning, she had said. She wouldn't wait.

The book, I was almost certain, already knew whether I'd be on time.
