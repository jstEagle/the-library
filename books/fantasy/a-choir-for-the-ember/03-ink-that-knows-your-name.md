## 3. Ink That Knows Your Name

I did not sleep.

This will surprise no one who has ever found a book that reads *you* back, but I want it on the record that I tried. I blew out the lamp. I lay down in my rented room above the tannery with the Codex of Ash wrapped in my spare shirt like something contagious, and I stared at the ceiling beams and listened to the town of Grayhollow settle into its creaks and snores, and every few minutes my eyes drifted back to the shape under the shirt the way a tongue drifts back to a broken tooth.

Around the third hour I gave up, relit the lamp, and unwrapped it for the fourth time.

The page had not changed. That was somehow worse than if it had. If it had changed—if new ink had crawled across it while I slept—I could have told myself I'd imagined the whole thing, that I was a tired scholar hallucinating his own life onto vellum. But no. The words sat there in that old brown script, patient as gravestones:

*On the eve of the Frostfair moon, the speaker and the seeker collide at the sign of the Crooked Antler. She spills his wine; he spills her secret without knowing it. They agree to meet when the market bell rings.*

*They do not know they are already written.*

*Nor that the writing ends badly.*

I had read that last line perhaps forty times now, and each time it landed like a stone dropped down a well—a long fall, then a small, cold splash.

The speaker and the seeker. Astrid Eriksen and me. Last night she'd knocked a cup of sour red wine into my lap, laughed at my face, and then—when I'd babbled about the Codex like an idiot, because I babble when I'm nervous, which is always—she'd gone very still and said, "Meet me tomorrow. Market bell. Don't tell anyone why."

And I had walked home floating on air, and opened a hundred-year-old book, and found our evening recorded in it like a diary entry from a ghost.

Here is what I should have done: burned it. Any sensible person burns a book that knows their name. But I am a scholar, and scholars are people who have decided, against all evidence, that knowledge is worth whatever it costs. So instead I spent the night doing what I do best—cross-referencing.

The Codex is not one prophecy. It's dozens, maybe hundreds, entries in different hands spanning a century, and most of them are gibberish or mundane or both. A flood in a village I've never heard of. A queen's stillbirth. Someone named Ottel breaking a leg on some stairs. But threaded through them, recurring every few pages like a refrain, is a phrase that makes my hands go cold:

*Reach the Ember before it gutters.*

*The Ember gutters; the crown-thing stirs.*

*Only the speaker and the seeker may reach the Ember before it gutters, and only together, and only before the last frost-moon.*

*If the Ember dies, the thing that wears the king wears everything.*

I wrote those lines out three times in my own notebook, because copying things is how I think, and by dawn I had arrived at two conclusions. First: King Aldric—the Aldric, the Hollow King himself, whose face is on every coin and whose eyes, if you believe the ballads, haven't truly looked at anything in thirty years—is host to something. Something old and hungry, kept fed by a fire beneath Thornfell that is going out.

Second: according to this book, Astrid Eriksen and I are the only two people alive who can stop it.

Which would be flattering, except for the other entries. The ones near the end. The ones I made myself read only once, and then covered with my hand like a child covering a scary picture:

*The speaker burns, or the seeker does, unless—*

*Unless they reach the Ember before it gutters.*

*After the Ember, nothing more is written of either of them.*

Nothing more is written. For a book that has just spent a page describing the exact tilt of a woman's laugh, that silence is the loudest thing in it.

The market bell rang.

I wrapped the Codex back in its shirt, tucked it under my arm, and went to keep a date with my own death—or possibly my own destiny, the book wasn't specific, books never are—and I confess that on the walk to the square I rehearsed approximately eleven opening lines and abandoned all of them the moment I saw her.

Astrid Eriksen stood by the fountain feeding crumbs to pigeons, and the pigeons were not behaving like pigeons. They were arranged around her in a loose half-circle, heads cocked, listening, and every so often one would bob and she would nod back, and I swear on every dead philosopher I love that they were having a conversation.

She saw me watching. The half-circle scattered.

"You're early," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm always early. Early people live longer." She looked me over—the shadows under my eyes, the shirt-wrapped bundle clutched to my chest like a shield—and her expression flattened into something wary. "You didn't sleep."

"I found it," I said. "Last night. The Codex of Ash."

Something moved behind her face, quick as a fish under ice. "And?"

"And it knows us." My voice came out lower than I intended. I glanced around the square—stalls going up, a fishwife arguing with a cooper, nobody close—but I stepped nearer anyway and lowered my voice further. "It knew we'd meet. Before we met. It wrote down last night, Astrid. Word for word. Your wine in my lap. The tavern. You telling me not to tell anyone." I swallowed. "There's a line about us. 'The speaker and the seeker.' And it says—" I stopped, because saying it aloud in a market square felt like handing a knife to the morning.

"It says what?" Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when someone is standing on thin ice and trying to distribute their weight.

"Come somewhere private," I said. "Please."

* * *

She chose the place, which meant she chose it well: a disused shrine halfway up Grayhollow's terraced streets, dedicated to some hearth-goddess whose name had worn off the lintel decades ago. Wind came through the empty doorway and stirred dust that glittered in the light like slow snow. No one came here. Even the pigeons seemed to consider it beneath them.

I set the Codex on the altar slab and unwrapped it, and watched her face while I did.

Astrid Eriksen has the kind of face that gives away exactly as much as it decides to give away, which is to say almost nothing, which is to say I noticed immediately that she recognized the book. Not curiosity—recognition. The look of someone greeting an enemy they'd hoped never to see again.

"Where did you get that," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"A dying archivist in Vaelmoor sold it to me for the price of a funeral. He said it had been sealed for a century. He said—" I hesitated. "He said it was waiting for someone. I thought he was being dramatic. Archivists usually are. It's the dust."

"Open it," she said. "To the page."

I opened it to the page and turned it toward her, and I watched her read her own yesterday in a dead man's handwriting, and I watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"*She spills his wine,*" she read aloud, very quietly. "*He spills her secret without knowing it.*" Her jaw tightened. "What secret? What did I spill?"

"You talked to the pigeons," I said gently. "In the square. Just now. Or you were about to. And last night, when I mentioned beast-speakers, you flinched like I'd drawn a blade."

The silence stretched long enough that I could hear the wind arguing with the doorway.

"My mother was burned for it," she said at last, to the altar, not to me. "When I was nine. In a village that doesn't exist anymore because everyone in it agreed to forget her name. So." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and furious and frightened all at once, which is a difficult combination to wear and she wore it like armor. "Yes. I flinch."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be quiet." She bent over the page again, reading faster now, hunting. I watched her finger stop on the lines I'd circled in charcoal the night before—*reach the Ember before it gutters*—and move on, and stop again, lower, on the lines I hadn't shown anyone because I hadn't let myself finish reading them.

Her lips moved. "*The speaker burns, or the seeker does.*"

"That's not necessarily—"

"Shut up, Wren." She said my name like she was testing its weight. Then, quieter: "Burns how? When? Where?"

"The book isn't specific. Books never are. Prophecies least of all—they're written by cowards, my old master used to say. They tell you *that* and never *how*, because *that* is enough to ruin your life and *how* would let you do something about it."

"Then what good is it?"

"That's the interesting part." I took a breath, because this was the speech I'd rehearsed eleven versions of, and none of them survived contact with her actual face. "Most of these entries already happened. There's a flood from sixty years ago, a fire in a convent, a dozen small deaths—all of them came true, exactly, down to details the writers couldn't have known. This book doesn't guess, Astrid. It records. Which means the parts that haven't happened yet aren't warnings. They're appointments."

"Appointments." She straightened up. "You're saying we're expected somewhere."

"I'm saying the Ember is real. Under Crownspire, under the palace, the last dragon-fire in the world, the thing the priests say keeps the seasons turning and the king—keeps the king. And it's dying. And this book says that when it dies, something gets out. Something that's been wearing Aldric for thirty years." I tapped the page. "And it says the only two people who can reach it in time are a speaker and a seeker. I've spent four years and my entire inheritance looking for this book. I'm the seeker. That part I'll accept. But the speaker—"

"No." She said it fast and final, a door slamming. "No. Whatever you're about to ask me, the answer is no. I guide smugglers through passes. I talk to wolves when no one's watching, and mostly they ignore me anyway. I am not a hero out of a prophecy. Heroes don't hide in the mountains. Heroes don't have ash where their mother's name used to be."

"Astrid—"

"I said no."

And that might have been the end of it—her walking out of that shrine, me standing there with a cursed book and a crushed heart, both of us returning to our separate doomed lives—except that was the moment the raven came through the doorway.

It didn't land so much as arrive, dropping out of the gray air onto the altar beside the Codex with a sound like a struck drum, and it fixed one wild black eye on Astrid and spoke.

Now, I need to be precise here, because precision is all I have. Ravens croak. Everyone knows ravens croak. What came out of that bird was croaking—harsh, ragged, ugly croaking—and yet I understood it, the way you understand a word shouted in a language you don't speak when the word happens to be *fire*. Later Astrid told me that's how it works for everyone near a true speaker: the gift leaks. It was the first time in my life I was grateful for her secret spilling.

*"White coats on the north road,"* the raven rasped. *"Three days out. Asking after a woman who talks to beasts. Purifier's writ. They have her mother's name."*

Astrid went white. Not pale—white, the color of paper, the color of the page.

"They have her mother's name," she repeated, in Common, as if translating it for herself made it worse. "Solveig." The name came out of her like a splinter. "Inquisitor Solveig. She led the pyre party. She was—" Astrid's voice caught and she pushed through it. "She loved my mother. Before. Before the Church found out what she was. Solveig signed the writ herself. I've seen it. I stole a copy when I was fourteen, because I wanted to know why, and her signature was at the bottom, and underneath it she'd written *with sorrow*. With *sorrow*." She laughed, one hard note with no humor in it anywhere. "Three days."

"Then you have three days to be gone," I said. "Over the pass, into the lowlands, some city big enough to lose yourself in—"

"And run forever? They don't stop, Wren. Purifiers don't retire. They're not men, they're a weather system." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. When she spoke again her voice had gone terribly calm, which I would learn is the most dangerous thing her voice can do. "Show me the rest."

"The rest of—"

"The book. All of it. Every entry about the Ember, the king, the speaker, the seeker. Everything." She dropped her hands and looked at me, and behind the fear there was something else taking shape, something with edges. "Because if I'm going to burn anyway, I want to know if there's a version where it means something."

So I showed her. We knelt on the cold floor of a forgotten shrine while the wind read over our shoulders, and I turned the pages one by one and translated the worst of the old script, and she listened without interrupting, which I suspect cost her more than anything else that morning. The Ember guttering nightly. The crown-thing drinking its light. The binding that thins. The price the fire always exacts—one entry, half-erased, that made her go still for a long moment before she asked me to read it again, and then said, "Nothing," when I asked what was wrong.

By the time I closed the cover, the light through the doorway had gone from gray to gold. Half the day, gone. It felt like less than an hour.

"Well," she said finally. "Options. One: we run, and they catch me eventually, and I die on a pyre like my mother, and the Ember goes out on schedule, and the thing in the crown eats the world. Two: we run, and they don't catch me, and the Ember goes out on schedule anyway, and the thing in the crown eats the world slightly later." She ticked them off on grim fingers. "Three: we go to Crownspire, find a fire under a palace full of Purifiers, unbind a king possessed by something older than the Church, and maybe—*maybe*—the book runs out of things to say about us."

"When you put it that way, option three sounds nearly reasonable."

"Wren." She turned to face me fully, and this close I could see she hadn't slept either, and that her hands were shaking, and that she was holding them still through sheer administrative will. "Why do you want to follow it? Truly. Not the scholarly answer. The book killed your certainty of a normal life in one night. Most people would burn it and grieve."

I thought about lying. I got as far as composing the lie—it was elegant, it involved duty and scholarship—and then I looked at her and threw it away.

"Because I've spent my whole life in libraries reading about people who did things," I said. "Brave people, foolish people, people the ink remembered. And I always assumed I'd die among the margins. Footnotes. And then one night a book wrote my name next to yours and said the world ends unless we do something about it, and I know how that sounds, I know how it *looks*—" I gestured helplessly at the Codex, at the shrine, at the entire absurd situation. "But Astrid, for the first time in my life, I'm in the story instead of the bibliography. That's a terrible reason. I'm aware it's a terrible reason. But it's mine."

She studied me for a long moment. Somewhere outside, the market bell rang the hour, and neither of us moved.

"You understand it wants us dead," she said. "Or burned. One or the other, unless."

"Unless."

"And you understand that if I do this—if I use the gift openly, in the capital, in front of the Church—I'm not hiding anymore. Ever again. Whatever happens at the end of that road, I come out of it known."

"Yes."

"And you understand that I will turn back. The moment it looks hopeless, I will cut your purse and vanish into a mountain range you couldn't find with a map and a prayer, and you'll deserve it for dragging me into this."

"Also yes."

"Fine." She picked up the Codex, weighed it in her hands like something that might still bite, and held it out to me. "Then we're allies. Not friends. Allies. Fear makes strange bedfellows and I intend to keep my boots on."

I took the book. Our fingers overlapped on the worn leather for a moment longer than strictly necessary, and I am a scholar, so I noted it, and filed it, and said nothing.

"One condition," she added, turning toward the doorway and the gold light beyond it. "We go by way of the high passes. There's a pack north of Grayhollow—wolves. Old ones. If anyone remembers what the Ember was like the last time it dimmed, it's them, and I'd rather hear it from teeth than from paper."

"Of course," I said. "How far?"

"Two days' hard climb." She paused at the threshold, silhouetted against the sky, and glanced back at me with something that was almost, almost a smile. "Keep up, seeker. The ink's already watching."

Behind us, on the altar, the Codex lay closed and silent.

I have wondered since whether, in that moment, a fresh line was drying somewhere in its middle pages—two figures leaving a ruined shrine, one carrying a book, one carrying a grief—and whether it ended, as so many of its lines do, with the words *they did not know what waited on the north road.*

Because the raven had said three days.

It was wrong.
