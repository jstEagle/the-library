## 4. What the Wolves Remember

We left Hallowmere before the third bell of morning, when the town was still the color of dishwater and the only witnesses were a milk-cart horse and a beggar who'd seen everything and reported nothing for forty years.

Wren walked beside me with his satchel clutched to his chest like a firstborn child, and every hundred paces or so he would glance back at the town as if it might be following us. It wasn't. Towns rarely do. But I understood the impulse—the Codex of Ash had a way of making a person feel pursued by their own future.

"You're quiet," he said.

"I'm always quiet."

"No," he said, "you're *quiet-quiet*. This is a different quality of quiet."

I liked him better when he was being insufferable. Insufferable was honest. The alternative—this careful, watching tenderness he'd developed since showing me the page with my own death inked on it in a dead man's handwriting—made my skin itch like wool in summer.

"I know where we're going," I said. "That's all."

"Where?"

"Somewhere the king's hunters won't think to look."

"And that is?"

I looked at him. He looked back, waiting, with the patience of a man who had spent his life waiting for libraries to open.

"The wolves," I said.

He stopped walking. Somewhere behind us, Halvard swore at a mule with real feeling.

"You want to *consult* them," Wren said. "The wolves. As one does."

"As one does," I agreed, and kept walking, because if I stopped I would have to explain, and explaining meant admitting how much of my life had been spent in conversations no one else could hear, and I wasn't ready to hand him that much of myself before breakfast.

* * *

The pass north of Hallowmere is called the Shepherd's Regret, which tells you most of what you need to know about it. The trail narrows to the width of a coffin lid, drops away into gray nothing on the left, and rises into rock on the right so steep the mountain seems to be leaning over to listen. Our caravan—six smugglers, two mules, one scholar, one fugitive priestess who had joined us at the gate with the cheerful fatalism of someone who'd already been excommunicated once and found it survivable—took it single file, in silence broken only by Halvard's ongoing theological dispute with the lead mule.

Sister Maren walked behind me. She was a small woman built like a bundle of kindling, with ink stains up both forearms and the eyes of someone who had read things she wasn't supposed to and enjoyed every forbidden minute of it.

"You're going to see the Greyfall pack," she said. Not a question.

I nearly missed a step. "How—"

"A beast-speaker fleeing Crownspire's writ goes to wolves. It's in the old heresies." She smiled faintly. "Everything worth knowing is in the old heresies. That's rather the point of them."

"And what do your old heresies say about the Ember?"

Her smile faded. She glanced ahead at Wren's back, then at the trail, choosing her words with the care of a woman crossing ice.

"That it isn't a fire," she said quietly. "That it's older than fire. And that the crown has been drinking from it for thirty years, and no one drinks forever without something noticing." She pulled her hood up against the wind. "Ask your matriarch. If she's still alive, she'll remember more than any book I've burned."

* * *

We found the Greyfall pack at dusk, in a hanging valley where the pines grew crooked from wind and the snow held the last light long after the sky had gone dark. There were eleven of them, sprawled across a granite shelf like lords at a feast, and they let us come because they knew me—or rather, because they knew my mother's name in my mouth, which among wolves amounts to the same thing.

Halvard took one look at the largest of them—a male the size of a pony with a scarred muzzle—and put his hand very deliberately on his axe.

"Don't," I said.

"They're looking at me."

"They're always looking at you. You're the one holding an axe."

He lowered it slowly, muttering something about a life he'd imagined having, once, that had involved fewer animals judging him.

The matriarch came forward out of the shadows at the valley's throat. Her coat had gone silver at the shoulders, her left ear was a torn fringe, and her eyes were the color of old amber with something caught inside. The younger wolves parted for her the way water parts around a keel.

*Little ember-daughter,* she said. Her voice in my head was dry leaves and river stones, and beneath it, very deep, a patience so old it felt geological. *You smell of fear and ink. Fear I understand. The ink is new.*

"This is Wren," I said aloud, because some part of me needed the words to exist in air, needed witnesses, even if the witness couldn't hear her side. "He carries a book that knows our names."

*I know,* said the matriarch. *The ravens have been shrieking about it since moonrise. Ravens shriek about everything. But this—they say the pages move.*

"They move."

She considered that. Around her, the pack had risen and arranged itself in a loose crescent—not threatening, exactly. Attentive. Eleven pairs of amber eyes fixed on Wren, who stood very still with the expression of a man who has just realized he is the subject of a lecture he hasn't been admitted to.

"What is she saying?" he whispered.

"She says the ravens gossip worse than washerwomen."

"That's—I *knew* the ravens were talking about me."

"Everyone talks about you. You collided with a smuggler and became a prophecy; you should learn to live with fame."

The matriarch huffed—a wolf laugh, dry as dust—and turned, and began to walk up the valley. *Come, little ember-daughter. The young ones will watch your soft ones. What must be remembered takes time, and time is the one thing I have too much of.*

* * *

She led me alone to a hollow beneath a lightning-split pine, where the snow was trampled to bare earth and the bones of a hundred winters lay picked clean and bleached. She settled onto the earth with the sighing care of the very old, and I sat across from her, knees drawn up, hands in my sleeves, the way I used to sit before my mother's fire while she told stories she shouldn't have been telling.

*Ask,* said the matriarch.

"The Ember," I said. "Under Crownspire. The book says it's guttering. It says if it dies, something gets loose. Something called Vaskr."

At the name, every hair along the matriarch's spine lifted. It was a small thing, a ripple, like wind over grass—but I had grown up reading wolves the way other people read weather, and that ripple frightened me more than any snarl could have.

*You say that word as though you learned it yesterday,* she said. *I smelled it before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. I was young then. Younger than you are now.* Her gaze went past me, through me, to something a century away. *The fire under the crown-city was bright, then. Bright enough that we hunted by its glow on the clouds, three valleys south. The two-legs called it their god's lamp. We called it what it was: the last heart of the last dragon, banked but burning, keeping a door shut.*

"A door to where?"

*To nowhere good.* She licked her torn ear, a fussy gesture that did not match her voice at all. *There are things older than hunger, child. Things that were hungry before there was anything to eat. Your kind built walls of stone and story to keep them out, and the strongest wall was fire. Dragon-fire. Because dragon-fire doesn't just burn—it remembers burning. It refuses to forget what it is, and things like Vaskr cannot abide being remembered. They need to be forgotten to slip through.*

"Vaskr got through anyway."

*Not through. In.* Her lips peeled back from yellowed teeth, and it took me a moment to realize it wasn't a snarl. It was grief. *A bargain. A crowned head, a desperate hour—I don't know the shape of it, the two-legs' wars were far away and loud. But I know what the ravens saw afterward: a king who walked differently. Who stopped visiting his wife's grave. Whose shadow moved wrong in torchlight, lagging half a heartbeat behind him, like a dog on a leash it resents.* She shook her great head. *Thirty years the thing has worn him. Thirty years drinking slow from the Ember, because it cannot take the fire whole—the fire remembers, remember? So it drinks. And each year the light on the clouds grows dimmer, and each year my bones ache worse, and the deer grow strange and the birds fly wrong, and the young ones dream of teeth in the dark.*

I wrapped my arms tighter around my knees. Above us the stars were coming out, cold and indifferent, and somewhere south, hidden under a mountain city full of bunting and forced celebration, a king was smiling with someone else's mouth.

"The book says we have to reach the Ember before it gutters. Me and Wren." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "A smuggler and a scholar. Against thirty years of nightmare."

*Books are honest that way,* the matriarch said. *They wait until you're committed, then tell you the rest.* She leaned closer, and her breath washed over me, warm and rank and ancient. *So ask me the true question, little ember-daughter. The one you carried up this valley like a stone in your belly. Ask what reaching the Ember costs.*

My mouth went dry. "Does it cost?"

*It always costs.* Her eyes held mine, amber and pitiless and kind, all at once, the way only old creatures can manage. *Fire is not charity. The Ember keeps the door shut by remembering, and it is dying, and a dying fire needs feeding. Not wood. Not flesh.* She paused, and I watched her choose the words with the same care Maren had used on the trail, and I understood, suddenly, why everyone spoke of this gently. *Memory. A memory, freely given. That is the toll. That is what it has always taken from those who come to feed it. The fire eats what you love best, because what you love best burns brightest.*

The hollow seemed to tilt. The stars wheeled. Somewhere above, a night bird cried and was answered, and the ordinary world went on as though it hadn't just reached into my chest and rearranged the furniture.

"Freely given," I repeated.

*That is the covenant. It cannot take. You must bring it yourself, with your own feet and your own will, and hold it out, and not look back.* Her tail curled around her paws. *The last one who fed it—a priest, a hundred years ago, the one whose book your scholar carries—he gave it his memory of his brother. Walked down the mountain after, alive and whole, and spent the rest of his life wondering why the road home felt so empty.*

I thought of my mother. Of course I did. I thought of her hands, her voice cracking on the hymns, her face through smoke, and my chest clenched around the thought like a fist around a knife—if the Ember wanted that, wanted the one memory I had defended my whole life by hiding, by lying, by running—

But it wouldn't. It would want—

And then I thought of Wren. His terrible, wonderful certainty. The way he'd said *I want to follow it* with his whole ridiculous heart. The page with our names. The kiss the book hadn't written yet but would, someday, in ink that knew us both.

Something went through me then, cold and total, like stepping through ice.

No. No, I wasn't going to think about that. I filed the thought away in the deepest drawer I owned, the one where I kept my mother's name and my gift and every other thing that could get me killed, and I locked it, and I smiled at a wolf.

"Thank you," I said.

*You didn't hear me,* she said softly. *You heard what you needed to survive hearing. The rest will keep.* She rose, stiffly, joints crackling like green logs, and touched her nose briefly to my forehead—an honor, I knew, given perhaps once in a generation. *Go with the ember-daughter's luck, child. And when you stand before the fire—*

"When?"

*If,* she corrected, with a wolf's honesty. *When you stand before it, if you stand before it: do not decide beforehand what you will pay. Decide only that you will pay honestly. The fire can tell the difference between a sacrifice and a transaction, and it despises transactions.*

* * *

I walked back down to the camp with my face arranged into nothing at all. It's a skill. Twenty-three years of practice. My face could sit through a tax inspection, a funeral, and a marriage proposal from Halvard's least favorite client without flickering.

They'd made a fire. Halvard was teaching Pip—for the child had caught up with us at the north gate, having pickpocketed a Purifier scout and decided that wherever trouble went, she went—to whittle, badly. Maren was mending a sock with theological intensity. And Wren sat apart with the Codex open on his knees, and when he saw me he closed it, quickly, guiltily, and I felt the locked drawer in my chest rattle on its hinges.

"Well?" he asked. "What did she say?"

"Wolves mostly talk about elk," I said, sitting down across the fire from him. "Elk, and which of the young ones is courting disgracefully. Honestly, you'd be disappointed."

His eyes searched my face—that careful, watching tenderness again—and for one horrible moment I thought he'd see through it. Scholars see patterns. That's their whole disease. And I was a pattern with a piece missing, and missing pieces cast shadows.

But the fire cracked, and Pip stabbed herself in the thumb and swore with a fluency that made Halvard proud, and the moment passed.

I told no one. Not Maren, who probably already knew. Not Wren, who would have offered—would have insisted—on paying in my place, and the matriarch had told me the truth of that too, in her way: the Ember takes what you love best. It would know if he loved me. It might even know if I loved him, before I did myself.

That was the worst part. Not the price. The price was terrible, but prices are simple—you weigh, you pay, you walk away lighter. The worst part was sitting across the fire from a boy who thought our deaths were the worst thing the book had in store for us, knowing that the book had barely started, knowing that somewhere in those moving pages the Ember's toll was already written, maybe in his handwriting-shaped ink, waiting for him to read it and look at me the way he was looking at me now.

I put another branch on the fire and told myself the heat in my eyes was smoke.

Above the valley, far to the south, the clouds over Crownspire glowed faintly red—the Ember's light, dimmer than any raven remembered it. And beneath it, in a palace full of smiling guards, the king's hunters were sharpening something.

I just didn't yet know it was for me.
