## 5. The Company of the Crooked Road

There is a particular kind of mathematics that governs the life of a scholar, and I had always assumed it was simple: knowledge divided by effort equals wisdom. I had spent eleven years at the University of Vellmar proving this equation wrong in increasingly expensive ways, and it took one week on the run from the king's Purifiers to teach me the correct formula. It is this: *survival equals friends, divided by how many secrets you're all willing to keep at once.*

I was thinking about this — or trying to think about it, which is different — while Astrid Eriksen argued with a mule.

"You can't just *refuse* to cross the ford," she was saying, hands on her hips, in the exasperated tone people usually reserve for children and politicians. The mule — a gray, long-eared creature with the moral convictions of a bishop — stared at her with what I could have sworn was contempt. "The water's knee-deep. *Knee-deep.* I've seen you swim rivers for a stale biscuit."

The mule said nothing, because mules don't talk. But Astrid tilted her head, the way she did when she was listening to something the rest of us couldn't hear, and then her expression shifted from exasperation to something almost like respect.

"He says," she reported, "that the water smells wrong. That there's something upstream that died and he's not carrying our stolen goods past a corpse on principle."

"Is that true?" I asked.

"How would I know? I don't speak mule." She said it loudly, for the benefit of the two smugglers loading the cart behind us, and then, much more quietly, for me alone: "He's probably right. Something did die up there. I can smell it too. Just... less specifically."

This was, I had learned, how it worked with Astrid. The gift was never announced. It was laundered — run through coincidence and guesswork and a well-timed *huh, funny* until the truth of it was so diluted no one could be prosecuted for it. Her mother had burned for less than what Astrid did before breakfast. I thought about that a great deal in those first weeks, watching her spend her impossible self in tiny, deniable installments.

The Codex rode against my chest in its oilcloth wrapping, and I had taken to checking it the way other men check for their purse. It had been quiet since the tavern — quiet in the way a held breath is quiet — and I did not trust it. Books that know your name are not books that stay known.

"We'll ford downstream," Astrid decided, and patted the mule on the nose with genuine affection. "Fine. But I'm noting for the record that you're a diva."

The mule bared its teeth at her, which I have since learned is mule for *noted.*

* * *

Halvard Stone's camp sat in a fold of the Thornfell foothills where the trees grew crooked and the road grew crookeder — a smuggler's camp, all of it technically invisible and none of it actually hidden, because Halvard's philosophy was that the best place to conceal a thing was in plain sight among forty other things that looked exactly like it. He received us sitting on a barrel outside his own tent, a man built like a door that had been left out in the weather, with a beard you could lose a child in and eyes that missed nothing except, famously, the point of most conversations.

"Astrid Eriksen," he said, by way of hello. "You're late."

"The mule had objections."

"The mule is smarter than you. Who's the student?"

"Wren Calloway. Scholar. Sort of."

"Scholars cost money and cause trouble."

"He's already caused the trouble," Astrid said. "He's carrying it."

Halvard looked at me the way a man looks at a suspicious coin — weighing it, testing the edge, listening for the ring of base metal. I did my best to look harmless, which for me has never required much effort, and said, "I can pay."

"Everyone can pay. The question is what happens after the money's spent." He stood, and it was like watching a hill decide to relocate. "Astrid. You're asking me to point my people at the crown. You understand what that means. Not the king — the king's a story we tell taxes with. The *crown.* The thing that's been wearing him for thirty years. You're asking me to bet forty lives on a book."

"And a beast-speaker," Astrid said.

Halvard was quiet for a long moment. I watched him look at her, and I watched her look back, and I understood that this conversation had been happening between them for years, in installments, the way she laundered everything.

"You could have told me," he said finally, and there was no anger in it. Only a kind of tired grief, like a man finding out his favorite knife was never sharp. "All these years. All these roads. You could have told me what you are."

"You'd have had to decide what to do about it," Astrid said. "I didn't want you to have to decide."

"That," said Halvard, "is the most honest cowardice I've ever heard." He scratched under his beard, which was his version of deep thought, and then he said the thing that I would think about for the rest of my life, though I didn't know it yet. "Fine. But hear me, girl. I'm not doing this for prophecy. I don't hold with prophecy — prophecy is just gossip with a better tailor. I'm doing it because thirty years ago I ran brandy for a king who could still laugh, and I'd like to see him laugh again before I die. And because—" he jabbed a thick finger at Astrid, then, inevitably, at me, "—because you two look at each other like a couple of idiots, and idiots in love are the only people in this world who ever actually change anything. Everyone sensible just adapts."

So that was Halvard: a man who claimed to be in it for the money and the memory and the sentiment, in roughly that order, and lied about the order.

* * *

Sister Maren we found — or she found us, which she would insist was theologically distinct — in a border-town chapel that had been deconsecrated so thoroughly it was now a tannery, which she considered a professional indignity but a useful cover. She was sixty, small, and built like a question mark that had been asked too many hard questions, and she was scrubbing hides when Astrid pushed open the door, and she took one look at us and said:

"You're the ones from the book."

I confess I dropped my satchel.

"How—" I began.

"Child, I was exiled for preaching that the crown is a parasite. Do you think I stopped reading when they took my vestments? The Codex of Ash has three copies of its contents circulating in the heretical underground, and I've memorized two of them." She dried her hands on her apron, unhurried, a woman who had made peace with being interesting to very few people and was now adjusting to being interesting to the wrong ones. "The question is not whether you exist. The question is why it took you this long to find me."

"We didn't know you existed," Astrid said.

"No one ever does. That's rather the point of exile." Maren looked me over with eyes the color of a winter sky, and I had the distinct sensation of being read — not my face, but my intentions, which is far more invasive. "You're the reader. The one the book chose."

"It didn't choose me. It just... happened to me."

"Mm. That's what chosen feels like from the inside." She untied her apron with the finality of a woman hanging up one life and reaching for another. "I have waited nineteen years for someone to come ask me about the Ember. Nineteen years of scrubbing hides and preaching to no one. You will forgive me if I don't pretend to deliberate."

"You could deliberate a little," Astrid said. "For appearances."

"Child, I have been *deliberating* since before you were born. The deliberation is done. The answer is yes. Now — tell me everything, and leave nothing out, because I will know, and I will be insufferable about it."

So we told her, there among the hides and the reek of the tannery, and she listened the way scholars only wish they could listen — completely, hungrily, without once interrupting to demonstrate what she already knew. And when we finished, she was silent for a moment, and then she said, softly, in a voice that had clearly been rehearsed in solitude for two decades:

"The Ember is not dying *of* Vaskr. It is dying *for* him. There is a difference, and the difference is everything." Then she picked up a small bundle — her books, her one spare habit, a knife she wore with more piety than skill — and walked out of her exile without looking back.

* * *

Pip we did not find. Pip found us, in the manner of weather.

We were three days out from Halvard's camp, making for the high passes, when a goat wandered into our campsite, ate half of Astrid's spare shirt, and was followed by a child — small, filthy, quick-eyed, perhaps eleven years old, with the unbothered air of someone who has been self-employed since before they could count.

"That's Brindle," the child said, by way of introduction, jerking a thumb at the goat. "She says sorry about the shirt. She doesn't mean it. Goats don't mean anything, it's the beauty of them."

"You talk to goats," Astrid said. It wasn't a question.

"I talk to everything. Goats are just the only ones who answer honestly." The child — Pip, we learned, no surname, or rather several, deployed situationally — looked around the camp with a professional's eye. "You're running from the white-coats. You've got a book that matters. And you're going up the mountain, which is stupid, because the passes are watched." A pause. "I know the unwatched ones."

"Why would you help us?" I asked.

Pip looked at me with the pure, unblinking assessment of a child who has never once been lied to successfully by an adult, and said: "Because you're the first people in two years who didn't try to send me to an orphanage. Also Brindle likes the girl. Brindle's judgment is better than mine."

"Brindle ate my shirt," Astrid said.

"Brindle's judgment is *passionate,*" Pip amended.

Halvard objected, of course. He objected in the full, ceremonial manner of a gruff man who has already lost the argument — pointing out that children get killed on mountains, that pickpockets get hanged, that we were not running a charity. Pip listened to all of it, eating our dinner, and then said:

"You're right. It's dangerous. So's staying in Crownspire's gutters, and there the thing in the palace eats kids like me first, 'cause nobody misses us." A shrug, small and terrible. "I'd rather be missed."

Halvard looked at the child for a long time. Then he said, gruffly, "You eat your vegetables," which in Halvard's language was apparently an adoption ceremony, because Pip beamed like the sun coming up over a profitable heist.

* * *

That night we made a proper camp — a real fire, the first we'd risked in weeks, because Maren argued that a company that hides forever has already lost — and I sat looking around the circle and felt something I didn't have a word for yet.

There was Halvard, whittling a thing that was either a duck or a failure, telling a story about a customs official in Dunmarrow with such relish that two of his smugglers had tears in their eyes. There was Maren, mending Astrid's eaten shirt with the fierce tenderness of a woman who had spent nineteen years with no one to mend things for. There was Pip, asleep sitting up against Brindle's flank, the goat's chin resting on the child's head like a paperweight. There was Astrid, at the edge of the firelight, talking quietly to a raven that had landed on her knee — and for once, for one night, she wasn't hiding it. She was just talking, and the raven was croaking back, and no one in the circle looked away or reached for a torch.

She caught me watching and raised an eyebrow. *Well?* the eyebrow said.

"Nothing," I said. "I was just thinking that I've spent my whole life studying dead things in libraries, and this is the first time I've ever been inside a living story."

"That's the wine," she said.

"There's no wine."

"That's how bad your scholarship is."

I laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the tavern, since the page that knew my name — and around the fire the others laughed too, at nothing, at everything, at the sheer absurdity of a smuggler, a priest, a thief, a beast-speaker, and a fool with a book setting out to unmake a god in a crown. We were, by any rational measure, doomed. The Codex said so. I had read it.

But doom, I have come to believe, is mostly a matter of scheduling, and that night, for one hour, we were simply people around a fire, and it was the richest hour I had ever owned.

Then the raven on Astrid's knee went rigid.

It croaked something — two notes, low and fast — and Astrid went white in the firelight. She listened, head tilted, and I watched the warmth drain out of her face the way water drains from a pulled plug.

"What is it?" I asked. "What did it say?"

She looked at me, and then at Halvard, and her voice came out flat and cold as the mountain stone beneath us.

"It said the white-coats aren't behind us anymore." She stood, and the firelight made her shadow enormous against the trees. "They're ahead. They're at the river ford. And they're not hunting a smuggler's caravan."

Her eyes found mine.

"They're asking after a scholar with a book."
