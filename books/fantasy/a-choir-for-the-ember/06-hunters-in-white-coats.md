## 6. Hunters in White Coats

I've been running cargo through the Thornfell passes for twenty-two years, and in that time I've learned three things worth teaching: never trust a bridge you didn't shake yourself, never gamble with a man who smiles before the dice land, and never—*never*—let a job change shape halfway through.

The job changed shape halfway through. That's how I know I'm getting old.

It started with the girl, Astrid, standing at the head of the column at dawn with her face tilted up like she was listening to something none of us could hear. Which, as it turned out, was exactly what she was doing. But that morning I didn't know it yet. That morning she just looked like a woman smelling rain, and I thought nothing of it, because I'm an honest smuggler and therefore a terrible reader of people.

"The ford's wrong today," she said.

"The ford's been there since before my grandfather," I said. "Fords don't get wrong."

"This one did." She wasn't arguing. That's what unsettled me about her from the start—she didn't argue, she *reported*, like a scout reporting weather. "There are men on the far bank. Eight of them. Maybe nine."

I squinted across the Greywash where it ran shallow and quick over its stones, all white noise and morning mist. I saw water. I saw willows. I saw the kind of empty that makes a man feel good about his choices.

"Your eyes better than mine?"

"Something told me," she said, and would not say more, and I let it go, because in my experience a person who won't explain their luck is usually a person whose luck is better than their explanations.

So we held back. We made cold camp on our side of the river, no fire, and waited for the day to tell us what it wanted. And around midmorning the day told us: nine riders came down out of the trees on the far bank in coats white as fresh paper, and they spread along the waterline like a sentence being written.

Purifiers.

Every smuggler on this side of the mountains knows what Purifiers mean. They don't take bribes—that's the whole horror of them. A customs officer you can buy. a toll-keeper you can charm. A Purifier looks at you the way a laundress looks at a stain, and everything you are is the stain.

"Halvard." Wren Calloway came crouching up beside me, all elbows and scholarly nerves, his book bag clutched to his chest like a child. The boy had been with us five days and had already asked me more questions than my own mother managed in thirty years. "Is that—is those—"

"Purifiers, aye."

"They're looking for someone specific. Solveig's writs—they post them in the university quarter. There's a beast-speaker reported in the Thornfell caravans. That's why—" He stopped himself. I watched him decide not to finish the thought, and I thought: clever boy, and also, coward boy, and mostly, boy who has already guessed something he doesn't want to say in front of the crew.

Because here's the thing I hadn't said out loud either. Five days earlier, on the pass above Keld Hollow, when the rockslide came down and had us trapped like rats in a boot, I'd seen Astrid Eriksen stand in front of forty tons of fallen mountain and speak to it—not shout at it, not pray at it, *speak to it*, the way you'd speak to a mule you'd worked with for years—and the mountain had listened. Rocks had shifted. A gap had opened. And then she'd turned around with a face like a slammed door and dared any of us to mention it.

Nobody had mentioned it. Smugglers are superstitious, but we're superstitious in the practical direction: whatever saved your skin, you thank quietly and don't examine. Still, I'd been chewing on it for five days like a gristle I couldn't swallow. And now nine men in white coats stood on the far bank hunting exactly that, and the clever scholar beside me was choking on the same gristle.

"Options," I said, because that's what captains do; we make options sound like plans.

"Wait for dark?" offered Brenna, who handled our river work.

"Water's too high after dark to cross loaded," Brenna's brother answered himself. "And they'll have watchers upstream."

"Give them the scholar," suggested Oskar, mostly joking, and Wren made a sound like a stepped-on cat, and Pip laughed so hard she fell off the rock she was sitting on, which nearly gave away our position to the entire royal service, which is Pip in miniature.

"We could split," Maren said quietly. She'd gone very still, the way she does when the crown comes up. Our exiled priest had taken one look at the white coats and gone gray. "They'll have a writ. If they board us and find heresy documents—" she patted her satchel, which contained enough contraband scripture to hang us all twice over— "it goes badly regardless of who they're hunting."

"So nobody boards us," I said. "We cross fast, upstream, where the willows break sightlines. We put the river between us and them and we keep moving."

"And if they follow?"

"Then we've got a river, a forest, and a head start." I looked at Astrid. She was still watching the far bank with that tilted-head listening look, and I swear to every crooked god I trade under, her lips were moving. Just slightly. Just barely. "Girl. You got anything to add?"

She lowered her chin slowly. "The bear says we should hurry."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on a man's shoulders like wet wool.

Oskar said, faintly, "The bear."

"There's a bear downstream," Astrid said, in the tone of a woman describing traffic. "Fishing. He says the white-coats smell like ash and iron and he doesn't like them. He says—" she tilted her head again, listening to something we couldn't hear, and something crossed her face that I can only call grief, "—he says he remembers fires. He says they burned things near his den last spring."

Wren had gone white. Not afraid-white. *Understanding*-white. All the pieces falling into place behind his eyes at once—the rockslide, the ravens that always seemed to warn us, the way she stood at dawn listening. I watched him arrive at the truth and I watched him do the single bravest thing I saw him do in the whole cursed affair, which was: nothing. He kept her secret with his face. He just looked at her, and she looked back, caught, exposed, waiting for the flinch—and he didn't flinch. He looked at her like she was a page he'd been searching a library his whole life to find.

Well. I'm getting ahead of myself. There was still a river between us and a firing line.

"Right," I said, into the silence. "New plan. We cross upstream, quiet as ghosts, and nobody tells anybody else's secrets until we're safely rich. Move."

* * *

We almost made it.

That's the epitaph of my whole career, if anyone asks: Halvard Stone, he almost made it.

Upstream the Greywash runs between granite shelves, knee-deep and fast, loud enough to drown footsteps. We went in single file, packs held high, Pip riding on Maren's shoulders with her arms locked around the priest's neck, and the water bit through boots like a knife through cheese. Cold enough to make a grown man sing high notes. We were two-thirds across—I could see the far bank, the willows, freedom and profit on the other side—when the white coats came through the trees.

No shouting. That's the thing people get wrong about Purifiers. They don't bellow warnings or read proclamations. They just appear, and start working, the way a butcher starts working.

Crossbows first. A bolt skipped off the water six feet from Brenna's head and buried itself in a stone shelf with a *crack*. Then another. Then a voice, level and carrying, a woman's voice with frost in it:

"By writ of Inquisitor Solveig, in the name of King Aldric, stand in the water and be counted."

"Counted as what?" I shouted back, because apparently I'd rather die witty than live sensible.

"As what you are."

Astrid had frozen mid-river. Water to her thighs, pack overhead, and I saw her do the arithmetic everyone else was too busy panicking to do: they weren't here for contraband. They were here for *her*, and every soul in this river was standing next to the reason.

"Surrender the speaker of beasts," the frost voice called, "and the rest of you walk free. This is the mercy of the crown."

And that—gods forgive me—is when I understood what kind of company I'd assembled. Because not one of them moved toward her. Not Oskar, not Brenna, not even greedy old Oskar. Pip slid off Maren's shoulders and planted herself in the water in front of Astrid like a fence post, all four and a half feet of her, arms crossed. Wren stepped up on her other side. Maren started praying out loud in the old tongue, the heretical version, the one about fire being holy in itself and not needing a crown's permission.

"Touching," said the frost voice. "But sentiment is not a legal defense."

The crossbows came up. Nine of them. And Astrid Eriksen, thigh-deep in freezing water with death aimed at her, did the thing she'd spent her whole life not doing.

She spoke out loud. To the forest.

"*Brother!*"

Not a scream. A summons. It rolled down the river valley and came back off the granite twice, and the third time it came back, something answered—a huffing, groaning, earth-moving answer, crashing through the willow scrub below the ford, and out of the green burst the biggest brown bear I have ever seen or ever want to see again, wet to the shoulders, salmon-blood on his muzzle, moving like a landslide with opinions.

He hit the shallows between us and the bank. Rose up. Nine feet of him, maybe ten, roaring loud enough to bend the reeds, and I watched nine crossbows reconsider their entire worldview. Two bolts flew anyway—one took the bear high in the shoulder and he didn't even seem to notice, just dropped to all fours and *charged*, and the far bank erupted into screaming white laundry scattering through the trees.

What followed was not a battle. It was an eviction. The bear didn't kill a one of them—he broke a crossbow, broke a wrist, sat on one poor lad hard enough to rearrange his ambitions—and inside two minutes the ford belonged to us and the Purifiers belonged to the horizon.

Astrid waded ashore after him, and what happened next I'll carry to my grave. She knelt in the mud in front of that enormous bleeding animal, and he pressed his great ruined head against her small chest, and she wrapped her arms as far around his neck as they'd go and spoke to him—really spoke, low and continuous, words and sounds I half-caught and half-didn't, thanking him, grieving for him, promising him things—and he answered in that grinding rumble, and it was the most natural conversation I have ever witnessed between any two creatures, and it was the most forbidden thing in the kingdom.

She pulled the bolt from his shoulder with her bare hands and dressed the wound with strips of her own shirt, and the bear let her, sighing like a tired old man, and when she finally stood, she knew. You could see it land on her in stages: the crew staring, the scholar staring, the priest staring, the child staring. Every secret she'd ever kept, drowned in one afternoon in the Greywash.

"I heard," she said finally, to all of us, chin up, hands bloody to the wrist, voice steady as bedrock. "You all heard. So. Now you know what I am."

Nobody spoke. Pip broke it, naturally. "Can you teach me to talk to goats?"

Astrid blinked. "You talk to goats?"

"A bit. They're gossips."

And that was it. That was the whole trial. Oskar shrugged and said the bear could carry his pack anytime, Brenna started counting our lost cargo out loud, Maren touched Astrid's shoulder and murmured something that made her close her eyes, and I said, "Congratulations, you're hired permanent, the pay's terrible," and turned to deal with the boats.

But Wren Calloway walked straight up to her, past the blood and the bear-smell and the enormity of what she'd just done, and said, quietly, only for her, though I heard it because I hear everything:

"You shouldn't have to hide. Not from us. Not ever again."

And Astrid Eriksen—who faced down nine crossbows without blinking—looked at that skinny scholar like he'd struck her. Like kindness was the one weapon she had no defense against. She didn't say anything. But she didn't look away either, and neither did he, and I turned my back on the pair of them because some things aren't meant for a captain's eyes.

* * *

We made eight miles before dark and camped in a hollow of old oaks, exhausted, alive, and changed. The bear—Brother, she called him, Brother—had vanished into the hills with her thanks still hanging in the air, but I noticed the ravens had found us again, three of them sitting in the branches like unpaid sentries.

I took first watch, being old and sleeping badly anyway. Around midnight I gave it up and walked the perimeter, and that's when I saw them: Astrid and Wren, sitting apart at the edge of the firelight, not touching, not even talking much. He was showing her something in that black book of his, and she was leaning in to see it, closer than reading requires, and the firelight was doing that thing firelight does to two people who are trying very hard to look at a book.

I'm a smuggler, not a poet. But I've watched a lot of cargos move in the dark, and I know contraband when I see it. The most dangerous goods in the world are the kind two people smuggle toward each other without knowing it, because there's no price on them until it's too late, and no road back once they're delivered.

I cleared my throat from a polite distance. "Watch change," I said.

They sprang apart like teenagers. I pretended not to enjoy it. Mostly.

"One thing, girl." I crouched by the coals and fed them a stick. "What you did today—it saved us, and it painted a mark on you big as the sun. Solveig doesn't lose people at fords. She writes them down, and she comes back with more coats."

"I know."

"And you—" I pointed the stick at Wren. "Whatever's in that book that started this, it's got her name in it next to yours, or I'm a customs officer. Which means the two of you are cargo now. My cargo. And I have never once lost cargo I decided to protect."

"That's not true," said Pip, from somewhere in the dark. "You lost the entire shipment of candied ginger in—"

"That was a *donation*, small thief, to the local economy—"

They laughed. All of them, waking one by one around the dying fire, laughing at me, with me, together, and I sat there in the smoke and let it wash over me, this ragged company of hersetics and thieves and one beast-speaker and one lovesick scholar, and I thought: well, old man, you've smuggled gunpowder, stolen relics, and a fugitive archbishop's wine collection, but you've never carried anything half so likely to explode.

I was right, as usual. Though I didn't yet know the half of it.

Because just before dawn, one of the ravens dropped out of the oak tree onto Wren's pack, tapped the black book twice with its beak, and said—in a voice like gravel and gate-hinges, clear as church bells, in front of every single one of us:

"*Riders behind you. Riders ahead of you. Choose faster.*"

And nobody screamed. And nobody ran. And Astrid just looked at the bird, and the bird looked at her, and she said, "Thank you, Sister," while the rest of us sat there recalculating everything we believed about the world before breakfast.

The road ahead, I realized, had just gotten a great deal more expensive.
