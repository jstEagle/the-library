## 7. The City Under the Crown

I was exiled from Crownspire eleven years ago for the crime of saying out loud what every priest in the Sanctum whispers into their wine, and I want it noted that I did not weep when I saw the city again. My eyes watered. That is a different thing entirely. It was the smoke. Crownspire has always had smoke from its ten thousand chimneys, and I have always had a sentimental disposition that I disguise as theology.

We came in on the coal road, mixed among the winter carts, because Halvard's smugglers know more ways into a walled city than the city knows gates. Astrid rode wrapped in a shepherd's cloak with a raven on her shoulder that she pretended was a trained pet and everyone pretended to believe. Wren carried the Codex against his chest like a man carrying a bomb, which is more or less what it was. Pip rode in a coal cart, which she declared the best seat she'd ever had, and then sneezed black for an hour, which she declared unfair.

And I rode at the back of the column, watching the crown of the mountain come up over the ridge, and I thought: *I know what lives in that light.*

Because that is the thing about Crownspire that no pilgrim song tells you. The city is built in rings around the mountain's crown, and at the very top, above the palace, above the Sanctum, there is a glow. On clear nights it looks like a second sunset that refuses to finish. They call it the King's Light. They say it is the blessing of the Ascended on Aldric's line. Children are taught to point at it and say *thank you.*

It is a fire. It is the last fire. And it is dying.

* * *

We entered through the Tanners' Gate at midmorning, which Halvard chose because, he explained, the gate captain owes him money and is therefore constitutionally incapable of looking at him too closely. The city swallowed us the way cities do—smell of tallow and wet wool, the cry of pie-sellers, a drunk already singing at an hour that would embarrass a sober man. Pip vanished twice in the first hundred paces and returned both times with her pockets heavier and her face the picture of innocence, which in Pip's case is a masterpiece of forgery.

"Stay close," Astrid murmured, and I realized she wasn't talking to us. The raven on her shoulder had gone rigid, head cocked, listening to something in the air that the rest of us were not invited to hear.

"What is it?" Wren asked.

"Crows," she said. "The city crows. They're... wrong." She said it quietly, but I heard the fear under it. A woman who has spent her life listening to beasts knows when the beasts are frightened, and there is no weather that frightens crows like a crown does.

We took rooms at the Drowned Bell, an inn in the low ring whose proprietor asked no questions because Halvard paid him in the currency of not asking questions, which is the only currency that holds its value in a capital. From our window, if I leaned out and looked up past the smoke, I could see the palace terraces climbing toward that unsetting light.

"It's dimmer," I said.

Wren came to stand beside me. "You can tell? Already?"

"I was ordained in the Sanctum of the Flame, Wren. I spent six years learning to read fire the way you read your book. I know a guttering candle from a healthy one the way a physician knows a sick child from a sleeping one." I pulled my head back in. "That light has lost a third of itself since I last saw it. Maybe more. It doesn't blaze anymore. It *clings*."

"And Vaskr feeds on it," he said. "That's what your heresy says."

"That's what the *truth* says. The heresy was saying it in a pulpit." I sat down on the room's one decent chair, which creaked under me in a way I chose to interpret as solidarity. "Very well. Theology lesson. You've earned it, and Astrid is pretending to nap, and Pip is definitely picking someone's pocket, so it may as well be now."

* * *

Here is what they teach in the Sanctum, the version that got me exiled for teaching it too well:

Before the Ascended were the Ascended, they were dragons. This is not disputed; it is simply not emphasized. The Ember beneath Crownspire is the last of their fires, tended—so the old liturgies say—since the founding, when the first king made a covenant with the fire: *warmth for the city, and the city's faith for the fire.* A dragon's fire is not like our fire. It does not burn wood or coal. It burns *meaning.* Attention. Devotion. The Ember has been fed on worship for a thousand years, and that is why the city has stood through every winter, every plague, every war the lowlands have thrown at it.

But worship is a thin broth. And something else learned to feed at the same table.

"Vaskr," Wren said.

"Vaskr." I let the name sit in the room. Even saying it felt like stepping on consecrated ground in muddy boots. "The old texts call it a crown-thing. A *bindwight*. It is not a demon, whatever the Purifiers scream. Demons come from outside. Vaskr was made *here*, out of us—out of fear and obedience and the particular human talent for giving power away. Thirty years ago it found a king. Or a king found it; the order of events matters less than you'd think. It settled into the crown like a taproot into soil, and it has been drinking from the Ember ever since."

"Drinking it *dry*," Wren said. "The prophecy. *Before it gutters.*"

"Before it gutters. And here is the part that should keep you awake tonight." I leaned forward. "The binding is not a cage, Wren. That's the lie the Sanctum tells itself. A binding is a *diet*. Vaskr is bound to the crown, and the crown is bound to the Ember, and so Vaskr cannot leave, cannot spread, cannot take a second host. But it can *feed*. Every prayer the Sanctum sends up to the King's Light passes through Vaskr on its way to the fire, and Vaskr takes its tithe. And the Ember, which was meant to be fed by a whole city's love freely given, is instead fed by fear, filtered through a parasite. Fear is a poor meal. It keeps the fire alive the way gruel keeps a man alive—technically, and joylessly."

"And when the Ember dies?"

"Then the covenant breaks. And whatever the binding has been *preventing*—" I spread my hands. "Thirty years of a thing wearing a king like a glove, Wren, and the glove is getting thin. When the fire goes out, the binding doesn't just end. It *inverts*. Everything Vaskr has swallowed comes back out."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Then, in the careful voice he uses when he is trying to sound like a scholar instead of a frightened young man, which fools no one: "How long?"

"The fire dims a little more every night. I watched it for six years before my exile and it dimmed a little every year. In the last decade, if it has kept that pace—" I did the arithmetic I had been doing since the mountain, the arithmetic I hated. "A season. Perhaps two. Perhaps less, if fear makes it hungry. And there is a great deal of fear in this city. You'll see."

* * *

We saw it that afternoon.

There was to be a procession—the Feast of the Unwaning Light, one of the high holy days, which the Sanctum had scheduled, I noted sourly, with the timing of an institution that knows its fire is failing and needs the faithful looking elsewhere. Halvard groaned and said processions meant closed roads and closed roads meant no moving cargo, but Wren went pale in the way of a man who has just realized his prophecy book might be about to become relevant, and Astrid's raven screamed once, a sound like a nail drawn from green wood.

So we stood in the crowd on the Processional Way, pressed among furriers and fishwives and a truly alarming number of children on their fathers' shoulders, and we waited, and the bells began.

I have heard the bells of Crownspire my whole life. They are meant to sound like joy. That afternoon they sounded like a summons.

The parade came up the Way under a canopy of white silk, and the crowd roared, and I roared with them because eleven years of exile does not burn the reflex out of you, and then the king came into view, and my throat closed.

Aldric the Third. Thirty years on the throne. I had seen him twice before my exile, from a distance, at ceremonies: a big man then, broad through the shoulder, with his wife on his arm and a laugh that the ballads said could be heard three terraces down. The ballads do not sing of him now.

He rode a white courser that walked as if it were carrying something it was afraid to spill. He was not old—fifty, perhaps, no more—but he sat the horse like a man of eighty, spine curved, head bowed as though the crown itself were a weight of iron instead of gilt. And his face—

His face was the worst of it. It was not a dead face. A dead face would have been a mercy, would have let the crowd pretend. It was a *performing* face. As the horse carried him past our stretch of the Way, he lifted his head, and he smiled, and he raised one hand in the blessing gesture, fingers spread, and the crowd screamed with love.

And his eyes did not move. The smile did not reach anywhere near them. It was a smile painted on a door, and behind the door, something was looking out through the keyhole, and I have stood in the Sanctum's inner dark with only the Ember's light on the wall, and I have read the forbidden pages, and nothing in my whole prepared-for-anything life had readied me for how much I wanted to look away and how completely I could not.

"He sees us," Astrid said beside me. Very quietly. Not to me—to the raven, or through the raven, to whatever the crows of Crownspire were telling her. "The thing in him. It's—looking. Not at the crowd. At—" She stopped.

"At the beast-speaker," I finished, because someone had to say it. "Don't look up. Smile. Wave. We are four loyal subjects enjoying a holy day."

Wren's hand had found Astrid's sleeve. She wasn't looking at the king anymore; she was looking at the ground, breathing the way people breathe when they are holding something down. And the king passed, and the smile held, and just before the white silk canopy swallowed him again his hand came down from the blessing and—this I will swear to on any relic you name—his fingers curled, once, inward, like a man trying to make a fist inside a glove that is not his.

Then he was gone up the Way toward the palace and the light, and the crowd began to sing the hymn, ten thousand voices, and I stood in the middle of it with my heart hammering and did not sing, because I knew the words, and the words were a lie I had been exiled for naming.

*What the fire warms, the crown commands. What the crown commands, the fire feeds.*

* * *

Pip found us at the inn that evening, breathless, coal-smudged, and triumphant, with news that rearranged the whole shape of our doom.

"There's a boy," she said, "a chimney-boy, works the palace flues, and I bought him a meat pie and he told me things, because everyone tells me things, Halvard says it's because I have an honest face, which—" she paused to let us appreciate the joke, and Halvard, who had not laughed in three days, laughed, and I think we were all grateful to him for it. "Anyway. The king doesn't sleep in the royal bed. The servants say he goes down. Every night. There's stairs behind the chapel of the inner keep, down past where the flues end, and the boy's heard—through the stone, he says—you can hear it through the stone—"

She stopped, and for once in her short criminal life, Pip looked frightened.

"Heard what?" Wren asked.

"Singing," she said. "One voice. Every night, same hour. The king, down in the dark, singing to something. And the something sings back."

The fire in the inn's hearth popped. Outside, far above the rooftops, the King's Light shone down through the smoke, and I looked at it, really looked, and it flickered. Just once. Just slightly. The way a candle flickers when a door opens somewhere in the house and a draft finds it.

Or the way a voice falters when it is trying to finish a song.

"The binding thins at night," I said. "That's in the oldest texts—the *Hour of the Thin Veil*, we called it, and the Sanctum turned it into a bedtime prayer so no one would ask what it meant. One hour, every night, when the crown's grip loosens." I looked around the table: Halvard with his arms crossed, Wren with the Codex's outline showing through his shirt, Astrid with the raven's reflection sharp in her eyes, Pip with pie grease still on her chin. My congregation. Gods forgive me, my congregation. "If the king sings in that hour, then some part of Aldric is awake down there. Some part of him has been awake for thirty years."

"Then we're not rescuing a city from a monster," Astrid said slowly. "We're rescuing the man inside it."

"We may be," I said, "if the man inside it still wants rescuing. And if the thing wearing him doesn't already know we've come." I turned to the window, to that unsetting, clinging, dying light. "Because I stood on the Processional Way today, and I watched it smile at ten thousand people, and I am a fair judge of faces, children, and that was not the smile of a king who does not know his enemies are in town."

Above the palace, the King's Light flickered again.

And this time, it did not stop.
