## 8. Choir of Small Gods

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being small: you hear everything.

Grown folk talk right over your head like you're a fence post. Guards walk past you humming. Priests discuss heresies in doorways while you sit on the step picking at your boots, and they'd sooner notice a mouse than notice me — which is funny, because the mice and I are old colleagues by now.

So when the company went to ground in Crownspire's undercity, hiding beneath the crown of a king who'd burn every one of us if he caught us, Halvard grumbled that we were blind as moles. Astrid just looked at me across the campfire and smiled the way she does, like she knows something the rest of the world hasn't been told yet.

"Pip," she said, "how's your rat?"

"Her name is Duchess," I said, "and she's a lady, not a rat."

"How would you feel about recruiting an army?"

I'd felt plenty of things in my nine years. Hungry mostly. But I had never once felt *important*, and that word went through my chest like warm soup through a cold belly.

* * *

The undercity of Crownspire is older than the city above it, which is saying something, because the city above is old enough that its own grandmother is a legend. We holed up in a cistern hall left over from some forgotten dynasty — vaulted ceilings, dry floors, a trickle of clean water running through a stone channel, and enough shadows to hide a regiment. Sister Maren said the old kings built it for the Ember's servants. Halvard said it was built for smuggling, because everything good in this world was built for smuggling. Wren said something scholarly about drainage engineering and everyone ignored him kindly.

My corner was behind a fallen pillar near the water channel, and that's where I conducted business.

Duchess came first. She was a big grey female with one ear chewed short, and she ruled the grain cellars under the tanners' row with an iron paw. I'd known her since I was seven, back when I lived off what fell from market carts and she lived off what fell from mine. We understood each other. She didn't speak words exactly — none of them do, not like Astrid's beasts do it — but they push pictures and feelings into your head, and if you're quiet enough inside yourself, you can push back.

I sat cross-legged in the dark with a heel of bread and let her come to me.

*Duchess,* I pushed at her. *Friends. Many friends. Big work.*

She took the bread with delicate little hands, which I respect enormously in anyone, and sent back a picture: whiskers, whiskers everywhere, a tide of grey bodies pouring down a stairwell. Rats don't think in ones and twos. They think in floods.

*I'll take it,* I thought, and grinned so hard my face hurt.

The pigeons came next, because pigeons will do anything for a fool who feeds them, and I am the biggest fool on this mountain. And then the cats — well. The cats required negotiation. Cats always require negotiation. That's how you know God made them last, after He'd learned everything about pride.

Old Tomcat — we called him Chancellor, because he strutted like one — drove a hard bargain: fish, daily, plus tribute of chin-scratches, plus the understanding that no dog would ever be permitted within his jurisdiction. In exchange, the gutter-cats of Crownspire would watch.

And oh, they watched. You have no idea what cats see. They sleep eighteen hours a day, but their eyes are open for the other six, and they go where no guard thinks to look: rooftops, rafters, windowsills, the laps of bored wives who gossip while they pet them.

Within three days, I knew things about Crownspire that Halvard's whole fleet couldn't have bought with gold.

* * *

I reported to the company around the fire that night, standing on a crate because I'm not ashamed to stand on crates. Standing on crates is how empires get built.

"The rats say there's a grain cellar under the palace kitchens," I announced, "with a wall that doesn't match. Duchess walked it herself. There's a gap at the bottom, big enough for her, and behind it the stones sound hollow."

"Hollow stones," Halvard said, chewing on a pipe he never lit because tobacco costs money. "In a palace wall."

"Rats know hollow walls better than masons do," Astrid said. "They live in them." She was smiling at me again. I decided I liked being important very much and might keep doing it forever.

"The pigeons report the palace roofs," I went on, warming up now, pacing my crate like a general. "There's a courtyard where the guards change shift at dusk, and for the length of the changing — Chancellor says maybe two hundred heartbeats — nobody watches the east stairwell door. The cats say the Inquisitor Solveig keeps white coats hanging outside the prison block to air, which means the prison block has windows, which means—"

"—which means light," Maren finished softly. She'd gone still in that way of hers, hands folded, like a woman listening to a sermon only she could hear. "Child, do you understand what you've built?"

"An army?" I said hopefully.

"A choir," she said. "Every creature in this city singing us its verse. The Ember made them all, you know. Every smallest thing carries a spark of It. Most people forget that." Her eyes were shining. "Most people think prophecy comes from mountaintops and temples. It comes from gutters too."

Halvard snorted. "It comes from smugglers, more often."

"It comes from whoever listens," Astrid said quietly, and looked at me, and I had to look at my boots for a bit because my eyes were doing something embarrassing.

Wren, who had been scribbling notes the whole time because scholars can't help themselves, asked, "Can they count? The rats? Can they tell numbers?"

"Duchess can count to five," I said. "Past that it's all 'many' or 'too many.'"

"That's more mathematics than most Purifiers manage," Halvard said, and even Maren laughed, and for one evening, sitting in our stolen hole under a tyrant's city, we were almost happy.

That's the trick of it, I think, looking back. Doom is heavy, but it's lighter when you split it between enough people. Even the four-legged ones. *Especially* the four-legged ones.

* * *

Over the next week, the network grew teeth.

Duchess mapped the undercity grain routes — every cellar, every culvert, every crack in the foundations of Crownspire, and believe me, the foundations of Crownspire have cracks like an old man has regrets. The pigeons tracked patrols from above, timing their rounds so precisely that Halvard started calling them "the feathered almanac." The cats brought gossip. Cats bring the best gossip. A chambermaid tells a cat everything, because a cat's face never judges.

It was Chancellor who brought the news that stopped the fire talking dead.

He arrived past midnight, trotted straight past my fish offering — which told me everything, because Chancellor has never once ignored a fish — and pressed his forehead against Astrid's knee, which is a cat's way of saying *this concerns you specifically.*

Then he gave her the picture.

Astrid went white. Not pale — *white*, like snow at the top of the pass. Wren caught her arm.

"What? What is it?"

"White coats," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Purifiers. Dozens of them, arriving at the palace gates. And—" she swallowed "—they're asking after a beast-speaker. They have descriptions. Of *me*. From Thornfell. From the rockslide." Her hand closed slowly into a fist. "Someone talked. Someone saw me that day and sold my name."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke. Even Duchess, who'd followed Chancellor in, went motionless on the stones.

"They're sweeping the lower quarters tomorrow," Astrid continued. "Street by street. House to house." She laughed, and it was the worst laugh I ever heard, thin as ice before it breaks. "They're hunting a girl who talks to animals, and the whole animal city is telling me where they'll search first."

"Then we move tonight," Halvard growled, already on his feet, and the meeting turned to maps and whispers and plans drawn in spilled flour on the floorboards.

But I kept hold of Duchess a while longer, there in the dark behind the pillar, scratching her chewed ear the way she likes.

*We won't let them take her,* I told her.

She sent back a feeling I didn't expect. Not comfort. Something older. A picture of a trap closing, and then another picture — a door opening somewhere else, far away, deep down, with warmth coming through it like a hearth seen from a winter road.

*Deep,* I pushed at her. *What's deep?*

Duchess looked at me with those green lamp-eyes, and for the first time since I'd known her, my tough grey lady seemed afraid.

*Down,* she answered. *Something down. Old. Fading. Like a fire going out.*

I didn't know what she meant then. I wish, in a way, that I still didn't. Because the next morning, when Astrid laid out Duchess's map of hollow walls and hidden stairs and whispered, "There's a way down, under the palace itself," I felt proud again — proud of my army, proud of my clever rats.

I didn't yet know that the Codex in Wren's pack was writing pages faster than any of us could read them.

I didn't yet know that one of those pages, written in ink that knew her name, described white-coated men closing around Astrid Eriksen like a net — and that the rats were already whispering to me the first line of it:

*Many coats. Too many. Coming at dusk.*
