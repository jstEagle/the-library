## 9. The Page That Bleeds

There is a particular kind of silence a book makes when it is waiting for you to look at it. Most people never learn to hear it, because most books are only paper and patience. But I had spent three years of my life chasing the Codex of Ash, and somewhere in those years I had learned its silences the way sailors learn weather.

That night, in the loft above Halvard's stable, the Codex was silent the way a held breath is silent.

I want to be clear about something before I go on: I am not a brave man. I am a man who reads. Bravery requires you to act without knowing the outcome, and reading is the opposite of that — reading is knowing every outcome in advance and choosing anyway, which is either cowardice or prophecy, depending on who's asking. So when I sat down on my straw pallet with the Codex on my knees and felt it *waiting*, my hands shook. Not from cold. The loft was warm enough, what with the horses below and Pip asleep in the hay like a small, larcenous barn cat.

I opened it.

The pages had changed.

This should not have surprised me. A book that records your first meeting with a woman word for word — down to the ale she knocked into your lap, down to the exact insult you fumbled — has already forfeited any right to behave like an ordinary object. But there is a difference between a book that remembers and a book that *anticipates*, and I learned that difference at roughly the speed of falling.

The entry was dated tomorrow.

Not "tomorrow" in the vague way prophets use dates, all stars and portents and "when the third moon weeps." Tomorrow, with a morning hour attached. And the handwriting — the same cramped, ash-brown script as every other page — described a place I knew intimately, because I had slept in it last night: the river ford two miles east of Halvard's camp, where the water ran shallow over grey stones and Halvard had sworn on his mother's grave that no king's men had crossed in a year.

*They will come at first light,* the Codex wrote. *White coats, six of them, led by one whose boots do not splash. They are hunting a beast-speaker. They will find her at the ford, because she stops to speak with the heron, because she cannot help herself, because kindness is the trap they have built their whole order around.*

My stomach did something complicated and unpleasant.

*Astrid Eriksen will be taken alive. The scholar will run. The scholar always runs.*

"The scholar," I said aloud, to a book, in a hayloft, at midnight. "The scholar has some notes about that."

Pip stirred in the hay. "You talkin' to me?"

"Go back to sleep."

"You're talkin' to a book." A pause, muffled by straw. "Weirdo." Then she was snoring again, which is a talent I envied profoundly for the next four hours.

Because here is what I did instead of sleeping: I read the entry forty times. I read the entries after it, which continued for another six pages in the same confident hand — the ford, the capture, a cell beneath a palace I'd only ever seen in engravings, an Inquisitor named Solveig whose name made the ink seem darker somehow, though I told myself that was lamplight. I read until the words stopped being words and became a corridor I was walking down alone.

And then I did the thing that every sensible person would have known not to do.

I took out my pen.

Understand my reasoning, such as it was. If the book could write forward, then perhaps the forward-writing was not fixed but provisional — a draft, a rough copy, something amendable. Scholars amend things. It is our entire personality. I had spent my career correcting mistranscriptions in dead men's marginalia; how different could this be? One word. That's all I would change. *Taken alive* to *escapes north*. A single verb. The smallest possible surgery.

I dipped my pen. I touched nib to parchment, over the words *will be taken alive*.

The burn came up through the pen like a shout.

I have burned myself before — candle wax, a cook-pot, once an entire sleeve when I fell asleep reading too close to a lamp, which Halvard now considered my defining anecdote. None of it was this. This was not heat sitting on the skin. This was heat going *in*, as if the fire had opinions about me specifically, as if the book had reached up my arm and taken hold of something with fingers made of noon. I heard myself make a sound I hope never to hear again. The pen dropped. The mark it left on my palm was not a blister. It was a letter — a small, precise character in the Codex's own script, seared clean into the meat below my thumb, the way a brand marks cattle.

I sat there cradling my hand and breathing like a landed fish, and the Codex lay open and innocent, and the entry had not changed by so much as a hair.

*The scholar will run,* it repeated, further down the page, almost gently. *The scholar always runs.*

"No," I whispered. "No, I don't. I'm still here, aren't I? I came back for her at the tavern, I came back for her at the ford plans, I—" I stopped, because I was arguing with a book, and losing.

The worst part — worse than the burn, worse than the letter branded into my hand — was the thought that arrived while I sat shaking, quiet and reasonable as a clerk:

*Maybe this is why you were chosen.*

Not to help. Books don't need help. To *witness*. To stand at the side of the road with his pen and his useless learning and record how it ends. Every prophet I'd ever studied had a companion who survived them. Someone had to carry the story out of the burning building. Maybe that was the whole of my role in this grand design: the survivor. The one who runs.

*The scholar always runs.*

I did not sleep. I sat with the Codex closed on my knees and my branded hand curled against my chest, and I watched the roof beams go grey with dawn, and I hated myself with a thoroughness that would have impressed my old tutors.

* * *

Astrid found me at first light, because of course she did. She had a way of arriving exactly where the bad thing was, like weather.

She took one look at my face and said, "What did the book do?"

"How do you know the book did anything?"

"Because you look like a man whose book did something." She crouched in front of me. Down in the yard, Halvard was shouting the caravan awake, and the sound of it seemed very far away. "Wren. Show me."

So I showed her. The palm, the brand, the small ash-colored character sitting in my skin like it had paid rent. And then, because hiding things from Astrid Eriksen had stopped being possible around the second week of knowing her, I showed her the page.

She read it standing, very still, the way she went still around animals — that total, listening quiet. Her eyes moved down the entry once, twice. At the line about the heron her jaw tightened. At the line about being taken alive, nothing moved in her face at all, which frightened me more than flinching would have.

"When?" she said.

"First light. Which is — it's now, Astrid, it's *now*, we have to—"

"I know what time it is." She handed the book back with strange care, as if returning someone else's child. "Six of them. White coats."

"Purifiers. Yes."

"And one whose boots don't splash." She looked east, toward the ford, toward the thin ribbon of river visible between the trees. "Inquisitors wear oiled leather on the soles. My mother told me. Before." The word *before* did what it always did when she used it — opened a door and shut it again in the same breath.

"We can go around," I said. "North crossing, the gorge — Halvard said it adds two days but—"

"They'll expect the gorge. It says so." She tapped the page without touching the ink. "It says everything, Wren. It says I stop to talk to a heron. Do you know what that means? It means even my stupid little kindnesses are written down. Even the parts of me I thought were mine."

"Astrid—"

"Is that what you were doing last night?" Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "Trying to change it?"

The guilt arrived on schedule. I had rehearsed this confession through the grey hours — rehearsed it, abandoned it, rehearsed it again — and now that the moment was here, all my careful sentences dissolved and left only the true ugly thing underneath.

"I tried to rewrite it," I said. "One word. I tried to change *taken alive* to *escapes north*, and it — the book burned me. It put its letter in my hand. Like it was telling me the price of editing." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "And then I sat there thinking, maybe that's the joke. Maybe whoever — whatever chose me didn't choose a hero. It chose a witness. Someone to watch you get taken and live long enough to write it down. The scholar always runs. It says that too, did you see? Twice. It's very confident about me."

"Wren."

"I've been sitting here since before dawn trying to work out whether telling you helps or just makes you slower, and I genuinely don't know, because everything I know comes from a book that appears to be *writing us*, and I—"

"Wren." She took my branded hand. Gently, the way she handled hurt birds, and I suppose that's what I was at that moment — a hurt bird with a pen. She turned the palm up and studied the little seared character, and her thumb passed over it once, feather-light, and the burn did not hurt at all under her touch, which I decided not to mention because it felt like too much to admit before breakfast.

"Listen to me," she said. "You tried."

"Tried what?"

"To change it. You saw a bad thing coming and you tried to scratch it out with a pen. That's the bravest stupid thing anyone's done since I met you, and I met you the day you wore an entire tankard of ale." Her mouth did the thing that wasn't quite a smile but lived next door to one. "The book says you run. Fine. Let it. Runners come back. I've watched you do it three times now."

"That's not — the point is I might be *chosen to fail*, Astrid. There's a difference between failing and being built to fail, and I don't know which one I am, and I dragged you into this, I showed you the page that first night, I'm the reason you know any of—"

"Wren Calloway." She said my full name the way Halvard said *cargo*, with weight and ownership. "If you finish that apology I'm going to feed you to the heron."

I closed my mouth.

"You didn't drag me anywhere," she said. "I was already running. I've been running since I was nine years old. All you did was hand me a map with an ending on it, and yes, the ending is terrible, and yes, I'd like to burn the book most mornings. But you also stayed. You're staying right now, covered in your own failure, telling me all of it instead of hiding it like everyone else in my life has ever done." She squeezed my branded hand, carefully, on the unburned side. "Forgiven. You were forgiven before you started. Now stop apologizing and help me think."

"But I—"

"Forgiven. *Before.* Move on."

Down in the yard, Halvard bellowed something about wheels and idiots, and Pip's voice rose cheerfully over it, accusing a goat of theft. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who did not yet know that six men in white coats were already moving through the trees toward a shallow ford and a woman who couldn't walk past a heron without saying good morning.

Astrid was already turning away, pulling her hood up, and I saw with a lurch what she meant to do.

"You're going to the ford anyway."

"I'm going to the ford," she agreed.

"It *says*—"

"It says a lot of things." She glanced back at me, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not fear, not resignation. Defiance, aimed upward, at whatever kept the ledger. "It says I'm taken alive. Well. Books have been wrong before." A pause. "Once. Probably. Somewhere."

I scrambled up, slinging the Codex into my satchel, and followed her down the ladder because I am, whatever the ink claims, constitutionally incapable of letting her walk toward a trap alone.

At the bottom, Halvard caught my sleeve. He'd gone still in the way that meant he'd already read the sky, or Astrid's face, or both. "Trouble?"

"Purifiers," I said. "At the ford. First light."

He didn't ask how I knew. He'd stopped asking that question days ago, which was its own kind of faith. He just turned and roared at his smugglers in a voice that scattered birds off the eaves, and began, with the calm of a man rearranging furniture in a burning house, to lie to his crew about the route.

Astrid waited for me at the gate, hood up, her raven — the one she called Ash, because her sense of naming was as subtle as her sense of self-preservation — shifting on her shoulder with its head cocked toward the east.

"It knows we know," she said quietly. Not to me. To the bird, or to the morning, or possibly to the book itself. "Let's see what it writes about *that*."

Ash croaked once, low, and I swear the sound had a shape to it, and the shape sounded like a warning.

We walked toward the ford together, and behind us, in my satchel, I felt the Codex go silent again —

the held-breath silence,

the waiting-for-you-to-look silence —

and I did not dare open it to see what it had written about the next hour.

But my branded hand throbbed in time with my heartbeat, and each pulse spelled the same small ash-colored truth:

*somewhere, it is already finished writing.*
