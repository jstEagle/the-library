## 10. First Kiss, Last Warning

The thing about rooftops is that nobody looks up. That's the whole trick of them. People spend their lives staring at each other's boots and never once think to tilt their heads back, which is why I've spent half my life on roofs and the other half wishing I were on one.

Tonight Crownspire was wearing its winter best. Frost had crept up the slate tiles in fern-patterns, and below us the city glowed like a hearth banked for the night—ten thousand windows breathing yellow light into the dark, smoke rising from a thousand chimneys in straight, frozen columns. Somewhere down there King Aldric was parading through his forced festival, smiling his hollow smile at crowds who clapped because not clapping had consequences. Up here there was only the cold, the stars, and Wren Calloway sitting close enough that our shoulders touched.

He'd brought blankets. Of course he'd brought blankets. The man planned everything—except, apparently, how to sit next to someone without turning the color of a boiled beet.

"You're shivering," he said.

"I'm always shivering. It's called being alive in Thornfell."

"I have—" He patted the blanket pile like it might bite him. "There are extra."

"Wren." I took one before he could strangle himself with generosity. "Breathe. You look like you're about to defend a thesis."

"This is considerably harder than a thesis," he muttered.

I laughed, and the laugh came out softer than I meant it to, and that was the trouble with Wren Calloway. Everything with him came out softer than I meant it to. Three weeks ago I'd wanted to burn his precious book. Two weeks ago I'd been teaching him which berries wouldn't kill him while he lectured me about marginalia. A week ago Halvard had caught us laughing by the fire and given me a look over the stew pot that said *oh, you're doomed*, and the worst part was that he'd been right.

Below us, the festival drums thumped their hollow rhythm. Above us, the stars did what stars do—burned without asking anyone's permission.

"Do you ever think about it?" Wren asked. "The book. What's written."

"Constantly. Usually while trying very hard not to."

"The kiss is in there, Astrid. Page two hundred and six. I checked again this morning. It doesn't say when. Just—" His voice did the thing it did when he was reciting, going precise and careful, like words might crack if he handled them wrong. "*'On the high places above the frozen city, the beast-speaker and the witness will finally stop pretending.'*"

"Stop pretending," I repeated.

"That's all it says."

"Huh." I pulled my knees up, watching our breath tangle in the air between us. "You know what bothers me most? Not that it knows we'll kiss. That it bothered to write it down at all. Like some monk a hundred years dead sat at his desk thinking, *yes, this bit is essential to the fate of the kingdom.*"

"Maybe it was."

"Maybe nothing's essential. Maybe the book just likes gossip."

Wren laughed—that surprised bark of his, the one that escaped before his dignity could catch it. And then neither of us said anything for a while, which was its own kind of conversation. The kind I was better at than talking.

I should explain something about me. I talk to beasts. Wolves, ravens, bears, once a very judgmental mule named Bartholomew. And every creature I've ever spoken to has one thing in common: they mean exactly what they say. A wolf wants meat and pack and territory, and says so. There's no subtext in a raven. No wolf has ever looked at me across a rooftop and thought forty-seven things and said none of them.

Humans are exhausting.

And yet here I was, exhausted willingly, on a freezing roof, watching a scholar pretend to study the constellations because looking at me directly had become too dangerous for him.

"Pip asked me today," Wren said finally, "if we were married."

"*What.*"

"She said—and I quote—'you argue like married folk but worse, 'cause married folk know what they're mad about.'" He was smiling into his collar. "Then she charged me a copper for the observation. She's charging everyone coppers now. Halvard paid two to hear about Maren's heresy trial. I think she's building a war chest."

"She's building an empire. Good. Someone in this company should have financial sense." I hesitated. Then, because the roof makes people honest: "What did you tell her?"

"That it wasn't any of her business. Then I gave her three coppers not to ask again."

"And did she take them?"

"She took them and immediately told Solveig's patrol route anyway. She said information flows freely; questions cost extra."

I laughed properly this time, head back, and the sound went up into the cold where nobody could tax it. And when I came back down, Wren was looking at me. Not at the stars. At me. With that expression he got sometimes—the one that made me feel like a text he couldn't put down, except kinder than that, warmer than that, like maybe he was the one being read.

"Astrid," he said. "Can I ask you something and you won't make a joke?"

"That's a big ask. That's my entire defensive architecture."

"Please."

The *please* undid me. It usually did.

"Ask," I said.

He turned to face me fully, cross-legged, blanket around his shoulders like a nervous bat. "Everything the Codex has written so far has come true. The rockslide. The meeting. The hunters. Your capture—it hasn't happened yet, but it will, we both read it. And I keep wondering..." He stopped. Started again. "When you look at me. When we—when I—" He exhaled in frustration. "Words are supposed to be my whole profession and I can't—"

"Try smaller words," I said gently.

That earned me half a smile. "Do you feel things because they're true? Or because they're written?"

* * *

There it was. The question under all the other questions, the one I'd been stepping around for weeks like ice over deep water.

Because here's the truth: I didn't know. Every time my heart did something stupid around Wren Calloway, a small cold voice in me asked whether it was mine or the book's. Whether I'd fallen for him or simply arrived, on schedule, at a scene some dead monk had blocked out centuries before I was born. Whether love could be prophecy, or only its puppet.

A wolf would never have to wonder this. A wolf loves and eats and grieves without footnotes.

"I don't know," I said. Because lying to him felt worse than the truth, even the useless truth.

Something flickered across his face—not hurt, exactly. Recognition.

"Me neither," he admitted. "Every time I want to hold your hand, I think: page two-oh-six. Every time you laugh at something I say, I check inside myself to see if the laughter came first or the ink did." He looked out over the city. "It's like being handed a gift and then finding the receipt."

"That's the most scholar sentence ever spoken."

"It's the truest sentence I own."

I studied him. The frost catching in his hair. The way his hands were wrapped around each other like they needed restraining. The way he'd crossed half a kingdom chasing a book and somewhere along the way stopped caring about the book more than the people carrying it.

And I thought about my mother. About the last thing she ever told me before they took her: *"They can burn the speaker, little bird, but not the speech. What's true stays true no matter who says it or why."*

"What's true stays true," I murmured.

"What?"

"My mother used to say that. About my gift. That it didn't matter who hated it or feared it—the speaking was still real. The wolves were still saying what they said." I turned to face him. "Maybe it works for this too. Maybe it doesn't matter if the book wrote it first. If it's written because it's true, instead of true because it's written—then the order doesn't matter. The feeling is still ours. We'd have felt it anyway. We just... found out early."

"You believe that?"

"No," I said honestly. "But I want to. And wanting it feels like mine. Nobody wrote down my wanting. I'd bet my life the monk didn't put *'and on the high places, Astrid Eriksen decided of her own free will to be brave'* in the margins."

"He might have," Wren said. "He seems thorough."

"If he did, I'll haunt him. I know how to speak to dead things too. Probably. Haven't tested it."

Wren laughed, and the laugh broke something loose in the space between us, some last plank of pretense falling away, and suddenly the distance between us was very small and very loud.

"Astrid," he said. Quiet now. No thesis-defense stammer. Just my name, held carefully, the way you hold something that might fly off.

"Yes."

"If I kiss you, and it turns out the book made me do it—I need you to know that I was going to do it anyway. Book or no book. Since the tavern. Since you threatened to feed me to a mule."

"Bartholomew would have refused. He's picky about scholars."

"Astrid."

"Yes," I said. "Kiss me before I find another joke. I've got dozens. It's a bottomless supply."

So he leaned in, slow enough that I could have stopped him, fast enough that I couldn't talk myself out of it, and kissed me.

* * *

Here is what I expected: fireworks, choir music, the heavens opening. Here is what actually happened: warmth. Just warmth, and the taste of cold air and bad tavern tea, and Wren's hand coming up to cup my jaw like I was a page he was afraid to smudge, and the shocking, simple fact of being kissed by someone who knew about the wolves and the ravens and the price waiting under the mountain, and kissed me anyway.

No fear in it. That was the thing that undid me. Everyone who'd ever learned my secret had stepped back from me—even Halvard, bless him, had taken a full day. Wren leaned *in*. Had been leaning in since the tavern.

When we broke apart, our breath mingled and hung between us like a small ghost, and Wren had the dazed expression of a man who'd walked into a door he'd been walking toward for weeks.

"Oh," he said.

"Yeah," I agreed.

"That was—"

"Don't analyze it. Scholars ruin everything by analyzing it."

"I'm going to analyze it later. Privately. Extensively. Possibly in footnote form."

"There it is."

I rested my forehead against his, and we stayed like that while the festival drums thumped their empty rhythm far below, and for one whole minute I forgot to be afraid. Forgot the Ember guttering under the mountain. Forgot Vaskr wearing our king like a coat. Forgot the pages that described my capture, my cell, whatever price waited at the end of this road. For sixty seconds I was just a woman on a roof kissing a man who smelled like ink and cold, and it was the freest I had felt since my mother's arms.

Which is, of course, exactly when the universe collects its debts.

Because here's what I didn't know yet. Couldn't have known. Miles away, back at camp in the smugglers' cellar where we'd left our gear, the Codex of Ash lay closed in its oilcloth wrapping. And at the precise moment Wren's lips met mine—before, actually, by the count Pip would later swear to—the book opened itself. Just a crack. Just enough. And fresh ink crawled across page two hundred and six in that patient, ancient hand:

*"On the high places above the frozen city, the beast-speaker and the witness will finally stop pretending. She will taste of cold air and cowardice overcome. He will think, absurdly, of footnotes. Neither will know that the ink dries as they breathe."*

Word for word. Before.

Pip found it an hour later, doing her rounds, and came running up the mountain path with a lantern and no sense of stealth whatsoever, screaming our names hard enough to wake the ravens.

We were still on the roof when she crashed through the trapdoor, red-faced, breathless, waving the book like a fishwife with a herring.

"It did it AGAIN," she panted. "It wrote your kiss BEFORE you kissed! I watched the ink come! Well—I heard it. Rats told me. Rats can't read, obviously, but they know what writing looks like, it looks like little scratches, and the book was making scratch-sounds all by itself—" She thrust the Codex at Wren. "Read it. Read the part about the footnotes. How'd it know about the footnotes?"

Wren read. I watched his face do several complicated things.

"'She will taste of cold air and cowardice overcome,'" he read aloud, faintly. "'Neither will know that the ink dries as they breathe.'"

Silence, except for Pip breathing like a bellows and the distant drums.

"So it watched us," I said slowly. "Or it *is* us. Or—" My voice came out flatter than I intended. "It wrote my kiss down before I felt it, Wren. Which means the feeling existed before I felt it. Which means—"

"Don't." He closed the book, gently, the way you'd close a coffin. "Don't finish that sentence. Because I'll answer it with the only argument I have left, which is this: the book also said we die. Books don't get to decide that. Books don't get to *have* you." His hands were shaking, and not from cold. "Whatever it wrote, it wrote about something real. The real thing happened first in *us*, Astrid. Ink is just... memory that got there early."

"Ink is memory that got there early," I repeated. "Put that in your footnotes."

"It's already in my footnotes."

Pip looked between us with the naked disgust of a twelve-year-old witnessing affection. "This is why I charge coppers," she announced, tucked the Codex under her arm like contraband, and vanished back down the ladder, leaving us alone with the frost and the stars and the terrible new arithmetic of our lives.

* * *

We stayed until the fires below burned low. And I wish I could say I held the warmth the whole night through, but the truth is the cold voice found me again near dawn, when Wren had gone down to relieve Halvard's watch and I sat alone with the city smoking beneath me.

Because the book hadn't only written the kiss. Pip, in her hurry, had read the whole page aloud before leaving—children have no reverence—and there'd been more after the footnotes line. A short entry. Four words of it, in the same patient hand, dated to tomorrow:

*"The white coats come."*

I sat on the roof edge and listened to the city breathe and thought about my mother in her burning, and about the price the Ember exacts—a memory freely given, the old wolf-matriarch had said, and I still hadn't told a soul—and about a scholar whose kiss tasted like cowardice overcome.

Tomorrow, the white coats.

I sent a raven to warn the camp. Then I climbed down to break the news, rehearsing all the way how to tell Wren Calloway that the book had given us one perfect hour on a roof—and billed us for it.

The white coats were already moving.
