## 11. The Purifier's Net

I've been a smuggler for thirty years, and in that time I've learned exactly three things worth passing on. One: never trust a man who smiles before breakfast. Two: the mountain always wins, so bribe it early. Three: if a job starts feeling like destiny, cut the ropes and row.

We were three days out of Crownspire's shadow, camped in a hollow above the Graywater ford, and I was feeling destiny all over me like wet wool.

It started with the ravens. Astrid had been talking to them since dawn—not *talking*, of course, because talking to birds gets you burned, but standing at the treeline with her head cocked like a woman listening to a conversation only she could hear, which was precisely what she was doing. I'd known her since she was nine years old and lied to my face about a magpie with the confidence of a customs officer. I'd never once caught her at it. But I'd watched her for fifteen years the way you watch weather, and I'd made my peace with the truth I didn't say out loud: my beast-speaker talked to beasts, and I would gut any man who made her regret it.

"Ravens say white cloaks on the north road," she told me, crouching by the cold fire. "Six of them. Maybe eight. They're not in a hurry."

"Nothing about Purifiers is ever in a hurry," I said. "It's their whole aesthetic. Dread travels better at a walk."

She almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "They're looking for a beast-speaker."

"Then we put you on a mule and point you south and—"

"No." She said it the way she said everything that mattered: quiet, flat, final. "The Codex says—"

"I don't care what the book says. The book's been wrong about exactly nothing so far, which is exactly why I don't care for it."

Wren Calloway came out of the trees at that moment, arms full of kindling, looking like a scholar who had been told he could go outdoors and had taken the instruction too literally. He'd been carrying that Codex against his chest like a second heart for a week now, and I'd noticed he'd stopped sleeping. When a man stops sleeping over a book, the book is winning.

"The ford," Wren said, dropping the kindling. "The page updated this morning. I didn't want to say anything in front of Pip."

"Say it in front of me, then. I've got a face for bad news. It's the only face I've got."

He sat down heavily on a root. "It describes the ford. It describes *her* being taken. White cloaks, a net of cold iron, an inquisitor with gray in her braid." He looked at Astrid, and I watched the boy try not to fall apart in front of us, and I'll admit the boy had grown on me, the way a splinter grows on you. "It names Solveig."

Astrid went very still. I'd seen her still before—around wolves, around the Ember-talk, around anything that touched her mother. Stillness was her armor. She'd learned it young.

"You know the name," I said.

"My mother did." Her voice was careful, like walking on river ice. "Solveig Vane. She trained under her, before. Before the pyre." She looked up at me, and there it was, the thing she never asked for because asking was a debt and debts got people burned. "Halvard."

I sighed the sigh of a man who already knew how this was going to go, because I'd known since the ravens. "You're going to say we should split up."

"I'm going to say you should split up. Take the cargo south, take the crew, take Wren—"

"The book says I'm there too," Wren said miserably. "I'm in the net. It says I fight. It says I'm *bad at it*."

"You are," I said. "I've seen you swing a sword. It's like watching a man argue with a door."

"Halvard." Astrid's voice cracked, just a hairline, just enough. "Eight Purifiers. Cold iron. If they take me, they take me. But if they take all of you—"

"Then here's what's going to happen," I said, and I stood up, because this was a standing-up speech, and I'd earned maybe four of those in my life and this was going to be one of them. "You're going to tell me the plan you've already made, and I'm going to improve it, and nobody is splitting up, because splitting up is what a captain does with cargo, and you—" I pointed at her, and my finger was not entirely steady, which I'll deny to my grave, "—you are not cargo. You're the reason half these idiots are still alive. So is he. So is the goat-talking pickpocket asleep in the wagon. We are a company of the Crooked Road, and crooked roads don't fork. They just get worse."

Sister Maren, who had appeared the way she always did, silently and at the worst moment, said, "That was almost a sermon, Halvard. Careful. That's how heresy starts."

"Good. I could use a second heresy. The first one's working out fine."

* * *

The ford at Graywater was a wide, shallow sprawl of gray stone and colder water, the kind of crossing where you can see trouble coming for a mile and it comes anyway, because there's nowhere else to go. That's why it's a ford. That's why it's also, historically, where people get killed.

I'd spent the morning being wrong on purpose. That's a smuggler's art—looking like a fool so nobody checks the fool's wagons. We came down to the water loud and slow, Halvard the fat merchant with his mules and his complaints, and I complained beautifully. I complained about the tolls, the weather, the price of oats, my feet, my back, the government, and the government again for good measure. Pip rode in the second wagon under a tarp with the good brandy and instructions to stay down and stay quiet, which for Pip was like asking the river to flow uphill.

They let us get halfway across before they showed.

White cloaks, eight of them, out of the alders on the far bank, moving in that unhurried Purifier way, like the law of the land made flesh and mildly bored. And in front of them, a woman on a gray mare, gray braid, gray eyes, a face like a door that had been locked for thirty years and had forgotten there was ever a key.

Inquisitor Solveig Vane.

I'll tell you what I felt, since this is my chapter and honesty's cheap when the reader can't interrupt. I felt the profit die. I felt thirty years of careful, greedy, cowardly arithmetic come due all at once, because here's the sum I'd been avoiding: everything I owned—the fleet, the routes, the name that opened doors in four duchies—was worth exactly nothing in a world where they burned Astrid Eriksen. I'd known it for years. I'd just never had to hold the number up to the light.

"Halt the crossing," Solveig called. Her voice carried over the water like it had been issued by the crown, which in a way it had. "By writ of the Purification, this company is detained for questioning."

"Questioning!" I shouted back, all wounded commerce. "Inquisitor, I'm a licensed trader with papers from the—"

"I know who you are, Halvard Skalle." She rode her mare into the shallows, and the white cloaks fanned out along the banks, cold iron nets coiled on their arms like sleeping snakes. "I've known you for years. I've simply never cared enough to stop you. Your cargo today is not brandy."

Behind me, very quietly, Astrid stepped down from the wagon into the river.

"Don't," I said, without turning. "Astrid. Don't you dare."

"It's the ford or the pyre, Halvard." Her voice was calm. Her hands were shaking, and only I could see them. "Eight of them. If I run, they hunt the whole company to find out why I ran. If I stand—"

"If you stand, they take you."

"The book says they take me anyway."

"And I'm telling you the book can go to—" and that's when the net came out, and everything went to the flames all at once.

They were fast, the Purifiers. I'll give the bastards that. Two of them came across the shallows at a dead run and the net opened in the air like a gray flower, and Astrid didn't run, didn't scream, didn't do any of the things a sensible person does. She stood in the waist-deep water, threw her arms wide, and *spoke*.

I heard it as a sound like wind through a throat. Wren told me later what she actually said, and I've decided not to repeat it, because some words are hers. But the mountain heard. The forest heard. And out of the alders above the north bank came a brown bear the size of a granary, moving like the mountain itself had stood up and taken offense.

I want it on the record that the Purifiers broke. Not all of them—Solveig sat her mare like a stone saint—but four of eight turned and ran, and there's no shame in it. There's a bear, and then there's a bear that a woman in the river is *talking to*, and one of those things you train for and one of them you don't.

The net came down anyway.

Cold iron, they make it in the palace foundries, blessed and quenched in something that isn't water. It hit Astrid like the sky falling. She went under, and the bear roared and charged the wrong men, and Wren Calloway—scholar, heretic, a man who argued with doors and lost—waded in swinging a boat oar and got flattened for his trouble, exactly as the book had promised. Pip burst out of the tarp like a furious cat and bit a Purifier's ankle, which accomplished nothing and which I will treasure forever. Maren stood on the bank with her arms raised, singing, of all the damned things, singing that heresy of hers at the top of her voice while the world ended around her.

And Solveig Vane rode her mare through the shallows, dismounted, and knelt in the water beside the net.

I was twenty feet away, held by two men who'd decided I was more valuable alive, and I saw her face. I'd expected triumph. I expected the cold satisfaction of the hunter. What I saw was worse. I saw a woman looking down at a soaked, struggling, furious girl in a net of cold iron, and I saw her look *old*. I saw her mouth move, and the wind took most of it, but I caught three words, and they weren't a prayer and they weren't a sentence.

They were: "You look like her."

They took Astrid. Of course they took Astrid. The book said so, the ravens said so, the whole rotten architecture of destiny said so. They put her in a wagon with bars and rode north toward Crownspire, and Solveig looked back once—at the bear, at the burning wreck of our crossing, at Wren sitting in the shallows with blood in his hair and an oar in his hands and a look on his face I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors for fifteen years. The look of a man doing arithmetic.

* * *

That night we camped in the ruins of an old watchtower, because ruins are honest about what they are. Pip had stopped crying, which worried me more than the crying. Maren was praying, or singing, or whatever Maren did that was both. Wren sat with the Codex open on his knees, and he wasn't reading it. He was staring at it like it was a snake that had bitten someone he loved.

"So," I said, and I stood up, because apparently this was going to be a standing-up speech day, and a man gets maybe four in a life and I was already over budget. "Here's where we are. Our cargo is confiscated. Our route is burned. Every coin I've salted away in four duchies is now worth nothing, because I am officially a smuggler whose beast-speaker got taken by the crown, and there's no coming back from that. I'm ruined. Professionally, financially, and in the eyes of every guild master from here to the coast."

Pip sniffled. Maren looked up. Wren closed the book.

"So," I said, "we're going to need a new business model."

I turned to the road, north, toward Crownspire, toward the city with the crown on it and the thing under the crown, and I pointed at it like I was giving it the finger, which morally I was.

"Rescue," I said. "It's a growth industry. Terrible margins, but the benefits are unbeatable."

Wren made a sound that was half laugh and half sob, which I've decided is the official sound of the Company of the Crooked Road. Pip stood up and wiped her face and said, "I know the sewers," in the voice of a child offering a knife to a war, and Maren said, "I have heretical contacts in every parish," and Wren said, "The Codex says—" and stopped himself, and I watched him decide, right there, that the book was a map and not a master.

"Good," I said. "Then here's the plan. We go to the fleet. Every boat, every cousin, every debt owed to Halvard Skalle, and there are more than you'd think, because I'm a sentimental old fraud and I've been collecting favors for thirty years like other men collect coins. We turn the whole crooked fleet north. And we take back our beast-speaker."

I looked at them, my idiots. The scholar with the blood in his hair. The child with the knife. The priest with the heresy. The bear was somewhere in the dark, following us at a distance, because it had decided we were Astrid's, and Astrid was ours, and that was apparently enough for a bear.

"Anybody want out?" I asked. "Now's the time. No shame in it. There's a road south and it's a good road."

Nobody moved. Pip yawned. Maren went back to her singing. Wren looked north.

"Right," I said, and my voice did something I'm going to blame on the smoke. "Then somebody wake me at dawn. And somebody keep an eye on that bear. If it's going to be family, it can at least take a watch."

Far off in the dark, the bear huffed, and I swear on every coin I've ever stolen that it sounded like a yes.
