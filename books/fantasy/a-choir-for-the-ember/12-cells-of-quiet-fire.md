## 12. Cells of Quiet Fire

They took my boots first.

I want you to understand that I have been robbed by professionals. Halvard's crew once stripped a toll-house to its floorboards in the time it takes a bishop to clear his throat, and even they would have paused at the indignity of the Purifiers' inventory ritual: my boots set side by side on a shelf, laces coiled with military precision, as though the boots themselves might attempt escape and needed to be discouraged.

"Name," said the woman at the desk. She had the flat voice of someone who has written down ten thousand names and expects to write down ten thousand more.

"Astrid Eriksen."

"Gift?"

"No gift." The lie came smooth as river water. It had been coming smooth for thirty years, since the night my mother told me that the word *beast-speaker* was a death sentence delivered in two syllables, and then went to her own pyre proving it.

The clerk wrote something anyway. They always do. In Crownspire, suspicion is a form of bookkeeping.

Then they marched me down. And down. And *down*—past levels where the torches gave out and the light came from somewhere else entirely, a faint gold seep in the stone like honey pressed through cloth. The stairwell grew warm. My jailers grew quiet, the way people go quiet near a sickbed.

I thought: *this is wrong.* Prisons are cold. Everyone knows prisons are cold; it is half their function. But this one was warm as a hearth-room, and the warmth had a pulse to it, slow and vast, and when I put my palm against the wall—because I am, apparently, constitutionally incapable of not touching things—the stone hummed against my skin like a cat the size of a mountain.

One of the guards saw me do it. He flinched. Not at me. At the wall.

*Interesting*, I thought, which is what I always think right before everything goes catastrophically sideways.

* * *

My cell was clean, warm, and windowless, which meant it was either the nicest prison in the Nine Holds or the worst kind of lie. A cot. A bucket behind a screen. A slot in the door through which bread appeared twice a day, dense enough to drive nails with.

I did what any sensible smuggler does in a cell: I made friends with the local wildlife.

There were rats, naturally. There are always rats, and unlike humans, rats don't pretend they aren't curious. Within an hour I had three of them sitting on the cot's footboard while I explained, in the low whistles and clicks that no one else has ever been able to hear, exactly how much trouble we were all in.

The big one—a scarred female I named Solveig-the-Lesser, out of spite—told me things. Rats tell you everything; they have no concept of privacy or tact. She told me the guard rotation changed at midnight. She told me the man who brought the bread was afraid of the stairs. And she told me, in the roundabout way rats describe geography, that the warmth in the walls came from below, from a chamber so old the rats' grandmothers' grandmothers had refused to nest there, because *the fire in that place dreams, and dreaming fire asks questions.*

I sat very still for a long time after that.

Because I knew what dreamed beneath a mountain. Every child in Thornfell knew, in the way children know things they're never taught—that the Ember under Crownspire was the last dragon-fire in the world, that it had burned since before the city, before the kingdom, before names. That the king's crown was bound to it somehow, priest-magic nobody explained, and that if the Ember ever died—

Well. Nobody explained that either. That's the thing about state religions. The important parts are always the parts nobody explains.

That night, when the corridor outside went quiet, I heard it for the first time.

Not with my ears. With whatever the thing inside me is—the thing I've spent my whole life folding up small and hiding under my tongue. It was a sound like wind moving through a forest of embers, like a choir sung by something with too many throats, and underneath it, unmistakable, unbearable:

Grief.

I have heard grieving animals all my life. A vixen crying over her drowned kits. An old cart-horse mourning its stablemate. Grief has a shape, and this had it, magnified past bearing, filtered through stone and iron and a hundred feet of mountain—and still it reached me lying on a prison cot, and still it made my eyes sting.

"Oh," I said aloud, to no one. "Oh, you poor enormous thing."

The rats looked at me like I'd lost my mind. Fair.

* * *

It took me two days to work up the courage, which is embarrassing, because I talk to wolves for fun.

On the third night I pressed both palms flat to the wall, closed my eyes, and did the thing I'd done ten thousand times to foxes and falcons and one deeply unimproved mule: I opened myself and *listened*.

The silence on the other side lasted long enough that I almost laughed at myself. Of course. Thirty years of hiding, and the first being I voluntarily speak to is a dying god-fire, and it makes me wait like a shy girl at a dance.

Then: ***small-voice.***

Its voice was not sound. It was warmth shaped into meaning, a hearth-light that fell on my mind like snowfall in reverse.

***You hear. After all the long dimming, one comes who hears.***

"I hear," I whispered. "What are you?"

A pause. When it answered, the grief in it was so old it had worn smooth, like a stone in a riverbed.

***I was called many names before your kind forgot them. Your word is Ember. I am the last.***

"The last dragon-fire."

***The last of the fires that were. The others went out singing. I go out alone.***

And there it was, plain as a butcher's bill. I thought of Wren, wherever he was—alive, I had to believe, alive and furious and doing something brilliant and stupid with that book of his—and of the page he'd shown me in the tavern's back room. *Reach the Ember before it gutters.* We'd read it like a riddle. It wasn't a riddle. It was a countdown.

"How long?" I asked.

***Longer than yesterday. Less than you hope.***

"That's not an answer."

***It is the only honest one I have left, small-voice. Would you like the dishonest ones? Kings deal in those. Ask your king.***

Something in the way it said *your king* made the hair on my arms rise. "Tell me about the king."

The warmth around me shifted. Dimmed. When the Ember spoke again, each word came slowly, the way you speak about the dead.

***Ask instead about the bargain.***

* * *

Here is what it told me, over the course of that night and the next, in pieces, the way you'd hand broken glass to someone—carefully, edge by edge, so neither of you gets cut worse than necessary.

Thirty-one years ago, King Aldric had a twin brother.

The histories don't mention him. Maren could probably explain why—exiled priests remember what official priests burn—but the Ember remembered him the way fire remembers everything it has ever touched. His name had been Vaskr, and he had been born wrong, or perhaps born *first*, into a body that drank light the way other bodies drink water. A hungry child. A hungry man. The old court mages had a name for what lived coiled in him, some worm-of-power older than the kingdom, and they had told the young king there was only one way to keep it leashed.

Bind it to the crown. Bind the crown to the fire. Let the Ember's patience hold what mortal chains could not.

"And Aldric agreed," I said.

***He begged for it,*** the Ember said. ***His brother was killing. Slowly, in secret, the way the hungry kill. Aldric loved him. Love is the oldest fuel, small-voice; it burns hotter than hate and leaves worse ash. He brought Vaskr to me himself. Held his head while the binding closed. Promised him—I heard the promise, I hold all promises sworn in my light—he promised his brother would feel nothing. That the worm would sleep and Vaskr with it, painless, until a cure could be found.***

"And?"

***And there is no cure. There never was. So the promise became a cage, and the cage became a throne, and the king has sat upon his brother for thirty years and called it ruling.***

I sat down on the cold floor without meaning to.

Every story I'd ever heard about the crown-thing—the whispers in smuggler camps, Halvard's dark jokes about the Hollow King, even Sister Maren's heresies—all of them painted a monster wearing a king. None of them had ever once suggested that maybe, just maybe, the monster was the prisoner too.

"Vaskr," I said slowly. "The thing in the crown. It's *him*. His body. His—"

***What remains of him. The worm wears what is left. Some nights the binding thins—when my light gutters lowest—and for a little hour the man wakes inside his own hands and watches what the worm does with them.*** A pause, and the grief turned sharp-edged, almost angry. ***He watches. He remembers. He cannot scream loud enough for anyone to hear. Do you understand now why I grieve, small-voice? I have held them both for thirty years. The brother who bound and the brother who is bound. Neither asked for what they carry. Only the worm is guilty, and even the worm is only hunger, and hunger cannot help what it is.***

The cell felt very small and very far from anywhere. I thought of my mother, burned for a gift she never chose. I thought of Astrid Eriksen, beast-speaker, spending thirty years folding herself small so the world wouldn't see her and be afraid.

Everyone in this story was hiding something. Even the fire.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked finally. "Why me?"

***Because you listen,*** it said simply. ***In all the long dimming, you are the first who came to the wall and listened. And because soon—so soon now—you will be brought before the worm, and it will offer you beautiful things, and you must know what it is before you decide what it deserves.***

"It will offer me things?"

***It knows you are here. It has known since they carried you past my door. It smells the listening in you.*** The warmth pulsed, and for a moment I could have sworn I felt something like fear radiating off a being the size of a mountain. ***It will offer you your mother, small-voice. It keeps dead voices the way I keep promises. It will open its hand and show you her face and ask one small betrayal in return.***

My throat closed. Somewhere above me, my mother's pyre was thirty years cold, and the smoke of it had never quite left my lungs, and this ancient dying thing in the dark had just told me that the enemy knew exactly where to press.

"What do I do?" I whispered. It was not a question I asked often. It was not a question I had ever asked a wall before.

***Remember what I told you,*** said the Ember. ***The brothers. The bargain. The hour each night when the man wakes and watches. If you can find that hour—if you can reach the man inside—then perhaps, perhaps, the story ends differently than the worm has written it.***

"And if I can't?"

***Then burn bright and die loud,*** said the last dragon-fire, with something that in anything else I would have called humor. ***It is what I intend.***

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I lay on the cot with the rats curled against my ribs for warmth—they'd decided I was theirs now, and rat loyalty, once given, is absolute—and I stared at the ceiling and turned the truth over and over like a stone with a sharp edge.

Vaskr was a man. Or had been. Was, in the buried hours, still—a twin, a brother, a boy born hungry through no fault of his own, caged by love and kept caged by regret. And Aldric—the Hollow King, the puppet, the smiling horror paraded through streets of forced celebration—was not a puppet at all. He was a jailer who had become a cell. He woke each night inside his own stolen body and watched, and remembered, and could not die.

I had come down this mountain ready to hate a monster. Instead I'd found two prisoners and a worm, and the whole rotten architecture of it built on a bargain made in desperation by a man who loved his brother too much to let him be killed and too little, in the end, to save him.

"There's no version of this where everyone walks away," I murmured to the ceiling. "Is there."

The rats stirred. From the walls, faint and patient, the Ember answered—not words this time, just warmth, the way you'd squeeze someone's hand across a sickbed.

And then, from the corridor outside, footsteps. Many of them. Unhurried. Stopping at my door.

Through the slot slid a tray—but not bread. On it lay a single white flower, fresh despite the stone and the dark, and a folded note sealed with the royal crest.

The rats went still. Down in the deep, the Ember's warmth pulled tight as a held breath.

***It knows you are listening,*** it breathed. ***Small-voice—it has begun.***

I reached for the note.
