## 13. The Hollow King Speaks

I wake the way a drowning man wakes: all at once, and already underwater.

The hour is always the same. The eleventh bell of night, when the Ember gutters lowest and the binding thins to gauze, and I surface through thirty years of someone else's living like a man coming up through black lake-water into air that tastes of his own mouth again.

My hands are on the armrests of the throne. They are always on the armrests of the throne at this hour, because Vaskr likes the throne, and Vaskr is what my hands have been doing while I was away.

"Hello, Aldric," I say to myself. My voice comes out rusted. Thirty years of disuse will do that. "Still here? Good man."

There is no one in the audience hall to hear me talk to myself, which is fortunate, because it would be treason on two counts: speaking against the king, and being the king and confessing to madness in front of witnesses. The hall is vast and cold and lit by a hundred candles that never burn down, because Vaskr orders them replaced before they can. He cannot abide anything ending. It is the one honest thing about him.

I have one hour. I have had one hour every night for thirty years, and I have learned to be efficient with it, the way a poor man learns to be efficient with bread.

First, as always, the inventory. I flex my fingers. Mine — for now. I roll my shoulders and feel the ache of a body kept too long at attention; Vaskr does not sleep so much as stand down, and he stands down badly. I am sixty-one years old and I have the posture of a spear. There is a gray hair on my sleeve. I pluck it out and hold it up to the candlelight like a trophy. *You missed one,* I tell him. *Sloppy.*

He doesn't answer. He never answers during my hour. He simply waits beneath my thoughts like a stone at the bottom of a well, patient as arithmetic, and I have stopped pretending I don't know exactly how patient.

Second, the walk. I always walk. The physicians — the ones who aren't Purifiers, which these days means the ones who are dead or exiled — would call it therapeutic. I call it reconnaissance. I go down the east corridor, past the tapestries of my father's wars, past the portrait of my wife.

Elira's portrait has been moved twice by Vaskr, into progressively darker corners, and twice I have carried it back with my own traitor arms during my hour, both times, until he gave up and let it hang where it hangs, above the little door to the servants' stair, where the light from the window catches her face at dusk. He tells himself he allowed it out of indifference. I know better. He hates that door. He hates that she used it. He hates everything that remembers her, because she is the one thing in this palace he could not wear.

She knew, you see. That is the part no chronicle will ever record, because no chronicler survived to write it. Elira knew what was happening to me before I did. She was a beast-speaker — quietly, illegally, the way people keep faiths that would get them burned — and she told me once, laughing over the wine, that the ravens had started saying my name wrong. Saying it *double*. As if there were two mouths trying to fit through one throat.

I thought she was teasing me. She was teasing me. That was the horror of it — she was right, and she made it a joke, because she loved me enough to want me to laugh before the laughing stopped.

Three months later she fell from the north tower. The official account says grief. The official account was written by a man who now serves Vaskr with such devotion that he has been promoted four times, each promotion coinciding neatly with the death of someone who remembered too much.

I do not know if she jumped or was helped. I have had thirty years to consider the question and I still cannot finish the arithmetic, because either answer unmakes me, and I need whatever is left of me unmade-adjacent rather than unmade. A king must keep something in reserve. Even a hollow one. *Especially* a hollow one.

* * *

Here is what the ballads will never sing, because the ballads are commissioned: King Aldric the Steadfast did not make a bargain with a devil. There was no crossroads, no midnight, no contract signed in blood. There was a dying twin brother and a desperate man and an old binding rite that the priests swore was theoretical.

Vaskr was born eleven minutes before me. Our mother said those eleven minutes explained everything about us both — that he arrived first and spent his whole life arriving first, louder, brighter, hungrier, while I followed in his wake tidying up. He burned through his inheritance by forty. Lands, wives, ships, patience. And then he burned through his health, and lay dying in the summer palace at thirty-nine, screaming at the gods for the indignity of it, and I — steady, dutiful, tidy Aldric — went to the archivists and asked whether the old rites of binding were truly theoretical.

They said yes. They lied, or they believed it, which amounts to the same crime. The rite was real. It worked. My brother's soul, which should have gone wherever souls go, came instead into the vessel prepared for it, and the vessel was me, and the theory had neglected to mention one small theological footnote: that a bound soul does not lie quiet like a tenant. It grows. It eats. It learns the shape of its house from the inside and begins, very slowly, to renovate.

For the first year we shared it like brothers, God help me. We argued about policy. He was better at it than I was — ruthless in ways I couldn't afford to be, and the kingdom prospered, and I told myself the headaches were grief and the lost hours were fatigue. By the second year I was waking in rooms I hadn't walked to. By the third, I was waking less often. By the fifth, I woke only at the eleventh bell, one hour, and I understood that the renovation was nearly complete, and that the house had a new owner who liked the view.

Thirty years. Do you know what a man can learn in thirty years of watching his own hands? I have watched Vaskr sign death warrants in my name with my handwriting, improved — he rounds my letters, he always thought my penmanship timid. I have watched him greet ambassadors with my smile, which he wears slightly too wide, like a coat borrowed from a taller man. I have watched him order the burning of beast-speakers, dozens of them, whole families, and I have stood inside my own skull screaming with no mouth to scream with, and I have learned that hatred, sustained long enough, becomes a kind of furniture. You live around it. You dust it.

And here is the thing I can only admit in this hour, in this empty hall, to an audience of candles: some of the warrants were mine.

Not the burnings. Never those. But early — that first golden year, when I still believed we were partners — I agreed with him. I agreed with him about the border lords, about the tax, about the necessity of the Purifiers. I shook my own head at my own doubts and said, *Aldric, you're too soft, listen to your brother.* I invited him in. Every hour of my captivity was purchased in advance with an hour of my consent, and no binding rite put that chain on me. I did.

So when they come someday — and they will come, the historians, the avengers, whoever survives — and ask me whether I was victim or accomplice, I intend to answer honestly: yes.

* * *

Tonight, though, tonight is different. Tonight I have work to do, and the work makes my heart behave strangely, quick and young, like a boy's.

Two weeks ago — during my hour, in the dark of my cell of a self — I felt something I had not felt in decades. The binding *shivered*. Not thinned — shivered, like a struck bell, like a rope taking sudden weight. Something below, near the Ember, had touched the old covenant. Someone had spoken to the fire in the fire's own language, or near enough.

And then, three nights ago, the rats told me.

Yes. The rats. Stay with me.

Elira taught me beast-speech in our first year of marriage, as a courtship game — she'd translate the palace cats' opinions of my councilors and I'd pretend to be scandalized. She said the gift wasn't words, exactly. It was attention. It was learning to mean things so plainly that anything with ears could read you. *Beasts don't lie, Aldric, because beasts don't decorate.* After she died I kept the practice the way widowers keep a pipe or a prayer — secretly, uselessly, at the window, meaning goodnight at the starlings. Grief dressed as hobby. The one treason Vaskr never found, because he never thought to look for love.

But the gift keeps, and the undercity knows me. Thirty years of a hollow king standing at his window at the eleventh bell, meaning small plain things at the dark — *cold, tired, alone, still here* — and the rats, who miss nothing, who run the true government of any city, noticed that the king's meaning was not the king's public meaning. Word travels. Rats travel. And so, three nights ago, a scarred old rat with half an ear sat on my windowsill and listened while I meant, carefully, slowly, the way you speak to someone across a great distance:

*Who else is awake down there?*

And the answer came back up the walls of Crownspire like water finding its level: a smuggler woman with a voice like an open door, who speaks to everything; a scholar with a book that writes itself; a child who talks to goats; a priestess heretic; bears turned to battle at a river ford; a network of pigeons and gutter-cats reporting on the palace's comings and goings with more accuracy than my spymaster, whom Vaskr pays in gold and who delivers perhaps a tenth of what the pigeons deliver for crumbs.

They are coming for the Ember. They are coming for *him*. And the woman — Eriksen, the wolves call her Astrid, daughter-of-the-burned-mother — the woman speaks the way Elira spoke. Plainly. Without decoration.

I have been waiting two nights for the courage to do what I am going to do tonight, because it may kill me, and I find I mind that less than I expected, and more than I'd like.

* * *

The window. The eleventh bell fading. Below, Crownspire lies under curfew snow, lamplit streets empty of everyone except patrols in white coats, and beyond the walls the mountain, and somewhere on the mountain or under it or already inside my own dungeons — the pigeons report a woman taken, white coats, the Inquisitor Solveig, cells below the west wing — *my* dungeons, beside the Ember, of course, Vaskr would keep her there, close to the fire he drains, a moth stored next to the flame—

She is in my cells. She is *in my cells*, three floors below me, and the binding shivers every time she speaks to the dark, and Vaskr has begun pacing. He paces during my hour now, at the edge of my awareness, like a dog hearing a whistle only it can hear. He is afraid. Thirty years, and I finally know what he looks like afraid, and it is the most beautiful thing I have ever seen.

I open the window. Cold pours in like a confession.

The starlings are gone for the season; it is rats' weather, cats' weather. I wait. Two minutes. Five. Then along the sill, out of the dark, comes the scarred matriarch herself, half-eared, frost on her whiskers, and behind her three of her people, and beyond them — I can feel them, the way Elira promised you could feel them — the whole listening undercity, hundreds of small ears pointed at one hollow king.

I do not speak. Speech decorates. I *mean*, as plainly as I know how, and I have had thirty years of practice at meaning one thing through clenched teeth, and that thing has always been *still here*. But tonight I mean more, and the meaning goes out of me in a flood, and I will render it here in words only because a chronicle demands words, knowing full well how much is lost in the translation:

*I am the king. What wears me is not. His name is Vaskr and he is my brother and I bound him into myself to save his life and it was the worst act of love ever committed in this kingdom. He feeds on the Ember. When the Ember dies he will not need me anymore, and he will pour out of this body into the world, and everything after that will be his. I have watched him for thirty years. I know his hours, his guards, his fears. I know the doors the servants use and the doors even he has forgotten. I know where he is weak, because I was there when he was weak, before either of us had a crown.*

*Below your cells, woman, there is a wall that is not a wall. My wife found it. Ask the rats; they remember her; she fed them from her hand in this window and they have not forgotten, beasts don't decorate, beasts don't forget.*

*I cannot fight him. One hour a night is not a weapon. But I can be a map. I can be a lamp left burning in the window of a house the thieves think is empty.*

*And if it costs me — if freeing the fire requires a crowned life, and I have read enough of the old rites to guess the price — then understand: I have been dead for thirty years. Everything from tonight forward is resurrection. Do not mourn me in advance. Mourn, if you must, the boy who said yes at his brother's bedside. He is the one who needs it.*

The matriarch's whiskers tremble. She means back at me — small, plain, undecorated — and what she means is the oldest word in any language, the one Elira used to send after me down from the tower window on bad council days:

*Stay.*

"I'll try," I say aloud, rusted voice, to a rat, in front of God and nobody. "No promises. I've never been good at promises. Just ask my brother."

The rats flow away down the wall like spilled ink running home, carrying my meaning into the dark of Crownspire, down through the stones, down toward the west wing, down toward the cells, down toward a woman who speaks plainly, who will understand, whose mother burned for the very gift that might now burn a hole in thirty years of night.

Behind my eyes, at the bottom of the well, the stone stirs.

He has heard. Of course he has heard — he lives in the house, he hears every hammer. The pacing stops. And I feel him turn his attention upward, toward me, toward the hour, toward the window and the cold and the fading echo of small feet, and for the first time in thirty years, Vaskr *speaks* during my hour. Not in words. In pressure. In meaning, plain and undecorated, the way I just taught him — no, the way I just reminded him I know how.

What he means is simple, and it settles over me like snowfall:

*You should have stayed asleep, little brother.*

"Yes," I agree, closing the window, turning back to the candles, straightening my stolen spine. "Everyone says that."

Somewhere below, faint through thirty feet of mountain stone, something ancient takes a breath — and holds it.
