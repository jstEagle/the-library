## 14. Rescue at the Guttering

**HALVARD**

I have smuggled salt past tax collectors, fugitive priests past Purifiers, and once—and I will deny this to my grave—a live snow bear dressed as a very large widow past three checkpoints in Crownspire's lower gate. In thirty years of crooked roads, I have learned one iron law of the trade: never go back for anyone.

The law exists because going back gets you killed, and dead smugglers move no cargo and feed no crews. Every captain who ever broke it has a name carved on a rock somewhere, usually by a grieving mother.

So when I turned my fleet around at Gullwater Ford and pointed every hull, sledge, and mule toward Crownspire's prison gates, I was breaking the only law I had left, and I want it recorded—I did it sober.

"Say it again," growled Brenna beside me on the lead sledge. "Slower. I don't trust my ears."

"We're going back for Astrid." The words tasted like copper and like freedom, which turn out to be the same flavor. "All of us."

"And if we die?"

"Then we die rich," I said, "in the only coin that matters."

Brenna spat over the side, which is her way of saying *I love you, Captain*. She'd have spat on the king himself if he'd asked nicely enough.

The plan, such as it was, came from a child and a priest and an army of rats, which should tell you everything about how the year had gone. Sister Maren stood at the prow mapping our route in the air with a stub of charcoal, her voice steady as a psalm: the Ember guttered lowest in the hour before dawn—the hour the old liturgies called the Guttering, when even the crown-thing's attention wandered. That was our door. One hour. Maybe less.

Pip sat cross-legged atop the cargo crates, whispering into a knothole where a rat with suspiciously intelligent eyes sat listening. Every few minutes the rat would twitch its whiskers and Pip would nod like a general receiving dispatches.

"Percival says the east postern has two guards," she reported. "Percival says they're bored. Percival says everyone in that prison is bored except the ones downstairs, and the ones downstairs are scared."

"The rats have names now," said Brenna flatly.

"They've always had names. You just never asked." Pip scratched the rat behind its ears. It closed its eyes in what I swear was smugness. "Percival also says the white-coats changed the watch schedule tonight. Something spooked them."

Something spooked them. In my experience, *something spooked them* meant either nothing or everything, and there was no way to know which until you were already bleeding.

We went anyway.

That's the thing about family. You don't get to choose them, not really—they choose you, usually at the worst possible moment, and then you're stuck hauling their coffins or their cargo or their hopeless causes up mountains you swore you'd retired from. Astrid Eriksen had walked into my caravan five years ago with hollow cheeks and a lie about knowing the passes, and she'd kept every secret but one, and the one she'd kept had gotten her caged beneath a palace.

You don't leave family in cages. Even if the law says so.

*Especially* if the law says so.

* * *

**PIP**

Rats are better than people and I'll fight anyone who says different.

People look right through you when you're small and grubby. Rats look *at* you. First thing Percival ever did, back in Crownspire's gutters, was steal half a sausage and bring me the other half, which is more sharing than my own blood family ever managed before they sold me to a chimney-sweep. So when the plan needed eyes inside the palace, I didn't need convincing. I just needed breakfast for four hundred friends.

The Guttering hour was cold enough that my breath made ghosts. We crouched in a drainage culvert below the prison's east wall while Percival's cousins ran messages along the stones—little paws drumming codes against my boot, which sounds silly until you realize it's faster than any signal-flag in the fleet.

"Two guards at the postern," I told Halvard. "One's asleep standing up. The other one's crying."

"Crying?"

"Homesick, Percival thinks. He keeps looking at a little painted wooden bird he's got in his pocket." I listened some more, head tilted, while the drums tapped against my heel. "His name's Oskar. He misses his mum's barley soup."

Halvard rubbed his face with both hands, the way he does when the world refuses to be simple. "We can't knock out a homesick boy, Pip."

"I know." I'd already thought about this, lying awake in the smugglers' camp while Wren muttered over his terrible book. "Send me instead. Small people don't scare guards. They just confuse them."

"That's exactly why we shouldn't send you."

"But it's exactly why you *will*," I said, and looked up at him, and did the face. Every kid knows the face. It's the one that says *you can say no, but you'll feel bad about it forever.*

Sister Maren put her hand on Halvard's arm. "Let her try. If the gods have any mercy left in this city, they're hiding in small places."

So I walked out of the culvert with my hood down and my hands empty and my heart going like a rabbit's, straight up to the postern gate, and said, "Mister? Mister Oskar?"

He jumped about a foot. "How d'you know my—"

"Your bird fell out of your pocket earlier. I kept it safe." I held out the little painted wooden wren—which Percival's cousin had lifted off the ground near the guardhouse an hour ago, because rats are generous like that. "It's pretty. My gran used to carve birds. Before."

Oskar took it with shaking fingers and looked at me the way people look at stray kittens, which is the most dangerous look there is because it comes right before kindness. "You shouldn't be out here, girl. Curfew."

"I know. I'm hiding from my uncle. He's not kind." True enough. My uncle had sold me too; it ran in the family. "Could I warm up by your brazier a minute? Just till the watch changes?"

And that's how, when the smugglers came over the wall on ropes greased with tallow, both guards were busy—one asleep, and one sharing his supper bread with a grubby child who thanked him so politely he nearly wept.

I felt bad about the bread. I stole his bird back later and returned it. Mostly.

* * *

**SOLVEIG**

For twenty-nine years I have served the Purification with clean hands and a clean conscience, or as near to clean as this world permits. I burned heretics. I burned beast-speakers. I burned a woman named Elin Eriksen when I was young enough to believe fire was mercy—that it was kinder than the things her gift might unleash.

I told myself that for twenty-nine years. Tonight the telling finally broke.

They brought me word at midnight: intruders at the east postern, smugglers' blades, a child's footprints in the frost. I went down into the cells myself, because something had been wrong in my chest since the Eriksen girl was taken—a splinter working deeper every day, and tonight it had reached the heart.

The cells beneath Crownspire are cut from the mountain's living stone, and the deepest corridor runs beside the old vault where the Ember sleeps—or used to sleep. As a novice I had been taught the Ember was a legend, a story to frighten scholars. But walking that corridor you could *feel* it: a warmth through the rock, faint as a dying man's pulse, and threaded through the warmth, something else. A sound. Like a choir heard through six feet of stone, singing very quietly because it was too tired to sing loudly.

And beneath the choir, another voice. Smooth. Patient. Hungry in a way that made my skin crawl though I could not have said why.

I found the intruders at the cell block door—four of them and a child, plus a scholar clutching a book like a shield. The big one, the smuggler captain, raised his blade without pleasure. The priestess stepped in front of him with her arms spread wide.

"Sister Maren," I said. "Exiled for heresy."

"Guilty." Her chin was high. "Shall we compare heresies, Inquisitor? Yours first. Tell me what the crown-thing is."

"There is no crown-thing. There is King Aldric, anointed of—"

"Look at him." Her voice dropped, gentle as a hand on a wound. "Inquisitor Solveig. Look at your king. When did he last blink?"

I had looked at Aldric that morning, presiding over the Purifier's oath. I had watched his eyes move across the assembled ranks without seeing us, watched his mouth smile without his face joining in, watched him stand too still between the motions, like a puppet resting between tugs of its string. Twenty-nine years I had called it majesty. Detachment. The weight of rule.

I had never once called it by its true name, because I had known, somewhere below the doctrine, what the true name was.

"You're lying," I said, and my voice cracked on it.

The scholar fumbled his book open. "Page two hundred eleven," he said hoarsely. "Written a century ago. Read it. *Read it.*"

I read it. The old ink crawled: an account of a binding, a bargain between twin princes, a thing named Vaskr sewn into a crown of iron, fed nightly on dragon-fire to keep it sleeping, wearing each successive king like a glove wears a hand. And beneath it, in ink darker than the rest, a single line that seemed to have been written recently, urgently, by a hand that knew:

*IT IS AWAKE AND IT IS HUNGRY AND YOUR FIRE IS DYING.*

The choir through the stone faltered. The hungry voice beneath it laughed.

I am not proud of the next minute. I stood in that corridor with my sword drawn and my faith in pieces, and I thought of Elin Eriksen in the flames, and I thought: *she spoke to beasts, and I burned her for it, and all the while the real monster wore a crown and smiled at me every morning.*

"Stand aside," said Maren softly. "Or don't. Either way, sister, I'm going through that door."

I looked at my Purifiers lining the corridor behind me—good soldiers, believers, children of the doctrine I had helped build. I looked at the cell block door. I thought about which side of history I wanted my bones buried on.

"Postern corridor is compromised," I said aloud, to my people, in my command voice. "Fall back to the upper levels. Contain the breach from above. *Now.*"

They hesitated. Then they obeyed, because twenty-nine years buys a lot of obedience, and they filed away up the stairs and out of the story.

Which left me alone with the heretics, holding my sword wrong-side-up, offering it hilt-first to a smuggler who looked like he wanted to laugh and cry at once.

"Don't mistake me," I told them. "I'm not with you. I'm against *it.* There's a difference."

"No," said Maren, taking my sword and handing it back the right way round. "There isn't. Welcome to the crooked road, sister."

* * *

**WREN**

I have read perhaps ten thousand books, and I can report that none of them prepared me for running.

Scholars do not run. Scholars walk briskly toward archives. Yet here I was, sprinting down a torchlit corridor with a century-old codex under one arm, my lungs filing formal complaints, following a child, a priestess, a defected inquisitor, and a pack of rats toward the last dragon-fire in the world, in order to break a prophecy I had once believed was simply a very committed piece of literature.

Astrid was waiting for us.

They hadn't broken the lock so much as negotiated with it—Pip's rats had gnawed the latch-cord and Astrid, I suspect, had asked the door itself, in whatever language doors speak when a beast-speaker knocks. She came out of the cell pale and unsteady, and when she saw me her whole face changed, and for one absurd moment in the middle of everything I forgot how to breathe.

"You came back," she said.

"You sound surprised."

"I sound like someone who read the part of the prophecy where nobody comes back." She gripped my sleeve hard enough to hurt, which I understood to be affection. "Wren. Listen. The Ember talks. It's *right there*, behind that wall—it's barely alive, and Vaskr's been drinking it dry for thirty years, and there's a rite, and there's a price—"

"I know," I said. "I read it. Page two hundred forty."

Her eyes went sharp. "Then you know what it costs."

"I know." And I knew she had known longer, and I set that anger aside in a box labeled *later*, because the wall beside us shuddered, and dust sifted down, and from everywhere and nowhere came a voice like silk drawn over teeth:

***Little thief. Little speaker. Bring me the scholar's book and I will give you back your mother's face.***

Astrid didn't flinch. That's the thing about her that I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe properly: the world offers her every terrible bargain imaginable, and she stands in the middle of the offer like a mountain stands in weather.

"She says no," Astrid told the wall, conversationally. "She says you can keep your rotten crown and choke on it."

The wall screamed. Stone split. Somewhere above us, alarms began to ring, and Solveig appeared at the stair-head with frost on her shoulders and urgency in her voice: "They know. All of them. The whole garrison. We have minutes, not hours."

"The Guttering ends at dawn," Maren said. "The rite must begin before it does. Down. Everyone down."

We went down.

* * *

**MAREN**

Let the record show—I have always wanted the record to show something; it is the vanity of priests—that I died the way I had lived: badly dressed, stubbornly positioned, and blocking a doorway.

The descent to the Ember's chamber was a gauntlet. Vaskr had woken fully now, and it wore the king's body like armor, and it had turned the palace's own faithful against us. We fought through two antechambers—Halvard roaring like the avalanche he resembled, Solveig holding the line with the terrible competence of the recently converted, the rats doing work I choose not to imagine in detail—and we reached the great vault door with the Ember's light bleeding gold through its seams.

And then the door began to close.

Vaskr's will, grinding the ancient mechanism shut. Behind it, I could hear the Ember singing its thin, brave, dying song, and Astrid pressed her hands to the stone and *screamed* at it in a language older than any scripture, and the door slowed—but slowing was not stopping, and stopping was not opening, and the mechanism would reverse unless something held it.

Something mortal. Something willing.

Well. I had spent forty years preaching that love is a door held open, and I have never been able to resist the opportunity to be literal.

"Maren—" Halvard saw it before I moved, because of course he did; the man is dense as oak about his own heart and clear as glass about everyone else's. "Maren, no—"

"Someone must hold it." I wedged myself into the gap, shoulder to the iron leaf, boots braced. The weight came down on me like a cathedral. "Sing, Halvard. Sing the heresy. All of it. Loudly."

"The *what*—"

"The hymn I was exiled for! The Binding-Breakers' verse! Your smugglers know it—they've been humming it around fires for a month thinking I didn't notice!" The door groaned; my spine sang a counterpoint. "Go!"

He went. God forgive me, he went, because I asked him to, and that is the whole tragedy and glory of Halvard: he cannot refuse the people he loves.

They passed me one by one. Pip, white-faced, gripping my hand for half a heartbeat. Wren, book open, tears freezing on his lashes. Astrid last, turning at the threshold, her eyes fierce and wet.

"Sister—"

"Go be the choir," I said. "I'll hold the door. It's what doors are for."

The weight increased. My knees buckled; my shoulders took what my knees surrendered. Through the narrowing gap I watched them descend into gold light, watched Astrid turn back one final time, and I smiled at her with everything I had left, which was considerable. I had always been told I had a smile that could stop traffic. Pity it couldn't stop doors.

But the hymn reached me even there—Halvard's rough bass leading, smuggler voices catching on, Solveig's clear trained soprano rising astonished out of her own throat, the Binding-Breakers' verse rolling down the stairwell like a tide:

*What god has bound, let love unbind;*
*what fire was given, let fire be kind...*

I held the door. I held it long enough.

I want that noted. Whatever else the record shows—hold it open, note that Maren of the Crooked Road held the door long enough, and that her last sight was not the enemy but the family, going down together into the light, singing.

The dark, when it came, was warm.

It smelled faintly of dragon-fire and bread baking.

I have had worse endings. I have certainly had worse congregations.

* * *

**HALVARD**

She held it. By every god she ever blasphemed, she held it, and we sang, and we got through, and the door sealed behind us like a tomb.

I didn't cry. Smugglers don't cry; it fogs the goggles. What I did was put my fist through a wall, which accomplished nothing and was therefore deeply satisfying, and then Brenna put her hand on my shoulder and said, "She'd have spat on your grief, Captain. Said it was self-indulgent."

"Aye." I wiped my face on my sleeve. Fog on the goggles. "Then we'd best make it worth the spit."

Below us, the chamber opened up like the inside of a struck bell, and there at its heart burned the Ember—small, no bigger than a hearth-flame, guttering like a candle in wind, and so beautiful that Pip made a sound like a hiccup and even Solveig went to her knees.

And standing between us and the fire, wearing the king's body with its strings cut and its face finally, horribly *awake*, waited Vaskr.

Astrid stepped forward. The Codex in Wren's hands flipped its own pages, racing ahead of itself.

"Read," Astrid said, and her voice was steady, and her eyes were on the flame, and mine were on her, because I break one law per lifetime and apparently the second one is *always come back.*

Wren drew breath and began.
