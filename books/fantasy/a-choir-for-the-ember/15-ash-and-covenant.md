## 15. Ash and Covenant

I have spent my entire scholarly career believing that knowledge is a kind of safety. That if you understand a thing—truly understand it, down to its roots and rotten heartwood—it cannot hurt you. A trap you can see is a trap you can step around. A grief you can name is a grief you can carry.

I would like to formally retract that belief, in writing, for the historical record.

The Codex of Ash had been quiet for two days. Quiet in the way a held breath is quiet. It sat in my pack between a change of socks and Halvard's emergency flask of plum brandy—don't ask how the flask got there, ask why Halvard considers it "emergency" when we drink from it every night—and every time I reached for my socks I felt the book watching me. Books don't watch. I know that. I have a degree that says books don't watch.

The Codex does not care about my degree.

It opened for me on the third night, in the ruins of an old waystation halfway up the mountain's shoulder, while the others slept and the wind did its best impression of a choir of the damned. It didn't open the way books open. It opened the way a door opens when the person inside has decided you've waited long enough.

The pages turned themselves to the end.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the last comfort I have left. The Codex of Ash, a book sealed for a hundred years, whose prophecies had until now arrived in drips and riddles and one deeply unsettling account of a spilled tankard of cider—turned its own pages to the end, and showed me the ritual.

*The Unbinding of King from Crown*, the heading read, in ink that was still wet. Not figuratively wet. I touched it and came away with black on my finger, and the black smelled like woodsmoke and something underneath the woodsmoke, something old and mineral and alive, like the inside of a mountain.

I read it three times. The first time for meaning. The second time for method. The third time because my hands were shaking too badly to stop at the end of the second.

The ritual itself was almost disappointingly simple. That's the thing about the old magics nobody tells you: the terrible ones are always simple. No seven-day vigil, no relics, no blood of innocents on an altar of obsidian. Just voices. A choir. Maren's heresy—the one that got her exiled, the one she'd taught us half-embarrassed around the campfire like a woman confessing a love affair—was not heresy at all. It was the first verse of the binding's counter-song. The Ember had been waiting a hundred years for someone to sing the rest.

*Let the smuggler keep the rhythm*, the Codex instructed, and I actually laughed, alone, at midnight, in a ruin. Halvard keeps rhythm the way a landslide keeps rhythm, but fine. The book knows things. Apparently the book knows Halvard.

*Let the child carry the small gods to the threshold.* Pip. The rats. Of course the rats. Somewhere under Crownspire right now, a rat the size of my boot was probably being informed of its holy commission, and I would have given a great deal to see Pip's face when it happened.

*Let the priest's song and the scholar's reading be one voice.* Wren reads. That's me. That has always been me. I have spent my life reading things other people wrote, and here at the end of everything, the job description held. There was something in that that felt less like destiny and more like a very elaborate joke with a punchline a hundred years in the making.

*And let the beast-speaker speak to the fire, and the fire answer.*

And then, beneath all of it, in the same wet black ink, smaller, the way a person lowers their voice to deliver the part that matters:

*For the Ember is not a lamp to be trimmed but a heart to be fed. It has burned a hundred years on borrowed fuel, and it will not rekindle for nothing. The fire asks a price, and the price is always the same: one memory, freely given. Not a trinket. Not a sorrow. A cherished thing. The giver must love what they surrender, or the fire will not accept it, and the binding will fail, and the thing in the crown will walk free.*

I read that paragraph four times before I could make my eyes move past it.

One memory, freely given. A cherished thing.

And the Codex, because it is apparently incapable of mercy, turned one more page and showed me the rest: a list. Not a list of options. A record. Entries in that same wet ink, dated across the last hundred years, each one a name and a memory and nothing else.

*Eirik Halvorsen, 43 years past. Gave: the sound of his daughter's laughter.*

*Signe, 61 years past. Gave: her wedding day, entire.*

*Ylva Eriksen, 30 years past. Gave:—*

I stopped breathing.

Ylva Eriksen. Astrid's mother. The beast-speaker who burned. I had read the histories, the official ones, the ones the Purifiers wrote with their clean white hands: *the witch Ylva, executed for consorting with beasts.* But the Codex didn't say *executed*. The Codex said *gave*, and the entry after Ylva's name was the longest in the book, and it wasn't a memory at all.

*Ylva Eriksen, 30 years past. Came to the Ember of her own will, before the Purifiers came for her. Gave: every memory of her daughter, so that the daughter would never be questioned, never be hunted, never be known. The fire accepted. The mother burned anyway, by the king's order, so that the daughter would believe the lie and live.*

She didn't die to protect a secret. She died to protect *Astrid*—and she paid the Ember in advance for the privilege, emptied herself of her own child first, so that when they burned her there would be nothing left in her that the fire could ever come asking for. Nothing that could lead them back down the mountain to a girl with a gift and her whole life ahead of her.

She chose to forget her daughter so her daughter could live unremembered.

I sat down on the floor of the ruin. I don't remember deciding to sit down. The floor was cold and the brandy flask was right there and I didn't touch it, which for me constitutes a heroic act of restraint.

And then, because I am apparently a glutton for punishment, I did the arithmetic.

Ylva Eriksen went to the Ember thirty years ago. She knew the price. Which means she knew what the Ember was, what it wanted, how the unbinding worked. And Astrid—Astrid, who talks to wolves the way I talk to books, who has spent her whole life listening to things the rest of us can't hear—Astrid would have gone looking for answers the moment the Codex named her. Chapter four, if we're being precise. What the wolves remember. The old matriarch of the Thornfell pack, who was alive the last time the fire dimmed.

The wolf would have told her. The wolf would absolutely have told her. Wolves don't do subtext.

Astrid has known the price since the beginning. Since the campfire, since the river ford, since the first kiss on the rooftop with the frozen city glittering below us like someone had spilled a jewelry box across the dark—she has known that the fire at the bottom of the mountain is going to take something she cherishes, and she has known exactly what she intends to give it, because I have watched her look at me for weeks now with an expression I was vain enough to read as simple love, and it was never simple, it was *inventory*. She was memorizing me. She was saying goodbye in installments and I stood there being charming about it.

She's going to give the fire her memories of me.

I know this the way you know a stone is going to fall before it falls. I know this the way she knows what a raven is saying. It is written in the shape of every kindness she's done me since the wolves told her, in the way she's been teaching Halvard the routes she usually keeps to herself, in the way she made Pip promise—made *Pip promise*, a child who has never kept a promise in her life—to look after my glasses.

I got up. I walked across the ruin. I stood over Astrid's bedroll in the dark and I watched her sleep and I was so angry that the anger had nowhere to go and just sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

She let me fall in love with her knowing she was going to delete herself from my side of it. She let me read her the Codex entries, she let me plan, she let me *hope*, and the whole time she was carrying this like a stone in her shoe, alone, because that is what Astrid does. She carries things alone. She has been carrying things alone since her mother burned, and she is so practiced at it that she does it in her sleep, and the loneliest person I have ever met has decided that the solution to her loneliness is to make sure there's less of her left to be lonely with.

I stood there for a long time, hating her a little, and loving her so much it felt structural, like a load-bearing wall.

Then she turned over in her sleep and murmured something to the darkness—not words, a sound, a soft clicking chirp, and I realized she was dreaming in fox, or bat, or some language I will never speak, and that even unconscious she was keeping company with the world in a way I never could, and the anger cracked down the middle and what was inside it was not forgiveness exactly but something adjacent. Understanding's rough draft.

Because here is what I understood, standing over her in the dark: she wasn't lying to me. She was doing the thing her mother did. The Eriksen women have a single move, passed down like an heirloom: they pay the whole price themselves and tell no one, so that no one they love has to stand at the counter and watch them pay it. Ylva emptied herself of Astrid to save Astrid. Astrid is going to empty herself of me to save the king, the city, the Ember, all of us—and to spare me the one conversation she must be dreading more than the fire itself.

It is the most loving thing anyone has ever done to me and I have never been so furious in my life.

I went back to my corner of the ruin. I looked at the Codex. It lay open, patient, smug, its wet ink drying by degrees, and I had a thought that arrived with the calm of a man who has already jumped and is simply waiting for the ground.

*The fire wants a cherished memory, freely given. It doesn't say whose.*

The ritual needs a beast-speaker to speak to the fire. Fine. Astrid speaks; Astrid must always speak; that part is hers and I will not steal it from her. But the price—the price is a toll, not a title. The Codex lists a smuggler's daughter's laughter and a bride's wedding day. It has never once listed a memory that belonged to the beast-speaker. It lists what the *giver* cherishes.

So let me give.

I know exactly what I would give. That's the awful, wonderful clarity of it. I have an entire life of memories and precisely one of them is the one the fire would smell on me the moment I opened my mouth: the tavern. The tankard of cider. The exact weight of the moment I turned around and there she was, furious about her boots, lit like a painting someone had made of a woman I hadn't met yet but was already in love with. The Codex wrote it down word for word before it happened. Let it take the original. Let it take the tavern and the cider and the first hour and the first laugh and the first time she said my name like she was testing whether it was load-bearing. Let it take all of it.

She would still know me. That's the part that matters, the part I built the whole argument on while the wind screamed and the ink dried: she would wake after the ritual with her mind intact, her mother's face returned to her, her whole self hers—and I would be a stranger. A kind stranger. A scholar she'd met on a road. She wouldn't grieve me, because grief needs a before, and the before would be ash.

She would be free. Free of the price, free of the stone in her shoe, free of thirty years of Eriksen women paying silently for everyone they love. The debt ends with me. The covenant closes on my side of the ledger.

Is it fair? No. It is spectacularly unfair. It is the single most selfish selfless thing I have ever conceived, and I conceived it while looking at a sleeping woman who would hate me for it, and I am going to do it anyway, because I have read every book in the Thornfell archives and I can report with scholarly authority that love is not a feeling. Love is a decision you keep making after the feeling gets expensive.

I turned to the Codex's last blank page. I picked up my pen. My hands had stopped shaking, which should have been my first warning.

"New terms," I whispered to the book. "Amendment to the covenant. The memory given at the rekindling will be mine. Wren Calloway. Cherished beyond reason. Freely given. Witness it and write it down, since writing things down is what we do to each other."

The ink rose to meet my pen. Of course it did. It wrote as I wrote, my words in its voice, and beneath my amendment it added a line of its own, small and wet and terrible:

*The fire accepts what is offered with a whole heart. But the fire does not choose the hour. And the mountain is not finished with you.*

I looked up.

Astrid was awake. She had been awake for some time—I could tell by the stillness of her, the hunter's stillness—and she was watching me across the dark ruin with an expression I had never seen on her face before, and could not read, and was suddenly very afraid of.

"Wren," she said quietly. "What did you just write?"

And before I could answer—before either of us could say another word—a horn sounded far down the valley. Not a hunter's horn. Not a smuggler's signal. A war horn, deep and brazen, the kind they only sound in Crownspire when the city itself is in danger, and beneath it, rolling up the mountain like a tide, we heard the thing no one had heard in thirty years:

The king's voice, coming out of the king's throat, screaming in a language that predated the kingdom.
