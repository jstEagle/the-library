## 16. Vaskr Unmasked

I have served the Purifier's Order for thirty-one years, and in that time I have learned exactly three things about faith. First: it is easier to burn a thing than to understand it. Second: the men who shout loudest about purity are usually hiding something under their floorboards. Third—and this is the one that matters tonight—when your god starts giving orders that make your stomach turn, it was never a god. It was only ever a man with better posture.

Let me tell you how I learned the third thing.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes do, with paperwork.

The archive beneath the Bastion of White Flame is organized by heresy, alphabetically, because Inquisitor-General Doss believed that sin, like everything else, should be filed where you can find it. I had spent the evening cross-referencing reports from the river ford—the ambush that should have ended the beast-speaker and instead ended six of my best Purifiers, trampled by a bear that moved like it understood tactics. Which, I now suspect, it did.

Among the recovered effects was a page torn from a book. Not burned, as our doctrine demands of prophetic text, but *kept*. Kept by my own second, Brother Anselm, folded into his breastplate like a love letter.

I read it by candlelight. It described the ford ambush. It described the bear. And at the bottom, in ink that seemed to shift when I wasn't looking directly at it, it described me:

*The woman who loved Eriksen's mother will stand aside, and then she will not.*

My mother-in-law used to say the past always comes for you eventually, but nobody warned me it would arrive via confiscated prophecy.

Because yes. Elira Eriksen. The beast-speaker we burned nineteen years ago. I knew her before the Order knew her name. We were young together in the border garrison, two girls who shared bread and secrets and, for one winter I have never confessed to anyone living or dead, a blanket. When the accusations came, I told myself the Order's justice must be right, because if it was wrong, then I had stood in the square and watched them light the pyre while the woman I loved screamed my name, and I had done nothing.

I have done nothing very consistently for nineteen years. It is remarkable what a career you can build on it.

So when I read that page—when some dead scholar's ink reached out of the paper and took me by the throat—I did what any sensible officer does. I went to find answers in the forbidden section.

* * *

The sealed vaults beneath the Bastion hold everything the Order has decided the world is safer not knowing. I had the rank to enter; what I lacked was permission, which has never once stopped me. Inside, behind a lock older than the dynasty, I found the archive within the archive: the private records of every Inquisitor-General since the founding. And there, in the hand of Aldric's own confessor, a report so heretical it made my teeth ache.

*Aldric has not slept in thirty years,* it read. *He does not eat unless watched. He speaks to the crown when he believes himself alone, and last night, the crown answered him. I heard it. God forgive me, I heard it laugh.*

Below that, decades of entries, each one worse. The king's confessor documenting, year after year, the slow erosion of the man inside the gold. And interleaved among them, dispatches from the Ember's keepers—before the Order dissolved their chapter—recording the fire's dimming in careful liturgical columns. Every entry noted the same correlation: as the Ember guttered, the king grew stronger. As the fire faded, Aldric's decrees grew crueler, his eyes emptier, his voice—

His voice changed. That was the detail that undid me. There were samples. Transcriptions. Thirty years of royal proclamations copied out by clerks who thought they were doing honest work, and if you read them in sequence, you could hear it happening: the accent shifting, the cadence tightening, the warmth draining out word by word until what remained spoke fluent commandment and nothing else.

I sat in that vault until my candle drowned in its own wax, and I did the arithmetic no one in the Order had ever been permitted to do.

We had not been purging heresy for thirty years. We had been pruning the garden of the thing that wore our king.

* * *

I brought it to the Inquisitor-General, because that is the chain of command, and the chain of command is the first thing a soldier reaches for when the ground opens under her.

Doss read the documents in silence. Then he looked up at me with an expression I had seen him wear over condemned prisoners—a kind of gentle pity, the look of a man deciding whether a thing deserves a quick fire or a slow one.

"Solveig," he said. "Who else has seen these?"

"No one."

"Good." He rose, straightened his white coat, and smiled. "Then it's still just the two of us who know the king is damned, and only one of us who needs to."

I want to be clear about what happened next, because I have replayed it enough times to get the details right. Doss did not reach for a weapon. He reached for the bell-cord. And when the guards came—not my guards, his—he said, in a voice pitched to carry down the corridor, "Inquisitor Solveig has been compromised by heretical texts. Detain her for purification."

Thirty-one years. Six hundred interrogations. Two thousand souls sent to the flame on evidence thinner than this, and I had never once doubted the machinery, because doubting meant thinking about Elira, and thinking about Elira meant standing in that square again.

Now the machinery had turned its lovely face toward me, and I discovered I had exactly one skill left worth anything: I had spent three decades learning how heretics escape.

I went out through the records furnace, because everyone watches the doors and no one watches the place where the Order burns its own history. I came up in the coal yard smelling of ash, which felt appropriate, and I ran.

* * *

Here is what I know about Crownspire at night: it has more alleys than prayers, and I have needed both. I went to ground in the tannery district, in a room rented under a name I hadn't used since the garrison, and I waited for morning and the sound of boots.

The boots came at dawn. But they weren't hunting me.

They were marching in formation—hundreds of them, white coats immaculate, moving through the streets in patterns I had never authorized, toward positions I had never assigned. And above them, on the palace balcony where the king gave his festival addresses, King Aldric stood in the grey half-light, and he was smiling.

Aldric did not smile. In thirty years of public life, the man had produced perhaps four smiles, all of them photographed into legend precisely because they were so rare. This smile was wide and easy and wrong, the way a mask is wrong—fitted well, but fitted by someone who had only ever seen faces from the outside.

"Children of the Crown," he called down, and his voice carried impossibly far, the way it had carried lately, the way voices don't. "For thirty years you have been promised a purification. Today I give it to you—not piecemeal, not by pyre and petition, but whole. Every hidden thing. Every beast-lover, every book-burner's secret, every lie this city tells itself in the dark." The smile widened. "Bring me the beast-speaker. Bring me the scholar. Bring me the book. And bring me everyone who helped them, because kindness is also heresy now, and we are going to be *thorough*."

The crowd below cheered. That was the worst part. They cheered, because it was the king, and the king's voice still worked on people like weather works on crops—unquestioned, ancestral, soaking in before you notice you're wet.

But I wasn't looking at the crowd. I was looking at the balcony rail, at the moment the king turned back inside, believing himself unobserved.

For half a heartbeat—one breath, no more—the smile fell off his face like something dropped. And underneath it was not triumph. Underneath it was a man drowning, mouthing something at the empty air of his own palace, something urgent, something desperate, and his hands came up and clawed at the collar of his own coat as though trying to tear off a garment sewn to his skin.

Then the smile climbed back on, and the king went inside, and I stood in my borrowed room with my heart hammering and finally, *finally*, let myself think the sentence I had been outrunning since the vault:

There are two of them in there. And only one of them is guilty.

* * *

By noon, the city had teeth.

Purifiers on every corner. Lists nailed to church doors—not of heretics, but of *suspected sympathizers*, a category broad enough to include anyone who had ever fed a stray dog. The beast-market was put to the torch, and I watched from a rooftop as the handlers tried to save their animals and the white coats cut them down for it, and the thing wearing the king's face watched from the palace heights, and I swear on Elira's grave it was *enjoying itself*.

Anselm found me at dusk. My own second, the man whose breast pocket had carried a page of prophecy like contraband scripture. He came alone, unarmed, hands visible, which in our profession is either surrender or a trap, and I had a knife at his throat before he finished saying my name.

"You read it," he gasped. "The page. You read it and you ran, so you *read* it—"

"Talk fast."

"The ford. I was there. I saw the bear look at her—" His voice cracked. "Solveig, I saw the bear *obey* her, and I didn't report it, because reporting it meant watching us do to her what we did to her mother, and I couldn't. Nineteen years I've carried that square around in my chest. I burned Elira Eriksen's name into the ledger myself. I was twenty-two and I thought I was serving the Flame." He laughed, wet and awful. "And today the king ordered a city-wide purge with a smile he doesn't know how to wear, and every man I trained is following him, and I need to know if you saw it too or if I've finally gone mad."

I lowered the knife.

"I saw the vault," I said. "I saw thirty years of a confessor's terror. I saw the king fight his own face on a balcony this morning." I looked at him for a long moment. "You kept a prophet's page in your armor, Anselm. You've been a heretic longer than I have. You just lacked initiative."

He wiped his eyes and grinned like a man stepping off a ledge into open air. "What do we do?"

"What we should have done nineteen years ago," I said. "We stop being what they tell us we are."

* * *

The archive came out through the coal chute, crate by crate, over six hours, carried by eleven Purifiers who had each been given one document and one question: *Do you serve the Flame, or the thing that wears the king?*

I expected hesitation. I got fury. That surprised me less than it should have. Thirty years of orders that curdled the stomach leave a residue in a person; I had simply assumed I was the only one who noticed the taste. Anselm said later, "We all felt it. We just thought it was holiness hurting us into shape." Faith, again. Easier to burn a thing than understand it—but somewhere in the burning, some of us had started to smell our own smoke.

By midnight we had the confessor's full record, the Ember-keepers' dimming ledgers, the transcription series proving the voice-change, and the oldest item of all: a fragment, pre-dynastic, describing a binding ritual performed "beneath the mountain's crown, upon twin blood, one crowned and one cast out"—and naming the price of unbinding in words that made the young scribe who copied it refuse to finish the job.

I read that fragment twice. Then I wrapped it in oilcloth against my ribs, next to where a different wound used to live, and gave the order.

"We go to the smugglers' fleet at the Salt Quay. Tonight. All of it goes with us—the records, the fragment, the eleven, and me." Anselm stared. "Inquisitor, they'll shoot us."

"They'll try," I said. "But their captain strikes me as a man who respects good merchandise, and we are carrying the only inventory in Crownspire that can kill a king."

* * *

The streets fought us on the way. Of course they did. The purge had teeth by then, and we were eleven white coats walking openly through a city that had learned to hate the color, and twice we had to prove ourselves the fast way—with fists and flat blades, no killing, because I had decided something on that rooftop and I intended to keep it: I was done making widows.

At the Tanner's Bridge, a patrol blocked the crossing—young ones, barely shaved, torches high, and their sergeant called out the challenge: "In the name of the King's purity, stand!"

And I walked to the front of my eleven, pulled off my white coat, and held it up where the torchlight could see it.

"In the name of the king," I called back, "look at his face tomorrow. Look closely. Look for the man behind it, fighting. Because I have read the sealed archives, boy, and I am telling you as the highest-ranking officer to defect in the history of this Order: the thing giving your orders is not Aldric, and it is not the Flame, and when it is dragged into the light—and it *will* be—every one of you will have to answer for tonight. Choose which side of that ledger you want your name on."

Silence. Torches wavering. And then, from the back of the patrol, a voice—young, terrified, furious: "My sister's on the sympathizer lists. She feeds cats." A pause. "Bridge is open."

That is how revolutions actually start, in case you wondered. Not with trumpets. With one scared kid deciding his sister's cats matter more than his sergeant's approval.

* * *

Halvard met us at the Salt Quay with a loaded crossbow and the hospitality of a landslide. It took Sister Maren—alive, singed, furious, alive—to talk him down, and it took the confessor's journal, read aloud by lantern-light on a deck reeking of tar, to turn his scowl into something worse: grief.

"So it was never the king," he said at last, quietly, in the voice of a man re-pricing thirty years of hatred. "All those edicts. The border taxes that starved my village. The burning laws." He looked up at me. "You're telling me I've hated a hostage."

"I'm telling you the war has a different shape than the one you enlisted for."

Maren touched the fragment—the binding rite, the twin blood, the price—and her face went pale in a way I recognized from funerals. "This is real," she whispered. "This is the rite. This is how you unmake it." Then she looked at me, and her eyes were wet and terrible. "Do you understand what it costs? Do you understand what it asks?"

"I understand it asks," I said. "I've paid asking prices before." I thought of a square, a pyre, a name screamed across nineteen years. "Everyone in this Order has. The difference is that this time, we get to choose what we're buying."

Wren Calloway took the fragment with shaking hands. The scholar. Younger than my boots, eyes like a man who'd read too much and lived too little, and holding in his palms the reason the Codex had hunted him across a country. Behind him, half-hidden in the shadow of the mast, stood Astrid Eriksen—Elira's daughter, Elira's eyes, Elira's stubborn chin—and when our gazes met, I braced for the hatred I had earned.

She didn't give it to me. She just nodded, once, slow, like a door deciding whether to open.

Later, maybe. There would be a later, or there wouldn't, and either way I had cargo to deliver and a debt the size of a pyre, and for the first time in nineteen years I knew exactly which direction to walk.

Above the harbor, the palace blazed with light—all its windows lit at midnight, every tower crowned in flame-colored lanterns, the whole rotten pile glowing like a coal refusing to die. And I understood, looking at it, what the thing inside had already understood: the Ember was nearly out, the prophecy was nearly ripe, and it was done pretending.

It had worn a king for thirty years.

Tonight it would start wearing a war.

* * *

We sailed on the black tide, eleven traitors, one priest, one child, one scholar, one beast-speaker, and a hold full of the truth the Order had spent a century locking underground. Behind us, Crownspire burned its innocent and called it cleansing. Ahead of us lay the mountain, and beneath the mountain, the last dragon-fire, and beneath even that—buried deeper than any archive, older than any crown—the price.

I stood at the stern and watched the city shrink, and I took out the oilcloth packet, and I read the fragment's final line one more time by moonlight, the one the young scribe couldn't finish copying.

*One memory, cherished above all others, freely surrendered to the flame.*

I had spent my whole career taking things from people. It felt strange, and right, and terrifying, to be sailing toward the one fire that would ask me—or her, or him, or any of us—to give.

The mountain waited. So did the thing wearing the king.

We went anyway.
