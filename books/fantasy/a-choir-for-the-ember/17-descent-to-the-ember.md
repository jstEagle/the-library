## 17. Descent to the Ember

The stairs down into the mountain were older than the city above them, and they hated us for coming.

I don't know how else to describe it. Stone has a voice if you listen long enough—not words, not like wolves or ravens or even rats, but a kind of groaning patience, a deep slow *wait* that goes on for centuries and then, when someone comes clattering down with torches and bad intentions, curdles into something like resentment. The vaults beneath Crownspire had been waiting a hundred years for this, and they let us know it.

The first stair collapsed behind Halvard before he'd taken his third step.

"Subtle," he said, looking back at the rubble where we'd just been standing.

"The mountain's awake," I said. "It knows why we're here."

"Does it approve?"

"No."

"Good," said Pip, from somewhere near my elbow. "I'd hate to think we came all this way for a friendly mountain." She had a rat on each shoulder—Bramble and Thistle, she'd named them—and they rode her like tiny furry captains surveying their troops. "Rats say the way down gets worse. Rats also say there's cheese in the guardroom two levels up, so their priorities may be compromised."

"Their priorities are fine," I said. "Cheese is honest. It doesn't pretend to be anything else."

Wren walked beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched when the passage narrowed, which was often. He hadn't spoken much since the cells. Since Maren. None of us had, really. Grief makes its own silence, and we carried Sister Maren's silence down those stairs like a bier, all of us holding one edge of it.

She was still back there, at the door she'd held. Solveig's people had wanted to give her rites; Halvard had wanted to carry her out whole. In the end we'd wrapped her in her own exiled priest's cloak and left her where she fell, because the door behind us was closing and the Ember was guttering and dead women do not get less dead while you argue about them. We would come back for her. That was the promise. Promises to the dead are the easiest kind to keep, because the dead never ask you to prove it.

* * *

The second vault was flooded to our knees, and the water was warm.

That was the worst part. Not cold mountain water, not snowmelt—warm, blood-temperature, smelling faintly of iron and old smoke. The Ember's heat, bleeding down through the stone, dying by inches.

"It's like wading through soup someone forgot on the stove," Pip said, and then, quieter: "Sorry. That was disrespectful probably."

"No," I said. "It's exactly right. It *is* forgotten soup. A fire that kept everything alive for a thousand years, and now nobody tends it and it's going out."

Wren had the Codex strapped to his chest under his coat, and I could feel him touching it through the fabric, the way you touch a wound to check it's still there. He'd told me what the last pages said. He'd told me about the price. And I had stood there in the flickering light of his little lamp and let him believe I was hearing it for the first time.

Because what was I going to say? *"Oh yes, the wolf matriarch told me in chapter four"*? No. What I actually said was: "Then we pay it together, whatever it is."

And he'd looked at me with those scholar's eyes that saw too much and believed too easily, and he'd said, "Together," and I had felt like the greatest liar in Thornfell, which, given my competition—the crown-thing currently wearing the king—was saying something.

The wolves had told me the truth weeks ago, in their cold high den: *the Ember always exacts a price, and the price is always a memory freely given.* They hadn't known whose memory. Neither did I, not until the Ember itself spoke to me through the cell wall, ancient and grieving, and asked me—not demanded, *asked*, the way only truly old things ask—for something cherished.

I knew what it would take. I'd known since the cells. And I had decided, in the dark, alone, that I would pay it without telling anyone, because that's what you do when you love people. You eat the cost yourself and pass them the bread.

Astrid Eriksen, beast-speaker, smuggler's guide, champion of concealment. One last secret. My best one.

* * *

The third vault fought back.

We heard it before we saw it—stone grinding against stone, a rhythm like knuckles cracking—and then the golem unfolded itself from the far wall, and I understood that it hadn't been a wall at all. It had been *waiting*. Vaskr's last defense, or maybe its first: a guardian of packed earth and vault-stone, tall as three men, with eyes of banked coals that looked at us with something worse than hatred.

Indifference. We were dust to be swept.

"Halvard," I said.

"I see it."

"Pip, back. Wren, *back*."

"I can help—" Wren started.

"You can help by being alive to read your book at the end of this!" I snapped, and immediately regretted it, because fear sounds like anger and he didn't deserve either. But he went, pulling Pip with him, and that was the right call, and I'd apologize later if there was a later.

Halvard drew his smuggler's axe—he'd named it *Debt Collector*, which told you everything about Halvard—and grinned the grin he grinned before every bad idea. "Big fella's got no joints," he observed. "No joints means no tendons. No tendons means you can't cripple it. You can only bury it."

"Burying works," I said. "Burying is traditional."

The golem swung first. Halvard rolled under the arm—I will never understand how a man that size rolls like that—and put Debt Collector into its knee, which shattered his axe-head and accomplished nothing else except making the golem aware of him personally. It backhanded him across the chamber. He hit the wall the way cargo hits a dock, and slid down, and got up, because of course he did, because Halvard had never met a wall he wouldn't argue with.

Meanwhile I did the thing I do.

I reached out past the stone-groan and the coal-eyes and found what lived underneath. Because here's the secret nobody tells you about golems: something animates them. Something has to. And whatever it was—a splinter of Vaskr's will, a bound spirit, an ember-spark pressed into service—it was *miserable*. It had been standing in the dark for a hundred years, holding up a ceiling, and it was so tired, so endlessly, bone-deeply tired.

*I know,* I told it. *I know. I'm tired too.*

Its swing faltered. Just a fraction.

*You don't have to hold this wall anymore. The fire below is going out anyway. Everything you're guarding is ending. Let go.*

The coal-eyes flickered. The great arms sagged. And then, with a groan that shook grit from the ceiling, the golem sat down—actually *sat*, folding itself onto the floor like a laborer at the end of a double shift—and began, very slowly, to crumble into the heap it had always secretly wanted to be.

Pip peeked around Wren. "Did you just talk it into retirement?"

"Somebody had to offer it a pension."

Halvard limped over, rubbing his ribs. "Remind me never to make you angry at me."

"You couldn't afford it."

"That's what worries me."

* * *

Beyond the golem's chamber, the passages changed. Older. Rougher. The vault-stone gave way to raw mountain rock, and the warmth became heat, and the heat became something that pressed on you like a hand. The air shimmered. My skin tightened. Every animal instinct I owned screamed the same message: *fire below, old fire, first fire, GO DOWN CAREFULLY.*

The Codex, against Wren's chest, had begun to hum.

He stopped walking. "Astrid."

"I hear it."

"It's never done that."

"It's excited," Pip said. "Books purr. Everyone knows that."

But it wasn't purring. It was *straining*, the way a hound strains at a leash, and when Wren pulled it out and opened it, the pages were turning themselves, faster and faster, until they stopped at a page dense with fresh ink that was still—still, even now, in the cool of the tunnel—drying.

Wren read it. His face did something complicated.

"What?" I said. "What does it say?"

"It says..." He swallowed. "It says: *'The speaker of beasts stands at the last door. The thing in the crown offers her the face of her mother. She refuses.'*" He looked up. "Astrid, that hasn't happened yet. That's *ahead* of us."

"Then the book's finally useful," I said, "because now I know I refuse. Saves me deliberating."

"Astrid—"

"Wren." I took his face in my hands. His stubble was three days past respectable and his eyes were wet and brave and terrified, and I loved him with the whole stupid complete force of my heart, which is exactly why the next few hours were going to cost me everything. "Whatever's behind that door, whatever it offers, whatever happens—we walk through it together, and we walk out of it together, and anyone who tries to price-tag that can bill the mountain. Yes?"

"...Yes," he said, in the voice of a man agreeing to something he'll spend chapters trying to prevent.

"Good." I kissed him, quick, there in the hot dark with the mountain groaning around us. "Let's go meet the monster."

* * *

The last door wasn't a door. It was a mouth.

The passage simply ended, opening into a vast cavern lit from below by a light that had no business being as dim as it was. The Ember sat in a bowl of black glass at the cavern's center—a fire the size of a bonfire, when once, the stones told me, it had filled this chamber to the ceiling. It guttered as we watched. Flames leaned, straightened, leaned again, like an old man fighting sleep in his chair.

And between us and it, dressed in the king's body, stood Vaskr.

King Aldric's face smiled at us. It was a good face, weathered and kind-shaped, and the smile sat on it like a mask nailed slightly crooked. The king's hands hung loose at his sides. The king's eyes were wrong—too bright, too focused, lit from inside by something that had never once needed sleep.

"The beast-speaker," Vaskr said. Its voice was Aldric's voice, deep and worn, but layered underneath with something dry and vast, like wind through a burned forest. "And the reader. And the child who talks to vermin. And the smuggler." Its gaze settled on Halvard. "Your fleet is worth more than this errand, captain. Consider that."

"My fleet," Halvard said, "is worth exactly what I decide it's worth. Right now it's worth a rescue. Do keep up."

Vaskr ignored him. It had eyes only for me, and I felt its attention like a physical weight, like standing under a waterfall of cold.

"I know what you carry," it said. "Not in your pack. In your *head*. Your mother's face. Her voice, singing the burning-songs. The smell of the smoke." It stepped closer, boots ringing on black glass. "You lost her to fire, little speaker. Burned by men chanting Purifier hymns. And you have carried her ever since, every day, every year, because she is all you have of her."

"Stop," Wren said.

"She thinks of her sometimes and cannot breathe," Vaskr continued, relentless, gentle, horrible. "I can give her back. Not a ghost. Not a dream. The memory entire—her laugh, her hands, the way she said your name. Whole. Yours again, forever, as vivid as the day the smoke took her." It spread the king's hands. "All you must do is step aside. Let the fire finish dying. Walk away with your mother, and let the world above sort out its own crown."

The cavern was silent except for the Ember's failing breath.

And here is the truth I will tell honestly, because someone should: for one moment—one single, endless moment—I *wanted* it.

I saw her. Mother, in the kitchen of our crooked house, flour on her hands, singing off-key, calling me *little wren* before I'd ever met a real one. I smelled the bread. I heard the laugh. Thirty years of grief, undone, handed to me like a gift, and all it would cost was everyone in this cavern, and the king, and the city, and the light of the world.

One moment. Maybe two.

Then I looked at Wren. At Pip, with her rats pressed flat against her shoulders, trembling. At Halvard, bleeding from the ear, standing between us and the thing in the king's body without any plan whatsoever, because that was simply who Halvard was.

My mother burned so that I could live free. She did not burn so that I could buy her ghost with my friends' blood.

"No," I said.

Vaskr tilted the king's head. "Think carefully."

"I did. For thirty years. That was the thinking." I stepped forward, past Wren, past Halvard, until I stood closer to the thing in the crown than to my own family, and I let it see every inch of what I was. "You want to know what she gave me, that day on the pyre? Not fear. *Instructions.* Live loud. Love hard. Keep nothing hidden that matters. And I have spent my whole life breaking every one of those rules—hiding my gift, hiding my heart, hiding everything—until a fool scholar fell on top of me in a tavern and ruined all of it." I smiled, and I meant it, and it felt wonderful. "So no. I won't take my mother back as a bribe. I'll keep her the way she was: mine, and gone, and worth more than anything you're selling. And I'm going to walk past you now, and speak to the fire, and unmake thirty years of your smug tenancy. Step aside, Vaskr. Or don't. Either way, I'm done talking to puppets."

The silence stretched.

Then Vaskr laughed—a terrible sound, the king's laugh with ash in it—and the king's body straightened, and the temperature of the room dropped though the fire burned beside us.

"So be it," it said. "The binding thins at moonset. The ritual requires a crowned life, and the crowned man is *mine*, and you are four small creatures standing at the bottom of a hole." It raised one hand, and the shadows along the cavern walls began to move, peeling away from the stone, gathering. "Come, then, speaker. Let us see what your fire says when it hears your price."

Behind me, Wren's hand found mine. The Codex hummed against his chest, pages already turning toward words that hadn't happened yet.

Above us, faint through a mile of stone, the bells of Crownspire began to ring midnight.

Moonset was coming.
