## 18. The Last Dragon-Fire

### Wren

There is a particular kind of terror that comes from reading aloud in a room where the book is listening.

I had imagined the ritual a hundred times on the road down—Wren Calloway, heroic scholar, voice steady as a bell, pages turning themselves at the proper moments. In the imagining I wore better boots. In the imagining my hands did not shake.

In truth my hands shook so badly I could barely hold the Codex open, and the Codex, patient as a cat, waited for me to manage myself before it would let the words resolve.

The Ember's chamber was not what I had expected. I had pictured fire—a great hearth of the world, dragonflame banked like coals in a grate. Instead we descended into a vault of black stone shaped like the inside of a throat, and at its center burned something small. A fist of light. An ember, truly—the last coal of a fire that had once been a sky's worth of dragons—and it guttered with every pulse, dimming a little more each time, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

Around it, carved into the floor in spirals a century deep, ran Maren's heresy. The words she had been exiled for. *The crown is not holy. The king is not the crown. What is bound can be unbound.*

"It's all here," Maren whispered beside me, and her voice cracked on it. Thirty years of exile, of sleeping in ditches and being spat at in temple towns, and her crime had been carved into this floor by hands that had hoped someone would come read it. "They knew. Someone always knew."

"Then let's give them their answer," I said.

Above us—three vaults up, past the collapsing stairways and the groaning stone—Solveig held the line. We could hear it even here: the muffled thunder of Purifier shields, the shriek of steel, and beneath it all a sound I will never forget, a sound like a city-sized animal waking up angry. Vaskr had felt us coming. Vaskr was done pretending to be a king.

Halvard crouched by the tunnel mouth with his crossbow, looking like a man who had bet his whole fortune on a hand he didn't understand and was only now reading the cards. Pip sat against the wall with a rat on each shoulder and one in her hood, feeding them crumbs with the calm of a general reviewing troops. And Astrid stood closest to the Ember, close as anyone dared, her head tilted in that way she had when listening to something no one else could hear.

"Astrid," I said. "It's time."

She turned to me and smiled, and gods forgive me, but in that moment—with the mountain shaking and an ancient thing screaming through three floors of stone—I thought only that I wanted a hundred years of that smile, and that prophecies are a thief's trade, stealing futures from people who'd have gladly paid for them another way.

I began to read.

### Halvard

I've fought in maybe forty engagements in my life, and I'll tell you the honest truth about all of them: they were mostly waiting. Waiting for the arrow, waiting for the order, waiting for the other fool to make his mistake. Battle is ninety parts standing still and ten parts screaming.

This was the first battle I ever fought that was mostly singing.

Up top, holding the stairway against half the Purifier legion, Sister Maren had gotten it into her head that the ritual below needed a choir. Something about how binding-songs were sung into the world and had to be sung out of it, or some theology I didn't follow. What I followed was this: Maren started humming, then two of my smugglers picked it up, then six, and then there were twenty-odd cutthroats and cargo-runners standing on a broken staircase singing a forbidden hymn while arrows came out of the dark at them.

And it worked. That's the part that still turns my stomach, a little. It worked. Every note pushed back against whatever Vaskr was doing to the stone, and the men sang louder, and Sefa—who once threw a customs officer off the *Kestrel* for shorting our pay—took an arrow through the shoulder and kept singing without missing a beat, glaring at the wound like it had interrupted her.

I don't sing. Never have. My mother said I hummed like a cart with a bad axle. But I stood there in the smoke with my crossbow braced on a dead Purifier's shield and I moved my mouth along anyway, because you don't let your family carry a song alone.

Solveig fought beside me. The Inquisitor herself, white coat gone grey with ash, sword work like a hymn of its own. Three days ago she'd have put me in chains for saying what I'm about to say, so I'll say it quietly: she fought like a woman making up for lost time. Every stroke was an apology. Every Parry was thirty years of serving the wrong master, spent all at once.

"Halvard," she shouted between exchanges. "How many do you count?"

"More than we've got arrows!"

"Then we count faster!" And she laughed—laughed, in the middle of it—and I decided right then that if we lived, I was going to buy her a drink and ask her about Astrid's mother, because a woman who laughs like that has a story worth the price of the bottle.

The stone screamed. Not the men—the *stone*, the whole throat of the mountain, a sound like a hinge the size of the world. Dust fell. The song faltered.

Maren's voice rose over all of us, cracked and old and absolutely certain, and we found the melody again.

Below us, Wren began to read the words that mattered.

### Solveig

I burned a woman once. Her name was Lise Eriksen, and she spoke with beasts, and the church called it abomination and I called it duty, and I stood at the front of the crowd with my white coat clean and my heart clean and my hands folded, and I watched.

Thirty years I have told myself it was duty. Tonight, holding a stairway against the soldiers I had trained myself, I finally admitted what it was. It was fear. She looked at me as they lit the pyre—not with hate, which I could have carried, but with pity—and I have been running from that look ever since, into doctrine, into rank, into the clean certainty of the white coat.

Her daughter is below me now, doing what I helped make necessary.

A Purifier sergeant came around the landing with six men, and I knew every face. Young Brandt, who cried when his first prisoner wept. Old Corvin, who taught me the sword forms. They saw my coat and hesitated—one heartbeat, that's all a hesitation is—and in that heartbeat I shouted the thing I had no right to shout:

"The king is not the king! Look at his eyes! You've seen his eyes—you've *seen* them—"

Brandt lowered his shield a finger's width. Corvin didn't lower anything, but he stopped advancing, and stopping, for Corvin, was a revolution.

Then the mountain screamed again, and the far wall of the stairwell split like an egg, and what came through it was not a man.

I want to write down what Vaskr looked like when it finally stopped wearing Aldric. I find I cannot. My hand refuses. Let it be enough to say: it had borrowed a shape from our nightmares, the ones we don't tell our children we have, and it filled the stairwell with itself, and every soldier on both sides of that fight understood in the same cold instant that everything we had ever served, crowned or robed or sworn, had been furniture in its house.

Corvin broke and ran. Brandt dropped his shield entirely and stood staring, tears cutting through the ash on his face, whispering a child's prayer.

I raised my sword at the thing that owned my life's work and heard myself say, quite calmly, "Not today."

And then the song below swelled, and the stone under Vaskr blazed with carved light, and it shrieked—a real sound, a hurt sound—and I learned that monsters can be made to bleed after all, if you have the right words and enough fools willing to sing them.

### Astrid

The Ember spoke to me the way my mother used to speak when she was very tired: quietly, with long pauses, saving its strength for the words that mattered.

*You came,* it said. Its light pulsed, dimmed, pulsed. Each dimming lasted longer than the last. *Little speaker. You came too late by a year, and just in time by a breath.*

"I brought the words," I said. Wren's voice rolled above me, steady now, the scholar finding his footing at last, and behind his voice the smugglers' hymn came down through the stone like water through cracks. "I brought the choir. I brought—" I gestured helplessly at the vault, at the ragged miracle of us. "I brought everyone."

*Words bind and words loose,* the Ember said. *But the loosing has a price. There is always a price. You know this.*

My stomach went cold. "You told me. A memory. Freely given."

*A cherished one. The fire does not accept scraps.* The light leaned toward me, gentle as a hand. *It must be something you would fight to keep. That is what makes it fuel.*

I had known since the wolf-matriarch told me, four chapters of my life ago. I had carried it through fords and prisons and a kiss on a frozen rooftop, and I had told no one, and I had told myself I hadn't decided yet. Lies are easiest when you say them to yourself; you already know all the tells.

But I had decided. Somewhere on the road, watching Wren laugh at Pip teaching goats to pick pockets, I had decided.

"Not my mother," I said.

The Ember flickered. *She is the deepest root of you—*

"No." My voice came out harder than I meant. "You can't have her. She's the reason I can hear you at all. She died for this gift, and if I give her away, then they won—then the pyre gets her twice." I swallowed. "Take something else."

*There is nothing else that burns bright enough.*

"There is." And here is the strange thing, the thing I will not be able to explain later, because later I will not remember explaining it: I smiled. Even then. Even knowing. "There's a scholar up those stairs reading your unbinding out of a book that writes itself. He collides beautifully. He quotes things at inappropriate moments. He looks at me like I'm a text he's afraid to annotate." My eyes were stinging. "He's the best thing that ever happened to me, and he doesn't know what I'm about to do, and you will take him out of my head and leave the shape of him behind, and I will hate that shape every day for the rest of my life. Take him. Take my memories of Wren Calloway. Freely given."

The vault went quiet. Even the fighting above seemed to hold its breath.

*Little speaker,* the Ember said, and its voice was grief itself, ancient and tired. *Do you know what you offer?*

"I know exactly what I offer. That's why it's worth anything."

*It will not hurt less for being chosen.*

"Good," I said. "Let it cost. Let it cost like a real thing costs."

Above me, Wren's voice reached the final passage. The carved spiral blazed. The Ember drew a breath that pulled the air of the whole vault toward it—

And Vaskr tore free.

I felt it happen the way you feel a tooth come out: a horrible loosening, a wrongness suddenly absent. Through the stone, through three floors of screaming mountain, the thing that had worn a king for thirty years ripped itself loose from Aldric's body and rose, vast and furious, its binding unraveling thread by thread as Wren read and the smugglers sang and Solveig bled on the stairs above.

It came for the Ember. Of course it did. Starving things go to the food.

And the Ember said to me, in the last moment before everything happened at once: *Then hold my light, little speaker. Speak for me when I am too small to speak for myself. And when the price comes due—it will come due tonight—do not flinch.*

"I never flinch," I lied, one last time, and stepped forward into the firelight as the dark came down the stairs toward us, wearing the shape of nightmares, wearing the face of a king, wearing thirty years of stolen country.

Behind me, Wren kept reading. He didn't know yet. He didn't know, and I had one breath left to be glad of that.

The Ember flared. Vaskr screamed. And somewhere above, an old priestess's heresy became true at last, sung by smugglers, in the dark, at the bottom of the world.
