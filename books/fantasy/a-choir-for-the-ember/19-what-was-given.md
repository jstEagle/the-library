## 19. What Was Given

I have been a passenger in my own skull for thirty years, so you will forgive me if I take a moment to describe what it is like to be the driver again.

It is like waking from a dream in which you were drowning, only to find yourself drowning.

The binding thins at the hour of the guttering — that was always the pattern, one hour each night when Vaskr's grip loosened and I could feel my own hands as mine. But tonight the chamber shakes, and the grip does not merely loosen. It tears. Somewhere above me — or below me; in my own body, direction is a matter of opinion — there is shouting, singing, the smell of scorched stone, and a girl's voice speaking to fire in a language that has no words but understands them anyway.

And then I am alone inside myself for the first time since I was nine-and-twenty years old.

My hand moves. My hand. I watch it rise toward my face with the fascination of a man watching a bird land on his finger. I touch my own cheek. The cheek is wet. Apparently some part of me has been weeping all along, in the dark, where Vaskr could not see and did not think to look.

"Hello," I say aloud, and my voice cracks like an old door. "Hello, Aldric."

It sounds mad, greeting oneself. Thirty years of madness earns a man certain liberties.

* * *

Let me tell you about my brother, since no one else living remembers him rightly, and the histories — the ones written by men who feared the crown more than they loved truth — have made him a monster in the clean, simple way that histories make monsters.

His name was Vaskr. He was born four minutes before me, which he never let me forget, and he had a laugh like a cart going downhill, uncontrolled and joyful and slightly dangerous. We were twins, and in Thornfell the old law says twins are one soul split between two bodies, and so whatever crown came to one must be shared by both. That was the bargain our father struck with the Ember before we were born: two kings, one throne, one fire to keep them honest.

The Ember kept us honest. That was its whole purpose. It burned beneath Crownspire and it listened, and when a crowned head turned toward cruelty, the fire dimmed, and everyone knew, and shame did its quiet work. For nine years of my reign it worked. Vaskr sat beside me at councils and made jokes during treaties and stole food off my plate because he said a king who couldn't defend his own bread couldn't defend a kingdom.

Then came the famine winter. Then came the Purifiers' founding, and the burnings, and the first time I signed an order I knew was wrong because stopping it would cost me the lords whose gold kept the kingdom fed. The Ember dimmed. Everyone saw. And Vaskr — my brother, my other half, the better half if I am honest, though he'd have argued just to argue — stood up in council and said, "My brother's heart is failing the fire. Let mine serve instead."

He meant it as sacrifice. I know that now. I did not know it then, because I was proud and exhausted and thirty years old and carrying a kingdom on a spine that had begun to bend. When the priests came with the binding rite — the one Maren died for calling heresy, and she was right, gods rot her enemies gently, she was right — I believed we were sharing the burden. One soul, two bodies, the fire fed by consent.

What the rite actually did was fold Vaskr into the Ember's shadow and fold his shadow into me, and something in that folding went wrong. Something hungry got in through the seam. The thing that wore my face afterward was not my brother. It was what remained of my brother after the fire chewed on him for a century of minutes, and it learned quickly that grief tastes sweeter than joy, and control sweeter than either.

I watched it rule through my eyes. I felt everything. That was the cruelty of the binding — not blindness, but perfect sight without a single finger's worth of agency. I felt my wife's hand go cold in mine and could not squeeze back. I heard her teaching the palace hawks to speak, laughing at their crooked words, and I loved her so much in that hour that I think — I have always thought — the love itself was what finally taught Vaskr fear. A thing that feeds on a kingdom cannot digest a widow's grief. It choked on her dying. It has been choking ever since, and taking it out on the world.

She taught me beast-speech before she died. She said every king should know a language his ministers don't, in case he ever needs to say something true. I laughed then. I am not laughing now, lying on cold stone while a smuggler's daughter speaks to the last dragon-fire in the tongue my wife gave me, and I understand every word.

* * *

The chamber is chaos and I am useless in it, which is familiar enough that I almost find it comforting.

Astrid Eriksen stands at the edge of the Ember's basin with her arms open and her head bowed, the way one stands before a thing too large for reverence to hold upright. The fire before her is a candle-end, blue at the heart, guttering — and yet when she speaks to it, it leans toward her like a dog hearing its name. I cannot hear what she says. Beast-speech is not sound, exactly; it is meaning wearing sound as a coat, and she has taken the coat off entirely. She is speaking pure meaning into the fire, and the fire is answering, and the answer makes her flinch.

Behind her, the scholar — Calloway, the heretic with the book — is reading from the Codex in a voice that shakes and steadies and shakes again. The smugglers are singing. Saints preserve me, the smugglers are singing Maren's heresy, the binding-hymn turned inside out, an unbinding-song, and their voices are rough and off-key and absolutely certain, and I think: this is what a choir is. Not voices that match. Voices that refuse to stop.

And above, at the stair, Solveig stands with a drawn sword against her own Purifiers, holding the line, screaming orders in a voice I remember from another lifetime — she was my wife's friend, once, before my wife's death taught her that grief is best worn as armor.

Vaskr comes apart from me like a rotting bandage peeling off a wound.

I will not describe it. Some things a man keeps even from his memoirs. Suffice to say that when it is done, I am kneeling on the stone in a puddle of my own sweat and sick, and the thing that wore me is standing across the chamber wearing nothing at all — a shape like a man made of smoke and hunger, my brother's outline filled with something else, something that looks at Astrid and says, in a voice like a hinge that has never been oiled:

"You would give it your memories. All of them. Even him." Its head tilts toward Calloway. "Even the ones you haven't made yet."

"I know the price," Astrid says. Her voice does not shake. I envy her that. I have been shaking for thirty years and never once managed to hide it. "Freely given. That's the covenant. That's the whole of it."

"It will take the wrong ones," Vaskr says. "Fire is not careful. Fire takes what burns brightest." And here it does something terrible: it smiles with my brother's mouth. "Give me the mother instead. I kept her, little speaker. Her voice, her face, the smell of her kitchen. I have carried them thirty years waiting for someone worth trading them to. Betray the book, betray the boy, walk away, and I will put your mother back inside your head whole."

I see it land. I see the want move through her like weather. And I see her stand in the middle of that weather and say, quietly, "You're lying. The Ember doesn't keep. It only asks. You're the one who keeps, and everything you keep rots."

Vaskr stops smiling.

That is when I reach for my sword.

* * *

It lies three feet from my knee — the sword Dawnfall, my father's, the one Vaskr retired to a wall the day the binding took, because a puppet needs no blade. My arm has not obeyed me in thirty years. I look at the sword and I do not trust the reaching.

But the Ember is listening. The Ember has always listened. And the Ember knows — it has known since the famine winter, it has known through every year of my hollow reign — which twin is asking.

My fingers close on the hilt.

The metal is cold, then warm, then singing. Old law runs through it like blood through a vein: *the binding required a crowned life.* I heard the priests whisper it the night they folded my brother into me. A binding of that size wants an anchor, and anchors of that size are only found in two places — a crown, or a grave. They used a crown. They told themselves it was mercy.

It was a debt. And debts to fire come due.

"Aldric." Vaskr has turned to face me, and for one moment — one naked moment — the smoke falls away from its eyes and what looks out at me is my brother, four minutes older, laughing like a cart going downhill, terrified. "*Brother.*"

"I know," I say. My voice is wrecked and steady. "I know what you were. I know what they made you. I have spent thirty years being sorry in a place where sorry does no good." I get one knee under me. Then the other. I stand, and standing feels like the first honest act of my reign. "This isn't punishment, Vaskr. It's the end of a bad bargain. Both halves of it."

"The fire will take you with me," it says. "Crowned life. Yours is the only crown left in the room."

"Yes," I say. "I did the arithmetic some time ago. In the dark. There wasn't much else to do."

Behind me, Astrid says my name — my real name, the one no one has spoken aloud in thirty years — and there is grief in it already, grief spent in advance, and I want to tell her not to waste it. Grief is heavy. She'll need her arms free for what comes next. But there is no time, and anyway, kings are owed nothing, least of all comfort.

I raise Dawnfall.

"For the famine winter," I say, walking forward, "which was mine to prevent. For the burnings, which bore my seal. For my wife, whom I could not hold. For my brother, whom I could not save." The Ember's light is rising behind Astrid now, climbing from candle-blue to gold, and the heat of it walks up my arm and into my chest like a hand remembering an old friend. "And for every person in this kingdom who looked at my face on the coin and hoped I was kinder than I was."

Vaskr lunges. Of course it does. Hunger does not negotiate.

I meet it at the basin's edge, and the sword goes through smoke and brother both, and the Ember rises up around us like a wave deciding, at long last, to be a wave.

* * *

Burning is not what they say. They say it is quick, or they say it is agony, and both are lies told by people who have never been kindling for a god.

Burning is being *read*. The fire opens you like a book and reads every page, and where it finds rot it cleans, and where it finds love it lingers, the way any reader lingers on the passages that matter. It reads my reign and grieves. It reads my marriage and stays a long while on the hawk-language, on her laugh, on the last morning when she brought me bread she'd stolen off my own plate because she said a king who couldn't defend his breakfast deserved to lose it. The Ember reads that page twice.

Then it reads my brother. What is left of him, down in the deep of the thing I am killing. And the Ember — ancient, grieving, nearly gone an hour ago, and now blazing whole because a girl on the mountain road once taught wolves to trust her — the Ember does what I could not do in thirty years.

It forgives him.

I feel it happen. I feel my brother's smoke-shape loosen, unravel, and underneath the hunger there is a boy of nine-and-twenty with a cart-wheel laugh, and the fire holds him the way you hold something breakable, and he looks at me across the burning and says — not in words, in meaning, in the coatless language my wife taught me —

*Told you my heart could carry it.*

*It could,* I answer. *It always could. It was the rest of us that couldn't.*

And then the fire closes over us both, and it is warm, and it is, at the very last, exactly as bright as it was the day we were born under it.

* * *

I do not die all at once. The Ember is courteous that way. It lets me stay long enough to see.

I see the light pour up through the vaults of Crownspire like dawn arriving early and unapologetic. I see the rats cheering, or whatever rats do instead, and Pip laughing with her head thrown back, and Halvard weeping without shame, big hands over big face, a man discovering that his heart survived thirty years of pretending he didn't have one. I see Solveig drop her sword in the doorway and sit down on the stairs like a woman whose whole religion just apologized to her.

I see Wren Calloway close the Codex and hold it against his chest, and I see him turn to Astrid with an expression I recognize. I wore it once. It is the expression of a man who has just understood the price of a thing after the bill has already been paid.

Because the fire is keeping its books. I can feel it doing the arithmetic even as I come apart. A rekindled flame of that magnitude wants fuel, and the covenant is specific: *a cherished memory, freely surrendered.* Astrid offered. The fire reached for what burned brightest in her — and I feel its hesitation, feel it weigh the mother-memory against the newer thing, the scholar-thing, the shape of a collision outside a tavern and every page that followed — and I want to shout *no, take me instead, I'm already burning, I'm cheap at the price* — but the covenant does not take substitutions. Freely given means freely given. It means hers.

The fire takes Wren Calloway out of Astrid Eriksen's memory the way you lift a hot coal out of a hearth: carefully, completely, leaving no ash behind.

She doesn't cry out. That is the worst of it. She sways, and the scholar catches her elbow, and she looks at him with the polite blankness of a stranger, and somewhere in the vaults above us a choir of smugglers sings the last verse of an unbinding-song to a king they never trusted and a fire they never doubted, and I spend my final moment of consciousness wishing — genuinely, specifically, with the last coin of a bankrupt soul — that I could stay long enough to help her relearn him.

Someone should. Love that size shouldn't have to start over alone.

But that is not my chapter anymore.

The light takes me. It is warm. My brother is laughing somewhere ahead of me, like a cart going downhill, and my wife's hand finds mine in the gold, and this time — this time — I squeeze back.

* * *

They will find the sword first, I expect. Dawnfall, cooling in the ash at the basin's rim, its work finished, its debt paid. Then the crown, if Vaskr's people didn't melt it — a circlet of black iron with nothing left inside it, hollow as a promise. Let them bury the crown in the ash. Let them plant something over it. Fruit, maybe. My wife would have insisted on fruit.

And let them write it down honestly, whoever writes the histories now. Not *the king was saved.* Not *the king was slain.* Write: *the king was returned, briefly, and spent himself well.*

There are worse epitaphs. I collected enough of them, these thirty years, to know.

Above me — above my ashes, rather — the Ember blazes whole for the first time in a generation, and its light climbs the mountain and touches the snowfields and the passes and the road where, if the Codex has anything left to say about it, two strangers are going to meet again in a year's time, and one of them will ask the other's name, and the name will mean nothing to her, and everything will begin anyway.

Good, I think, as the gold closes over me like water. Good. Begin anyway.

That was always the whole secret of it.
