## 20. The Girl Who Forgot

I woke to the smell of goat.

Not the smell of *Pip's* goat specifically—though Bramble was there too, judging by the warm weight of her chin on my ankle—but the general, unmistakable reek of a room full of animals who had decided I was worth guarding. Which meant either I was dying or something important had happened while I slept. Given that my whole body felt like someone had wrung me out like Halvard's laundry and hung me up to dry, I was betting on both.

"Careful," said Pip, from somewhere near my elbow. "She's doing the face."

"What face?" asked Halvard's voice, from further away, gruffer than usual in the way it gets when he's frightened and pretending not to be.

"The one where she looks like she's about to argue with the ceiling."

"I don't argue with ceilings," I croaked. "Ceilings never win."

The room laughed—a wet, ragged laugh, the kind people do when they've been crying and are trying very hard to stop because crying would mean admitting how bad it had been. My eyes were still gummed shut with sleep, so I did what any sensible smuggler does: I took inventory by ear.

Halvard, near the door, arms crossed—I could hear leather creaking. Pip on the bed beside me, small and fidgety, probably having been told not to touch me at least four times. Sister Maren—

No. The silence where Maren should have been. A candle guttered somewhere, and nobody moved to fix it, and I understood before my eyes even opened that we had paid for this room in more than coin.

I opened my eyes.

Firelight. Stone walls. The undercity sanctuary beneath Crownspire, the one the rats had shown us, now hung with drying herbs and somebody's mended socks. Solveig stood against the far wall in her white coat, which she'd stopped wearing properly—it hung open like she couldn't decide whether she was jailer or guest. And Aldric—

Aldric wasn't there. And the way everyone looked when I glanced toward the empty chair where he might have been told me everything about that, too.

"You're awake," Pip said, and then ruined the gravity of it entirely by bursting into tears and hugging me hard enough to crack a rib I was fairly sure had already been cracked.

"Pip. Pip. Ribs."

"Sorry." She didn't let go. "You were asleep for four days. Four days. I talked to you every day. Bramble says you could hear goats even asleep but I think Bramble is flattering herself."

"Bramble is always flattering herself," I said into her hair, and held on, because holding on was free and everything else lately had cost more than I wanted to count.

* * *

They fed me soup. It was terrible. Halvard had made it, which explained the terrible part; the man could navigate a mountain pass blindfolded in a blizzard but treated seasoning like a personal enemy. I ate all of it anyway, because my body informed me that if I didn't, it would simply begin eating itself out of spite.

And through all of it—the soup, the stories, Pip's breathless account of rats in the walls and Solveig turning her coat and the door Maren held until it could not be held anymore—one person sat quietly across the fire and did not speak.

The scholar.

I knew him. That was the strange thing. I knew him the way you know a word you've read a thousand times but can't quite say aloud. Brown hair going grey early at the temples. Ink stains on his fingers like he'd been born holding a pen. Eyes that were red-rimmed and careful, careful, careful, watching me the way you watch a bird with a broken wing—wanting to help, terrified of making it worse.

"Wren," Halvard said, following my gaze. "Wren Calloway. He carried you down out of the vaults. Nearly killed himself doing it."

"I remember the vaults," I said slowly. "Falling. The fire coming back. I remember—" I reached for it and my hand closed on smoke. "I remember singing. Everyone singing. Maren's heresy, the one that got her exiled, and Halvard bellowing it like a sea shanty."

"Aye," Halvard said softly. "We made a proper choir of it."

"And then?"

"And then the fire took its price," Wren said.

His voice was quiet and level and it cost him visibly, like each word was a stone he had to lift with both hands. I looked at him—at this stranger who had carried me, who watched me like that—and felt something turn over in my chest. Grief without an object. Love without a memory. It was the most unsettling sensation of my life, and I had once spent a full night negotiating with a bear who believed I owed him honey.

"The Ember takes a memory," I said. "Freely given. I know that much. The wolves told me." I frowned. "Did I tell anyone? What I gave?"

Silence. The specific, coordinated silence of five people who had agreed beforehand not to answer that question.

"You promised," I said to Halvard.

"We promised," he agreed, "not to tell you what you paid. Your choice. Your price. You were very clear about it, Astrid. You made me swear on my ship, and I don't swear on my ship for just anything."

"I hate you."

"I know, lass." His voice cracked on it. "I know."

* * *

That night I lay awake listening to the fire and taking inventory of myself, the way I used to inventory a cargo hold before a crossing. Everything seemed present. My mother's face—there, sharp as ever, the burn-scarred memory of her hands teaching me to whistle to a sparrow. My first wolf, the winter I was nine. Halvard finding me half-frozen at fourteen and pretending he'd only stopped for directions. The taste of stolen plums. Every scar, accounted for.

Except.

There was a shape in me, the way there's a shape left in snow after something warm has lain there and gone. Not an absence exactly—an absence you can ignore. This was worse. An absence with edges. Something had fit here, perfectly, for months, and now it was gone and my heart kept reaching for it anyway, like a tongue returning again and again to the gap where a tooth was.

I got up. I wrapped a blanket around my shoulders and went to sit by the fire, because sleeping was impossible and I have never in my life been good at lying still with a question.

He was already there. Of course he was. The scholar sat with a book in his lap he wasn't reading, and when I came over he didn't smile—he looked almost afraid to smile—and shifted to make room on his bench without being asked.

"You're Wren," I said.

"Yes."

"Halvard says you carried me."

"It was mostly downhill," he said. "And mostly other people. I just refused to put you down."

"Why?"

The question came out sharper than I intended. He flinched, and I watched him wrestle with whatever answer lived behind his teeth, and finally he said, gently, "Because you're worth carrying."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. But it's the one I'm allowed to give."

I pulled my knees up and stared into the fire. "Everyone keeps looking at me like I'm a funeral. Pip cries when I say your name. Halvard can't meet my eyes. Solveig—" I gestured vaguely at the white coat draped over a chair like a shed skin. "Solveig apologized to me twice today and I don't even know her well enough to accept it. So either I did something magnificent or something unforgivable, and given that we apparently won, I'm guessing magnificent. And there's a hole in me shaped like something I can't name, and every single person in this room knows what filled it except me."

Wren was quiet for a long time.

"You asked the Ember for something," he said at last. "Something enormous. And the Ember set its price, and you paid it, freely, knowing what it cost. That's all I can tell you. You knew. You chose. You didn't hesitate."

"Was it worth it?" I asked. "Whatever I bought. Was it worth the hole?"

He looked at me then, and his eyes were wet, and he smiled—not the careful not-smile from before but something broken and true, like sunrise through a cracked window.

"Yes," he said. "It was worth everything. I'd stake my life on it. In fact—" and here his voice did something complicated, "—several of us did."

* * *

The days found their shape. That's the cruel mercy of grief, even grief you can't name: the body insists on continuing. I learned the new city by walking it. Crownspire without Vaskr was a different animal entirely—people spoke to beasts openly in the markets now, a girl selling apples while her mule complained about the price of oats, a cat directing traffic outside the baker's with the air of a minor official. Aldric's edicts had been struck from the law-books; the Purifiers' coats had been dyed brown, which Solveig said was symbolic and Pip said was just funny.

I should have been happy. Some mornings I almost was.

But the shape followed me. It followed me through the market, where I caught myself buying two of something and not knowing why. It followed me onto the rooftops, where I kept drifting to one particular ledge above the frozen quarter and standing there feeling like I was waiting for someone. It followed me into sleep, where I dreamed of a kiss I have no memory of receiving—just the *fact* of it, warm and certain, like a word on the tip of the tongue that never comes.

Once I found Pip sitting on the steps of the sanctuary, feeding Bramble crusts, and she looked up at me with those too-old eyes and said, "Do you want me to tell you?"

"Tell me what?"

"Anything. Everything. I remember all of it. I remember the river ford, and the bear, and the rooftop, and—" Her voice wobbled. "I remember you laughing. You laughed more than you ever let yourself, before. Did you know that? Around him."

My chest did the thing. The gap-thing. The tooth-thing.

"He made you brave," Pip said. "That's all I'll say. He made you brave, and you made him real. And you told us not to tell you, and I'm keeping my promise, but Astrid—" She stood up, fierce, chin trembling. "You should know that whatever's missing, it wasn't taken. You *gave* it. There's a difference. A big one. You were magnificent."

I crouched down and hugged her, because there was nothing else to do with magnificence when you couldn't remember owning it.

* * *

Wren and I developed a routine, though neither of us named it. He read in the evenings; I whittled things I didn't need; the fire did most of the talking. Sometimes I caught him watching me with that expression—the one like a man reading a book whose ending he knows and cannot change—and sometimes I caught myself watching him back, and neither of us ever said a word about it, because saying a word about it would have required words we didn't have.

One night I broke first.

"Do I know you?" I asked. "From before. Before the vaults. Did we—were we friends?"

He turned a page he hadn't read. "We met on a mountain road," he said. "I walked into you outside a tavern in Thornfell's shadow. Knocked your satchel into the mud. You threatened to have a raven follow me home."

"That sounds like me."

"It was very much you." He almost smiled. "You called me 'the disaster with the book.' For weeks."

"Weeks." I turned that over. Weeks meant time. Time meant—my stomach dropped through the floor, because the shape in me suddenly had dimensions, suddenly had a history, and I understood with horrible clarity that whatever the gap was, it hadn't been small. It hadn't been a week or a kindness or a debt. "Wren. How long did I know you?"

He was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"Astrid—"

"How long?"

"Long enough," he said, "that keeping my promise is the hardest thing I have ever done. Harder than the vaults. Harder than the burning pages." He closed the book. "You asked me once why I stayed, when staying hurt. Do you remember asking me that?"

"No," I whispered.

"Well," he said softly. "You did. And I stayed because some things are worth witnessing even when they break your heart. Especially then."

I wanted to shake him. I wanted to cry. I wanted, more than anything, to reach into my own chest and pull the shape out and hold it up to the firelight and finally see what it was. Instead I said, "Will you at least tell me if I was happy?"

His eyes went bright and wrecked and certain, all at once.

"You were," he said. "You are. You will be."

* * *

Later—much later, when the sanctuary had gone quiet and even the rats had tucked themselves away—I sat alone with the truth I was building out of splinters. I didn't need to be told. That was the worst discovery, the one I made in the dark with my own heartbeat for company: I didn't need anyone to break their promise. The evidence was everywhere. It was in Halvard's gentleness with me, a man who loved like a landslide and mourned like one too. It was in the second apple in my market basket. It was in the way the whole company orbited Wren Calloway like he was fragile glass, and the way Wren orbited me like I was the sun he'd lost permission to look at.

I had loved someone. Deeply enough that the Ember had wanted it—had *chosen* it, out of everything I carried, because the Ember takes what glows brightest. And I had walked into that fire with my eyes open and traded the best of myself to save a king, a city, and a fire that keeps the dark off the world.

I hated it. Not the sacrifice—I'd make it again, and some stubborn core of me knew it. I hated the *hole*. I hated that love could be spent like coin, that the heart could pay a debt the mind never sees. I hated that somewhere in me, some piece of me was still waiting on a rooftop for a kiss I'd already had, still buying two apples, still turning toward a voice that had gone quiet.

And underneath the hating, quieter than all of it, something my mother used to say rose up like a coal refusing to die:

*The mind forgets, little storm. The heart keeps its own ledger.*

I sat by the dead fire until dawn, hand pressed flat against the ache in my chest, and made myself a promise in return: whatever the shape was, whoever had filled it—I would find them again. Not because I remembered. Because my heart kept insisting, in its stubborn beast-speech way, that it was still speaking to someone.

Across the room, Wren turned a page in the dark. I heard him pause. I heard him write something down—quick, careful, the scratch of a pen that sounded almost like hope.

And I thought: *whatever he's writing, I intend to read it someday.*

Even if I have to become a stranger to do it.
