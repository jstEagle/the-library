## 21. Pages Written Forward

The Codex of Ash had been, for most of its hundred-year life, a book that behaved like a debt collector. It wrote your name down. It wrote what you'd lose. It wrote the hour of it, in ink that smelled faintly of struck flint, and it never once wrote *I'm sorry*.

So when I sat down in Halvard's back room with a mug of plum brandy and the book open on my knees, and found the page after Astrid's sacrifice already filled—*the fire takes what was freely given; the scholar watches her wake and does not tell her*—I did not weep, because I had made a promise about that too, and promises were suddenly the only currency I trusted.

What I did instead was turn the page.

Blank.

I turned another. Blank. Another. The paper was good paper, thick as sailcloth, the kind that costs more than a mule, and every leaf of it was empty as a winter sky. A century of prophecy, and now nothing. It felt like watching a river stop mid-current—not peaceful, exactly. More like being owed something.

"You're frowning at it," Pip said from the doorway. She had a rat on each shoulder these days, which she claimed was fashionable. "Books can't help their faces."

"This one can," I said. "This one's been making faces at me since the day I met it."

"Maybe it's finished." She came in and looked over my arm, unbothered by the blankness the way only children and goats are unbothered by anything. "Maren used to say prayers end. That's what makes them prayers and not curses."

Sister Maren had said a great many things, most of them heretical, all of them true, and she had died holding a door so that the rest of us could walk through it. I still caught myself turning to tell her things. I suspected I would for the rest of my life, and I had decided to consider that not grief but evidence—that she had mattered enough to leave a shape behind.

"It's a book of prophecies," I said. "It doesn't get to be finished. It gets to be wrong, or fulfilled, or burned by someone with better sense than me."

Pip scratched one of the rats under the chin. "You read the last entry yet? The real last one?"

"There isn't—" I stopped. Because there was. I had turned past it too fast, the way you skip the final pages of a letter you're afraid to finish. I went back three leaves, hands not entirely steady, and there it was, in that same flint-smelling ink, small and patient as a seed:

*A year hence, on the mountain road above Thornfell, two strangers will meet. One will ask the other's name. Neither will know why the question matters. Both will answer anyway.*

No date beyond the season. No deaths appended. No price listed. Just a meeting, and a question, and the strange mercy of the word *strangers*.

I read it four times. On the fourth reading my eyes blurred, which I attributed to the brandy, and which Pip—being eleven and merciless—did not attribute to anything at all, just watched me with the flat sympathy of a child who has seen too much to be fooled and too little to pretend otherwise.

"That's her," Pip said. Not a question.

"That's her," I said.

"And you're the other stranger." She said it gently, which from Pip was more alarming than mockery. "You could stay away. Let some other scholar have the road."

I closed the book. The blank pages whispered against each other like snow settling.

"Pip," I said, "do you know what I spent six years doing before all this?"

"Losing arguments to goats?"

"Also that. No—I spent six years proving that the Codex was real. Six years of archivists laughing at me, of my thesis rejected twice, of my own father telling me that some books are just cages people build around the future because the present frightens them. And he was right, do you know that? He was entirely right. The Codex is a cage. It caged Astrid. It caged a king for thirty years. It caged me into believing I was chosen to witness rather than to help, and I nearly let that belief make me useless at the exact moment usefulness mattered." I tapped the cover. "But here's the thing nobody laughed hard enough at: a cage is still built by someone. Page by page. Choice by choice. Somebody wrote the first line, and somebody kept writing, and somewhere along the way everyone forgot they were allowed to put the pen down."

"So put the pen down," Pip said.

"I intend to. But first—" I opened the book again to that last entry, and I felt something in my chest that I hadn't felt since before the Purifiers, since before the cells, since before I watched the woman I love look at me with polite, warm, utterly vacant eyes and ask if we'd met. "First I want to know what this is. Because it isn't a cage, Pip. Look at it. There's no doom in it. There's no *unless*. There's no price. It's just—"

"A date," she said.

"A date," I agreed, and heard how absurd it was, and laughed until the rats complained.

* * *

Halvard found me an hour later on the roof, where I'd gone to be alone with the book and the mountain air and the specific misery of a man re-reading his own future like a lovesick boy re-reading a letter.

He lowered himself onto the shingles beside me with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and handed me a second mug without asking. That was Halvard. He'd smuggled contraband through half the passes of Thornfell for twenty years, lost crew, gained family, buried a priest, and his response to all of it was to keep a kettle hot and an opinion to himself until asked.

"Well?" he said.

"It's done writing," I said. "Prophecies, I mean. Blank pages from here to the back cover."

"Huh." He drank. Below us Crownspire was rebuilding itself in fits and starts, scaffolding climbing the palace like ivy, the great bells ringing off-schedule because the new council couldn't agree on a schedule and Aldric's old one had been, as Halvard put it, "written by a crown-thing with no ear for music." Somewhere down there Solveig was reorganizing the Purifiers into something that might eventually deserve a name other than terror. Somewhere down there, Astrid was teaching the market children the names of the pigeons, and the pigeons were telling her things, and everyone pretended this was normal, because in a free city it was becoming normal, and that fact alone could have made me weep on any given Tuesday.

"You've read the last entry," Halvard said. Not a question either. Smugglers and children—the whole company had stopped asking me questions months ago, on the theory that I'd answer them anyway.

"Yes."

"And?"

"And it says we meet on the mountain road a year from now. As strangers."

Halvard was quiet for a while. A long while, for him. When he spoke, his voice had the gravel-soft quality it got when he talked about his dead, or his crew, or anything else he loved. "My father ran cargo his whole life. Never once asked where a road ended. Said roads aren't for ending, they're for keeping going on." He turned the mug in his big hands. "You're treating that last page like a sentence. It's not a sentence, boy. It's a route."

"The difference being?"

"A sentence tells you where you stand. A route tells you where to walk." He drained his mug and stood, knees cracking like green wood. "She doesn't remember you. That's a fact. You remember her. That's also a fact. Facts don't care which one hurts. But a road—" he pointed one blunt finger at the mountains, blue and white and indifferent in the evening light, "—a road goes both directions. Always has. Always will."

He left me there with the brandy and the book and the mountains, and I did what scholars do, which is think too much and then write it down.

Here is what I wrote, in my own hand, in my own notebook, with my own stubborn mortal ink:

*The Codex told us we would die unless we reached the Ember. We reached the Ember. Maren died. Aldric died. Astrid paid a memory of me, freely given, and the fire took it, and the fire lives, and the thing that wore a king for thirty years is ash. Every word the Codex ever wrote came true—but not one word of it happened because the Codex wrote it. It happened because people chose. Halvard chose loyalty over profit. Solveig chose truth over doctrine. Maren chose a door. Astrid chose to speak to a dying fire with her whole heart open, knowing the cost, and paid it without telling me, because she loved me enough to spare me the choosing.*

*Which means the Codex was never a prophet.*

*It was a promise.*

*Someone—a scribe, a seer, a frightened man or woman a hundred years ago—looked at the shape of what was coming and wrote it down, not to trap us but to warn us: here is the road, here is the toll, here is who must pay it. And then they wrote the way out. Reach the Ember before it gutters. Two names. One chance. A warning is only a promise kept by whoever's brave enough to act on it.*

*And the last page isn't a prophecy at all. It's a promise too. Mine.*

I closed the notebook. The wind came down off the crown of Thornfell carrying woodsmoke and pine and, faintly, the mineral breath of the mountain's deep places, where a fire now burned whole and golden and would burn, the wolves told Astrid, for a thousand years. She talked to it sometimes, she said. The Ember. She didn't remember why she felt she could, but she could, and the fire answered her like an old friend, and neither of them mentioned the cost.

Neither did I. I had promised.

* * *

Winter passed the way winters do in a city learning to be free: badly, loudly, and with a great deal of communal soup. I stayed. There was no reason to stay, academically speaking—my thesis had been vindicated posthumously by events, the University offered me a lectureship, my father sent a letter that managed to apologize and condescend in the same paragraph, a feat I hadn't known possible—but I stayed because leaving felt like a second forgetting, and I had promised myself I wouldn't make Astrid pay for my absence too.

We were friends. That was the word we'd all agreed on, carefully, the way you step around a frozen pond. Friends. She taught me wolf-courtesy and raven-etiquette. I taught her to read Old Imperial, and she learned faster than any student I'd ever had, and when she laughed at my pronunciation of the gutter-cats' dialect I felt the laugh go through me like sunlight through a window in a house I no longer lived in.

Once—only once—I almost broke my promise. Midwinter, at the festival, she caught her sleeve on a brazier and I grabbed her wrist to steady her, and she looked up at me with the firelight in her eyes and said, "Wren. Why do you look at me like I'm a book you've read?"

And I said, "Because you're the best thing I ever studied," which was true, and safe, and cost me nothing, and she laughed and cuffed my shoulder and called me a flatterer, and I carried the moment home like a coal in cupped hands and set it down carefully and did not let it burn anything.

Spring came. Then summer, fat and green. And then the year began to run out, the way years do, one day at a time, each one a page the Codex refused to write.

I checked the book weekly. Blank, blank, blank. Except the last entry, unchanged, patient: *a meeting on the mountain road, a year hence, between two strangers.* The season named in the margin was autumn. High autumn, when the larches go gold and the passes are still open and the light comes down the valleys like something poured.

In late summer, Halvard announced he was running a caravan north over Thornfell's shoulder—legitimate cargo, mostly, wool and iron, with the usual unofficial remainder—and that he needed a caravan scholar to negotiate tolls with the ravens, since the ravens had developed opinions about tariffs now that beast-speech was legal and the little feathered extortionists knew it.

"You want me on the road," I said.

"I want you on the road," Halvard agreed, not looking at me, very busy with a ledger.

"On the mountain road. Above Thornfell. In high autumn."

"Funny how routes work out." He finally looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious about it. "Astrid's guiding. First time she's taken a contract since—" He stopped. Started again. "She says the mountain misses her. Says the wolves asked after her. Says it's time she stopped hiding in the city being sad at pigeons." He snapped the ledger shut. "I didn't arrange anything. I want that understood. I'm a smuggler, not a matchmaker. The road arranged it. Roads do that."

I thought of the last page. *Two strangers.* Which meant she wouldn't know me—which meant, gods, it meant the meeting had to happen clean, without her history sitting between us like a third traveler. Which meant I would have to board that caravan as what I had been the night we collided in the tavern: a stranger with a bag full of dangerous books and no idea what he was walking into.

Except this time I would know. This time I'd be a stranger who had read ahead.

"Tell me one thing," I said to Halvard. "When she meets me on that road. Will she feel it? Even a little? Something old stirring?"

Halvard considered me for a long moment. "Her mother's stories say the heart keeps what the mind loses," he said. "Astrid told me that herself, last week, apropos of nothing, staring at the pass like it owed her money." He shrugged, enormous and gentle. "You'll have to ask her heart yourself, boy. I only drive the wagon."

* * *

The night before departure I sat with the Codex one last time in the room Pip had claimed as hers, surrounded by maps she'd stolen from the new council and a goat she'd stolen from absolutely nowhere legitimate.

"Read it to me again," she said. "The last one."

I read it. *A year hence, on the mountain road above Thornfell, two strangers will meet. One will ask the other's name. Neither will know why the question matters. Both will answer anyway.*

"Both will answer anyway," Pip repeated. She was quiet for a moment, chewing a strand of hair, and then she said, in the small fierce voice she saved for truths: "That's the whole prophecy, isn't it? That's everything it wanted to say. All that death and doom and fire, and at the bottom of the book, the actual message is just—two people answering each other anyway."

I looked at the blank pages after it. All that good paper. All that room.

"No," I said. "That's not the whole message. The blank pages are part of it too. A hundred years of warnings, and then silence—because the warnings worked. Because people kept the promises." I closed the cover and rested my hand on it, feeling the faint warmth the book still held, like a hearthstone hours after the fire. "Prophecy is just a promise someone kept, Pip. And a blank page is a promise nobody's made yet. It's not emptiness. It's permission."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she leaned over and kissed the top of the goat's head, which was her version of sentiment, and said, "Write something good, then. First line of the new book. Make it count."

And because I am, at the end of everything, a scholar—a man who cannot leave a page unwritten, who believes with his whole heretical heart that words are how we hold the world together—I opened the Codex to the first blank leaf, took up my pen, and wrote:

*On the mountain road above Thornfell, in the gold of autumn, a traveling scholar met a guide with storm-grey eyes, and asked her name.*

I paused. My hand shook. The ink waited, flint-smelling, patient, mine.

Then I wrote the rest:

*She told him. And though he had known her once, and she had forgotten, and the knowing had cost more than either of them could reckon—it did not matter, because the road goes both directions, and hearts keep what minds lose, and a promise is only a promise until someone keeps it.*

*He intended to keep it.*

Below that, nothing. Room. Permission. A whole blank book, and a morning coming, and a caravan loading in the yard below with wool and iron and one gruff captain pretending not to watch, and a mountain waiting with its gold light poured down the valleys like something poured for us specifically, like the world setting out two cups.

Somewhere up there, a woman who had forgotten me was saying goodbye to her pigeons, and feeling—for reasons she couldn't name—an old thing stir, like a song remembered from someone else's singing.

Tomorrow, a stranger would ask her name.

I packed my bag. I sharpened my pen. I went down to the road.
