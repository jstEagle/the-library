## 22. A Choir for the Ember

A year ago I would have told you that the strangest thing in the world was a woman who could hear a wolf's opinion of the weather. I would have been wrong, and I would have been lying about it too, because the strangest thing in the world is actually a city that sings.

Not metaphorically. Crownspire sings. Every evening at dusk, when the Ember's light comes up through the mountain's crown like a held breath finally let go, the whole city stops—bakers, blacksmiths, goats, gulls—and sings Maren's heresy back to the sky. The Choir for the Ember, they call it. It started with eleven smugglers who couldn't carry a tune in a wheelbarrow and a dead priest's verses scrawled on a prison door, and now it has forty thousand voices and a goat section that refuses to stay on key no matter how many times Pip yells at them.

I sing it too. Badly. Wren used to say my singing sounded like a raven being audited.

*Used to.* There's the word that lives in my chest like a splinter I can't find the edge of. I've learned to stop probing it. Mostly.

It's spring again, one year since the fire came back to the mountain, and I'm walking the Thornfell road because Halvard needs someone to meet a shipment of Ember-glass at the pass and because I need, once in a while, to be alone with the mountains without an entire city listening to me be its hero. Fame, it turns out, is just loneliness with better catering. Everyone in Crownspire knows my name. Half of them want to shake my hand. The other half want me to talk to their cat, which has opinions about the neighbor's dog that they feel require diplomatic mediation.

The road is mud and meltwater and the smell of pine waking up. A raven keeps me company for the first mile—old Vaskr-tongue habit, riding my shoulder like a black judgment—and then peels off toward the fish market, because even prophecy's leftovers have better things to do than watch me think.

Here's what I know, one year on. Here's the ledger.

Aldric is dead, and there's a statue of him in the square that everyone agrees makes him look too grim, and everyone also agrees that's accurate. He burned with the thing that wore him, and the last thing he did with his own mouth—the mouth Vaskr had borrowed for thirty years—was say *thank you* in beast-speech, in the grammar his dead wife taught him, aimed at me. I think about that more than I think about almost anything. A man spends three decades as a puppet and spends his final free minute speaking a language he learned for love. If there's a better argument for what we're building here, I haven't heard it.

Solveig runs the Purifiers now, or what's left of them, renamed and reformed into something that guards instead of hunts. She came to my door last winter with her hat in her hands and told me about my mother—not the burning, the living. The laughing. How Ingrid Eriksen used to steal honey cakes from the temple kitchens and blame the crows, and how the crows never once contradicted her, because even then they knew whose side they were on. I cried. She pretended not to notice. We're friends now, in the way of two people who have each survived the same fire from opposite ends.

Halvard is rich, which disgusts him, and generous, which disgusts him more. He's converted the smuggling fleet into a trade company called Crooked Road Freight, on the grounds that "if you're going to be legitimate you may as well keep the name honest." He still calls me girl. I still let him.

Pip is twelve and terrifying. She runs the undercity's beast-post—the network of rats and pigeons and gutter-cats she built during the war, formalized now into something the city council officially relies upon and privately fears. Last month she negotiated a labor agreement between the harbor cats and the dockworkers. It took four days and ended with both parties getting fish. Diplomacy, she informed me, is mostly fish.

And Wren—

Wren Calloway is a name I know the way you know a word in a language you don't speak. I know he was a scholar. I know he found the Codex of Ash. I know he read our deaths in it and followed them anyway, all the way down the mountain and into the dark, and that when the price came due he tried to pay it himself and the book burned his hands for the attempt. I know these things the way I know the history of Thornfell: true, important, and belonging to someone else.

Because here is the thing nobody will tell me straight, though I have asked, though I have cornered Halvard and Solveig and even Pip, who can lie to a goat but not to me: whatever I gave the Ember, I gave it whole. There's a Wren-shaped absence in me, clean-edged, like a tooth pulled long ago. My tongue sometimes reaches for a memory the way it reaches for the gap. And everyone I love has agreed, all of them, to keep his secret—to not tell me what I paid, or for whom, or why the kind scholar with the wet eyes stood at my bedside the morning I woke and said nothing except *welcome back.*

They think they're protecting me. They are. But I have a mother's stories in me too, and one of them says the heart keeps what the mind loses. That the body remembers what the soul paid out. That love isn't stored in the head like grain in a granary—it soaks into everything, the way smoke soaks into wool, and you can wash the wool a hundred times and still, on a cold morning, catch the smell of the fire.

So I walk the mountain road in spring, and I wait for the smoke-smell.

* * *

The pass road is empty in the good way, the old way. Ravens arguing over a carcass. A marmot whistling at me that the snowmelt's early and the wolves are grumpy about their dens flooding, which tracks, because the wolf-pack sent word through three species of intermediary that they'd like the humans upstream to stop building mills. I add it to the list. Being the beast-speaker of a free city means being a very small, very tired diplomat with fur on one side of every table.

Halfway up, where the road bends around the shoulder of the mountain and the pines open onto the long drop toward the valley—I stop.

There's a man coming up the trail.

He's got a scholar's walk, which is to say he walks like a man reading while moving, head tilted at some internal page. Traveling cloak gone soft with weather. Satchel. Ink stains on his fingers even at fifty paces; I can see them, because I've spent a year learning to look at hands. He's muttering to himself, and when he gets closer I'll probably realize he's actually talking to something—a bird on his pack, maybe, or a beetle on his boot—because that's the world now, everyone talks to everything, and half of everything answers.

But none of that is why I stop.

I stop because my heart does something it hasn't done in a year. Not grief. Grief I know; grief is the gap-tooth ache, the reaching tongue. This is different. This is older. This is the feeling of walking into your childhood house after years away and smelling bread you didn't bake. Something in me stands up and turns toward him like a compass needle finding north, and I actually put a hand flat on my own sternum, like I'm checking whether I left the stove on.

*Well,* I say to myself. *Hello, stranger.*

My mother's stories say the heart keeps what the mind loses. I used to think that was comfort-talk, the kind of thing you tell children at funerals. But standing on this road with mud on my boots and a year of miracles behind me, I understand it differently. Whatever I gave away, I didn't give it to nothing. The Ember didn't eat my memories of him. It *took them into custody*, the way the mountain holds fire, the way the Codex held prophecy—kept somewhere, kept safe, kept *warm*. And things kept warm have a way of leaking.

The man looks up. Sees me. Stops.

And here's the part I'll tell my grandchildren, if I get grandchildren, if the world stays kind enough for grandchildren: he smiles at me like he's been carrying this smile up the mountain for a year, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for exactly this bend in the road. Not a stranger's smile. A homecoming smile, worn smooth with use, the kind of smile you give someone you've already said goodbye to once and refused to do twice.

"Beautiful day," he says. His voice does something to the air. The ravens down the slope go quiet, which ravens never do, which I choose to interpret as the mountain holding its breath along with me.

"It's mud season," I say. "Everything is brown and everything is wet."

"I like brown." He shrugs. "Brown is honest. Green lies about how hard it worked."

I laugh. It comes out of me before I've decided anything, quick and startled, and the sound of it lands in my own ears like a word in a language I used to speak. Because that's the thing—that's the whole thing, the thing I've been circling for a year like a moth around a shuttered lamp: he is funny in a way that fits a shape inside me. His humor slots into my humor like a key I lost the lock for. Nobody else in Crownspire makes jokes like that. Nobody else in the world, I'd bet the fleet on it.

"You're staring," he says, and he's blushing, ink-stained fingers pushing hair out of his eyes. "Sorry. People always stare at the mud comment. It's not even my best one."

"What's your best one?"

"That depends entirely on how long you plan to stand there looking at me like I owe you money."

"I'm deciding whether you do."

"Fair." He sticks out his hand. "Wren. Wren Calloway. I'm a scholar—or I was, before the war, and then I was a witness, and now I'm mostly unemployed, which sounds worse than it is. I'm traveling. Collecting stories. There's supposed to be a wolf-pack up this road that remembers the old days, and I'm told they'll talk to people now, which—" he laughs, a little helplessly, "—a year ago I'd have called that heresy. Now it's Tuesday."

"Wolf-pack's two miles up," I say. "Matriarch's name is Grey-Muzzle. Lead with the mill complaint, she respects grievance."

His eyebrows go up. "You speak for them."

"I speak *with* them. There's a difference. Took me thirty years to learn it." I take his hand. His grip is warm and ink-stained and my pulse does the stove-left-on thing again, harder, and I hold on maybe a half-second past polite because my body is casting a vote and I've learned to count those ballots.

"Astrid," I say. "Astrid Eriksen."

Something crosses his face when I say it. Quick, like a bird across a window—there and gone, a shadow of a shadow. Grief, maybe. Or joy. Or the particular expression of a man watching a promise come due. I catalog it and let it go. Everyone in this country looks at me like that sometimes. Hero-shaped holes make strange shadows.

"Eriksen," he repeats softly. "Yes. I thought—you look like the stories."

"The stories get my nose wrong."

"They really do." And he grins, and it's *wry*, it's the exact wryness that lives in my own mouth, and I think: *where did you learn to smile like me?*

We fall into step without deciding to. That's the other thing. We walk together the way water finds downhill—no negotiation, no decision, just gravity doing what gravity does. He tells me about the roads he's walked since winter, the towns where the Choir has spread beyond the mountains, a fishing village that sings it in harmony with actual seals. I tell him about the goat section. He laughs so hard he has to stop walking, bent double on the muddy road, and I stand there watching him laugh and feeling my ribs hum like struck bells, feeling the gap in me fill and refill with something that isn't the missing thing but rhymes with it.

"So," he says, catching his breath, wiping his eyes. "The Codex of Ash. You'd know about it, obviously. Whole country knows the story. Book that wrote the future, burned itself out saving the king, went blank." He hesitates. "Except—this is the strange part, the part I've been chasing for six months. One page didn't go blank."

We've stopped at the bend where the pines open and the whole valley shows itself: Crownspire below with the Ember's glow rising off it like dawn that never quite finishes, the trade roads crawling with carts and beasts and people talking to all of it. Behind us, the high passes. Ahead, the mountain road going up into green.

"One page," he says, "describes a meeting. On this road, roughly this spot, roughly today. Two travelers. It doesn't give names—it never gives names anymore, it just... describes. 'Two strangers meet where the pines open. One asks the other's name.' And then the page ends. Blank after that. Like the book wrote the beginning of something and left the rest for us." He's looking at me with those wet-bright scholar's eyes, careful, hopeful, terrified. "Which is either nonsense or the most important sentence anyone ever wrote, and I've spent a year deciding it's the second one."

My heart is doing something I don't have a name for. It's not the gap-ache. It's not the stove-warmth. It's both at once, braided, the way grief and hope braid in a body that's survived enough to hold them together. My mother's stories say the heart keeps what the mind loses. I am standing in the keeping-place now. I can feel it all around me like the smell of smoke in wool.

"Two strangers," I say slowly. "One asks the other's name."

"That's the entry. Word for word."

"And did the strangers in the book like each other? Did it say?"

He goes very still. Somewhere above us a raven says *well?* —ravens have no tact, never have—and Wren Calloway, scholar, witness, keeper of a promise I don't yet know he made, looks at me across a foot of mountain mud and a year of silence and says:

"The book didn't say. But I've read it a hundred times, and I think—" his voice catches, steadies, "—I think whoever wrote it was hoping."

The wind moves through the pines. Below us, faint and far, Crownspire begins its dusk song—forty thousand voices and a goat section, singing a dead priest's heresy to a living fire. And I look at this stranger with my own face's smile, and I feel the shape of everything I gave away pressing warm against the inside of my chest, patient as a seed, and I decide that prophecy is just a promise somebody kept, and that I intend to keep this one.

"Well then," I say, and put out my hand again, and mean all of it. "Tell me your name."

He takes my hand like it's the answer to a question he's been asking his whole life.

"Wren," he says. "My name is Wren."

"Wren," I repeat, and the name lands in me somewhere below memory, somewhere the mind can't reach and the heart never leaves, and it fits, it *fits*, the way a key fits a lock that forgot it ever had a door. "I'm Astrid. Astrid Eriksen. And I think—" I laugh, and it comes out wet, and I don't care, "—I think we should walk together a while. The wolves are two miles up, and they take some convincing."

"Take all the time you need," says Wren Calloway, falling into step beside me like he's done it ten thousand times in a life I can't remember and a future I can finally see. "I'm not going anywhere."

And the mountain, which remembers everything, which kept what I paid and warmed it all this year like a coal under ash, lets its light rise a little brighter over Crownspire—and if you listen closely, the whole valley is singing.
