## 1. The Keeper's Quiet

The oil smelled of rosemary and iron, the way it always had, the way it had when Mara was small enough to sit on her mother's hip while those same hinges drank from the same tin spout. She worked it into the Gate's lower hinge first — the left one, which complained loudest in damp weather — pressing her thumb against the pin until she felt the give, that soft sigh of metal accepting what it needed. Then the middle hinge. Then the right. She had done this every morning for eleven years, and her mother had done it every morning for twenty-three before that, and somewhere behind that arithmetic lay a number so large Mara preferred not to look at it directly.

The Hollow Gate did not look like much. That was the first thing everyone said, the visitors who came up the shepherd's path once a year out of obligation or curiosity: *that's it?* Two posts of grey stone, taller than a man but not by much, standing alone in the meadow where the valley of Vell ran out of itself. No wall attached to either side. No door between them, though everyone called it a gate. Just the gap, and the treeline beyond it, dark firs climbing the shoulder of the hill like mourners who had arrived early and were waiting to be told what they were mourning.

Mara oiled the posts too, working the cloth along the carved channels that spiraled up their length. The channels were old work, older than Vell's oldest records, and no one knew what they were for. Water never sat in them. Rain ran off them strangely, as if the stone were faintly warm. When she pressed her ear to them in winter she could hear something that was not wind.

She did not press her ear to them today. Today there was oiling, and after oiling there was the walk of the boundary, and after the boundary there would be dusk, and at dusk there were the names.

* * *

She walked the edge the way her mother had taught her: slowly, with her palms open, as if greeting each tree by hand. The wards were not visible — they never were, except in fog, when they showed themselves as a faint silvering in the air, like breath on glass — but Mara could feel them the way you feel a held note in your own chest. A hum. Not sound exactly. More like the memory of sound, lodged under the breastbone.

The hum was strongest at the Gate and thinned as she walked north along the treeline, and she had long ago stopped noticing how it changed her. In Vell, magic was ordinary the way weather is ordinary: the miller's wife charmed sour milk sweet, the ferryman coaxed his rope to bite the current, children caught fireflies in cupped hands and made them glow all night without harming them. Small magics. Household magics. But none of it followed anyone past the treeline. Step beyond the firs and you were only flesh and fear, and everything you'd ever charmed went quiet in your pockets.

Mara had learned this at seven, chasing a runaway lamb past the last fir, and had stood in the strange flat silence beyond it — no hum, no warmth, her mother's charm-bracelet dead as river stones on her wrist — until hands pulled her back. Her mother had not scolded her. Her mother had only looked at the treeline for a long time, with an expression Mara had spent years trying to name and had finally given up on, and said, *Now you know why someone must stay.*

Why the magic stopped at the trees. Why the Gate stood where it stood. Why no one else ever volunteered for the keeper's work — not the council, not the priests of the low chapel, not one of the hundreds of souls whose names Mara recited into the dark every evening. These were questions she carried the way you carry a smooth stone in a pocket: touched often, never examined. Asking them felt dangerous in a way she couldn't justify, the way some doors in old houses are painted shut and everyone agrees, without discussing it, to let them be.

Besides — and this was the thought she returned to, the one that let her sleep — the work was good. The valley was safe. Eleven generations of keepers had kept it so, and the names stayed remembered, and the stars came out over Vell every clear night like a promise kept in writing.

* * *

Dusk came early that far north, pooling in the valley bottom first and then rising like water filling a bowl. Mara lit the lamp at the Gate — a small iron thing on a hook driven into the left post, burning oil she rendered herself from tallow and a herb whose name was also part of her inheritance — and then she stood in the gap between the posts, facing outward, toward the country that wasn't on any map.

There was no country there. That was the official truth of it, taught in Vell's schoolrooms: beyond the treeline lay wild land, unclaimed, unmapped, of no interest to anyone. The maps in the council house showed the valley ringed by forest and then by nothing at all, a cartographic shrug. Ilex Vane had once told her, drunk on plum wine at the harvest fair, that he'd tried to survey past the firs three times and three times his instruments had lied to him — compasses spinning, distances refusing to sum — and that the fourth time he'd gone anyway, and come back with pages that got him exiled. Mara had not asked to see the pages. She regretted that now, sometimes, in the way you regret an unopened letter.

She faced the darkening firs and began the names.

This was the heart of the work, the part that mattered, the part her mother had drilled into her with a seriousness that had frightened child-Mara more than any ghost story: every soul in Vell, spoken aloud, in order, oldest first, every dusk, so that the valley stayed *inside the world*. She had never been told what would happen if the names went unsaid. She suspected nobody alive knew. It was simply the shape of things, the way tides are the shape of seas.

"Alsen Brack, born under the eaves of the mill, ninety-one years," she said, and the words went out into the blue air and did not come back. "His wife, Tamsin Brack, who planted the orchard on the south slope. Their daughter Ferren, who keeps bees..."

She worked through the mill families, the ferry families, the shepherds of the high pasture, the priest and the priest's skeptical cat (her mother had included the cat; Mara saw no reason to break tradition). Four hundred and six names. On festival days the whole valley came up the path and said the names together, four hundred and six voices braided into one long rope of sound, and the hum in Mara's chest swelled so large she could hardly stand it — a fullness, almost painful, like grief and gratitude sharing one body.

But festivals came twice a year. Every other dusk, it was only her.

"Old Wenna, who remembers the flood year. Her son Doss. His daughters, Ivy and Sorrel..." Her voice carried strangely in the meadow, flattened by the coming dark, and she had learned not to listen for an answer because there never was one. The Gate did not reply. The firs did not reply. The hum received the names the way the sea receives rivers — without comment, without refusal — and somewhere in that vast silent accounting, Vell went on being real.

Four hundred and six. She finished as the first star cleared the ridge, and stood a moment with her eyes closed, letting the quiet settle back over the meadow like snowfall.

It was a good life. She believed that. She had arranged her belief carefully, stone by stone, the way you build a cairn: the work is holy, the valley is safe, loneliness is the price of the office and the office is worth the price. Some evenings the cairn held. Some evenings — and tonight was shaping into one of them — she felt the stones shift under the weight of the sky, all that enormous dark pressing down on the little lit gap where she stood, and she thought: *eleven generations, and not one of them wrote down why.*

Her mother had died in this meadow eleven years ago, in autumn, quickly, between one dusk's names and the next. Mara had taken up the recitation that same night, voice cracking on the B-names, and had not missed a single dusk since. She sometimes wondered if her mother had known she was dying — keepers seemed to know things they shouldn't — and whether that was why, in her last lucid hour, she had gripped Mara's wrist hard enough to bruise and said, with terrible clarity:

"If the humming ever stops, girl, don't go looking for the reason. Come find me."

Which was nonsense, since her mother was ash on the wind off the orchard slope, and Mara had filed the sentence away among the other painted-shut doors of her inheritance, unexamined, unopened.

Tonight the starlight came down clean and cold. Tonight the hum under her breastbone was steady as a sleeping heartbeat. Tonight was ordinary, and ordinary, Mara reminded herself, was the entire point of the work — the long unbroken string of ordinary dusks, each one a stitch holding two edges of the world together.

She banked the lamp, shouldered her satchel, and turned toward home.

Behind her, in the gap between the posts, the carved channels in the stone gave off the faintest warmth, and the dark beyond the firs lay still as held breath — and high above the treeline, where the first stars of evening should have been gathering one by one, the sky waited, empty, patient, already beginning to forget how.
