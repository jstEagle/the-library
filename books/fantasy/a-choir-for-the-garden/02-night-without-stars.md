## 2. Night Without Stars

The evening began the way four hundred evenings had begun before it, and Mara Winterbourne would remember that ordinary rhythm for the rest of her life: the way her boots crunched the gravel path, the way she counted the gate's hinges under her breath — six on the near side, six on the far — the way she tipped the oil can and let it whisper down each one until the metal drank.

The Hollow Gate did not look like much. That was the first thing every child in Vell learned and the last thing they believed. It was a ring of grey stone set into the hillside where the valley narrowed to a throat of pines, tall as two men and wide as a barn door, its arch furred with lichen that no one had ever been allowed to scrape away. Behind it, the country went on for another half-day of walking before the treeline — orchards nobody tended but which never rotted, meadows that stayed green past any sensible season. Beyond the treeline was the starless country, though Mara had never seen it herself. Nobody had. The Gate stood between Vell and whatever was not-Vell, and it hummed.

That was the word everyone used. *Hummed.* Low and constant, felt more than heard, a note that lived in the back teeth if you stood too close. Farmers swore their cows gave richer milk when the wind carried it downhill. Children pressed their palms to the stones on festival days and claimed they could feel it singing through their bones. Mara had stopped being able to hear it years ago — or rather, she had stopped being able to *not* hear it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat until someone reminds you it's there. It was simply part of her, the way the ache in her knees was part of her mother's mother, who had kept this same gate before her.

She worked by habit now, her hands moving without instruction. Oil the hinges. Clear the moss from the drainage channel at the base so winter melt wouldn't pool against the stones. Then the names.

This was the part outsiders never understood, if outsiders ever came, which they did not. At dusk, every dusk, the keeper of the Hollow Gate recited the valley aloud. Every name. Every farmstead from Lowmarch to the mill at Sorrel Water, every family line still living and three generations dead, every well and bridge and boundary oak. Four hundred and eleven names, give or take the births and burials of the season, spoken slowly, in order, facing the Gate as though reading them off an invisible ledger held against the stone.

*Harrow Farm. The Alderbridge. Tallow Lane. The house of Pell and his daughter Sef, and Sef's son Odo, born this spring, God keep him.*

It took the better part of an hour. Her voice carried down the valley on still evenings like this one, and folk in the nearest farms said you could set your clocks by it. They thought it was a blessing. A charm against bad weather. Mara had let them think so because the truth was harder to explain: that names were load-bearing. That somewhere in the architecture of the world, Vell rested on the fact of being remembered aloud, and that if the recitation ever stopped — if even one name were left out too many seasons running — something would quietly come loose.

Her mother had taught her that. Her mother had taught her everything about the Gate except the thing Mara most wanted to know, which was why there had only ever been one keeper, why no one else ever volunteered despite the modest stipend the council paid, why magic could not follow Mara so much as a hundred paces past the treeline — her lamp sputtering, her wards going dumb, her own small tricks (a warming charm, a finding-spell for lost sheep) failing utterly as though the land itself refused to know her. She had asked once, at fourteen. Her mother had looked at her with an expression Mara had spent twenty years trying to decipher — grief? guilt? both wearing the same tired face? — and said only: *"Some questions are doors, love. Don't knock unless you mean to open them."*

Then her mother had gone into the hills one autumn morning and not come back, and the council had sent a letter saying only *the tenth voice is concluded; the Gate endures*, and Mara had inherited the oil can, the ledger of names, and the silence.

She finished the recitation as the light died. The sky over Vell was clear and enormous, pricked with the honest stars of early autumn, and the moon was climbing fat and white above the eastern ridge. Mara capped the oil can, brushed stone dust from her skirt, and permitted herself the small nightly luxury of standing a moment longer than necessary, listening to the hum, feeling it settle into her chest like a cat coming home.

That was when the stars began to go out.

Not all at once. Not dramatically. One by one, west to east, in a slow rolling wave, as though someone were drawing a shutter across the heavens. Mara's breath caught. She had seen clouds, eclipses, the green shimmer of the northern lights twice in her life — she had seen the sky do strange things. But this was not weather. This was *methodical*. Star after star winking out with the tidy finality of candles pinched by a careful hand, and the moonlight thickening strangely as the dark spread, turning silver to a flat, sourceless grey, the color of paper, the color of nothing.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to the Gate. "No, no —"

The hum faltered.

She had lived her whole life inside that sound and had never once imagined its absence, and now she understood that a person could be wounded by a silence. It didn't stop the way a song stops. It *stumbled* — caught, resumed thin and ragged, caught again — like a singer losing her place, like ten voices trying to hold a chord while one by one they were pulled offstage. The note dropped lower. Lower. She felt it leave her teeth, her breastbone, the gravel under her feet vibrating faintly and then not at all.

Mara ran to the Gate and laid both palms flat against the stones. Cold. The stones had never been cold. In thirty years of keeping, through frosts that split water troughs, the Gate had held the warmth of its own singing like a banked fire, and now it was cold as riverbed rock, and beneath her hands she could feel — barely, terribly — the last thread of the hum fraying like a rope under strain.

"Stay," she whispered. "Hold. *Hold.*"

The wards went next.

She felt them rather than saw them — the border-wards strung along the valley's rim, invisible threads her mother had shown her how to sense, humming their own smaller harmonies around the whole circumference of Vell. They fell like cut strings. All of them, all at once. Mara gasped as the sensation tore through her: a dozen taut lines snapping slack in the same instant, and the valley suddenly, horribly *open*, the way a house is open when every door and window blows wide in a storm. Wind moved through her wards' absence that wasn't wind at all. It smelled of nothing. Of unwritten paper. Of the space where a memory should be and isn't.

And then the edge of the valley simply stopped being there.

Mara turned toward the treeline — the orchards, the unrotting apples, the meadows — and found a darkness standing where the land should have continued. Not shadow. Shadow would have had shape. This was an absence with edges, a rectangle of not-night beyond which the stars had already been extinguished, and it was *moving*, sliding inward along the ground with the patience of spilled ink, and where it passed, the grass didn't die. It *stopped having ever been*. She watched a fencepost forget itself — not fall, not burn, just cease, leaving a gap in a fence that now had no reason to be a fence, and then the fence followed it, and then the field.

Mara did the bravest and most foolish thing of her life: she grabbed her staff, lit her lantern with hands that shook, and walked toward it.

"*Vell!*" she shouted into the dark, hurling the name like a stone. "*Harrow Farm! The Alderbridge! Tallow Lane!* If you want us forgotten you'll have to work for every syllable—"

Something crashed through the pines to her left.

She spun, lantern raised. Out of the black came a man at a stumbling run — gaunt, mud-splattered, coat torn at the shoulder as if something had very nearly gotten a grip on him, a leather map-tube bouncing against his back. He was shouting too, hoarsely, words that made no sense: "—it's eating the survey marks, do you understand, I staked the whole perimeter and it ate my *measurements*—" And then he saw the Gate, or what little of its shape the lantern caught, and he stopped so abruptly he nearly fell, staring at it with an expression she'd never seen on a living face: recognition and terror in equal measure, the way a drowning man looks at shore.

"You're real," he rasped. "God help me, you're actually — the Gate is *real*."

"Who in the world are you?" Mara demanded. "Get behind the wardline, get *behind* me —"

"There is no wardline." He laughed, a cracked, awful sound. "Woman, I've been walking your borders for nine days. There hasn't been a wardline since moonrise."

He dug into his coat and thrust something toward her: a compass, brass-cased, its glass crazed with age. But the needle was wrong — she knew instruments, knew north, and this needle wasn't pointing anywhere near it. It swung lazily, insistently, around to point directly at her.

At her. Or at something she was.

"What does it do?" she breathed.

"It doesn't point north." The man wiped mud and worse from his face with a shaking sleeve. "Ilex Vane. Cartographer. Exiled, before you ask — exiled for mapping things your council decided shouldn't exist, which is a long story and we may not live long enough for it. This—" he tapped the compass, and the needle trembled toward her like a dog scenting home, "—this points at memories. Strong ones. Whole ones. It led me out of the starless country on the promise that somewhere ahead there was a person who *remembered things properly*." His eyes, hollow-rimmed and fever-bright, fixed on hers. "You're the strongest thing it's found in a hundred miles. Which means either you're the keeper, or you're carrying something worth remembering."

Behind them, the darkness slid another yard up the hillside, and where the orchard had been, there was now smooth, unmarked, unremembered ground.

"The Gate's gone silent," Mara said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm in the way of people standing on ice that has already begun to crack. "The wards are down. The stars are going out. My mother told me never to cross the treeline, and I am beginning to understand that she meant it the way you mean *don't touch the stove*."

"I need to see the Gate's foundations," Ilex said. "Tonight. Before the rest of it goes."

"Why?"

"Because," he said, and for the first time his voice lost its fevered edge and became something quiet and dreadful, "I've seen the other side of this valley. And the country that's eating yours — Mara Winterbourne, keeper of the Hollow Gate — used to have a Gate of its own."

The needle of the compass shivered, swung — and pointed past them both, out into the dark, toward the vanished fields, toward whatever waited where the stars had been.
