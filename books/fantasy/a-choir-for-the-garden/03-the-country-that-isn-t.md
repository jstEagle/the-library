## 3. The Country That Isn't

The border did not break so much as it simply stopped pretending to be there.

Ilex felt it happen under his boots — a sensation like stepping off the last stair when you'd braced for one more. One moment his feet were on Vellish soil, moss and granite and the particular damp of a valley that had been mapped to death by men who thought maps were a form of ownership. The next moment, nothing beneath him was anything at all, and then — after a vertigo of blankness that lasted either a heartbeat or an hour — grass.

He went down hard on one knee and stayed there, breathing through his teeth, waiting for the compass in his hand to tell him where he was.

It told him *home*.

That was the trouble with the thing. He'd made it himself, in a fit of genius he now recognized as hubris, from a needle of lodestone quenched in his own tears and housed in a casing of whalebone. It didn't point north. North was a human invention, an agreement, and this country had never agreed to anything. Instead it pointed at whatever Ilex most wanted found — or rather, at what wanted finding, which were not always the same thing. Right now the needle strained toward the horizon like a dog at the end of a rope, quivering, insistent, and the direction it chose was one that existed on no chart he had ever drawn.

Behind him, Mara Winterbourne stood at the place where the treeline should have been. There was no treeline. There was no line of any kind — just Vell's meadows running out into grass that continued without interruption, as though the valley had always been a lie about its own edges.

"The wards," she said. Her voice was steady the way a bridge is steady: something holding weight that would prefer not to be. "They didn't fall. They just... ended. Like a sentence someone stopped writing."

"Sentences get finished or abandoned." Ilex rose, brushing wet grass from his knee. "This one was abandoned."

"You talk too much for a man who fell down."

"I talk exactly enough for a man who's been walking into places that erase people for six years." He held up the compass. "It wants us to go that way."

Mara looked at the needle, then past it, at the land. The light here was wrong in a way he couldn't immediately name, and then he could: it was the light of late afternoon that never commits to evening. Golden, but thin. The shadows lay at angles that suggested a sun neither of them could find.

"That's not a direction," Mara said quietly. "There is no that way. There's no map for any of this."

"No," Ilex agreed. "There isn't. That's why they exiled me."

She glanced at him — a flicker, quick and assessing, the look of a woman deciding whether a tool was worth carrying — and then she started walking, because of course she did. Keepers, he was learning, did not deliberate the way other people did. They simply moved toward the wound.

He followed. The compass pulled. And the erased country opened around them like a book whose words had been scraped away but whose pages still remembered being turned.

* * *

The first field was mid-harvest.

Ilex stopped at its edge and felt something in his chest go quiet and enormous, the way it did before an earthquake, in the seconds when the ground has decided but hasn't yet said so.

Wheat, if wheat could be called that — tall, heavy-headed, gold gone grey at the tips like hair on an old man. Scythes stood leaning against shocks of grain. A hat sat on a fencepost. And across the whole sweep of the field, frozen in the act of working, stood perhaps thirty figures.

Not statues. Statues have intention. These had *posture* — the particular slump of a body that has been bending since dawn, the hitch of a shoulder mid-motion, a woman with one hand raised to shade her eyes as though the sun she was looking into might still come out from behind a cloud. They weren't stone. They were almost transparent, like figures seen through frosted glass, and where their faces should have been sharpest they were faintest, worn soft as river stones.

"They're not dead," Mara breathed beside him. She had gone very still. "Ilex, they're not dead. Look — the grain. It's still growing. Slowly, but—"

"It's fading slower than they are." He crouched, touched a stalk. It resisted his fingers with the mild stubbornness of living things. "Everything here is being deleted at different rates. The land holds longer than the buildings. The buildings hold longer than the animals." His throat tightened. "People hold least of all."

"Why?"

"Because people are mostly memory." He said it flatly, because saying it gently would have been unbearable. "A body can be described without anyone remembering it. But a person — a person is the sum of everyone who knows them. Take the knowing away and all that's left is meat standing in a field, forgetting how to be anyone."

One of the harvesters, near the fence, was closer to gone than the others. Ilex could see the fence rail through her chest. As he watched, her raised hand drifted — not moving, exactly, but *settling*, the way sediment settles in still water — until it hung at her side, and the gesture she had been making for who knew how many years was simply no longer part of her.

Mara made a sound low in her throat, small and involuntary, and put her hand flat against the fencepost as though steadying herself against a ship's roll.

"I've kept names alive my whole life," she said. "Every dusk, every name in the valley, spoken aloud. I thought that was the work. I thought—" She stopped.

"You thought the gate kept things out." Ilex stood. "Maybe it does. Maybe what it keeps out is the sight of this."

The compass needle swung, patient and merciless, deeper inland.

* * *

The village, when they reached it, was set for supper.

That was the detail that broke him, later, when he let himself think about it — not the ruined chapel or the empty well or the doors hanging open on dark rooms. The tables. Every house they passed had a table laid for a meal: bowls, spoons, a loaf under a cloth gone stiff with age, a jug with a skin of dust inside it like frost on a window. Places set for families. Chairs drawn out at angles that suggested someone had been about to sit, or had just risen, or had been called away to answer a knock.

Nobody had answered the knock. Nobody had come back to the table. The meals had waited through years that the eaters no longer remembered having lived, and now they waited through years the tables themselves were beginning to forget, because the wood was paling, the grain of it smoothing out, becoming less table and more idea-of-table.

"In Vell," Mara said, in the doorway of the fourth house, "we say grace naming everyone present. So no one eats unremembered." Her voice had a crack running through it now. "Who graced these tables? Who named them? If no one says your name—"

"Then the Hush gets you faster." 

They both turned.

The voice had come from the garden behind the house — a child's voice, level and unhurried, the voice of someone reciting a lesson learned so thoroughly it had become part of the bones. Standing between rows of bean plants gone silver-grey was a girl of perhaps eight, small and straight-backed, wearing a coat cut down from an adult's and patched with fabric from three other coats. In her arms she carried a ledger. Not a book — a ledger, fat and swollen with inserted pages, tied shut with string.

Her face was the first fully solid thing Ilex had seen since crossing over. Eyes dark and absolutely present. Cheeks that held color. When she looked at them, she *looked*, with a completeness of attention that made him feel briefly transparent himself — another thing in this country going faint at the edges.

"You said the Hush," Mara said carefully. "You know its name."

"Everyone knew its name once. Then they traded it." The girl untied the ledger's string with fingers that moved like she'd done it ten thousand times. "Along with everything else. My name is Wren. Wren Aldous Merren Cotter Bracken — those are the four families that raised me, back when families could raise children here instead of just fading at them. Say it back."

"Wren Aldous Merren Cotter Bracken," Mara repeated.

"Good. You're the keeper. Your mother was Sera Winterbourne, born Sera Lune of the Choirhouse, tenth voice of the ten, taken as surety when the bargain was renegotiated the year you turned two. She sang the low part. She used to hold her notes a beat longer than the score asked because she liked the way they felt leaving her." Wren said all of it without inflection, the way one reads a shipping manifest, and then looked up, and her eyes were suddenly, terribly young. "I remember her. She's the only one who remembers her besides me. Do you want to know her, or do you want to stand in the doorway grieving?"

Ilex watched Mara take that like a blow she refused to acknowledge. Watched her jaw set. Watched her step down into the garden as if crossing a threshold she'd been walking toward her entire life without knowing it.

"How," she said, "do you remember all of it? Everything they sold?"

"Because nobody bought mine." Wren hugged the ledger to her chest. "I was born after the last payment. Too new to be collateral, too small to be worth taking. But I was born *of* them, so I came out full — a whole country's worth of memory in one small boat, and the Hush circling, hungry, waiting for me to spill." She patted the ledger. "So I write it down. Every day I write what I remember, and every day I check yesterday's pages, because sometimes the ink goes pale overnight. Words fade here too, if nobody means them."

"And if the pages fade?" Ilex asked. "If you run out of paper?"

Wren looked at him with the patience of the very old housed in the very young.

"Then I keep it in my head, cartographer. All of it. The songs, the surnames, the shape of the harbor before the water forgot how to be deep. I am the last complete memory of this country, and I am eight years old, and I have not slept a full night since I was four." She turned to Mara. Something shifted in her face — the first crack in the competence, a hunger so naked it hurt to witness. "Your mother's voice is still in the world. I know where. The compass man's needle knows too — I've been watching it twitch toward the Choirhouse since you crossed the grass. But the sky is going out, keeper. Have you noticed the sky?"

Ilex had. He hadn't let himself think it. Now he looked up through the bean rows and saw it plainly: the thin golden light dimming, and above it, where stars should have begun, a darkness with nothing in it at all — except, far off and very high, a single point of white, small as a pinhole, burning with what looked like effort.

"One star left," Wren said. "When it goes, the bargain closes. Whatever's owed gets collected. Both countries. Everything." She slung the ledger onto her back with a strap worn shiny by use, and held out her hand — to Mara, not to him, though her eyes included him the way you include a knife you might need. "So we don't have time for grief. We'll do our grieving after, if there's an after. Come on. I know a road the Hush forgot to eat, and I need to teach you a song before your voice gives out, and you—" this to Ilex, sharp as a tack, "—are going to explain why a Vellish exile carries a compass that points at *my* country."

The needle trembled toward the ruins on the horizon, and the last star held, and they went.
