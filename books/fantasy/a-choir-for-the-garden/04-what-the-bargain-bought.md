## 4. What the Bargain Bought

Wren remembered the plague.

Not the one from the books — there were no books anymore — but the real one, the one that had come like weather and left like grief. She remembered it because remembering was her job now, though nobody had ever asked her to do it and nobody was left alive to pay her. She was eight years old. She had been remembering for as long as she could remember, which was a very long time indeed, longer than the sky above the drowned orchard, longer than the road that forgot itself halfway to the hills.

She sat cross-legged in the middle of the frozen barley field while the two strangers argued behind her about whether to go forward or back. The woman — Mara, with hands that smelled of lamp oil and hinge-grease — kept glancing at Wren like she was afraid of her. The man — Ilex, who carried a compass that pointed at things instead of places — kept looking at the horizon like he could map his way out of a country that refused to be drawn.

Wren ignored them both. She was busy.

The barley stood around her in golden rows, each stalk caught mid-sway, leaning toward a wind that had stopped blowing years ago. If she touched a stalk, it crumbled — not into dust exactly, but into the idea of dust, into something soft and gray that smelled faintly of nothing at all. So she didn't touch. She only watched, and held what she saw inside herself, the way you hold water in cupped hands.

That was how the memory came, the way it always came when she sat still too long: not like a door opening but like a floor giving way, gently, so that she fell backward through her own skull into a time before her time.

* * *

The country had a name then.

It was called **Sorrelmere**, and the name tasted like cold well-water and bread crusts, and everyone wore it without thinking, the way you wear your own skin. Wren knew this because she had been told it once by her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother, who was not a ghost and not alive either but something in between — an echo that had gotten stuck in the family the way a song gets stuck in a room.

In the memory, Sorrelmere was green all the way to its edges. There were eleven towns and forty-one villages and one city built on terraces above a river that ran silver in autumn because the salmon came up it so thick you could walk across their backs. There were harvest festivals where they burned a wicker sun, and winter festivals where they buried a wicket moon and dug it up in spring, laughing. There were songs — oh, there were songs. Songs for churning butter and songs for sharpening scythes, lullabies with words no adult understood but every child sang anyway, a wedding song so beautiful that brides were known to weep before the first verse ended.

And then the forgetting came.

It did not arrive like armies arrive, with banners and noise. It arrived quietly, the way rot arrives in fruit — from somewhere deep in the center, spreading outward, patient. First a shepherd on the eastern slopes forgot his own flock and walked home to a wife who suddenly wasn't his wife, because she'd forgotten him too, or he'd forgotten her; no one could tell afterward whose forgetting came first. Then whole streets in the terraced city woke up unable to name the river below them, pointing at the silver water and calling it *that thing*. Then children began to forget their mothers' faces between supper and bedtime, standing in doorways at dawn looking at women who loved them desperately and who were already becoming strangers.

The healers tried everything. They tied red thread around wrists so people would know their own names were written there. They tattooed family marks onto infants' palms. A famous woman in the capital invented a machine of bells and mirrors that was supposed to shake memories loose again, and it worked for nine days, and on the tenth day the woman forgot she had ever built it, and the machine gathered dust in a square that no one could remember the purpose of, until even the square went.

People did not die of the forgetting. That was the horror of it. They lived on, hollowed, smiling politely at their own children, tending fields they couldn't say why they tended. Sorrelmere filled up with strangers wearing familiar faces, and the country went on being inhabited and stopped being *known*, which Wren understood — even at eight, she understood — was a slower and crueler kind of dying.

So the desperate ones went looking for help. And help, as it always does in stories, answered.

* * *

It called itself the Hush, though that was not its name, because it had no name; it had given up names long before Sorrelmere was anything more than river mud. The desperate ones found it — or it found them, Wren was never sure which, and suspected the difference mattered enormously — in a hollow beneath the western hills, in a place where sound behaved strangely and candles burned without melting.

What it offered was simple. What it asked was simple. That was the trap, of course: simple bargains are always traps, but starving people do not read the fine print of their own salvation.

*I will stop the forgetting,* the Hush said, in a voice that was less a voice than the shape silence leaves when it moves through a room. *I will hold your memories safe, every one, forever. No child will lose a mother's face. No farmer will wake a stranger in his own bed.*

*And the price?*

*Your name. Your history. Your songs. Give them to me, and I will keep them the way I keep everything — perfectly, completely, untouched by time.*

The council of Sorrelmere — eleven mayors and forty-one village heads and the Queen of the Terraces, whose name was Alveric and who wept through the entire negotiation — debated for six days and six nights. Some said never, that a name was the spine of a people. Some said the people would not survive to need a name. And in the end, watching a delegation of mothers carry in a child who had forgotten how to speak — not forgotten language, just forgotten that words belonged to her — Queen Alveric closed her eyes and signed.

*We agree,* she said. *Take our name. Take our history. Take our songs. But keep us. Keep us whole.*

And the Hush, courteous as ever, said: *Done. You will survive. Survival is my gift. Memory is my collateral. We will call it even — for now.*

For now. Wren felt those two words in her chest like swallowed stones. For now. The oldest lie in any bargain, the little hinge on which all interest swings.

* * *

Because here is what the desperate ones did not understand, sitting in their candlelit hollow, signing away three centuries of butter-churning songs and wicker suns: nothing given to the Hush was destroyed. It was *held*. Held the way a lender holds a deed. And the Hush, which had been hungry since before hunger had a name, discovered that holding was not the same as having.

A name locked in a vault feeds no one. A history sealed away keeps no one warm. The Hush held Sorrelmere's entire self in its vast quiet vaults and grew *hungrier*, because that is what happens to hoarded things and to hoarders alike. So it began to charge interest.

The first interest was small. Every generation, a few more memories slipped — not lost now, merely *collected*, moved from the people's keeping into the Hush's. A village's founding day, gone. The recipe for funeral bread, gone. Faces of grandparents, thinned like paint washed in rain. Each century the withdrawals grew larger, bolder, and no one could protest, because protesting requires remembering what was taken, and that was precisely what had been taken.

By the time Wren's echo-grandmothers began whispering warnings down the family line, Sorrelmere was a country living inside its own debt. The fields still grew, the tables were still set, the harvest still came in — but the harvesters could no longer say what festival the harvest was for. Meals were cooked and eaten by people who could not remember learning to cook. The country survived, immaculate and empty, a garden tended by sleepwalkers.

And the Hush grew fat and courteous and terribly, terribly patient, waiting for the final payment — the last complete memory, the one item that could not be subdivided, the whole of Sorrelmere in a single small container.

Which was why Wren existed.

* * *

She came back to herself in the barley field with tears freezing on her cheeks, though the air was warm enough; the tears had simply been falling for a very long time, in some other part of her.

Mara was crouched in front of her now, oil-smelling hands hovering, afraid to touch. "You went somewhere," Mara said. "Where did you go?"

"Backward," Wren said. Her voice came out rusty, older than eight. "This country used to be called Sorrelmere. Did you know that? Nobody knows that. I'm the only one." She wiped her face with her sleeve, leaving a damp streak. "They sold it. Everything. The name, the songs, the weddings, the wicker suns. To buy time against the forgetting."

Ilex had stopped pretending to study the horizon. His compass lay open in his palm, and its needle had swung around to point directly at Wren, quivering, eager as a dog scenting meat. He looked down at it, and then at her, and Wren watched him understand — really understand — what she was, and she hated the way his face went pale, because pity was almost worse than fear.

"The thing they sold it to," Mara said slowly. "You said *sold*. Sold to what?"

"The Hush." Wren said the word carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "It's not cruel. That's the worst part. It's just — hungry. It holds everything they gave it and it's still starving, and every year it takes a little more interest, and every year the country gets thinner, and someday—" She stopped. Someday soon, actually. She could feel it in the fading of the barley, in the way the sky had begun to go out like a lamp running dry. "Someday it will collect the whole debt. And I'm—" 

Her voice cracked, and she was eight years old again, suddenly, entirely.

"I'm the last one who remembers everything. Grandma-echoes hid me from it my whole life. Fed me the memories drop by drop so the Hush wouldn't notice me filling up. But it's noticed now. It always notices. And if it takes me—"

"It won't," Mara said, and the fierceness in her voice surprised them both.

But Wren shook her head, because she knew the arithmetic better than anyone alive. Debts like this don't forgive. Debts like this compound. And somewhere out beyond the frozen orchards and the tables set for meals no one would eat, the Hush was already turning its attention toward the small girl in the barley field — the final payment, walking around on two legs, humming a wedding song under her breath just to keep it alive.

Then she heard it. Far off, past the treeline where the world called Vell began — a sound she had never heard in her whole long-short life.

Someone was singing toward them. And the compass in Ilex's hand spun wildly, needle straining toward the sound like a heart toward its name.
