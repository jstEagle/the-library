## 5. Ten Voices, One Song

The compass did not point north.

It had never pointed north — Ilex had known that from the first hour he'd stolen it from the Guild's sealed cabinet, the night before his exile was announced. Its needle was a sliver of bone-white glass, and it swung toward whatever a person could not bear to have forgotten. For Ilex it had always trembled toward his mother's kitchen: the smell of scorched barley, the song she hummed off-key while kneading dough. He had spent eleven years pretending he didn't know what the needle meant, because a cartographer who mapped memory instead of terrain was a cartographer the Guild burned.

Now it pointed up the valley of the vanished country, toward a broken silhouette on the far ridge, and Wren walked ahead of it like the needle had a string tied to her heart.

"That's the Choirhouse," she said, without turning around. "I knew it when I was nobody. Before they started taking me apart."

"You were never nobody," Mara said behind him. Her voice had gone rough over the last two days, scraped thin by everything she'd learned about her own valley. She carried her lantern low, as if the light itself might be overheard.

Ilex said nothing. He was busy hating the building on the ridge.

He had seen it before. That was the thing coiling in his stomach like a swallowed hook — he had seen it in drawings, in a survey commissioned by Vell's council, in ink he himself had mixed and ruled and lettered with a steady hand. Eleven years ago. A young cartographer with a reputation for finding things that didn't want to be found, given a sealed brief and a purse heavy enough to silence his questions. *Locate the structure at the valley's border. Determine its function. Report only to the Council.*

He had found it. He had reported it. And whatever the council had done with his report, they had done swiftly, and then they had arranged his disgrace so carefully that even he had half believed his own crime.

He looked at Mara's back, at the set of her shoulders under the worn keeper's coat, and felt the hook turn.

* * *

The Choirhouse had been built to be heard from a distance. Even ruined, even roofless, its shape told you that: walls curved like the inside of a cupped hand, tiers of stone benches rising in a spiral, every surface angled inward toward a central dais where ten shallow basins were cut into the floor in a ring. Acoustics. Someone had designed this place the way an instrument-maker designs a lute — not as shelter but as resonance.

Frost ferned across the benches. The air inside tasted of old copper and held snow, though no snow fell anywhere else in the country-that-wasn't.

"It's warm," Wren whispered, and it was true; there was a faint living warmth rising out of the stone, the way a hearth stays warm hours after the fire dies. "It remembers being used."

Mara knelt at the ring of basins. She ran her fingers around the rim of one and came away with dust that glittered faintly, like ground glass or crushed frost. "These are worn," she said. "Smooth. Something sat here. Something sang here, over and over, for—" She stopped. Ilex watched her count the basins, lips moving. "Ten."

"Ten," Wren agreed, and climbed onto the lowest bench, folding herself cross-legged with the terrible patience of a child who has waited through worse than cold. "Sit down, both of you. It's easier if you're sitting."

"Wren—"

"When I touch things, sometimes they give me what they remember." The girl's eyes were too old, the way wells are deep. "You'll want to be sitting."

Ilex should have warned them. He knew, in the way he knew the needle of the compass, that touching the dais would open something. But his mouth had gone dry, and some cowardly part of him wanted Mara to learn this from the stone rather than from him — wanted, just once more, to not be the one holding the knife.

Mara pressed her palm flat into the nearest basin.

The Choirhouse woke.

Not sound exactly — sound came later, after the shock — but the *shape* of sound, the sensation of standing inside a throat about to sing. Frost shivered off the benches. The dark above the broken roof filled with a light that had no source, a pale gold like lamplight through honey, and into that light stepped figures made of remembered voice alone: ten of them, indistinct, shimmering, arranged in a ring around the dais. They had no faces. They had postures. One leaned forward eagerly. One stood with arms crossed. One — the tenth, at the ring's end — had her hands folded the way Mara folded hers when she was afraid and refusing to show it.

And then they sang, and Ilex understood everything at once, the way you understand a map all at once when the scale finally resolves.

They were not ten people singing one song. They were one song wearing ten bodies. Each voice carried a single strand — a low drone like the valley's bedrock, a bright thread like water over stones, a line that rose and fell with the seasons, a line that held names, hundreds of names, recited in rhythm until the names became melody. The strands braided. Where they braided, they became something else entirely: a wall of music, a boundary of harmony, a gate that was never stone at all.

*The Gate is sung,* Ilex thought, and the thought arrived with the force of a door slamming. *It was always sung. There is no wall. There never was.*

Beside him Mara made a small sound, and he turned and saw her staring at the tenth figure with an expression he had no words for — grief and recognition tearing in opposite directions.

"That's my mother," she said. "That's her stance. She stood like that when she lied to protect me." Her hand was still in the basin. She didn't take it out. "She died when I was nine. Everyone said she died."

The vision answered her, because the stone remembered what the valley had been made to forget. The scene shifted: the same ring of voices, but fraying now, the fourth strand gone hoarse, the seventh gone silent altogether, and the Hush pressing in at the edges of the light like frost claiming a window. And the keepers singing faster, harder, spending themselves — because that was the price no one had written down. A verse per generation. A voice per verse. The keepers didn't retire. They were *used*, braided into the song until there was nothing left of them but their strand, and then the country took the next daughter, the next son, whoever the bloodline offered.

Except the tenth strand. The vision showed him that too, cruelly clear: the ninth keeper failing mid-verse, the song buckling, the Hush pouring through the gap like smoke through a cracked seal — and a bargain struck in desperation, a voice offered as *collateral* rather than spent. Held. Kept. Not dissolved into the song but stored away whole, intact, a hostage against future need.

Mara's mother hadn't died. She had been filed away, like a deed, like a promissory note, in whatever vault the Hush kept its assets.

The light guttered. The ten figures thinned to outlines, to suggestions, to frost-faint traces on cold stone, and then the Choirhouse was only a ruin again, and the silence rang worse than the song had.

Mara withdrew her hand slowly. She looked at her palm as if it belonged to someone else.

"They told me she drowned," she said. Her voice was very level, which is how Ilex knew it was the most dangerous it would ever be. "The whole valley told me. My father told me. There was a grave. I put flowers on it every year for twenty years." She laughed once, a short terrible sound. "There's no body. There was never a body. You can't drown in your own valley's song."

"Mara." He reached for her shoulder and she flinched away from the touch, and he deserved it, and he let his hand fall.

"She's alive." Mara stood. The lantern light caught her face and Ilex saw that she had stopped being the woman who oiled hinges and recited names at dusk; something had finished hardening in her, the way metal finishes in a quench. "Eleven years. She's been held for eleven years as *collateral*, and I tended the instrument of her imprisonment. I oiled its hinges. I sang its names back to it every dusk like a good little keeper, keeping the cage locked and calling it devotion."

Wren slid off the bench and took Mara's hand instead — the child's small fingers lacing through the keeper's scarred ones, uninvited and unrefused. "We'll get her back," Wren said simply, the way children state physics. "That's why we're here. The compass knows. I know. We just have to finish the song, and finished songs don't hold hostages."

Ilex stood in the ringing quiet and understood that his moment had come, had perhaps been coming since the treeline, and that every hour he delayed it made the confession heavier and the trust more expensive.

"There's something you need to know," he said. "Before we go further. Before you decide anything else about me."

Mara turned. In the lantern light her eyes were winter-grey and absolutely patient, which was worse than anger.

"I wasn't exiled for mapping what shouldn't exist," Ilex said. "I was exiled so no one would ever check my work. The council hired me eleven years ago to find this place. Not to study it. To find it and report its weaknesses." His voice came out flat, cartographer's cadence, coordinates and bearings. "I found it. I mapped the acoustics. I identified the dais, the ring, the number of voices required. I gave them everything they needed to end the singing forever — to free Vell from paying keepers, from losing daughters, from the whole bloody tithe. And three weeks after my report reached the council chamber, the wards began to fail. Not all at once. Thread by thread. As if someone had taken my map and started cutting."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above the broken roof, another star went out — he felt it the way you feel a tooth loosen, a small wrongness in the sky's pressure.

"So either they acted on my report," he finished, "or my report taught them how. Either way, Mara — the unraveling of your valley runs through my hands. I broke the song. Or I handed the knife to the people who did."

Mara looked at him for a long time. Then down at Wren's hand in hers. Then at the ring of empty basins, each one worn smooth by a voice that had been spent and discarded, generation after generation, while Vell slept safe and called it peace.

"No," she said at last, and her voice had changed — not softer, but aimed. "You didn't break it. You drew the map. There's a difference, and I'm going to need the man who draws maps, because we have eight verses to recover and one star left, and I intend to finish what my mother started." She picked up the lantern. "Starting with whatever the Hush is holding in its vaults — starting with her."

The compass needle swung, trembling, toward the dark heart of the vanished country, where the last star hung low over a horizon that had no name.

And beneath their feet, so faint it might have been frost settling, the Choirhouse hummed — ten strands of a song, waiting for an eleventh.
