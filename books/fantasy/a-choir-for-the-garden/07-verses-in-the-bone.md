## 7. Verses in the Bone

Wren counted the stars the way other children counted sheep.

There had been a hundred and eleven when they crossed the border. Then ninety-four. Then sixty, then thirty, and now, as the three of them picked their way along a road that kept forgetting to be a road, Wren counted nine.

"You keep looking up," Ilex said, without turning around. The cartographer walked like a man reading a book only he could see, his compass held out before him in its cage of brass. The needle didn't point north. It pointed at whatever was most remembered nearby, and right now it shivered between two directions, torn, like a dog hearing two whistles.

"Somebody has to," Wren said. "You're busy looking at everything else."

"That's my job."

"It was your job. Now it's mine." Wren hugged the bundle of cloth tighter — inside it, the three verses they'd already found, wrapped like a sleeping thing. "When the last one goes out, everything goes with it. Me first. I'm the most made of remembering."

Mara glanced back at him. She had a way of looking at Wren that was half worry and half arithmetic, as though she were calculating how much of him was left. "How long?"

Wren shrugged, which was a lie, and they both knew it. "The Hush took the sky first," he said instead. "It takes the far things before the near things. That's how it works. That's how it always worked, even before. You forget the name of a mountain long before you forget your own hands."

"Then we keep the verses close," Mara said, "and we keep you closer."

Wren didn't answer, because his throat had gone tight, and because ahead of them the land had opened into a valley of its own — a small, drowned one, sunk to its rooftops in water the color of old glass. And rising out of the middle of it, tilted like a tooth, was a bell tower.

The compass needle snapped toward it and went still, quivering with something that looked, absurdly, like eagerness.

"There," Ilex breathed. "The first verse. It's there."

"It's underwater," Mara said.

"It was a bell tower," Wren said. "Bells were made to be heard through anything. Even this." He looked at the water and the water seemed to look back, patient and gray and full of the shapes of streets. "The country put its songs where the Hush would have to work hardest to get them."

* * *

They waded in at the old market street, where shopfronts stood open under the water and loaves of bread had turned to soft gray stone on their stone shelves. Wren kept one hand on Mara's pack strap and one on the submerged wall, because the water remembered the street even when the street didn't, and it pulled you along the old paths whether you wanted to go or not.

The tower leaned over them as they reached it, its belfry just breaking the surface, its windows dark. Inside, the stairs went up and the stairs went down, and the water had filled everything to the third landing. They climbed what they could and then swam the rest, down into the flooded heart of the tower where the bell had once hung.

The bell was gone.

In its place, hanging from the great oak beam by three rusted chains, was a knot of sound. That was the only way Wren could describe it, even to himself. A shape made of ringing, coiled like a sleeping bird, and when Mara's fingers touched it, the shape opened.

The verse went into them like cold well water.

Wren heard it all at once: a woman's voice, young, singing the counting-song mothers sang at harvest, except the words were the old words, the country's words, the ones that had been sold. *One for the sowing, two for the rain, three for the door we'll open again—* He felt Mara stiffen beside him, felt Ilex go very still, and then the verse settled into his bones and the water around them got *clearer*, the drowned street below sharpening from gray suggestion into cobblestones, into doorways, into a child's red boot lying on its side in a shop that had once sold ribbons.

"It hurts," Mara said. Her voice shook. "Why does it hurt?"

"Because it's true," Wren said. "True things are heavy. You've been carrying paper versions your whole life." He treaded water, gasping, laughing a little, because the wonder and the ache were the same thing and he couldn't separate them. "It's supposed to hurt. That's how you know it's yours."

They surfaced in the belfry, dripping, and Wren wrapped the second verse beside the first, and the cloth bundle felt warmer now, and heavier, the way a sleeping child is heavier than a sleeping cat.

Above them, through the broken louvers, the sky had gone the color of a bruise. Seven stars.

* * *

The soldier was standing in a wheat field that had forgotten how to grow.

They came upon him at dusk, or what the country still pretended was dusk — the light simply dimming, the way a candle dims when the wax is gone rather than when the day ends. The soldier stood at the edge of the field at attention, his pike planted, his uniform rotted to lace and shadow. He had been there a long time. The wheat had grown up around his boots and then given up, and now gray stalks stood in a circle around him like an audience that had forgotten why it came.

"He's still on watch," Ilex said quietly.

"He's a verse," Wren said. He could feel it the way you feel a fire in the next room. "The third one. The compass is screaming, can't you hear it?"

Ilex looked at the compass. The needle was spinning in tight, frantic circles. "It doesn't scream. It points."

"Then it's pointing at him."

They approached the soldier together, and up close Wren could see the impossible thing: the skeleton inside the uniform was *standing*. Not propped, not hung on the pike. Standing, the way a man stands who has decided he will not sit down, and the bones had simply agreed. One fleshless hand rested on the pike's shaft. The other was raised — not in salute. In *song*. Fingers spread, mid-gesture, the way a singer's hand moves when the note is too large for the throat alone.

"He was the last one singing," Wren said. His own voice came out small. "When the Hush came for the villages, someone had to keep the verse going while the others ran. He stood here and he sang it, and the Hush took everything else and left him, because—" Wren stopped. He knew why. He had always known why, the way he knew everything the country forgot. "Because a song needs a body to end in. And he never let it end."

Mara stepped forward. She was crying without any drama to it, tears just running down her face like weather. "Soldier," she said, the way you'd speak to a sleeping person you didn't want to wake. "We've come to take the verse the rest of the way."

The skull turned. It took a long time, the grinding patience of bone, and then the empty sockets were facing them and Wren felt the verse rise in the dead man the way bread rises — slowly, inevitably, from something that had every reason to be finished.

The soldier sang.

There was no voice in him. There hadn't been for a hundred years. But the verse didn't need a voice; it needed only the shape of one, and his ribs were still shaped right. It came out as wind through a rib cage, as the rattle of a pike in a fist, as the memory of a marching song that had once been a lullaby, and the words poured into Wren and Mara and Ilex like a river into dry fields:

*Four for the soldier who would not kneel, five for the door, six for the wheel—*

When it finished, the skeleton bowed. Actually bowed, one hand still on the pike, and then it came apart — not collapsing, but *releasing*, bone by bone settling into the wheat like a man finally allowed to sit down after a very long shift. The pike fell last. The sound it made was the kindest sound Wren had ever heard.

The wheat around them turned gold. Just in a circle, just for a moment, just one ring of it, gold as it had been before the bargain — and then gray again, but the gray was different now. It was gray like a thing waiting, instead of gray like a thing finished.

Six stars. Wren didn't say it out loud.

* * *

The third verse was the worst one, and Wren knew it before they found it, because the compass had stopped pointing outward.

They were walking along a ridge road above a lake of white fog — the Hush's fog, the fog that had eaten the border, the fog that was eating everything — and Ilex had gone quiet the way he went quiet when he was working something out, and then he had gone quiet a different way, a worse way, and Wren had watched the man slowly understand that the needle was aimed at *him*.

"No," Ilex said.

"It doesn't lie," Wren said. "It points at what's most remembered. Right now, on this whole ridge, in this whole dying country, the most remembered thing is inside you."

"I'm not from here. I'm from Vell. I'm—" He stopped. The compass needle didn't waver. "I was hired to find the Choirhouse," he said, to Mara, in a voice like a confession already halfway out. "That's not all of it. There's something before that. Something I can't—" His hands were shaking. "There's a room in my memory with the door bricked up. I've never been able to open it. I thought everyone had a room like that."

"They do," Wren said gently. "Most people's just aren't load-bearing."

Mara took the compass from him. She held it out, needle steady, pointing at his chest, and then she looked at him with an expression Wren couldn't read — something between fury and pity and the beginning of forgiveness, all fighting for the same face. "Whatever's behind the brick," she said, "we need it. And you need it more."

"I'm afraid of it."

"Good," said Wren. "The verses all live behind afraid."

They made camp because it had to be done sitting down — Ilex insisted on that much dignity — and Mara built a small fire out of gray wheat stalks that burned without warmth or color, and Ilex sat with his hands open on his knees like a man waiting for a verdict, and Wren put his small hands on either side of the cartographer's head the way his grandmother had once shown him, in a memory so old it was the oldest thing he owned.

"Brick by brick," Wren said. "I'll hand them to you. You throw them."

The first brick came out as a smell: woodsmoke and river water and bread. Ilex made a sound like a man struck. The second came out as a voice, a woman's voice singing the counting-song, the *country's* counting-song, and Ilex said, "That's my mother's voice, that's not possible, my mother was from Vell—" and then the third brick came out and he stopped arguing.

He had been born here.

He had been born in this country, in a town whose name came back to him like a fish surfacing — *Lowmere, my town was Lowmere* — and he had been carried across the border as an infant in the last years before the gate closed, one of the last children out, and his mother had stayed because someone had to keep singing, and the Hush had taken her voice for the Choirhouse and her name for itself, and the council of Vell had found the grown Ilex years later, a young mapmaker with a hole in his memory shaped like a country, and they had hired him to do the one thing his buried grief would never let him refuse: find the Choirhouse and end the singing forever, so that no more mothers would be spent like coins.

He had succeeded. That was the brick at the very bottom, the one he threw hardest: he had found it, and he had broken it, and his exile had been staged to hide the success, and everything since — the compass, the wandering, the stumbling out of the dark — had been a man walking in circles around a door he'd bricked up himself.

When it was done, Ilex sat by the colorless fire and wept the way stone weeps, without sound, without permission, and the third verse came up out of the ruined room in him whole and shining, the mother-verse, the one sung over cradles:

*Seven for the child carried away, eight for the mother who had to stay—*

And the fog on the lake below them *recoiled*, just slightly, the way a great animal flinches from a flame it didn't expect.

Wren looked up. Five stars. Four.

But when he looked at his own hands, he could see the lines in his palms, sharp and certain, and when he looked at Mara and Ilex they were *bright* — outlined, lit from within, more real than the ridge, more real than the road, more real than anything else left in the country. Each verse had made them heavier and the world lighter, and Wren understood, with the terrible clarity of a child who remembers everything, what that meant.

The song was choosing them. And the Hush had noticed.

Far out on the white lake, the fog had begun to move against the wind, gathering, patient, courteous — and in the silence between four stars and three, Wren heard it speak for the first time, in a voice like an empty room learning manners:

*You have my verses. Now let us discuss what they cost.*
