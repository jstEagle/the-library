## 9. The Last Star

The Choirhouse had been waiting for them. That was the only word Mara could find for it — not ruined, exactly, though its roof had fallen in a century ago and the rain had eaten the pews down to gray lace, but *waiting*, the way an instrument waits in its case. The walls still stood. The altar at the center of the nave was a single slab of white stone worn smooth as a throat, and above it, where a window should have been, there was only open sky — and in that sky, one star.

It hung low and red, like an ember someone had cupped in their palm to keep from the wind. Everything else was black. Not night-black; Mara knew night, loved it even, the honest dark over Vell with its honest stars scattered thick as spilled salt. This was a different blackness, a blackness with appetite, and it pressed against the broken walls of the Choirhouse like water against glass.

"It's watching us," Wren said. The child's voice was steady, but her hand had found the hem of Mara's coat and gripped it the way she'd been gripping things all day — as if letting go meant being let go of.

"Then let it watch," Mara said, and was surprised by how little her voice shook. "It's going to hear something."

Ilex was already moving through the nave, his compass out, the needle swinging in slow, drunken arcs that had nothing to do with north. He'd stopped trying to explain the thing days ago. It pointed at memories now, and here, in the country that wasn't, it pointed everywhere at once — every wall, every stone, humming with what had been sung into it. "The acoustics are intact," he said, and there was something raw in his voice, something that might have been awe or might have been guilt. "They built this place to hold sound the way a dam holds water. Whatever you sing in here will stay sung."

"Good." Mara set her lantern on the altar. Its light made a small gold island in all that dark, and she thought, absurdly, of her kitchen in Vell, of the kettle she'd left on the hook, of the dusk-names she would never recite again if this went wrong. She pushed the thought away. There was no room for it tonight. Tonight there was only the song.

She climbed onto the altar — it felt wrong to stand below it, below the place where ten generations of keepers had lifted their voices — and looked down at the two people who had walked out of the world with her. Ilex, the disgraced cartographer who had, three nights ago, confessed everything: the council's coin, the staged exile, the years he'd spent hunting the Choirhouse so Vell could stop paying for its keepers. He had broken the song once. Now he stood beneath her with his arms spread wide toward the darkness, planting his feet like a man bracing against a flood, and said, without turning around, "Whatever happens, don't stop."

Wren sat cross-legged at the altar's base, small and grave as a stone saint, her eyes closed. "I'll hold it," she said. "All of it. Every verse. Nothing gets lost while you're singing." And Mara believed her, because Wren was the last complete memory of a whole country, and remembering was the one thing the child did better than breathing.

Mara closed her eyes.

The first verse came up out of her like water from a deep well — the drowned bell tower verse, salt-stained and slow, the one they'd pulled dripping from the flooded crypt with their sleeves rolled and their lungs burning. She sang the tolling of it, the way the bell had rung under the water for sixty years, unheard, unceasing, faithful. The star above the altar pulsed.

The second verse was harder. It lived in the bones of a soldier who still stood sentry at the border of the vanished land, standing guard over a war no one remembered declaring, and when they'd taken the verse from him — gently, singing it free the way you'd ease a splinter from flesh — the skeleton had finally, after all those years, lain down. Mara sang the marching rhythm of it, the long patience of it, and felt the walls of the Choirhouse begin to hum in sympathy, old stones remembering their purpose.

The third verse she almost couldn't sing at all. It was Ilex's — the childhood he'd sold to the council's memory-brokers to buy his silence, the mother whose face he'd traded away without knowing he'd ever had one. They'd found it folded small inside him, hidden so well he'd wept when it unfolded. When Mara sang it now, Ilex made a sound low in his chest like a man taking a wound he'd been owed for twenty years, and held his ground anyway, arms wide, facing the dark.

And then the verses braided.

That was the only word for it. The three recovered songs twisted together with the seven that lived in Mara's blood — the verses every keeper carried without knowing, passed down not in books but in the body, in the shape of the throat, in the instinct that had always told her to speak the valley's names aloud at dusk. Ten voices. One song. Her voice carried all ten now, or seemed to, and the Choirhouse rang like a struck bell, and the star above the altar burned brighter, redder, closer —

— and her mother's voice rose through her.

There was no other way to describe it. It came up through Mara's chest like heat through floorboards, a second voice layered inside her own, lower and warmer, a voice she hadn't heard since she was nine years old singing nonsense in a kitchen while her mother laughed and kneaded bread. *The tenth voice,* the Choirhouse seemed to say. *Collateral. Held.* Not dead. Never dead. Taken as surety for a debt, kept singing somewhere in the dark all these years, and now, through her daughter's throat, finally audible again.

Mara's eyes were streaming. She didn't stop singing. She couldn't have stopped if the world ended around her, and in a sense it was ending around her, because the darkness beyond the walls had begun to move.

* * *

The Hush did not come as a monster. Mara had half-expected teeth, shadow, some vast shape out of the plague-years' worst stories. Instead the absence simply arrived — a region of the nave where the lantern light fell wrong, where sound went to die, where the air had the texture of a held breath. It gathered between the fallen pews, courteous as a diplomat, patient as debt.

*I have come to conclude our transaction,* it said, and its voice was the silence after a question no one answers. *You have the verses. You have the vessel.* It turned — or oriented itself — toward Wren. *Deliver the child, and your mother is returned to you whole. The valley of Vell is spared. The bargain concludes as written.*

Mara sang on. Her mother's voice sang with her. The Hush waited, because the Hush was very good at waiting; hunger always is.

The song reached its final phrase, the one that should have sealed the gate for another generation, the one her mother's voice had been taken to guarantee — and Mara stopped.

The silence that followed was enormous. Ilex's arms were trembling. Wren's eyes had opened, wide and wet and fixed on Mara with a terrible hope. The Hush inclined itself, courteous, expectant.

"No," Mara said.

Her voice cracked on the word, and she let it crack. She climbed down off the altar on legs that barely held her, and she put herself bodily between the Hush and the child, and she said it again, louder, her mother's warmth still ringing in her chest:

"No. I'm breaking the bargain."

*The terms are not yours to break.*

"They are now. I'm the tenth keeper's daughter and the eleventh keeper by blood, and I'm telling you the debt is cancelled — not paid, *cancelled*, do you understand? Because I know what you are now." She took a step toward the absence, which was madness, which was suicide, and did it anyway. "I've read the old bargain. I've sung the old verses. You weren't summoned, Hush. You weren't born. You were *made* — made by desperate people who traded everything they had just to survive, and what they made you out of was the part of themselves they cut away to make the trade. The hunger. The wanting to be remembered. You're not a devourer. You're an amputation that learned to walk."

The Hush was silent. In that silence, Mara heard — or imagined she heard — something almost like breath.

"So I'm not going to pay you," she said. "And I'm not going to fight you. I'm going to do something no one has ever offered you." She turned and held out her hand to Wren. "Come here, love. Both of us. All of us."

Wren came. Ilex came too, abandoning his post at the wall, because whatever was about to happen, he would not face it apart from them. The three of them stood together before the altar, before the single red star, before the courteous absence that had eaten a country.

"You want to be remembered," Mara said. "That's all you've ever wanted. So here is my offer, and it's unprecedented, and it terrifies me more than you do." She drew a breath that tasted of starlight and dust and her mother's kitchen. "A new verse. Not the old ones — not the ten voices of the gate, not the bargain, not the debt. A new verse, written by the forgotten themselves, sung by the forgotten themselves, with you in it. Not as the Hush. As the eleventh voice. Part of the choir instead of the thing the choir keeps out."

*There is no such verse,* the Hush said. But its voice had changed. Mara heard it clearly now, underneath the courtesy: thin, and old, and starving.

"There wasn't," Mara agreed. "So we'll write it. Right now. Wren remembers everything this country forgot — every name, every harvest, every lullaby. Ilex can chart it. And I—" her voice caught, steadied, "—I have my mother's voice in me, and she has been waiting thirty years to sing something *free*. We'll write you a verse, Hush. We'll sing you into the song. And when the gate dissolves — and it will dissolve, tonight, because I'm done holding countries apart like quarreling children — you won't be the hunger outside anymore. You'll be the harmony within. Memory, like weather, crossing freely both ways. Everyone remembered. Even you."

The star above the altar flared.

The Hush trembled — there was no other word for it, the absence itself shuddering like a man offered bread after decades of famine — and when it spoke again, the courtesy had fallen away entirely, and what remained was small and vast at once, ancient and infantile, a voice like the space between bells:

*Nobody has ever asked me to sing.*

"I know," Mara said gently. "That was always the crime. Not yours. Theirs." She looked at Wren, at Ilex, at the red star burning down to its last ember. "So. Shall we?"

Wren was already grinning through her tears, already opening her mouth, already spilling names — hundreds of names, thousands, the whole resurrected census of a country that wasn't supposed to exist. Ilex had his journal out, sketching staves in the margins faster than she could speak. And high above the broken roof of the Choirhouse, the last star began, very slowly, to change color — red to gold, ember to dawn —

— and somewhere far away, on the far side of a gate that no longer wanted to be a gate, Mara's mother opened her mouth to sing.
