## 1. The Price of Ink

The ink ran out at the bottom of the page, as it always did, and Edmund Hartwell sat for a moment in the small silence of the nib scratching nothing, and thought: fifteen years.

Fifteen years since he'd signed himself over to the Hollow Choir to pay a debt his father had left like an inheritance nobody wanted. Fifteen years of copying charm ledgers in the scriptorium above the Vessing countinghouse, where the light came through leaded windows the color of old bruises and everything smelled of tallow, dust, and the particular sourness of sanctified paper. He was thirty-four years old. He had copied, by his own rough arithmetic — and Edmund's arithmetic was very good; it had to be, or the Choir would have eaten him alive decades ago — somewhere north of four hundred thousand words of blessings, denials, disclaimers, and receipts.

None of them were prayers. That was the first thing they taught you. The Hollow Choir did not pray. The Choir *recorded*.

He dipped the nib again, wiped it against the pot's lip the way Brother Anselm had shown him when he was nineteen and terrified of everything, and continued.

*Item forty-one: one (1) knot-charm, household, minor warding, sold to Mrs. Ilva Brenner, cooper's widow, three coppers. Blessing administered per rite 12-C. Efficacy presumed. No follow-up required.*

*Efficacy presumed.* Four words that did more theological heavy lifting than anything in the Choir's actual canon. Nobody had ever followed up on a charm in the history of the institution. Nobody wanted to know if the knots worked, because if they worked, then something was listening, and if something was listening, then the whole careful architecture of the Banishment — the gods outlawed, the shrines bricked over, the hymns rewritten into accounting forms — was built on sand. And if they didn't work, then the Choir was selling frightened people knotted string at three coppers apiece, which was a different kind of scandal, the kind that got scribes reassigned to the salt marshes.

So: *efficacy presumed*. Everyone slept better. Except, Edmund suspected, everyone.

His tally column stood at the right edge of the ledger, neat as fence posts. Debt remaining: eleven years, two months, and change. He did the math the way other men said their morning devotions. Eleven years. He would be forty-five when the last line cleared. His father had died owing the Choir for a single unlicensed healing — one healing, performed on Edmund's mother, too late to save her anyway — and the debt had passed down like a bloodline. The Choir called it *continuity of obligation*. Edmund called it what it was, but only in the privacy of his own skull, where even the Choir couldn't audit.

"Brother Hartwell."

He didn't jump. Fifteen years teaches you not to jump. He finished the stroke he was on, blotted it, and turned. Sister Ondine stood at the end of his desk with her hands folded and her face arranged into the expression the Choir issued along with its grey robes: pleasant, vacant, immovable.

"You're wanted downstairs," she said. "Blessing rotation. Chapel of the Small Hours."

"Blessing rotation is Thursday," Edmund said. "Today is Tuesday."

"Thursday's roster changed." A pause with weight in it. "You're on the public line."

Edmund looked at her. She looked back with the serene blankness of a woman who had delivered exactly as much information as she intended to deliver and would deliver no more, ever, under torture or bribery or the promise of eternal salvation, none of which existed anymore according to official doctrine.

The public line. The open chapel where anyone off the street could come and receive a standard blessing — a touch to the brow, a recitation from the Book of Quiet, the little formula that said, in essence, *nothing is watching you, and isn't that a comfort*. It was the least demanding duty in the building and therefore always assigned to whoever had most recently displeased someone. Edmund reviewed his week. He had done nothing except arrive early, copy accurately, and eat lunch alone. Which meant either he'd displeased someone without noticing — possible; he'd had practice being invisible but invisibility wasn't foolproof — or somebody up the chain wanted a warm body on the public line today, and his name had come out of the ledger because his name always came out of the ledger eventually. Debts were like that.

"Fine," he said, and capped his ink.

* * *

The Chapel of the Small Hours occupied the ground floor of the countinghouse, and it had been designed, Edmund had long ago decided, by someone who hated both chapels and hours. It was low-ceilinged and windowless, lit by oil lamps behind frosted glass so that no shadow anywhere had edges. The altar was a desk. The pews were benches with numbers stenciled on them, like a waiting room, because that was what it was: a waiting room for a god who had been formally evicted fifteen years before Edmund was born, and who had, officially, gone.

The queue this morning was short. Seven souls, mostly the usual: an old man with a cough who came every week for a blessing that did nothing and seemed to find the doing-nothing restful; two women with a dispute about a boundary wall, wanting the neutral presence of the Choir while they shouted; a boy of maybe ten, sent by his mother, holding a coin in his fist like it might bite him.

Edmund took his place behind the desk-altar, folded his hands, and began.

The rite was simple enough that he could have done it asleep, and some mornings he suspected he had. Touch the brow. Speak the lines. The lines went: *In the quiet after the singing, we keep the books. Nothing is owed to heaven. Nothing is owed by you. Go in order.*

Go in order. Not go in peace — peace implied a peacemaker, and the peacemakers had all been bricked into their shrines with the rest of the pantheon. Order was what the Choir offered instead. Order you could audit.

The old man came first. Brow, lines, coin into the tray. The coughing woman after him — no, the boundary-wall women, who shouted at each other through the entire blessing and thanked him warmly afterward, which told you most of what you needed to know about what the blessing was actually for. Then the boy.

The boy climbed onto the bench across the desk, held up his chin with the grave courage of the very young facing institutions, and closed his eyes.

Edmund reached out. Touched two fingers to the small warm forehead. Opened his mouth for the lines.

And the lamp behind the frosted glass went out.

Not flickered. Went out — all of them, at once, the whole chapel dropping into a brown half-dark that smelled suddenly of cold ash, and Edmund's fingers were still on the boy's brow and the words were still in his mouth and they wouldn't come. He knew them. He had spoken them perhaps sixty thousand times. They were simply *gone*, the way a stair is gone in the dark, and for one full second — one second, an eternity in ritual time — Edmund Hartwell knelt there with his hand on a child's head and nothing in him but a vast ringing silence, like standing in a burned orchard.

Then the lamps came back. All of them, evenly, as though nothing had happened, and the words arrived in his mouth mid-line as if they'd never left:

"—nothing is owed to heaven," he heard himself say. "Nothing is owed by you. Go in order."

The boy opened his eyes. "Did it work?" he asked, in the way children ask, meaning nothing and everything.

"It worked," Edmund said, because that was the answer, the only answer, the answer printed on the inside of his skull in Choir script. "Go home to your mother."

The boy left a copper in the tray and went. The queue moved. The old man with the cough received his blessing and found it restful. The world resumed its ledgers.

But Edmund's hands were not steady for the rest of the hour, and he noticed, and he noted that he noticed, the way he noted discrepancies in a column: small, contained, flagged for later review. Because here was the thing. Here was the thing he would spend the walk home failing not to think about.

Lamps didn't do that. Oil lamps guttered, yes, smoked, yes, drowned in their own oil if the wick sat wrong — but they did not all go out at once, in a windowless room, in dead still air, and come back together like a held breath released. And words didn't leave a man's mouth after fifteen years of repetition unless something had taken them, and things did not take words in the Hollow Choir's chapel, because there was nothing in the Hollow Choir's chapel to take them. That was the entire point of the architecture. That was the entire point of the *country*.

Vessing had outlawed its gods before Edmund's grandmother was young. Every shrine bricked, every icon melted for candle-holders, every hymnal recalled and pulped into ledger paper — Edmund wrote on the pulped hymns daily; sometimes he liked to imagine he could still hear a melody in the margins, though he'd never told anyone that, not even — well. Not even her. The Banishment had been thorough, orderly, and final, and the Choir's founding document said so in language so clean it made your teeth ache: *The accounts between heaven and earth are closed. Let no man reopen them.*

Closed accounts. Audited, balanced, sealed with wax and doctrine.

So why, Edmund asked the frosted glass, the desk, the cooling spot on a child's forehead — why had the room held its breath?

* * *

He stayed late in the scriptorium, past the bell that ended the working day, past the point where anyone would notice or care. This was not unusual. Edmund staying late was as reliable as Edmund's arithmetic; the scriptorium was the one room in Vessing where he felt like a thing with edges. Rows of desks, rows of lamps, the soft industry of thirty pens moving in near-unison. Somewhere below, the night market was setting up in the tannery lanes — he could smell the woodsmoke drifting up through the ventilation grates, hear the far-off cry of the chestnut sellers — and Edmund Hartwell, model scribe, pillar of continuity, would be down there within the hour doing the one thing guaranteed to hang him: selling charms. Little ones. Harmless ones. Knots and folded papers and whispered nonsense that had never once worked, as far as he knew, in fifteen years of side business.

That was the joke he kept locked in his chest where the Choir couldn't audit it: the lawbreaker whose crime was fraud. He broke the Banishment laws nightly and nothing happened, because nothing he sold did anything. The gods were gone. The knots were string. Everybody was safe, including him, especially him.

Except tonight the arithmetic wouldn't sit still.

He pulled a fresh ledger toward him — the master registry, the one that tracked every charm sold through official channels in the Vessing district — and began, not copying, but reading. Actually reading. When had he last actually read these entries? Years. You stopped seeing the words after a while; they became water you swam in.

*Item 3,311: knot-charm, minor warding, sold to T. Ellery, cartwright. Rite 12-C. Efficacy presumed.*

*Item 3,314: knot-charm, minor warding, sold to —*

He stopped.

There was a smudge on the page. Not ink — ash. A faint grey fingerprint at the margin of item 3,314, the kind of mark a lamp leaves when it gutters and a sleeve passes through the smoke. Except the scriptorium lamps hadn't guttered. He'd have sworn it. He'd have sworn it on the Book of Quiet, which was the strongest oath available to a man who no longer believed in anything strong enough to swear by.

Edmund rubbed the smudge with his thumb. It didn't smear. Ash doesn't smear on ledger paper; it sits there like it's waiting.

He looked at his hands. Clean. He looked at the lamp beside him. Burning straight and calm, not a tremor in the flame. He looked around the scriptorium at the other late workers — Brother Anselm, nearly deaf, bent over his columns; two novices whispering over a shared pot of ink — and none of them had looked up, none of them had seen anything, because there had been nothing to see.

*Efficacy presumed,* said the ledger, in his own handwriting, fifteen thousand times.

*Presumed*, Edmund thought, and the word tasted different tonight. Presumed meant *assumed without checking*. Assumed without checking meant *afraid to check*. And the Choir — the great grey machine of the Choir, with its rites and rosters and its eleven-year hold on his life — the Choir had been afraid to check for fifteen years, and had built an entire country's worth of paperwork on top of the fear, and called the paperwork faith.

He was tired. That was the explanation he chose, dipping his pen, squaring the ledger, closing the day. Fifteen years of ink makes a man see smoke where there's only smudge. Tomorrow the lamps would behave and the words would stay in his mouth and the arithmetic would sit still, and he'd sell his harmless knots in the dark and come home and copy his harmless lines, and the account between heaven and earth would remain exactly as closed as it had been his whole life.

He blew out his lamp. In the dark, for half a heartbeat, the scriptorium smelled of cold ash and something older underneath it — orchards, maybe, or rain on burnt ground, or a voice singing a tune he almost knew.

Then it was just tallow and dust again, and Edmund Hartwell went down the stairs to the night market with his pocketful of string, telling himself the same lie he'd told for fifteen years:

Nothing is listening.

Somewhere below, in the tannery lanes, a chestnut seller's brazier flared green, and no one noticed but him.
