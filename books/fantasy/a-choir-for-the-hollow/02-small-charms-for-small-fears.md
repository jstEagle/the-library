## 2. Small Charms for Small Fears

The night market in Vessing began where the streetlamps gave up.

Edmund had learned this fifteen years ago, his first winter selling charms, when he'd set up too close to the lamplight on Cordwainer's Row and a warden had strolled past twice before Edmund understood the geometry of the thing. Light was law. Law was light. The Choir's patrols walked the bright streets and considered the dark ones beneath their dignity, which meant the dark ones belonged to everyone else—to the fishwives hawking eels past midnight, the knife-grinders, the fortune-tellers with their cupped candles, and Edmund, with his tray of protective knots laid out on a square of black cloth that made them look, if you squinted, like nothing at all.

He'd chosen his corner for its shadows the way other men chose wives: carefully, after long observation, with an eye to what would keep him alive.

"Knots," he said now, low, as a pair of drovers passed. "Charmed against fever, against false friends, against doors that won't stay shut. Small charms for small fears."

One of the drovers paused—a big man with hay still in his collar—and picked up a knot of red cord, turning it over in fingers like ham hocks. "Do these work?"

Edmund smiled the smile he'd been smiling for fifteen years. It was a good smile, practiced, warm without promising anything. "They work the way a fence works," he said. "Not against everything. Against most things."

"And what's the rest of things?"

"The rest," Edmund said, "you handle yourself."

The drover laughed and bought two—one for himself, one threaded onto his wife's name-day bracelet back home. Two coppers. Edmund took them without letting his fingers linger on the coins, because lingering was how you got careless, and careless was how you ended up in the Choir's holding cells below the Scriptorium Annex, answering questions from men who wrote down your answers in beautiful script.

Here was the joke of his life, the one he told no one: Edmund Hartwell was a criminal six nights a week, and every seventh day he knelt in the white galleries of the Hollow Choir and copied out the ledgers that catalogued crimes exactly like his own. *Charm, minor, protective—confiscated. Vendor: unknown. Disposition: burned.* He wrote *burned* forty times some weeks, in his fine scribe's hand, and each time he thought about the knots in his own coat pocket, warm from his body heat, and felt something he refused to call hypocrisy because calling it that would mean looking at it directly.

It wasn't even really magic. That was the second joke. The knots were just cord and repetition—he braided them while reciting his grandmother's counting songs, the ones in Osselmarki, the language nobody spoke anymore except him and the dead—and whatever settled into the braid was less a blessing than a habit, the way a well-worn chair holds the shape of the person who sat in it. He sold comfort. He sold the feeling of having done *something*. In a city this size, that was worth two coppers to half the population.

The Choir called it trafficking in proscribed thaumaturgy. Edmund called it rent.

The night moved around him. A woman bought a knot against nightmares and told him, unprompted, about her sister's husband, which Edmund gathered was itself a nightmare of some kind. A boy of maybe ten traded him a bent nail and half a meat pie for a luck-braid, watching Edmund's hands work with the fixed hunger of someone who wanted, more than anything, to be able to make a thing himself. Edmund showed him the first pass of the braid—the over-under, thumb anchoring the third strand—and watched the boy's face do the thing faces did when they realized a mystery was actually just craft.

"Don't tell anyone I taught you," Edmund said. "Craft's not illegal. The talking about it is."

"What happens if you get caught?" the boy asked, mouth full.

"They burn the knots." Edmund folded his black cloth over his remaining stock. "And ask me very polite questions until I wish they'd burned me instead."

The boy considered this with the seriousness of the young. "Is it true there used to be gods? Real ones? My gran says her gran saw one drown a river."

"Your gran's gran," Edmund said, "saw a flood, and people see floods better when they're frightened." He said it in the Choir's cadence, the approved catechism, *the heavens are hollow, the heavens are ours to fill*—and hated the taste of it in his mouth, because somewhere under his ribs he wasn't sure it was true, hadn't been sure since he was nine years old and kneeling at his mother's bedside doing something no catechism covered.

But that door stayed shut. All his doors stayed shut. That was the whole architecture of his survival.

He was packing up early—wind coming off the river, the market thinning, a warden's lantern bobbing two streets over with terrible patience—when she found him.

She came out of the alley behind the tannery row like something poured rather than walking, a small woman in a dyer's apron still stiff with indigo stains, carrying a bundle that cried. Not loudly. That was the worst part, Edmund thought later. The bundle cried the way fires die down—not gone, but running out of what it needed to keep going.

"Please," she said. "The knot-seller. They said at the fish stalls there's a knot-seller."

"You've got the wrong—" Edmund started, automatic, and then looked at the bundle, and stopped.

The child inside it was perhaps four years old, though fever had thinned him toward some earlier age, some age before the world got hold of him. His skin had the grey-yellow gloss of a lamp low on oil. His breathing came in little pulled threads, each one costing. The mother held him the way you hold something you have already begun to grieve but cannot stop protecting—too tight, angled away from the wind, her whole body curved around his like a wall around a garden.

"I know what this is," she said, fast, before Edmund could do the arithmetic of risk out loud. "I know it's illegal. I don't care. The physicians' hall wants eight marks for the consultation and four more for the medicine, and I've sold everything sellable, and the temple infirmary takes only those who can tithe, so please, please, I heard your knots work, I heard it from Tessa whose roof-beam didn't fall, I'll pay you—"

She was digging in her apron. What came out was a sad handful: three coppers and a tin button and something wrapped in wax paper that turned out to be half a honey cake, saved, clearly, from some occasion that had deserved a whole one.

Edmund looked at the money. Then at the child. Then at the warden's lantern, which had stopped moving, two streets over, in the way that meant its bearer was checking a shuttered window or sharing a pipe with a friend, and would move on soon, and might come back.

"Put it away," he said quietly. "All of it."

"But—"

"*Put it away.*"

She flinched. The bundle cried its thread of a cry. And Edmund Hartwell, who had spent fifteen years perfecting the art of not being involved, heard himself say, "Sit down here, out of the wind, and give him to me for a moment. Just a moment. And then you take him straight to Mother Cazia at the bathhouse on Tanner's Walk—she owes me a debt and doesn't charge widows. Tell her Edmund sent you. She'll grumble. Let her grumble. Grumbling is free."

The woman sat. She handed the boy over with the terrible care of the drowning passing off their last plank. He weighed almost nothing. Through the thin blanket Edmund could feel the fever banking itself in the child's body like coals raked over, patient, waiting for fuel.

He should have sold her a standard knot. Fever-guard, double-braided, two coppers, works the way a fence works. That was the transaction. That was the whole safe shape of his life: small charms, small fears, nothing that would ever make anyone remember his face.

Instead he set the boy across his knees, and took three lengths of cord from his kit—red, white, and the black he never sold—and began to braid.

He told himself it was just craft. Over-under, thumb anchoring the third strand, the rhythm pulling him down into the old counting-song before he could decide whether to let it come. *Een, twee, dree, de wind ken't de weg nae huus toe* —one, two, three, the wind knows the way home—it was a nonsense rhyme, a skipping song, Osselmarki children had sung it for centuries while jumping rope in villages that were no longer on any map. It carried no power. It couldn't. Power had been outlawed out of the world by men far more thorough than the Choir, and besides, Edmund was nobody, a ledger-clerk with ink-stained cuffs, a man whose prayers had never once—

Once.

He braided faster.

The song went through him like water through a lock, and where it passed, something *turned*, the way a key turns—not force, not light, none of the things the old stories promised, just a quiet shifting of weight, as if the night had been holding its breath since his mother's deathbed and had finally, finally exhaled. The cords warmed in his fingers. Not metaphorically. Not the warmth of friction. Warm as a held hand.

The fever broke between the fourth verse and the fifth.

He felt it happen—felt it through his palms, through his knees, a soft subsiding in the small body like snow sliding off a roof. The thread-pulled breathing deepened. Lengthened. Evened into sleep, real sleep, the heavy unguarded sleep of a child whose body has remembered how to work. The grey left his face the way tide leaves a shore, gradually and then all at once, and color came up under it, pink and ordinary and impossibly, obscenely healthy.

Edmund sat very still.

Around him the night market went on—a knife-grinder's wheel shrieking somewhere, a woman laughing at a joke Edmund would never hear—but all of it sounded suddenly far away, as if he stood behind glass. Because this did not happen. This had *never* happened. Fifteen years of knots and not one of them had ever done more than make a frightened person feel less frightened, and here was a dying child breathing like a sleeper in summer, and the cords in Edmund's hands cooling slowly, innocently, like embers after rain, and the counting-song still echoing in his skull in a voice that was almost—almost—his own.

"Is he—" The mother's voice cracked apart. "What did you—"

"He needs soup," Edmund heard himself say, steady, reasonable, a scribe's voice, a clerk's voice, while underneath it something in him screamed with a joy and terror so large it had no words. "Broth, tonight, and again at dawn. Mother Cazia will show you how to bring the fever's shadow down gently, the last of it. Go. Now, while the streets are quiet."

She took the boy and the sleeping weight of him nearly buckled her, she clutched him so hard, and she looked at Edmund with an expression he had no ledger-category for—worship, maybe, or the beginning of it, gratitude grown past the borders of gratitude—and said, "What's your name? What do I—what do I *call* you?"

"Nobody," said Edmund Hartwell, whose name had been written once already in a file with a black bar through it, though he didn't know that yet. "Call me nobody. And forget this corner. Don't come back here. If anyone asks, the fever broke on its own. Fevers do that."

"Do they?" she whispered.

"Almost never," Edmund admitted. "But we'll let them think so."

She went. He watched her go, the bundle heavy and easy in her arms, until the alley took her. Then he sat alone in the dark with his tray and his black cloth and his hands, which had stopped being merely his hands sometime in the fourth verse and were now, apparently, instruments of something the world had spent a century and a half pretending did not exist.

He should burn the stock. That was the sensible thought, arriving late and dressed in the Choir's own voice: *Charm, major, unauthorized healing—confiscated. Vendor: known. Disposition: burned.* He should pack up, go home, feed his ledgers their tidy lies tomorrow morning, and never braid another knot as long as he lived, because whatever had answered him tonight had been paid for in advance by a nine-year-old boy at a deathbed, and debts like that did not stay quiet.

He looked down at the black cord he never sold. It lay across his palm, cool now, ordinary, dumb as string.

"Small charms," he said aloud, to no one, to the empty corner, to whatever had leaned close in the fourth verse. "I deal in small charms for small fears. Whatever you think this is—we're going to need to talk terms."

The wind off the river moved through the market and died the lamp of the knife-grinder next door and relit it again, which lamps did not do, which lamps absolutely did not do, and Edmund Hartwell sat in the dark laughing in a way that was halfway to sobbing, because fifteen years of careful, gray, invisible life had just come apart at the seams over a honey cake and half a meat pie.

Two streets over, unbeknownst to him, a warden finished his pipe and marked in his round-book a single line: *Night market, tannery corner—knot-seller active again. Watch.*

* * *

The next evening, Edmund did not return to the tannery corner. He sold instead from the steps of the disused rope-walk, further east, deeper in the dark—and told himself the relocation was routine caution, rotation, hygiene, anything but flight.

It changed nothing. All day at the scriptorium his hands had itched with the memory of the braid; twice he caught himself mouthing the counting-song over inventory tallies, and once—only once, quickly, guiltily—he pressed his thumb to a smudge of spilled ink on a novice's practice sheet, and the smudge *lifted*, cleanly, like a stain persuaded rather than scrubbed. He stared at the blank paper for a long time. Around him, forty other scribes copied on in silence, pen-nibs ticking like slow clocks, and no one looked up, and no one ever looked up, and Edmund understood with cold clarity that the safety of his entire life had always rested on that fact.

That night, at the rope-walk, the first customer was the mother. Of course it was. Word traveled in the dark markets faster than light traveled in the bright ones.

She didn't buy. She brought broth, wrapped in cloth, still hot—"Mother Cazia sends it, she says you're a fool and a saint and she'll thank you when she's dead and not before"—and the boy with her, walking, *walking*, pink-faced and furious about bedtime, waved at Edmund with the imperious goodwill of the recently resurrected.

"He sleeps," the mother said simply. "He ate a whole bowl and asked for more. The physician at the charity hall examined him this morning and declared it a spontaneous remission and wrote it in his book, and his book will be read by other physicians, and none of them will ever know." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "I know what I saw. I know what I *paid*."

"You paid nothing."

"I paid attention." She gripped his wrist, hard, indigo-stained fingers. "There are others like my Tam, Mr. Nobody. Others the halls turn away because they can't pay, others the infirmary turns away because they can't tithe. Word is moving. By week's end there will be a queue at your corner a mile long, and I am telling you as one mother to one man: some of those mothers will talk to the wrong ears. Bright ears. Do you understand me?"

He understood her. He looked past her shoulder, down the rope-walk's long dark throat, and saw what she meant: shapes gathering, hesitant, hopeful, drawn by a story that had sprouted overnight in the rich soil of desperation. A limping man. A woman with a bandaged hand. Someone holding a covered cage, for reasons Edmund declined to speculate on.

Small charms for small fears, he thought. Except fear was never small. Fear was a flood, and he had spent fifteen years building fences along a river he did not understand, and last night the river had noticed him.

"Go home," he said to the mother. "Keep Tam indoors a week. And if anyone asks—whoever asks—you bought a knot and the fever broke on its own. Fevers do that."

"Fevers do that," she repeated, like a lesson learned, and went.

Edmund sold eleven knots that night—ordinary ones, honest frauds, cord and repetition and comfort—and turned away everyone with anything worse than insomnia. It cost him. Each refusal had a face. But he turned them all away anyway, and packed up early, and was folding his black cloth when he felt it: a change in the air, faint as a shift in pressure before rain, coming from the direction of the city's heart.

The bells of the Hollow Choir began to ring.

Not vespers. Not the alarm. Something older, rarer—a peal Edmund had heard exactly once in fifteen years, on the anniversary of the Banishment, when the whole hierarchy processed in white and sang the great hymn to an empty heaven. The bells rang it out across Vessing, bronze on bronze, and in the streets below the rope-walk people stopped and looked up, puzzled, uneasy, asking each other what it meant.

Edmund knew what it meant. Somewhere in the white galleries, someone had opened a ledger and written something that could not be unwritten. He could feel it the way he'd felt the fever break—through his palms, in the roots of his teeth.

He shouldered his tray and walked home fast, a clerk again, gray again, invisible again, telling himself the bells had nothing to do with a honey cake and a counting-song and a child who slept.

Behind him, on the windowsill of his narrow room, where no one had entered and no dust had stirred, the ash had begun—very quietly—to gather.
