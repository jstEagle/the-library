## 3. The Cantor's Visit

The audit notice arrived on a Tuesday, which Maren Solle considered a small mercy. Tuesdays were ledger days in the Vessing scriptorium, and no scribe could claim surprise at being audited on the day he was already drowning in his own columns.

She read the assignment slip twice, standing in the corridor outside the Cantors' Hall while incense smoke drifted past her like something fleeing. *Scribe E. Hartwell. Charm ledgers, years nine through fifteen. Discrepancy review.* Her own handwriting had requested it — she'd submitted the request eleven days ago through proper channels, proper forms, the proper little machine of the Choir grinding her intention into procedure until it came out the other side looking like an accident.

That was how everything honest got done in the Hollow Choir: disguised as paperwork.

Maren was thirty-one years old and had been a cantor for twelve of those years, which meant she could sing the Litanies of Absence in her sleep, had done so once, actually, waking her roommate with a flawless rendition of the third movement that the woman described afterward as "the loneliest thing I've ever heard." The Litany was the Choir's central rite — sung daily in every temple across Vessing, a hymn of thanks that the gods were gone and would not be coming back. *We are delivered from their appetites. We are delivered from their mercy.* Twelve years, and Maren still could not decide whether she believed the second line.

She clipped the audit notice to her board, squared it, and told herself the flutter under her ribs was indigestion.

* * *

Edmund Hartwell's desk was the fourth one along the south wall of the scriptorium, and she knew this without checking because she had known it for six years, ever since the Choir, with its characteristic genius for irony, had assigned them to the same archive rotation. Six years of passing him ink pots. Six years of correcting each other's transcriptions. Six years of a conversation conducted entirely in the margins of other people's documents, which was, she suspected, the only kind of conversation either of them trusted.

He looked up when she stopped at his desk, and she watched him do the thing he always did — the half-second pause, the flicker of calculation behind his eyes, as if every visitor were a bill come due.

"Cantor Solle." He rose. Scribes rose for cantors; it was protocol. "I wasn't told to expect an audit."

"I requested it personally." She set her board down on the corner of his desk, where it wouldn't touch his papers. She noticed she did this automatically now, kept her work from mingling with his, and wondered what it would mean if she stopped. "Years nine through fifteen. There's a discrepancy pattern in your charm disbursements I want walked through."

"Discrepancy pattern," Edmund repeated. His voice was even. His hands, folding themselves on the desk, were very carefully still. "Should I send for my supervisor?"

"Your supervisor approved the audit." She pulled the first ledger toward her. "It's just you and me, Scribe Hartwell. And the Choir's arithmetic."

For a moment neither of them moved. Then he sat, and she sat across from him, close enough that their knees nearly touched beneath the desk, and began.

The work itself was simple, or should have been. Charm ledgers recorded every protective knot, blessing-scrip, and warding token issued by the scriptorium — all of them harmless by law, all of them blessed in the Litany's name, all of them, per the Reconciliation Edicts, containing exactly nothing. The gods were gone. The charms worked because people believed they worked, and belief was permitted, and the difference between a charm and a prayer was a line the Choir had spent fifteen years drawing and redrawing.

But Edmund's columns didn't add up. Not dramatically — never dramatically, that was the thing about him, everything about Edmund Hartwell was calibrated to pass unnoticed — but the way a river doesn't add up to its banks. Small overages. Knots issued that weren't logged. Scrips blessed twice and distributed once, the surplus unaccounted. Over seven years it accumulated to perhaps four hundred units of unrecorded charm-work, and four hundred units was enough to hang a man in Vessing if anyone cared to do the arithmetic.

Maren had done the arithmetic. Eleven days ago, cross-checking distribution tallies against issuance records, she had found the gap, felt her stomach drop through the floor of the Cantors' Hall, and then — this was the part she couldn't explain, the part she'd been turning over ever since like a stone with something warm underneath — she had requested the audit herself rather than flagging the file upward.

There was a word for what she'd done. She knew the word. Cantors learned it in their second year of training, alongside the rites of detection and the binding formulas: *suppression of evidence*. It carried a penance of forty days' silence and, in aggravated cases, loss of office.

She turned the page.

"This entry here." She tapped a line from year eleven. "Forty knots issued, thirty-six logged. Where are the other four?"

Edmund looked at the entry for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had the flatness of a man reading his own eulogy. "Damaged in handling. Logged as spoilage."

"Spoilage is logged separately. There's no spoilage record for this batch."

"No," he agreed quietly. "There isn't."

She waited. He said nothing else. Outside the scriptorium windows, Vessing went about its afternoon — cart wheels on wet cobbles, a fishmonger crying the day's catch, the bell of the Hollow Temple tolling three o'clock in a city that had outlawed its own gods and called the outlawry peace.

"You understand what happens next," Maren said. "If I report these numbers as they stand."

"I understand."

"Four hundred units is a capital threshold. You know that better than anyone — you copy the statutes often enough."

"I copy them," Edmund said, "every week."

Something in the way he said it — not bitter, not pleading, just *tired*, tired down to the grain — made her want to put her hand flat on the table between them and leave it there. She did not. Instead she turned another page, and another, walking him through his own seven years of quiet theft, and he answered each question with the same terrible honesty of a man who has already been convicted and is simply confirming the sentence.

Except he wasn't confessing. That was what unsettled her most, reviewing it later. He never once asked for mercy. He never once lied, either — not exactly. Every answer he gave was technically true and structurally evasive, a wall built entirely out of accurate statements. *Spoilage. Handling losses. Clerical error.* She was a trained interrogator of discrepancies and she could not crack it, and somewhere around the fourth hour she understood that she didn't want to crack it, and that understanding frightened her more than any number in any ledger.

* * *

They broke for tea at dusk.

This was also protocol — audits exceeding six hours required a refreshment interval, documented on form 12-C, which Maren filled out with a hand that was steadier than she felt. The scriptorium emptied around them. Other scribes gathered their things and left in twos and threes, nodding to her as they passed, and she nodded back with the smooth blank courtesy of rank, and then the room was theirs alone, lit by lamps and the last grey light coming through the high windows.

Edmund made the tea. She hadn't asked him to; he simply rose, took the pot from the warming shelf, and poured two cups, and handed her hers the way he always had, handle turned toward her right hand, because six years ago she'd mentioned once — once — that she held her cup awkwardly left-handed, and he had remembered forever, the way he seemed to remember everything, filing it away in whatever careful ledgers he kept behind his eyes.

"Thank you," she said.

"You're welcome, Cantor Solle."

"Maren."

A pause. "Maren."

She drank. The tea was cheap scriptorium blend, over-steeped, slightly bitter, and it was the best thing she had tasted in weeks, and she hated herself a little for that.

"The numbers," she said finally. "Walk me through it differently. Not as an auditor. Just—" She stopped. Started again. "Why?"

Edmund turned his cup slowly on its saucer. For a long time she thought he wouldn't answer. Then:

"When I first came to the scriptorium, there was a woman who came every month for a knot for her son's cradle. Fever season, year ten. She couldn't afford the fee, so I—" He shrugged, a small motion. "Issued her one off the books. And then the next month there was someone else. And it became a habit. Habits become patterns. Patterns become four hundred units."

"And did it work? The knot. Did the boy live?"

"He lived." Something moved behind Edmund's eyes, some door opening briefly onto a room she wasn't allowed to see. "Fever broke in a night. The mother swore it was the charm."

"It wasn't," Maren said automatically. Doctrine. "Charms contain nothing. The fever broke because fevers break."

"Of course," Edmund said. "The fever broke because fevers break."

He said it the way a man repeats a password he no longer believes opens anything, and Maren heard it, and set down her cup, and made the decision that she would spend the rest of this story paying for.

"I'm going to log this audit as conducted," she said, "with minor clerical irregularities noted and resolved. Spoilage misfiling. Nothing material."

Edmund went very still. "You can't—"

"I can. I'm the cantor of record. My signature closes the file." She was already pulling the audit form toward her, writing in the neat, damning, merciful hand of her office. "What I can't do is un-see the pattern, Edmund. Four hundred units doesn't happen by accident. Someone taught you to keep two sets of books, or you taught yourself, and either way you're sitting on a fire that's going to catch eventually, and when it does, the person holding the audit stamp won't be me."

"Then don't hold it."

"I have to hold it. That's the job." She signed. Blew the ink dry. Looked up at him, and let six years of margin-conversations rise into her voice for exactly one sentence: "You're the only irregularity in my life I've never wanted to resolve."

The words hung there. Edmund stared at her, and for a moment — one unguarded moment — his whole careful face came apart, and what was underneath wasn't guilt or fear but something rawer, something that looked almost like grief, as if kindness were the one thing he had no ledger for.

"Maren," he said softly. "You shouldn't have done that."

"I know."

"They'll audit the auditor, eventually. They always do."

"Then they'll find a cantor whose files are immaculate except for one scribe she couldn't bring herself to burn." She stood, gathering her board, restoring the distance between them the way she restored order to everything — quickly, competently, before it could hurt her. "Fix your numbers, Edmund. Whatever you're doing out there — and I'm not asking, because I don't want to know — make it smaller. Make it invisible. Because next time the discrepancy finds you, it won't be me holding the lamp."

At the door she paused. Protocol said she should not look back. She looked back anyway.

He was standing by his desk with the untouched tea in both hands, watching her, and the lamplight caught the ash-grey of his eyes, and she thought — absurdly, irreverently, in a way that would have earned her forty days' silence if spoken aloud — that he looked less like a scribe than like a man standing in a doorway deciding whether to stay in the house or step out of it.

"Goodnight, Edmund."

"Goodnight, Maren."

She walked back to the Cantors' Hall through the blue evening, past the Hollow Temple where the evening Litany was beginning, hundreds of voices singing *we are delivered, we are delivered* to a heaven that had given no sign of listening for fifteen years, and Maren found, walking, that she was humming something else instead — a fragment of melody she didn't recognize, old and strange, that she was certain she had never learned.

She filed the thought away with the rest of the day's irregularities.

Unresolved.
