## 4. Prayers Answered

The trouble began with bread.

Edmund heard about it the way he heard about everything in Vessing—secondhand, over a ledger, from a man who talked too much. Brother Anselm had been copying out tithe schedules when a baker's apprentice came pounding up the temple steps, flour still on his sleeves, insisting that his master's loaves had risen without yeast, without warmth, without anyone touching them at all. They had risen in the cold pantry overnight, forty of them, split and golden and smelling of something no one could name—not honey, not clover, but something older, like a field after rain.

Anselm told this story with the flat delight of a man reporting someone else's misfortune. "The master baker's thrown the whole batch in the river," he said. "Says they're unclean."

"Probably just warm weather," Edmund said, and kept his pen moving.

It was warm weather. It was always something. A charm that worked too well, a candle that burned blue, a dream that lingered past morning. Fifteen years in the scriptorium had taught Edmund that the world was full of small anomalies, and that the Choir's entire function was to file them into categories small enough to ignore. *Atmospheric disturbance. Contaminated grain. Mass hysteria among the poorly catechized.* There was a form for everything. He had copied most of them.

But three days later it was wells.

The first was in Tanner's Row—a well that had been dry for eleven years, bricked over and forgotten, suddenly found brimming at dawn, so full it wept down its own stones. Then another on Salt Lane. Then two more on the same street, as if water were remembering where it used to live. The well-wardens came with their measuring rods and their official buckets and pronounced the aquifer "restored," which was the Choir's word for *we don't know*, and the people of Salt Lane drew water all day with a kind of careful joy, glancing over their shoulders between bucketfuls.

Edmund read the incident reports because reading them was his job. He tallied them because tallying was his job. And by the end of the second week, the column of figures in the margin of his working ledger had grown long enough that he caught himself staring at it the way you stare at a debt you know is coming due.

Fourteen answered prayers. Documented. Verified by wardens. Filed under *anomalous natural phenomena*.

Fifteen years ago, there had been none. Not one. That was the whole point of the Hollowing—the great rite, the great erasure, the reason the Choir existed and the reason Edmund owed it fifteen years of his life. The gods were gone. The gods were gone, and prayers went up like smoke into a sealed room, and nothing ever came back down. That was the bargain the country had made with itself: safety for silence. You could ask heaven for anything you liked, and heaven would keep its mouth shut, and everyone agreed to call that peace.

Now the silence had a crack in it, and things were coming through.

* * *

On the seventeenth day, a woman in the market pressed her hands to Edmund's sleeve without asking and said, "You're choir-badged, aren't you? Tell me it's true. Tell me the Widow Cressa's boy really walked."

He didn't know what she meant. He said so.

"The fever child," she said. "Up on Chandler's Hill. Dead two days—laid out, candles lit, the whole of it—and yesterday morning he sat up and asked for porridge." Her grip tightened. "The wardens came and took him to the infirmary. For observation." She said the last words carefully, the way you handle a knife by the blade. "Is that what happens now? When children come back?"

"I don't—" Edmund started, and then, because she was looking at him with the particular hunger of someone who has decided you are an authority: "I'll find out. I'll make inquiries."

She let go of his sleeve. She thanked him. She went back to her stall selling eels, and Edmund stood in the fish-smell and the crowd with his badge feeling heavier than it had that morning, and understood that he had just lied to her twice: once by promising, and once by the badge itself, which said *the Choir will handle this* in a language both of them had been raised to trust.

That night he pulled the incident reports again—all of them, back to the beginning of the season—and laid them out across his narrow desk in the scribes' dormitory like a hand of cards he'd been dealt against his will.

Bread. Wells. The fever child. A blind fiddler on Rope Walk who could see, according to six witnesses, though he himself refused to comment and had stopped playing in public. A field of cabbages behind the tannery that had come up through frozen ground in straight rows no one had planted. A prayer-scroll, sold legally by the Choir itself as a devotional exercise, that had returned to its purchaser bearing an answer written in a hand no cantor recognized.

Edmund sat very still and looked at the last one for a long time.

Prayer-scrolls didn't work. Everyone knew they didn't work; that was why they were safe. You wrote your petition, you burned it or buried it per the rubric, and the act of writing was supposed to be good for the soul regardless of outcome. It was liturgy as ledger-entry. Debit your longing, credit your discipline. He had copied out ten thousand of them. He had probably written the template the fiddler's scroll came from.

And now one had come back *answered*, in an unknown hand, and somewhere in the Hall of Reconciliation a committee was deciding whether that constituted a miracle (heretical), a fraud (criminal), or a clerical error (impossible).

He thought about the mother from the night market. He hadn't been able to stop thinking about her since—the way she'd held the knot-charm like it was already working, the way her son's fever had broken two nights later, clean and total, the kind of recovery that made physicians shrug and mothers kneel. He had told himself it was chance. Chance was the official explanation and chance was comfortable and he had worn chance like a coat for fifteen years.

But coats stop fitting.

He gathered the reports, squared their edges, and slid them back into the archive slot where they would be read by no one whose job it was to connect them. Connecting them was not his job. His job was ink and ledgers and keeping his head at exactly the height that attracted no attention, and he had gotten very good at his job, and he intended to go on being good at it until his debt was paid and he could be merely a man instead of a man owned by an institution.

Then he blew out his lamp, lay down in the dark, and did the thing he had not done in fifteen years, the thing the whole architecture of his life was built to prevent.

He prayed.

Just once. Just a test. Just a single sentence, silent, aimed at no one, the way you throw a stone down a well to hear how deep it goes:

*If anyone is there—I'm sorry. I don't remember how to talk to you.*

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. He was a scribe, not a saint; he was a debtor, not a door—

He fell asleep before he finished the thought, and dreamed of ash falling upward, and woke with his heart going like a hammer and no memory of why.

* * *

The Choir's response, when it came, was a masterpiece of the institution's oldest craft: saying nothing at enormous length.

High Cantor Verelund issued a Pastoral Directive, read aloud at every service in every district chapel, and Edmund sat in the congregation of Saint Lowmarsh on the appointed evening and listened to it roll over the pews like oil poured on water.

*"Beloved, be not troubled by rumors of wonders. The age of intervention is ended, sealed by the sacrifice of the Hollowing, and what remains is ours to steward: bread, water, work, and one another. Reports of answered petitions are under investigation. In the meantime, guard your hearts against the old hunger. What answers a prayer is not always what prayed-for hoped. The empty heaven is a safe heaven. The empty heaven loves you."*

*The empty heaven loves you.* Edmund turned the phrase over during the closing hymn and decided it was the most honest line the Choir had produced in a century, because it admitted, right there in the liturgy, that heaven was empty—that the love was unilateral, undeliverable, a letter sent to a demolished house. Most of the congregation sang the hymn with their eyes closed. A few did not close their eyes. He watched one of them, a tanner with burn-scarred forearms, watch the cantor on the dais with an expression of such naked, furious hope that Edmund had to look away.

After the service, notices went up on the chapel doors. *Report anomalous occurrences to your district warden. Do not attempt private devotion beyond prescribed forms. Do not accept unsolicited blessings.* That last one stopped Edmund mid-step. Unsolicited blessings. As if grace might ambush you in an alley, and the correct response was to decline it politely and notify the authorities.

Patrols doubled within the week. Wardens walked the night streets in pairs now, lanterns hooded, and their questions changed. They had always asked merchants about contraband charms—Edmund knew the standard questions intimately, having spent years arranging his own stall to survive them. Now they asked different ones. *Any unusual prosperity? Any illness reversing? Any dreams reported by multiple parties?* They had a new form for it. Edmund knew because he was assigned to copy it, four hundred times, for distribution to every district office.

He copied it. His pen moved; his mind ran ahead of it like a dog off its lead.

Because here was the thing he could not put in any ledger, the thing that sat in his chest like swallowed ice: some of those questions were questions *he* could answer. Unusual prosperity—he'd sold a luck-knot to a lamplighter in autumn and the man hadn't dropped a single glass since, which was either skill or something else. Illness reversing—the fever child, the mother's son. Dreams reported by multiple parties—Edmund had dreamed of a grey figure in a burned orchard twice this month, and he had told no one, and the fact that he needed to tell no one, that telling was unthinkable, was itself an answer to a question he hadn't known the patrols were asking.

They weren't hunting charms anymore. Charms were small game. Something in the machinery of the Choir had scented larger prey, and the doubled patrols, the new forms, the directive with its oily reassurances—they weren't denial. They were a net being quietly woven around a city, and everyone inside it was being measured against the mesh.

He finished the four hundredth copy at dusk. Brother Anselm collected the stack, flicked through it, and paused.

"You've got ink on your face," Anselm said. "Under your eye. Like a bruise."

"Long day."

"Mm." Anselm stacked the pages square. "You hear about the shrine on Grayfriars Steps? Little wayside niche, been empty since the Hollowing. Somebody's left fresh flowers in it. Every morning. Wardens cleared them out three times and by dawn there's more." He shook his head slowly. "Verelund says it's provocateurs. Says someone's doing it to stir unrest." He looked at Edmund with mild, terrible curiosity. "You believe that?"

"It's not my place to believe things," Edmund said. "I copy them."

Anselm laughed, took his papers, and went. Edmund sat alone in the scriptorium as the light failed, listening to the building settle around him—the tick of cooling stone, the far-off footsteps of wardens making their rounds through halls built to house a god that was no longer invited.

Provocateurs. Yes. Someone leaving flowers in the dark, night after night, knowing the wardens would come, knowing the flowers would return—someone patient enough to lose that fight daily and win it anyway. Either the most stubborn loyalist in Vessing, or someone who knew something the rest of the city was only beginning to suspect.

Or something that didn't need hands to carry flowers.

He cleaned his pen. He banked the scriptorium lamps. And as he pulled the door shut behind him, he noticed—small, easy to miss, the kind of detail a man trained in noticing details trains himself not to notice—that along the stone lintel above the archway, in a hairline seam that had been bare mortar that morning, a thread of pale growth had appeared overnight. Lichen, probably. Some spore off the river wind.

Except lichen didn't grow in a straight line.

Except the line, when he tilted his head, resolved into marks—tiny, deliberate, older than any script the Choir taught, the kind of marks you find on boundary stones and grave-goods in museums and nowhere else.

Edmund stood in the corridor a long moment, heart loud in his ears, and then he did the sensible thing, the survival thing, the thing fifteen years had drilled into his bones: he walked away without touching it, without reporting it, without letting his face change.

But that night, before sleep took him, he found himself whispering into the dark of his cell, testing the depth of the well again, unable to stop himself:

*What do you want from me?*

And the dark—patient, vast, attentive in a way darkness has no business being—seemed to lean closer, and somewhere across the sleeping city a bell tolled once, though no bell in Vessing rang at that hour, and in the morning there would be a summons on his desk in Maren Solle's precise cantor's hand, requesting a full audit of his ledgers, effective immediately.
