## 6. The Debt Collector

The rain came to Vessing the way debt did: gradually, then all at once.

Edmund heard it before he saw it—a soft percussion on the scriptorium's high windows, a sound like a thousand small fingers tapping for attention. He kept copying. The quill moved through the ledger's ruled lines with the mindless fluency of fifteen years: *one knot of protection, three coppers; one blessing against fever, five; one warding sigil for doorways, seven.* The Choir's prices never changed. That was the point of them. A faith that had outlawed its own gods could not afford mystery, so it sold certainty by the line, and Edmund Hartwell had spent half his life transcribing certainty in a hand so neat it looked printed.

He paused at the bottom of the page and did what he always did at the bottom of the page. He tallied.

*Remaining balance: 4,112 marks, 6 coppers.*

Fifteen years ago the number had been 5,000 even. His father's debts, inherited along with his father's funeral costs and his father's name, which the Choir had found useful—a family that had once held minor office in the old temple administration made for a scribe who asked fewer questions. Or so the recruiting cantor had said, sliding the contract across the desk like a plate of food Edmund was expected to be grateful for. *Sign here, boy. Ink is cheaper than blood.*

He had signed. He had been twenty-two and grieving and stupid with it, and he had thought, in the way the young think, that fifteen years was an ocean of time.

Now the ocean was nearly crossed and he stood on its far shore holding nothing but a tally.

"You've stopped writing," said Brother Anselm from the next desk, without looking up. Anselm was seventy, half-deaf, and had been one year from debt-freedom for the past six years. "When you stop writing, the whole room stops writing. It's uncanny."

"The rain," Edmund said. "It's distracting."

"It's rain." Anselm dipped his pen. "In my day we copied through hail."

Edmund almost smiled. Almost. Then the scriptorium door opened—not the ordinary creak of a clerk slipping in with fresh ink, but the heavier, deliberate swing of someone who wanted the room to know he'd arrived—and every pen in the place stopped at once, exactly as Anselm had predicted pens would.

Brother Quillon stood in the doorway with rain on his grey shoulders and let the silence do his introduction for him.

He was not a large man. That was the first thing everyone noticed and the last thing anyone remembered, because everything else about him was arranged to make size irrelevant—the stillness, the patience, the way his pale eyes moved across a room like a man reading a ledger and finding errors. He wore the enforcer's plain cassock, unmarked by rank, because Quillon did not need marks. Everyone knew what he was. In the fifteen years Edmund had served the Choir, he had seen Quillon perhaps a dozen times, and each time had felt the same cold arithmetic settle over him: *this is the man who decides which mistakes get forgiven and which get filed.*

"Brother Quillon." The head scribe, Dame Corvel, rose from her desk with the careful courtesy of a woman greeting weather she couldn't argue with. "We weren't told to expect an audit."

"I'm not auditing." Quillon's voice was quiet, dry, almost kind. "I'm collecting. Master Hartwell." Those pale eyes found Edmund across the room as if the intervening desks were glass. "A word. Somewhere with a door, if you please."

There was a small reading alcove off the scriptorium, walled on three sides, with a door of frosted glass that showed shadows but not faces. Edmund walked to it feeling every pen resume behind him like a heartbeat returning. He opened the door. Quillon entered first, which was also deliberate—enforcers never put their backs to a room they hadn't read.

Edmund closed the door. Through the frosted glass, the scriptorium became a smear of grey shapes and lamplight.

"You're wondering why you," Quillon said.

"I assumed it was about my ledger."

"Your ledger is clean. I checked this morning before you arrived. Four thousand one hundred twelve marks remaining, six coppers, no discrepancies in eleven years." Quillon took the alcove's single chair without being offered it, folding himself into it with the economy of a man who wasted no motion on anything, including sitting. "Eleven years is remarkable, Master Hartwell. Most scribes drift. Rounding errors. Lost pages. Little thefts of time. You don't drift. You are the straightest line in this building."

"Thank you," Edmund said, because there was no other answer that wasn't a question.

"Don't thank me yet." Quillon laced his fingers over his knee. Rain ticked against the alcove's narrow window. "Tell me what you know about the night market."

The cold went through Edmund so cleanly and so fast that he was briefly grateful for fifteen years of practice at standing still. *They know.* The thought arrived whole and terrible, and he set it down carefully inside himself the way he'd set down a full inkpot, without spilling, without looking at it directly.

"The night market?" he said. "On Candle Lane? I know where it is. Everyone does."

"And what's sold there."

"Bread. Fish. Secondhand coats. The usual things people sell when they can't afford to sleep."

Quillon watched him for a long moment. Outside the frosted glass, a shadow passed—Dame Corvel, moving her charges along, keeping heads bent. The Choir's scriptorium ran on the principle that whatever happened behind doors was nobody's business until it was everybody's.

"There have been reports," Quillon said at last. "Charm-sellers. Working charms, not the licensed blessings your office produces. Knots that hold. Sigils that don't fade. Prayers—" he paused on the word as if tasting something off, "—that get answered. The Office of Vigilance takes these reports seriously. Unlicensed theurgy is a corruption of the ledger, Master Hartwell. Every working charm sold outside our counters is a mark stolen from the debt you all carry. Do you understand the theology?"

"I understand the arithmetic," Edmund said.

"Good. Arithmetic is more honest." Quillon unfolded one hand and gestured, mildly, at the world beyond the glass. "Here is the service I'm offering. Your debt stands at four thousand marks. A scribe your age, working your wage, clears that in another eleven years. Eleven years, Hartwell. You'll be forty-eight. Half your life spent paying for a dead man's sins." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "But the debt can be paid in service. One verified report of an unlicensed seller—one name, one address, one conviction—retires two hundred marks. Two hundred. Ten names and you walk out of here free."

The number hung in the damp air between them. Two hundred marks per soul. Edmund turned it over and felt, beneath the fear, something harder begin to form—something with edges.

"That's generous," he said.

"It's practical. Informants who work for forgiveness inform better than informants who work for coin. Coin they spend. Forgiveness they hoard." Quillon's eyes didn't leave him. "You know the market. You're out in the city evenings—I've read your movement permits. You walk Candle Lane. You walk Tanner's Row. You stand in crowds, and crowds are where the sellers work, because sellers need crowds." A beat. "You must have seen something."

"I see bread," Edmund said. "I see coats."

"Master Hartwell."

"Brother Quillon." Edmund heard his own voice come out level, and was distantly surprised by it, the way you're surprised by calm water where you expected rapids. "I've spent fifteen years learning that the safest thing a man in debt can say is very little. But I'll say this much. If I had seen a charm-seller—if I knew a name, an address—I'd give it to you. Not for the marks. Because unlicensed workings are dangerous. People get hurt when magic is done badly." He paused, and the pause cost him something, and he paid it. "But I haven't seen any. And I won't invent some, because a false report retires my debt with a lie, and then I owe a different creditor entirely."

Quillon regarded him the way a jeweler regards a stone that has just declined to scratch.

"No," he agreed. "You wouldn't invent them. That's rather the problem with you, Hartwell. You're honest in the ways that are inconvenient and silent in the ways that are expensive." He rose from the chair, unhurried, smoothing his cassock. "So I'll leave the offer open. Indefinitely. Think of it as a standing order in your ledger. Two hundred marks, entered whenever you choose to claim them. There's no deadline. Debts like yours don't have deadlines. They have horizons."

At the frosted-glass door he stopped, one hand on the frame, and spoke without turning around.

"One more thing. Curiosity, nothing more. Your file notes a grandmother. Maternal line. Born Osselmark, before the Closure." The name of the vanished country dropped into the little alcove like a stone into a well, and Edmund heard it fall a long way before it landed. "Files note everything. Even things that shouldn't matter anymore, since Osselmark no longer exists. Funny, isn't it? We erased the country from every map we print, and still the files remember. Files are loyal that way." Now he turned, and his pale eyes were almost gentle. "Did she teach you anything? Songs. Stories. Old words."

"She died when I was nine," Edmund said. "I barely remember her voice."

"Mm." Quillon considered him. "That's a pity. Voices are the hardest thing to erase. Maps are easy." He opened the door onto the lamplight and the resumed scratching of forty pens. "Good evening, Master Hartwell. Keep your ledger straight. It's the only thing in this building that will keep you."

Then he was gone, his footsteps fading down the corridor with the unhurried rhythm of a man who had all the time in the world, because the debt did too.

* * *

Edmund did not go back to his desk. He told Dame Corvel he needed the privy, which was true in the sense that he needed somewhere small and dark to stand while his hands finished shaking.

The privy yard was cold and smelled of wet stone. Rain came down in earnest now, drumming the slate roof, running in bright threads off the eaves. Edmund gripped the yard's iron rail and breathed, and made himself look directly at the thought he had set down so carefully in the alcove.

*They know.*

No. Not that. Not yet. If they knew, there would have been no offer—there would have been a cell, a binding rite, the long quiet erasure the Choir performed on the ones it caught. Quillon's visit had been a fisherman's pass across still water, a lure dragged slowly, watching for the shadow beneath. The questions about the night market. The question about his grandmother. *Osselmark.* Why that, of all things? Fifteen years, and the Choir had never once mentioned his grandmother's name, and tonight Quillon had said it the way a man sets a second hook beside the first.

Unless the water itself had changed. Unless the reports were real—working knots, answered prayers—and the Choir was frightened enough to drag every pond in Vessing.

Edmund thought of the mother from the market, four nights ago. The sick boy. The knot he'd tied with hands that shook worse than these, whispering the words his grandmother had sung over him when he was small and feverish, words he'd told himself were only a lullaby, only memory, nothing. And the boy—alive. Sitting up. Asking for soup. The mother's face when she'd pressed the coins into Edmund's palm, that stunned, ruined gratitude, as if she'd watched the impossible happen in a rented room above a chandler's shop.

*I haven't seen any,* he had told Quillon. *And I won't invent some.*

It was even true, in the way Quillon would appreciate: technically, precisely true. Edmund had seen no charm-seller. He had only seen himself.

He laughed, once, quietly, and the laugh came out wrong—too close to a sob—so he swallowed it and stood in the rain and let the cold do its work. Fear, he discovered, was strange stuff. For fifteen years it had been water in him, taking the shape of whatever held it: the scriptorium's shape, the ledger's shape, the contract's shape. But tonight, under Quillon's pale eyes, it had begun to harden. To calcify. To become something with structure and weight, something that could bear load.

Because here was the arithmetic Quillon hadn't written down: *two hundred marks per name.* And Edmund knew a seller. Knew him intimately. Knew the tremor in his hands and the price of his knots and the exact words he whispered over the thread. He could retire his entire debt tomorrow with a single honest report.

He would die in this ledger first.

The thought didn't feel brave. It felt like setting a bone—clean, necessary, agonizing, final. Something in him had been dislocated for fifteen years, bent politely out of true to fit the Choir's ledgers, and Quillon's visit had knocked it back into place with one question about his grandmother. He stood at the rail and let the new alignment hurt.

*Files are loyal that way,* Quillon had said. *Maps are easy. Voices are the hardest thing to erase.*

And unbidden, unasked-for, rising out of fifteen years of careful forgetting, Edmund's grandmother's voice came back to him—low, cracked, singing in a language the maps no longer carried, a hymn about an orchard and ash and a door that opens outward. He hadn't thought of that song since he was nine years old. He had buried it so deep he'd convinced himself it wasn't there.

The rain fell. Somewhere across the city, in a hundred rooms he would never see, prayers were being answered, and the Choir was doubling its patrols, and Brother Quillon was filing his evening's observations in a hand as neat as Edmund's own.

Edmund pushed off the rail and went back inside, back to his desk, back to his pen. He wrote charm ledgers until the lamps burned low, in the straightest line in the building.

And when he finally climbed to his narrow room above the counting-house and blew out his candle, he did not notice—would not notice until morning—the fine grey dust settling on his windowsill, drifting down through the rainless dark from no cloud at all, arranging itself, grain by patient grain, into letters.

His name.

The ash spelled his name.
