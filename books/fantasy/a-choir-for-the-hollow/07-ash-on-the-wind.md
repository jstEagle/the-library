## 7. Ash on the Wind

The dream began, as it always did, with smoke.

Not the choking kind—the smoke of Vessing's tanneries that clung to wool and lungs alike—but a clean smoke, sweet almost, like cedar burned in a cold room. Edmund stood at the edge of an orchard he had never seen and yet knew the way you know your own hands. Rows of blackened trees stretched away into grey light, their branches raised like arms mid-surrender. The ground crunched under his feet with a sound like old paper.

And someone was singing.

The voice came from deep among the trees, thin as a thread pulled through cloth. It was not a hymn—he knew every hymn the Choir permitted, had copied out four hundred of them in his fifteen years of service, and this was none of them. The melody moved the way water moves under ice: visible, present, but going somewhere you couldn't follow. The words were in no language he'd studied, and yet his mouth shaped along with them, silent, as though his lips remembered what his mind did not.

He walked toward it. In dreams you walk toward singing; there is nothing else to do.

The figure stood between two dead apple trees—a shape of grey, not cloth-grey but ash-grey, the grey of something that had been burned down to its essential self and kept standing anyway. It had no face he could fix on. Every time his eyes tried to settle on features, they slid off, the way a thought slides when you reach for it upon waking. But he could see its hands. They were long and patient, folded in front of it, and they were moving slightly, keeping time.

*You know this song,* the figure said—or sang, or simply was understood—and Edmund felt the truth of it land in his chest like a stone dropped into a well. He did know it. He knew it from before the Choir, before the ledgers, before debt became the architecture of his days. He knew it from a kitchen with a low ceiling and a woman humming while she stirred something on the stove, her back to him, her voice threading through steam—

His grandmother. His grandmother's kitchen, in the rooms above the charm-shop she'd kept until the Choir bought the building out from under her, and she'd sung this song while her hands worked, and little Edmund, six years old, sitting on the floor with his wooden blocks, had hummed along without knowing what language it was, without asking, because children don't ask about the songs their grandmothers sing. They assume the world will always contain them.

*Say it back,* the grey figure said, and held out one long hand.

Edmund opened his mouth—

*

He woke with his heart slamming against his ribs and the taste of smoke in his throat, real smoke, or the memory of it, and for three full seconds he lay in his narrow bed in his narrow room above the scriptorium annex and stared at the ceiling beam he had memorized years ago, counting its knots the way other men counted sheep.

Then he smelled it again. Not memory-smoke. Actual smoke—faint, sweet, cedar-sweet.

He sat up.

The window was closed. He always kept it closed; the annex rooms faced the incinerator court where the Choir burned its spoiled charms and failed blessings, and the draft carried grit. But there, on the inside sill, on the flat painted wood where no wind could have left it, lay a fine dusting of grey ash. And in the ash, drawn by some finger too precise to be accidental, too deliberate to be anything but intentional:

**E D M U N D**

Seven letters. His name. Written in ash inside a locked room, on the wrong side of the glass.

Edmund sat very still. Fifteen years in the Hollow Choir teaches a man certain disciplines: how to keep his breathing even during audits, how to let his face go smooth as vellum when a superior asks a question he cannot afford to answer honestly. He used all of them now. He breathed. He counted the knots in the beam. He told himself the reasonable explanations—one per knot. Someone had climbed the wall. Someone had slipped powder under the sash. A trick, a test, Brother Quillon's quiet way of checking whether a debtor flinched.

None of the explanations survived contact with the letters themselves. The strokes were too even. No hand writing blind against a sill would produce an E with serifs so clean, a D so perfectly round. This was written by something that could see in the dark, or something that didn't need to see at all.

*You know this song.*

"Grandmother," Edmund said aloud, very quietly, to the empty room. "What did you bring home?"

The ash didn't answer. Ash never does. But the smell of cedar smoke hung in the room like a guest who has been invited nowhere and intends to stay.

He should sweep it away. That was the disciplined response, the ledger-keeper's response: remove the anomaly, record nothing, tell no one. If Quillon ever learned that a Choir-bound scribe woke to find his own name spelled in ash by no human hand, the questions would not stop for a decade. *Where were you born? Who was your mother? Who was her mother? Have you ever dreamed of fire? Do you dream of singing?*

Instead, Edmund did the most dangerous thing available to him.

He reached out and touched the ash.

It was cold. Colder than the sill, colder than the glass, cold the way a stone in shade is cold at dawn—as if it had never known warmth at all, as if warmth were a country it had been exiled from. It clung to his fingertip and did not smear. When he lifted his hand, the letter E remained perfect beneath the gap where his finger had been, and on the pad of his thumb sat a single grain of grey that gleamed faintly, like a seed.

He wiped it on the blanket and got up, and dressed, and did not look at the window again until he was leaving, and then he looked, because he was weak, because he was curious, because some door in him had swung open in the night and would not swing shut.

The name was still there. All seven letters. Patient as winter.

* * *

The scriptorium that morning smelled of ink and wet wool, forty scribes bent over their desks in the grey light from the clerestory windows, scratching out the week's charm-ledgers in the Choir's tight vertical hand. Edmund took his seat, uncapped his ink, ruled his margins, and discovered that his hands were not steady.

The first line came out crooked. He scraped it back with a knife and started over. The second line wavered on the descenders. He put the pen down.

Across the aisle, old Brother Fenwick glanced up. "Late night, Hartwell?"

"Bad sleep."

"The city's full of bad sleep lately." Fenwick lowered his voice, which among scribes meant gossip was coming. "Third well on Tanner's Row came back yesterday. Sweet water, they say. And the baker on Cordwainer Street swears her loaves rose overnight with no starter—rose twice, she says, once in the pan and once in her chest, if you follow me." He chuckled. "The Choir says it's gas in the aquifer. Gas in the aquifer making bread rise and wells fill. My aunt lived eighty years in this city and never once saw gas do a kindness."

"Hm," said Edmund, who was very good at saying *hm* in ways that ended conversations.

But Fenwick wasn't finished. "My wife lit a candle to Saint Vered last night—harmless folk practice, the Choir looks away—and this morning the candle hadn't burned down at all. Not a hair shorter. She's frightened of it. Frightened of a *kindness*, Hartwell." He shook his head. "Fifteen years I've copied the doctrine. *The gods are gone; the world is better ordered without them.* I believed it the way you believe the floor. Now the floor keeps creaking."

"The floor always creaks," Edmund said. "It doesn't mean anyone's walking on it."

"No," Fenwick agreed mildly. "But you listen harder, don't you. When you're alone at night." He returned to his ledger, and Edmund stared at his own blank page and thought about ash that wrote names, and songs in no living language, and a grandmother whose shop the Choir had bought and whose country the maps had lost, and he picked up his pen and made himself copy the morning's tally:

*Charm knots issued: 212. Blessings administered: 96. Failures: 0.*

Zero failures. Three weeks ago there had been failures every day, small ones, deniable ones. Then the mother in the night market with the sick boy, and the knot that worked impossibly well, and since then—nothing failed. Everything worked. The whole machinery of small mercies ran smooth as oiled gears, and Edmund had begun to understand, with a dread that sat in him like swallowed river-stone, that the machinery was running on something. That somewhere behind the walls of the world, something had turned its attention toward Vessing, and everything touched by that attention now functioned perfectly, the way a room functions when a very important person enters it and everyone stands a little straighter.

Even his own charms. Even him.

At midday the bell rang for the blessing hour, and the scribes filed into the chapel annex where Cantor Pellin, a round man with a voice like warm oil, performed the daily office over them. Edmund knelt with the rest. Pellin raised his hands. Pellin sang the opening line of the Great Hymn—the hymn sung to an empty heaven for fifteen years, the hymn whose beauty was supposed to be enough, whose beauty was the whole point, faith as craft rather than communion.

And the chapel went silent.

Not quiet—silent. The sound fell out of the air the way sound falls out of a room when a door closes. Pellin's mouth kept moving. His voice was gone—not hoarse, gone, swallowed, and the forty kneeling scribes heard nothing at all while his lips shaped the sacred syllables, and in that terrible padded silence Edmund heard, underneath everything, faint and far and unmistakable, a thread of melody moving the way water moves under ice.

The same song. The orchard song. Coming from everywhere and nowhere, from under the floor, from behind the walls, from inside his own ribs.

Pellin's voice returned mid-phrase, cracking, off-key, and he finished the office white-faced and fast, and dismissed them twenty minutes early, and no one spoke about it, because that is what institutions do instead of speaking.

Edmund walked back to his desk on legs that didn't feel entirely his own.

*You know this song,* the grey figure had said. *Say it back.*

That night he dreamed again.

* * *

This time the orchard was closer. Or he was deeper in it. The blackened trees stood around him at arm's length, and the crunching ground gave way here and there to bare soil that steamed faintly, as though the fire had passed through only recently, as though the burning were not an event but a condition, ongoing, perpetual. The grey light came from no sun. There was no shadow anywhere, and no direction.

The singing was louder. Close enough now that he could hear the structure of it: verses in that tongue he shouldn't know, and between them, refrains in words he *could* understand, plain words, worn words, words like *come home* and *the table is laid* and *you were small, you were loved, you forgot.* Each refrain landed in him like a hand pressed flat against a bruise he'd forgotten he had.

The figure stood at the orchard's heart, beside a tree that was different from the others. The others were charred skeletons. This one had bark, brown bark, living bark, on exactly one side of its trunk—the side facing the figure—as though everything the figure looked at remembered how to be alive.

"You," Edmund said. His dream-voice was steadier than his waking one. "You've been in my room."

*I have been in many rooms,* the figure said. Its voice was not loud. It was the voice of something that had waited so long that urgency had burned away and only patience remained. *Rooms, windowsills, wells, loaves of bread. I am not subtle, Edmund Hartwell. I am thorough.*

"Who are you?"

Something like amusement moved through the grey of it. *You know the doctrine. Say it.*

"The gods are gone," Edmund recited, the way he'd recited it since childhood, the way he'd copied it ten thousand times. "The world is better ordered without them."

*Better ordered.* The figure turned its head—it had a head, he realized, a suggestion of one, a shape where a head would be—and looked at the one living stripe of bark. *I taught your grandmother that prayer. Did you know that? Before the banishment, before the Choir, when Osselmark still existed and her name was spoken kindly. She prayed it in my hearing, gladly, and then her country was unwritten and her god was unmade, and she crossed the border with one child and one song and spent fifty years pretending the song was a lullaby.*

Edmund's throat was dry. In dreams your throat is never dry unless the dream wants it to be. "She never said. Not once. Fifty years and she never said."

*Because saying it aloud makes it true, and true things can be hunted. She was protecting you the only way left to her. With silence.* The figure's folded hands unfolded, slowly, and one long finger pointed—not at Edmund, but at his chest, at the space behind his breastbone. *And then you were seven years old, and your mother was dying, and you prayed. You prayed the only prayer you had, the one in the lullaby, the one you thought was nonsense-sounds. And something heard. Something answered. And the answer marked you, Edmund. Doors get marked.*

"I'm not a door," Edmund said. "I'm a scribe. I have a debt. I copy ledgers."

*Yes,* the figure agreed gently. *You are a scribe with a debt who copies ledgers, and last week a dying boy in the night market opened his eyes because a knot of string touched his fever, and you have been telling yourself since that it was coincidence, and you will keep telling yourself that right up until the moment you can't.* The finger withdrew. The grey shape seemed to gather itself, the way a listener gathers. *I am called Ashenveil. I was the god of that country that no longer exists. I am not asking you to worship me. Gods past a certain age stop wanting worship; it sits in us like stones. I want something smaller and much harder.*

"What?"

*Home,* said Ashenveil. *I want to go home. And the only doors out of this world are people, and nearly all the doors are sealed, and you, Edmund Hartwell, are one of the last unsealed hinges in a country that erased itself rather than be ruled by what came after. Your grandmother's silence bought you fifteen hidden years. The hiding is ending. The Choir will find you—they are already looking, they have already noticed that prayers are being answered and that answers need sources. When they find you, they will seal you, and sealing is not death, it is worse; it is being made into a wall. I offer you the alternative. Open the way. Come home to a country that exists elsewhere, whole and unburned, where your family's name is spoken kindly, where no one has ever heard of a debt.*

"And the price?" Edmund asked, because he was a ledger-keeper, because every entry has a debit column, because fifteen years in the Hollow Choir had taught him that nothing is offered without a tally. "There's always a price. What does it cost to open a door?"

For the first time, the grey figure hesitated.

*Ah,* it said softly. *You would ask that. Your grandmother asked it too.* It folded its hands again. *Sleep now. Ask me again when you are ready to hear it. The answer does not improve with waiting, but you will.*

The orchard dimmed like a lamp guttering. The last thing Edmund saw was the single stripe of living bark, and the last thing he heard, fading, was the refrain—*you were small, you were loved, you forgot*—

*

He woke before dawn with the taste of cedar smoke and his heart beating slow and heavy, not panicked this time but resolute, like a drum keeping marching time. The room was freezing. His breath fogged.

On the windowsill, the ash had changed.

EDMUND was gone. In its place, in the same precise, unhurried hand, new letters had been written, and this time they were not a name. They were a question, and the question was:

**W H A T   W I L L   Y O U   P A Y ?**

Edmund sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, watching the grey light come up over the incinerator court, watching the letters hold their shape, patient as winter. Somewhere across the city, a bell rang for prime, and forty scribes scratched out their tallies, and the Choir denied the wells and the bread and the candles, and Brother Quillon was, at that very moment, no doubt reading reports of answered prayers and building his patient nets.

And in a locked room above the scriptorium annex, a scribe with a fifteen-year debt looked at a question written in ash by a god, and did not sweep it away.

He found he already knew part of the answer. He just didn't know yet how much it would cost, or who else would pay it alongside him.

He dressed. He went down to work. Behind him, on the sill, the ash waited for the next chapter of his life to begin.
