## 9. A Country That No Longer Exists

The map room of the Hollow Choir occupied the lowest floor of the scriptorium, below even the charm ledgers, in a chamber where the air tasted of dust and old decisions. Edmund had been down there perhaps four times in fifteen years, always on errands—fetch a folio, return a folio, sign for a folio. The maps were kept under lock because the Choir believed geography was doctrine. What was not on the map had never been. What had been removed from the map had never existed.

He told himself he was there for an errand. He had signed out a folio of boundary disputes from the reign of Aldous III, which was true, and which gave him forty minutes alone among the shelves before Brother Lassiter came looking.

Forty minutes to find a country that did not exist.

* * *

He started with the atlases because it seemed logical. The great bound volumes stood along the north wall, their spines cracked, their brass corners green with age. He pulled the newest first—a survey commissioned thirty years ago, its pages smelling faintly of the vinegar used to treat the vellum—and turned to the plate showing the northern marches.

There was Vessing, rendered in careful ink, its walls a neat polygon, the river Vess curving through it like a signature. There were the eastern hills, the salt road, the border stones. And then, north of all of it, past the last labeled landmark—the Tollhouse at Grayford, drawn as a small square with a roof—there was nothing.

Not blank nothing. The cartographers had been too thorough for that. It was nothing with texture: hatched lines suggesting terrain no one had bothered to name, a few contour marks, and then, at the very top of the plate, where another country should have begun, a band of plain unmarked paper. The kind of emptiness that wasn't absence but erasure. You could see the ghost of it if you tilted the page against the lamplight—a palimpsest shadow where ink had been scraped away, the faint bruise of mountains that had once been drawn and then un-drawn.

Osselmark.

His grandmother's word for home. He'd been six when she died, maybe seven, and what he remembered of her was mostly hands: hands kneading bread, hands braiding thread into knots while she hummed, hands pressed flat over his ears during thunderstorms as she murmured words that weren't prayers, exactly, or weren't only prayers. She'd called the humming *the old songs*, and when his mother caught her at it she'd gone white and said, *Mother, please, not in front of the boy,* and his grandmother had stopped, and the songs had stopped, and two years later she was in the ground and the songs were nowhere at all.

Except in him. He hadn't known that until recently. He hadn't known anything until the night market, until the mother with the sick son, until ash spelled his name on a windowsill.

Edmund set the atlas down and pulled the next one, older, and the next, working backward through the decades like a man digging toward a root. In each successive volume the northern territory shrank. Thirty years ago: hatched nothing. Fifty years ago: a region labeled only *THE NORTHERN WASTE — UNSETTLED*. Eighty years ago: *OSSELMARK (DISPUTED)*, with borders drawn in dotted line, the cartographic equivalent of a cough. And in the oldest atlas, one so old its plates were hand-colored and its paper soft as cloth, there it was entire: OSSELMARK, in letters tall enough to read across a room, with cities named—Vaelmoor, Kessing-holt, the Grayford crossing itself on the Ossel side—and rivers, and ranges, and a coastline he had never once heard mentioned in fifteen years of copying documents about the world.

A whole country. A hundred thousand lives, languages, lullabies. Scraped off the page like wax off a tablet.

And underneath the name, in smaller type, a note in the old orthography: *Herein the gods walked openly; herein they were unmade.*

* * *

"Looking for something, scribe?"

Brother Lassiter stood at the end of the aisle, keys jangling at his belt, his face arranged in the mild expression of a man who suspected everything and would settle for being disappointed.

"Boundary disputes," Edmund said, lifting the folio he'd actually signed for. "Aldous the Third. The Grayford tolls."

"Grayford." Lassiter's eyes flicked north, toward the wall of atlases, then back. "Long way to argue about a tollhouse."

"People have argued about less."

"They have." Lassiter didn't move. He was sixty, maybe older, and he had spent those years among these shelves, and Edmund understood suddenly that this room was Lassiter's parish, and every book in it a congregant, and Edmund a stranger handling them without washing his hands. "You know what's strange about the northern waste, Hartwell? I've catalogued every volume in this room twice. Do you know how many maps show anything north of Grayford?"

"I couldn't say."

"One." Lassiter smiled without warmth. "One map. Sealed archive, sub-vault, restricted to cantors and above. Everything else got scraped. You know why we scrape rather than burn?"

Edmund said nothing.

"Burning leaves ash," Lassiter said. "Ash can be read. Scraping leaves a wound in the page, and wounds heal over clean—if you keep them clean." He held out his hand for the folio. "Your forty minutes are up, scribe."

* * *

That night Edmund did not go to the market. He sat in his narrow room above the chandler's shop with a single candle and tried to remember his grandmother's voice, and found he could not—not her voice itself, only the shape of the things she'd said. This frightened him more than Quillon's visits, more than the ash on the sill. A debt could be worked off. A voice, once gone, was gone.

So instead he did what he knew how to do. He wrote.

He took a fresh quire of cheap paper and began a list, because lists were honest things, and because his whole life had been ledgers and he trusted them more than he trusted memory. At the top he wrote: *What I know of Osselmark.* Beneath it:

*My grandmother's name was Sela Hartwell, born Sela Voss. She came south before my mother was born. She never said why.*

*She braided charms the way I braid them now. I thought I learned the craft from her. I think now she learned it from a country.*

*She sang three songs. I remember none of the words. I remember the tune of one.*

*She said, once, when I asked where she was from: "North of the tollhouse, past the wheat, there is an orchard. Ask me again when you're older."*

*I never asked again.*

He stared at the list a long time. Then, beneath it, in smaller letters, as though writing quietly might keep the fact from hearing itself:

*The Choir erased a country. My grandmother was that country. I am what's left of it, and I sold knots in a night market for fifteen years pretending I was nobody's heir.*

* * *

Sleep did not come, so he went walking.

Vessing at the third hour was a city holding its breath. Since the wells had refilled—since the bread had risen unprompted in three bakeries on Chandler's Row, since the widow on Tanner's Lane swore her dead husband's voice had sung her awake—the patrols had doubled, and the curfew bells rang earlier each night, and still the city muttered. He passed a shrine alcove where someone had left fresh bread on the empty god's shelf, an offering to a name that had been outlawed for eighty years. The bread was already gone. Someone had eaten it, or something had.

He walked north without deciding to. His feet knew the way his mind refused: up the Vess road, past the tanneries, along the pilgrim path that climbed toward the Grayford crossing and then simply stopped being maintained, as though the road itself had agreed to forget. He walked until the city walls were behind him and the wheat fields opened on either side, silver-grey under a half moon, and he stood on the last mile of a road that officially went nowhere, and looked north.

There was nothing to see. Hills, dark. Sky, enormous. Somewhere beyond the horizon line, a country that had been scraped off the world's memory, and somewhere inside that country, an orchard his grandmother had promised to tell him about when he was older, and he had never asked, and now no one alive could answer.

Grief arrived without drama. It simply sat down in his chest, quiet as a tenant, and began unpacking. Not grief for the dead—he had made his peace with the dead long ago, or told himself he had—but grief for the inheritance he'd never claimed. For the songs he'd half-learned and let fade. For the language of knots his grandmother had braided and he had copied without understanding, selling them for coppers to people afraid of the dark, when they had been meant for something so much larger than fear.

He had spent fifteen years keeping his head down, and the cost of that, he saw now, standing in the wheat with the moon climbing, was not safety. The cost was this: he was the last speaker of a country, and he had let himself be illiterate in it.

"You should have asked me again," he said aloud, to no one, to her. "I'm older now."

The wind moved through the wheat, and for a moment—it might have been the wind, it was probably the wind—the stalks bent in a pattern too regular, a ripple that traveled against the breeze, northward, like a finger pointing home.

* * *

He was back inside the walls by dawn, having slept two hours in a hay barn and told himself the walk had cleared his head, which was a lie, and signed in at the scriptorium, which was also a lie, in the sense that he signed his own name to a day of work he no longer believed in.

Midmorning, Maren found him at his desk.

She set a cup of tea beside his elbow without a word—their old ritual, tea as conversation when conversation was dangerous—and stood looking at the northern window while he drank. When she finally spoke, her voice was low enough that only he could hear it.

"The archive page you've been asking after," she said. "The sealed one. Sub-vault."

Edmund's cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"I haven't—"

"Don't." She still wasn't looking at him. "Lassiter reported the inquiry. Standard procedure. What isn't standard procedure is that the report crossed my desk for countersignature, and I countersigned it *lost in transit*." Now she turned, and her face was the composed cantor mask he knew so well, except for her eyes, which were doing something he had never seen them do. "Edmund. Whatever you're looking for north of Grayford. Whatever your grandmother—" She stopped herself. Closed her eyes briefly. Opened them. "I can't help you. I want you to understand that. I am not able to help you. But I need you to tell me one thing, and I need you to tell me true."

"Ask."

"Are you in danger?"

He looked at her—at the woman who audited his ledgers and covered his discrepancies and brought him tea in a room full of people who would burn both of them for less than what passed between them unspoken—and he felt the grief in his chest shift, make room, take on a lodger.

"Not yet," he said.

Maren nodded slowly, as if filing the answer somewhere she could retrieve it later. "Then don't be," she said, and took the empty cup, and left him with his pen uncapped and his list unfinished and the certain knowledge that whatever came next, it would come for both of them.

That afternoon, copying charm ledgers, Edmund wrote the same wrong figure four times in a row, and each time he caught it, and each time he thought: *north of the tollhouse, past the wheat, there is an orchard.*

And each time, beneath the thought, quieter, patient as winter: *come and see.*
