## 10. Quillon's Net

Brother Quillon had a theory about lies, and it was this: a lie was a knot. You didn't cut it out. You pulled the right thread and watched the whole thing come apart in your hands.

He was thinking about knots on the morning he sat across from a dying woman in the Saltrow tenements, holding a charm that worked.

The charm was nothing to look at—a length of red cord, knotted seven times, frayed at one end where someone had worried it with their teeth. It hung from the neck of a boy of perhaps six who lay sweating through a fever that should have killed him three days ago. The fever had broken instead. The physician Quillon had brought along, a Choir man named Aldous with soft hands and softer convictions, kept saying *remarkable* in the tone other men said *impossible*.

"Who gave you this?" Quillon asked the mother for the third time.

She was a laundress named Petra Voss, or so she'd claimed, and she was lying, but her lie was badly tied. "A peddler," she said. "I don't remember."

"You remembered enough to buy from him."

"I was frightened. My boy was dying. You don't remember faces when you're frightened." Her hands were folded so tight the knuckles had gone white. She was looking past him at the wall, which meant she wasn't looking anywhere near the truth, which meant the truth was somewhere behind her eyes, close and hot.

Quillon turned the cord over in his fingers. He did not touch the knots directly—he wore gloves for that, thin ones, oiled against residue—but he held it up to the grey light from the window and studied the workmanship. Seven knots. Even spacing. A tension pattern that spoke of hands that had made thousands of these, hands that no longer thought about what they were doing.

Amateur charms failed. That was the whole architecture of his life's work: the little magics people bought in dark corners were mostly rope and superstition, and when they seemed to work, they worked because fevers broke on their own and grief needed somewhere to put its money. In nineteen years as an enforcer of the Doctrine of Silence, Brother Quillon had confiscated four thousand two hundred and eleven illicit charms, and exactly none of them had done anything a strong belief couldn't have done alone.

This one had done something. The boy's fever hadn't broken. It had been *taken*—drawn down the knots and out through the frayed end like water through a wick. Aldous had found the residue still cooling in the cord, faint as breath on glass, and gone quiet in a way that frightened Quillon more than any noise could have.

Residue meant source. Residue meant that somewhere in Vessing, magic was not merely being sold. It was being *made*.

And after fifteen years of silence—after the gods were banished and the doors sealed and the whole country taught to pray into a pillow—magic being made meant only one thing, if you believed the oldest pages of the archive, the ones most cantors never saw: somewhere, a door had cracked open.

*Patience*, Quillon told himself. He set the charm back on the boy's chest, gently, because taking it would tell whoever made it that the net was closing, and Quillon did not want the fish warned. "Leave it," he told Aldous. "It's spent anyway. Its work is done."

"Its work is done," Aldous repeated, and wrote it down, because Aldous wrote everything down, which was why Quillon tolerated him.

At the door, Petra Voss caught his sleeve. "You won't take him? The boy? They said—the others said the Choir takes children who've been—"

"The Choir takes no one tonight." Quillon removed her hand with two fingers, courteously, the way you'd lift a moth off a page. "But you'll want to think carefully before you speak of the peddler again. To anyone. Because next time I knock on this door, I'll be asking different questions, and I'll already know the answers."

Her face went through fear and arrived somewhere beyond it, at the flat country of the desperate, where there was nothing left to lose and therefore nothing to protect. "He'll come back Thursday," she said. "They always come back Thursday. The night market, Saltrow Bridge, under the third lamp. He sells the good luck knots. Everyone knows him."

Everyone knew him, then. And yet no one had reported him, which was its own kind of information. In a city where the Choir paid well for names, a magic-seller known to everyone and named by no one was either beloved or protected, and both possibilities interested Quillon enormously.

He thanked her, and left, and stood a long while on the tenement stairs smelling of lye and sickroom steam, feeling the old patience settle over him like a coat he'd tailored to fit.

The impatient enforcers burned out young. They wanted confessions by Friday, arrests by Sunday, promotions by quarter's end. Quillon had learned early that the Doctrine rewarded a different virtue entirely. The Choir had spent fifteen years teaching a nation to be quiet; it could afford men who knew how to wait.

* * *

Thursday came gray and cold, with a wind off the river that smelled of coming frost. Quillon dressed down for it—wool coat, no collar, boots scuffed at the toe. He had a face that helped: broad, forgettable, the face of a man you'd trust with your cart for an hour and never think of again. He took up a position at the mouth of Saltrow Lane with a cup of cider he had no intention of drinking, and he watched the bridge.

The night market assembled itself the way rot spreads: slowly, then all at once. First the lamp-lighters, then the pie carts, then the fortune-teller with her painted wagon, then the small dark commerce that lived between the stalls like rats between walls—fence goods, false papers, love philters, and, if you asked the right wrong person, worse.

Under the third lamp, at half past nine, a man set out a folding table and a lantern and began arranging cords.

Quillon watched him for two hours before he let himself believe it.

Edmund Hartwell. Scribe, second class, scriptorium of the Hollow Choir. Fifteen years on the rolls, debt-credentialed, punctual, unremarkable. Quillon knew him the way a fisherman knows every stone in his favorite pool—not personally, but professionally. Hartwell appeared twice a year in the enforcement ledgers, always in the margins: present at a raid, questioned and released, seen speaking briefly with this or that minor offender and never once implicated. Fifteen years of sitting at the edge of other people's crimes without ever falling in. Most men couldn't do that once. Hartwell had made it a career.

And now here he was, under the third lamp, selling knots to the poor.

Quillon moved closer by degrees, the way fog moves, unhurried, arriving everywhere at once eventually. He drifted along the stall line, pausing at a knife-grinder's cart, a fruit stand, letting the crowd carry him. From six feet away he could see the workmanship on the cords—same tension, same even knots, same hands. His maker. Fifteen years of harmless trinkets, and now, suddenly, charms that ate fevers.

Something had changed. Something had *woken*.

Hartwell himself looked like a man running a fever in reverse. There was a brightness under his skin that hadn't been in the enforcement sketches, a quickness to his movements, and—Quillon noted this with professional pleasure—a new habit: he flinched when anyone said the word *blessed*. He flinched when a customer thanked the knots instead of him. He flinched, once, at a bell ringing in some distant steeple, though the bells rang every hour of every day.

A guilty man, then. Or rather: a man newly guilty. Fifteen years of small sins had been a kind of innocence, and whatever he'd done to make the Voss boy's charm work had ended it, and he knew it had ended it, and part of him was waiting for the knock on the door.

Good. Frightened men ran, and running men left tracks. But better still: frightened men talked, if you got them alone and spoke softly enough.

Quillon bought a luck knot.

He counted out the coppers himself, met Hartwell's eyes, and held the gaze one beat longer than commerce required. Hartwell's hands did not shake. His smile did not slip. But something behind his face leaned away, the way a candle leans when a door opens elsewhere in the house.

"Pleasant evening for it," Quillon said pleasantly.

"They say the weather turns tomorrow," Hartwell said. A seller's patter, empty as a drum. Both of them knew exactly what they were doing, and neither of them blinked first, and the knot changed hands between them like a duel conducted with string.

Quillon walked away with the charm in his pocket and his heart beating slow and deep, the way it did when the net was nearly woven.

* * *

In his rooms above the enforcement office, Quillon laid out everything he had on Edmund Hartwell, which was everything the files held plus fifteen years of his own marginalia, and read it again from the beginning like a man rereading a letter for what it doesn't say.

Born Vessing, district unstated. Mother deceased—records thin there, a gap in the parish rolls that Quillon had circled years ago and never resolved. Father unknown. Indebted to the Choir at age eleven—unusual, very unusual, debts were contracted by adults or inherited by families, and a child indentured alone was a paperwork anomaly that someone had signed off on and someone else had chosen not to examine. Educated in the scriptorium. Competent. Quiet. No vices on record, no friendships flagged, no disciplinary entries beyond two trivial ink allowances.

Fifteen years, and the file read like the biography of a chair.

That was the thing about chairs: somebody built them. Somewhere behind this blankness there was a story with blood in it, and Quillon had begun, in the last three days, to suspect he knew which blood. He had pulled the old archive request he'd filed and shelved twice—*inquiry: pre-Banishment door registries, Osselmark holdings*—and each time the response had come back the same: *redacted, pending cantor review*. Pending for fifteen years. Someone senior was sitting on those pages, and senior people did not sit on pages unless the pages were load-bearing.

He thought about the word the old texts used. Not sorcerer, not vessel. *Door*. A thing built into the wall of the world, through which the banished could come home. The Doctrine of Silence had sealed the doors a generation ago—or claimed to—and the Choir's founding lie, the one Quillon had carried like a stone in his boot his whole career, was that all the doors were closed forever. If one had reopened—if one had never properly closed—

Then everything Quillon had done for nineteen years was maintenance work on a dam with a crack in it, and the crack had a name and a folding table under the third lamp of Saltrow Bridge.

He could arrest him tonight. That was the honest truth of it, and Quillon made himself look at it squarely, the way he looked at everything: one writ, four wardens, done before matins. The charge was trivially provable—illegal thaumaturgic sale, the cord itself evidence enough. Hartwell would go to the sealing cells, and whatever he was, door or not, they'd pour the nullity into him and scrape him smooth, and the city would stay quiet, and Brother Quillon would receive a commendation and keep working until his knees gave out.

And the crack in the dam would remain. Because sealing a door did not close it—it had never closed them; that was the secret under the secret, the thing the oldest cantors had whispered and the newest ones denied. Sealing a door just stopped it up with a person. The door stayed. The door waited. And someday the seal thinned, or died, or was born again in someone's bloodline, because bloodlines were stubborn things, and the whole ugly cycle began anew.

Unless you proved it first. Unless you traced the lineage, documented the awakening, mapped the network of the awakened—because there would be a network, there was always a network, the faithful gathered around their miracles like cold hands around a fire. Unless you delivered to the High Cantor not one sealed scribe but the *proof of doctrine*: that the doors live, that they reopen, that the Banishment was a bandage over a wound the Choir never actually treated.

Then they might finally do it properly. Then they might close them for good.

That was the prize. That was worth one more week of knots.

Quillon banked his fire, checked the oil in his lamp, and wrote in his private ledger, in the shorthand only he could read:

*Hartwell, E. Confirmed source. Awakening recent — est. <1 mo. Motive unclear. Protection unclear (market silence). Do NOT arrest. Observe contacts. Note: speaks with cantor Solle, M., quarterly audit schedule — significance TBD.*

Maren Solle. He'd been turning that thread over too. A rising cantor, precise, ambitious in the way the Choir rewarded—quietly, without visible appetite. She audited Hartwell's debt ledger four times a year, more often than protocol required. Their tea meetings were logged in the scriptorium social register, which existed because the Choir spied on its own staff with the same diligence it applied to heretics, and the register noted a frequency of visits that had no bureaucratic justification whatsoever.

Cantors did not waste hours on scribes they didn't need. Either Solle was investigating him—which would be useful, and Quillon could simply wait for her to find things—or she was attached to him, which would be more useful still, because attached people lied for each other, and a lie told by a cantor was leverage no writ could match.

Either way: watch the woman, and the man's secrets would walk themselves into the light holding her hand.

Quillon blew out the lamp and stood at his window a while, looking down at the sleeping street. Frost was forming on the rooftops, silvering the world, and somewhere out there under the third lamp the market was packing up, and a scribe with bright skin and frightened eyes was folding his table and carrying his small illegal mercies home through the dark.

*Soon*, Quillon promised him silently, with real warmth, almost affection. He had liked Hartwell at the stall, in the way one likes any adversary who stands up straight. *Not tonight. Not this week. I'm going to let you show me everything you are first—every name you protect, every prayer you answer, every door you are. And then I'm going to close you.*

Below, a church bell rang the hour, and the sound fell flat on the frozen air, unanswered, as it had for fifteen years.

Almost, Quillon thought, unanswered.

Almost.
