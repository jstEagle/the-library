## 12. Confession at Vespers

Vespers began, as it always began, with the hymn to no one.

Maren Solle stood in the third row of the Cantors' Gallery with two hundred voices around her, singing *The Hollow Anthem* into the vaulted dark above the nave. The acoustics of the Great Choir were engineered to perfection—every note rising, multiplying, filling the dome until the silence at the top of it seemed less like absence and more like attention. That was the trick of it. That had always been the trick of it. The architects had built a room that made emptiness feel like listening, and for fifteen years Maren had let it work on her the way it worked on everyone.

Tonight it didn't. Tonight she heard the gap at the top of the dome for exactly what it was: a gap. The hymn rose and broke against it and came back down as echo, nothing more.

She sang anyway. Her voice was trained too well to do otherwise. Beside her, Cantor Ivo Dellet leaned into the descant with his eyes closed and his face slack with devotion, and Maren thought: *he hears something. He has always heard something. What is wrong with me that I hear nothing?*

Or—worse, the thought arriving like a splinter driven under the nail—*what is wrong with all of us that we have learned to call this enough?*

The hymn ended. The congregation filed out into the lamplit streets of Vessing, where bread was rising unprompted in bakery windows and dead wells were filling with sweet water, and the Choir's official position—posted on every notice board in the city—was that these were *coincidences, anomalies, and in no wise miracles*, and that any citizen repeating claims to the contrary would be subject to review.

Maren stayed behind. She had told the verger she needed to re-copy a page of the evening rite, and he had believed her, because Maren Solle did not lie. That was the reputation she had spent a decade building, stone by careful stone, and tonight she was going to spend it.

The High Cantor's office was at the top of the Bell Tower, up a spiral stair that smelled of tallow and cold stone. His name was Aldous Verrek. He was sixty-one years old, thin as a candle, with the pale, unlined face of a man who had never once raised his voice, and he had run the Hollow Choir of Vessing for twenty-two years with the gentle, total efficiency of a man sorting paper.

He was sorting paper when she knocked. He did not look up.

"Cantor Solle." His voice was soft. It always was. "You're late for your own doubt."

Maren's stomach dropped through the floor. "Excellency—"

"Sit." He set down his pen and finally regarded her, and his eyes were kind, which was the most frightening thing about him. "You've been standing outside my door for eleven minutes. I could hear you deciding. You decide the way you sing—very precisely, with a great deal of breath control."

She sat. The chair was hard and straight-backed, built for penitents.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," Verrek agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That's the trouble with doubt. It never knows what it means. It only knows what it wants, which is to be confessed." He folded his hands on the desk. "So. Confess."

The word opened something in her that she had been holding shut with both hands for six days—since the archive, since the redacted page, since the child's name *E. Hartwell* had surfaced under her cantor's rite like a body rising in a pond.

She had meant to come in with a controlled confession. A small one. Doubts about the answered prayers, she had planned to say. Questions about the Choir's response. A loyal servant requesting clarification. She had rehearsed it on the stair, word by word, the way she rehearsed everything.

What came out instead was: "I think we're lying to them."

The lamplight flickered. Verrek said nothing, which was his way of giving you rope, and Maren, who had watched him do this to a dozen lesser cantors, took the rope anyway.

"The bread. The wells. The woman on Candle Street whose fever broke in an hour. The Choir calls them anomalies. We've posted denials in six districts and doubled the patrols in four, and the patrols are arresting people for *praying*, Excellency. For praying in their own homes." Her voice was shaking and she hated it. "I have served the Choir since I was nine years old. I learned the rites. I learned the silences. I was taught that the gods were dangerous and the silence was mercy, and I believed it, I *believed* it the way you believe the floor will hold you. And now prayers are being answered all over my city and we are telling people it isn't happening, and I stood at Vespers tonight and sang the Anthem and for the first time in my life I heard the hole at the top of it."

"Beautifully put," said Verrek.

"Excellency—"

"You think I haven't heard the hole?" He rose, unhurried, and went to the window. Below, Vessing lay in its grid of lamplight, ten thousand hearths, ten thousand households where someone was whispering to the dark and the dark was, apparently, whispering back. "I have heard it for forty years, Cantor Solle. I have sung into it longer than you have been alive. You are not telling me anything I do not know. You are telling me something you have only just learned, and you have the poor manners to present it as a discovery."

Maren's hands had gone cold. "Then why—"

"Because the lie is the mercy." He turned back to her. "You were taught that, and you believed it, and now you don't, and you want me to tell you which of us is right. I can't. I can only tell you what I know, which is this: the last time the gods had open access to this world, the country of Osselmark stopped existing. Not fell. Not was conquered. *Stopped*. Every map redrawn. Every record burned by its own priests, who understood, at the very end, what they had invited in. Two hundred thousand people, Cantor Solle, unmade so thoroughly that their own grandchildren cannot name them. That is what an answered prayer looks like at scale."

"The people in Vessing aren't asking for that. They're asking for bread."

"They were asking for bread in Osselmark too." Verrek came back to his desk and sat, and folded his hands again, and looked at her with that terrible kindness. "You didn't come here to argue theology. You came here because you found something in the archive."

The room went very still.

"I don't know what you—"

"Page one-one-four of the Sealed Register," he said mildly. "Accessed six days ago under your cantor's seal. A page that has not been opened in fifteen years. Do you know how I know? Because I sealed it. And because the archive lights a candle in this room whenever a seal is lifted, and I have been watching that candle for six days, waiting to see whether you would come to me or whether I would have to come to you." He smiled, faintly. "I'm glad it was you."

Maren's heart was a fist in her chest. Every exit she mapped out of this conversation led somewhere worse than where she sat.

"You read the page," Verrek said. It was not a question.

"Yes."

"Then you know the word *doors*."

"I know the word," she said carefully. "I know it was a classification. I know one entry was redacted."

"Redacted by me, fifteen years ago, on the night of the Purge of the Choirs—the last time anything answered, before now. Do you want to know what was under the redaction?"

*E. Hartwell.* The name sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She thought of Edmund's hands copying ledgers, Edmund's careful, self-erasing neatness, Edmund's face when she'd asked him about the discrepancy in his charm sales and he'd looked at her with those tired honest eyes and told her a truth that was too small to be the whole truth.

"No," she said.

Verrek studied her for a long moment. The candle on his desk burned without flickering, and Maren understood, with a lurch, that the candle was still lit from her archive visit—that it would burn until she confessed fully or until it burned down, and that Verrek would sit here all night either way, patient as paper.

"Cantor Solle," he said gently, "I am going to tell you something that very few living people know, and I am going to tell it to you because I believe you are one of the finest cantors this Choir has produced, and because I believe that what you are feeling right now—this crack, this cold—is not doubt. It is fear. And fear, properly directed, is the beginning of wisdom."

He leaned forward.

"The doors were never a myth. They were a lineage. Certain bloodlines, thinning over generations, through which the banished could return if the conditions were right—and the conditions, we have learned, are *belief under pressure*. A city full of people praying hard enough. The Choir has spent fifteen years doing two things, Cantor Solle. We have been keeping the silence. And we have been finding the doors."

"Finding them," Maren repeated. Her mouth was dry. "And then what?"

"What do you think we do with a door, Cantor? We seal it. There is a rite. It is not pleasant for the door, but it is painless in the way that sleep is painless, and it is infinitely kinder than what comes through otherwise." He said it the way he signed requisition forms. "We have sealed eleven doors in fifteen years. Quietly. Without trials, without spectacle, because the last thing a frightened city needs is to learn that the gods have addresses."

Eleven. Eleven people. Eleven lives folded shut like ledgers, and the Choir had sung Vespers every evening as though nothing were owed to anyone.

"And the redacted name," Maren said. Her voice came out level. She would always be proud of that, later—that her voice came out level. "The twelfth."

"Was a child," Verrek said. "Seven years old when we identified the lineage. Too young to seal—we don't seal children; the rite requires a self to erase, and a child's self is still being written. So we did something more delicate. We placed the child under observation. We arranged for the child to be bound to the Choir by debt, so that its life would be spent in plain sight, under our eyes, doing harmless work." He tilted his head. "You've met him, I think. You audit his ledgers."

The stone in her chest cracked clean through.

Edmund. Fifteen years of ledgers. Fifteen years of harmless charms and careful handwriting and a debt that never shrank, and she had sat across from him a hundred times, had drunk his tea, had let her hand rest near his on the table and called it restraint—and the whole time the Choir had known. The whole time his small gray life had been a cage with her assigned as one of the bars.

"You've been watching him," she said. "All this time."

"We've been *keeping* him," Verrek corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is that he is alive and unsealed and free to sell his little knots in the night market, which yes, Cantor, we know about, and have always known about, because a door who believes he is getting away with something is a door who stays predictable." He folded his hands. "But the conditions have changed. The city is praying. The residue is rising. And I am an old man who would very much like to close the last account on my desk before I die."

Maren stood up. She didn't remember deciding to.

"You're asking me to seal him."

"I'm asking you to *test* him." Verrek's voice remained soft, but something underneath it shifted—she heard it the way she heard the hole in the dome, a structural note. "There is a rite. The Rite of the Clear Mirror. A binding rite—old, gentle, and precise. It doesn't harm. It looks. It passes through a person the way light passes through water, and if there is a door in them, the Mirror shows it. I am asking you to perform it, because he trusts you, and because the rite requires a hand the subject does not flinch from." He paused. "And because if you don't do it, I will give it to Brother Quillon, and Quillon's version of looking is not gentle, and Quillon's version of finding is an arrest, and a trial, and a sealing done in a cell instead of a chapel. You have met Quillon. You know I am not exaggerating."

She did know. Everyone knew.

"You're giving me a choice between betraying him gently and betraying him badly," Maren said, "and calling it a choice."

"I'm giving you the only choice there is." For a moment—just a moment—the kindness dropped out of Verrek's face, and what was underneath was very old and very tired and had been sorting paper for twenty-two years. "I have sealed eleven doors, Cantor Solle. Do you know what they all had in common? Every one of them, at the end, thanked us. Not because the rite is kind. Because what waits on the other side of a door is worse than erasure, and they had all, by the end, heard it knocking." He looked at her. "The redacted name is not a person to me. It is a fuse. And this city is full of matches. I will not watch Osselmark happen twice."

Maren stood in the lamplight with her whole life rearranging itself around her. Nine years old, in the novitiate, learning the first hymn. Nineteen, taking the gray cord. Twenty-six, assigned to audit the charm ledgers, and a scribe with ink-stained fingers offering her tea and saying, *you look like someone who carries too much, Cantor*—the first person in the Choir who had ever seen her instead of her office.

*He trusts you.*

Yes. That was the blade of it, and Verrek had placed it precisely, the way he did everything.

"If I do this," she said, and her voice was steady, God help her, it was steady, "and the Mirror finds nothing—then he's clean, and this is finished?"

"Then he's clean," Verrek said, "and I will close the account, and you will never speak of this room again."

"And if it finds something?"

Verrek looked at her for a long, long moment. The candle between them had burned down to a thumb's width of white.

"Then you will have saved his soul," he said, "the only way it can still be saved. And you will report to me, and we will do what must be done, and you will sing Vespers on Sunday like every other cantor in this city." He picked up his pen. "You have three days, Cantor Solle. The residue is rising faster than my projections. Say yes."

The word sat in her mouth. She tasted it. She thought of Edmund's face across the tea table, the exact angle of his tired smile. She thought of the child's name under the redaction, and the seven-year-old who had been given a cage instead of a childhood, and she thought—she made herself think—of Osselmark, of a country unmade so completely that its own grandchildren could not mourn it.

She thought of ten thousand hearths in the lamplit dark below the window, whispering into a silence that was starting, horribly, to whisper back.

"Yes," said Maren Solle.

It was the easiest lie she had ever told, because it was not a lie at all. She did mean to perform the rite. She did mean to lay her hands on Edmund Hartwell's shoulders and speak the old words and let the Clear Mirror pass through him like light through water.

She simply had not yet decided what it would find.

Verrek did not look up from his papers. "Three days," he said. "And Cantor—the candle. Blow it out on your way."

She blew it out. The dark that rushed in was total, and in it, for half a heartbeat, Maren heard—or imagined she heard, or would spend the rest of her life not being sure—something on the far side of the tower window, out over the sleeping city, singing.

She went down the stairs two at a time, with the rite's words already forming in her mouth, and the shape of the betrayal already, irreversibly, chosen.

* * *

The scriptorium lamps burned late. She knew they would; he always worked late, her scribe, her door, her fifteen-year problem, hunched over his ledgers in the pool of yellow light with his careful ruined hands.

Maren stood in the doorway of the Great Scriptorium and watched Edmund Hartwell copy out a column of harmless charms, and rehearsed the first line of the Rite of the Clear Mirror under her breath, and hated herself so completely and so quietly that it felt, almost, like devotion.

"Edmund," she said.

He looked up. And smiled—that tired, honest, unbearable smile—and asked her if she'd eaten.
