## 13. The Binding Rite

They came for him on a Tuesday, which felt insultingly ordinary.

Edmund had always assumed that if the Choir ever turned its full attention on him—if the machinery of denials and patrols and Brother Quillon's patient smile finally ground toward his door—it would happen at night, with torches, with shouting. Instead it happened at ten in the morning, in the scriptorium, while he was mid-line on a ledger of candle-tithes.

"Brother Hartwell." A junior warden he didn't know by name stood at the end of his desk. Behind her, two more wardens waited with the studied blankness of furniture. "Cantor Solle requests your presence in the Chapel of Reckoning."

"Maren?" He caught himself too late. "Cantor Solle."

The warden's eyes flicked up from a paper she was pretending to read. Everyone in Vessing knew what first names meant between people. Everyone knew they were evidence.

"She requests you," the warden repeated, as if he hadn't spoken.

He set down his pen. Squared the ledger. Blotted the line he'd been writing—*candles, tallow, forty gross*—and left it half-finished, which bothered him more than it should have. Fifteen years of finishing lines. Fifteen years of closing every ledger neatly so no one would ever have reason to open one.

"Am I permitted to know why?"

"You are requested," the warden said again, "in the Chapel of Reckoning."

Which answered nothing and told him everything.

* * *

The Chapel of Reckoning was not a chapel in any sense Edmund had ever found comforting. It had no pews, no altar, no image of anything—because the faith that outlawed its own gods could hardly keep pictures of them, and had never managed to invent replacements worth carving. What it had instead was a floor.

The floor was the point. A single slab of white stone, older than the building around it, older than the city, quarried—who knew where—from something that wasn't quite marble and wasn't quite bone. The Choir called it the Ledger Stone. When a soul was placed upon it and the proper rites were sung, the stone did what all good ledgers do: it recorded. Debts owed, debts paid, oaths kept, oaths broken. And, though this appeared in no official text Edmund had ever copied, it recorded nature. What a person *was*, beneath the paperwork of their life.

He'd seen it used twice. Once on a merchant accused of smuggling relics—the stone had shown only debt, mountains of it, and the man had wept with relief. Once on a girl of nineteen who had been heard singing to something in a well. The stone had gone grey under her, then black, and the wardens had carried her out before she finished screaming, and Edmund had never learned her name because names stopped being written down after that.

Now he stood at the edge of that floor in his best coat, hands folded like a man at a funeral, and Maren stood across from him in full cantor's vestments—grey wool, silver cord, the hollow circle of the Choir pinned at her throat—and she would not look at him.

"The rite is routine," said High Cantor Verel, presiding from a wooden chair that might have been borrowed from a kitchen for all the ceremony it carried. Verel was small, dry, and precise, a woman assembled entirely out of rules. "A formality following recent irregularities in the city. Every senior scribe will undergo it. You are simply first alphabetically."

That was a lie. Edmund knew it was a lie the way you know rain is coming—not through argument but through the ache in old scars. Scribes were not audited by the Ledger Stone. Scribes were furniture. Whatever irregularities Verel meant, whatever Quillon had whispered into whichever ear, this was aimed at him with the care of an archer who had already measured the wind.

And Maren. Maren standing there in vestments, holding the cantor's bell, refusing his eyes.

She knows, he thought. Not suspected. *Knows.* She has lifted something she shouldn't have, read something redacted, and now she stands there about to aim the Choir's oldest instrument at the thing she loves, because she was ordered to, because she agreed—

Because she agreed, and hated herself for it. He could see the hating. It was in the way her thumb pressed white against the bell's handle. In the way she'd braided her hair with a severity she hadn't worn since her induction year. Maren armored herself in precision when she was frightened, and right now she was dressed head to foot in fear pretending to be procedure.

"Brother Hartwell," Verel said. "Remove your shoes. Step onto the stone."

He did. The stone was cold through his stockings, cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature—a cold like being remembered by something vast and indifferent.

Maren raised the bell.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about being searched: it feels like being read aloud.

The rite began with the bell—one clear note that didn't fade so much as *open*, the way a door opens—and then Maren began to sing, low and formal, the Canticle of Inventory. Edmund knew every word. He had copied it eleven times over fifteen years, in three different hands, and had never once thought about what the words *did*.

Now he felt them.

It started at his feet and rose like water filling a boot. A pressure, a turning-over, a patient riffling—as if someone were going through the drawers of him. His debts surfaced first; he felt them catalogued, itemized, the charm-ledgers, the interest accrued, the fifteen years of small lawful dishonesties filed away without comment. Then his oaths: the oath of scribal service, sworn at twenty-two with ink still wet on his fingers. Kept. Kept. Kept.

Then the search went deeper, past the paperwork, down to the floorboards of him. To what he *was*.

And here was the moment—the one he would think about later, lying awake, turning it over like a coin worn smooth—the moment he understood that Maren already knew what the stone would find.

Because her song changed.

Not much. A half-tone. A breath held where a breath should have been spent. Anyone else would have heard nothing. But Edmund had spent fifteen years learning to hear what people didn't say, in ledgers and in market stalls and in the careful silences of a woman who loved him inside an institution designed to make love impossible. And Maren's voice, when it reached the verse that touched the deep places, flinched.

She was steering it.

The Canticle of Inventory was supposed to be a plumb line dropped straight down. Hers was bending—curving around something, the way water bends around a stone, the way a hand searching a pocket for a coin can be made, gently, to search around the coin instead. She was singing the rite correctly enough to satisfy Verel, and wrongly enough, by a margin of half-tones and held breaths, to leave him unopened.

She was protecting him. From inside her own betrayal.

And that—that was worse than the stone. That was worse than the wardens at the doors and Verel's dry eyes and the cold climbing his shins. Because it meant she had known, and had said nothing, and had chosen to warn him the only way left to her: by lying with her voice in front of the entire hierarchy of her faith, gambling both their lives on a deception measured in half-tones.

He let her find nothing.

That was the discipline of it. Every instinct in his body wanted to brace, to hide, to clench down around the secret like a fist around a coal—and he knew, with the strange clarity the stone's cold gave him, that clenching was exactly how people got caught. The stone read tension the way it read debt. So he did the hardest thing he had done in fifteen years of hard small things: he relaxed. Completely. He let his mind go soft and honest and boring. He thought about candle-tithes. About the unfinished line in his ledger. About the exact weight of a protective knot in the hand, twine and wax and a copper bead. About tea. About the way steam curled off a shared cup on a winter evening in a room that smelled of ink, and a woman across the table who looked at him like he was a sentence worth finishing.

Let them see that, he thought. Let that be the whole of me. A tired scribe who thinks about tea.

The pressure climbed past his ribs, past his throat, settled behind his eyes like a thumb pressed to a bruise. Somewhere above him Maren's voice held its careful curve, and he sang back at her, silently, with the shape of his own emptiness: *I know you know. I'm sorry. Keep going. Don't stop.*

Her voice did not waver. Her eyes, fixed on a point slightly above his head, did not move. But her free hand—the one not holding the bell—had closed, slowly, into a fist at her side, and the knuckles were white as the stone under his feet.

* * *

The note ended the way it began: by opening, and then by closing, and the silence afterward had weight, like a book shut firmly.

Edmund stood on the Ledger Stone and felt the search withdraw, drawer by drawer, leaving everything apparently as it found him. The cold drained out through his soles. His knees informed him, quietly, that they had been locked for some time.

Verel consulted a slate she held in her lap, on which some attending clerk had chalked the stone's findings—or the absence of them.

"No anomaly," she said. It was not a relief in her mouth; it was an entry. "Debt within parameters. Oaths intact. Nature—" a pause, the smallest frown, "—unremarkable."

*Unremarkable.* Edmund nearly laughed. Fifteen years of keeping his head down, and the Choir's oldest instrument had finally pronounced its verdict: this man is furniture. He wanted to frame it.

"Thank you, Cantor Solle," Verel said. "Your rendering was... adequate."

Adequate. Not *precise*. Not *flawless*. Verel was assembled out of rules, and rules had ears, and somewhere in that dry skull a small flag had been planted—half a tone's worth of flag. Edmund watched Maren receive the word without blinking, bow the correct degree, and understood that she had heard it too. Adequate was the sound of suspicion deferred, not dismissed.

"You are dismissed, Brother Hartwell," Verel said. "Return to your duties. Further rites may be scheduled."

Further rites. Of course. A fisherman doesn't trust one cast.

He stepped off the stone. His legs held. He bowed—to Verel, to the wardens, last and longest to Maren, a bow of exactly the depth protocol required, no more, and in that exactness he poured everything he couldn't say: *I saw you. I saw what it cost. I'm not angry. I'm terrified for you.*

She did not return the bow. Protocol didn't require it—a cantor outranked a scribe—but every other cantor he'd ever stood before had given the courtesy nod anyway, the small human discount on hierarchy. Maren gave him stone. Maren gave him the full frozen face of someone who has decided that if she permits herself one crack, the whole dam goes.

Good, he thought, walking out past the wardens, past the incense-less air, into the corridor where morning light lay in pale bars on the flagstones. Hold the dam. Hold it until we're somewhere the water can go.

* * *

He made it four steps down the corridor before her voice reached him, pitched for officials and perfectly empty:

"Brother Hartwell. Your ledger from this morning—the candle tithe. You left a line unfinished. Bring it to my office for completion before end of day."

Every warden in earshot heard a cantor enforcing scribal discipline. Only Edmund heard the rest, the sentence underneath the sentence, written in the grammar they had built together over years of tea and almosts: *come to me, alone, and we will speak in the only language they haven't learned to audit.*

"Yes, Cantor Solle," he said, and turned, and walked back past her without looking at her face, because looking would have ruined everything, because her composure was a wall and his heartbreak was on the other side of it disguised as obedience, and walls like that came down at a glance.

At the corner of the corridor he allowed himself one look back. She was already turning away, bell in hand, spine straight as a ruled line, the hollow circle of the Choir catching the light at her throat.

But her hand—the one that had made a fist during the rite—was trembling.

She opened it, briefly, to steady it against her vestments, and Edmund saw what she had been holding inside it without knowing: the small stub of blackboard chalk cantors used to mark rehearsal notes, snapped clean in half.

Snapped, he understood, sometime during the Canticle of Inventory. By a fist that had clenched despite itself.

She knew what he was. She had lied to her godless church to keep its oldest eye blind. And now she had ordered him to her office—which meant she had something to say, or something to give him, or something to burn.

End of day, he thought, walking back to his desk, to his ledger, to his half-finished line about candles. He picked up his pen. His hand was steady. It was the steadiest lie he had ever told.

He wrote: *candles, tallow, forty gross,* and then, beneath it, in the margin, so small it was barely a mark, the way you write a name in ash on a windowsill: *end of day.*
