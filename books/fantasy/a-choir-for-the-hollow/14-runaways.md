## 14. Runaways

### Maren

The binding rite was scheduled for the ninth hour of morning. Maren had until then to decide what she would tell the High Cantor, and she had already decided, sometime around the fourth bell, that she would tell him nothing at all.

She stood in her cell in the Cantors' Wing with Edmund's ledger open on her desk—the one she'd audited three weeks ago, the one with discrepancies she'd chosen not to report—and beneath it, hidden in a folio of obsolete hymnals, the archive page she should have surrendered the moment she lifted the redaction.

*E. Hartwell.*

A child's name. A door's name. Fifteen years sealed away by men who were mostly dead now, and she was the only living soul who knew it besides the man himself, and possibly whatever god had been whispering to him through his windowsill.

She had rehearsed a dozen versions of obedience. She would administer the rite, find nothing—or find something, and report it, and let the machinery do what machinery did. That was the version the girl who entered the Choir at eleven would have chosen. That girl had sung the great hymn to an empty heaven and called the echo sufficient.

But the woman who sat here now kept seeing Edmund's face during the rite rehearsal—not fear, exactly. Recognition. He had known what she was looking for. He had let her look and found nothing for her to see, and he had watched her flinch at her own mercy, and neither of them had said a word, because saying a word would have made it real, and real things had to be reported.

Maren Solle had spent twenty-two years learning the difference between silence and peace. This was not peace.

She dressed in traveling clothes under her cantor's robes—plain wool, nothing that would mark her as Choir if the robes came off. She took the small strongbox from beneath her floorboard: forty marks, a letter of passage signed with a superior's seal that she had forged twice before for audits and could forge again, and her grandmother's knife, which was technically contraband and which she had never once reported to herself.

Then she went to find Edmund Hartwell before Brother Quillon's wardens did.

* * *

She found him at the scriptorium, where else, hunched over a charm ledger like the ink might forgive him. His hands stilled when he saw her face.

"You know," he said quietly. Not a question.

"I know." She glanced at the two junior scribes copying psalters across the room. "Not here."

They walked out through the Cloister of Counted Hours, past the sundial that hadn't cast a true shadow since the Banishment—some old joke of the architects, or some old grief—and out the pilgrims' gate where the guards knew her face and didn't question a cantor escorting a bonded scribe on temple business.

Only when they reached the tannery lane, where the smell covered conversation, did she stop and turn to him.

"The binding rite is this morning. Quillon petitioned for it himself. If you're in the city at the ninth hour, they'll seal you, Edmund. And sealing isn't—" Her voice caught on the word. She had read the old accounts. Sealing wasn't death. It was worse: a door closed so thoroughly that what remained behind couldn't remember being open. "It isn't survivable. Not in any way that matters."

Edmund looked at her for a long moment. Fifteen years of careful, rationed affection between them, and now there was no time left to be careful with it.

"You're supposed to be the one administering it," he said.

"Yes."

"And instead you're telling me to run."

"Instead I'm telling you to run." She pressed the forged letter of passage into his hand. "There's a warden checkpoint at Grayford tollhouse on the north road. My credentials will cover one traveler. Two is harder, but the letter says I'm auditing a border shrine, and shrines need a lay assistant, and you are, officially as of this morning, my assistant."

"Maren." He said her name the way people say a prayer they've stopped believing will be answered. "If they catch you—"

"They won't."

"If they catch us—"

"They won't," she said again, and hated how much it sounded like hope rather than knowledge. "Edmund. The Choir has known about doors for fifteen years and said nothing. They sealed them and hid the pages and let the rest of us sing to an empty room. Whatever we owe them, we paid it already. Both of us. In advance. For years."

He looked down at the letter, then at her, and something in his face broke open—not despair, for once, but the thing underneath despair, which was want.

"The north road," he said. "Toward Osselmark."

"Toward Osselmark."

He exhaled, and it was almost a laugh, and it was almost a sob, and she understood both, because she felt them too: terror with its hand around her throat, and underneath the terror, for the first time in twenty-two years, air.

"Ninth hour is in ninety minutes," she said. "We should be past the aqueduct by then."

They ran.

* * *

### Edmund

The last time Edmund had run through Vessing, he'd been nine years old and late for choir practice at a church that no longer existed. He had forgotten how the city felt at speed—the cobbles striking up through his boots, the awnings snapping past, faces blurring into color. Running changed a city. Running made it honest: just distance, just breath, just the animal arithmetic of getting away.

Maren ran ahead of him, robes abandoned somewhere back along the tannery lane, plain wool now, hair bound up under a market-woman's scarf. She moved like someone who had planned this longer than she'd admitted—even to herself. She cut through the fish market without hesitating at the turns. She knew which alleys the patrols avoided because the drains flooded. Twenty-two years of institutional life, and apparently some part of her had been mapping exits the whole time.

At the aqueduct they stopped in the shadow of the arches, both of them bent double, breathing hard. Somewhere behind them, muffled by stone and water, the ninth bell began to ring.

Edmund counted the strokes. Nine. Somewhere in the scriptorium, a cantor's chair sat empty. Somewhere in the High Cantor's chambers, a rite was being prepared for a subject who would never arrive. By noon, Quillon would know. By nightfall, every gate within thirty miles would have their descriptions.

"I keep waiting to feel worse," Edmund said.

"Don't," Maren said. "It'll come. Save it for when it's useful."

"You've done this before."

"Audits. Never anything like this." She straightened, pushed the scarf back from her forehead, and looked at him, and in the grey light under the aqueduct her face was stripped of everything cantorial—all the composed authority, all the careful distance—and what remained was just Maren, tired and frightened and absolutely resolved. "But I've spent my whole life following rules I didn't write. I'd like to spend one day finding out what happens when I don't."

They crossed the aqueduct on the maintenance ledge, single file, the city dropping away below them—the rooftops, the temple spires, the great dome of the Hollow Choir catching the morning sun like a bowl held up to a heaven that had stopped listening. Edmund looked back once. He had sold charms in that city for fifteen years, harmless knots and whispered blessings, half-charity and half-confession, and he had told himself he was keeping his head down. Now, from above, he could see what he'd actually been doing: standing very still inside a machine, hoping stillness would be mistaken for consent.

The pilgrim road began beyond the last farmsteads—a band of ancient paving stones running north through wheat country, worn smooth by feet that had walked it when Osselmark still existed. His grandmother had walked it. She had sung on it, probably, songs he only half-remembered, melodies that surfaced in his dreams wearing ash.

They walked all day. By afternoon the city was a smudge behind them and the road was empty except for crows and the occasional shrine-post—little stone pillars at the roadside, each one defaced centuries ago, the carved names chiseled off during the Banishment. Maren touched each one as they passed. Edmund pretended not to notice, and then decided to stop pretending.

"You can say it," he offered. "Whatever you're thinking about them."

"I'm thinking they were somebody's grandmothers too." She said it simply, without performance, and kept walking.

Near dusk they reached a waystation—an old pilgrim shelter, roof partially collapsed, walls thick enough to hold warmth. There was a hearth, and dry bracken, and a spring that still ran cold and clean, because water had never agreed to the Banishment.

Maren built a fire while Edmund set the wards—not charms, not anymore; those were for sale, and he was done selling. These were older gestures, half-remembered from a childhood he'd never examined: salt at the threshold, a knot tied in a length of twine and hung above the door, four words spoken under his breath in a language he shouldn't have known and did.

Osselmarki. His grandmother's tongue. It fit in his mouth like a key fitting a lock that had rusted shut.

Maren watched him from across the fire. "What was that?"

"Ward. Old style." He sat down heavily beside her. "My grandmother taught me. I thought I'd forgotten. Apparently I filed it somewhere the Choir couldn't audit."

"There's a lot of you I haven't audited," Maren said, and then, before the words could curdle into awkwardness, she leaned against his shoulder. Just that. Just weight and warmth and the fire cracking, and outside, the enormous dark country rolling north toward a border that appeared on no map.

"I keep thinking," Edmund said, "that I should feel trapped. Fleeing. That's what fleeing is supposed to feel like."

"And instead?"

Instead it felt like the night market, he wanted to say. Like the moment after a charm works impossibly well and the buyer looks at you with wonder instead of suspicion. Like every time Maren had stayed for tea and neither of them had named what staying meant. Freedom, it turned out, felt exactly like the best moments of his constrained life—which meant those moments had always been free, and he'd simply never claimed them.

"Like I've been running for fifteen years," he said. "This is just the first time anyone can see it."

Maren laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, but real. Then she reached into her coat and produced, absurdly, a tin of tea leaves and two tin cups.

"We're fugitives," Edmund said.

"Fugitives drink tea." She set the cups by the fire. "I've been carrying this since dawn. I decided if I was going to betray twenty-two years of service, I was going to do it properly, with provisions."

They drank tea in a ruined waystation on a road to nowhere, and it was, Edmund thought, the finest cup of his life.

Later, wrapped in their cloaks on opposite sides of the fire, she spoke into the dark.

"Edmund. When we get there—to the door, if we get there—what does Ashenveil actually want from you?"

He was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"He wants me to open it," he said finally. "And I think—I think what he wants in exchange is more than he's said. Gods are like creditors that way. The first offer is never the whole ledger."

"Then we renegotiate," Maren said, with such flat cantorial authority that he laughed despite everything. "I've audited your ledgers for years, Edmund Hartwell. No one gets the better of you on terms. Don't start letting a god try."

He fell asleep smiling, which frightened him more than the wardens did.

### Maren

She woke before dawn to the sound of hooves.

For one frozen instant she was back in the Cantors' Wing, late for matins, heart slamming—and then memory arrived, all of it, the rite unattended, the road, the fire burned to embers, Edmund asleep across from her with ash-grey light on his face.

The hooves were on the road. Southbound, moving fast. More than one horse.

She crossed to the broken wall and looked out through the gap. Four riders, maybe five, dark-cloaked, moving at a trot along the pilgrim road. Warden formation—she knew it from drills, from diagrams, from a lifetime of institutional geometry. But they weren't searching the verges. They weren't riding paired sweeps the way a pursuit rode. They rode like a message, like men carrying orders rather than hunting a target.

They passed the waystation without slowing. One rider turned his head as he went by—just slightly, just enough—and Maren pressed herself flat against the cold stone and did not breathe.

The hoofbeats faded north.

She stayed at the wall a long time after they were gone, watching the road unspool toward the horizon, where the wheat country rose into hills, and beyond the hills, according to maps that no longer existed, a country waited that had erased itself rather than be erased.

"Edmund," she said softly.

He was awake. She could tell by his breathing—by the fact that his breathing had stopped entirely.

"Riders?" he asked.

"Five. Northbound. Wardens, but not hunting formation." She turned back to him. In the pre-dawn grey his eyes were wide and dark, and she saw the thought land in him before he said it:

"They're not looking for us."

"No."

"They're heading for the border too." He sat up slowly. "Or for the shrine. Or for—" He stopped. His hand had gone, unconsciously, to the windowsill of his own memory, where ash had spelled his name. "Something's already ahead of us, Maren. Something got there first."

Outside, the sky was turning the color of old parchment, and far off in the northern hills, thin as a thread, Maren heard—or thought she heard, or dreamed she heard—a sound like singing.

It was not coming from the road.

It was coming from everywhere.
