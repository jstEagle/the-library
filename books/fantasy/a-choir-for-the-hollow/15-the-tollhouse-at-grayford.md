## 15. The Tollhouse at Grayford

The road to Grayford was the kind of road the Choir pretended didn't exist—rutted, unmarked on any map printed after the Banishment, lined with hedgerows that had gone feral in the way of all things left alone long enough. Edmund walked it with a blister forming on his left heel and a knot-charm warm against his sternum, and he found, to his considerable surprise, that he was humming.

He hadn't hummed in fifteen years. There had been nothing to hum. The scriptorium did not reward melody; it rewarded precision, silence, and the particular posture of a man who had learned to take up as little of the world as possible. But somewhere past the second milestone, with Maren Solle walking three paces ahead of him with her cantor's grey cloak pinned up under her arms like a woman wading, he caught himself working through the shape of an old tune his grandmother used to sing over the wash basin—and instead of strangling it in his throat, he let it live.

Maren's shoulders went still.

"You're singing," she said, without turning around.

"I'm walking."

"You're walking *and* singing." She stopped, turned, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't parse—something between alarm and a hunger so naked it embarrassed them both. "Edmund. Do you know what that song is?"

"It's a washing song. My grandmother—" He stopped too. The morning light came through the hedge behind her and made a halo of her flyaway hair, and he thought: *she has been beautiful this whole time and I rationed my looking.* "It's just a song."

"That is Ossic. That is *pre-Banishment* Ossic, and I have heard it exactly twice in my life, both times from sealed archive cylinders that I was forbidden to play." Her voice dropped. "You've been carrying a banned hymn in your mouth for fifteen years and humming it at laundry time like it's nothing."

"My grandmother wasn't forbidden anything," Edmund said. "She was dead before anyone thought to ask her permission."

They stood there on the empty road, two criminals in sensible boots, and Maren laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly pressed flat, but real. Edmund realized he would commit quite a lot of crime to hear it again.

"Grayford by dusk," she said, resuming the walk. "The tollhouse sits on the old pilgrim bridge. There'll be wardens."

"There are always wardens."

"Yes," she said. "And for once, one of them answers to me."

* * *

They smelled the tollhouse before they saw it: woodsmoke and boiled mutton and wet stone, the smell of every checkpoint in every country that had ever decided movement was a privilege rather than a birthright. The bridge over the Grayford river was older than the Banishment—pilgrim-cut stones worn into shallow bowls where centuries of knees had knelt—and the tollhouse crouched at its near end like a toad that had learned architecture.

A lantern burned in the window. Two figures inside, one at the desk. A third, armed, pacing the far end of the bridge itself.

"The warden on the bridge is the problem," Maren murmured. They had stopped in the shadow of a hay barn fifty yards out, close enough to hear the river going dark below. "The toll-keeper takes coin and doesn't care whose. The warden cares about *faces*. He'll have the new bulletins—the ones with your description."

"I don't have a description. I'm a scribe. We're famously forgettable."

"You were forgettable when you were copying ledgers." She turned and took his chin in her hand—not tenderly, but the way one straightens a picture frame—and studied his face in the last of the light. Her thumb moved once along his jaw. It was the most either of them had touched in fifteen years of careful proximity, and neither of them breathed through it. "You're not forgettable now. You're a man who walks away. That reads different on a face."

"What do you suggest?"

She let go of him and reached into the inner pocket of her cloak, where she kept the small tin box of cantor's implements: the bone stylus, the vial of consecrated ink, the wax seals stamped with the Choir's hollow circle. Everything in that box was designed to bind. She had spent twenty years learning how.

"Now," she said, "we use it to lie."

* * *

The plan was simple because simple plans were the only kind that survived contact with wardens. Maren would go in first, alone, presenting herself as Cantor Solle on circuit inspection—a plausible errand; the Choir sent auditors up the old pilgrim roads twice a year to verify that no unauthorized devotion had taken root in the villages. She would establish herself, present papers, occupy the toll-keeper's attention. Edmund would come in behind her as her hired porter, hood up, carrying her document case, and would say nothing except perhaps a grunt if addressed directly. Grunts were within his abilities.

The charm was not part of the original plan. The charm was insurance, and Edmund paid for it in the barn's lee while Maren kept watch, working the cord with fingers that remembered even when his mind wanted to forget. It was a passage-knot—an old market trick, pre-Banishment, designed to make the wearer briefly unremarkable. Not invisible. Just *not worth noticing*, the way a fencepost isn't worth noticing, the way a man carrying luggage never is.

It was also, technically, one of the seventeen categories of working explicitly named in the Choir's Index of Prohibited Devotions. Article Nine. Edmund had copied the Index four times in his career and knew Article Nine the way sinners know their favorite psalm.

"You're doing what?" Maren said, when she saw the finished knot.

"Insurance."

"Edmund, if they search you and find a worked charm on your body, credentials won't matter. They'll burn you and file me as either complicit or deceived, and *deceived* gets me re-educated."

"They won't search me. I'm a porter. Porters get patted down for weapons, not sins." He tied the cord at his wrist, under the sleeve. "And if something goes wrong—if they look at my face too long—I'd rather have a fence between us and Article Nine than your good intentions."

Maren stared at him. He watched her do the arithmetic every Choir-raised person did automatically: risk, exposure, deniability, cost. He watched her arrive at the answer the arithmetic always gave, and then he watched her do something the arithmetic never accounted for.

She reached out and tucked the knot's loose end under his cuff, neatening it, the way his mother used to.

"All right," she said quietly. "But Edmund—if it comes to a choice between the knot and running, you run. Don't be brave. Bravery is for people with less to lose."

"I have nothing to lose."

"You have me," she said, "and you keep forgetting to count me."

Then she pulled her hood forward, squared the document case against his chest, and walked toward the lantern light with the unhurried gait of institutional authority, and Edmund followed in her shadow like a fencepost, feeling his heart attempt to exit through his throat.

* * *

Inside, the tollhouse was low-beamed and amber-lit and smelled overwhelmingly of mutton fat. The toll-keeper was a wide woman with a ledger of her own—everyone had ledgers, Edmund thought wearily, the whole world was just debts arranged in columns—and a voice like a cart on gravel. The warden stood apart by the door to the bridge, young, earnest, with the fresh-faced zeal of a man who had joined the Choir out of genuine faith and had not yet been ground down into bureaucracy. He was the dangerous kind. The tired kind could be bought or bored. The earnest kind had to be managed.

"Cantor Solle." Maren set her seal-stamped travel writ on the desk without fanfare. "Circuit audit, western reaches. I'll need passage across and a word about devotional compliance in the village."

The toll-keeper squinted at the wax. "Hollow circle's right. Haven't seen a cantor out here since spring."

"We rotate. May I see your toll registry? And your record of blessings performed at this crossing?"

"Blessings?" The toll-keeper laughed. "Nobody's blessed anything on this bridge since the Banishment, cantor. People pay their coin and cross."

"*That*," Maren said, with the weary gravity of a woman who has delivered this lecture many times, "is precisely what I'm here to discuss."

It was masterful. Edmund, standing behind her with the document case and his eyes appropriately lowered, felt something swell in his chest that had no business swelling during a felony. She was good. She was *institutionally* good—she deployed boredom like a weapon, buried the warden's attention under procedural tedium, made the mere idea of inspecting her porter so administratively unappetizing that the young warden's gaze slid off Edmund twice and returned to the fascinating spectacle of a cantor scolding a toll-keeper about blessing registries.

The toll-keeper stamped a pass. The toll-keeper took coin. The toll-keeper asked no questions, because questions were not in her ledger.

It was the warden who broke the pattern.

"Your porter," he said.

Edmund's blood went to ice water.

"What of him?" Maren said, not turning.

"He's got a pilgrim's mark on his wrist. Under the cuff. I saw it when he shifted the case." The young man's brow furrowed with the pure, terrible sincerity of the recently converted. "Pilgrim knots are prohibited work, cantor. I'm required to inspect."

*He saw it.* Of course he saw it. Earnest men always saw the thing you most needed hidden; it was practically a theological principle. Edmund felt the passage-knot humming faintly against his pulse and understood, with horrible clarity, that the knot only made him unremarkable—it could not survive being *remarked upon*. The moment the warden spoke its name aloud, the magic curdled. He was suddenly, fully visible, a man standing in a tollhouse holding contraband against his skin.

Maren turned around slowly.

"Warden," she said, "what is your name?"

"Ferrin. Warden Ferrin, cantor."

"Warden Ferrin. Do you know what I am carrying in this case?" She lifted it from Edmund's shoulder and set it on the desk with a soft, deliberate thump. Inside were her rites, her inks, her binding stylus—enough sanctioned power to level a village's worth of unauthorized prayer. "Do you know what I am authorized to do to a man wearing a worked knot?"

Ferrin hesitated.

"I am authorized to unmake him," Maren said quietly. "Article Nine sanctions it. I could peel that charm off his soul and leave the rest of him a husk that signs confessions. That is the law you are so eager to invoke." She stepped closer, and her voice did not rise; it *descended*, into registers Edmund had only heard her use in the choir loft, registers that made the candle flames lean. "So I will ask you once, Warden Ferrin. Would you like to watch?"

The silence stretched. The toll-keeper had gone very still over her ledger, pretending with great professionalism to be elsewhere.

"No, cantor," Ferrin said finally.

"No, what?"

"No... thank you, cantor."

"Then open the bridge." She turned back to the desk, collected the stamped pass, and added, almost as an afterthought, "And Warden Ferrin—the next time you see a mark you don't understand on a laborer's wrist, consider that some marks are placed there *by the Choir itself*, for penance service. Ask your superiors before you embarrass us both again."

Ferrin flushed scarlet and unlocked the bridge door.

* * *

They crossed in silence. The river ran black beneath the pilgrim stones, and halfway over Edmund's legs simply stopped working, and he stood gripping the cold rail while the adrenaline drained out of him in a long, shaking breath.

"You lied to him," he said.

"I improvised."

"You told him the knot was penance service. That's—that's a specific Choir practice, Maren, that hasn't existed since—"

"Since 1141. I know. I invented it. He's nineteen and earnest and he hasn't read deep enough in the archives to catch it, and by the time he does, we'll be a hundred miles gone." She came to stand beside him at the rail. Below, the river carried the lantern light away in pieces. "Are you angry?"

"I'm—" He laughed, and it came out cracked. "I spent fifteen years making sure you never had to dirty your hands. I built my whole life around it. Keeping the ledgers clean, keeping the charms small, keeping *you* on the right side of every line I crossed. And now you've committed fraud, impersonated authority, threatened a warden with an unlawful rite, and covered a felony with a fake penance doctrine, and you did it *better* than I ever sold a charm in my life."

"Well," Maren said. "One of us had to be good at crime."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she looked back, and the space between them—which had been a careful, courteous, fifteen-year distance—was simply gone. Not closed. Gone, like a wall that turns out to have been painted on.

"Maren," he said. "Do you understand what you did back there? Not the lying. The rest of it. You saw the knot and you *chose* me. In front of a warden, with your seal on the table—you chose me over everything you built. There's no walking that back. When Ferrin figures it out, and he will, your name goes beside mine in the bulletin."

"I know."

"You could still turn around. Walk back to Vessing, report that I forced you, that I deceived you, that the porter stole your case—I gave you the story, Maren, I handed it to you at the bridge and you didn't take it."

"I know," she said again, and then, quieter, with the terrible calm of a woman stepping off a ledge: "I've known since the archive room, Edmund. Since I read your name under the redaction and sat there all night deciding whether to hand it up the chain. I chose you before the bridge. The bridge was just the first place anyone was watching."

The river went on below them, indifferent and dark. Somewhere behind them, the tollhouse lantern glowed, and Warden Ferrin was probably already doubting himself, and doubt was a seed that grew toward Vessing at the speed of a letter.

Edmund reached out and took her hand. No ceremony. No asking. Fifteen years of rationed looking ended in the simple fact of his palm against hers, callus to callus, scribe to cantor, criminal to criminal.

"So we're both of us damned now," he said.

"We were always going to be. The question was only ever whether we'd be damned separately." She laced her fingers through his. "Together's better. Together's *efficient*."

He laughed again—really laughed, helpless, standing on a bridge older than the god-banishment with a forged pass in his pocket and a forbidden hymn rising in his throat—and this time he didn't strangle it. He sang the line his grandmother used to sing, quiet, just a few notes over the black water, and Maren leaned her head against his shoulder and listened like a woman hearing scripture read aloud for the first time.

They crossed the rest of the bridge together.

Behind them, unnoticed by either, a pigeon stirred in the tollhouse eaves—a courier bird, Choir-banded, blinking in the lantern glow. It had been fed and watered by Warden Ferrin not an hour past, and bound to his message-tube was a small folded note, addressed in a young man's careful, earnest hand:

*To the Office of Circuit Oversight, Vessing. Re: irregularity observed at Grayford crossing. Request verification of penance-mark protocol, ref. Article Nine.*

By dawn it would be flying south.
