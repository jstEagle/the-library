## 16. What Maren Believes

They walked until the pilgrim road gave up pretending to be a road.

By the third day out of Vessing the cobbles had thinned to gravel, then to packed earth, then to a track that wound through wheat gone feral—shoulder-high, gold-headed, leaning over them like a crowd at a procession. Nobody had harvested here in years. The old pilgrim way to Osselmark had been closed for a generation, and the land knew it. It grew wild in the spaces where devotion used to keep it tidy.

Maren's cantor boots were not made for this. They were made for choir floors and archive tiles, for the measured distances of the Basilica of the Hollow, and by noon of the second day she had developed blisters with opinions. She said nothing about it. Edmund noticed anyway—he noticed everything about her, which was one of the things she both loved and resented—and that evening he sat her down on a fallen milestone, knelt without asking permission, and cut her boots open along the seams with his scribe's knife.

"You're ruining Choir property," she said.

"I'm ruining my property." He worked the leather apart with his thumbs, gentle as a man handling a relic. "I've been paying for those boots for fifteen years. Every charm I sold, every ledger line. I did the arithmetic once. I own roughly half a boot."

She laughed before she could stop herself—a real laugh, ugly and loud, startling a pheasant out of the wheat. And then, because laughing opened something, she found herself saying, in the flat voice she used for inventory: "I reported a woman last spring."

Edmund's hands went still on her boot.

"Her name was Odile Ferrin. She lived near the Tanner's Stair. She kept a garden that grew wrong—too fast, too green, through a frost. The neighbors talked. I was doing a census audit of unlicensed blessings and I heard the talk, and I wrote it down." Maren looked at the wheat, at the sun going long and orange behind it. "I didn't even see the garden. I wrote it down because someone said it, and writing it down is my function. Wardens went. They sealed her. Do you know what sealing looks like, Edmund? I'd never seen one. They don't hurt you. That's the terrible part. They just take the part of you that wanted anything, and they leave the rest standing there, polite and empty, thanking everyone."

The milestone was cold under her. Somewhere behind them, miles back, Vessing smoked quietly with its riots and its martial prayer-law, its wells refilling against all doctrine, its bread rising in defiance of the Ledger. Ahead of them lay a country that wasn't on any map, and a god who wanted her lover, and a debt neither of them had ever named aloud.

"And I stood there," Maren said, "watching Odile Ferrin thank the wardens, and I thought: this is mercy. This is what we do. We are the mercy of Vessing. And then I thought—" Her voice caught on it, fifteen years of catching on it. "And then I thought, *who told us that?*"

Edmund finished the boot. He tied off the cut seam with waxed thread from his kit, neat little stitches, the work of hands that had mended books all their life. Then he sat beside her on the milestone, shoulder to shoulder, and waited. He was very good at waiting. Scribes were. You learned that a silence held more than a question ever could.

"The Choir serves the people," she said. "That's what they taught us at induction. *We sing so they don't have to.* That was the whole theology, Edmund. The gods were dangerous—the gods answered, and answers breed wars, and prayers breed fanatics, and so we took the answering away and left only the singing. We became the buffer between humanity and heaven. Every hymn, every rite, every redaction—it was all protection." She turned her hands over, looked at the ink stains in the creases of her fingers, permanent as scars. "But lately I keep thinking about the word *buffer*. A buffer doesn't protect you from the thing. A buffer protects the thing from being reached. And I've started wondering who we're protecting, and from what, and why the High Cantor's office has a budget line for sealings that grows every year while the poor-relief fund shrinks, and why nobody—not once in my twenty years—has asked what happens to the power the doors would let through if we simply... let it be shared instead of hoarded."

"Hoarded," Edmund repeated softly.

"That's the word I've been refusing to use for two years." She laughed again, smaller this time. "There. I've said it. Cantor Maren Solle believes the Hollow Choir serves its own silence. Say it back to me so I know it's real."

"The Choir serves its own silence," he said, obedient as always to her, and then, less obediently: "It took you twenty years to say that?"

"It took me twenty years to survive saying it." She bumped her shoulder against his. "You've been breaking the law since before I met you. What took you so long?"

He didn't answer right away. The wheat moved around them in slow gold waves, and far off a nightjar started its churring call, and Maren realized they had stopped walking an hour ago and neither of them had suggested making camp, because both of them understood that whatever came next needed stillness, needed dusk, needed the particular honesty that only arrives when you can't quite see each other's faces anymore.

"Maren," Edmund said. "When I tell you this, you have to remember that I haven't done anything. Yet. That's important. I need you to hear the whole thing before your training takes over."

"My training isn't going to take over."

"Your training has been taking over since you were nine years old. It's a very well-trained training." He took a breath. "There's a god waiting for me at the end of this road."

She had known, of course. Some part of her had known since the ash on his windowsill, since the dream he'd described with his voice carefully level, since the orchard he'd recognized like a face. Knowing a thing in the abstract and hearing it spoken in the dark by the man you love are different countries, and she felt herself cross the border now, everything familiar dropping away behind her.

"His name is Ashenveil," Edmund went on, when she didn't speak. "He was the god of Osselmark. Of my grandmother's country. When the Choir banished the gods, most of them scattered or starved or went quiet. He didn't. He stayed at the border of the world, at the burned orchard, and he waited. Fifteen years ago something touched me—I'll tell you that story someday, the whole of it, I promise—but the short version is that I'm a door. My blood can open the way back. And Ashenveil has been... patient. Kind, even. He speaks like someone tired, Maren. Like someone who has been alone so long that kindness is the only language he has left. And he made me an offer."

"What offer?"

"He'll dissolve my debt. All fifteen years of it. Every ledger line, every charm, every hour I've sold to the Choir since I was nineteen years old. Freedom, clean and total. In exchange, I open the door. I let him come home through me."

The nightjar stopped. The wheat kept moving. Maren found that her hands had gone cold in her lap, and that her mind—the beautiful, treacherous, well-trained instrument of it—was already drafting the report. *Subject Hartwell, E., confirmed door, in contact with residual divinity, en route to—* She watched the report assemble itself in her head with the detachment of a woman watching a machine run, and she was horrified, and she let the machine run, because she wanted to see how far it would go before she stopped it.

It didn't stop on its own. She stopped it. She reached into the mechanism of herself and put her hand on the gears and held them still, and it cost her something physical—a shudder, a catch of breath—that Edmund saw, because he saw everything.

"You're composing the warrant," he said. Not accusing. Just seeing.

"Yes."

"And?"

"And I'm putting it down." She turned to face him. His face was barely visible now, a pale shape against the darker dark of the wheat, but she looked at it anyway, hard, memorizing it. "Do you understand what you just did? You handed a cantor of the Hollow Choir the exact confession that gets people sealed. You gave me Odile Ferrin. You gave me yourself, gift-wrapped, and you trusted me to hold it. That's either the bravest thing anyone has ever done in front of me or the stupidest, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Both," Edmund said. "Probably both."

"Why?" And her voice cracked on it, finally, the composure going the way of the blisters, the duty, the twenty years. "Why would you trust me with that? After the binding rite—after I searched you and lied about what I found—you owe me nothing. You should hate me."

"I tried to." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said true things. "On the road, the first night, while you slept. I got as far as hating you for about an hour and then I remembered you cutting the redaction off my name in the archive, and I remembered your face when you flinched during the rite, and I couldn't make the hate hold. It kept collapsing into the other thing." He paused. "Also, you're the only person alive who knows what I am and hasn't run. That buys a lot of credit."

"I should run."

"Should you?"

"I should drag you back to Vessing in chains and be promoted for it." She heard herself say it and heard, underneath it, the truth: *I would rather be sealed than do it.* The words wouldn't come out in that order, so she said the next best thing. "Edmund. If you open that door—if you let him through—what happens to the world? To Vessing? To the people whose prayers start getting answered by something with a god's appetite? Have you asked him that?"

"I've asked him everything. He answers everything. That's what frightens me. Quillon never answers anything; the Choir never answers anything; you lot deal exclusively in silence and redaction. And here's this creature at the edge of the world answering every question I've ever had, patiently, kindly, forever." Edmund pulled his knees up, wrapped his arms around them, made himself small in a way she'd never seen him do. "And I still don't know if kind is the same as good. But Maren—I know what the alternative is. I've lived the alternative for fifteen years. The alternative is ledgers. The alternative is Odile Ferrin thanking her wardens. The alternative is a whole country erased from every map so thoroughly that even its gods have to wait at the border like beggars."

The full dark had come down now. The first stars were out, indifferent and ancient, and Maren thought: *they were over Osselmark too, once. Nothing erased the stars.*

"If I help you," she said slowly, testing each word like a rotten floorboard, "I'm not a cantor anymore. There's no coming back from this. Not a demotion, not a penance posting. If the Choir learns what I know and what I've chosen, they will seal me, Edmund, and I will stand there empty and grateful, and everything I am—all of it, the archives, the rites, the twenty years—will just be a body they keep politely running."

"I know."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm not asking." He turned to her fully. "That's the thing I need you to understand. I'm not asking you to help me. I'm telling you what's true because you deserve truth more than I deserve safety, and because—" his voice did something complicated— "because if I walk into that orchard carrying secrets from you the way the Choir carries secrets from everyone, then I've become them. I'd rather lose the door than become them. So: no bargain, no plea. Just the truth, laid on a milestone, in the dark, between two criminals. Take it or leave it."

Maren sat with it. She sat with it the way she'd been trained to sit with difficult texts—at length, in silence, letting the difficult parts surface on their own. And what surfaced, eventually, was not fear. It was anger, white-hot and clarifying, at fifteen years of a church that had taught her that truth was a controlled substance, rationed by office, redacted by rank. The Choir had made her a custodian of silence. This tired, frightened scribe on a broken milestone had just handed her the whole unredacted page and asked for nothing.

*That,* she thought, *is the difference. That is the entire difference.*

"Ashenveil offered you freedom," she said at last. "What does he think freedom costs?"

Edmund was quiet for a moment too long.

"Edmund. What does he think it costs?"

"He says the door opens only once," Edmund said. "And whoever opens it has to pass through. He can't hold it from either side. The opener goes through, or it stays shut." He said it fast, flat, the way you read a ledger entry you don't want read aloud. "I found that out after we'd already run. I was going to tell you. I'm telling you now."

Maren stared at the place where his face was. The nightjar started up again somewhere, absurdly, and the wheat went on moving in the dark like a congregation that refused to disperse, and she understood suddenly, completely, why he had told her the truth tonight. Not for absolution. For witness. He was going to walk into a burned orchard and step through a door into a dead country, alone, and he wanted one person in the living world to know exactly what he was choosing and why—one honest record, one unredacted page, so that somewhere, afterward, his name would exist outside of ledgers.

"No," she said.

"Maren—"

"*No.* You don't get to hand me the truth like a funeral and call it a leap of faith. That's not faith, that's a will and testament." She was on her feet without deciding to be, her ruined boots in the wheat, her voice shaking. "You want a witness? Fine. I'm your witness. But witnesses don't stand at the back of the room, Edmund. Witnesses go where the thing happens. So you don't get to decide alone anymore. That ended tonight, on this milestone, when you told me. Whatever's at that orchard—whatever he offers, whatever it costs—we weigh it together or not at all."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, in the dark, she heard him laugh—a small, cracked, astonished sound, the sound of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"All right," he said. "Together."

"Together," she said. "Now get up. We're losing the night, and I refuse to arrive at a god's front door looking like I wasn't serious."

She reached down and pulled him to his feet, and they stood there a moment in the wheat, hand in hand, two small criminal shapes under the enormous indifferent stars—and neither of them saw, three fields back along the road they'd traveled, a lantern pause, consider, and go dark.
