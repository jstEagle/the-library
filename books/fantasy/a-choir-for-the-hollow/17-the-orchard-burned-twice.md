## 17. The Orchard Burned Twice

The road stopped pretending to be a road two miles before the orchard.

It narrowed to a cart-track, then to a footpath, then to a suggestion of packed earth threading through wheat that grew taller than Maren's shoulders. Nobody had harvested here in years—maybe decades. The grain had gone feral, seeding itself season after season into a golden wilderness that hissed and bowed around them as they walked, so that from any distance they would have been invisible. Edmund found the thought comforting. He'd been visible his whole life: to ledgers, to enforcers, to the small arithmetic of debt. Two days of walking north out of Vessing with a stolen cantor's seal in his pocket, and invisibility felt like wealth.

"You're limping," Maren said.

"I'm favoring my left foot, which is different."

"It's the same thing with worse vocabulary." She stopped, shifted her pack, and looked back the way they'd come. The pilgrim road lay behind them like a dropped thread. Somewhere on it, presumably, were men who smelled divine residue and carried sealing irons. "How far?"

"Past the next rise." He didn't know how he knew. That was the part he'd stopped arguing with somewhere around Grayford. Since the night ash had spelled his name on his windowsill, certain knowledge simply arrived in him, unbidden and unearned, the way other people knew which hand was theirs. *Past the rise. Past the burned orchard. The door is near.* "There's a well first. Or there was."

"There was?"

"In the dream it was dry. In the dream everything was dry except me."

Maren's face did the thing it did when she wanted to ask something and decided the asking could wait until evening, when they'd have firelight to hide behind. She'd gotten very good at deferring questions on this journey. So had he. It was a strange kind of intimacy, this mutual postponement—the sense that they were both carrying letters neither dared open yet.

They crested the rise at late afternoon, when the light had gone long and amber, and Edmund stopped walking so abruptly that Maren walked into his back.

Below them, filling a shallow valley like a held breath, lay the orchard.

*Burned twice,* Ashenveil had said, or the dream had said, or memory had said—he no longer separated the sources. And it was true even now, even after however many years of weather. You could see both burnings written in the trees. The older fire had taken the trunks low and clean, leaving black columns shoulder-high, their tops snapped or rotted away. The newer fire—recent, within the last few years by the look of it—had swept through the undergrowth between them, scorching the ground black in patches that rain had begun to soften at the edges. Between the dead columns, new growth stood waist-high: suckers and shoots rising green from roots that had refused to die.

"It's growing back," Maren said quietly.

"The Choir burns it every spring," Edmund heard himself say. Again that borrowed knowledge, arriving like a letter addressed to someone else and opened anyway. "There's a warden detail out of Grayford. They ride out, they burn the shoots, they ride back. They've done it for fifteen years. Nobody remembers why anymore. It's just what's done."

"Fifteen years." Maren's voice had gone careful. "Since the banishment."

"Since the last time something tried to come through."

She looked at him then—not at the orchard—and he saw her doing the arithmetic she'd been doing since the archive, since the redacted page, since the name *E. Hartwell* surfaced from beneath fifteen years of official ink. Fifteen years since a boy in Vessing prayed over his dying mother and something answered. Fifteen years since the Choir sealed whatever that boy was and filed the evidence where no cantor was meant to find it.

"We should go down before dark," he said, because if he kept standing there she would keep doing that arithmetic aloud, and he wasn't ready. There were things he still hadn't told her. There was one thing, mostly. One thing shaped like a dying woman's sickroom and a child's voice cracking on a prayer, and he had carried it alone for fifteen years and had not yet learned how to set it down in front of another person without expecting it to be used against him.

"Edmund." Maren touched his sleeve. "Whatever's down there—it called you *here*. In your dream. Before we ever left the city. I want you to tell me if you're afraid of it."

He considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty; she'd traded her whole life for honesty three days ago on the pilgrim road, when she'd turned her back on the Choir and walked north beside a man she'd been sent to audit.

"I'm afraid of it being real," he said. "I've spent fifteen years making sure nothing about me was real enough to matter. Real is what gets people sealed."

"And if it offers you something?"

"Then I'll finally get to find out what I'm worth when I'm not for sale." He smiled, and hated how thin it came out. "Come on. We'll want water before the well goes dry again."

* * *

The well sat at the orchard's heart, exactly where the dream had put it, and it was not dry.

Edmund knelt at its lip and looked down into darkness that gave back no reflection—or gave back too much of one, a shimmering that moved when he didn't. The air rising out of it smelled of rain on hot stone, of the moment after a storm breaks, and underneath that, faintly, of woodsmoke and apple blossom. A smell that had no business coexisting. A smell from two different years of the same burning.

"Maren," he said. "Don't drink yet."

She was already crouched beside him, cantor-trained, running her fingers along the well's stone rim. Her lips moved slightly—reading, he realized. There were characters cut into the rim, old ones, worn nearly smooth, and where wear had erased them the stone seemed to be remembering them, the grooves re-deepening under her fingertips like scars fading in reverse.

"This is Ossene script," she said. "Pre-banishment. I've only seen it in plates in the restricted archive." Her finger traced a line of it. "This says—hmm. It's a dedication. 'To the god who waits at the edge of the field.'" She paused. "That's not one of the sanctioned names."

"Ashenveil."

"You know it already."

"I know it the way you know a word you've heard through a wall your whole life. Never clearly. Just the shape of it." He pulled back from the well and sat on his heels. The feeling had been building since the rise—that vertiginous doubling, the sense of standing in two places at once, in the present valley and in the dream-valley, in his own body and in some older body that had knelt at this same stone. "My grandmother sang songs in a language she told me was just nonsense she'd made up. Lullabies. I thought she was an odd old woman who liked gibberish. She was singing Ossene, wasn't she. All those years, in a country that had erased hers from every map, she was keeping the language alive in the only place they couldn't confiscate it—in a child's bedtime."

Maren was quiet a moment. "The Choir never banned lullabies," she said. "We banned scripture, rites, shrines, names. We were very thorough about everything adults might take seriously." Something moved across her face—shame, or its cousin. "Children were beneath our notice. That was the flaw in the whole design, wasn't it. You can outlaw a faith, but you can't outlaw a grandmother."

"No," Edmund said. "You can't."

He rose and walked out among the burned trees.

The wheat ended at the orchard's edge as though at a border—no fence, no marker, just a line beyond which the grass went short and silver-grey, feeding on ash. Inside, the ground was soft underfoot, spongy with fifteen years of leaf-fall and fire-death composting together. The black columns rose around him, hundreds of them, regular as a planted thing should be, and between each pair the green shoots stood at knee height, trembling slightly in a wind he couldn't feel on his own face.

Ashenveil was here.

Not visibly. Not as the grey figure of the dream, singing in the smoke. But the presence thickened the way weather thickens before a storm—you couldn't point to it, but your joints knew, your teeth knew, the fine hairs on your arms knew. Edmund stopped in the middle of the orchard, equidistant from everything, and let it press against him like deep water.

*You came.*

"I came." His voice sounded strange out loud, flat against all that listening dark. "You could have asked, you know. Instead of haunting my windowsill."

*Would you have come if I'd asked?*

He thought about it. Really thought, standing among the dead trees with the woman he loved fifty feet behind him pretending to study a well.

"No," he admitted. "I'd have found a reason. I'm very good at reasons. Fifteen years of practice."

*So I did not ask. Doors are not summoned, Edmund Hartwell. Doors open. Or they do not.* The voice was everywhere and nowhere, in the rustle of the shoots, in the well-water's smell, in the space behind his sternum where his heartbeat had always lived and now apparently shared lodgings. *I have waited a long time. I can wait while you decide. But not indefinitely. The Choir is coming. They always come. It is the one hymn they still remember.*

"They're coming because of me."

*They are coming because of what you are. It is not the same thing, though they have spent fifteen years teaching their people otherwise.* A pause, and when the voice resumed it had shifted register—lower, wearier, almost gentle. *You are afraid I will make you into a weapon. Or a gate they break through. Or a martyr. You have imagined every shape this could take except the true one.*

"Which is?"

*That I am tired, and you are tired, and tired things recognize each other.* The shoots around him stirred. *I do not want worship, Edmund. Worship is what got us all here—mine as much as theirs. I want to go home. And home, it turns out, has a door, and the door has your family's name on it, and I cannot turn the handle. Only you can. That is the entire sum of what I want from you. Less than the Choir wants. They want you sealed. I only want you willing.*

Edmund stood very still. Behind him, faintly, he heard Maren's footsteps stop, heard her breath catch—she could feel it too now, the pressure of the presence, the way the air had gone taut as a drumhead. She was a cantor; she'd trained her whole life in the discipline of empty heavens, in singing confidently at nothing. She had never once stood in a place where something was actually listening.

"Edmund?" she called. Careful. Steady. Cantor-steady, the voice they taught you to use during audits and interrogations, and he loved her for using it gently.

"Give me a minute," he said. Then, lower, to the dark between the trees: "If I open it—if I even consider opening it—I need to know something first. The full price. Not the version you told me in the dream. The version you'd tell me if you weren't afraid I'd run."

Silence. Long enough that he thought the presence had withdrawn. Then:

*You have changed,* Ashenveil said, and there was something in the voice that might, in anything else, have been respect. *Very well. Come to the shrine when the light fails. Bring your cantor, if she will come. What I tell you, she should hear too—because it concerns her, more than either of you yet understands.*

"What does that mean?"

But the pressure was lifting, draining out of the air the way heat leaves a stone at dusk, and the orchard was just an orchard again—burned, half-alive, smelling of rain that hadn't fallen. The shoots hung motionless. The well murmured to itself.

Maren reached him as the last direct light slid off the valley rim. She took his hand without ceremony, the way she'd started doing somewhere past Grayford, as if they'd always done it and the previous thirty-odd years had merely been a clerical error. Her fingers were cold. Cantors ran cold; he'd teased her about it once, in another life, over tea in a scriptorium that now felt like a portrait of people who had never existed.

"It spoke to you," she said.

"It spoke. Yes."

"What did it want?"

"That's the problem." He looked up through the black columns at the sky going violet between them. "It wants less than anyone's ever wanted from me. And somehow that's more frightening than the alternative." He turned back toward the well, where the shrine entrance waited—because of course there was a shrine entrance; the dream had shown him that too, steps descending beneath the well-house, down into the earth where Osselmark had kept its last standing holy place, the one the fires had never quite reached because you cannot burn what is underground. "There's something it wants to tell us. Both of us. At the shrine. When the light fails."

Maren followed his gaze to the well-house—a low stone structure, roofless, its walls furred with moss, and beneath it the suggestion of stairs going down into a dark that was not entirely dark. Even from here, even in the failing light, he could see that the darkness below had texture. Depth. A waiting quality.

"Edmund," Maren said slowly, "the light fails in about twenty minutes."

"I know."

"And you're telling me a god wants to talk to us in an underground shrine tonight."

"I'm telling you what it told me. You're free to draw your own conclusions. You're a cantor; conclusions are supposed to be your whole profession."

"Don't joke." But her grip on his hand tightened rather than loosened, and when he glanced at her, her expression wasn't fear. It was something rawer and more complicated—the look of a woman standing at the edge of the doctrine she'd built her life on, watching it end at a line of silver grass. "Fifteen years I sang to an empty heaven, Edmund. Fifteen years I made myself believe the emptiness was the point—that faith without answer was the purest form. And now I'm standing in a field where something answers, and I don't—" Her voice caught. She steadied it with visible effort. "I don't know how to pray anymore. I don't know if I ever knew, or if I only knew how to perform."

"Then don't pray," Edmund said. "Just walk down the stairs with me. Whatever's down there, it gets both of us or neither."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, half disbelief. "*Both of us or neither.* When did you become the brave one?"

"About three days ago, when you committed treason for me. Bravery's contagious. Like most bad habits."

They gathered their packs from the well's rim. The sun touched the western ridge of the valley, and the orchard's shadows swung around like compass needles finding north, all of them pointing, Edmund noticed, toward the well-house. Toward the stairs. Toward the door.

As they crossed the silver grass, the first shoot they passed brushed against Edmund's ankle—and he felt it, distinctly, unmistakably, like a hand.

Small. Green. Patient.

Reaching.

* * *

The stairs were steeper than the dream had shown, or perhaps dreams are merciful editors. Forty-one steps, counting them was automatic, a scribe's tic, and each one was cut from stone that grew warmer underfoot as they descended, which was wrong, which was entirely wrong, cellars are cold, the earth is cold, but this stone remembered fire the way the orchard above remembered it—twice over, and had survived both times.

At the bottom, the dark opened.

Edmund had expected a crypt. What he found was a room built to hold a congregation—low-vaulted, wide, its walls flowing into benches carved from the living rock, and at the far end an apse where a single lamp burned. Not oil. He could see that even from the stairs. The flame sat in a stone dish and fed on nothing, and it had, by its own waxless persistence, been burning since before the banishment, since before the fires, since before a boy in Vessing knelt beside his mother's bed and broke the world open with a wish.

The walls were painted. Faded, smoke-darkened, but legible: figures dancing in orchards, figures pouring wine at long tables, figures standing in a doorway of white light with their arms open. And above the apse, rendered larger than all the rest, a grey figure whose edges dissolved into birdsong—into painted birds, dozens of them, flying out of its shoulders and hands and hair as though the figure were not a person at all but a season, a weather, a thing the sky did once a year.

"Ashenveil," Maren whispered. She'd gone pale. Not frightened-pale. Recognizing-pale. "The sanctioned texts call it the Grey Heresy. The god of unanswered prayers. They taught us it was a corruption—a thing that preyed on desperate people, that promised miracles and delivered ruin." Her eyes moved across the paintings: the dancing, the feasting, the open arms. "That's not what this looks like. This looks like harvest."

"It looks like my grandmother's kitchen," Edmund said quietly. "Every winter festival of my childhood. I thought those were just her traditions. Her oddness." He stepped forward, and the lamp-flame leaned toward him like a plant toward sun. "They didn't erase a heresy, Maren. They erased a country's ordinary joy and called the erasure holiness."

Behind the apse, the wall was different.

He felt it before he saw it—a change in air pressure, a faint cool draft, the sensation of standing near a window in winter. The stones there were fitted differently, tighter, and between them ran a seam so fine it might have been drawn in ink. A seam in the shape of a door. Tall, narrow, human-proportioned. Proportioned, he realized with a lurch of vertigo, precisely to *him*—to his height, his breadth, the exact span of his shoulders, as though the wall had measured him from across fifteen years and four hundred miles and cut itself accordingly.

The lamp flared.

And the grey figure above the apse opened its painted eyes.

*Sit,* said Ashenveil, from everywhere, from the warm stone, from the flame, from the seam in the wall that fit Edmund like a keyhole fits a key. *Both of you. What I have to tell you is short. But it will cost you everything you think tomorrow is going to be.*

Maren's hand found Edmund's in the dark. Her grip said: *whatever comes, we hear it together.*

They sat on the carved bench, side by side, in a shrine that should have been rubble, beneath an orchard that refused to stay dead, and the god who waits at the edge of the field began—at last, after fifteen years of silence—to name its price.
