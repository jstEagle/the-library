## 18. Hunters in the Wheat

Brother Quillon had been a warden for twenty-two years, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the others: mercy was a discipline, not a feeling.

He practiced it now, riding at the head of the column through the wheat country south of Vessing, while behind him six wardens kept their horses to a walk and their voices to nothing. Discipline. The fugitives ahead of them—a scribe and a cantor, of all the improbable pairings—had three days' lead, maybe four. A lesser man would have pushed the pace and scattered his riders across half a province chasing shadows. Quillon did not chase shadows. He let the shadows come to him.

Beside him, on a dun mare that looked too small for her, rode the tracker.

Her name was Ilsa Venn, and she was not Choir. She was one of the border-folk, a hedge-witch's daughter gone respectable the way a knife goes into a sheath—still sharp underneath, just easier to carry. The Choir licensed perhaps a dozen of her kind: sensitives who could smell what ordinary noses could not. Quillon had worked with two others over the years and had found them both half-mad and wholly useful. Venn was different. She was quiet the way deep water is quiet, and when she looked at you, you felt catalogued.

"Tell me again," he said, "what you're smelling."

She didn't answer at once. She'd turned her face into the wind, eyes half-closed, nostrils flaring like a woman deciding whether a stew had spoiled. The wheat around them moved in long silver waves, and somewhere far off a lark was making an idiot of itself against the sky. It was, Quillon thought, obscenely beautiful country for what they were doing.

"Bread," Venn said finally. "Old bread, baked and gone stale. And under it—" her mouth twisted, "—under it something sweeter. Like honey left out for a season. That's the residue. That's *him*."

"The scribe."

"The door." She corrected him without heat, the way you'd correct a child who'd called a wolf a dog. "Hartwell is just the name it wears. What I'm following is older than his name. It's been leaking out of him for weeks, maybe months. Every charm he ever sold that actually worked—that was it, seeping through the cracks." She glanced sideways at Quillon. "You've smelled it too. Don't pretend you haven't. Everyone in Vessing has. That's why the bread rose. That's why the wells filled. He's not the cause of everything, but he's the crack everything's pouring through."

Quillon let that sit. He was a patient man; patience had gotten him further in the Choir than piety or swordwork ever had. "And the woman? Solle?"

Venn shook her head. "Cantor-trained. They teach them to seal themselves tight—you can't read a cantor unless they want to be read. But she's traveling with him, sleeping near him, and no seal holds forever. By now she smells like him." A pause. "By now she probably *is* him, a little. That's how it works with doors. They don't knock. They just leave doors open behind them, and people walk through without knowing."

"You talk about him like a plague."

"I talk about him like weather," Venn said. "Plague you can hide from. Weather you can only endure or redirect." She lifted her chin toward the horizon, where the wheat gave way to a dark line of hills. "He's turning southwest. Off the pilgrim road. There's nothing southwest but ruins."

"There's Osselmark," Quillon said.

The name dropped between them like a stone into a well. Even Venn, who believed in nothing the Choir didn't license, went still.

"No," she said. "There's where Osselmark was. Different thing entirely."

"Not to him," Quillon said. "His grandmother came from there. We checked the parish rolls before we ever left Vessing. Hartwell's line goes back to the border villages—the ones that were Osselmarki right up until they weren't." He watched the hills. "Doors run in blood. You told me that yourself."

"I did." Venn's voice had gone flat. "Which means his grandmother might have been one. And hers. Five generations of hinges, Brother Quillon, and every one of them sealed by men like you, and every sealing leaves scar tissue, and scar tissue—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening. "It doesn't matter. Scar tissue or not, he's heading for the last standing shrine, and if he reaches it unbound, we won't be talking about weather anymore. We'll be talking about flood."

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below a ruined tollhouse, and Quillon walked the perimeter himself because he always did, because a warden who delegated vigilance deserved whatever found him asleep.

The wardens were good men. That was the thing people outside never understood, the thing Maren Solle's superiors would never admit even to themselves: the Choir's wardens were not brutes or zealots or careerists, or at least not mostly. They were farmers' sons and clerks' daughters who had taken vows against the dark because someone had to, back when the dark was real enough to need vowing against. Warden Pell, youngest of the six, sat sharpening a knife he would probably never use, humming a hymn under his breath—the old one, the Hollow Hymn, the one sung to an empty heaven. He hummed it the way you'd hum a lullaby your mother sang, without any thought about whether anyone was listening on the other end.

"Pell," Quillon said.

The young man looked up. In the firelight his face was open, earnest, faintly embarrassed at being caught humming. "Brother."

"What do you think happens if we fail?"

Pell considered it honestly, which Quillon appreciated. Most men would have given the pious answer. Pell gave the true one. "I don't know, Brother. I've heard the stories from the border villages. Wells that whisper. Bread that rises with nobody kneading it. My aunt says her neighbor's cow gave milk that glowed." He laughed a little, uncertainly. "Sounds mad when I say it out loud."

"It isn't mad. It's happening." Quillon crouched by the fire. "Here's what happens if we fail. At first, nothing. Miracles are pleasant. People love being blessed. Then the blessings start choosing sides—one village favored, the next passed over—and neighbors start asking why the god loves them less. Then the god starts asking things back. Gods always do. That's not cynicism, Pell, that's history. We have fifteen years of empty heaven because fifteen years ago heaven was full, and full heavens make demands, and the demands get paid in blood, and the ones who pay first are always the small folk who never asked for a god at all." He held the younger man's eyes. "We aren't hunting a scribe and his lover. We're hunting the first day of the rest of everyone's life. If we bring him in quietly, bound and sealed, most people will never know how close it came. That's the mercy. Not for him—for them."

Pell nodded slowly. "And for him?"

"For him," Quillon said, "we offer the rite. Painless. Dreamless. He'll feel nothing and remember nothing and be, at the end of it, a man with no debt and no door and no god leaning on his shoulder in the night. Some of the sealed have called it peace." He stood. "Some of them were lying. But we offer it all the same, because we are not monsters, Pell. Whatever they say about us in the night markets. We are not monsters."

Pell said, "No, Brother," and went back to his knife, and Quillon walked on along the perimeter believing every word he'd said, which was the trick of it, the whole trick of a life like his: you built the creed sentence by sentence until it held your weight, and then you stood on it, and it held, and you never once looked down at what it was standing on.

* * *

On the third morning, Venn found the shrine road.

She did it the way she did everything—without announcement. Quillon woke to find her already saddled, standing at the edge of the camp staring southwest with an expression he had not seen on her before, and it took him a moment to name it, because he had never seen fear on Ilsa Venn and hadn't expected to start now.

"It changed in the night," she said, when he reached her.

"What changed?"

"The smell." Her hands were steady but her voice wasn't, quite. "For three days it's been a trail—bread and honey, getting fresher as we closed. Last night it stopped being a trail." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were very wide and very calm, the calm of a woman standing on a beach watching the water pull back from the shore. "Now it's everywhere. The whole wind smells of him. As if he's not moving anymore. As if he's—" she searched for it, "—*arriving*. Like the smell isn't coming off him anymore. Like it's coming *toward* him. From the other side."

Quillon felt the hair rise on his arms and hated it. Twenty-two years, and he could count on one hand the times his body had known something before his mind did. This was one. "How far?"

"A day. Maybe less. The old shrine in the burned orchard—it has to be. There's nothing else out there worth arriving at." Venn swung up onto her mare. "Brother Quillon. I want to say something, and I want you to hear it as professional counsel and not as a hedge-witch's superstition, because it's both."

"Say it."

"When we take him—if we take him—do it fast. No speeches, no rites in the field, no giving him time to speak to it or it to speak through him. Bind him, blindfold him, and ride. Because a door that knows you're coming—" she paused, and finished flatly, "—a door that knows you're coming is already open a crack, and things come through cracks."

"We'll do it by the book," Quillon said. "The book exists so that fear doesn't make our decisions."

"Yes, Brother," Venn said, in the tone of a woman agreeing with a man standing on train tracks, and said nothing more.

* * *

They found the first sign at midday: a shepherd's hut, abandoned, its door swinging. Inside, the hearth was cold but the bread box was full—six loaves, fresh, warm, no oven lit within a mile. Warden Pell touched one and snatched his hand back as if scalded, though it was only warm.

"Leave it," Quillon said. "Mark the spot. We move."

They found the second sign an hour later, and this one Quillon did not order marked, because there was nothing to mark. A field of wheat, a mile wide, and running through the middle of it a path where the grain bent away from the walkers—not trampled, *bent*, stalks curving aside in smooth arcs, as though the wheat itself had leaned back to let two travelers pass without touching them. The path ran straight as a ruled line toward the hills.

Venn dismounted and knelt in it. She pressed her palm flat to the bent stalks and stayed there a long moment, and when she rose her face had gone the color of tallow.

"It thanked them," she said.

"What?"

"The field. Whatever moved it. It wasn't afraid of them, it was—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "Residue reads as emotion, Brother. Fear, hunger, longing—that's what gods smell like, mostly. Longing. This doesn't read as longing. It reads as *welcome*. Like a house that's been swept for guests." She looked down the silver path toward the hills. "Fifteen years I've tracked residue for the Choir. I have never once smelled welcome."

"Then it's a trap," Quillon said. "Whatever waits for him wants him there. Which means whatever waits for him has plans, and plans can be broken. Mount up. We ride through the night."

"Brother." Pell, hesitating at the edge of the path, his horse refusing it, backing and tossing its head. "The horses won't go in there."

"Then we go on foot," Quillon said, and stepped into the wheat himself, because a warden who asked his men to walk where he would not was no warden at all, and because the creed needed standing on, and because—though he would have cut out his tongue before saying it aloud—some part of him wanted to feel what Venn felt. Wanted proof. Wanted, just once, to stand close enough to the divine to know it was real, even as an enemy.

The wheat bent away from him too. It felt like walking through a crowd that had decided, silently and all at once, to make room.

He did not tell the others that. He filed it away in the place where he kept everything that did not fit the creed, a room in himself he visited rarely and left quickly, and he set the pace hard, and by dusk the burned orchard stood black against the last light, and the smell of bread and honey was so thick it was nearly a taste.

* * *

They came to the orchard wall as the stars came out.

The trees were dead—had been dead for fifteen years, since the burning—but as the wardens crouched in the ditch beyond the wall, Pell made a small strangled sound and gripped Quillon's sleeve. Quillon followed the boy's pointing hand and saw it, and felt the floor of his creed shift beneath him by one inch, no more, but an inch is enough.

Blossoms. On the dead trees, white blossoms, opening as they watched, slow as a held breath released. Out of season. Out of life. Out of anything the book accounted for.

"That's not possible," Pell whispered.

"Nothing that's happened this week is possible," Venn murmured. "That's rather the point of it."

Quillon raised his hand for silence and studied the shrine beyond the trees—a low stone building, roof fallen in, walls furred with moss. Faint light moved inside it. Not torchlight. Something warmer, and steadier, and wrong.

Six wardens, one tracker, himself. Against a scribe, a cantor, and a god.

He had led worse odds into better causes. He began assigning positions in a voice barely above breath, and the men moved to their places with the discipline he had drilled into them for years, and it was going exactly as the book said it should, and that was when Venn caught his arm again.

"Listen," she breathed.

He listened. Beneath the wind, beneath the creak of blossoming branches, there was singing. Two voices, low and intertwined—a man's and a woman's, rising from the shrine, and Quillon knew neither song nor words, and yet some cellar-deep part of him knew the tune, knew it the way you know a lullaby, and his chest ached with a homesickness for a place he had never been.

"They're not hiding," he said slowly. "They know we're here."

"Yes."

"And they're singing."

"Yes." Venn's grip on his arm tightened. "Brother Quillon, I'm going to say the thing you hired me never to say. Turn us around. Whatever's in there, it isn't a fugitive cornered at a shrine. It's a homecoming. And I have seen what happens to men who interrupt a god's homecoming, and it is in none of your books, because none of them survived to write it down."

Quillon looked at the blossom-light spilling through the ruined doorway. He thought of Pell humming the Hollow Hymn to an empty heaven. He thought of the sealed, and whether they had truly been at peace, and of the inch-wide crack beneath his creed, and of the wheat bending aside like a crowd making room.

"We hold position," he said. "We wait for dawn. And then, Venn, we do our duty—quietly, cleanly, and with mercy. Whatever sings in there, it is singing to a man we are sworn to protect from it. That is the whole of the law, and I have kept it for twenty-two years, and I will keep it tomorrow."

Venn withdrew her hand. In the dark, she said, very softly, "And if the law is what's singing?"

Quillon did not answer, because in that moment the singing stopped—

and a third voice joined the silence, vast and grey and patient as winter, and it spoke one word into the minds of every soul crouched in the ditch,

and the word was *come*,

and it was not speaking to them.
