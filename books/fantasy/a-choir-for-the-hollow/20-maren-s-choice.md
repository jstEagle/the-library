## 20. Maren's Choice

Maren woke before dawn to the sound of Edmund packing.

It was not a loud sound. That was the thing about Edmund—fifteen years in the scriptorium had taught him to move like ink drying, quiet and irreversible. But she had spent three nights sleeping beside him on the floor of the shrine's undercroft, and she had learned the grammar of his silences. The way he folded a shirt told her more than a confession. The way he squared the corners of his bundle told her everything.

He was leaving. And he was leaving alone.

She sat up in the dark. The undercroft was cold enough that her breath showed, and the walls of names rose around them on every side—Hartwell, Hartwell, Hartwell, five generations of them carved in a script older than the Choir, older than the banishment, older than the country that no longer appeared on maps. Above them, faint through the ruined shrine, the presence of Ashenveil pressed down like weather. Not a voice. Not yet. A pressure, the way the air feels before a hymn begins.

"Edmund." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Sit down."

He didn't stop folding. "It's early. You should sleep."

"Sit down, or I swear by every rite I ever swore by, I will bind you to that floor and sing the unbinding so slowly you'll feel every knot."

He sat. That was the thing about Edmund too—he knew when she meant it.

* * *

She had known, she realized, for two days. She had known and had done what cantors do best: she had filed it. She had taken the knowledge—the way he'd gone silent after Ashenveil named the price, the way he'd stopped saying *when we cross* and started saying *when I cross*, the way he'd begun teaching her, patiently, casually, the words of the opening hymn, as if rehearsing her for a part in a play whose ending he'd already written—she had taken all of it and shelved it where cantors shelve the things that would break them if they looked at them straight.

She was a cantor of the Hollow Choir. She had spent eleven years learning to hold a faith that had outlawed its own gods, to sing hymns to an empty heaven and call the emptiness reverence. She knew how to not-look. It was the first thing they taught you in induction: the great hymn, sung to nothing, and you pretend it is enough, and then you pretend the pretending is enough, and somewhere in the layers of pretense you find something solid enough to stand on.

But she was not in the choir loft now. She was on a cold floor in a dead country, in love with a man who had decided her life for her, and the shelving had collapsed.

"When were you going to tell me?" she asked.

Edmund's hands went still on his bundle. In the grey pre-dawn light filtering down through the broken shrine above, his face was a study in careful blankness—the same face he wore across a ledger, the same face he'd worn under her own binding rite when he'd let her search him and find nothing. She hated that face. She had kissed that face. She had fled her whole life for that face.

"Tell you what," he said.

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack. Let him hear it. "Don't do the scribe's trick. Don't make me say the whole sentence so you can stand there and look innocent of it. *When were you going to tell me that you're going to open the door and walk through it alone and send me back?* When, Edmund? At the threshold? After? Were you going to write me a letter?"

He said nothing. The nothing was an answer.

Maren stood up because sitting near him had become unbearable, and she paced the narrow aisle between the walls of names. Her footsteps echoed off the carved syllables. Hartwell. Hartwell. Hartwell. She stopped in front of one of them—a woman's name, Sefa Hartwell, with a date from a century the maps had already begun to forget—and pressed her palm flat against it, as if the stone could lend her its patience.

"Do you know what I did to get here?" she said, not turning around. "I confessed doubts to the High Cantor. I let her put a binding rite in my hands and point it at the man I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I used the rites they gave me to hunt you, and then I burned my own credentials to lie for you at Grayford. I am a criminal now. Do you understand that? Not in the romantic way. In the *Quillon will find us and there is no clemency for cantors who defect* way. I have no Choir to go back to. I have no vespers, no office, no stipend, no name in the rolls. I traded all of it. And I would trade it again, that's the obscene part, I would trade it every morning for the rest of my life, and you—" Her breath hitched. "You decided I don't get a vote."

"Maren—"

"You're going to tell me it's the only way." She turned to face him. "You're going to tell me the door opens once, and whoever opens it must pass through, and you've thought about it, and this is the arithmetic. I know. I was there when the god said it. I heard it too. And here is what I've noticed about your arithmetic, Edmund Hartwell, scribe, door, *liar*: it always ends with you alone and everyone else safe. Your mother's death. Your fifteen years of debt. The charms you sold in the night market for people too poor to pray. Every sum you've ever run, the remainder is always you, alone, paying. You've been balancing your whole life toward this."

"That isn't fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. The god isn't fair, the door isn't fair, the Choir that sealed your grandmother and your mother and everyone with your blood into walls of names isn't fair. Fairness left this country before we were born. So don't stand there and tell me about fairness. Tell me about *us*."

The word landed between them like a dropped coin.

"There is no *us*," Edmund said softly, "if you're dead."

"And there's no *me* if you're gone!" It came out of her at a volume that sent echoes skittering down the corridor of names, and she didn't care, she wanted the dead Hartwells to hear it, she wanted Ashenveil to hear it, she wanted the whole hollow architecture of the world to hear it. "Do you think I came here for the theology? Do you think I burned my life for a door? I came here for *you*. For the man who makes tea wrong and apologizes to it. For the man who sold a charm to a weeping mother at midnight and couldn't look at me the next day because it had worked. For fifteen years of ledger discrepancies I chose not to report because looking at your handwriting was the best part of my week. You are not allowed to spend me like coin, Edmund. You are not allowed to decide that my love is a debt you can settle by leaving."

He was on his feet now. She hadn't seen him rise; he moved like ink drying, even in anger. "And what would you have me do?" His voice was low and shaking. "Take you with me? Through the door? You heard what he said. The door opens once. It holds for the opener and no one else. If I try to bring you, it fails, or it takes you into something neither of us understands, or—" His jaw worked. "Or it takes me and leaves you on the wrong side of a sealed door in a country that hunts people like me. Maren. *Maren.* If I go alone, you live. You go back to Vessing, you take the archive pages, the redactions, everything you found, and you break the Choir open from the inside, and you build something that doesn't seal children into walls. That's not me spending you. That's me—" his voice broke, and he mastered it, the way he mastered everything, and she suddenly understood that this was the most expensive composure he had ever bought— "that's me making sure the only person who ever chose me gets to keep living a life worth the choosing."

"That's not your decision."

"It's the only decision I have left."

"Then it's the wrong one."

"Maren—"

"Because you've done the arithmetic wrong," she said, and now she crossed the aisle to him, and took his face in her hands, and made him look at her. His eyes were wet and furious and terrified, and she loved him so much it felt like a wound. "You've counted the cost of you going through and the cost of you staying and the cost of me coming with you, and you've forgotten to count the cost of what it does to me to be *sent away*. You think sending me back is a gift. Edmund, being left is the one thing I have been afraid of since I was six years old and the Choir took me from my mother's house and told me the hymn was enough. Being left is *the thing*. It is the shape of every bad night I have ever had. And you have decided, alone, in the dark, folding your shirts, that you will hand me the thing. You'll dress it up as safety. You'll dress it up as a mission, break the Choir, rebuild the world. But underneath it, it's the thing. It's everyone who ever left. And I am telling you—" her hands were shaking against his jaw, "—I am telling you that I would rather stand at a door with you and lose you to the crossing than be packed into your bundle like a shirt and left behind *for my own good*."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere above them, through the ruined shrine, the pressure of Ashenveil shifted—she felt it in her teeth, the way you feel a storm front. The god was listening. Of course the god was listening. Gods were made of listening; that was the whole horror of them.

"You're asking me," Edmund said at last, very quietly, "to choose between your life and your feelings."

"I'm asking you to choose *with* me, not *for* me. That's all. That's the entire request. It's the smallest thing anyone has ever asked of you, and it's the only one you've never paid."

* * *

He pulled away from her. Not cruelly—Edmund was incapable of cruelty, which somehow made it worse—and walked to the wall of names, and stood before it with his back to her. She watched his shoulders. She had learned his shoulders the way she had once learned plainsong: line by line, until she could sing them in the dark.

"My grandmother's name is here," he said. "Sefa Hartwell. No—she's further down. This one is my great-aunt." He touched a name. "The Choir sealed them. All of them. Doors, one after another, generation after generation, and every time, someone stood where I'm standing and did the arithmetic and decided that the door was too dangerous to leave open, and the person was too dangerous to leave *whole*. They didn't kill them, you know. I used to think the sealing was a killing. It's worse. They erase the part of you that answers. They leave the body walking around, paying debts, copying ledgers, and the part that could have been a door to something larger is just—gone. Hollow." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "I used to think that was what was wrong with me. The hollowness. I thought I was broken. Fifteen years I thought I was a broken man working off a broken debt, and it turns out I was *full*. Full of a country. Full of a god. Full of every Hartwell who ever got sealed shut."

"Edmund—"

"And you want me to believe," he said, turning now, and his face in the grey light was the face of every Hartwell on the wall, "that if I open the door and walk through it, I'm doing to you what the Choir did to them. Sending you back hollow. And you're right. You're right, and I hate it, and I have been folding shirts at four in the morning for two days because I cannot find a way to be the man who does that to you." His breath shuddered. "But I also cannot find a way to be the man who lets you die in a collapsing doorway in a dead country because I wanted company at the crossing. So. There it is. That's my whole ledger. Two columns, and every entry is terrible, and the sum at the bottom is me, alone, and I am *so tired*, Maren. I am so tired of the sum always being me."

She went to him. She didn't have an argument left; arguments were spent. She put her arms around him and felt him shake, this quiet man, this door, this hinge on which two countries turned, and she held on the way you hold a rope in weather.

"Then let's find a third column," she said into his shoulder. "You're a scribe. You of all people know a ledger is never finished. There's always another line. Find it with me. Not for me. With me."

He didn't answer. But he didn't pull away, and after a while his arms came up around her, and they stood there in the corridor of the sealed and the named, two criminals in a country that didn't exist, holding the only thing either of them had never had to pay for.

* * *

It was full light when the sound came down through the broken shrine.

Maren heard it first—eleven years of vespers had tuned her ear to the wrong kind of silence, and this was the silence that comes *before* boots. She lifted her head from Edmund's shoulder.

"What?"

"Listen."

Wind through the wheat above. Birdsong, then no birdsong. And beneath it, faint and rhythmic, the sound she had hoped never to hear again outside the walls of Vessing: the cadence of the Choir on the march, warden-boots keeping time to a hymn she had sung since childhood, sung now as a hunting call.

Edmund went white. "Quillon."

"He found us." She was already moving, gathering her satchel, her rites, the pages of the archive she had sworn to carry out of this country or die holding. "The tracker. The one who smells divine residue—we've been sleeping inside a god, Edmund, we might as well have been ringing a bell."

Edmund stood very still for one long moment, and she watched him do it—watched the arithmetic run behind his eyes, the terrible familiar ledger, *I could give myself up, they only want the door*—and she grabbed his wrist hard enough to bruise.

"No," she said. "No sums. Not now. We run, we fight, we sing, we do whatever it takes, but we do it *together*, and if you balance this ledger without me I will come back from whatever side of any door to haunt you. Do you hear me? Say it."

The hymn above was getting louder. Dust sifted down through the broken shrine, and in the dust, for the first time, she heard Ashenveil—not words, not yet, but a low gathering tone, like a choir drawing its first breath.

Edmund looked at her, and something in his face shifted, some old knot loosening or tightening, she couldn't tell which.

"Together," he said. "Now run."
