## 21. Skirmish at the Shrine

The wheat told them first.

Edmund had been standing at the orchard's edge for the better part of an hour, watching Maren walk the rows of burned trees with her cantor's satchel open, taking readings he didn't understand and she couldn't explain. The wheat field between them and the pilgrim road moved in a long slow ripple, like a hand drawn through water—and then stopped moving all at once, every stalk stiffening in the same breath.

Birds went up from the far hedgerow. Not scattering. Lifting.

"Maren," he said.

She turned. In the grey light her face was already doing the arithmetic—she'd been raised on patrols, on the choreography of warden movements, and her body knew what stillness in a wheat field meant before her mind caught up. "How many?"

"More than two."

"Then we run."

They didn't make the treeline. They made it as far as the second row of dead apple trees before the first bolt came out of the wheat—Choir-work, white and cold, a line of unmaking that took the top off a stone wall Edmund hadn't known was there until it ceased to exist. Dust. Ringing silence. Then his ears filled again and the shouting started, voices he knew by cadence if not by name: wardens calling formation, calling *hold*, calling the words of a rite he'd heard recited over condemned charm-sellers in the market square.

*Down,* Maren's hands said, shoving him against the bole of an apple tree older than both of them together, bark soft as ash under his palms. She had her cantor's staff out—not a weapon, never officially a weapon, but the iron ferrule was glowing now, and she'd planted it in the soil like a flag. "Wardens of the Hollow Choir!" she called, and her voice did something Edmund had only heard once before, at a debtor's tribunal, when she'd talked three enforcers into walking away from a family of four. It got larger than her throat. "I am Cantor Solle, Second Voice, Vessing Chapter! Stand down and name your authority!"

"Warden-Captain Ferren," the wheat answered. "Acting under writ of the High Cantor himself. Cantor Solle, you are recalled. Your credentials were voided at dawn."

Edmund watched that land on her. Watched her shoulders take the weight of it—the chapterhouse, the choir stalls, the vestry where she kept her spare robes pressed and her hymnal annotated in three colors of ink. Voided at dawn. While they slept in a shepherd's hut six miles back, someone in Vessing had erased her with a signature.

"You're making a mistake," she said, quieter.

"So is he." Ferren's voice came from somewhere left of center, moving. "Hartwell. We know what you are. There's no shame in it—no more than there's shame in a fever, or a flood. Come out with your hands open and we'll do this gently. There's a rite for you. A kind one. You won't feel a thing after the first minute, I'm told."

*Told.* Edmund pressed his spine harder into the tree. That word was doing so much work. Told by whom. By the archive clerks who kept the sealed pages, by the men who had redacted a child's name fifteen years ago, by whoever decided that *painless* was the part worth advertising about being unmade.

"They'll seal you," Maren said to him, low, not looking away from the wheat. Her hand found his wrist. "You understand that, yes? Not kill. Seal. You'll be alive inside it. Some part of you, awake, forever, behind glass." Her grip tightened. "So whatever happens, don't be gentle with them. Don't be the man who goes politely."

Another bolt took the tree beside theirs and split it down its whole length, and the halves fell away from each other like a book opening.

"I'm not going anywhere," Edmund said—which was a lie, because even as he said it something was pulling at him from below, from the ground itself, patient as water finding a crack.

He'd felt it since they reached the orchard. Since before the orchard, if he was honest—a low hum in his blood that had started the night ash spelled his name on the windowsill and had grown steadily since, through Grayford, through the tollhouse, through every mile of old pilgrim road. Ashenveil's presence, thickening, the way the plan had promised. But this was different. This wasn't the god waiting beyond some door. This was the ground under his feet, the dead roots in the soil, the whole wounded acreage of Osselmark's last shrine, and it was *leaning toward him*. Like a crowd leaning toward a speaker. Like a congregation toward an altar.

Like something starving toward bread.

"Hartwell!" Ferren again, closer now. Wheat stalks bent and parted along a line Edmund could track by sound alone. "Last call, scribe. Open hands or we open fire."

Maren stepped out from cover. All the way out—no tree at her back, staff raised, ferrule blazing. "Your writ is void too," she said, loud enough to reach every hidden muzzle in that field. "The Choir has lied to you since before any of us took our vows. The gods aren't returning to conquer. One of them is trying to come *home*—"

The volley cut her sentence in half. Three bolts, white lines stitching the air, and Edmund saw her turn the first aside with a gesture that cost her something visible—a grunt, a stagger, the staff shrieking—and the second she split, but the third got past her guard and took her across the shoulder and spun her down into the dirt, and everything in Edmund Hartwell went very quiet and very simple.

Later he would try to describe it to himself and fail. He would reach for metaphors—a door opening, a dam breaking, a held breath released—and discard all of them, because none of them captured the essential fact of it, which was that nothing broke. Nothing tore. What happened was that fifteen years of careful, ledger-kept, charm-priced smallness simply *stopped being load-bearing*, the way a wall stops being a wall the moment you admit it was never holding anything up.

He walked out from behind the tree.

Bolts came at him—he registered them distantly, white threads in his peripheral vision—and they came apart. Not deflected. *Unwoven.* He watched one unravel mid-flight into ordinary light, into nothing, and understood that he wasn't stopping them; he was deciding, with some faculty deeper than decision, that they had never really existed.

The wheat around his feet began to grow.

Not metaphorically. Stalks that had stood brown and frost-burned since autumn greened from the root upward, racing the season, seed heads swelling fat and gold in the space of heartbeats. The dead apple trees around the orchard's rim shivered—and then bloomed, every one of them, out of season, out of reason, white flowers bursting along blackened limbs like snow falling upward. Where the split tree lay in halves, new wood crawled across the wound and knitted. Under Edmund's boots the scorched earth softened, darkened, smelled suddenly of rain and worms and living things turning soil, and grass came up through the ash in a green tide that followed him as he walked.

He heard a warden scream—not in pain. In something worse. He heard another one start laughing and couldn't tell which frightened him more.

"*Hold position!*" Ferren, and for the first time the captain's voice had cracks in it. "Rite of Severance, form the circle—do it NOW—"

They formed the circle. Eight wardens, white cords, chanting the old binding words in the old flat way, and Edmund felt their rite settle over him like a net thrown on water—felt it actually touch him, cold and bureaucratic, a form being filled out with his name in the blank. And the ground rose up to meet it.

Roots. He never saw them move but they must have moved—thick pale roots surging up through the new grass, gentle as sleeping animals turning over, and they took the wardens' staves and cords and *drank* them. The rite guttered. One warden's cord crumbled to dust in his hands. Another's chant dissolved into a coughing fit, and when he spat, Edmund saw, absurdly, impossibly, a small green shoot lying in the spittle.

*It doesn't want to hurt them.* The knowledge arrived calm and total, in a voice that might have been Ashenveil's or might have been his own. *It wants to heal them. They just have to stop holding weapons.*

"Drop the cords," Edmund heard himself say. His voice came out wrong—layered, resonant, a boy's voice and an old voice and something geological underneath both. Every head in the clearing turned. Every face he saw wore the same expression: awe curdling into terror, terror hardening into duty. "Please. Put them down. No one here has to—"

Ferren threw a sealer.

Not a bolt. A canister—glass and lead, Choir-forged, the kind they used on buildings, on wells, on whole villages in the old stories. It arced high over the wheat, trailing white smoke, and burst above the orchard's heart, and the world went *grey*.

Edmund knew grey. Grey was the scriptorium. Grey was the ledgers, the debt columns, the fifteen years of copying blessings that worked just well enough to keep people docile and not well enough to remind anyone what blessing used to mean. The sealer's cloud came down over the blooming trees and the flowers died in real time, petals greying and dropping, and the young wheat yellowed where the smoke touched it, and the hum in Edmund's blood stuttered—

And held.

Because the earth beneath him refused. He felt it refuse—felt the whole shrine-ground flex under the grey like a living thing shrugging off a nettle, felt the poison smoke thin and shred as something vast and patient exhaled upward through the soil. The grey broke apart. Fell as harmless dust. And in the ringing clarity after, Edmund stood revealed at the center of a ring of flowering dead trees, lit like an altar, hiding nothing, able to hide nothing, ever again.

Every warden in that field had seen him now. Whatever he'd been—a scribe, a debtor, a charm-peddler, a rumor—that man was gone. What remained couldn't be redacted. Couldn't be filed. Couldn't be sealed without the whole world knowing exactly what the Choir did to the ones it caught.

Power, he thought dizzily, kneeling in the new grass, was not safety. Power was the moment you became visible to everyone, forever, and could never go home to a small room and lock the door.

Maren's hand closed on his arm.

"We're leaving," she said, blood on her teeth, shoulder hanging wrong, eyes wide and wet and absolutely certain. "Now, while they're still on their knees. Edmund—they're praying. Look at them."

He looked. The wardens had dropped their cords. Not all of them—but four of eight, kneeling in the resurrected wheat, and one of them, a boy hardly older than the mother's son from the night market all those weeks ago, was whispering something Edmund recognized with a full-body jolt. His grandmother's dialect. An Osselmark harvest-blessing, half-remembered, mangled, *alive*.

"Don't answer him," Maren said fiercely, reading his face. "Don't you dare answer him here, with crossbows behind those trees and Ferren still standing. Later. Do you hear me? Live first. Answer prayers later."

*

They ran for the shrine itself—or what remained of it: a low stone foundation half-swallowed by the hillside, roofless, doorwayless, a mouth of dark stone in the orchard's heart. Behind them Ferren was screaming orders, rallying the four who still held cords, but his voice had lost its arithmetic; Edmund could hear the doubt in it now, spreading like the green in the wheat. You couldn't order men to fight a field that healed itself. Not twice.

Maren went down the shrine steps first, one-armed, staff clattering ahead of her on the stones, and Edmund turned at the threshold for one last look at the morning.

The orchard was in bloom. Every burned tree, white with blossom, gold light slanting through it, and the wheat running to the horizon in waves of impossible green. It looked like a country that had never been erased. It looked like a memory insisting on itself. Somewhere under his ribs, Ashenveil's patience had changed quality—no longer the stillness of waiting but the stillness of a man standing at his own front gate, hand on the latch, watching Edmund with something that had no right to feel so much like gratitude.

*They will regroup,* the god said, or the wind said, or the ground said through the soles of Edmund's feet. *And next time they will bring the thing they use on doors.*

"What thing?" Edmund asked aloud.

*Go down. Now.*

He went down.

The steps narrowed, turned, descended further than the ruin's footprint should allow—old pilgrim engineering, the kind of architecture a country builds when it expects to need hiding places. Maren waited at the bottom, grey-faced, cradling her shoulder, and when Edmund's hand found the wall for balance the stone warmed under his palm like a cheek turning toward the sun.

"There's a passage," she managed. "Under the shrine. Old maps mention an undercroft—sealed since the Banishment. If the Choir ever found it, they never recorded—" She stopped. Her eyes had gone past him, down the corridor, to where the darkness was doing something darkness shouldn't do.

It was glowing. Faintly, deeply, the way a banked fire glows—a warm gold seam running along the floor of the tunnel ahead, as if the stone itself had veins, and the veins were filling.

And along the walls, as their eyes adjusted, letters were surfacing. Thousands of them. Names, scored into the rock in a dozen dead scripts, emerging one by one out of the dark like faces coming up out of deep water—some ancient, some recent, some scored over and over as if written and struck through and written again, and near the entrance, at the height of a child's hand, freshly legible though it had been cut decades ago:

*E. HARTWELL.*

Maren read it. Edmund watched her read it. Neither of them spoke, because down in the dark beneath the last shrine of Osselmark, the walls had begun, very softly, very patiently, to sing.
