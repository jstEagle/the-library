## 24. Fifteen Years of Silence

The undercroft had a way of making silence feel like an argument.

Edmund sat against the wall of names with his knees drawn up, watching Maren across the low-burning stub of candle they'd allowed themselves. Above them, through the shrine's broken roof, he could hear the wind moving through the wheat—Osselmark's wheat, though no map in Vessing admitted the field existed. The stalks made a sound like whispering, like a congregation settling into pews.

Quillon's offer still hung between them, three hours old and not yet decayed. *Submit to sealing and live.* The warden had said it gently, which was somehow worse. Mercy with a knife hidden in its sleeve.

Maren hadn't spoken since they'd bolted the undercroft door behind them. She sat on the far side of the candle, cantor's robes torn at the hem, her hands folded in her lap the way she folded them during audits—precise, patient, unreadable. But Edmund had known her for fifteen years, and he could read her hands the way other men read weather. Her thumbs were pressing hard against each other. That meant fear.

"You nearly said yes," he said quietly. "Back there. When he offered."

Her thumbs stilled. "I nearly did a great many things back there."

"You were going to take it for me." He kept his voice flat, factual, because if he let any feeling into it, the feeling would win. "You were going to say *he accepts* before I could open my mouth. I saw you draw breath for it."

"I saw you about to die," she said. "Forgive me for inhaling."

"It isn't death, Maren. That's what nobody understands. Sealing doesn't kill you. It just—" He searched for the word and hated every candidate. "*Unmakes* the part that's me. The rest walks around afterward. Eats. Sleeps. Copies ledgers." He almost laughed. "I've seen sealed doors. In the archive, when I was young. They sit in their chairs by the window and they're perfectly calm. That's how you know. Nobody's perfectly calm who's still all there."

Maren looked at him across the flame, and in the candlelight her face seemed younger than her years and older than her office, both at once. "Then tell me," she said. "If we might die tomorrow—or worse, if one of us might be made *calm*—tell me the thing you've never told me."

He knew immediately which thing she meant. There was only one.

He had been carrying it for so long that it had grown a shell, the way oysters grow shells around grit. Fifteen years of selling charms in the night market, fifteen years of small illegal mercies, and underneath all of it this single stone: the reason he had started. He had never said it aloud. Saying it aloud would mean letting someone hold the shell, and whoever held the shell would know exactly where to strike.

But Quillon was above them in the wheat with thirty wardens and a tracker who smelled divine residue like smoke. And Ashenveil waited somewhere past the door shaped like Edmund, patient as winter. Tomorrow there would be no more secrets worth keeping. Only choices.

"His name was Tam," Edmund said, because it was easier to begin with someone else. "The boy whose charm worked too well, back in Vessing. His mother bought a knot from me for his fever. You remember. You audited the ledger entry."

"I remember everything you ever sold," Maren said. It wasn't a confession. It was an accusation she aimed at herself.

"Everyone assumes I sell charms out of greed. Or rebellion. Small sins for small money." He turned the candle stub a quarter-turn, watching the wax pool. "Nobody ever asks why a man who serves the Choir—the man who *invented* half the disclaimers in the Choir's anti-charm pamphlets, incidentally, I'm very proud of those—why such a man breaks the law he writes."

"Edmund."

"When I was nine years old," he said, "my mother was dying."

The words came out smaller than he expected. Nine years old. It had been so long since he'd stood inside that memory instead of beside it that he'd forgotten how tall the room had been, how high the ceiling of their rented room in the Weft district, how the light came down in one dusty column like something holy that couldn't be bothered to help.

"The coughing sickness. Half the Weft had it that winter. The Choir's physicians came through once, wrote names in a book, and told us to keep the windows shut and pray to the Hollow—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the empty heaven the Choir sang to— "to whatever was listening. Which was nothing. Which was the entire point. The gods were outlawed, so prayer was a formality, like bowing to an empty chair. We all bowed anyway. What else do you do?"

Maren had gone very still. Cantor-still. But he could see her knuckles whitening around each other, and he understood suddenly that she was bracing—that some part of her had already guessed, the way she always guessed, and had spent years choosing not to know.

"She was dying on a Tuesday," he went on. "That's what I remember most absurdly. A Tuesday. Market day. The whole city buying cabbages while my mother drowned slowly on dry land, four floors up, in a bed that smelled of vinegar and tallow." His voice caught; he pushed through it. "My father was dead already. Coal dust, the year before. So it was just me and her and the empty heaven, and the physician's apprentice had said *by week's end* in the tone people use for weather."

"Edmund." Her voice cracked on his name. "You don't have to—"

"I prayed." He said it fast, before the shame could close his throat. "Not the way the Choir taught us—not the hollow formalities, not *we ask nothing, we expect nothing, blessed is the silence*. I prayed the old way, the outlawed way, the way my grandmother used to mutter over her stew in a language the neighbors called gibberish. Ossish. I didn't even know I remembered the words until they were coming out of my mouth. I knelt on the floorboards with my forehead pressed down and I begged. Not *please*. I bargained. I offered. I said, *take anything of mine, take my whole life, take my name, only let her breathe.*"

The candle flame leaned as if listening.

"And something answered."

Maren's breath left her in a thin thread.

"Not a voice. Not a figure of light. Nothing so decent." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "It was more like—like a lock turning somewhere very far away. Like a door I hadn't known was in the room swinging open, and cold air coming through, and the cold air was *interested* in me. Attentive. Have you ever had the feeling of being watched, but kindly? Being watched by something that has been waiting a long time for you to speak?"

"Yes," Maren whispered. "In the orchard. Under this shrine. All of it."

"Well. Then you know." He wrapped his arms around his knees. "My mother breathed again that night. Just like that. The rattle stopped. The fever broke by morning, and by the following Tuesday she was complaining about the price of cabbages, and the physician's apprentice stood in our doorway looking at her like a man reading a page that contradicts itself."

"And you?"

"Me?" He shook his head slowly. "I was marked. That's the word the sealed archive uses—I've read it now, thanks to you, thank you for that, by the way, for committing treason with a stylus on my behalf—but I knew the word before I read it. I felt marked. Like a brand under the skin. For weeks afterward, things happened near me. Bread rose unprompted. Our neighbor's dead well filled overnight. A sparrow with a broken wing flew out of my hands mended. Small things. Impossible things. And every time, that attentive coldness brushed past me, pleased, like a dog bringing back the ball."

"Your mother," Maren said carefully. "Did she—did she understand what you'd done?"

"She understood better than I did." His chest tightened. "She was Osselman, remember. Her mother had sung the hymns before the burning. She took my face in her hands the day the fever broke and she said, *you opened something, little one, and doors don't close just because you want them to*, and then she taught me the first rule of being a door." He swallowed. "You know what it was?"

"No."

"*Be nothing,*" he said. "*Be so boring, so obedient, so forgettable, that whatever hunts doors will walk right past you.*" He spread his hands, indicating himself: the scribe, the clerk, the man of ink and ledgers and mild disclaimers. "So I became nothing. I joined the Choir at eleven because it was the safest building in Vessing—the hunters don't search their own scriptorium. I learned the rites so thoroughly I could perform them blindfolded, because a door who knows the sealing rites knows how to seem already sealed. I copied ledgers for fifteen years and sold harmless knots in the night market, harmless ones, deliberately harmless, because—"

"Because you needed to give it away," Maren said softly. "The answering."

He blinked at her. "What?"

"That's what I could never reconcile. The audit discrepancies. Your charms work, Edmund—they *work*, the fever-knot, the finding-charm, the bread-charm—and yet you sell them for pennies to people who'll never know they broke the law. I thought it was guilt. I thought you were paying penance." Her eyes were bright and wet in the candlelight. "But it isn't penance. You're a well. Someone dug you open when you were nine years old, and water rises in you whether you want it or not, and selling charms was just—you letting people drink. Small cups. Controlled pours. Because if you didn't pour it out yourself—"

"It would pour itself," Edmund finished. "Yes. Twice, in fifteen years, it got away from me. Once in the market, with Tam's fever-knot. Once when I was fourteen and angry at a warden who beat a beggar outside the scriptorium—I didn't touch him, I swear I never touched him, I just wished, and his hand withered for a month." He closed his eyes. "After that I stopped wishing. Do you understand? Fifteen years of not-wishing. Of keeping the well capped. Of being the most boring man in the Hollow Choir."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to shield it.

"Why now?" Maren asked. "Why tell me this tonight, of all nights, after—after I nearly handed you to Quillon?"

"Because you didn't." He unfolded himself and crossed the space between them, kneeling in front of her, taking her folded hands and prying them apart the way he'd once pried open a redaction, layer by careful layer. "Because you drew breath to accept his offer, and then you let it out, and then you lied to a Brother of the Choir to my face and ran with me into a country that doesn't exist. Because I have spent fifteen years being nothing to everyone, Maren, and I am so tired." His voice finally broke, and he let it. "And because if tomorrow goes wrong—if Quillon takes me, or the door takes me, or Ashenveil takes me—I refuse to arrive at whatever comes next still carrying this. I want one person in the world to know what I am. Not a door. Not a debt. Not a discrepancy in a ledger. A boy who loved his mother enough to break heaven open for her."

Maren's hands turned in his grip until she was holding him instead.

"My mother lived another nineteen years," he said into her shoulder. "Nineteen years of Tuesdays. She died in her sleep at sixty-one, warm, full of stew, mid-complaint about the price of onions. Whatever answered my prayer kept its bargain. It took nothing." He paused. "Except everything else."

They stayed like that for a long time. The candle burned lower. Somewhere above them, in the burned orchard, the wind changed direction, and the wheat-sound stopped, and in the new quiet Edmund heard—faintly, impossibly—singing. A woman's voice, in Ossish, singing a lullaby his grandmother had sung, a song no living throat should know.

Maren heard it too. He felt her go rigid against him.

"That's the shrine," she whispered. "The walls of names. They're—Edmund, the names are *warm*."

He lifted his head. Down the length of the undercroft, in the dark beyond the candle's reach, the carved names along the walls had begun to glow—a faint gold rising out of the stone like embers breathing, five generations of Hartwells, and hundreds of others besides, letter by letter catching light. And beneath the glow, the singing grew clearer, and Edmund realized with a jolt that it wasn't one voice.

It was a choir.

Every name on the walls, singing together, and the song was the one he'd been trying not to remember—the hymn his grandmother had taught him, the one Ashenveil had been humming in his dreams since childhood, the opening verse of something vast that ended, he suddenly understood, at a door.

"They're teaching it to me," Edmund breathed. "The rest of the verses. I only ever knew the first."

Maren gripped his arm. "Then we'd better learn fast," she said. "Because listen—"

Beneath the choir, beneath the singing names, from far off across the wheat, came another sound: boots. Hundreds of them. And a bell—the Choir's marching bell, tolling vespers, tolling war.

Dawn was coming. And dawn was coming armed.
