## 25. The Choir Sings Without Gods

The fire was small and mean, the kind Maren could have made in her sleep—which, in a way, she had, three times now since Grayford, her hands moving through the motions of camp-craft while her mind moved somewhere else entirely. Edmund sat across from her on a fallen log, turning his boots over to dry by the flames, and she watched him and thought: *I sang to an empty heaven for eleven years, and I never once noticed it was empty until this man's charms started working.*

She didn't say that. She said, "Your left boot is closer to the flame than your right."

"I know." He turned it anyway.

They were two days out of Grayford, camped in the shoulder of a hill where the old pilgrim road bent north toward country that no longer appeared on maps. The wheat here grew wild and waist-high, unharvested for years, whispering in a wind that came down off the borderlands smelling of stone and old rain. Somewhere behind them, Vessing. Somewhere ahead of them, Osselmark—or its ghost. Somewhere inside Maren, a question she had been carrying since the binding rite, since before that, since the ledger audit, since maybe always: *what exactly did I give my life to?*

Edmund looked up. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The cantor thing. Where you fold your hands and stare into the middle distance like the answer is going to be dictated to you."

"It usually is," she said. "That's rather the point of being a cantor."

He smiled, but he didn't push. That was one of the thousand small things she loved about him and would never say aloud: he knew when a door was locked and didn't rattle the handle. He went back to his boots.

Maren fed a stick to the fire and let herself fall backward through fifteen years, because there was no one watching, and because she suspected—had suspected since the undercroft of names, since *E. Hartwell* in faded redacted ink—that whatever she was running toward required her first to be honest about what she was running from.

* * *

She was sixteen when they brought her up the Spire stair for the first time, and the Spire was everything they promised.

They had promised her heaven, more or less. Not in so many words—the Choir was careful with words; words were its trade—but in the shape of the promise. *You will stand closest to the veil,* the recruiting cantor had told her mother, in their kitchen in Lowmarsh, while Maren pretended not to listen and listened to every word. *Your daughter's voice will be heard on both sides of it.* Her mother had wept. Nobody in Lowmarsh wept over good news, so Maren understood even then that something enormous was happening, something that cost tears either way.

The induction hall sat at the top of the Spire, a dome of white stone ribbed with gold, and forty novices stood in a ring on the floor while the full Cantorate assembled above them in tiers, robed in grey, faces shadowed by the hoods of office. Maren remembered counting them—she counted things when she was frightened, a habit she'd never lost—and getting to sixty-three before the High Cantor rose.

His name was Aldous Vey then. He was High Cantor still. She would stand in front of him in eleven years and confess her doubts to him, and he would hand her a binding rite and a name, and she would take both without flinching, and hate herself in the specific way of someone who has agreed to a thing before she has finished deciding whether to agree. But that evening she was sixteen, and Aldous Vey's voice filled the dome like water fills a cup.

"Children," he said. "Tonight you will sing the First Hymn. You have learned the notes. You have not yet learned what the notes are *for*. Listen."

And he told them the story—the story every child in Vessing knew, but told properly now, adult-sized, with all its teeth.

Once, he said, the world had gods the way a body has blood. They were everywhere; they were in everything; they answered when called. And the world drowned in them. Every village had its god, every river its temper, every harvest its appetite, and the appetites grew. Gods fed on devotion, and devotion fed on fear, and fear fed on miracles, and the miracles grew crueler as the gods grew hungrier. Whole nations bent themselves out of shape around the whims of beings who loved their worshippers the way a man loves a meal.

"And so," Vey said, "the founders did the bravest thing anyone has ever done. They did not fight the gods. You cannot fight a god. They *outgrew* them. They built the Choir—not to worship, but to *manage*. To hold the hymns, keep the rites, remember the names—and to sing nothing back. A faith with the gods removed, like a bone set and splinted, so the world could walk again."

He paused. Sixty-three hooded figures above. Forty children below. One enormous silence.

"The Hollow Choir," he said, "sings to an empty heaven on purpose. Because the emptiness is ours. We made it. We keep it. And someday—" and here his voice did something Maren had no framework for at sixteen, something warm and terrible at once, "—someday, when the world is strong enough, we may open our mouths and find that what answers us is not hunger. But not yet. Not yet. Tonight, you sing into the quiet, and the quiet is your vow."

Then the First Hymn began.

Maren had sung it a hundred times in the novice dormitories, note-perfect, and it had always felt like a technical exercise, a ladder of intervals climbing toward a ceiling. But sung in the dome, with sixty-three trained voices above and thirty-nine other children beside her, it became something else. It rose. It climbed the ribs of white stone and found the apex of the dome and pressed against it—

—and stopped.

There was a place in the hymn, the fourth movement, where the music reached for something. Every singer felt it. The harmony opened like a held breath, the melody leaned upward, the whole architecture of sound strained toward a resolution that was written into the score and never arrived. Composers called it the *deferred cadence*. Novices called it the Gap. It lasted four bars, and in those four bars the hymn asked its question—*are you there, are you there, are you there*—in a language older than words.

And nothing answered.

Four bars of silence, held by four hundred voices, in a room built to carry a god's reply, and the reply did not come, and then the hymn gathered itself and resolved downward instead, gracefully, into the closing movement, which was beautiful, and final, and about acceptance.

Maren stood afterward with her heart hammering and tears on her face and did not know which part of her was crying. Beside her, a boy named Tam Wess—she remembered his name for the rest of her life, though she never saw him again after second year—leaned over and whispered, with the awful honesty of the young:

"Did you feel it? The Gap? Like the song was knocking on a door and—"

"Novice Solle. Novice Wess." A senior cantor had materialized between them, mild as milk. "Silence in the dome, please."

But Maren had heard it. The knock. The four bars where the music reached for a door that wasn't there.

That night, in the novices' dormitory, she lay awake and waited for the doubt to arrive, the great crisis of faith that surely followed a night like that. She had read about such crises. Doubt was supposed to come like a storm and shake you and either break you or make you true.

It didn't come. What came instead, around midnight, was worse. What came was a memory of the closing movement—how lovely it was, how *complete*, how the deferred cadence resolved at last into a chord that needed nothing outside itself—and she caught herself humming it, and humming it, and feeling better each time.

*Oh*, she thought, in the dark, at sixteen. *That's how it works.*

* * *

They taught her the rest slowly, the way you teach a body anything: by repetition, until it stops being a decision.

First year, they taught her doctrine. The gods were real—that was never denied; denial was superstition, and the Choir prided itself on being the only honest institution in the world. The gods were real, and hungry, and gone, and the absence was an achievement. "Other faiths lie," Cantor-Preceptor Ives told her class, pacing before them. "They claim their god hears. We know ours doesn't. We are the only church in history built on verified silence. Take pride in that. Anyone can believe in a voice. It takes discipline to love the quiet."

Second year, they taught her the practical arts: ledger-keeping, charm-classification, the taxonomy of folk magic. This was the work nobody romanticized. The Choir's daily business was not mystery; it was paperwork. Every hedge-charm, every knot sold in a night market, every whispered prayer over a sickbed—all of it was catalogued, graded, and suppressed or tolerated according to schedules Maren eventually mastered in her sleep. Harmless charms were permitted, within quotas, because total prohibition bred black markets, and black markets bred the other thing. The dangerous thing. The answering thing.

Third year, they taught her the rites of sealing, and this was the year the pretending became load-bearing.

She had imagined sealings as battles. In truth they were mostly administrative. A person—a hedge-witch, a prayer-singer, some farmer whose well water had started murmuring back at him—was brought to a chapter house, examined, and *quieted*: a rite that closed whatever channel had opened in them, gently, permanently. Most subjects wept with relief. Whatever had been speaking to them had not been kind. Maren sealed nine channels in her third year and slept fine after eight of them.

The ninth was a girl of nineteen named Odile Brant, who had been hearing her dead brother singing lullabies through the floorboards, and whose face, when the channel closed, went through grief the way a field goes through fire—everything burning, and then ash, and then a terrible clean blankness. Afterward Odile thanked them. Thanked them sincerely. Said the singing had been keeping her from mourning properly.

Maren performed the closing movements of the rite with steady hands and then went back to her cell and sat on the edge of her cot and did not move for a long time.

Because here was the thing she could not put into any report: the lullabies had been *beautiful*. She had heard them, through the rite's listening-spell, before she closed them. A dead brother's voice, coming through the floor of the world, singing a child's song to his living sister—and it had been tender, and patient, and sad in the ordinary human way, and there had been no hunger in it at all.

Doctrine said hunger. Doctrine said the gods were appetite wearing affection's face. Doctrine had sixty-three hooded authorities and five hundred years of verified silence behind it.

The lullaby had four bars of melody and no doctrine at all.

Maren sat on her cot and held the two facts in her hands like stones, weighing them, and then she did the thing she would do ten thousand times over the following decade, the thing that was the true curriculum of the Cantorate, the lesson beneath every lesson:

She put the stones down. She chose the heavier institution over the lighter truth, because the institution had structure, and salary, and her mother's proud tearful face, and a dormitory of sisters who would notice if she broke, and because a girl of twenty cannot rebuild her entire self on the evidence of one lullaby.

She chose the Choir. And then, because choosing is not the same as believing, she learned to pretend—and discovered that pretending, done thoroughly enough, at enough vigils, at enough sealings, through enough Gaps in enough hymns, becomes indistinguishable from faith. The body believes first. The mind follows, grateful for the peace.

By her thirtieth year, Cantor Maren Solle was famous within the Spire for her composure. *Solle doesn't waver*, the preceptors said. *Solle sings the Gap like it's a resting chord.* She audited ledgers with a flawless eye. She sealed channels without trembling. She had risen fast and would rise further, and if there was a locked room in her chest with a lullaby inside it, she had bricked it over so smoothly that even she sometimes forgot the door was there.

* * *

The fire cracked. Edmund had lain back against the log, boots on, hat over his eyes, ostensibly asleep. The wheat whispered. The wind came down off the borderlands with its smell of stone.

Maren realized her hands were folded in her lap, cantor-fashion, and unfolded them.

Fifteen years of pretending. That was the arithmetic she kept arriving at, these days, on the road: fifteen years for him in a scriptorium, fifteen for her in the Spire, two people paying down debts to the same hollow institution in different currencies. He had paid in ink and small illegal mercies. She had paid in silences—one per Gap, one per sealing, one per night she hummed the closing movement instead of asking the fourth movement's question.

*Are you there.*

Nothing. On purpose. By design. The emptiness wasn't a wound in the world; it was a policy.

And now prayers were being answered in Vessing, bread rising unprompted, wells refilling, knots working that should have been thread and nothing more—and the Choir's response was denials, patrols, martial order, the machinery of suppression grinding forward as if the sky itself were a ledger discrepancy to be audited into compliance. As if the founders' great splinted bone could simply be re-broken and re-set whenever the world tried to walk on its own.

And she had agreed to test Edmund with a binding rite. She had stood in front of Aldous Vey—sixty-three hoods, one voice, the same voice that had filled the dome when she was sixteen and told her the quiet was a vow—and she had said yes.

She looked at Edmund asleep against the log, hat over his eyes, breathing slow, and felt the brickwork over that locked room in her chest begin, at last, to crumble. Not dramatically. Brick by brick. The way institutions actually fall.

"Edmund," she said quietly.

The hat tilted up. One eye. "Mm?"

"When we get to the shrine—when we get wherever we're going—I need to tell you something about the night I was inducted. About the hymn. There's a gap in it, four bars where the music asks if anyone's there, and for fifteen years I've been singing past it." She paused. "I think Ashenveil lives in those four bars. I think maybe he always has."

Edmund took the hat off. He looked at her across the fire for a long moment, and then he said, in the voice of a man setting down something heavy:

"Maren. Before you tell me that—you need to know what happened the night my mother died. Because I've been carrying it since I was nine, and I can't walk through that door with it still shut."

The wind moved through the wheat, and far to the north, faint as a memory, something that might have been singing seemed to answer it.
