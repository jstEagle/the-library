## 30. Cantor Against Choir

The High Cantor had not raised his voice in thirty years. Maren knew this the way she knew the shape of her own hands—by long familiarity, by repetition, by the slow accumulation of small proofs. Serel Voss spoke the way frost formed: evenly, inevitably, without effort or heat. When he wanted a room silent he did not command it; he simply began to listen, and everyone else fell quiet out of shame.

So when she came up the shrine stairs into the burning light of dawn and found him standing at the head of forty wardens with his cantor's stole draped over his armor like an afterthought, it was his silence that frightened her most.

"Maren Solle." He said her name the way he read from the ledger of the dead—accurately, without grief.

"High Cantor."

"You are standing on the wrong side of a holy war."

She looked behind her, down the stairwell into the undercroft's dark, where Edmund waited at the threshold of a door that was not yet a door. Then back at Voss, at the wheat field beyond him bending under the weight of armed men, at the tracker crouched at his heel like a hound that had caught a scent too old for its nose.

"There's no war here," she said. "There's a man and a country that your predecessors erased."

"Our predecessors," Voss corrected gently, "did nothing of the kind."

* * *

He walked toward her alone. The wardens held their positions among the wheat, spears grounded, faces hidden behind the blank porcelain half-masks of full rite-armor—the kind worn only when the Choir expected to fight something that could answer back. Maren had seen that armor twice in fifteen years. Both times, afterward, there had been funerals.

Voss stopped six paces from her, close enough that she could see the fine lines around his eyes, the ink stain on his left thumb that no amount of scrubbing ever fully lifted. A scribe's stain. She had loved that detail about him once, early in her induction, when she still believed that a man who carried ink in his skin must carry truth somewhere in him too.

"You were my best student," he said. "Do you know why?"

"Because I could hold four-part harmony in my head."

"Because you asked questions and then stopped asking them at exactly the right moment." He folded his hands. "You always knew where the edge of the doctrine was, and you never stepped past it. I have been waiting fifteen years to find out whether that was wisdom or cowardice."

"It was faith," Maren said, and heard how thin it sounded even to herself.

"Yes," Voss said. "That is what I hoped you'd say. Because then this will hurt less." He glanced past her, down the stairwell, and something moved across his face—not anger, not fear. Recognition. "You've read the sealed archive. Chapter Nine of the Foundational Rite. The page with the black line through it."

Maren's mouth went dry. "How do you know what I've—"

"Because I put the redaction there myself, twenty-two years ago, when I was still a sub-cantor with steady hands and no doubts worth naming." He said it the way another man might describe mending a roof. "And because you have been walking around for three weeks smelling of unlocked secrets. Doubt has a scent, Cantor Solle. The tracker behind me can smell divine residue. I can smell the other thing."

The wheat whispered. Somewhere below, faint as breath, Edmund was singing.

* * *

"Tell me," Maren said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Tell me what the Choir has known. All of it. If you're going to kill us anyway, grant me that much rite."

Voss almost smiled. "We are not going to kill you. That is the tragedy you haven't understood yet. Hartwell perhaps. You—no. You are going to kneel, and be restored, and spend the rest of your life grateful that you were given one more chance to not know things." He paused. "But very well. You asked. It has been so long since anyone asked me directly that I find I want to answer."

He turned slightly, so that he faced the east and the rising sun, and when he spoke again his voice took on the cadence of lecture, the register she had sat under for years in the cold amphitheater of the Cantorate.

"The gods did not leave, Maren. We did not banish them. Those are the two lies every novice learns first, and they are both wrong, and they are both necessary." His hands stayed folded. "The gods are not elsewhere. They are *here*—pressed against the skin of the world like water against ice. They never went anywhere. What we did was seal the doors."

"Doors," she said. "People."

"People," he agreed. "Rare vessels. Perhaps one in ten million born with blood thin enough, self hollow enough, that a god could pour through them if invited. In the old countries—Osselmark, and the three others whose names we also removed—they called such people doors, and they built shrines around them, and they worshipped, and the worshipped starved them, and the wars of succession between god-factions burned whole provinces." He unfolded his hands at last, and she saw they were trembling, very slightly, and understood that this was not calm. This was a man holding something heavy in the same position for decades. "The founders of the Choir ended that. Not by fighting the gods—you cannot fight weather—but by sealing every door they could find, generation after generation, until the pressure behind the ice grew so great and so distant that the world forgot it was ever wet."

"And the sealing," Maren said. "What happens to the person?"

"They stop being a door." Voss's voice did not change at all. "Which is to say: they stop being. Not death—we are not murderers, whatever Hartwell believes. Erasure. The vessel is emptied so thoroughly that no trace remains. No name in any ledger but ours. No memory in any mind but the sealed archive. Their mothers forget their faces." He looked at her. "It is painless. I have performed it eleven times. The last was a girl of nine, in Grayford, who had begun answering her neighbor's prayers without knowing she was doing it. She smiled the entire time."

Maren's knees nearly buckled. She locked them. "Eleven times."

"Twelve, if we count tonight."

* * *

"But that isn't the part," she said, and her voice cracked now, finally, usefully. "That isn't the part that made you build a machine out of silence. Sealing doors to end the god-wars—that's a horror with a reason inside it. But you said *hoard*. Down in the dark just now, before the wardens came, before all this—I heard the resonance in the undercroft walls, and I heard what the rites were shaped around, and it wasn't protection. Protection doesn't rhyme the way our unbinding verses rhyme. So tell me the real part, Serel. Tell me why."

For the first time in thirty years, the High Cantor of Vessing looked away from her.

"Scarcity," he said.

The word hung in the wheat-smelling air like smoke.

"A god who answers prayers is a god who distributes power," Voss went on, quietly now, the lecture gone out of his voice, leaving only the man. "Bread rises. Wells refill. The sick wake whole. Do you know what that does to a city? Within a season, no one needs the Choir. Within two, no one needs the state. Within a year, every grudge and grief in Vessing gets settled by whoever prays hardest, and the strong pray loudest, and we return within a single generation to the world the founders burned themselves out escaping." He turned back to her, and his eyes were tired in a way she had never been permitted to see. "Miracles are not gifts, Maren. They are currency. And a currency that falls from the sky destroys every economy built beneath it—including ours. Including *ourselves*. The Choir does not hoard silence because silence is holy. We hoard it because silence is *ours*. It is the only thing we have ever owned. Take away the scarcity of grace and you take away the reason forty thousand people rise before dawn to sweep our floors."

Maren stood very still.

She had expected many things from this conversation. Fury. Denial. The smooth doctrinal deflections she could have recited alongside him. She had prepared arguments on the walk down from Grayford, sharpened rebuttals like knives, rehearsed the moment she would strike through his sophistry and stand revealed in her own clean conviction.

She had not prepared for him to simply *tell her*, in the flat voice of a bookkeeper reading a column that always balanced, that everything she had sung to for fifteen years was a warehouse.

"That's it," she said. "That's the whole of it. The hymns, the vespers, the empty heaven we all pretended was listening—the great hymn, the one they taught us to sing with our hearts open—it was inventory control."

"The hymn is beautiful," Voss said. "I have never once believed otherwise. Beauty was the price we paid. Someone had to make the silence sing, or the people would have noticed it was only silence." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice further, and here at last was something raw. "You think I don't know? I have conducted the Dawn Office nine thousand times, Maren. Nine thousand mornings I stood in that choir loft and poured my voice into a heaven I helped keep empty, and told myself it was mercy. Told myself the alternative was fire. Some nights I even believed it." His hand rose, briefly, toward her face—stopped. Fell. "Come back. That is all I am asking. Kneel, take the restoration rite, forget the archive page, forget Hartwell's name, and go back to being the finest cantor this institution has produced in a century. The world will not thank you. But it will keep standing, which is more than thanks."

* * *

Below them, faintly, the singing changed key.

Maren felt it in her sternum before her ears caught it—a shift in the pressure of the air, the way a room feels just before a storm crosses its threshold. The wheat around the wardens bent all at once, all in the same direction, though there was no wind. One of the porcelain-masked figures took an involuntary step backward.

Edmund's voice, rising through the stone. Not the trained harmonies of the Choir. Something older, rougher, sung slightly off-pitch in the way of songs learned at a grandmother's knee and never written down—and it was *working*. She could feel the old resonances waking in the shrine's foundations, notes the Choir's own unbinding verses had been plagiarized from, degraded, defanged, and she thought: *we didn't invent our rites. We stole the locks from the door and called the theft a religion.*

"He's opening it," Voss said. There was no alarm in his voice. Only arithmetic. "Then it is twelve."

"No," Maren said.

"Cantor Solle—"

"*No.*" And the word came out of her with fifteen years behind it, with every Dawn Office and every sealed archive and every night she had lain awake beside a man she loved and said nothing because saying something would have cost her the only home she'd ever had. "You want to know what I believe, Serel? I believe you've told me the truth, and the truth is worse than any lie, and I am *grateful*, do you understand? Because I have spent three weeks dying by inches, wondering if my doubt was treason, wondering if the hollow feeling in the hymn was my own brokenness—and it wasn't. It was the building. It was always the building. You kept the silence and called it God, and you made *me* keep it too, and the cruelest thing—the cruelest thing—is that you did it while believing you were kind."

"I did it believing I was necessary," Voss said. "Kindness is for after."

"There is no after!" Her voice broke across the wheat field and the wardens' masks turned toward her, forty blank faces, and she found she did not care. "There is only the silence you maintain and the lives you erase to maintain it—a girl of nine, Serel, you wrote it in your own hand, *she smiled the entire time*—and you want me to kneel back into that and call it peace?" She pulled the cantor's stole from around her neck. It was white wool, blessed at her induction, damp with sweat and shrine-dust, and it weighed nothing at all in her hands and everything in the world. "I renounce the office. I renounce the rite. I renounce the silence."

She dropped the stole into the dirt between them.

Something extraordinary happened then, and Maren would remember it for the rest of her life, in whatever life she had left: the wheat around the fallen stole bowed inward, gently, the way a congregation bows at the elevation of the host. As if the ground itself had been waiting fifteen years for someone to put something down.

Voss looked at the stole. At her. And in his face, for one unguarded instant, she saw not the High Cantor but the sub-cantor with steady hands, twenty-two years younger, pressing black ink over a child's name and telling himself it was mercy—and she saw that he had been waiting all this time for someone to refuse him. Hoping for it, maybe, in the place where hope goes when it's forbidden. Wanting, with the whole starved bottom of his soul, to be proven wrong.

"You'll die for this," he said softly.

"Probably," Maren agreed. "But I'll die *loudly*. Which is more than you offered the girl in Grayford."

Behind him, the tracker made a sound—a high, keening note, half fear and half longing—and tore off its mask. Beneath it was a young woman, hardly older than Maren had been at induction, weeping without knowing she was weeping, staring at the shrine as if it were calling her by name.

And the ground beneath all of them began, very quietly, to sing.

* * *

Voss straightened. Whatever had passed across his face was sealed away now, filed, archived—redacted, Maren thought, the way he redacted everything—and when he raised his hand, forty spears came up out of the wheat like a crop of iron.

"Take the shrine," the High Cantor said. "Wound no one who yields. Kill anyone who opens that door."

Maren was already running for the stairwell, stole-less, office-less, lighter than she had ever been in her life—and behind her she heard Voss call her name one last time, not in command but in something almost like grief:

"You asked your questions, Solle. I always wondered what you'd do when you finally stopped stopping."

She took the steps three at a time, down into the singing dark, toward the gap in the world shaped like the man she loved—and the last thing she heard from the world above was the wardens beginning to advance, and the first of them, the weeping girl with the tracker's gift, refusing to move.
