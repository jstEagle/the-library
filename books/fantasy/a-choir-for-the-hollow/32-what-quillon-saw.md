## 32. What Quillon Saw

Brother Quillon had killed the lamp in his left hand without noticing.

He noticed now. The warden beside him—Desset, a good man, a dull man—was saying something about formation, about holding the stairhead, and Quillon heard it the way you hear rain on a roof: present, irrelevant. His attention had gone somewhere else entirely, through a gap in the world that should not have existed, and what was on the other side of that gap had taken his voice with it.

The threshold stood at the heart of the undercroft, where no door should stand and no wall remained to hold one. It was not a doorway of stone or timber. It was an absence shaped like a tall man with ink-stained fingers—the shape of Edmund Hartwell, scribe, criminal, heretic, door—and around its edges the air bent like heat over a summer road. Through it came light. Not Choir light, not the pale administrative glow of sanctioned blessings. This light was the color of late afternoon in a country Quillon had never seen and had been taught, thoroughly and officially, did not exist.

Through it he could see orchards.

His blade was still up. He registered this distantly, the way you register a hand gone numb. The steel trembled—not from fear, though there was plenty of that, sloshing cold in his belly—but because his arm had stopped taking orders. Some older part of him, some boy-shaped part, had seized command and refused to strike.

"Brother?" Desset said. "Brother Quillon, do we advance?"

Quillon did not answer. He was looking at apple trees heavy with fruit that had never grown in Vessing's gray soil, fruit the color of lamplight, and beyond them a village with smoke rising straight from its chimneys because there was no wind, and beyond *that*—he squinted, he leaned, he hated himself for leaning—a range of hills wearing snow like a collar, hills that appeared on no map in the Choir's entire archive because every map showing them had been burned by men who wore his own vestments.

Osselmark.

Not ruins. Not the scorched stubble and broken shrine-walls his trackers had led him through these past three days. Not the country-as-wound the histories described, the godless scar left when the banishment came. Osselmark *whole*. Osselmark as it must have been before—as it perhaps still was, elsewhere, on the far side of the absence shaped like Edmund Hartwell.

*It isn't real,* said the training, forty years deep, laid down in lecture halls by cantors with dry voices and dry eyes. *It is a lure. It is the oldest lie the old gods told: the country that exists if only you'll step sideways out of your life. Every heresy begins with a window like that.*

But the training had never accounted for the smell.

Bread. That was the thing that broke him, later, when he tried to assemble the moment into words for the inquiry he knew would come. Not glory, not music, not any of the grand seductions scripture warned against. Bread. Woodsmoke and bread and underneath both something green and growing, the smell of a garden after rain, and his mouth flooded with saliva like a starving man's because Quillon could not remember the last time he had smelled anything growing. Vessing did not grow things. Vessing processed, Vessing recorded, Vessing filed its dead and taxed its living, and the only green in the capital was the moss on the prison walls.

Through the threshold, a child laughed.

Quillon's blade dipped another inch.

* * *

He had been nine years old when the Choir took him, and he remembered the day with a clarity he distrusted, because memory was a liar and faith was its alibi. But this much he trusted: he remembered being hungry. He remembered his mother trading their second blanket for a blessing that didn't take, a licensed charm from a licensed seller, stamped and tariffed and useless, while she coughed blood into a rag. He remembered praying—properly, correctly, kneeling on the cold boards with his hands folded exactly as the pamphlet showed—and remembering that heaven had gone quiet the way a house goes quiet after the argument ends. Not peaceful. Finished.

And he remembered a Choir recruiter finding him in the market, a big warm-handed man who bought him a meat pie and said, *You're angry, boy. Good. Anger is just love with nowhere to go. We have somewhere for it to go.*

Forty years. Somewhere for it to go.

He had climbed from orphan ward to acolyte to warden to Brother, and every rung had been paid for with the same currency: certainty. Certainty that the gods were gone because they deserved to be gone. Certainty that magic was debt and doors were disease. Certainty that the Hollow Choir, singing its great hymn to an empty heaven, was the honest thing left standing in a world of liars—and that men like him were the reason anyone slept safe.

He had told himself that story so long it had worn grooves in him, cart-tracks in mud, and everything ran along the grooves and nothing ever overflowed.

Until a girl named Maren Solle turned her cantor's art against her own hierarchy on the steps above his head. Until a scribe named Edmund Hartwell sang a hymn in a dead language and made the dark under the world bloom open like a wound that turned out to be a mouth, singing back.

Until now. Until bread and apples and a child laughing in a country that had been erased so cleanly that even its name felt contraband in Quillon's mouth.

"Brother." Desset again, closer now, tugging his sleeve. "The wardens are asking for orders. Wrenn says the divine residue is—" the tracker paused, visibly wrestling with his own senses— "it's everywhere, Brother. It's like standing in a river. Do we fall back to the stair?"

Fall back. Advance. Hold. The vocabulary of his whole life, and none of it fit. Because the question before him was not tactical. It had never been tactical. The question was the one the recruiter had answered for him with a meat pie forty years ago, and the answer had always been *anger*, and now, staring through the gap in the world at a woman in the distance—through the threshold he could see her, an old woman in a headscarf shaking apples down from a tree with the easy violence of someone who had done it ten thousand times—the anger had nowhere to go, because nothing on the other side of the door had ever hurt him.

The gods hadn't hurt him. He saw that suddenly, horribly clearly. The gods had simply *left*, or been pushed, and what remained had been the Choir, and the Choir had fed on the silence and called the silence holiness.

*We sealed them,* Maren had shouted, earlier, her voice cracking across the shrine like a bell falling downstairs. *Do you hear me? They didn't abandon us. We built a lock and called it a covenant.*

He had thought it heresy then. Standing here, smelling bread, he found he could no longer locate the difference between heresy and truth except that one of them required him to keep his sword up.

* * *

Hartwell stood at the threshold with his back to it, which meant he was shielding it, which meant he expected Quillon to try to close it, and Quillon understood—with the strange floating clarity of a man whose certainties have all been confiscated at once—that closing it would be the easiest thing in the world. One rite. One binding, spoken by a Brother of the Choir with a blade in his hand and wardens at his back, and the gap would seal, and Osselmark would go back to being a rumor, and the silence would resume its reign, and Quillon would sleep safe, and the safe sleep was the whole product, wasn't it, the entire inventory of his church: we sell nothing but the absence of terror, and we price it in everything you are.

The old woman in the headscarf looked up. Straight at him, across whatever impossible distance the threshold crossed, and Quillon went cold from scalp to heels because she was looking *at* him, seeing him, a warden crouched in the dark of a ruined shrine with murder in his hands, and she did not scream or flee or curse him.

She set down her basket. She studied him the way you study weather. Then she picked up the basket again and went back to her apples, unhurried, unafraid, as if armed men from another world were a thing a person simply got used to.

That undid him more than anything. More than the light, more than the hymn, more than Maren's accusations ringing off the undercroft walls. Fear he could have fought. Righteousness he could have met with righteousness. But that small domestic indifference—*oh, another one, they keep coming through there*—treated his holy war as background noise, treated forty years of his devotion as a fact of minor local interest—

He lowered his blade.

It happened without decision. Later, when Desset asked him—when everyone asked him, over and over, in the weeks of hearings that followed—he would say he chose, and there was truth in that, but the deeper truth was that his body chose first, the boy in the orphan ward choosing before the Brother could stop him, and the steel came down to his side with a soft clank that sounded, in the hush of the undercroft, like a verdict.

Desset stared at him. "Brother?"

"Hold position," Quillon said. His voice came out rusted. "No one advances. No one speaks a rite. That is an order."

"But—the High Cantor's directive—"

"I know what the directive is." He knew it by heart; he had carried it himself, sealed in wax, up three flights of stairs. *Seal the door. Seal the door-bearer. Painless, merciful, necessary.* He had read those words a hundred times on the road here and each reading had felt like armor.

Now they felt like the second blanket his mother had traded away for a charm that didn't work.

"Brother," Desset said, low, urgent, the note of a good soldier watching his officer come apart, "what do you see?"

And that was the cruelest question of all, because Quillon could not answer it honestly without ending his own career, his own faith, his own self, right there in the ash. What did he see? He saw a country. He saw supper being made somewhere past the orchard, because the smoke from one chimney had changed color, gone blue and thin. He saw a dog trotting along a fence line. He saw ordinary evening in a place the world had agreed to mourn as destroyed, and mourning it, the world had excused itself from ever wondering why.

"I see," Quillon said slowly, "that I have been guarding an empty room my whole life and calling the emptiness God."

Desset blinked. Behind him, half the wardens had drifted forward despite the order, drawn to the light the way moths are drawn, the way the hungry are drawn, and their faces—Quillon made himself look, made himself see it—young faces, hard faces, faces he had drilled and blessed and led—they were all doing what his face was doing. Coming apart. Quietly. In ranks.

One of them, a girl named Petra with a burn-scarred jaw, whispered: "Is that—is it true, Brother? Is that really—"

"It's Osselmark," Quillon said. And saying the name aloud, in defiance of every archive, every edict, every burned map, he felt something tear loose in his chest—not pain, worse than pain, pain he had a discipline for. It was weight. Forty years of it, lifting.

* * *

Hartwell was watching him. The scribe stood at the hinge of worlds with ash in his hair and blood on his lip—someone had hit him during the assault; Quillon's people, his responsibility—and he watched Quillon the way a man watches a wolf decide.

"You see it," Hartwell said. Not a taunt. A confirmation, almost gentle, and that gentleness stung worse than contempt would have.

"I see it."

"Then you know I'm not lying. About any of it. The Choir knew. They've always known." Hartwell's voice cracked on the word *knew*, and Quillon heard beneath it fifteen years of ledgers, of small lawful charms sold illegally in night markets because the lawful ones didn't work, and Quillon understood suddenly, completely, why the man had done it. Not rebellion. Repair. Selling working charms in the cracks of a system that sold broken ones at full price.

"I know," Quillon said.

"And you'll still try to stop us. You'll have to. It's your whole—" Hartwell gestured vaguely with a bloody hand— "everything."

Quillon looked at his blade. Plain steel, Choir issue, blessed by three cantors whose names he'd forgotten. He thought of the rite of sealing, the *painless erasure* the High Cantor had promised, mercy as a euphemism for unmaking. He thought of his mother's rag of blood. Of the meat pie. Of the empty heaven and the choir singing anyway, beautifully, into nothing, year after year after year, and everyone agreeing not to notice that no one was listening on the other end.

"No," he said.

Hartwell frowned. "No?"

"I won't stop you." Quillon sheathed the sword. The sound it made going home into the scabbard was the loudest thing he'd ever heard. "I don't know what I am now. I don't know what I believe. I know the Choir lied about the founding, and I know Maren Solle is telling the truth about the sealing, and I know—" he stopped, swallowed, started again, humiliated and freed in the same breath— "I know that woman out there shook apples off a tree and didn't care that I exist. No institution I've ever served has let me be that unimportant. It was the kindest thing I've felt in forty years."

Behind him, Desset made a small wounded noise. Petra had gone very still. The wardens were looking at their Brother with the expressions of children watching a lighthouse go dark—terror and, underneath the terror, unmistakably, relief.

"The High Cantor will have you struck from the rolls," Desset whispered. "Struck from the *rolls*, Brother. You'll be unpersoned. Unnamed."

"Yes," Quillon said. And then, testing it, tasting it: "Good. There's a country over there that knows how to say names kindly. I'd like to find out how that's done."

He did not know yet whether he meant to cross. He did not know whether the door would even permit it, whether it wanted him, whether a man made of grooves could walk on ground without them. He knew only this: his blade was down, his order stood—no rites, no advance—and between the Choir behind him and the orchard ahead, for the first time since a recruiter bought a hungry boy a meat pie, Brother Quillon belonged to no one.

Above them, muffled through stone, the assault resumed. Wardens' boots on shrine flagstones. The High Cantor's voice, silver and terrible, beginning a rite of unbinding that would bring the whole ruin down on their heads.

Quillon turned to face the stairwell, drew his blade again—not to fight the light, but to hold the dark—and gave the last order of his old life:

"They're coming. All of you who can still choose: choose fast."
