## 36. Letters to Nowhere

The first letter Maren wrote, she addressed to the Choir.

*To the Office of Reconciliation, Vessing Chapter, from Cantor Maren Solle, regarding the disposition of my credentials—*

She got four lines in before she stopped. The ink dried on the nib while she stared at the page. Four lines of cantor's script, each one a small act of self-erasure: *I respectfully submit. I acknowledge the irregularity. I request consideration.* Words she had spent fifteen years learning to shape into armor, and now they sat on the paper like shed skin, too thin to hold anything she actually meant.

She fed that letter to her stove instead of sending it.

The second letter was harder, because it was honest.

*Edmund.*

Just his name, and then nothing for a long time. She sat at the little table in her rented room above the dyer's shop—the room still smelled of mordant and wet wool, even after three months—and watched the candle gutter and thought about all the things she could not say to a man on the other side of a door that only opened one way.

*I am angry at you,* she finally wrote. *I want that recorded somewhere other than my own head, because my own head keeps softening it at night. You pushed me through. You kissed me like a signature on a contract I never agreed to. You gave me a debt the way your whole life was built out of debts, as if love were just another ledger-line, as if I could pay it off and be done with you.*

*And then I walked back across a country that had forgotten its own name, and every mile I was angrier, and by the time I reached Vessing I wasn't angry anymore, and that frightened me worse than anything Quillon ever did.*

*Here is what I have learned since. The Choir is fracturing exactly the way I told you it would. Half the cantors who heard my testimony have gone quiet in the way people go quiet when they are deciding which side of history to stand on. The High Cantor has been recalled to the capital for "consultation," which everyone understands to mean judgment. There are children in the lower city singing songs their grandparents were beaten for knowing, and no one is stopping them, because no one is sure anymore whether stopping them is law or habit.*

*You did that. Or rather—you opened something, and what came through wasn't a god so much as permission.*

*I miss you. I hate that I miss you. I hate more that I don't regret it—not the shrine, not the door, not the choice you made without me. I would make it again. That's the part I can't put in any report.*

*Maren*

She read it twice. Then she folded it, sealed it, and set it on the table, where it stayed for eleven days while she worked, testified, argued, slept badly, and told herself she was waiting for a delivery route that went anywhere near the old pilgrim road.

There was no such route. There was nowhere to send it. There was no Osselmark on any map, no postal office that recognized the country where Edmund Hartwell had gone to teach children songs. The letter had an address the way a prayer had an address—which was to say, none at all, and yet somehow, somehow, she could not shake the feeling that it would arrive anyway.

So she kept writing.

---

The letters accumulated through autumn the way sediment accumulates: slowly, then all at once. She wrote on the nights she couldn't sleep, which after the tribunal hearings was most of them. She wrote on cheap paper bought from the stationer who had once sold Edmund his charm-ledger stock, and if the man noticed whose name she asked for, he had the grace not to say.

*Edmund—I testified again today. Third time. The room was different this time; they'd moved us to the great hall, and there were observers from two other chapters. When I finished, an old cantor in the third row stood up and said, "I sealed doors. Eleven of them. I was told it was mercy." And then he sat down and wept, and nobody stopped him, and I thought: this is what the end of a lie sounds like. Not a crash. A room full of tired people admitting things.*

*Edmund—I dreamed about the binding rite last night. The one I performed on you, or tried to. In the dream I let it find you. In the dream I reported what I found, and you looked at me with such gentleness while they took you away, and I woke up with my heart going like a trapped bird. I've decided to write down the dreams too. You always said a ledger should record everything, even the entries that shame you. Especially those.*

*Edmund—it snowed. First snow. The gap in the shrine didn't close. I checked. Don't ask me why I checked. I was in the neighborhood. I was in the neighborhood because I am always in the neighborhood, and I have stopped pretending otherwise.*

*Edmund—I taught a hymn today. My first. Not a Choir hymn—an orchard hymn, one of the ones your grandmother's people sang before the burning. I learned it from a woman in Grayford who remembered fragments, and I stitched the missing verses myself, and I want you to know that if I stitched them wrong, it's your fault for leaving me the only person alive who cares enough to fix them. The children sang it badly. It was the best sound I have heard in fifteen years.*

She burned that one the same night she wrote it. She couldn't say why, except that some letters felt too alive to keep in a drawer, the way some songs felt wrong sung indoors.

---

It was the Grayford woman—Sera, her name was, Sera of the fragmentary hymns—who told her about the smoke.

Maren had come back to the ruined shrine outside Grayford in late autumn, ostensibly to survey the site for the historical commission she now half-served, half-defied. The truth was simpler and she'd stopped lying to herself about it: she came because the shrine was the closest thing to a conversation she had left.

The undercroft was open to the sky now. The assault had brought part of the ceiling down, and the commission had voted—narrowly, bitterly—to leave the ruin unrestored. *Let it stand as it fell*, the motion read. *A monument to what was done here, and what was undone.* So the broken stones lay where the fighting had thrown them, and the walls of names remained visible, five generations of Hartwells chiseled by hands that had hoped someone would someday read them, and in the center of the floor, the gap.

The crack. Not a wound—a crack. That was how she had come to think of it, in the months since. The door had closed behind Edmund, but it had not sealed clean. There remained a seam in the world, thin as a knife-cut in cloth, maybe a hand's width tall and half as wide, hovering a foot above the scorched flagstones. Through it, if you knelt and put your eye close, you could see—light. Not the light of this world. A light with weight to it, amber and slow, like honey held up to the sun. Sometimes it shifted, the way light shifts when something large moves between it and you.

She had never seen Edmund through it. She had stopped kneeling to look after the fourth month, because looking had begun to feel like drowning politely.

But Sera, who had come along to sing at the site—the woman insisted the place wanted songs, and Maren had long since given up arguing with her about what places wanted—Sera crouched by the crack with a twist of dried apple wood and a scrap of parchment, and without ceremony, lit the parchment and held it into the seam.

The smoke went *in*.

Not up. In. It bent sideways, impossibly, drawn through the gap like water finding a drain, and for a moment the amber light flickered—stuttered—the way a lamp flutters when someone passes it quickly.

Sera looked up at Maren with the mild expression of a woman reporting weather. "My grandmother used to do this," she said. "Before the ban. You burn a letter to someone past the border, and the smoke carries it. Nobody knew if it worked. Everybody did it anyway."

"And did it?" Maren asked. "Work?"

Sera smiled and tucked the apple wood away. "Nobody ever came back to say."

---

That winter, Maren burned forty-one letters.

She developed a liturgy without meaning to. She would travel to the shrine on the first day of each month—traveling papers signed now by an office that technically no longer had authority over her, one of the small absurdities of the interregnum—and she would kneel at the crack with the month's letters bundled in oilcloth, and she would feed them to the flame one at a time, holding each into the seam until the smoke bent inward and the light stuttered.

She never saw a reply. She never expected one. That was the discipline of it, and also the grief: a correspondence conducted entirely on one side, addressed to a country that existed elsewhere, delivered by combustion.

*Edmund—the tribunal concluded. The Choir, as an institution, will be dissolved by spring. What replaces it is being argued over by people who all believe they are right, which means it will be argued over for years, which I think is probably healthy. I have been offered a position in whatever comes next. I haven't accepted yet. I keep thinking about what you said in the undercroft, that institutions aren't evil, they're just deaf, and the work is teaching them to hear. I don't know if I believe that anymore. But I'm considering it, which is more faith than I had in summer.*

*Edmund—Quillon came to see me. Not to arrest anyone. He wanted to know if I thought what he saw through the threshold counted as conversion or hallucination. I told him it counted as both, and that the difference mattered less than what he did next. He's teaching now. Did you know that? Brother Quillon, who hunted you across three provinces, spends his days showing farm children how to bind a hedge against frost without asking anyone's permission. He still wears the grey. He says it's practical. I think it's penance. Both can be true.*

*Edmund—I found your ledger. Your actual charm-ledger, from the Vessing stall, in a box of confiscated effects the commission was cataloguing. Fifteen years of small kindnesses in your cramped, careful hand. I sat in the archive and read every entry. Do you know what struck me? Not the charms. The margins. Every margin had something written in it—a joke, a complaint about the weather, a note that Mrs. Aldery's cough sounded better this week. You weren't selling magic, Edmund. You were keeping parish records for a parish that didn't know it had a priest. I cried in the archive. The archivist pretended not to notice, which is the kindest thing the institution has ever done for me.*

*Edmund—I'm not going to tell you about the nights I can't sleep. You'd only worry, and worry travels badly through smoke. Instead I'll tell you this: the well in the temple courtyard, the one that went dry the year the gods were banished—it refilled. On its own. No charm, no rite. Just water, rising, the way water does when nothing is holding it down anymore. I stood at the lip of it for an hour. I thought about you standing at the lip of a door, choosing. I thought: he would have liked this. Simple. Unprompted. Free.*

Some months she wrote one letter. Some months six. The bundle for the deep-winter burning was thick enough that she had to tie it with cord, and she caught herself weighing it in her hand like bread, like something nourishing, and laughed alone in her room until the laughing turned into the other thing, and then stopped that too, because mourning that refused despair had rules, and one of them was: you get to cry, but you don't get to stop writing.

---

In the spring of the second year, the letters changed.

They grew shorter. Not from diminishing feeling—from distillation. She had said the large things. What remained were the small ones, and the small ones, she discovered, were the ones that mattered.

*Edmund—the plum tree outside my window bloomed early. I took it as a message and then decided that was superstition, and then decided I didn't care.*

*Edmund—a child asked me today if the god Ashenveil was kind. I said I didn't know, but I knew a man he talked to, and the man was kind, and I was willing to bet the god learned it from him.*

*Edmund—I burned your name into the new register today. Not the Choir's register. Mine. The open choir—we voted on a name, we're calling it the Choir of Whatever Listens, which is either blasphemy or honesty, and we couldn't decide, so we kept it. Your name is in the founding members' column. I forged your mark. You'd have hated it. You'd have secretly loved it.*

*Edmund—today I did nothing worth reporting. I mended a cloak. I ate bread. I watched the river. I am recording this deliberately, because I spent fifteen years believing that only extraordinary entries deserved ink, and I am unlearning it, and I want you to witness it, wherever you are, however smoke travels.*

And then, in high summer, a letter she almost didn't burn:

*Edmund—I met someone who is going to ask me to marry him. I can see it coming the way you see weather coming. He's a bridge engineer from Grayford. He's steady and funny and he has never once made me feel like a case to be managed. I am telling you this because you told me to live loudly, and because I need you to hear it, and because if some part of you on the other side of that crack feels a pang at these words, then good—that means the smoke works, and you're real, and somewhere a country exists where you are teaching children to sing slightly out of tune.*

*I haven't said yes. I'm not going to decide based on a ghost-pang. I'm going to decide based on my life, which is here, which is loud, which is mine.*

*But I wanted you to know that I checked.*

She held that letter over the flame longer than any other. The smoke rose straight for a moment—rose the way ordinary smoke rises, toward an ordinary sky—and then, just before the last corner curled, it bent. Inward. Hard. The amber light beyond the crack flared bright enough to throw her shadow backward across the flagstones, and she heard—or felt, or imagined, she would never be able to prove which—a sound like a single voice, far away, saying her name.

Not *Maren Solle*. Just *Maren*. The way he said it.

She knelt there a long time after the ash settled, one hand flat on the warm stone beside the seam, and the stone was warm, though the season was cold, and she understood suddenly that the warmth had never been the fire's.

"You absolute fool," she said softly, to a man who could not hear her, in a country that did not exist. "You heard that one, didn't you."

The light pulsed once. Gently. Like laughter, or like a hand squeezed once and released.

She got up, brushed the ash from her skirts, and walked back down the pilgrim road toward the living world, and if her step was lighter than it had been in two years, that was between her and the road.

---

The last letter of that year, she wrote on the anniversary of the opening.

*Edmund—two years. The crack hasn't widened and it hasn't closed. The commission calls it stable. I call it patient. Everything true, I've noticed, is patient.*

*The new order is unbuilt and building. There are more of us than there were. The children in the lower city sing louder. Sera has started a school. Quillon grows roses now, of all things, and pretends not to care whether they bloom, and cares enormously. I said yes, by the way. To the engineer. You may consider this the debt collected. Live loudly—I did, I do, I will. But I want it noted in the ledger, in the margins, in the place where the real records are kept: I said yes, and I thought of you while I said it, and that is not disloyalty to either of you. It's just what a whole life looks like. It has more than one name in it.*

*This is my last letter for a while. Not because I'm stopping. Because I've realized something, kneeling here month after month: I've been writing to nowhere because I thought nowhere was where you were. But nowhere isn't a place, Edmund. It's a direction. And lately, when the smoke bends inward, when the light stutters, when the stone stays warm under my hand—I've started to wonder if the direction has been pointing both ways the whole time.*

*So: last letter for a while. Not goodbye. Just a change of address.*

*Yours in the margins,
M.*

She folded it small, the way he used to fold his charm-papers, creased tight so it would fit through narrow spaces. She lit it at the crack itself, touched the burning corner to the seam, and let the fire take it slowly, watching the smoke bend inward, watching the amber light hold steady, brighter than she had ever seen it, bright as a held breath.

And then—

The light *answered*.

Not a pulse this time. Not a stutter. The seam shivered along its whole length, and from within it came a faint, dry rustle, like paper moving against paper, like a page turning somewhere on the far side of the world.

Something was coming through.

Maren went very still, kneeling in the ruins with the last of the ash drifting down around her, and watched as a pale shape slid through the crack—thin as a leaf, folded small, creased tight so it would fit through narrow spaces.

A letter.

Addressed, in a cramped, careful hand she would have known anywhere, in any world, in any life:

*To Maren. From nowhere.*
