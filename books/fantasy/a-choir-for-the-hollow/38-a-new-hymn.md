## 38. A New Hymn

The building had been a granary before the fracture, and before that—according to the woman who sold it to Maren for almost nothing—a chapel of some older faith whose name nobody remembered. It stood at the edge of the Weavers' Quarter, where the streets went crooked and the city's new order hadn't yet decided what it wanted to be. The roof leaked in two places. The floor was scored with the ghost-lines of old grain bins. There was no altar, which was the point.

Maren stood in the doorway on the first morning and let the cold air of the place settle over her like a garment she was trying on for size.

She had resigned her cantor's post eleven days ago. Resigned—not fled, not been expelled, though both had been discussed behind closed doors she could still hear through, sometimes, in dreams. She had walked into High Cantor Verel's office with her credentials and the evidence she'd carried back from Grayford and the shrine, laid both on his desk, and waited while he read. He had read for a long time. When he looked up, his face had the particular blankness of a man watching a wall crack in a way that could not be repaired.

"You understand," he had said, "that if this goes where it is going, there will be no Choir to return to."

"I understand," Maren said, "that there may finally be something worth returning to that isn't a Choir at all."

He had signed her release without another word. It was, she suspected, the kindest thing he had done in thirty years of office—or the most cowardly, depending on how history sorted it out. Perhaps those were the same act, seen from different sides of the ledger. She was learning to stop sorting things into ledgers altogether. Edmund would have laughed at how long it was taking her.

The granary needed work. So did everything else in Vessing, these days. The city had spent six weeks tearing itself along its oldest seam—the faithful against the fearful, the ones who wanted their gods back and the ones who remembered what it cost when gods answered—and then, exhausted, had begun the slower, harder business of deciding what to build in the wreckage. Martial prayer-law had collapsed when its own wardens started weeping during enforcement patrols. The Choir still existed, technically, still sang vespers to an empty heaven every evening, but fewer people came each week, and the ones who came came for different reasons than they used to. Some came to mourn. Some came to argue. One old man came every night to sit in the last pew and shake his fist at the rafters, which Maren privately thought might be the most honest prayer the Choir had received in fifteen years.

What Vessing did not have—what no one seemed willing to build—was a third option. A place between the silence and the siege.

Maren had a small inheritance from her mother. She had a granary. She had a voice trained in rites designed to fill an empty heaven with borrowed purpose, which meant she knew better than anyone alive how to sing to nothing and mean it.

Now she meant it differently.

* * *

The first notices she posted were so plain they were almost an apology:

**A CHOIR FOR ANY WHO SING. No faith required. No faith forbidden. Bring your own words or borrow ours. First gathering: dusk, the old granary on Tanner's Row.**

She expected three people. She got forty-one.

They arrived the way stray animals arrive at a bowl set out by someone who didn't expect them—cautiously, in ones and twos, testing the air. A baker's widow who had lost two sons to the riots. A former warden who kept his hands folded very tightly, as if they might otherwise do something. Two sisters who had prayed at the shrine before the fracture and sworn off all of it after, and come anyway, because swearing off is not the same as being free. A boy of perhaps twelve who had never belonged to anything and hovered near the door the whole evening, ready to bolt.

Maren had prepared a speech. She abandoned it.

"Here's what I know," she said instead, standing in the grain-scored light of the last window. "I spent nine years singing hymns written for gods we'd outlawed, to a heaven we'd sealed shut, and I told myself it was enough because everyone around me told themselves the same thing. That's what an institution is, mostly. Everyone agreeing not to check whether the song lands anywhere." She paused. "I'm done pretending. I don't know if anything listens. I know that *you* listen. And I know that somewhere far away, past a door that's standing ajar, someone is listening too. So we're going to sing to whatever's out there. Gods if they're listening. Neighbors if they're not. Ourselves, always. That's the whole liturgy. That's the entire theology. Sing, and see."

Silence. Then the baker's widow, in a cracked and unlovely voice, began to hum an old harvest tune—the kind of tune that predated the Choir, that had survived in kitchens and fields where institutions never thought to look. The warden joined her, badly. The sisters harmonized. The boy by the door did not sing, but he stopped hovering, and sat down on a grain bin, and stayed.

And the air—

Maren would spend years trying to describe what happened next, and never quite manage it. The air did not speak. It did not shimmer or thunder or perform any of the miracles the old texts promised. What happened was smaller and stranger and infinitely more frightening: the room grew *warm*. Not heated—warmed, the way a house is warm when people are home inside it. The draft from the broken roof eased. The candle flames leaned, all together, toward the center of the room, like flowers finding sun.

It lasted four seconds. Then it was gone, and forty-one people stood looking at each other in ordinary lamplight, and nobody spoke for a long moment, and then the widow laughed—one short, startled bark of a laugh—and said, "Well. Someone left the stove on."

But Maren had felt it. Felt the attention, faint and vast and patient, the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck in an empty street. Something had listened. Not commanded. Not demanded. Listened.

She thought: *this is what he found.* Somewhere beyond the door, in a country erased from every map, Edmund Hartwell was teaching children songs, and something on the other side of the crack in the world had leaned close to hear.

She finished the evening hoarse and shaking, and locked the granary door behind the last stragglers, and stood alone in the dark with her hand pressed flat against the wood, laughing until it turned into something else entirely.

* * *

Word spread the way word does—not through proclamation but through retelling, slightly wrong each time, which was exactly right. By the fourth gathering there were ninety people and no room for them, so they sang in the yard. By the seventh, someone had repaired one of the roof leaks without being asked, and someone else had built benches from salvaged grain-bin planks, and a carpenter named Odell had carved a sign that read, in letters deliberately plain: **THE OPEN CHOIR — SING TO WHAT LISTENS.**

Maren insisted on no collection plate. The Choir had taught her everything about the machinery of obligation—how debt binds, how scarcity hoards, how an institution keeps its people by keeping them needing. The Open Choir would run on the opposite engine. People brought what they had: bread, candles, a secondhand harmonium, an hour of labor, a voice. When she protested once that they couldn't sustain it on generosity alone, the widow fixed her with a look.

"Cantor Solle. You gave us back our own voices. You think bread is going to bankrupt us?"

She was still *Cantor* to half of them, though she'd asked them not to be. Old habits. She was learning that you cannot legislate affection out of a title any faster than you can legislate it in. So she let them call her Cantor, and she called herself nothing at all, and slowly, week by week, she discovered that an identity belonging to no institution was not a void—it was a room with all the windows open.

There were hard nights. A delegation from the remnant Choir came to observe, taking notes with expressions like men auditing a fire. A mob of the fearful gathered once outside the fence, shouting that answered prayers had killed their sons, their daughters, their livelihoods—and Maren walked out alone, unarmed, heart hammering, and offered them the only thing she had: "Come in and shout at us, then. Shout is a kind of singing. We've room for it." Half of them left. Three of them came back the next week, sullen and silent, and one of those three eventually sang, in a voice like gravel, a lament for his brother, and the whole choir learned it and sang it back to him, and he wept openly in the third row and never missed a Thursday again.

That was the difference, Maren realized, between the Choir she'd served and the thing growing under her hands. The Hollow Choir had sung *at* an empty heaven and called the echo holiness. Here, they sang *to each other*, and the echo had faces.

But always, underneath, the other listener. Always the warmth in the air on certain notes, certain nights. Never commanded, never demanding worship, never asking for anything at all—which unsettled her more than demands would have. Fifteen years in the Choir had taught her that power wants. Whatever stirred at the edge of their songs wanted nothing. It simply attended, the way a grandmother attends a child's recital: present, pleased, undemanding.

Ashenveil, she thought once, mid-hymn. Or whatever remained of him on this side of the door. Or maybe just the memory of a country that existed elsewhere, bleeding warmth back through the crack Edmund had left open. She had stopped needing to know. That, more than anything, told her how far she'd come.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and with it the first true test of what they'd built. Fever swept the Weavers' Quarter—the ordinary, mortal kind, the kind no god had ever been obliged to answer and none would now. The old Maren would have processed to the Cathedral, filed the proper intercessions, received the proper denials. The new Maren did something simpler and more radical: she opened the granary day and night, turned it into a fever-house, and put the choir to work.

Not praying. *Working.* They carried water, boiled linens, sat with the dying so no one crossed alone. The widow knew herb-lore; the warden had field-surgeon training from his service; the sisters cooked. They sang, too—not intercessions, not petitions, just songs, constantly, filling the sickrooms with harvest tunes and laments and one ridiculous drinking ballad the boy (who now never left) had taught everyone. The dying heard human voices. That was all the miracle on offer, and it was enough, and Maren watched it be enough and understood at last, in her bones, what Edmund had tried to tell her on the pilgrim road: that the hollow was never in heaven. It was in the pretending. Fill the room with actual presence—hands, voices, breath—and the absence above stops mattering.

On the worst night, when the fever peaked and three lay dying at once, the whole choir gathered in the yard in the snow and sang the great hymn—the Hollow Hymn itself, the one Maren had been inducted with, the one sung to an empty heaven—but they changed one word. Where the old text said *to the silence we lift our vow*, they sang *through the silence*. Through, not to. Past the ceiling, past the sealed sky, past the crack in the world a hundred miles north, to wherever a scribe with ash-brown eyes was teaching children the names of trees.

The air went warm again. Warmer than before. Warmer than it had ever been. The snow directly above the yard, for the space of one verse, fell upward.

Nobody spoke about it afterward. But everybody remembered.

* * *

Spring found the Open Choir with two hundred voices, a repaired roof, a waiting list for Thursdays, and a problem Maren had not anticipated: people wanted doctrine. They wanted to know what to believe. They kept approaching her after services with careful, hungry questions—*is the god real, should we pray to him, will he answer, are we allowed*—and every time, she felt the old machinery trying to rebuild itself through her, the institution coalescing around whatever warmth they'd felt, wanting to codify it, gate it, own it.

So she did the thing that scared her most.

One Thursday in late spring, she climbed onto the grain-bin platform and held up a single sheet of paper.

"This is the complete doctrine of the Open Choir," she said. "I wrote it myself. Listen close, because I'll only say it once." She read:

"*Something is listening. So are we. Be kind to both.*"

She folded the paper and set it on the platform. "That's it. If anyone tells you there's more—if anyone starts selling you the rest, charging you for the rest, making you earn the rest—they're lying, and they're building the same prison we tore down, just with newer bars. The warmth you feel isn't a bargain. It isn't a debt. It has never once asked us for anything, and we are not going to become the people who ask on its behalf." Her voice caught, and she let it. "Someone taught me that. Someone who spent fifteen years paying a debt that was never real, and walked through a door rather than let anyone else be owned by it. Everything good in this room traces back to that choice. Remember him."

They did remember him. In the way communities do—obliquely, in practice rather than statuary. They called the repaired roof "Hartwell's shingles" because the timber had come from a wagon bound for the shrine. The boy took to leaving one bench empty at every gathering, and when newcomers asked why, the regulars just smiled and said, "For whoever's coming back." Nobody explained further. Faith, Maren was discovering, could survive perfectly well without explanation. It was certainty it couldn't survive.

* * *

Summer. The longest day. The choir held its first festival—no rite, no rite at all, just music from dusk until dawn, the granary doors flung wide, the whole quarter invited. People came from districts that had rioted against each other in the winter. They sang warily at first, then less warily, then not warily at all, and somewhere past midnight the boundaries dissolved the way boundaries do in music, which is the only place they dissolve easily.

Near dawn, Maren stepped outside into the grey-gold light and the cooling air, and stood alone in the yard, throat raw, heart full past capacity.

The air moved.

Not warm this time. Present. Utterly, unmistakably present, the way a person is present when they step up beside you and say nothing, needing no introduction. Maren did not turn. She knew—Irrevocably knew—that if she turned she would see either nothing or everything, and neither was hers to see. That sight belonged to someone else, on the other side of a crack in the world.

Instead she spoke softly, to the morning, to the presence, to the space between:

"He's happy there. Tell him. However you carry things across—you can carry that."

The presence stayed a long moment. Long enough that she felt something like amusement, something like gratitude, something almost like a message forming—though whether it came from a tired old god, or the country itself, or simply her own heart speaking in a borrowed register, she never tried to determine.

Then it withdrew, gently, the way a hand withdraws from a shoulder.

And in its wake, on the dust of the granary doorstep, the breeze had arranged a small scatter of pale ash into a shape too deliberate to ignore: a single line, neat as a scribe's pen-stroke, in a hand she would have known anywhere in the world.

*Write to me.*

Maren stood very still. Dawn came up around her. Inside, the last singers were trailing into silence, and the first birds were starting, and the whole enormous ordinary world was waking into a day that belonged to no institution, no god, no ledger—only to whoever chose to live it.

She looked at the ash-written line for a long time.

Then she went inside, found paper and ink among the choir's supplies, and sat down at the grain-bin table as the light strengthened, and began, for the first time in her life, to write a letter with no address—only a direction, and a door, and a name she intended to say aloud the whole way there.
