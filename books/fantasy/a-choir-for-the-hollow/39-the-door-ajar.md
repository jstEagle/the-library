## 39. The Door Ajar

*From the papers of Maren Solle, recovered from the shrine at Grayford, undated:*

The first letter I burned was an apology. The second was a curse. By the tenth I had stopped keeping count, and by the twentieth I had stopped pretending the letters were for anyone but me.

I write them anyway. Every week, on the same evening, in the same chair I dragged from the ruined tollhouse to the edge of the undercroft, where the crack in the world still breathes its slow grey light. Three years now, and the gap has not widened and has not closed. It is exactly what Edmund left it as: ajar. A door held open not by hinges but by habit—by my handwriting, by the smoke of my burning, by whatever patience lives on the other side.

The Choir would call it superstition. But the Choir is three years dead as an institution, whatever it calls itself now, and I have learned that most institutions are just superstition with better filing.

So: tonight's letter.

*Edmund —*

*Vessing has streetlamps again. Real ones, lit by the new guilds, though half the city still swears the old wells taste sweeter since the prayers came back, and who am I to argue with a well?*

*I taught my first open choir class on Tuesday. Eleven people. A baker with a voice like gravel, two sisters who harmonize without meaning to, a boy of maybe nine who asked me if gods get lonely. I said I'd ask someone I know. He laughed. He thought I was joking.*

*I wasn't.*

*The High Cantor's trial concluded last month. You'd have found it tedious—all testimony and ledgers, which is fitting, since ledgers were the whole crime. Fifteen years of sealed doors filed under "administrative necessity." They are unsealing them now, one by one. Some of the doors hold nothing but old names. One of them, they say, holds something still breathing. I have asked to be present when they open it. Not to fight it. To sing to it, if it wants singing to. Most things do.*

*Maren*

I read it aloud to the crack, because that's what I do now, and then I fed it to the flame and watched the smoke lean—not drift, *lean*—toward the grey light, the way it always does, the way grass leans toward a fence line where a kind hand waits.

And then I waited.

You have to understand what waiting looks like when you're waiting on a god's postal service. It isn't dramatic. There is no thunder. What happens is this: the air above the crack goes still in a way air doesn't, the way water goes still before it remembers it's a mirror. And then, sometimes, if the season is right and the debt of weeks has been paid, something comes through.

Not paper. Paper won't cross; we learned that early. The door passes voice, or the shape of voice. What arrives is a warmth in the ash ring where I burn the letters—a warmth with edges, like a word spoken into your cupped hands. And in that warmth, faintly, a line or two of Edmund's voice, worn thin by distance but unmistakably his: dry, careful, a little amazed at itself.

Tonight it came.

*"Maren. The orchard's blooming. Actual blossoms—I know, I'm as surprised as you. Ashenveil says the trees remember being burned twice and decided against a third time. The children here sing your hymn back to front, which means they've made it better. Tell the baker his bread prayer was received and ruled upon favorably."*

A pause. Then, softer, closer, the way a voice gets when someone leans near:

*"Tell the boy who asked about lonely gods: yes. That's why doors exist."*

I sat in my chair until the warmth faded, and then I did what I always do, which is write down every word before it can escape me, and then I did what I never used to do, which is laugh out loud alone in a ruined shrine, loud enough that the wrens flew up out of the rafters.

Three years ago I could not have told you what joy sounded like in an empty room. Now I own the patent.

*From the papers of Edmund Hartwell, kept in the schoolhouse at Osselmark-in-exile-no-longer, undated:*

Maren thinks the letters only go one way at a time. She thinks I wait for her smoke and answer it. She's wrong, and I've decided not to correct her, because a man should be allowed one small mystery after everything.

Here is the truth: I write too. Every week. I write into the crack from my side, and Ashenveil carries the words across the way wind carries seed—not all of it lands, but enough. Enough that some mornings Maren burns a letter and hears me answering things she hasn't yet said, which unnerves her beautifully, and which she attributes to divine timing rather than to the fact that her husband-of-no-formal-standing has been eavesdropping on her drafts for years.

She leaves her drafts on the chair. She thinks the ruin keeps her secrets. Ruins keep nothing; that's their whole charm.

This week's draft, which I will pretend I haven't read:

*Maren — (draft, unsent, crossed out twice)*

*Edmund. The unsealed door in the Vessing archive held a sleeper. Not a god—an old cantor, sealed alive in 14— for asking the same question I asked. She woke confused and hungry and furious, and the first thing she demanded was tea, and the second thing she demanded was to know if the Choir had apologized. It has not. I have taken it upon myself to begin apologizing on its behalf, starting with her and working outward.*

*I find I am good at this work. Unbuilding. I used to think I was only good at copying. But copying is just unbuilding done politely.*

*Come home. Not to Vessing—the Vessing part is negotiable. Just come home. The door goes both ways now, doesn't it? You said the crack breathes. Well. Breathe through it.*

*— Maren*

*(the rest crossed out, which means she meant it)*

I sat with that draft for a long time, there in the schoolhouse with the evening coming gold through windows that look out on an orchard in blossom, on a country that exists, on children learning songs their grandparents were jailed for humming.

Because here is the thing I have not told her, and it is the last true thing I own: I have been afraid to come back. Not of the Choir—it barely has teeth anymore. Not of her—never of her. Of the arithmetic. Fifteen years of ledger-lines taught me that everything balances, that every gift is a debit somewhere, that no door opens without a price named aloud. I paid mine. I walked through alone. And some stubborn clerk in my chest keeps insisting that if I step back through, the account reopens, and this time the currency will be her.

But tonight the children sang her hymn back to front, and it was better, and I thought: *that's what happens when you stop guarding a song and start sharing it.* Doors too, maybe. Maybe a door that opened freely stays free. Maybe the whole lesson of my grandmother's country, burned twice and blooming anyway, is that some debts were never real—they were just written down by people who profited from the writing.

So I am going to answer her properly. Not through the crack. Across it.

Ashenveil says the threshold will hold for a crossing made in joy, where it failed for one made in sacrifice. The god has become almost cheerful about this, in its tired, patient way. "You built the hinge," it told me. "Hinges swing both directions. That is the entire point of hinges."

Gods. Give them fifteen hundred years of silence and they come back with opinions about carpentry.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow I walk to the shrine-from-the-other-side, and I put my hand flat against the grey light, and I say her name the way you say a word you've finally learned to pronounce.

*The country exists. So do I. Come see.*

*Final entry, Maren Solle's papers:*

He didn't write first. He never does—he lets me fill the silence and then answers the letter I was really writing instead of the one I sent. Three years of this. I ought to be annoyed. I am, a little. It's one of the ten thousand reasons.

Last night I burned the weekly letter and waited, and the warmth came, and in it his voice—but different. Closer than close. Not carried. *Standing.*

"Maren," he said, from the other side of a crack thinner than a coin, "step back a little."

I stepped back. Old habits.

And the grey light moved. It didn't widen like a wound opening—it swung, the way a door swings when someone on the far side lifts the latch, easy, unhurried, the way you open a door for family. Light the color of late afternoon poured through the undercroft, and it smelled—and I will swear this on any ledger you like—it smelled of blossom and woodsmoke and rain on warm stone, and underneath it, unmistakable after three years, tea.

He stood in the doorway. Older. Sun-browned. Wearing the expression of a man who has finally finished a very long piece of copying and is pleased with the result.

"You left your drafts on the chair," he said.

"You read my drafts?"

"Ruins keep nothing. That's their whole charm."

I had three years of letters in my throat and what came out was: "You absolute—"

"I know," he said. "Come home. The Vessing part is negotiable."

I looked back once—at the chair, at the ash ring, at the little pile of unburned letters I'd been saving against hope like a miser—and then I didn't look back at all, because he had put out his hand across the threshold, palm up, the way you offer something rather than take it, and I have spent my whole life learning the difference.

I took it.

The door stayed open behind us. Hinges, apparently, swing both ways. Somewhere on the far side a god was singing, off-key, in a choir of children who had improved the hymn, and somewhere on this side a city was unbuilding its old silence into something you could live in, and between the two, thin as a coin and strong as a vow, a door stood ajar—

—which is, it turns out, the only way doors were ever meant to be.

We walked through together.

The country exists. So do I. So, at last, do we.
