## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The morning poison was hemlock, cut with honey, which is how you know a court respects you: they don't waste good honey on the poisons you're meant to survive.

I took my dose at dawn, as I had every dawn for eleven years, standing in my narrow room above the kitchens with the shutter open to the grey light of the Valley of the Vow. The hemlock went down easy. It's the aftertaste you wait for — a faint sweetness at the back of the throat, like something rotting politely — and when it came, I counted my pulse to a hundred and pronounced myself alive for another day.

This is the rhythm of my life, and I will describe it plainly, because plainness is the whole trick of it. A food-taster who is interesting dies interestingly. A food-taster who is furniture eats breakfast.

At the seventh bell I presented myself to the king's under-steward, a damp man named Corvel who smelled of ink and anxiety, and I tasted the king's breakfast: barley porridge, salted river fish, bread, a compote of valley plums. I held each mouthful on my tongue for the count of thirty, swallowed, waited the full quarter-hour between dishes. The king, meanwhile, sat watching me with the fond, frightened patience of a man who has outlived two tasters and has decided the third is family.

"Eat properly today, Ivo," he said, when the fish passed. "You're thin."

"I'm always thin, Majesty."

"It worries me. A taster should look like she enjoys her work."

I did enjoy my work, in the way a man enjoys a wall he's leaning against. What I enjoyed, if anything, was the arithmetic of it: eleven years of measured doses, each one a little braver than the last, my blood learning the alphabet of death one letter at a time. Hemlock. Foxglove. The pale mushrooms that grow on the north side of the seal-stones and taste, I am told by people who eat them recreationally and survive, of almonds and regret.

I was, by any honest measure, the most poisoned woman in the valley. Possibly the world. The valley being sealed, I had no way to check.

* * *

The numbness started in my left hand two winters ago. Just the fingertips, at first — a curious absence, as if the fingers had been dipped in cold water and never quite dried. I told no one. Tasters with tremors are retired, and retired tasters are given a cottage and a stipend and are never again permitted near anything worth tasting, which is to say, never again permitted to matter.

By this spring it had climbed past the wrist. Some mornings I dropped the tasting spoon and had to pick it up twice, laughing as though I were clumsy, and the king would laugh too, because he is a kind man, and kind men laugh at the wrong things.

I dosed myself that evening as usual and sat with my hands in my lap like two dead birds, and I thought, as I thought every evening: *not yet. Another year. Another season. I only need it to hold until—*

Until what, I never finished the sentence. That should have told me something.

* * *

The child came to the valley gate on the last day of the plum harvest.

I wasn't there for anything so dramatic. I was there for the plums. The gate garrison takes a tithe of the harvest as it comes in from the terraces, and every barrel, every basket, every jar of preserved fruit gets a taster's sample, because poison in the king's wine cellar is tragedy but poison in the valley's stores is famine. So I stood at the little table by the gate with my tasting spoon and my notebook, and I sipped plum wine and spat plum skins, and I was bored in the deep, professional way that I had mistaken for peace.

The gate itself is the oldest thing in the valley — older than the king's line, older than the road, a double arch of black stone fitted without mortar, carved over with letters in no script anyone reads. The Vow-Wall runs out from it on either side, up the mountain shoulders and into the cloud, a grey seam in the world. Children are taught it keeps the wild weather out. Adults are taught it keeps everything else out too. Nobody is taught what *everything else* is. That's the valley for you: we seal the door and then agree not to look at it.

The child came through the gate at noon.

Not through the gate — *through* it, the way you'd walk through a doorway, except the gate hadn't opened, because the gate doesn't open, because the gate hasn't opened in living memory. One moment the arch was empty. The next, a small figure stood in the shadow of it, barefoot, in a coat too big, with dust on their face and their mouth already moving.

The garrison went for their spears, as garrisons do. I went for the child, as fools do, because the child was maybe eight years old and shaking, and because something in my chest had gone strange and cold, the way it goes before I taste something that will hurt me.

"Stop," I said, in the way you say it to a frightened animal. "Stop, I'm not —"

The child answered. Not in valley speech. The sounds came out low and layered, vowels I had no names for, and I should have understood nothing at all.

I understood half of it.

I don't mean I guessed. I mean the words arrived in me already half-translated, like a bell rung in a room I'd been in before, though I had never been in any such room. *— the door was open, the door was open, I ran —* something like that. Something like *I ran*.

"Slowly," I said, and my voice came out wrong, and the child cocked their head and said something else, and I caught the shape of *hungry* and the shape of *please*, and behind both, underneath both, a word I did not understand but felt in my teeth, the way you feel a storm coming in an old scar.

The garrison captain arrived at a run. "Stand back from it, Mistress Eastwick. It came out of the Wall. It could be anything."

"It's a child," I said.

"It came out of the Wall," he repeated, and I heard what he meant: *nothing comes out of the Wall. Therefore it is not a nothing. Therefore it is worse.*

And then the light changed.

I have no better way to put it. The noon sun on the black arch of the gate flickered — flickered, the way a candle does in a draught — and for one heartbeat the carvings on the stone were not carvings. They were bright. Lines of pale fire, running along the letters no one reads, and I swear to you the letters moved, rearranging themselves like ants disturbed under a log, and the air through the arch smelled of rain on hot stone, a smell that does not exist in the valley, where rain comes down the mountain and arrives smelling of nothing but rain.

Then it was stone again. Grey, dumb, sealed. The garrison crossed themselves with the wrong hand, which is what you do when you're frightened and your catechism leaves you.

I looked at my notebook, where I'd been recording plum samples. My hand, the numb one, was shaking so badly the ink had jumped. And in my chest, that cold strange feeling resolved itself into a sentence I finished at last, eleven years late:

*Until the seal breaks. I was always meant for the day the seal breaks.*

Because here is what I knew, standing there with plum wine on my breath and my dead fingers curled around a tasting spoon: my grandmother had been the taster before me. And her mother before her. And we had all dosed ourselves, and we had all endured, and I had always believed it was tradition, or stubbornness, or the particular madness of Eastwick women.

But immunity like mine isn't built by tradition. It's built *for* something. You don't breed a woman's blood to hold every poison in the world unless someday the world is going to need a woman who can hold it.

The child looked up at me and spoke again, and this time I caught almost all of it, and what I caught was a name.

Not the child's name.

*My* name — or the old shape of it, the valley's oldest name, the name carved on the gate, the name no one reads.

The garrison captain took my arm. "Mistress Eastwick. You've gone white. Shall I send for the physic?"

"No," I said, and put away my spoon, and looked a long moment at the black arch and the small dusty child standing in its shadow like a key left standing in a lock. "Send for the archivist. And someone find this child some bread."

Because whatever was coming through that gate had come looking, and of all the people in the valley, it had looked at *me* — and I wanted, before anyone told me what I already knew, to hear it said out loud by the one man in the valley who read dead languages for a living, and who wrote me letters I pretended not to reread.

Wren arrived within the hour, out of breath, ink-stained, delighted — Wren is always delighted by catastrophe, it's his worst quality — and the first thing he said when he saw the child's mouth moving was, "Ivo. That's not possible. That tongue died with the covenant."

"And the second thing?" I asked.

He looked at the flickering gate, then at me, and his delight went out like a snuffed lamp.

"The second thing," he said, "is that I think you'd better come and read your grandmother's papers."
