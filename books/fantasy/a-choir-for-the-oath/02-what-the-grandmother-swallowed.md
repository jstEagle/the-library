## 2. What the Grandmother Swallowed

My grandmother's house smelled the way it had always smelled, which is to say of dust and dried yarrow and something faintly bitter underneath that I had spent twenty years pretending was old plaster. It was not old plaster. I knew that now. Old plaster does not make your tongue tingle.

I stood in her bedchamber on the third morning after the child came through the gate, and I did not touch anything for a long time. This was not reverence. Reverence is what you feel for the dead when they have been kind to you. What I felt was closer to the sensation of lifting a cup at the king's table and understanding, one heartbeat too late, that this time the bitterness is real.

She had raised me in this room. She had taught me my letters on the slate by the window, fed me honeyed milk with something else stirred in that she called "winter tonic," and pricked my thumb every full moon with a bone needle, saying it was to keep fevers away. I had swallowed a hundred small cruelties before I was ten years old and called them love, because children do, because love is the only word small enough for them to carry.

The chest sat under the window where it had always sat. I had opened it a dozen times as a girl looking for ribbons and found only linen. Now I lifted out the linen and found, beneath it, a false bottom no deeper than a hand's width, and inside that:

A book bound in grey leather gone soft as skin. A stoppered vial of black glass, heavy, cold. And a letter sealed with wax the color of dried blood, addressed not to me but to *whomever the valley sends*.

I am a food-taster. My whole profession is the willingness to be first. So I broke the seal.

* * *

> *To whomever the valley sends —*
>
> *If you are reading this, then the child has come, or the seal has cracked, or both, and you are angry. Be angry. I was angry too, when my mother handed me this burden with her dying breath and told me I had been drinking poison since I could walk so that the Valley of the Vow might keep its silence.*
>
> *Here are the terms, plainly, since plainness was denied to me.*
>
> *The covenant is not a wall. It is a throat. It swallows magic the way a mouth swallows food, and like any throat it must be kept by a living body — a seal of flesh who holds the vow in her blood. The vow requires three things: that the seal be immune to poison (for the covenant is itself a poison to the world outside, and the holder must not die of her own work); that the seal be of the founder's line; and that the seal consent, freely, each year upon the winter solstice, to renew it.*
>
> *I renewed it forty-one times. My mother, sixty. The one who founded it — your great-great-grandmother, though they will have scrubbed her name from every record by now — renewed it once, at terrible cost, and then spent the rest of her life teaching her daughters how to swallow.*
>
> *The seal is dying. I can feel it the way you would feel a tooth loosening: everything still works, but nothing works well. Magic seeps through the cracks now in small amounts. You may have seen things. Lights in the marsh. Words in dead tongues coming out of living mouths. Do not doubt what you saw. Doubt is how they got me — I doubted for eleven years, and in those eleven years the seal weakened twice as fast.*
>
> *I am sorry. That word is too small. But it is the only one I have that you might believe.*
>
> *— Maren Eastwick*

I read it four times. Then I set it down very carefully on the bed, the way I set down cups I suspect, and went to the window and was sick into the yarrow patch.

Not because of the letter. Because of the *dosing*. Because every morning of my childhood there had been the winter tonic, and every full moon the bone needle, and I had thought — I had *believed* — that all of it was preparation for the king's table. For my profession. My grandmother had built me the way a mason builds an arch: stone by stone, load-bearing, and never once told the stones what they were holding up.

Immune to poison. Of the founder's line. Consent, freely, renewed.

Three terms. She had managed the first two without asking me. The third was supposed to be mine.

* * *

Wren found me there at dusk. Of course Wren found me; Wren finds everything, it is the whole of his profession. He came up the path carrying a lantern he didn't need yet and a stack of letters under his arm, and he looked at my face and at the open chest and said, "Ah."

"Ah," I agreed.

"I brought—" He hesitated, which Wren almost never does. "There are records. In the archive. Sealed folios, older than the kingdom's founding, that I was never permitted to catalog. Tonight the lock on the cabinet simply... wasn't locked anymore. As if it decided." He set the letters on the windowsill. "Magic doesn't knock, apparently. It picks locks."

"Read me the part about the seal," I said. "You've read them already. I can see it in your face. Read me the part I'm going to hate."

He read it. I hated it. The oldest folio said what my grandmother's letter said, except older and crueler: the seal must be replaced before it fails entirely, or the covenant collapses all at once, and the valley — which has spent two centuries swallowing the world's magic — drowns in the backwash. Not just the valley. The letter used a phrase I have not stopped hearing since: *the repletion of the throat.* Everything held back, returned at once. A dam does not leak politely.

"And outside?" I asked. "What happens to outside while we sit here safe behind our grandmother's little vow?"

Wren was quiet. Then, in the voice he uses for facts he wishes were opinions: "The outer provinces are going grey. Crops fail where crops never failed. Children born silent. There are petitions in the archive, decades of them, marked *return unanswered*. We thought they were exaggerations. The court thought they were exaggerations. The court is inside the valley, Ivo. The court is *why*."

The mute child. Speaking a dead tongue. Coming through the gate the week the seal began to visibly flicker. I thought of her sitting in the guardhouse wrapped in a blanket, watching everyone with those patient, unchildlike eyes, and I understood suddenly that she had not come *through* anything. She had been *sent*. Delivered, like a dose. Like a key left under the door of a house whose owner refuses to answer.

"She's not a refugee," I said. "She's a reminder."

"Or a replacement," Wren said softly. "The terms say the seal consents freely. Perhaps the covenant is offering you a way out. Take the child, teach her, let her inherit—"

"The child is six years old, Wren."

"Yes," he said. "That is rather the shape of the problem."

We sat in my grandmother's room while the light died, and neither of us spoke, and the silence between us was the particular silence of two people who have each realized the same terrible arithmetic and are waiting to see who says it aloud.

He said it first, because he is kinder than I am and always has been.

"You don't have to be the one."

"Don't I?" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Ivo Eastwick, bred and dosed for the purpose. Twenty-eight years of immunity, poured into me drop by drop by a woman who loved me enough to lie to me every single morning. If I walk away, either the valley drowns in backwash, or some other daughter of the line gets force-fed twenty years of tonics starting tomorrow, and I know exactly what those mornings taste like, Wren. I know *exactly*. I've been tasting other people's poisons my whole life. I suppose it was only ever practice."

"That isn't fair."

"No," I said. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. Fairness is for people whose grandmothers fed them honest porridge."

He reached across the dark and took my hand — the numb one, the one that can no longer feel the difference between silk and splinters — and pressed it between both of his, and for a moment I let myself want the ordinary life I had apparently never been offered anyway. A small house. An honest porridge. His ridiculous marginalia.

Then, from the guardhouse across the yard, the child began to sing.

Not loudly. Just a few notes, in that dead tongue, and the air above the yarrow patch shimmered like heat off summer stone — the seal flickering, answering, *straining* — and every hair on my arms rose, and the bitter thing under the smell of old plaster flared on my tongue like a name being called.

The solstice was nine days away.

"Wren," I said, "pack your ink. I need someone to take dictation."

He looked at me. "For what?"

"For a letter to a king," I said, "telling him his taster is resigning."
