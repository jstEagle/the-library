## 3. Beyond the Valley of the Vow

The seal, when I finally touched it, felt like the inside of a held breath.

I had walked to the valley gate a thousand times and never once noticed it. That was rather the point of seals. But with the child's small hand in mine—cold as a key left out overnight—I could feel it now, a tautness in the air like skin stretched over a drum, waiting for someone foolish enough to strike.

The child looked up at me. She had not spoken since the gate opened for her three days ago, but she pointed through the pass, toward the world beyond, and made a sound that was not quite a word. In the dead tongue of her people—the tongue I somehow half-knew, the way you half-know a song from before your birth—it meant something like *hurry*, or possibly *it hurts*. With her, I was learning, those were often the same thing.

"Right," I said, because a food-taster learns early that most courage is just swallowing before you've decided to. "Onward, then."

We stepped through.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because everyone who tells this story afterward will want to make it noble, and it was not noble. It was ugly. It was me on my knees in the bracken with my hands pressed flat against the earth like I was trying to hold the world down, screaming through my teeth while every poison I had ever tasted came home to roost.

You have to understand what immunity is. It is not armor. It is a ledger. For twenty years I have taken my morning dose—foxglove on Mondays, hemlock on Thursdays, whatever the king's enemies were fashionable that season—and my body has written each one down in its careful book: *known. accounted for. paid.* The valley's silence kept the book balanced. Magic never touched me; nothing ever compounded; the poisons stayed where I filed them, dead and catalogued.

Outside the seal, magic poured back into me like water into a dry riverbed, and water finds every crack.

The foxglove remembered itself in my chest. The hemlock woke in my spine. The nightshade I'd taken at fourteen, the arsenic dusted through the coronation feast, the slow drip of things whose names even Wren's archives didn't hold—all of it stood up inside me at once and said *we live here now*. My immunity did not break. It burned. It burned the way a wall burns when the fire has finally found the timber behind the plaster.

The child crouched beside me and put her hand on my forehead, and—this is the part I'll deny under oath—her touch took the edge off. Not all of it. Just enough. As if she were holding one end of a very heavy thing so I could breathe beneath the other.

"There you are," I rasped, when I could speak. "Useful child."

She almost smiled. It was the first almost I'd gotten from her, and I hoarded it.

It took an hour before I could stand. It took a day before I stopped tasting copper at the back of my throat. And by the time we crested the ridge above the lowland road, I understood two things with terrible clarity: first, that my grandmother had dosed me my whole life not to protect me from the court, but to prepare me for exactly this crossing; and second, that if I went back inside the seal now, the burning would stop—but the covenant would keep strangling the world, and the child would keep being hunted by everything outside that wanted what she carried.

So. Onward. Swallowing before deciding. It's a system.

* * *

Wren caught up with us at the ferry crossing, mud to the knees and furious in the quiet way archivists are furious, which is to say with citations.

"You were ordered to remain," I said.

"I was." He set down his satchel with the care of a man setting down an argument he intended to win. "The order was issued by the king's steward, who speaks for a king currently being poisoned, in a valley whose seal is failing, based on records I have read and he has not. I've decided the order was a clerical error." A pause. "Also, I couldn't let you go."

That last part he said quietly, to the river rather than to me, and I found I had no citation ready in reply.

Wren Hale, court archivist, is a thin man built entirely of elbows and marginalia, with ink stains on his fingers that I have privately always found more attractive than any face has a right to make them. For six years we have exchanged letters across the width of the castle—him sending me excerpts, me sending him complaints about the food, both of us pretending this correspondence was about the excerpts and the complaints. Six years. I am a woman who tastes death daily for a living, and I had been too much a coward to taste anything else.

"Ivo," he said now, and then stopped, because the child was watching us with the frank attention of someone who reads hearts the way he read ledgers.

"Not here," I said.

"No," he agreed. "Not here."

But that night, in the shelter of a ruined waystation with the child asleep between us, he reached across the cold hearth and took my hand—the poisoned, burning, unsteady hand—and held it as though it were a text he'd waited years to be allowed to open.

"I've drafted this confession forty times," he murmured. "In letters I never sent. You should know that, in case I do it badly aloud."

"You're doing it badly aloud," I said, "and it's still the best thing anyone's said to me."

He laughed, very softly, and I thought: *this is the worst possible hour*. The world beyond the valley was sick and magic-mad, the king was dying by inches, a child slept between us carrying a covenant in her blood, and my own body was a ledger on fire. There was no hour worse suited to love. Which is, I suspect, why it chose that one. Love has a poisoner's instincts. It waits until you cannot refuse the dose.

"Wren," I said. "When this is done—if it's done, if I'm—" I stopped. Started again. "I want there to be an after. Write that down somewhere. Make it official. Archivists can do that, can't they?"

"We can," he said. "Consider it entered into the record."

* * *

We reached the capital on the ninth day, and I knew something was wrong before the gates, because the beggars had stopped begging and started watching.

The city knew its king was dying. Cities always know; it travels down through them like damp through a wall. But the quality of the watching was wrong. People weren't grieving. They were *positioning*. And when we presented ourselves at the castle—Wren flashing his credentials, me giving my name and watching a chamberlain's face do something complicated at the mention of the Eastwick line—we were ushered not to the king's chambers but to a small room off the kitchens, and offered wine.

I tasted it before either of them could drink. Old habit. New stakes.

Bitterroot. Under honey, under plum, under a year of patient cellar-aging: bitterroot, the gentle poison, the one that kills so slowly the victim's physicians argue about causes at the funeral. The same signature I'd been finding in the king's meals for months and reporting, faithfully, to a steward who thanked me each time and changed nothing.

"Well," I said, setting down the cup. "Someone knows I'm coming."

The trap was not for us, though. We were meant to be witnesses—or corpses; the plan seemed flexible. Because within the hour, soldiers wearing the livery of Lord Aldous Vane—the king's nephew, the heir who had spent a decade cultivating patience the way other men cultivate vineyards—came for the child.

They were good soldiers. Quiet, competent, apologetic in the professional manner of men doing a job. Their captain had a paper signed by the steward himself, ordering the "foreign foundling" removed to "protective custody," and I watched Wren read the seal on that paper and go pale in a way I recognized, because I'd seen the same forgery technique in the kitchen accounts for two years. Someone inside had been signing the king's name. Someone inside had been feeding the king bitterroot by the spoonful while I reported it upward into deaf ears. Someone inside had known about the child before we did.

Vane's agent. Vane's poisoner. Sitting perhaps thirty feet from the king's bedside, tasting nothing, immune to everything, patient as cellar-age.

We got the child out through the laundry chutes—don't ask; Wren's knowledge of the castle's plumbing is one of his more alarming virtues—and ran, because running was all we had. Behind us, the captain shouted orders without urgency. Why would he hurry? They knew where we were going. There was only one place left.

The covenant-stone. The ruined shrine in the hills where my grandmother had once knelt and swallowed her oath whole.

"They'll let us get there," Wren panted, keeping pace beside me with the child on his back. "Ivo—they're *letting* us. Why would they let us?"

I already knew. I had known since the wine. Vane didn't need the child dead. He needed the covenant reforged—needed the valley's silence broken, needed magic loose in the world again—because a kingdom built on the old sealed order was a kingdom his uncle could rule, and a world remade was a world that could be *his*. He didn't want to stop the ceremony.

He wanted to attend it. And he wanted me, the last Eastwick, to do the one thing my grandmother's covenant demanded of a seal.

To die willingly, and mean it.

The stone rose ahead of us through the rain, black and patient, and around it, arranged with terrible courtesy among the standing graves, stood Lord Aldous Vane's men—at ease, unhurried, waiting for us to arrive exactly on schedule.

"Well," I said to Wren, to the child, to the rain. "We're expected."
