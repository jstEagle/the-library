## 4. A Choir for the Oath

The covenant-stone had been beautiful once. You could tell from the wreckage—fragments of pale granite scattered like a dropped dinner service, the ghost of carved script still legible on the largest piece, worn to a whisper by four hundred years of weather and one very bad decade of neglect. My grandmother's handwriting, if you could call it that. She'd written her oath in stone the way other people wrote in ink: permanently, and with an audience in mind.

I crouched at the base of it and set out my tools the way I set them out every morning of my working life. Small knife. Small cup. The poisoner's vial, taken from his dead hand three days ago, its contents gone the color of old honey.

"You don't have to do it this way," Wren said.

He'd said it eleven times since we left the capital. I'd counted. Counting was what I did instead of screaming.

"Yes, I do." I held the vial up to the grey light. "It has to be *willing*. That's the whole architecture of the thing. Grandmother didn't stumble into this—she chose it, drank something she could have refused, and made herself the lock. The child can sing until the mountains fall down, but the oath needs an anchor with a pulse." I smiled at him, and I'll admit it wasn't my best smile. "And as it happens, I'm the only person alive whose blood is already half covenant."

"That was done *to* you. The dosing. The breeding of your immunity. None of it was your choice."

"No," I agreed. "But this is. That's rather the point, Wren. It's the first thing anyone's ever let me choose."

The child sat on a fallen fragment of the stone, legs swinging, watching us with those ancient, unbothered eyes. She hadn't spoken—not in our tongue, not in hers—since the poisoner died. But she'd begun humming. Little runs of melody, five notes, six, always circling back to the same resting tone, like water finding its level. Practicing. Whatever she intended to sing, she was rehearsing it against the inside of her own silence.

I uncorked the vial.

It smelled like nothing. That was the horror of the man's craft—he'd spent two years feeding my king a poison refined past all scent, all taste, all warning, and he'd nearly succeeded because he understood the same truth I did: that the body believes what the tongue tells it. I had spent my life lying to my own body one dose at a time. Now I would tell it the last lie, or the first true thing, depending on how you kept your books.

"Ivo." Wren's voice cracked down the middle. He knelt beside me among the broken granite, and his hands hovered near mine without landing, the way they always did, as though touching me required permission he never quite asked for. "I have read every record in the archive about the original sealing. There are accounts from the witnesses. Do you know what they describe? Not agony. Not sacrifice. They describe *singing*. The whole valley heard it, they wrote—the seal going in sounded like a choir heard through a wall. If it must be pain, then let me—"

"It won't be pain." I took his hand. Finally, finally, I took his hand, and felt him go still as a held breath. "I've tasted everything the world knows how to make. This will just be one more flavor. And afterward—" I stopped. Afterward there was no afterward, not for me as I had been. There was only the stone, and the holding, and the long conscious dark of being a border between worlds.

"Afterward," I said, "you'll read to me. You promised."

"Every year," he whispered. "At midwinter. I'll bring the letters. All of them, even the terrible early ones."

"*Especially* the terrible early ones. Your comma splices are part of my comfort now."

He laughed, wet and awful, and kissed my knuckles like a man swearing fealty, which in a sense he was.

The child slid off her rock. She looked at the vial in my hand, then at me, and made a sound—not a word, but a question shaped like one. I understood her the way I half-understood her language: imperfectly, and with my whole chest.

"Yes," I told her. "Sing it over me. Make it mean something."

She took my free hand and pressed it flat against the largest fragment of the stone. The granite was warm. Four hundred years cold, and warm now, because magic had been leaking through the broken seal for months and the stone remembered what it was for.

I drank.

For a moment, nothing. Then every poison I had ever swallowed woke up at once—not as sickness, but as *recognition*, each one rising in my blood like a name called at a muster. The numbness in my hands caught fire and burned away. The taste came flooding up my throat, and oh, it was a choir, exactly as the old accounts promised: bitter notes and sweet ones, iron and flowers and smoke, every flavor of my whole hidden life singing together, and beneath it the child's voice, high and clear, in the tongue that was older than tongues, stitching each note to the next, stitching me to the stone, the stone to the valley, the valley to the world.

I felt the oath take hold the way you feel a door close in a distant room. Not violence. Certainty.

The last thing I saw with my own eyes was Wren's face, and I spent what remained of my mobility memorizing it, because I am a professional and I do not leave a task half-finished.

Then I was the seal. Alive, conscious, held.

* *

Midwinter. First year.

Wren came up the valley road with snow in his hair and a satchel of letters against his chest, and when he reached the stone he did not kneel, because Ivo had forbidden kneeling in the strongest terms available to a woman who was mostly granite now. He sat, instead, on the fallen fragment where the child used to sit, and unwrapped his hands from their gloves, and began to read aloud.

"The letter dated the ninth of thaw-month, in which I use the phrase 'a matter of some archival interest' as a flimsy pretext to see you, and you see through it immediately, and pretend not to, which I have decided is the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me—"

The stone was warm under the snow. It was always warm now. Travelers crossing the valley gate spoke of it: the way the air here tasted faintly of honey, the way grief seemed to set down lighter burdens before it left. The seal held. Beyond the mountains, the world breathed—magic moving through it again like blood through a limb woken from sleep—and the child had carried Ivo Eastwick's name into that breathing world, taught it to children in the old tongue, so that it would be spoken correctly, with the weight it deserved.

Wren read until the light went blue. Then he packed the letters away, pressed his palm flat against the granite—it was their custom; it had been her hand against the stone, once, and he saw no reason to change the address—and stood.

"You know," he said, as he did every year, "the silence isn't silent anymore. I don't know if you can hear me say that. But it hums. Five notes, six, always coming home to the same resting tone. The whole valley. Like a choir heard through a wall."

The stone was warm.

Somewhere under the snow, patient as a held breath, Ivo Eastwick listened—and if a seal cannot smile, then the warmth itself was the smiling, and the long note beneath the winter wind was her answer, and it was, at last, music.
