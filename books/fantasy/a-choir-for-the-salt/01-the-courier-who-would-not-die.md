## 1. The Courier Who Would Not Die

The morning the stranger died in my stall, I was selling luck to people who couldn't afford it.

That's the honest shape of my trade. A copper for a knotted thread that keeps fever from a child's door. Two coppers for a salt-blessed coin to tuck under a fisherman's tongue before he argues with the tide. None of it real, none of it mine—or so I told the Wardens, if they ever asked, and they had stopped asking years ago. Ten years of being nobody. Ten years of selling charms that were only knots and coins and confidence, while the small true thing I carried stayed folded up behind my ribs like a moth with its wings pinned shut.

The market at Lowbridge opened gray that day, the fog off the Vell harbor thick enough to chew. Gulls argued over the fish-criers' leavings. Somewhere a bell rang the hour wrong, as it always did, because the bell-ringer was paid by the tavern next door to ring it late.

I had just wrapped a false love-charm for a girl who deserved better than what she was buying when the crowd two rows over went strange. Not loud—strange. The way water goes still before something rises in it. People stepped back from a shape leaning against the pickle-barrel cart, then against the awning post, then not against anything at all.

He came down the row toward me like a man walking into wind that only he could feel. Gray coat. Gray face. And his eyes—

I have seen drowning men pulled from the harbor. Their eyes look like that: wide open on a world they've already left.

"Delacroix." My name came out of him in pieces, wet and cracking. "Orsen Delacroix."

Nobody knew that name. Nobody *used* it. In ten years I had been the charm-seller with no name worth remembering, the one the Wardens' lists skipped, the one whose little magics were too small to bother hunting. Hearing my own name in a stranger's mouth was like hearing a door open in a house I'd sealed shut.

"You're mistaken," I said, and reached for the lie the way you reach for a familiar tool. "I'm just a seller of—"

He fell across my table.

Charms scattered. Knots and coins and dried lavender skittered into the mud, and I didn't even flinch, because his hand had closed around my wrist with a grip that should not have belonged to a dying man. It was cold. Not dead-cold. *Sea*-cold, cold the way deep water is cold, and beneath it I felt something else—a hum, faint and wrong, like a tuning fork struck underwater.

"The guttering," he said.

"What?"

His other hand pressed something into my palm. Stiff paper, damp, edges furred with white crystal. "The guttering. You keep the door. You don't know you keep the door." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh, if laughter could drown. "Ten years, gatekeeper, and you never once looked behind you."

"I don't know what you—" But I did know. There is a thing under the old toll-house where I sleep, a seam in the cellar wall that sweats salt water no matter how dry the summer. I had always told myself it was the harbor finding its way in. I had always been very careful not to touch it.

"They'll tell you the seal holds," he said. His voice was going now, fraying like rope over an edge. "They'll tell you it holds and that's good. Ask them what it holds *against*. Ask them what's starving."

"Who's *they*? Who sent you?"

"Doesn't—" He coughed, and what came up was not blood. It was brine, clear and glittering, pooling on my table among the ruined charms. "Map. West of the drowned stair. The stone remembers what the law made it forget. You're the last one the door will still recognize."

"Recognize me? I'm nobody. I sell *knots*."

"You sell knots," he agreed, and smiled, and his teeth were crusted white. "And every knot you've ever tied has held. Think about that, gatekeeper. Think about what that means."

Then he began to come apart.

I want to say I understood it, even then. I want to say some part of me rose to meet the miracle instead of recoiling from it. What actually happened is that I sat frozen with a map crushed in my fist while a man dissolved in front of forty witnesses—not into rot, not into ash, but into seawater. It ran off my table in bright sheets. It soaked my sleeves. It filled the grooves of the wood and stood in the mud in little pools that caught the gray light and threw it back silver, and where each pool lay, tiny crystals formed, blooming like frost, like flowers, like salt roses.

The crowd did not scream. That was the worst part. They went quiet, and then they began, quietly, to leave—the particular quiet of people deciding not to have seen something. In Vell, seeing was dangerous. Seeing got you asked questions, and questions got you visited, and visits from the Wardens ended with doors marked in chalk and families gone quiet too.

Only one person didn't retreat. A hedge-witch I half-knew—Maren, herb-seller, exile twice over—stood at the end of my row with her basket on her hip, staring at the salt roses, and then staring at me. Her mouth shaped a word she was too careful to say aloud.

*You.*

* * *

I hid the map the way I'd hidden everything for ten years: fast, small, and inside myself.

It was stiff with crystal and damp with sea, and when I unfolded it under my table—one heartbeat, two—it wasn't ink I saw but lines scored into the paper itself, as if the map hadn't been drawn but *worn*, rubbed into existence by a thousand desperate fingers. Streets I knew. Streets I didn't. And west, past the edge of anything mapped, past the drowned stair the harbormaster's charts refused to acknowledge, a mark like a star with too many points, and around it, written in a hand that shook:

*the stone remembers*

I folded it into a knot.

Not a paper fold. A knot—the kind I tied for coppers all day long, except this time I put the small true thing behind my ribs into my hands and let it out through my fingers, just a breath of it, just enough. The map shrank. Folded through itself, tighter and tighter, until it was a bead no bigger than a peppercorn, and I strung it onto the cord I wore under my shirt where a luck-charm would hang, and it hung there against my skin, humming its underwater hum, and I closed my collar over it and became nobody again.

Which is when the Wardens came.

They swept Lowbridge market the way they always did: six of them in slate-gray, faces bare, which meant this was official and not a shakedown. The tall one—I knew her by sight, everyone did; Warden-scribe, ink-stained cuffs, eyes that read people like ledgers—walked the row while her subordinates questioned the fish-criers. I watched her stop at the pickle-cart. Watched her crouch. Watched her touch one of the salt roses and bring her fingers away glistening.

She looked up and down the row, slow, methodical, and her gaze snagged on my stall the way a hook snags on weed.

"Charm-seller." She crossed to me. Up close she smelled of ink and lamp oil. "You saw it."

"Saw what, Warden?" I kept my hands busy re-stringing scattered charms. Busy hands are honest hands; I'd learned that lesson in my first year. "Been head-down with stock all morning."

"A man died here." She turned a salt rose over with one finger. It did not melt at her touch, which seemed to interest her greatly. "Dissolved. Forty witnesses, and every one of them suddenly blind. Curious thing, blindness. It spreads fastest near the guilty."

"I sell knots and coins, Warden. If a man chose my stall to die in, he had poor taste and worse aim."

Something flickered across her face—not belief, not quite. Recognition, maybe, of the shape of a lie well-told, because she'd heard ten thousand of them. "What's your name?"

Here it was. The question I'd spent a decade making unaskable. I gave her the answer I'd built: a shrug, a grin, "Whatever's on the license, Warden, and the license says whatever the guild paid for." Small man. Small magic. Nothing worth writing down.

Her pen hovered over her ledger. Then, somewhere behind us, a commotion—the fish-criers arguing, a Warden shouting about contamination—and she glanced away, and wrote instead: *nothing further.*

But as she turned to go, she paused. "The guttering," she said quietly, not looking at me. "Do you know what that means, charm-seller?"

My blood went to ice water. "Never heard the word."

"Mm." She walked on. At the end of the row she stopped beside Maren, spoke briefly, low, and Maren's face did something complicated. Then the Wardens were gone, and the market exhaled, and I stood in my wrecked stall with a map knotted against my heart and the taste of the sea in my mouth.

That night I locked the stall and walked home the long way, checking reflections in dark windows, and let myself into the old toll-house, and went down to the cellar, and stood in front of the seam in the wall.

It was sweating worse than it ever had. Water ran down the stones in threads, and the threads were glowing—faintly, guttering, like the last of a candle drowning in its own wax. And through the wall, so soft I might have imagined it, I heard something breathing. Slow. Enormous. Labored.

A voice like a tide going out said, or seemed to say, or maybe I only wanted it to say:

*Gatekeeper. The door is starving.*

I put my hand flat against the wet stone, and for the first time in ten years, I knocked.
