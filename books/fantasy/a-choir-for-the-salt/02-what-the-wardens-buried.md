## 2. What the Wardens Buried

The archive beneath the Wardenhall smelled of iron and old paper, and Ilsa Vane had spent eleven years learning to love it the way one loves a locked room—thoroughly, and with a growing suspicion of what it kept.

She worked the night shift by choice. The other scribes went home to their lamps and their suppers; Ilsa stayed below with a candle stub and the ledgers of the founding era, cataloguing them into dust. It was quiet work. Invisible work. She had built her whole life on invisible work.

The ledger she opened that night was bound in grey hide gone soft as cloth, its spine stamped with the sigil of Aldous Renne—the founder himself, the marble face above the Wardenhall gates, the man whose law had emptied Vell of magicians for two hundred years. *No flame unlicensed. No craft unwitnessed. The Wardens keep the seal.* Every child could recite it. Ilsa had recited it at six years old with her hand on her heart.

The first pages were ordinary: inventories, oaths, supply requisitions for the garrison at the Threshold Gate. Then, tucked between a record of grain tithes and a list of executed charm-sellers, a page in Renne's own hand—and Ilsa knew his hand, had copied it a hundred times in her training—began without preamble:

*The fire is not dying. The fire is being taken.*

She read it three times before her candle stopped trembling.

*The dragon of Salt does not gutter of age or grief. It gutters because I have bled it. Each year the covenant-stone drinks deeper of the flame, and each year I carry what it drinks up the stair in my own veins, and I seal it in the vault beneath this hall. Let the histories say the beast is failing. Let the histories demand a ban on all small magics, so that no practitioner comes near enough to feel the truth of it through the stone. A city that fears magic will never look for it where it burns brightest—in my cellar, in my keeping, in my name.*

Below that, in a different ink, a later hand—Renne's successor, perhaps, or his accomplice—had written only: *Continued per protocol. Vault access restricted to the First Chair.*

Ilsa sat back. The archive breathed around her, ten thousand ledgers of a lie.

She thought of the executions she had recorded. Names, dates, method. *Burned at the Low Cross. Drowned in the salt-cistern. Hanged with their own charms about their necks.* She had written *justified* beside each one because the form required it, and her hand had never once shaken, and now her hand would not stop shaking.

Because she knew the vault. Every scribe knew of it—a sealed room below the reliquary that no First Chair had opened in living memory, officially empty since the founder's day. Officially empty. And every year, on the eve of the Seal-Rite, the First Chair descended alone and returned looking older, and the dragon of Salt guttered another degree, and the Wardens tightened the ban another notch, and the tide-marks on the seaward walls climbed another hand's width higher.

It wasn't protection. It had never been protection. It was a theft so large it had become a government.

Ilsa blew out her candle and sat in the dark and did the only thing a careful woman could do with a truth like that.

She began to copy it.

* * *

Orsen found Maren the way you find anything worth having in Vell's low quarter: by following the smell of burnt rosemary and the sound of an argument.

The hedge-witch had a stall at the very lip of the Brackish Market, past the charm-sellers, past even Orsen's own corner, where the cobbles gave way to mud and the mud gave way to the sea-stair. She sold remedies that worked and wards that didn't, loudly, to anyone who'd listen, which was either the bravest or stupidest thing Orsen had seen in ten years of hiding.

"You're Delacroix." Maren didn't look up from grinding. "The gatekeeper who sells love-charms. Everyone knows. You're terrible at it."

"I'm excellent at it."

"Your charms smell like nothing. Real ones smell like wanting." She looked up then—grey eyes, wind-scoured face, sixty if she was forty. "So either you've got no magic, or you've got some and you're strangling it. Which?"

Orsen glanced down the market row. Empty stalls, gulls, the grey slap of water against the stair. "The second one."

"Thought so." Maren set down her pestle. "And you came all this way to tell me that why? Because nobody strangles their magic quietly unless something's wrong with the seal. I've felt it in my teeth for a month. Like a toothache in the world." She wiped her hands on her apron. "So ask me, gatekeeper. Ask me properly, because I don't do favors and I don't do half-truths."

"The seal is starving the last dragon-fire," Orsen said. "Someone's been bleeding it for two centuries. I intend to reach the covenant-stone and reforge the covenant before Salt comes up the stair and takes the city. I need a hedge-witch who knows the old binding-words."

Maren was quiet a long moment. Somewhere behind her stall, a kettle muttered.

"That's a death," she said at last. "You're asking me to die."

"I'm asking you to come anyway."

"And there it is." But she was already pulling off her apron, already reaching under the counter for a bundle wrapped in oilcloth—her true kit, the one she'd never once sold from. "My grandmother drowned in the first flood after the ban. They called it weather. I've waited fifty years to call it what it was." She thrust the bundle at Orsen like a weapon being surrendered. "Don't make me regret trusting you. I'm too old to start over."

* * *

Kesh they did not recruit. Kesh simply appeared.

Orsen met her at the rehearsal hall of the Ember Guild—a licensed troupe, fire-dancers permitted under strict Warden charter, their flames measured and taxed. Kesh danced last in the lineup, and while the other dancers performed their careful, counted patterns, Kesh did something else entirely. Her fire moved like it loved her. It curled around her wrists in shapes no charter allowed, bloomed into wings, wrote letters in the air that dissolved before the Wardens' assessor could turn around.

She was mute—not tongueless, Orsen learned later, but having chosen silence at fourteen when the Wardens burned her village's shrine, and never choosing otherwise. She communicated in gesture and in flame, and when the performance ended and the crowd drained away, she stood in the empty hall with her arms crossed, looking at Orsen as though Orsen were the one who'd been auditioned.

Maren signed at her—clumsily, an old trader's pidgin—and Kesh answered in a flood of fluid hand-talk that made the hedge-witch laugh for the first time all day.

"She says," Maren translated, "that she's known about you for three years. That you watch her dance every solstice and your magic leaks out of you like a cracked jug. She says—" Maren paused, listening with her eyes. "She says the fire told her you'd come. Dragons talk to fire, and fire talks to her, and yours has been calling her name for months."

Orsen's throat went tight. "That's not possible. Nobody knows what I am."

Kesh smiled. It transformed her whole severe face into something young and fierce. She signed again, short and sharp.

"She says," Maren said, "that secrecy is just a cage you've gotten comfortable in. And she says she's coming. And she says—" the hedge-witch hesitated, "—she says we'll need more than a witch and a dancer and a frightened gatekeeper. We'll need someone inside the law."

* * *

Which was how, three nights later, a Warden-scribe stood dripping in the doorway of Orsen's stall after closing, rain running off her grey cloak, a stolen ledger clutched to her chest like a child.

"Ilsa Vane," she said. "Second Chair of Records. I have proof of what your courier died carrying, and proof of two hundred years besides." Her voice held steady the way a rope holds steady—under enormous tension. "I burned my career tonight. Possibly my life. So before you decide whether to trust me, understand that I have already decided to trust you, and I would very much like to sit down."

Orsen looked at her—at the Warden grey, at the sigil on her collar, at everything Orsen had spent ten years hiding from—and felt the last wall inside themselves groan like ice before a thaw.

Behind them, from the back room, Maren's voice: "Let her in, Delacroix. The tide's turning whether you open the door or not."

Orsen stepped aside.

Ilsa laid the ledger on the counter and opened it to the founder's hand, and by the light of a single protected candle, Orsen Delacroix read the words that ended their invisibility forever—and beneath the final line, fresh ink, still sharp:

*The vault will be opened at the next Seal-Rite. Three days hence. Come armed with witnesses.*
