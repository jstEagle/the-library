## 3. The Guttering

The gate did not open so much as remember them.

Orsen had stood before it for ten years—sweeping its threshold, oiling its hinges, reciting the Warden-mandated litany of closure—and never once had it answered them. Now, with Maren's hedge-sigils chalked across the lintel and Kesh's palms pressed flat to the salt-scabbed wood, the door exhaled. A long, tidal sigh, as if something vast on the other side had been holding its breath since the founding.

"Ten years," Orsen said quietly, "and it opens for a witch's chalk."

"It opens for anyone who asks honestly," Maren said. She was older than she looked, the way hedges are older than their flowers, and her hands did not shake even though her voice nearly did. "That's what frightens the Wardens. Not the magic. The honesty."

Kesh could not say anything at all. She only smiled—that quick, crooked smile that had survived a fire-dancer's exile, a burned stage, a city that called her art witchcraft—and stepped through first.

* * *

Salt was drowning.

Orsen had imagined the drowned realm a hundred ways in ten years of guarding its door: a sunken cathedral, a drowned garden, a kingdom of glass. None of it prepared them for the truth, which was simpler and far worse. Salt was a city still standing—towers, bridges, market squares—but the water was coming up through everything. It rose in the streets like breath through lungs. It wept from the walls. Fountains ran backward, drinking themselves into the sea that waited somewhere above and beyond, patient as arithmetic.

And everywhere, everywhere, the light was guttering.

Not sunlight. There was no sun here. The light came from below—from the deep basin at the heart of the city where the last dragon lay coiled around the covenant-stone like a moth around a dying flame.

"She's small," Maren said, and her voice cracked on the word.

She was. That was the horror of it. Orsen had grown up on stories of dragons the size of storms, wings that made weather, throats full of the first fire. This creature was barely larger than an ox, her scales gone dull as old coins, her ribs showing through the skin like the staves of a barrel. Each breath she drew made the whole realm flicker—the towers brightening, dimming, brightening—in time with her lungs.

"The guttering," Orsen said. The courier's word. *The guttering comes.* They had thought it meant a flood. It meant this: a candle going out, and a world going out with it.

They descended the great stair into the basin, and the dragon lifted her head.

Her eyes were the worst part. Not because they were terrible, but because they were *tired*—ancient, courteous, exhausted eyes, the eyes of someone who has been keeping watch far past the end of their shift and knows no relief is coming. She looked at Orsen the way one looks at a familiar face in a foreign country.

"You're the doorkeeper," she said. Her voice was embers settling. "The little thief of charms. I have felt you on the other side of my wall for ten years. You hide your light very well."

"I had practice," Orsen said. "Your city taught mine to hunt it."

"Yes." The dragon lowered her head again, wearily. "I know whose fault that is. Come. Read the stone. It will not wait much longer, and neither will I."

* * *

The covenant-stone stood taller than two men, black and veined with gold, and the words cut into it were not letters but *prices*. Orsen read them twice, then a third time, hoping they would change.

They did not change.

"One life's magic," Maren said flatly. "Paid entire. That's the renewal price. That's what the founder wrote."

"That's what the founder *wrote*," Orsen said. "Ilsa said the records were altered. Everything about him was altered. Maybe this too."

"Maybe." Maren touched the stone and flinched. "But the stone believes it. And the stone is the law here, whatever lies are written above."

Kesh crouched at the base of the stone, tracing the oldest carvings—the ones beneath the founder's additions, worn nearly smooth by time. She looked up at Orsen and pointed: at the old carving, at herself, at the crowd-shapes etched there, dozens of small figures with raised hands.

"Many keepers," Orsen translated slowly. "She says the original covenant wasn't one sacrifice. It was—" They stopped. Looked at the dragon. At the small, guttering body of her, burning ten years of herself into a seal built to be fed by a city, and fed instead by nothing but her own slow death.

"It was shared," Orsen finished. "He changed it. He made it a single life so no one would ever pay it, and the seal would starve, and everyone would blame magic itself instead of him."

Above them, faint as memory, came the sound Orsen had been dreading since the market: boots. Many boots. And the cold chime of Warden steel being drawn in unison.

"They followed us down," Maren breathed. "How—"

"The gate remembers honesty," Orsen said. "It also remembers force. They've been forcing doors for three hundred years." They turned to Kesh, and the words came out before they could soften them. "The passage. Can you hold it? If we need to fall back—"

Kesh nodded once. No hesitation. That was the thing about Kesh that frightened Orsen most: she spent herself the way other people spent coin, easily, gladly, without counting. Fire-dancers were trained to burn. Nobody had ever taught her when to stop.

The first Warden came over the rim of the basin with his lantern-spell blazing, and the ambush began.

* * *

There is no dignity in a fight in a drowning city. The Wardens came down the stair in ranks, chanting their null-litany, and the water rose to meet them as if Salt itself objected. Maren's sigils bloomed and burst; a hedge-witch's magic is small and mean and perfectly suited to stairs. Orsen fought with the only thing they had ever allowed themselves—small magics, charm-seller's tricks, a pocketful of misdirection and luck-bending—and discovered, in the space between heartbeats, that ten years of hiding had not made those tricks weak. It had made them precise.

But there were thirty Wardens. And the dragon could not rise. And the stone was singing now, a low grinding note, hungry, impatient, its price unpaid.

"We can't win this," Maren shouted. "We can't hold them and hold the passage—"

"I have the passage," Kesh signed, and smiled that crooked smile, and walked into the fire.

Not away from it. Into it. She planted herself at the head of the stair where the Wardens poured through, and she opened her hands, and every trick of her burned-out, exiled, magnificent art came home. Flame roared up around her—not wild, not cruel, but *hers*, shaped and danced, a wall of it, beautiful enough that half the Wardens faltered mid-chant simply to look.

"Hold the stone!" she signed to Orsen through the blaze, and her hands were already smoking. "This is what I'm *for*. Go!"

"No—" Orsen started forward and Maren caught their arm, and it was the worst moment of Orsen's life, worse than the courier dissolving, worse than ten years of hiding, because Kesh looked at them over her own fire and *nodded*, glad, certain, spending herself without counting, and the passage behind them blazed white and sealed—

—and the Wardens screamed and broke, and the stair went dark, and Kesh went down with it.

Orsen reached her first. Of course they did. She was still smiling. There was almost nothing left of her to smile with—her hands gray as ash, her fire all spent, her eyes fixed on Orsen with the calm of someone who has finished a dance exactly on the final beat.

"Told you," she whispered. Or mouthed. There was no voice left. "*For* something."

Then she was ash and warmth and the smell of a festival, and the covenant-stone rang like a struck bell, and the dragon's guttering light flared once—brighter, furious, grieving—and went low again.

Maren was crying without any noise at all. Orsen knelt in the wet ash and found they could not. Fury had burned the grief off, the way fire burns off paint, leaving bare metal underneath.

"He wrote the price," Orsen said, rising, turning to the stone, to the dragon, to the sealed and silent stair. "One life. One sacrifice. So that no one would ever pay it, and the world would starve slowly, and he'd die rich and safe and praised. He took a choir and made it a solo, and let the singer burn alone."

The dragon's tired eyes watched them.

"So we unwrite it," Orsen said. "Tonight. Before the Wardens dig through. We give the stone back its choir."

Behind them, deep in the sealed rock, something began to knock. Rhythmic. Patient. Wardens do not stop at walls; they become them.

The stone waited, and its price was still written, and Orsen put both hands against the black surface and began, for the first time in ten years, to shine.
