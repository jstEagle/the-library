## 1. The Weight of Small Charms

The frost came to Greyhollow the way debt came—quietly, then all at once, and never to anyone who could afford it.

Vesper Pemberton knew this because they had spent five years learning the exact weight of small things. The weight of a luck-knot: two fingers of braided red thread, a copper's worth, nothing in it but knot-craft and a seller's smile. The weight of a fever-salve: beeswax, willow bark, comfrey, and a lie told so often it had worn smooth as a river stone—*just herbs, mistress, just what my mother made.* The weight of an iron bell, which was no weight at all until it rang.

They were arranging their stall in Copperlane Market when the first bell of the morning sounded three streets over, and Vesper's hands went still on the awning rope without any decision from their mind. Five years of practice lived in that stillness. Five years since they had last spoken a true word, and every day since had been a lesson in how much of a person could be carried in silence.

*Just knots,* they reminded themselves, unrolling the cloth. *Just salves. Just a tired woman with chapped hands selling nothing.*

The market was building itself around them the way it did every morning: fishwives shouting down the price of herring gone gray, the tallow-chandler's boy hauling his stinking cart, old Marta Two-Crooks setting out her mended pots with the tenderness other people gave to children. Above it all, Greyhollow's chimneys breathed their thousand smokes into a sky the color of dirty pewter, and above *that*, if you knew where to look—and Vesper always knew where not to look—the mountain stood with its crown lost in cloud, wearing its court like a secret under stone.

Nobody looked at the mountain anymore. That was the arrangement. The Magistrate's edicts had been carved into the city gates twenty years ago: *No spellcraft. No hedge-arts. No word that binds.* And Greyhollow, being a practical city, had obeyed the way rivers obey dams—outwardly smooth, inwardly pressing. Magic hadn't died here. It had gone indoors, gone underground, gone into luck-knots sold by sellers who didn't know why their knots held and fever-salves that worked a little too well and cradles that never seemed to break no matter how many times they fell from carts.

Vesper kept their stall aggressively dull. They priced things too high for magic and too low for greed. They let customers haggle them down and complained about the cost of beeswax. Once, early on, a woman had wept over a fever-salve that saved her son, and Vesper had watched her through the window of her house for an hour afterward, terrified that gratitude would turn to suspicion, that she would look at the salve too closely, that she would say the word *witch* in a room where someone might repeat it. The woman had only ever come back to buy more. But Vesper had learned the lesson anyway: in Greyhollow, a miracle was more dangerous than a murder. Murders had explanations.

"Knots, Pemberton?" said a voice, and Vesper looked up into the round, wind-burned face of Doss, the cooper's man, holding out two coppers like an apology.

"For your wife's birthing," Vesper said, taking them. "Red thread. Hang it over the door."

"And it works?"

"It's thread," Vesper said. "It holds things together."

Doss laughed, satisfied, and went away believing he'd bought nothing, which was exactly what Vesper had sold him. It was a strange arithmetic, this life: to give people luck while insisting it was only string; to heal while swearing it was only wax. Some mornings Vesper woke with their gift humming under their skin like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing, and some mornings they woke with nothing at all, just the ordinary ache of a body that had been clenching itself shut for so long it had forgotten another way to stand.

The bells came closer at midmorning.

Everyone heard them before they saw anything—iron bells have a particular voice, flat and joyless, a sound with no bottom to it, and the whole market adjusted by degrees the way water adjusts around a dropped stone. Voices lowered. Haggling gentled. Somewhere a child was scooped up and given a sweet to keep it quiet. Vesper straightened their awning, squared their scales, and became, with the whole of their considerable discipline, a person of no interest whatsoever.

They came through the Fish Gate in pairs, as always: four witch-hunters in slate-grey coats, faces masked to the eyes, each carrying a staff tipped with an iron bell the size of a fist. Captain Serane Ovid walked at their head—Vesper knew her by her stride, long and unhurried, and by the pale scar that crossed her jaw like a signature. She never looked at stalls. She looked at *people*, and worse, she looked at the spaces between people, as though the air itself might confess.

The inspection was routine, which meant it was theater, which meant it was real. Ovid's hunters moved down the row of stalls with a list, weighing goods, ringing their bells over jars and bundles. Iron bells were the Magistrate's cleverest cruelty: they couldn't detect magic, not really—but they rang differently near a working charm, a thin sour note, and more importantly they made everyone *believe* they could hear something, and belief did half the hunt's work for free. Half the "witches" taken in Greyhollow these five years had been guilty of nothing but nervousness.

Vesper counted their breathing. Four counts in. Four counts out. Their charms were clean—knots cut and tied with cold hands, salves mixed on salted ground, everything done in deliberate mundanity until even the objects themselves believed they were ordinary. They had spent years teaching their craft to be quiet.

The hunter reached their stall. He was young, new to the mask, and he rang his bell over the luck-knots with the bored rhythm of a man doing arithmetic. Nothing. Over the salves. Nothing. Over the bundle of woven sigil-cords Vesper sold as "bracelets, plain work, good leather"—nothing, nothing—

And then Vesper reached out to move the bundle aside, politely, helpfully, the way any honest trader tidies their wares for inspection, and the topmost cord hummed.

Not rang. Not sang. *Hummed*—a single low note, felt more than heard, rising from the leather like warmth rising from a stone, and Vesper saw the young hunter's head tilt, saw his bell-hand pause, saw the whole grey machinery of the morning begin to turn toward them—

"That one's damp," Vesper said. Their voice came out steady, bored, faintly aggrieved. They plucked the cord up and held it to the grey light. "Rain got into the awning last night. Leather does that. Sings a little when it's wet and stretched." They flexed the cord between their fingers, and beneath the skin of their calm their heart was hammering against a door that had been locked for five years. "You want to test it? Go on. It'll do the same for you."

The hunter hesitated. Behind him, Captain Ovid had stopped walking. She wasn't looking at the cord. She was looking at Vesper's hands—at the steadiness of them, perhaps, or at something underneath the steadiness—and for one long moment the market held its breath along with Vesper.

Then Ovid said, "Damp leather," in a voice like a ledger closing, and moved on.

The patrol passed. The bells faded toward the Tannery Row. Vesper set the cord down with fingers that shook only after it was safe for them to shake, and pressed both palms flat against the cold boards of the stall, and breathed, and hated—quietly, thoroughly, in the practiced way of long habit—how easy it would be to lose everything to one warm note.

* * *

In the afternoon, Wren sang in Tanner's Square.

She always sang there on market days, on the steps of the old grain exchange where the acoustics were kind, and the crowd gathered the way water gathers—a few at first, then dozens, then a ring of faces turned upward. Vesper closed the stall early, as they always did, and took the long way around, and stood at the edge of the square beneath the dripping eaves of the chandlery, far enough back to be furniture, close enough to break their own heart on schedule.

Wren Ashgrave had a voice like the moment before rain. Not sweet, exactly—there was gravel in it, and smoke, and something older than either—but when she opened her mouth the air itself seemed to lean in to listen. She sang harvest songs and river songs and the old laments that the Magistrate's censors had never managed to stamp out because you cannot outlaw grief. She sang with her eyes half-shut and her chin lifted, and the coins that fell into her hat fell like applause.

Vesper loved her the way you love a fire in winter: from a careful distance, with full knowledge of what would happen if you got too close. They had loved her for three years now, maybe four—they had stopped keeping accurate accounts, the way you stop counting a debt you can't pay. They had spoken to her perhaps forty times. Bought apples from her brother's cart. Left a luck-knot in her guitar case once, anonymously, and then lain awake all night imagining her finding it, wondering about it, possibly *thanking* whoever left it, and had nearly been sick with the sweetness and the danger of it.

Because here was the arithmetic Vesper could not solve: every word they spoke was a key looking for a lock. One oath—one true sentence, one binding phrase, one careless *may this road rise to meet you* said with the old blood behind it—and reality would lean in and obey, and the iron bells would hear, and the grey coats would come, and everything Vesper had built this careful, suffocating, invisible life upon would burn. To speak truly near Wren was to endanger her. So Vesper stood in the rain-shadow and listened to the woman they loved sing songs about love, and said nothing, and called this safety, and knew it was also cowardice, and could not tell the difference anymore.

Today Wren finished with a lament Vesper didn't know—an old one, in the high minor mode, about a soul that wanders looking for its name—and the crowd sighed and scattered coins and drifted off into the grey afternoon. Wren packed her guitar, chatted with the apple-seller, laughed at something. Ordinary. Alive. Herself.

Vesper turned for home.

They were halfway down Chandler's Lane when they felt it—that prickle at the nape, the sense of being *regarded*, and they told themselves it was nothing, it was always nothing, five years of nothing. But the feeling followed them the length of the lane, patient as frost, and when Vesper finally glanced back there was nothing there at all except the empty street, the dripping eaves, and somewhere far off, very faint, the sound of an iron bell ringing a single sour note.

That night, locking the shop, Vesper caught their reflection in the dark glass and thought—not for the first time, but tonight with a strange new sharpness—*How long? How long can a person hold their breath?*

The candle guttered. Somewhere across the city, a dog began to howl, and did not stop, and under the howl, so faint Vesper would spend the rest of their life unsure whether they had imagined it, came a knocking at the world's door—three slow blows, like a fist, like a warning, like a beginning.
