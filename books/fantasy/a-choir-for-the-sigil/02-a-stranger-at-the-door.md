## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The knock came at midnight, and it was wrong.

Not the polite triple-rap of a neighbor, not the hammering of the Watch. It was a sound like a body sliding down a door — a slow, wet collapse of knuckles against wood, once, twice, and then nothing.

Vesper sat up in the narrow bed above the shop, heart already pounding, one hand closing around the iron poker they kept by the hearth for exactly this reason and never, in five years, had needed. Greyhollow at midnight was quiet the way held breath is quiet. The witch-hunters' bells had stopped ringing an hour ago; that meant either the patrol had turned down toward the harbor, or it meant nothing good at all.

They went down the stairs without lighting a lamp. Five years of practice: heel to toe, along the third stair from the top that didn't creak, past the shelf of luck-knots and fever-salves and rain-charms so carefully mundane they were almost insulting. Vesper's hand found the bolt on the door and rested there.

"Who's knocking?"

Silence. Then, faintly, a voice like paper tearing:

"Charmseller. Please."

The voice knew what they were. Not *shopkeeper*, not *Pemberton*. Charmseller — the old word, the word that had meant something once, before the Magistrate's edicts stripped every gifted name out of the city's mouth.

Every sensible instinct Vesper owned said *leave the bolt where it is*. They had spent five years being sensible. Sensible had kept them alive while everyone they'd trained with, fought beside, loved in the careless way soldiers love, had died or worse. Sensible was the oath of silence worn smooth as a river stone.

But the voice said *please* like a man saying his own name for the last time, and Vesper — hating themselves with a fluency that had become its own kind of prayer — drew the bolt.

* * *

He fell into the shop rather than through it, and Vesper caught most of his weight on the counter, scattering a tray of knotwork into the dark. He was young — younger than Vesper, anyway — with courier's leathers gone black with something that was not mud, and his eyes had the glassy shine of a man running a fever or running from something, and possibly both.

"They're coming," he said. "Behind me. You have to bar the door. Bar it and don't look."

Vesper barred the door. It was absurd — a plank of oak against whatever he meant — but their hands did it anyway, and then they turned him around and got him onto the floor behind the counter, where the lamplight wouldn't reach if anyone peered through the shutters.

"What happened to you?"

"Ran the frostline road." He laughed, and the laugh cost him something; Vesper heard it tear loose inside him. "Three days ahead of them. Thought I'd lost them at the weirs. I hadn't." His hand found Vesper's wrist with shocking strength. "Listen. Listen to me. There isn't time for the whole story and you wouldn't believe it anyway. I need — " He coughed, wet and deep. "I need someone who can *speak*."

"I sell charms," Vesper said. The lie came out smooth as oiled leather after five years of practice. "Luck-knots. Salves. Nothing spoken. If you need a physician—"

"I need a swordspeaker," he said, "and the city says there are none left, and we are both going to pretend that's true for as long as it takes me to die."

The word went through Vesper like cold water through cloth. They looked at the shuttered window, at the door, at the dark between the shelves where the shadows pooled like spilled ink. Somewhere out in the street, very far away or very close, an iron bell rang once — a hunter checking a ward-line, probably, routine, nothing — and Vesper's whole body flinched around the sound.

"You've made a mistake," they whispered. "There's no such thing anymore. There hasn't been for twenty years."

"That's what I was told too." He was smiling now, a terrible, blood-flecked smile. "And yet here I am, dying in a charmseller's shop in Greyhollow, holding a map that won't open for any tongue but yours. So either the world is lying to both of us, Pemberton, or you are."

*Pemberton.* Nobody in Greyhollow knew the name. Vesper had buried it under five years of *Maren*, of *the charm-shop on Tanner's Row*, of small harmless trades and smaller harmless days. Hearing it spoken aloud in their own shop felt like watching someone dig up a grave.

"How do you know my name?"

"Bramble," he said, as if that answered it, and maybe it did; couriers know things, couriers carry things, and this one had clearly carried something worth killing for. "Bramble Wick. I ran messages for people who needed messages run. Three weeks ago I was hired to take a map out of the mountain and lose it somewhere no one would ever speak it aloud again. Instead I'm going to give it to you, which is worse luck for you and better luck for everyone else, and I'm sorry about that. Truly." His grip tightened. "I'm sorry about all of it."

"Give me what? Bramble — you're bleeding out, let me get the salve, at least let me—"

"It won't matter." He said it simply, without drama, the way men say things they've already made peace with on some road in the dark. "What matters is that they don't get it back. Whatever comes through that door tonight — it doesn't get it back. Promise me."

"I don't make promises," Vesper said, and the irony of it was so sharp they almost laughed. A swordspeaker who swore nothing. An oath to hold no oaths.

"Then hold this instead."

His hand went inside his leathers and came out with a scroll-case, plain lead, dented, crusted dark along one seam. When he pressed it into Vesper's palm, Vesper felt it the way you feel a struck bell through a wall — a hum, low and patient, like something asleep that knows your footsteps.

The map. Even wrapped in lead and blood, even silent, Vesper could feel it *waiting*.

"What is it?" Their voice came out hoarse. "What does it lead to?"

"The court beneath the mountain." Bramble's eyes were losing focus, drifting toward the ceiling as though he could see through it, through the roof, through the miles of stone to wherever he'd been. "Where they keep them. All of them. Every soul the Magistrate ever sold to buy his pretty prosperous city. Shelves of them, Pemberton. Glass jars full of voices." His breath hitched. "I saw my brother's name on one of the vessels. He was executed for theft when I was nine. Executed." Another laugh, thinner. "Executed, and shelved."

Outside, the bell rang again. Closer. And beneath it — Vesper strained, hardly daring — beneath it there was another sound now, one that had no business existing on a Greyhollow street at midnight. A dragging. Slow. Unhurried. Like something crossing the cobblestones that had all the time in the world because nothing in the world could stop it.

Bramble heard it too. His face went grey around the blood-loss pallor.

"It followed me from the weirs," he breathed. "It doesn't run. It doesn't have to. It just — arrives." He seized Vesper's collar. "Bar the door. Don't open it. Don't look at it. It can't come in unless it's invited, and it *invites itself* by making you want to look. Do you understand? Whatever you hear through that door — a voice, any voice — do not answer it."

"What will it—"

"It'll say it's me," Bramble said. "In about a minute. That's how it works. It wears the dead." His smile was ghastly and calm. "So we'd best finish first."

* * *

He unrolled the map across the floorboards between them, and Vesper understood immediately why it needed lead to be carried.

It wasn't ink. It was *oath* — lines of true script burned into vellum in the old swordspeaker's hand, the hand Vesper had learned at twelve years old from a woman named Corvane who had died at the Weeping Gate so that others could run. The script lay flat and dead on the page, meaningless squiggles, locked. But Vesper's fingers hovered over it and the hum in their chest rose to meet it, five years of silence straining against five years of silence, and they snatched their hand back as if from a stove.

"No," they said aloud. "No. Find someone else. Find anyone else."

"There is no one else." Bramble's voice was fading now, each word costing more than the last. "That's the whole point of what the Magistrate did. He didn't kill the speaking-gift, Pemberton. He just killed everyone who had it. Except one." His eyes found Vesper's, and they were clear now, terribly clear, a man spending his last sight well. "You're the last. I checked. I checked for three weeks, and you're the last, and the map chose your city, and I think — I think maps are smarter than we are."

The dragging sound reached the door.

Stopped.

And then, through four inches of oak and iron, in a voice Vesper knew — *knew*, warm and easy, a voice that had shared watch-fires and bad wine and one perfect hopeless night in the reed-beds outside Carrowmere — a dead man said:

"Vesper? Vesper, it's Aldous. I found you. Open the door, love. It's so cold out here."

Aldous Fen had been dead for nineteen years. Vesper had watched the light go out of him. And still their body leaned toward the door, their hand rising toward the bolt, grief doing what no hunter could ever manage — until Bramble's grip closed on their ankle like a shackle.

"Don't," he whispered.

Vesper stood in the dark of their own shop, shaking, hand half-raised, listening to a dead friend beg in a voice that was almost right and wrong in ways that scraped the soul raw. *Almost right.* That was the horror of it. Whoever had built this thing had studied love closely enough to counterfeit it.

"Aldous is ash," Vesper said to the door, quietly. "I scattered him myself. Get out of my voice."

The begging stopped. In the silence that followed there was a sound worse than the dragging — a soft, papery shifting, like pages turning, like something taking notes. Then the steps moved on, unhurried, fading down Tanner's Row toward the harbor, and Vesper discovered they had been holding their breath long enough to see stars.

* * *

Bramble lasted another quarter hour.

Vesper spent it the way you spend a thing you cannot save: uselessly, beautifully, pressing fever-salve into wounds that salves don't touch, holding his head up so he could drink watered wine, listening while he talked in fragments — the mountain, the parlor of mirrors, a Broker whose face was different every time you blinked, a girl's voice sold out of her throat to pay a debt she never owed. He spoke of the vessels. He spoke of the shelves. He spoke, near the end, of music.

"There's a musician," he murmured. "In your square. The one who lost her voice midsummer. Everyone thinks she's mute from sickness." His hand found Vesper's, clumsy. "She's not sick. She *signed*. She signed herself away in the parlor, and her voice is in a jar on a shelf, and I heard it, Pemberton. I heard it singing in the dark with all the others." His eyes were nearly closed. "I thought you should know. I thought — whoever you are to her — I thought you should know she's in there."

Vesper could not speak. Wren. Wren who stood in the market square every evening and moved her lips to songs she couldn't sing while the crowd pretended not to notice. Wren who chalked her jokes on a slate and smiled with her whole body and had not, in five months, once met Vesper's eyes.

Wren, whose voice was in a jar beneath a mountain.

"Her name," Vesper managed. "On the vessel. What name is on the vessel?"

"Wren," Bramble said. "Just Wren. The Broker likes them simple. Easy to file."

Then his breathing changed, and they both knew the shape of it, and Vesper gathered him up and held him because there was nothing else in the world left to do.

At the very end, his eyes opened — fully, lucidly, one last flare of the courier who had run three days ahead of death — and he looked at Vesper and said, in a voice that cracked the words like ice on a pond:

"*By the sigil sworn and the steel unsheathed, I set my word against your word: come forth and answer, or stand forever refuted.*"

The challenge-oath.

The old words. The first words. The words every swordspeaker learned before they learned to hold a blade — the words that had not been spoken aloud in twenty years, because speaking them was a declaration of war against everything the Magistrate had built.

The map in Vesper's hands flared white-hot through the lead case. Every charm on every shelf began, softly, to hum.

"Answer it," Bramble whispered. "Someone has to answer it. Answer it for me."

And then he was gone, heavy and still in Vesper's arms, in a shop that smelled of lavender and luck-knots, while outside the iron bells of Greyhollow began — one district, then another, then all of them at once — to ring.

Vesper laid him gently on the floorboards. Stood. Looked down at the scroll-case burning cold in their hands, at the door where a dead man had worn a dead friend's voice, at the humming shelves full of small safe lies.

Five years of silence, and the past had walked through it anyway, bleeding, and asked them by name.

Vesper picked up the poker, broke the lead seal, and unrolled the map.

The oath-script woke like eyes opening.
