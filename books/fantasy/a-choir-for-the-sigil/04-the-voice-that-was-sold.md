## 4. The Voice That Was Sold

Wren had learned to count in silence.

She counted everything now—the cracks in the ceiling of the room she rented above the chandler's shop, the coins her brother Tam brought home and laid out on the table like penitents, the days since midsummer. One hundred and eleven. She counted them the way other people prayed, because counting was the only sound left to her that no one could take away.

Her voice was gone. Not hoarse, not whispered—*gone*, scooped out of her like the flesh of a pear, leaving only the shape of it behind. When she opened her mouth, air moved and nothing else did. The first week she had screamed anyway, alone in her room with a pillow over her face, screaming into nothing, feeling the muscles work and the throat shape itself around sounds that never came. It was like drowning in dry air.

So she counted. And she wrote. And she hummed.

The humming was the worst kindness and the worst cruelty. She could still feel music in her body—could feel where a melody wanted to go, the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. She'd sit on the step outside the chandler's with her fiddle in her lap, bowing it softly while she hummed beneath the notes, and passersby would smile at the tune and never know that the woman playing it was screaming inside it. The fiddle carried what her throat couldn't. But it wasn't hers. It was wood and gut and rosin, someone else's voice lent to her on loan, and every night she wiped it down and put it away and lay in the dark feeling the hollow where her own song used to live.

Tam didn't know. That was the thing that sat on her chest at night, heavier than the loss itself. Tam thought she'd caught a sickness of the throat in the summer heat, that it would come back the way voices do after bad fevers—slowly, croakily, one word at a time. He kept bringing her honey and lemon rinds and asking, too casually, *any better today?* And she would smile and shake her head and write on her slate: *Patience.*

*Patience.* As if patience could grow back what she had sold.

Because that was the truth she carried under her ribs like a swallowed stone: there was no sickness. There was a debt—her father's debts, old ones, the kind that grew teeth—and there were men who came in the spring with ledgers and no smiles, and there was a notice nailed to their door saying Tam would go to the debtors' prison on Gull Street unless the account was settled by midsummer's end. And there had been a stranger in grey who found her in the market and said, very kindly, *There is another currency, if you have something of value. The Broker pays fair prices for quality goods.*

She had asked what kind of goods. She had been so naive, even then, even desperate, that she had asked.

*A voice of your quality,* the stranger said. *The court beneath the mountain pays handsomely for a singing voice. You would be compensated. Your brother would be free. And you would sign here.*

She had signed. She remembered the parlor—she would remember it until she died, however long that was—remembered the mirrors everywhere, so many mirrors that she couldn't find the walls, remembered the man behind the desk whose face she somehow could not describe afterward no matter how hard she tried, as though her memory of him had been included in the sale. Remembered the contract unrolling across the desk like a tongue, and the sigil at its foot: a circle broken by three descending lines, like a ladder going down into dark water.

*Sign at the sigil,* he said. *In your own hand. The signature is the seal.*

And she had signed, and the pen had drunk something out of her that she felt leave through her fingertips, a warmth, a trembling brightness, rising up her arm and out, and then the man had smiled—she thought he smiled; she couldn't recall his face but she recalled the smile, wide and satisfied as a well-fed cat—and said, *Pleasure doing business. The debt is settled. Your brother will never know.*

*Your brother will never know.* Those words had seemed like mercy at the time.

Now they were just another wall around her.

* * *

On the morning it all cracked open, Wren was tuning her fiddle on the chandler's step when Vesper Pemberton came down Cutpurse Lane at a pace that meant trouble.

She'd known Vesper for two years—known them the way you know someone you watch from across a square, trading small talk over charm-stalls and market gossip, laughing at each other's jokes a beat too warmly. Vesper sold luck-knots and fever-salves and pretended, badly, not to watch Wren play. Wren pretended, equally badly, not to notice being watched. It was the longest, slowest, most cowardly courtship in Greyhollow, conducted entirely in glances and half-finished sentences, and Wren had told herself a hundred times that after the season turned she would say something—write something—find the courage somewhere.

Now Vesper crossed the square toward her with a rolled paper under one arm and a look on their face like a person carrying a lit fuse through a powder store, and all Wren's carefully rationed courage curdled into dread.

They sat down beside her on the step without asking. Close. Closer than usual. Their knee nearly touched hers, and she could feel the tremor in them, fine as a plucked string.

"Wren." Vesper's voice was low. "I need—I have to show you something. And I need you to tell me I'm wrong."

Wren set down the fiddle. Took up her slate. *Wrong about what?*

Vesper unrolled the paper between them.

It was a map—or the ghost of one, lines shifting faintly on the parchment like fish under ice, ink that wouldn't hold still. Wren's eyes went to it and slid off, again and again, unable to fix on any single route or border. But at the bottom corner, drawn small and precise, was a sigil.

A circle, broken by three descending lines.

Like a ladder going down into dark water.

The world went very quiet and very far away. The market noise of Greyhollow—cries, cart wheels, the clang of the tinker's hammer—all of it receded as if a door had closed. Wren stared at the sigil and felt the memory rise in her like floodwater: the mirrors, the smiling shape of a face she couldn't keep, the pen drinking warmth out of her fingers, her own hand moving in a signature that was also a lock turning.

*I know that mark,* she wrote. Her chalk shook. *I put it there.*

Vesper went still beside her. "What?"

Wren couldn't look at them. She wrote fast, chalk squeaking, because if she stopped she would never start. *I signed a contract with that sigil at the bottom. Midsummer. In a parlor full of mirrors. They took my voice and paid my father's debts and freed my brother from Gull Street, and I let them, and I have hated myself every day since, and now you're holding a map to the place where I sold myself.*

She held the slate up between them like a shield.

For a long moment Vesper said nothing. Then, very quietly: "That's why you don't sing anymore."

*That's why I don't do anything anymore.*

"I thought—" Vesper stopped. Started again. "All summer I thought you were ill. I brought you fever-salve twice. You let me believe—"

*I let everyone believe.* Wren's chalk pressed so hard it snapped. She picked up the stub and kept writing, the letters getting uglier. *Do you understand what I am? I'm not sick. I'm *sold*. There is a piece of paper under a mountain with my name on it and my voice in a jar somewhere, and some creature owns it, owns *me*, and I have spent four months smiling at my brother and letting him bring me honey for a wound that has no cure, because the truth would break him and it would change nothing. Nothing. So yes, Vesper. I lied. I lie every day. It's the only voice I have left.*

The chalk snapped again. She hadn't realized she was crying until a tear hit the slate and smeared the last word.

Vesper's hand came up—hesitated—and then closed gently over hers, chalk dust and all.

"You're not property," they said. It came out strange, formal, like a recitation. Like something learned long ago and never forgotten. "No soul is property. That's—that's the first law. The oldest one. I—" They stopped, jaw working, and Wren watched five years of careful hiding war behind their eyes with something older and fiercer. "I know how to get it back. The map leads there. To the court. And I'm going."

Wren pulled her hand free and wrote: *Then I'm coming with you.*

"No."

*You don't get to decide that.*

"It's not safe. What's down there—it buys people, Wren. It collects them. If it knows what you are—"

*It already knows what I am. I'm the receipt.* She underlined it twice, hard enough to score the slate. *You think I can stay here? Humming on a step while you walk into the place that ate my voice? Every day you're gone I'll die by inches wondering, and every day you're gone Tam will ask why his sister cries into her fiddle at night, and I will have to keep lying, and I am DONE lying.*

She was breathing hard. Somewhere across the square, a bell rang the hour, bright and indifferent.

Vesper looked at her—at the ruined chalk, the wet slate, the fiddle lying silent in her lap—and something in their face broke open. Not pity. Recognition. The look of someone seeing their own cage reflected in another's eyes.

"Every instinct I have," Vesper said slowly, "says to hide you somewhere deep and safe and go alone."

Wren raised an eyebrow. *And?*

"And every oath I ever swore says that no one gets left behind to be owned." They laughed, short and bitter. "I've spent five years breaking that oath by living quietly. I'm apparently done."

*Good,* Wren wrote. Then, before she could stop herself, before the cowardice that had governed two years of glances could catch her: *Because I have loved you since the winter market, you impossible, stubborn fool, and I am not spending whatever comes next pretending otherwise.*

She thrust the slate at Vesper and immediately wanted to die.

Vesper read it. Read it again. Color rose in their face—not embarrassment; something warmer, more startled, like sunrise catching someone unawares. When they spoke, their voice had gone rough.

"The winter market," they said. "The night the fiddler's fire went out and you played three hours in the cold so the carolers wouldn't lose their crowd. I stood by the pie stall the whole time. I bought four pies I didn't want just to have a reason to stay."

Wren stared at them. Four months mute, and this—*this*—was the moment she most wished for her voice, to say something worthy of it. Instead she did the only thing she could. She leaned over and pressed her forehead against theirs, and hummed—one low note, the note that lived at the bottom of her hollow, the closest thing to herself she had left—and felt Vesper shiver at the sound of it.

When she pulled back, she wrote: *When do we leave?*

"That's the problem." Vesper's face changed. The warmth drained out of it, replaced by something grim and urgent, and they glanced over their shoulder at the square—at the witch-hunters at the far fountain, iron bells hanging silent at their belts, and Captain Serane Ovid standing among them, head tilted, listening to something no one else could hear. Listening, Wren realized with a drop of cold in her stomach, in the direction of Vesper.

"They traced me," Vesper said quietly. "Last night. The map made me speak, and speaking carries, and Ovid has ears like a hound. We may already be out of time." They stood, rolling the map with shaking hands. "Pack nothing you love. Meet me at the chandler's back door at moonrise. And Wren—"

They hesitated. Looked down at her, and for one unguarded moment all the terror and tenderness in them showed plain.

"—whatever happens, don't let them take anything else from you. Not even your silence. Especially not your silence. It's the last thing that's still yours."

Then they were gone, quick and quiet down Cutpurse Lane, and Wren sat alone on the step with her snapped chalk and her wet slate and her heart hammering out a rhythm she couldn't follow and couldn't stop.

Above the chandler's shop, in the room she rented, there was a loose floorboard under the bed. Under the floorboard was a tin box, and in the tin box was the one thing she had kept from that mirrored parlor: the carbon copy of her signature, pressed through onto a scrap of blotting paper, which she had stolen on her way out without knowing why.

She knew why now.

Somewhere under the mountain, in a jar on a shelf, her voice was waiting. And she intended to walk into that court, find the shelf, and take it back—with the seal that had locked it away, if it came to that.

Wren looked once more toward the fountain, where Captain Ovid had begun, unhurried, to walk.

Then she went inside to pack.
