## 5. Iron Bells at Dawn

Serane Ovid had learned to read echoes the way other people read weather.

She stood in the mouth of Tanner's Row before dawn, the iron bell at her belt hanging silent and heavy as a sleeping wasp, and she listened to what the night had left behind. Frost silvered the cobbles. The city's lamps had guttered out hours ago, and Greyhollow in that hour between dark and morning looked like a woman pretending to sleep—breathing slow, eyes shut tight, waiting for footsteps.

The echo was still there. Faint. A thread of sound that wasn't sound, snagged on the corner of a shuttered stall, caught in the mortar of a chimney stack. Oath-magic. She hadn't felt it in five years—not since the last of the swordspeakers had been declared dead in absentia and the Magistrate's ledgers closed their final column—but she knew it the way you know a voice from childhood. It had a shape. It had a *weight*. And it had been spoken here, in her city, three nights running, growing louder each time like a man calling into a well.

"Captain." Her lieutenant, Doss, came up the row at a jog, breath steaming, his own bell muffled in wool as protocol demanded until contact. "The resonance map confirms it. Third reading, same quarter. The charm-seller's street."

"The charm-seller." Serane turned the name over. Pemberton. Vesper Pemberton, who sold luck-knots that were only knots and fever-salves that were only goose-fat and willow, who paid taxes on time and kept their head down and had never once, in five years of inspections, given her cause to open the iron cage she carried at her hip. "You think a knot-tier is speaking oaths?"

"I think something on that street is," Doss said. "And orders are orders."

Orders were orders. Serane had built her life on that sentence the way a drowning man builds a raft—lashing one plank to another, duty to duty, until it floated and she didn't have to look down at the water. She touched the bell at her belt. Cold iron, cast from the same pour as every hunter's bell in Greyhollow; it hummed near unlicensed magic, and it sang near oath-magic, and it had not sung for her sister's sake or anyone's, only for the Magistrate's quiet, profitable peace.

"Two bells forward," she said. "No muffle. If it's nothing, we knock politely and apologize for the hour."

Doss hesitated. "And if it's something?"

Serane watched frost form on the charm-seller's door, letter by letter, like the city writing its own warrant.

"Then we do what we're paid for," she said, and hated how easily the sentence came.

* * *

Vesper woke with Bramble's ghost screaming in the back room.

Not aloud—the dead courier never made a sound anymore, which was somehow worse. His presence pressed against the walls of the shop like weather against glass: a cold front of fury and grief that made the candle flames lean sideways and the luck-knots on their pegs tremble on their strings. He had been louder since the third reading of the map. Louder since Vesper had spoken the route's first line in true oath-tongue, alone, at midnight, and felt the words go out of them like blood leaving a vein—and heard, distantly, across the sleeping city, an iron bell begin to sing.

*They know,* Bramble did not say, but made unmistakable anyway. The temperature in the room dropped until Vesper's breath bloomed white. *They always know. I ran for six days and they knew.*

"I know." Vesper was already moving, pulling the floorboard over the cache where the map lay coiled in oilcloth, sweeping charms into a satchel with hands that shook only a little. Five years of practice at packing a life in ninety seconds. "I know, Bramble. Thank you."

*Don't thank me. Hate me. I brought this to your door because dying men are selfish.* The ghost's cold circled them, agitated, a dog that couldn't stop pacing. *The Broker's court is under the mountain and the mountain is three days north and you'll be dead in two unless—*

A knock at the shop door. Polite. Measured. Terrible.

Three knocks, a pause, and then the sound Vesper had dreaded every dawn for five years: the unmuffled ring of an iron bell, struck once, deliberately, the way hunters announced themselves when they already knew.

Vesper blew out the candle.

Through the gap in the shutter, grey light showed four figures in the street, and at their head a tall woman in a hunter's longcoat, frost on her shoulders, an iron bell swinging from her belt like a verdict. Captain Serane Ovid. Vesper had sold her a salve for chapped hands once, two winters ago, and remembered thinking: *she has kind eyes, and that is the most dangerous thing about her.*

The back window opened onto the alley. The alley dropped, after three crooked flights, into the flooded undercity—Greyhollow's drowned first life, canals and cellars from before the Magistrate raised the streets above the waterline. Every smuggler knew it. Every fugitive. Vesper had sworn never to need it.

Sworn things, Vesper was learning, had a way of coming due.

They went out through the window as the door came down.

* * *

Wren was waiting at the canal mouth, exactly where Vesper had told her to wait an hour ago by chalk note slipped under her door: *Trouble. Undercity. Bring nothing you love except yourself.* She stood on the flooded steps with her brother's old coat around her shoulders and a lantern held high, and when Vesper dropped out of the alley dripping and wild-eyed, she didn't ask questions. She had stopped asking questions midsummer, when her voice left her and questions became luxuries.

Instead she held up her slate.

*HUNTERS?*

"Hunters." Vesper took the lantern. Their hands brushed on the handle and neither of them pulled away fast enough, which was its own confession. "Ovid herself. Wren—I have to tell you what's happening, all of it, but not here. Can you walk the low tunnels?"

Wren wrote, erased, wrote again. *I've lived in Greyhollow my whole life. Can YOU?*

It was fair. Vesper had lived above the waterline for five years, careful and small, while Wren had grown up in the drowned streets, singing for coins on the canal ferries before the debtors took her voice. She knew the undercity the way Vesper knew oaths: in the body, without needing to think.

She led. Vesper followed. Behind them both, silent as falling snow, Bramble's ghost drifted with the grim satisfaction of a man finally being taken seriously.

* * *

The undercity was a cathedral of black water and brick. Their lantern threw rings of gold across the canal surface; overhead, the vaulted undersides of the new streets passed like the ribs of some enormous buried animal. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone and flood, they could hear bells—more than four now. The hunt was spreading through the upper city like ink through cloth.

Wren moved surely along the maintenance ledges, testing each footing with her boot before trusting it, pausing at junctions to read the old tar-marks the ferry-children used for navigation. Twice she stopped Vesper with a flat hand against their chest—once for a patrol of hunters crossing a grate overhead, their bells humming faintly, hungry; once for nothing Vesper could hear at all, though afterward, listening hard, they caught it: a deep, rhythmic groan in the walls, the sound the undercity made when a sluice gate opened somewhere and the water level began to shift.

At the third junction she sat abruptly on the ledge and began writing, fast, chalk snapping.

*I know that sigil. On the paper you showed me last night. The spiral with the tooth through it. I signed it.*

Vesper crouched beside her. "You signed it."

*In the parlor. Under the mountain. They gave me tea first, and a chair softer than anything I'd ever sat in, and a man with a face like a ledger asked me to sign a contract for my brother's debts. Eleven years of my family's debts, gone, in exchange for—"a consideration." I thought he meant money.* Her hand was shaking so hard the letters degraded. *He meant my voice. They put me in a glass room and told me to sing, and the room drank it. I felt it go. Like being unplugged from myself.*

"Wren." Vesper said it gently, and then, because gentle wasn't enough, said it again like an oath, which is what it was: "Wren. We're going to get it back. That's what the map is. That's why I'm going under the mountain."

She stared at them. In the lantern light her eyes were wet and furious and hopeful in a way that hurt worse than despair would have. Then she wiped the slate clean and wrote one word:

*WHY?*

And there it was—the question Vesper had spent five years not answering, the one that lived under the tongue like a coin under the mattress. Why did a charm-seller owe a street musician a war? Why had Vesper memorized the cadence of her humming outside the market square, catalogued the songs she could no longer play, learned to read her chalk-handwriting the way scholars read dead languages?

Bramble's ghost drifted close, and the water around him frosted. *Tell her,* he did not say, with terrible patience. *Or I will haunt you about it for eternity, and I am very good at haunting.*

"Because," Vesper said, "no soul is property. That's the first law. Older than the Magistrate, older than the bells. And because—" and here the vigilance cracked, five years of it, all at once, like ice off a roof in spring, "—because I listen to you hum outside the square every week and it's the best thing in this whole rotten city, and I love you, and I let them take your voice because loving you out loud meant becoming visible again, and I chose the hiding. I chose wrong. I'm choosing right now."

The lantern flame stood perfectly still between them.

Wren looked at Vesper for a long moment. Then she did something she hadn't done since midsummer: she reached up, cupped their jaw with one cold hand, and hummed—one note, low, rough, the ghost of music rather than music itself, all that remained of a voice that had bought her brother's freedom. It was the ugliest beautiful sound Vesper had ever heard.

Then she wrote: *Then stop apologizing and start walking. You talk too much for someone whose weapon is talking.*

Despite everything—the bells, the flood, the dead man at their shoulder—Vesper laughed. It came out ragged and real.

* * *

They made the eastern sluice tunnel as the water rose behind them.

That was when Bramble stopped drifting and planted himself across the passage like a fallen beam, and the temperature plunged, and frost feathered up the brickwork in spirals—the Broker's sigil, drawn again and again in ice by a dead man's rage.

*Promise me,* the ghost said, and this time it was almost audible, a voice like wind through a keyhole. *When you stand in that court, among those shelves of glass—you find the vessel with my soul in it. The Broker bought mine too, at the end. Did you think I carried that map out of charity? I was going to sell it BACK to them, buy myself out of the shelf, and instead they broke me on the mountain road like a bad contract.* The frost spread. *Promise me vengeance, swordspeaker. Promise me the vessels break.*

Vesper looked at the ghost—at the courier who had died to deliver a challenge-oath into the world, whether he'd meant to or not—and understood that mercy and vengeance were, tonight, the same road.

"I promise," Vesper said, and spoke it in true oath-tongue, and the word went out through the water and the stone like a bell rung underwater—and somewhere far above, faint but unmistakable, Captain Serane Ovid's iron bell answered it, singing.

Wren grabbed Vesper's sleeve. Through the tunnel mouth ahead, grey daylight showed the mountain road climbing north out of the city, frost-covered, empty.

Behind them, at the far end of the flooded gallery, lanterns. Many. Moving fast.

"She's not following the water," Vesper breathed. "She's following *me*. Every oath I speak, she hears."

Wren wrote on her slate, held it up in the lantern light, already turning toward the open road:

*THEN STOP SPEAKING AND START RUNNING.*
