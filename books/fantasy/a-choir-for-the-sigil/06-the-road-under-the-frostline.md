## 6. The Road Under the Frostline

The mountain road began where the map said roads should end.

Vesper had spoken the last line of the oath-tongue three days ago, on the flooded steps beneath Greyhollow, and the words were still working through them like fever. Every mile north, the magic sat louder in their chest—a banked fire they could not stop feeding, because the map drank oaths the way a lamp drank oil. *Speak, and I will show you. Speak, and I will lead you.* It was the first time in five years that anything had wanted Vesper to speak, and some traitorous part of them had missed it so badly it ached.

Wren walked beside them with her chalkboard slung across her back like a shield. She had stopped writing sentences somewhere past the second ridge and started writing only what mattered: *cold*, *rest*, *look*—and once, pressed into the frost of a boulder as they climbed, a small drawing of two figures leaning together under one cloak. Vesper had looked at it too long. Bramble's ghost had drifted ahead the whole while, translucent and furious, pointing the way with a dead man's certainty.

*Left at the split stone,* he said, though only Vesper could hear him now; the gift that let the dead speak through an oath-speaker was waking up along with everything else. *Left, and down, and mind the ice. I broke my ankle there in spring. It was a bad spring.*

"You've mentioned," Vesper muttered.

Wren glanced over. *Talking to your ghost again?*

"He's not my ghost."

*I'm nobody's ghost,* Bramble said, with dignity. *I am a courier between deliveries.*

The frostline took them at dusk on the third day. Below it, autumn still clung in brown and rust; above it, the world went white and silent, breath turning to lace, the pines bowed under snow like penitents. And there—at the split stone, left and down, exactly as a dead man promised—the road became a stair, and the stair became a door cut into the mountainside, its lintel carved with a sigil Vesper knew better than their own name.

An oath-mark. A true one, old style, the kind that hadn't been legal to carve since before Vesper was born.

Bramble hovered before it, and for the first time since his death his fury quieted into something almost like reverence. *They kept it lit,* he said softly. *All these years, they kept it lit.*

"Who's *they*?"

But the door was already opening.

* * *

The enclave lived inside the mountain's shoulder the way a wren lives inside a wall—small, warm, and utterly unbothered by the fact that the world believed it extinct. Vesper counted eleven hearths glowing in the cavern's terraced dark, herb-racks hanging from the ceiling like inverted gardens, goats in a stone byre, children—actual children—chasing each other between the drying sheds. Hedge-witches. The real kind, the outlawed kind, the kind the witch-hunters' iron bells were cast to find.

A woman met them at the threshold: broad-shouldered, silver-streaked, with burn scars laddering her forearms and eyes the flat grey of weathered slate. She looked at Vesper for a long moment. Then she looked at Vesper's hands—specifically at the backs of them, where five years of hiding had done nothing to fade the fine silver tracery of the oath-marks that bloomed whenever the gift woke.

"Well," she said. "A swordspeaker. Walking. Breathing." She stepped aside. "Come in before the mountain changes its mind about you. I'm Maren Ostwick. I buried three of your order. You'll forgive me if I stare."

Inside, the warmth hit like a wall. Someone pressed a bowl of barley soup into Wren's hands; someone else took Vesper's soaked cloak without asking; a girl of perhaps eight stood staring openly at the oath-marks until her mother pulled her away. Wren wrote quickly on her board and held it up: *Are we safe here?*

Maren read it, and something moved behind her slate-grey eyes. "Safe," she repeated, tasting the word. "Here? Yes. Here is the only safe left, and even that's a loan." She turned to Vesper. "Your ghost can come in too. We've room for the dead. We've had practice."

Bramble drifted through the doorway like smoke through a keyhole, and Vesper watched eleven hearths flicker in greeting—or warning—and understood that this place had survived twenty years of extermination by knowing exactly what walked toward its door.

* * *

They fed them. They dried them. And then, when the children were abed and the cavern had gone quiet except for the tick of cooling stone, Maren sat Vesper down beside the largest hearth, poured two cups of something dark and bitter, and said:

"Show me the map."

Vesper hesitated—but Wren was watching, and Bramble was watching, and lying to a woman who had buried swordspeakers felt like a particular kind of blasphemy. They unrolled the bloodstained vellum on the hearthstone. In the firelight, the oath-script shimmered faintly, hungry, waiting to be spoken.

Maren didn't touch it. She looked at it the way one looks at a snake one has learned to respect.

"The court beneath the mountain," she said. "You're going to the Broker."

"We're going to buy back a voice," Vesper said. "Hers." They nodded at Wren, who had gone very still.

Maren was quiet for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—not softer, but lower, the voice of someone setting down a weight they'd carried alone too long.

"Then you'd best know what you're walking into. And you'd best know why you're alive to walk into it at all." She sipped her cup. "Tell me, swordspeaker. Five years ago, when the Proscription came—when the bells were cast and the hunts began and every gifted soul in Greyhollow ran or burned—why do you suppose *you* were never caught?"

"I hid well."

"You hid adequately. So did forty others. They're ash." Maren set down her cup. "The Magistrate's hunters found everyone. Everyone but the last swordspeaker, who they circled for months and then—mysteriously—lost. Who do you think gave the order to lose you?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the byre, a goat complained. Vesper heard none of it. "That's not—" Their voice came out wrong. "The hunts were real. I saw the pyres. I smelled them."

"Oh, the pyres were real." Maren leaned forward. "Ask yourself what burned in them. Ask yourself why the condemned were never given back to their families. Why the bodies were carted north, up this very road, under seal, escorted by the Magistrate's own guard. Ask yourself why Greyhollow—poor, grey, starving Greyhollow—suddenly has full granaries and clean water and no plague, in a decade when every city around it rotted."

Wren's chalk was moving before Vesper could speak, fast and jagged: *The executions. The ones from midsummer. My brother's debtors—two of them were taken instead. Carted north.*

"Yes," Maren said simply. "Carted north. To the court beneath the mountain. To the Broker." She looked into the fire. "The Magistrate didn't outlaw magic, child. He *monopolized* it. Every soul the law condemns becomes currency. The Broker pays him in prosperity—harvests that don't fail, winters that don't bite, a city that shines while the world starves. That's the bargain. That's been the bargain for twenty years. Your exile wasn't mercy, swordspeaker. It was inventory management. A live swordspeaker who might someday be needed is worth more than a dead one. They kept you the way a miser keeps a coin he doesn't yet know how to spend."

Vesper's hands had gone numb around the cup. "No. No—I *watched* them hunt people. Serane Ovid nearly took me twice. The bells rang for me—"

"Bells ring loudest for the quarry they intend to miss." Maren's voice was almost gentle now, which was worse. "Fear keeps a hounded thing close to its den. You spent five years curled small in Greyhollow, half-dead with vigilance, useless to everyone including yourself. That was the design. You weren't spared, Vesper Pemberton. You were *stored*."

The word landed like a blade between ribs. Stored. Five years of terror, of silence, of flinching at iron bells, of watching Wren sing across a square and daring not to love her out loud—all of it curated. All of it a shelf in someone else's pantry.

Beside them, Wren made a sound. Not a word—she had no words—but a raw, humming note of fury, and her hand found Vesper's and gripped hard enough to hurt, and that was the only reason Vesper did not come apart entirely.

"And Bramble?" Vesper managed. "Why send a courier? Why now?"

At the name, the ghost rose from the corner where he'd been sulking, and his translucence sharpened with purpose. *Because the ledger changed,* he said. *Because the Magistrate has begun buying bigger than souls. Ask her about the Sigil. Ask her what the Broker is building.*

Maren heard none of it, but she answered anyway, as if the question had traveled by other roads. "Because the Magistrate is running out of condemned men, and getting greedy. There's talk in the low country of a working—a great working—that needs more voices than the prisons yield. The Broker wants a swordspeaker's oath to seal it. An oath that binds reality doesn't come cheap." Her grey eyes fixed on Vesper. "They sent Bramble because couriers are expendable and maps are patient. They expected him to die bringing it to you. They expected grief and guilt to drive you up the mountain, straight into the parlor, ready to trade. Everything you've done since midnight three days ago—the flight, the fear, the road—you have done *on schedule*."

The silence after that was total. Even the fire seemed to hold its breath.

Vesper looked down at the map on the hearthstone, at the blood that was not theirs soaking its edges, and felt the full architecture of the trap settle over them like snowfall: soft, silent, and deep enough to bury in. Every step had been chosen for them. Every fear had been budgeted.

Except.

"Then we change the schedule," Vesper said.

It came out quieter than they meant. But the oath-marks on their hands had begun to glow, silver-bright, and the map drank the light and shivered, eager, and Maren Ostwick looked at the last swordspeaker in the world and—for the first time—smiled.

"That," she said, "is what your order used to say. Before my generation burned theirs." She stood, joints cracking, and raised her voice toward the sleeping terraces. "Ostwick! Wake the others. Light the signal-fire. Our guest is going to need everything we have by morning—because if the Magistrate stored a sword for twenty years, you may be certain he's coming to check on his investment."

Far below, down the white throat of the pass, an iron bell began to ring.

Not hunting-howl. Not patrol-cadence. Something older, and slower, and deliberate—one toll, then silence, then another, climbing the mountain like footsteps.

Serane Ovid had found the trail.
