## 7. Serane's Bargain

The pass had a name on the old survey maps—*Kestrel's Throat*—but the hunters called it the Quieting, because up there the wind died and the cold came down like a held breath, and even the iron bells at their belts rang muffled and small.

Serane Ovid stood in that silence and listened for the thing she had been chasing for six days: the echo of an oath spoken true.

It was fainter now, worn down by distance and snow, but it was there, threaded through the mountain like a wire through wax. Oath-magic left a residue no ordinary witch could hear—a harmonic under the world, a wrongness in the note of things. Serane had been trained to hear it since she was nine years old, kneeling in the Magistrate's hall with a tuning fork of black iron pressed to her breastbone while an examiner watched her pupils dilate. *You have the gift of hearing, child,* they'd told her. *Not the gift itself. You will never speak power. But you will spend your life listening for those who do.*

They had not told her what it would cost to listen so long. They had not told her that after twenty years, you began to hear other things in the harmonics too—grief, mostly, and fear, and the particular frequency of a person running out of road.

She heard all three now, coming up the switchbacks below her.

* * *

Her squad was four strong: Doss and Merrick with their net-irons, young Pell with the lantern that burned witch-light, and herself with the bells. She had ordered them into a spread across the throat of the pass an hour before dawn, positions drilled so often they moved like clockwork, like teeth closing. It was a good trap. It was, in fact, the same trap she had used eleven times before, and eleven times it had ended with someone kneeling in the snow with iron at their throat, and once—with a boy of nineteen who had only wanted to charm rain away from his mother's roof—it had ended worse than that.

Serane did not think about the boy. She had gotten very good, over twenty years, at not thinking.

The fugitives came up the last switchback as the light went grey-gold behind the eastern peaks. She saw them first as movement against the snow: three figures where she expected two. The swordspeaker—Pemberton, though the dossier said *Vesper*, and the dossier was right, she could feel it, the oath-mark on them rang like a struck bell even at two hundred paces. The musician girl beside them, wrapped in Pemberton's coat, moving with the careful economy of someone conserving warmth. And between them, translucent as breath on glass, a dead man walking—the courier ghost, Bramble, flickering like a candle in draft.

A ghost guiding its own killers' quarry. Serane filed that away. The dead did not do favors for free.

"Positions," she murmured into the speaking-charm at her collar, and felt, rather than heard, Doss and Merrick shift in their hollows above the trail, Pell dropping back to close the rear. The trap's teeth closed until only the gap remained, and the gap was her.

She stepped onto the trail.

* * *

Up close, Vesper Pemberton looked less like the woodcut in the dossier and more like what they were: tired, frostbitten, and holding themselves the way people hold themselves when they have decided to die standing if it comes to that. Their hand rested on the hilt of a sword wrapped in cloth—an affectation, or a superstition, or both. Their eyes found Serane's bells first, then her face, and something in their expression shifted from fear to a bitter recognition.

"You're the one from the market," Pemberton said. "The inspection."

"I remember." She remembered everything; it was the curse of her trade. "Vesper Pemberton. Last swordspeaker. Wanted for unlicensed working of oath-magic, harboring a contracted soul, and—" she consulted the warrant by habit, though she knew it by heart, "—the murder of the courier Bramble Fen."

"He wasn't murdered," the girl said.

Serane looked at her. Wren Ashgrave, the dossier said. Mute since midsummer. Contracted soul, debt-bound, property of the Broker's court beneath Mount Cael. On paper she was not even a person—she was an asset in transit, and arresting her was technically a repossession.

On paper. Serane had stopped believing in paper around the time she started believing in harmonics.

"The ghost says otherwise," she said. "He's standing right beside you, screaming his death at anyone with ears."

"He's screaming about the Broker," Pemberton said quietly. "You know that already. Don't you, Captain?"

The wind chose that moment to move, a single long exhale down the pass, and Serane's bells shivered but did not ring. Above, she knew, Doss had his net-iron leveled. One word from her and it would be over in ten seconds. Kneeling. Iron. The wagon south. And then—

And then the mountain. The parlor beneath it. The shelves.

*Say nothing,* said the part of her that had survived twenty years. *Take them. Go home. Open the drawer.*

But the other part of her—the part that had been listening so long it had begun to hear grief in the world's harmonics—looked at Wren Ashgrave's face, at the fierce terrified love in it, and thought: *that is exactly how I would look, if I were climbing this mountain for her instead of hunting those who are.*

"Captain," Pell called from below, nervous, the lantern guttering. "Orders?"

"Hold," Serane said, and did not turn around.

* * *

Her sister's name was Liss. She had been eleven years older, a hedge-witch of no formal training who could coax tomatoes to ripen in Greyhollow's grey summer and quiet colicky infants with a hummed note, and when the purges came she had run, badly, and been caught within the week. There had been no trial worth the name. There had been a wagon, and a sentence read aloud—*condemned for unlicensed practice, execution by the state's mercy*—and then the wagon had gone not to the gallows square but to the mountain road, and Serane, sixteen years old and newly sworn to the hunt, had stood at her window and watched it go.

Everyone knew the condemned went to the mountain. Everyone agreed not to say what everyone knew. That was the bargain Greyhollow had made with its prosperity: the lamps never dimmed, the granaries never emptied, the fever never came—and no one asked what fed it. Serane had asked, once, years later, in the careful way you ask a question you already know the answer to, and her commanding officer had looked at her with something almost like pity and said, *Ovid. Your sister is at rest. Be grateful.*

At rest. In a glass vessel on a shelf in a mirrored court, murmuring forever, poured out in drops whenever the Magistrate needed the city's luck topped up.

Serane kept a drawer in her quarters. In the drawer was Liss's chipped clay bead-bracelet, a lock of hair gone brittle in wax paper, and a list—eleven names long—that she had compiled over six years from the files of the condemned, cross-referenced against the hunt's own records, each name a person whose soul should, by every law she enforced, be scattered and gone, and yet whose harmonics she had *heard*, faint and unmistakable, drifting off the mountain on certain windless nights. Eleven souls still singing. Still owned.

She had never told anyone about the list. A list like that was treason with ink on it.

* * *

"You're hesitating," Pemberton said. Not accusing. Observing, the way a swordspeaker observed anything—as if words were blades and they could see the edge of hers.

"I'm assessing," Serane said.

"You're hesitating," Wren said, and her voice—her actual voice, hoarse and rusted, the first words Serane had heard her speak—cracked on the second syllable. Both of them seemed startled by it. The ghost beside them turned its head sharply, as if the sound had hurt it, or healed it.

Serane let the silence stretch. Below, Pell's lantern trembled. Above, the snow creaked under Doss's shifting weight.

Here was the arithmetic she had done a thousand times on a thousand nights: four hunters, one exhausted swordspeaker, one mute girl, one ghost. Take them, and the echo-chase ends, and the Magistrate's ledger balances, and somewhere beneath the mountain a shelf gains three more vessels—one of them perhaps labeled *Ovid, S., traitor*, because Pemberton was right, she was hesitating, and hesitation was legible, and legible hesitation got hunters audited. Audited hunters got summoned to the mountain. Summoned hunters sometimes did not come back, and their families were told they'd died with honor.

Or.

Or she did the thing she had rehearsed in the dark for six years without ever quite believing she would do it.

"Pemberton," she said. "Your map. The route it shows. Does it pass the vaults?"

Pemberton's eyes narrowed. "What vaults?"

"The deep shelves. Where they keep the ones that aren't spent. The inventory." Her voice stayed level. Twenty years of practice. Only her hands, gloved in wool, betrayed her, pressing hard against her thighs. "There's a woman there. Or what's left of one. Dark hair, half-shorn on the left side—she cut it herself, badly, the night before they took her. She'd have been taken eight years ago. Her name is Liss Ovid, and if any part of her is still on that shelf, I want proof. Something that carries her harmonic. Something I can hear."

The wind moved again. Somewhere far above, ice calved off a cornice and fell with a sound like distant glass.

"And in exchange?" Pemberton said carefully.

"In exchange, my squad develops sudden blindness at this pass. Reports a rockslide. Loses the trail." She pulled off one glove, reached into her coat, and drew out the folded warrant—not the copy, the original, bearing the Magistrate's seal—and held it out over the snow between them. "I know what I'm offering. I've written the treason charge myself enough times to recite it. If you're caught, you'll say you took me hostage. If I'm caught, I'm dead, and none of you will mourn me, and that's fair."

"Why?" Wren asked. Her voice was stronger now, finding itself the way a foundering swimmer finds the surface. "Why would you—"

"Because I have spent twenty years enforcing a law that says no soul is property," Serane said, "and I have known for eight years that the law is a lie, and I have done nothing, because doing nothing kept me breathing." She looked at the ghost—Bramble, watching her with the terrible patience of the dead. "Then a dying man ran past my patrol in the rain six days ago, and I heard him swear an oath older than the Magistrate's whole rotten charter, and I have not slept since. So. Why? Because I am tired, musician. Because I am so *tired*."

She held out the warrant another inch. Her arm did not shake. She was proud of that, in a small and useless way.

"Proof of my sister," she said. "That's the price. Proof she existed, proof she suffered, proof she was *stolen*—not executed, stolen. Bring me that, and I'll open every door between here and the court, and if it comes to it I'll stand in front of you when the shooting starts. Fail, and I'll have lost nothing but the life I wasn't using anyway."

* * *

Above the pass, Doss's voice crackled thin through the speaking-charm: *"Captain? Window's closing. Weather turns within the hour."*

Serane did not answer him. She watched Vesper Pemberton look at the warrant, then at Wren, then at the ghost—and she watched the moment happen that she had seen on eleven faces before, kneeling in the snow: the moment when a hunted person realizes the hunter has become, impossibly, a fellow prisoner, and must decide whether that makes them an ally or merely a more dangerous animal.

Pemberton's hand left the sword hilt.

"Your sister's harmonic," they said slowly. "If we find her vessel—if we can bring you something she touched, something that still holds her sound—you'd hear it? Even through glass?"

"I've heard her through stone," Serane said. "Through eight years of stone and a city's worth of willful deafness. Glass won't stop me."

Something moved through the ghost then, a ripple like wind over water, and Bramble spoke for the first time in her presence—his voice arriving not through air but directly into the bones of her ear, the way the dead spoke, intimate and awful:

*"She keeps a list. Ask her how many names."*

Serane went very still.

"How many names, Captain?" Pemberton echoed, and their eyes—those oath-marked eyes, seeing far too much—fixed on hers. "The ghost says you keep a list. How many?"

The truth climbed her throat like a thing escaping a burning house.

"Eleven," Serane said. "Eleven souls the records say are scattered, that I have heard singing on the night winds off this mountain. Eleven names in a drawer in my quarters, and tomorrow, if your rockslide story holds, twelve." She pulled the glove back on, and her voice, when it came again, was the voice she'd had at sixteen, watching the wagon go. "Now. Are you going to take my warrant, swordspeaker, or am I going to have to arrest you after all? Because the weather's turning, and I have a sister to prove, and neither of us has time for either of our silences anymore."

Far above them, the cornice groaned. The first flakes of the storm came sifting down through the gold light, fine as ash, and on the wind beneath them—faint, unmistakable, impossible—Serane heard, for the twelfth time, the mountain sing.
