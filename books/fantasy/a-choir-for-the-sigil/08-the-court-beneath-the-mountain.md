## 8. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The tunnels began to sing three hours after we left Serane at the pass.

It was not music, exactly—or it was every music at once, worn down to a single sustained note by stone and time. The sound came up through the soles of my boots first, then through my teeth, then through the oath-mark on my wrist, which had not stopped aching since we crossed the frostline. Wren walked ahead of me with one hand pressed flat to the tunnel wall, her fingers spread as if she were reading it. Maybe she was. She had signed herself into this place once. Stone remembers what touches it.

*The walls are full of voices,* Bramble's ghost said, drifting beside my shoulder like a lantern that had forgotten how to be warm. *Can you hear them? I couldn't hear them when I was alive. Now I can't stop.*

I could hear them. Underneath the mountain's long note there were others—thin ones, cracked ones, voices singing fragments of songs I half-knew: a lullaby from the Saltmarsh district, a work-chant from the tanneries, a drinking song that had been banned in Greyhollow for being seditious and had been sung anyway, in cellars, for twenty years. Each fragment rose and faded like breath on cold glass.

Wren turned back to look at me. In the witchlight she was all shadows and cheekbones, and her eyes asked the question her voice could no longer carry.

"It's them," I said. "The executed. All of them."

Her hand curled slowly against the wall.

Serane walked last, and she did not touch the stone at all. She had not spoken since the pass, since she'd given us her cloak and her sister's name—*Lira Ovid, sold in the autumn of the Magistrate's third year, condemned for healing a child without license*—and pointed us down the road she was sworn to seal. Treason sat on her like armor now. She wore it straighter than she'd ever worn her badge.

The tunnel narrowed, widened, narrowed again, and always descended. Frost gave way to a strange dry warmth, and the warmth gave way to something older than either—the deep, patient heat of the mountain's heart, like standing inside a held breath. The singing grew louder. Or perhaps we simply grew closer to it, the way you grow closer to grief.

Then the tunnel opened, and I understood why they called it a court.

* * *

It was a cavern the size of Greyhollow's cathedral square, but where the square had sky, this had mirrors.

They covered everything—walls, ceiling, floor, the great ribbed pillars that rose like the ribs of some swallowed leviathan—and they were not honest mirrors. They showed the court as it might have been: chandeliers of unlit crystal, a dais of black glass, galleries where elegant figures should have been seated. But the galleries were empty, and the figures existed only in reflection, and the reflections did not quite match anything real. When I moved, a hundred versions of me moved a half-second late, wearing expressions I hadn't made yet. One of them was smiling. I made very sure not to find out why.

And between the pillars, rising in tiers like orchard rows, stood the shelves.

Glass vessels. Thousands of them. Small as thimbles, large as lanterns, each one holding a curl of light that moved the way smoke moves when someone is breathing inside it. And each one was murmuring. The singing tunnels had been these—the whole mountain was resonating with the sound of the stored dead, the way a shell holds the sea.

"There," Serane said. Her voice came out stripped of everything but need.

She was already moving, up the tiered aisles between shelves, reading labels written in a script that hurt to look at directly. I lost sight of her. Then I heard her make a sound I will never be able to describe—not a word, not a sob, something between them, the sound a person makes when a five-year wound is suddenly, terribly confirmed. When I found her again she was standing before a vessel no bigger than an egg, cupping it in both hands like water she was afraid to spill. Inside it, a curl of light pressed itself against the glass toward her palms.

"She knows me," Serane whispered. "Vesper. She knows me."

The light pulsed, slow and steady, and though no sound escaped the vessel, I swear I felt it against my skin—a rhythm, a cadence, the ghost-shape of a voice saying a name over and over. Lira. Lira. Lira.

"We're getting her out," I said. "All of them."

"Boldly said."

The voice came from everywhere—from the mirrors, from the shelves, from the mountain's held breath—and it was the most beautiful voice I had ever heard. That was the first thing wrong with it. It sounded like every singer I'd ever loved folded into one throat, like Wren's low register and my mother's laugh and the cathedral choir of my childhood all speaking in perfect unison through a single mouth.

He stepped out from behind the dais as if he had always been part of it. He was unremarkable in the way that well-made things are unremarkable: a tall figure in grey, a face you would forget on purpose, hands folded like a man waiting politely to be introduced. Only his shadow was wrong. It fell toward him instead of away, gathering at his feet like spilled ink returning to the bottle.

"The last swordspeaker," the Soul-Broker said, "comes down my mountain carrying a dead man's map and a hunter's treason. I could almost feel flattered."

Bramble's ghost flared beside me, bright with hate. *Him,* he snarled. *That's the one who—*

"I know who he is," I said quietly.

"You do." The Broker tilted his head, studying me the way a jeweler studies a stone for flaws worth keeping. "You knew who I was five years ago, too. That's why you ran. Not from the Magistrate's bells—from me. You understood that oaths are currency, and I am the only mint." He smiled. It was a good smile. Every offer he made tonight would be real, and I want that recorded plainly, because it matters: nothing he offered was a lie. That was the horror of him.

"You have my map," I said.

"I have had your map for twenty years. Bramble stole a copy. Copies return to their originals, given time." He extended one hand, and somewhere in the folds of his coat the bloodstained parchment appeared, smooth and clean, the blood gone as if it had never soaked in. "You came all this way for a voice. A small thing. A street musician's warble, traded honestly—signed, witnessed, paid. Her brother walks free because of it. Debts are debts, swordspeaker. Even you cannot argue with arithmetic."

Wren stepped forward before I could stop her. She stood in front of the Broker with her chin up and her chalkboard hanging forgotten at her side, and she looked at him the way you look at a debt collector who has come to your door—without fear, because fear is a luxury the indebted learn to live without. She pointed at her own throat. Then she pointed at the shelves. Then she drew one finger across the other, sharp: *give it back.*

"Ah," the Broker said softly. "There she is." And for one moment the beautiful voice faltered, and underneath it I heard something old and hungry and almost wistful. "Do you know what you are, girl? You are the only soul in this mountain that was *paid for gladly*. Do you know how rare that is? Most of them curse me. They curse me from inside the glass, century after century. But you—" he leaned toward her, gentle as a confessor, "—you signed with love. Your voice has been sitting on my shelf for a season, singing your brother's name. I have grown fond of it."

Wren went white. But she didn't step back.

"That's enough," I said, and my oath-mark burned, and the word came out in oath-tongue whether I meant it to or not—came out with weight behind it, the kind of weight that makes mirrors crack and candles gutter. The nearest vessels shivered in their niches. The murmuring rose, just slightly, like a crowd hearing its name.

The Broker looked at me with something like delight.

"There it is," he breathed. "Twenty years since anyone spoke true words in my court. Do you know what I could give you, Vesper Pemberton?" He began to walk, slow, along the shelf line, and the lights in the vessels leaned toward him as he passed, the way flowers lean toward sun. "Not promises. *Realities.* I can restore your gift to its full strength—not the starved trickle you've been rationing for five years, but the whole river. I can lift the Magistrate's warrant; his hunters would forget your face by morning. I can put wards around your little shop that no iron bell will ever ring against." He paused beside a vessel near the top tier. Inside it, a curl of light shaped itself, unmistakably, into the form of a small bird. "I can give the courier his life back. Bramble died owing me; I forgive debts. Ask him."

*I don't want it from him,* Bramble snarled, but his light flickered, and we both heard the wanting underneath.

"And Wren's voice," the Broker continued, turning, spreading his hands as if presenting a gift already wrapped. "Yours. Tonight. No duel, no risk, no cost to her—she sings tomorrow in your square, and neither of you ever thinks of this mountain again. All I ask in return is the map"—he weighed the parchment in his palm—"and your silence. Your true silence, swordspeaker. Sworn, sealed, permanent. No more oaths. Ever. You keep your magic, your safety, your love, your life. You simply never speak a binding word again."

The silence after that was enormous. Somewhere in the tiers, thousands of souls murmured on, patient as weather.

And here is what I will not pretend: I wanted it. Every piece of it. I wanted the river of my gift back the way a drowned man wants air. I wanted Wren's voice in my ears more than I have wanted anything, and the Broker knew it—he had built his whole court, his whole *economy*, on knowing it. His offers were real. That was the trap. Lies you can refuse. Truth, offered kindly, at the exact moment of your greatest hunger—that is how souls are actually bought.

I looked at Wren. She was watching me, and her eyes were wet, and she was shaking her head—slowly, firmly, *no*—because she had heard the offer too, and she knew exactly what it would cost, and it wasn't the map.

It was everyone else. Every vessel. Lira Ovid, pressing her light against the glass toward her sister's hands. The lullaby-singer from Saltmarsh. The banned drinking song. Five years of the Magistrate's executions, shelved and labeled and murmuring. My silence would buy two people out of a prison holding thousands.

"No," I said.

And then, because some words must be said in the old tongue or not at all, I let the oath-tongue rise in my throat, felt it fill my mouth like cold fire, and spoke loud enough for every mirror in the court to hear:

*"No soul is property. This is the first law and the last law, and I am its speaker."*

Every vessel in the mountain flared at once.

The Broker's smile faded for the first time, and his shadow surged up around him like floodwater, and he said, in a voice that was suddenly not beautiful at all:

"Then we duel."
