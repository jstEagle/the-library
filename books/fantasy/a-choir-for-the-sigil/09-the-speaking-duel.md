## 9. The Speaking-Duel

The court beneath the mountain was a hall of mirrors, and every mirror held a voice.

They lined the walls in their thousands—glass vessels no larger than lanterns, each one glowing with a faint interior light, each one murmuring. Not words. Something older than words. The sound of a soul remembering itself. Serane stood at Vesper's left with her hand pressed flat against a vessel near the door, her lips moving around a name she had not spoken aloud in nine years. Wren stood at Vesper's right, chalkboard clutched to her chest like a shield, and in the mirror-glass Vesper could see the reflection of her mouth opening and closing on sounds that had been sold out from behind her teeth.

And at the center of it all, upon a throne carved from a single vein of black quartz, sat the Soul-Broker.

He was not what Vesper had expected. He was not monstrous. He was *comfortable*—a broad, silver-haired man in a merchant's coat, with the patient smile of someone who had never once lost a negotiation because he had never once needed to raise his voice. The mirrors bent toward him the way wheat bends toward the sun.

"Vesper Pemberton," he said, and his voice was warm as a hearth, and it came from everywhere at once, from every vessel at once, so that the stolen dead seemed to speak with him. "The last swordspeaker. Do you know how long I have waited for you to walk down my mountain? I could have sent for you. I chose not to. Some things must arrive on their own feet, or they arrive broken." He gestured, gracious, to the map in Vesper's hands—to the bloodstained parchment that had led them through the singing tunnels, through the frostline, through five years of hiding undone in five days. "You have brought me back my property. Bramble was careless. Wren's voice was *paid for*. And you—" the smile widened, and it was still kind, and that was the worst thing in all the mirrored dark, "—you have brought me the one soul in Greyhollow I never managed to buy. I can restore everything, swordspeaker. Your name. Your safety. Her voice, unbroken, tomorrow morning, as if midsummer had never happened. All I ask is the map, and your silence. Forever. It is, I think, the one oath you have already sworn."

The vessels murmured. Somewhere among them, Serane's sister was listening.

Vesper had rehearsed this moment on the road, in the dark, in the space between Bramble's ghost's accusations and Wren's sleeping breath. And now, standing in it, they found the rehearsed words were gone—burned away like frost in sunlight—and what remained was the oldest thing they knew, the first oath ever spoken to them, at seven years old, by a teacher whose face they had long since forgotten:

*No soul is property. Not bought, not sold, not taken, not traded. Not by king, not by court, not by broker, not by god.*

"I do not bargain," Vesper said.

The warmth in the hall did not change, but the mirrors did. Every vessel flickered, just once, like a field of candles in a draft.

"Then you have come to die in a hole in the earth," the Broker said pleasantly.

"No." Vesper stepped forward, and felt the map grow heavy in their hand, felt the old oath-tongue rise in their throat like water rising in a well that had been sealed for five years. "I have come to *speak*."

They let the map fall. It landed on the mirror-floor with a sound like a struck bell, and every vessel in the hall rang with it.

"I am Vesper Pemberton, last of the swordspeakers, and I invoke the challenge-oath—*by word against word, by truth against trade, by the law that was before law*—against the keeper of this court. Answer me, Broker. Or be answered."

The word *answered* left their mouth and did not echo. It *landed*. The whole mountain felt it—a shudder through the quartz, a groan in the roots of the stone, as if something enormous that had been sleeping under the floor had opened one eye.

The Broker sighed, and stood, and for the first time his coat did not look comfortable; it looked like armor. "So be it," he said. "The old terms. Word against word. The court will judge what is true." He spread his hands, and the mirrors leaned in. "You should know, swordspeaker, what the old terms cost. Every word you spend, I may take from you. Every truth you claim, I may claim in return. You have five years of hiding in you. I will take every one of them. You have a woman beside you whose face you have memorized in the dark. I will take *her*."

"I know the terms," Vesper said. "I spoke them first."

"Then let the court begin."

* * *

The first exchange was simple, and it was a lie, and it nearly ended everything.

"The swordspeaker Vesper Pemberton," the Broker said, in his hearth-warm voice, "is a seller of charms. A maker of luck-knots. A coward who watched a woman lose her voice and did nothing for a hundred days."

And the words *were* true—that was the horror of the duel, that the Broker did not lie, he *weighed*, and his scale was vast—and Vesper felt the truth of it settle into their chest like a stone dropped into a well. The market stall. The fever-salves. The iron bells passing in the street while Vesper looked down and counted coins and said nothing. The square where Wren sang, and Vesper walking the long way around because hearing it was a wound and they were tired of bleeding.

"I am those things," Vesper said, and the words cost them—the memory of the stall went dim as they said it, the smell of the salves fading like a room they'd left years ago. "And I am also the oath that made me. *No soul is property.* That law I have kept, even hiding. Especially hiding."

The mirrors flickered toward them. The stone under their feet hummed.

The Broker moved on, faster now, and the duel became a stripping.

"You hid your gift for five years."

"I did, and I am here."

"You let Bramble die at your door."

"I did, and I carry his name, and I will spend it." Vesper did not know how they were still standing. Each answer took something. The memory of the shop—the smell of ink and dried lavender—gone. The memory of the night they'd first decided to run from the order, the rain on the road, the taste of fear—gone, hollowed out like a tooth. They were becoming a shape made only of the duel, and they could feel the Broker smiling at that, feel him *waiting*, because a swordspeaker with no past had no ground to stand on, and a swordspeaker with no ground would fall.

"You love her," the Broker said, and here he slowed, and the whole court leaned closer, and the vessels murmured like a tide. "Vesper Pemberton loves Wren Ash. And loved her in silence. And watched her sign her voice away to save a brother, and said *nothing*, because silence was safe. Tell the court, swordspeaker—what is love that will not speak?"

And Vesper opened their mouth to answer, and found nothing there.

Not because the Broker had taken it. Because it was true. Five years of loving in silence, of buying bread from her stall and never saying it, of listening to her music from the edge of the square like a beggar at a feast—and the Broker held that truth up to the mirrors and let it shine, and it was the ugliest thing Vesper had ever seen, and it was *theirs*.

"I—" Vesper began.

And the memory began to go. They felt it go—the square, the chalkboard, the way she bit her lip when she was concentrating on a chord—sliding out of them like water through fingers, and the Broker's smile was kind, kind, kind, *I am only taking what you never used*, and Vesper understood with terrible clarity that they would win every exchange and lose everything anyway, that the duel was built for exactly this, that the last swordspeaker would be defeated not by a stronger word but by their own five years of silence—

Wren sang.

Not a word. She had no words. She opened her mouth and gave the court the only thing she had left, the thing no one had thought to take because no one had thought it was worth anything—the hum, the songless sound she made when she thought no one was listening, the melody she had been carrying in her body all this time like a coal carried in cupped hands.

It was the song from the square. The one Vesper had walked away from a hundred times.

It had no owner. That was the thing. It had never been sold, never signed, never entered into any ledger beneath any mountain. It lived in her chest because she had never stopped singing it, not even mute, not even ashamed, humming it into her own silence for a hundred days because a song is not a sound—a song is a *giving*, and what is given freely cannot be bought, and what cannot be bought cannot be owned, and what cannot be owned—

The nearest vessel cracked.

A hairline fracture, a thread of light—and then a voice came out of it, a woman's voice, whole and astonished, saying its own name.

The Broker turned. For the first time, the Broker *moved quickly*, and it was terrible to see. "That is not—" he began, in his hearth-warm voice, reaching for the law, for the ledger, for the signature in his parlor, "that soul was *purchased*, there is a *document*, there is a *price*—"

"No," Vesper said.

And the word came out of them carrying everything the duel had not managed to strip away, everything underneath the hiding and the fear and the five hollow years—the first oath, the old law, the truth that had been true before the first broker drew up the first contract: *No soul is property.* Not bought. Not sold. Not taken. Not traded. **Not by you.**

The map on the floor ignited with white light. Every vessel in the court rang like a cathedral of bells.

Then they broke.

All of them. A thousand mirrors failing at once, and out of each one a voice—Serane's sister, saying her sister's name across nine frozen years; the executed of Greyhollow, the debtors and hedge-witches and smugglers and children, five years of the mountain's stolen choir, rising together in a sound that was not a sound, that was the *law itself* being sung, and the black quartz throne split from crown to root, and the Broker's coat of armor fell open and there was nothing inside it at all, there had never been anything inside it, only the trade, only the ledger, only the warm patient smile—and the smile was the last thing to go, unraveling like smoke in a wind that came from everywhere, from every freed mouth at once.

The mountain exhaled.

Vesper felt it pass through them—five years of voices, upward, through stone and frost and iron bells, toward a city that had forgotten what its own dead sounded like—and felt, in the same breath, the last of their own magic go with it. The oath-tongue drained out of them like color draining from a sky. They did not fall. They were held. Wren's arms were around them before they knew their knees had gone, and against their ear, impossibly, unmistakably, in a voice that was rough and rusted and *hers*—

"You came for me," Wren said. "You idiot. You beautiful idiot. You came for me."

The dust settled. The mirrors were only mirrors now, dark and ordinary, and the court beneath the mountain was silent for the first time in a century.

And then, far above them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, Vesper heard something that turned their blood to ice water.

Bells. Iron bells. Not one—*hundreds*. The whole hunt of Greyhollow, descending.
