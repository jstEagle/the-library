## 10. A Choir for the Sigil

The first thing Wren knew was warmth — a hand closed around hers, callused and still, and the smell of woodsmoke and fever-salve and Vesper.

She opened her eyes to the low ceiling of a hedge-witch's cottage, frost ferning the window glass, and Vesper asleep in a chair dragged up beside her cot, their head tipped back, mouth open, breathing like someone who had run a very long way and finally been allowed to stop. Their hands were folded in their lap like a person at a funeral. Like a person at their own funeral, maybe. Around each finger, where the oath-marks had burned silver for as long as she'd known them, the skin was plain and ordinary and human.

Wren tried to say *Vesper* and her voice came out cracked, rusted, half-swallowed by five months of silence — but it came out. It came out, and it was hers, and it was ugly and hoarse and the most beautiful thing she had ever heard.

Vesper woke the way they did everything: all at once, hand already moving to a knife that wasn't there anymore, because there were no knives left in them, no oaths, no sigils, nothing but a body and a name. Their eyes found hers. For one terrible heartbeat they looked at her mouth as if afraid of what might come out of it — or of what wouldn't.

"Say something," Vesper whispered. "Please. I need to know I didn't—"

"Hello," Wren said. Her voice broke on the second syllable and she laughed, which hurt, which made her laugh more. "Hello, you absolute fool. Hello."

Vesper made a sound then that Wren had never heard from them in five years of knowing them — not a word, not an oath, just a raw, unguarded noise, and then they were out of the chair and on the edge of the cot with their arms around her, careful of her ribs, careless of everything else, shaking. She held them back. Above them both, on the shelf, one of Bramble's old courier-tokens rattled once against the wood — the ghost's version of clearing his throat — and settled, satisfied, into silence.

"He stayed long enough to see it," Vesper murmured into her hair. "The vessels breaking. He said—" A wet laugh. "He said he'd have done it himself if he'd had lungs."

"What did it cost you?" Wren asked, because she had to, because she could now.

Vesper pulled back and looked at their own bare hands, turning them over like a stranger's. "Everything," they said simply. "And none of it that mattered." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and clear. "I remember you. That was the price they kept trying to take, and I still remember you. Every market morning. Every chalkboard message. The way you hum when you think no one's listening." Their voice frayed. "I'm nobody now, Wren. No order, no gift, no title. Just—"

"Just mine," Wren said, and kissed them, and outside the frost on the window began, very slowly, to melt.

* * *

Greyhollow learned what had happened beneath the mountain the way it learned everything: badly, loudly, and all at once.

It began with the voices. Five years of sold souls had come home when the vessels shattered, and the Magistrate's executions — those tidy public affairs, so final, so lawful — had begun returning their receipts. The condemned woke screaming in their graves. Families heard dead daughters singing in chimneys. And every freed soul carried the memory of the parlor beneath the mountain, the mirrored court, the shelves, the Broker's courteous voice, and — worst of all — the ledger. The ledger with the Magistrate's seal on every page.

Wren watched it fall from the steps of the Chalkmarket fountain on the third day after, pressed close to Vesper among a crowd gone strange and quiet. The Magistrate came out onto his balcony to address the unrest, and before he could speak, the crowd spoke first — hundreds of voices at once, the living and the returned, reciting his ledger line by line, entry by entry, in perfect unison. He had bought the city's prosperity with its people, and now the city read its own bill aloud.

He did not hang. Greyhollow was too tired for spectacle. Instead the crowd simply walked toward the palace, unhurried, unstoppable, a tide of ordinary faces, and the guards — who had sisters and brothers on those ledgers too — stepped aside. The Magistrate fled through a postern gate in a baker's coat and was never seen again, though for years afterward travelers swore they'd met a nervous man in the border towns who flinched at the sound of choirs.

Serane Ovid stood on the palace steps when the crowd arrived, in her iron-belled coat, and everyone who saw her thought: *now it begins, the hunt will turn on us all.* Instead she unbuckled the bells, one by one, and let them fall ringing down the marble steps.

"The hunt is ended," she said, in a voice built for courtyards and carrying. "What replaces it is this: no soul is property. Not by law, not by debt, not by bargain. Whoever says otherwise answers to me." She looked out over the crowd and found Wren's face, and something passed between them — the ghost of a frozen pass, a sister's name, a debt paid in treason. "I am told," Serane went on, drier now, "that there are those among you with gifts. I am told some of you have been hiding in my city for years, selling luck-knots under my hunters' noses." A ripple of uneasy laughter. "Good. Keep doing that. Consider it licensed."

Later, in the wrecked archive of the hunt's headquarters, Wren helped Serane sort the ledgers — matching names to fates, fates to families, the slow grim work of making a whole city's grief legible. Serane found her sister's entry on the fourth day. She read it standing, without a sound, and then she folded the page into her coat over her heart and said only, "She always wanted to see the mountain." And went back to work.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and honest — real cold, real snow, none of the Broker's purchased mildness. On the first true morning of it, Wren stood in the Chalkmarket square with chalk dust on her fingers and terror in her chest, looking at the crowd.

She had written the invitation herself, on every wall in Greyhollow, in her looping hand: **SINGERS. HUMMERS. WHISTLERS. ANYONE WITH A VOICE THEY THOUGHT WAS GONE. THE SQUARE. FIRST FROST. BRING NOTHING.**

They came.

Hedge-witches from the frostline enclave, wrapped in gray wool, their charm-bags jingling openly for the first time in a generation. Hunters in coats stripped of bells, awkward as reformed wolves. Freed souls who hadn't aged a day since their deaths, blinking at a city that had grown without them. Market women. Dockhands. Children. Old Tam from the fever-ward, whose voice had been sold to pay for his wife's medicine and who wept openly just to hear himself cough. And at the edge of it all, hood up out of habit before Wren gently pushed it back, Vesper — magic-less, mark-less, ordinary — holding a stick of chalk and a sheet of paper covered in Wren's notation.

"I can't sing," Vesper said, for perhaps the hundredth time.

"You don't need to," Wren said. "You never needed to speak to be heard. That was always your whole problem, love."

She stepped onto the fountain's rim. The crowd hushed. Somewhere above, clouds the color of pewter moved across a pale sun, and the snow came down fine and dry as flour.

Wren opened her mouth and sang.

Not a song anyone knew — a song she made in that moment, out of five months of silence and one night in a mirrored court, out of a brother saved and a voice lost and found, out of the wordless sound that had broken a Broker's claim upon the world. Her voice rose thin and clear over the square, and it was imperfect, and it wavered, and it was *hers*, unowned, unsold, worth exactly nothing on any ledger ever drawn.

And the choir answered.

It was ragged at first — the witches humming low, the hunters uncertain, the children shrill and delighted — but Vesper stood beside the fountain with the paper they couldn't read and didn't need to, lifting their empty hands, shaping the air, drawing the parts together the way they had once drawn oaths out of silence. They conducted with no magic at all, only attention, only care, only the particular gravity of a person who has given everything away and therefore has nothing left to lose. The harmonies locked. The square filled. The sound climbed past the rooftops and out over the drowned undercity and up the mountain road, into the singing tunnels where the walls remembered, and if the mountain itself gave one low note back — well. No one proved it. Everyone believed it.

Bramble's token, sewn now into the hem of Wren's coat, grew warm against her ankle, and she would have sworn she heard him keeping time.

When the last chord faded, the snow kept falling into the quiet, and nobody left. They stood in the white falling silence, hundreds of them, breathing together, and Wren looked at Vesper — flushed, laughing, crying a little, thoroughly, gloriously ordinary — and understood at last what they had both been circling for five years:

Belonging was never granted. It was built. Note by note, hand by hand, winter by winter, by people stubborn enough to stay.

Vesper leaned close. "So," they said. "First of something new. What do we call it?"

Wren smiled, took the chalk from their hand, and wrote it on the fountain's rim where the whole city could see, in letters that would be traced and retraced for a hundred years:

**A CHOIR FOR THE SIGIL.**

And far beneath the mountain, in the dark where the court had been, something ancient stirred in the emptied tunnels — listening, learning, and very slowly beginning, for the first time in an age, to sing along.
