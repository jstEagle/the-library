## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Grief

The soldier was dying in the shade of the tannery wall, and the whole market town of Vaelmoor pretended not to notice.

Theo Joubert noticed. Noticing was the only trade he had.

He had watched the man for three days now — a lean figure in a faded campaign coat the color of old rust, sitting where the tannery's shadow pooled longest, coughing into a rag that came away spotted red. The soldiers who passed through Vaelmoor on the king's road never lingered unless they were too broken to ride on, and this one had been broken thoroughly and quietly, the way a cart axle breaks: no drama, just a slow giving-out that everyone could see and no one would speak of. He had a name stitched inside his collar — Theo had checked while the man slept, because names mattered when you were selling what he sold — Corporal Edmun Rask, Third Lances, mustered out with a wound in his side that had gone bad the way wounds did in a valley where no healer's craft would take root.

Magic could not cross the border stones. Everyone in Vaelmoor knew it the way they knew the taste of their own well water. It was the valley's great boast and its great comfort: whatever wars burned beyond the ridgeline, whatever gifts and curses the wide world traded in, none of it crossed the stones. A soldier wounded by an ensorceled blade could not be healed here, but neither could he be cursed further. The valley held nothing and gave nothing, and its people wore that emptiness like a coat against the cold.

Except Theo. Theo carried something across those stones every day of his life, folded up small behind his eyes, and no border stone had ever once objected.

He crouched at the edge of the tannery yard now, ostensibly sorting through a basket of secondhand buckles he'd bought that morning for two coppers and would sell for five if Maren's bread ran short again. The buckles were real work, honest-looking work, the kind of work that made a man invisible. Invisibility was not a gift Theo possessed; it was a discipline, practiced daily like scales on a fiddle. You dressed in the color of dust. You walked at the pace of a man going nowhere important. You learned every face in town and let your own face learn nothing back. And above all — above everything, this was the first rule and the last rule — you never let them see you *take*.

Because taking was what he did. Not copying. Not borrowing, though he told himself borrowed, sometimes, in the gentler hours. When Theo laid his hand on a dying man's brow and drew the last bright coil of his life up out of him, the memory did not remain behind like a copied page. It left. Whole. The man died lighter than he'd lived, which was the mercy Theo offered and the theft in one motion, and there was no putting it back. He had learned that the hard way, years ago, in a way he did not let himself remember — which was easy enough, since forgetting was the one trick his gift performed on himself for free.

Corporal Rask saw him and tried to sit straighter, and failed, and laughed instead — a wet, rattling sound.

"You're the buckle boy," the soldier said. "Been watching you pretend to count those things for an hour."

"I'm very thorough," Theo said. "Buckles are deceptive."

"Sit down, buckle boy. My legs have opinions about standing and I'd as soon not hear them alone." He patted the ground beside him, and Theo sat, setting the basket between them like a small honest wall. Up close the soldier smelled of iron and vinegar and the particular sweetness underneath, the smell Theo had learned to know the way farmers knew rain coming. Three days, maybe four. Less, if the night turned cold.

"You're from over the ridge," Theo said. "From the war."

"Everyone's from the war. War's the whole country now." Rask looked out at the market square, at the women haggling over late apples, at children chasing a dog with a stolen sausage, at the ordinary golden afternoon light lying over everything like something worth keeping. "This place doesn't look real. You know that? Like a painting of before."

"It's real," Theo said. "It's just empty. Nothing magical ever gets in here. That's the deal the valley made, long ago. We keep nothing, we lose nothing."

Rask grunted. "My grandmother used to say the same about her cellar. Nothing in it, nothing taken from it." He coughed, long and deep, and pressed the rag to his mouth, and when he took it away he looked at the red on it with the mild surprise of a man reading someone else's letter. "I've got something I can't carry, buckle boy. Been trying to set it down for forty miles and it won't drop. You look like a man who carries things for people."

Theo went very still, the way he always went still when the work came looking for him instead of the other way around. There was a version of this conversation he'd rehearsed a hundred times, the one where he denied everything, where the gift stayed a rumor even to itself. But the man was dying, and dying men paid in truth whether you wanted it or not.

"What kind of things?" Theo asked.

"The kind you don't want." Rask turned his head, and his eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear. "I've seen what's coming over that ridge. I've stood in a place called Harrow Fen and watched men burn green fire, and I've done things in that fen I can't say out loud because saying them makes them mine forever. And I thought — if I could get somewhere quiet, somewhere clean, maybe I could put it down before the end. Then I heard talk in the last town. Talk about a young fellow hereabouts. Quiet fellow. Sells odd lots. People come away from him lighter." His mouth twisted. "Superstition, probably. But superstition's all I've got left, so I'll spend it. Can you do it? Take it out of me?"

Every rule Theo had said walk away. Rule one: never let them see you take. Rule two: never take from someone who asks, because asked-for memories sold strange, sold heavy, sold with the giver's consent clinging to them like burrs, and brokers docked you for it. Rule three, the one under all the others: stay small, stay dull, stay nothing, until the thirteenth moon turns and turns and turns and nobody remembers there was ever anything in Vaelmoor worth hunting.

But the man was dying, and he was asking, and his hand had come up off the ground and was hovering near Theo's wrist like a bird afraid to land.

"How much?" Theo said quietly.

Rask blinked. "How much what?"

"For the memory. I don't do charity. Charity gets people noticed." This was true, and also it was armor, and also — though he would never have admitted it, not under torture, not at knifepoint — it was a kindness. Men paid less shamefully for a thing they'd bought than for a thing they'd begged.

"I've got eleven coppers and a ring my mother gave me."

"Keep the ring. Give it to whoever buries you. Eleven coppers is fine." Theo glanced around the yard — the tannery hands at their vats across the way, backs turned; the market noise washing over the wall like surf; the long shadow holding them both like cupped water. No one watching. No one ever watched the buckle boy. "It will feel like cold water. Don't fight it. Fighting it tears things."

"What things?"

"You don't want to know. Close your eyes."

Rask closed his eyes. He was braver than most, Theo noted; most men prayed, or babbled, or gripped his sleeve hard enough to bruise. Rask simply breathed, slow and deliberate, a soldier following orders he didn't understand from an officer he trusted more than he should have.

Theo laid two fingers on the man's brow.

Here is how it worked, though Theo had never explained it to anyone and could not have explained it now: the world behind a person's eyes was a house, and grief was the thing in it that glowed. Fear glowed white and jagged. Love glowed warm and low, banked like a stove, and he never touched love — love was load-bearing; pull it out and the whole house came down on the living. But grief, guilt, secrets — those sat loose in the rooms of a person, heavy furniture the owner would give almost anything to be rid of, and Theo had learned to lift them the way a careful thief lifts a candle from a table without waking the sleeper: one smooth motion, weight transferred, nothing spilled.

He found the fen immediately. Of course he did; it was the thing Rask had carried forty miles to set down. It came up through his fingers like cold water, like drowning in reverse, and Theo held it — held *him*, held the corporal entire, a gray dawn and black water and men screaming in a language of green flame, held the moment Rask had cut down a boy no older than sixteen who had been reaching not for a weapon but for a locket, held the locket, held the years of marching after, held the whole terrible weight of it, all of it, everything —

— and it left Rask, and entered Theo, and became his.

That was the part no one understood. That was the part Theo kept folded up small behind his own eyes next to all the other folded things. The brokers believed he dealt in copies, impressions, echoes — that the original stayed with its owner and Theo peddled duplicates to collectors who liked to wear other lives like coats. Even Maren, sharp-eyed Maren, believed the ledger entries meant *impressions, standard grade*. If she knew the truth — that every memory Theo sold was gone from its owner forever, that he was not a copyist but a thief of the only kind that mattered, a thief of the past itself — then he would not be a curiosity to be patronized. He would be a horror to be hunted. And the crown's witch-hunters, when they came sniffing along the king's road, would not need charms or trials to find him. They would need only arithmetic: a man who sells what others have lost.

So he stole, and he lied about stealing, and he built a life on other people's losses the way you build a fire on other people's wood, and some nights the warmth of it felt like survival and some nights it felt like the worst sin there was, and he had stopped trying to tell those nights apart.

Rask opened his eyes. He looked — lighter. That was always the worst part, the part Theo couldn't watch straight-on. The soldier's face had smoothed like a bed being made. He breathed easier. He smiled at the afternoon.

"That's better," Rask said wonderingly. "Gods. That's — I can't find it. I know I ought to be able to find it and it's just — gone." He laughed, a clean laugh this time, and Theo felt the locket-memory shift inside his own skull, settling into its new room, learning its new walls. Sixteen years old, reaching for a locket. Theo would carry that now. He'd carry it until he sold it, and then someone else would carry it, and Rask would die tomorrow or the day after remembering nothing but a wound and a kindness.

"Was there—" Rask frowned faintly. "There was something. Someone. Wasn't there?" The frown passed like weather. "Doesn't matter. Here." He pressed eleven coppers into Theo's palm, warm from his pocket, warm from a dying man's hope. "You're a good lad, buckle boy. Whatever it is you actually do."

"Don't tell anyone what you think I do."

"Tell them what?" Rask said, and smiled, empty and easy, and Theo felt the theft close over like water.

* * *

Maren's shop sat at the crook of the Spire Road where it bent toward the market square, wedged between a chandler and a shuttered apothecary, and it looked from outside like nothing at all: dusty window, hand-lettered sign reading CURIO & APPRAISAL, a door that stuck in damp weather. Inside it was the richest room in Vaelmoor, if you knew what richness was. Shelves of labeled jars. Drawers of velvet-lined slots. And behind the counter, on a chain of office she wore like a joke and meant like a crown, a broker's lens — a disc of smoked glass through which Maren could read the provenance of any memory set before her, could tell a first-hand grief from a secondhand rumor, could price a man's worst hour to the copper.

She was pricing walnuts when Theo came in. Actually walnuts. She kept a bowl of them on the counter and cracked them with her thumb, which was the most openly magical thing anyone did in the valley, and she did it precisely because it wasn't magic at all, just a strong thumb and thirty years of practice. Maren believed in practice the way priests believed in gods.

"Buckles," she said, without looking up.

"And other stock."

"Mm." She cracked a walnut, picked out the meat, ate it. Only then did she raise her eyes, gray and unhurried, the eyes of a woman who had appraised ten thousand sorrows and been moved by perhaps nine. "Show me."

Theo set the memory on the counter the way he always did — not physically, there was nothing physical to set down, but in the manner of the trade, a slight forward lean, a loosening behind the eyes, the sensation of opening a door in his own forehead. Maren lifted the broker's lens to her eye and looked *through* him, and for a moment Theo stood in the shop feeling like a window with someone peering out of it.

"Harrow Fen," Maren said at last. She lowered the lens. Her voice had changed — not softer, exactly. More careful. "Green fire. That's crown-work, that's the eastern front, that's—" She stopped herself, which was unlike her, and cracked another walnut she didn't eat. "First-hand. Clean transfer. Recent." Her eyes came up sharp. "Whose?"

"A dying man's. With consent. He asked."

"With consent." Maren repeated it flatly, and Theo knew what the flatness cost her. Consent was rare. Consent was the difference between a broker's stock and a butcher's. "And he's dead?"

"Dying. Day or two."

"So the original owner passes with no claim outstanding. Good. That keeps the assizes off us." She was already writing in the ledger, the big black book that lived chained under the counter, the book whose existence Theo had spent three years pretending not to be curious about. "Harrow Fen material is moving well right now. Certain buyers over the ridge pay premiums for front-line impressions. Grief, guilt, secrets — standard categories. This has all three." She wrote a number, considered it, added a cipher, and slid the ledger aside. "Fourteen silver. And silence, as always."

"Fifteen. He had a ring he wanted buried with him. I'm paying for the burying."

Maren looked at him for a long moment. Theo held still under it. There were perhaps four people in Vaelmoor who suspected what he was, and Maren was the only one who suspected correctly, and neither of them had ever once said so aloud. Their whole relationship was a building with no doors: you could live in it, but you couldn't leave it or enter it, you could only already be inside.

"Fifteen," she agreed, counting coins onto the wood. "And Theo." She never used his name. The sound of it in her mouth was like a key turning somewhere he couldn't see. "Word from the ridge road. Riders in crown colors, asking after gifted persons. Asking politely, which is worse. If they reach Vaelmoor before the thirteenth moon turns—"

"They won't find anything," Theo said. "There's nothing here. Nothing crosses the stones. That's the whole point of the valley."

"Yes," Maren said. "That's what we tell ourselves." She pushed the coins toward him and went back to her walnuts, and Theo took his bread and his silence and his fifteen silver and stepped out into the ordinary golden afternoon, carrying a dead battle and a boy's last reach for a locket and the growing, unquiet knowledge that the ice he'd skated on all his life had begun, somewhere far out beneath his feet, to crack.

Behind him, in the shop, Maren opened her ledger to a page near the back — a page of buyers, of names across the border, of houses old enough to remember the war — and ran one finger down the column until it stopped.

On a name written in ink gone brown with age:

*Ashfall.*
