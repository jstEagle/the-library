## 2. Thirteen Bells

The proclamation came on a morning so cold the river wore a skin of ice, and it came without hands.

Theo heard it before he saw it—the wet, meaty sound of a hammer striking wood, though no hammer swung and no carpenter stood in the square. He was crossing the market with a sack of turnips he had not paid for and did not intend to, and the sound stopped him mid-stride the way a snapped branch stops a deer. Around him, others stopped too. A woman with a basket of eggs went still so completely that the eggs seemed to hold their breath inside her.

At the chapel door, where notices of sheep sales and lost dogs were usually pinned by the sexton's own trembling thumb, a sheet of parchment nailed itself to the oak. Three blows, three echoes, and then silence—except for the paper, which shivered as if it were alive and cold.

Theo knew that magic when he felt it. Not because he could name it, but because his gift woke in his chest like a dog lifting its head at a stranger's step. The parchment *carried* something. Grief, old and official, pressed into the fibers like wax into a seal. Whoever had written this proclamation had wept over it, or been made to weep, or had poured someone else's tears into the ink. The crown did nothing halfway, not even its cruelty.

He should have walked on. Invisibility was a discipline, and discipline meant never being the first to look at anything strange. But every soul in the square was already looking, and to be the only one not looking would be stranger still. So Theo Joubert turned with the crowd like a leaf turning with the current, and read the words that would unmake the careful architecture of his life.

BY ORDER OF THE CROWN, IN THE NAME OF THE KING'S PEACE AND THE SAFETY OF THE REALM:

Let it be known throughout Vaelmoor and all its valleys, from the border stones to the salt marshes, that persons gifted with unlawful arts are discovered among honest folk. Let it be known that these gifts are not blessings but wounds, through which the old war bleeds into the present age.

Therefore: all such persons must be surrendered to the King's witch-hunters before the thirteenth moon completes her turning. Any who harbor them share their guilt. Any who conceal them will be sought as they are sought. Any who reveal them will be rewarded as is right.

The moon wanes. So does the King's patience.

Below the words, in smaller script, was a list. Not names—the crown did not yet have names—but *signs*. Signs by which the gifted might be known: sleeplessness at the full moon; dreams that come true; knowledge of things untold; the ability to soothe or stir a heart too easily; the taking or giving of what is not one's own.

Theo read that last line twice. *The taking or giving of what is not one's own.*

They knew. Not everything—not his face, not his method—but the shape of him, drawn in outline like a wanted man sketched from a description shouted secondhand. His mouth went dry around the taste of turnip-dirt and fear.

Beside him, the egg-woman began to cry.

It was not loud crying. It was the kind that leaks, that embarrasses its owner, that she tried to smother with the back of her wrist while pretending to study the proclamation's grammar. And because Theo was what he was, her grief came off her in waves he could feel against his skin, warm as breath in winter: her son, her stupid brave son, who had once told her—laughing, drunk on festival wine—that he always dreamed the weather three days early. She had thought it a charm. She had told her sister. Her sister had told the miller's wife. The miller's wife had a cousin who collected rewards.

Theo could take it. That was the horror of his gift, the thing he never said aloud even to himself: he could reach into that grief and lift it out of her like a splinter, and she would stand in the square feeling only a vague, unplaceable lightness, and she would go home and make bread and never understand why the bread tasted of nothing. He had done it a hundred times. It was how he ate. It was how he survived.

He did not do it now. Some debts you pay by refusing payment.

Instead he stood in the cold and let her grief wash over him, and watched the square the way a drowning man watches the shore.

* * *

Fear moves through a crowd the way water moves under ice—you cannot see it, but you can see everything it lifts and tilts and carries.

By noon, half of Vaelmoor had passed the chapel door. They came in twos and threes, in the rhythms of ordinary life that had gone subtly wrong: the cooper who always whistled walking past in silence; the two grandmothers who had feuded for nine years over a fence line standing shoulder to shoulder, reading together, whispering; children shooed away from the notice by mothers whose hands shook more than the shooing required.

Theo watched from the porch of the chandler's shop, where he had appointed himself a loiterer with nowhere better to be. From here he could feel them all. That was the curse of his particular gift—it was not a door he opened at will so much as a window that never quite shut. Emotions came off people like heat off stones, and fear had a flavor he knew better than any other: coppery, bright, a little sweet, like blood held too long in the mouth.

Old Tam the ferryman read the notice and felt not fear but a fierce, stubborn anger, and beneath the anger something tender and terrified—he was thinking of the tinker's daughter, the one who could make sick animals well with her hands, whom everyone pretended not to notice noticing. Tam's anger said: *over my dead body.* Tam's tenderness said: *and it very likely will be.*

The innkeeper's wife read it and felt calculation, cold and quick—she was counting her guests, remembering who slept alone, who talked in their sleep, who had paid in coin that looked too new. Theo filed that away. Fear made people informants faster than loyalty ever made them protectors.

The priest read it last, and longest, and Theo felt nothing from him at all—which frightened Theo more than any grief or fury could have. Either Father Aldous felt nothing, which made him a stone in a river of terror, or he had learned, somewhere in his long gray life, to wall himself off so thoroughly that even a thief of feelings found no window. Theo made a note to steal from him someday, just to learn what was behind the wall. Then he flinched at his own thought. Even now, with hunters coming, the gift reached out its little grasping hands. It was not a habit. It was an appetite. That was the truth he kept buried deepest.

A boy ran through the square shouting the news ahead of itself: *"Thirteen bells! Thirteen bells! They're ringing it from Harrowgate—they say the hunters are already on the north road!"*

And the crowd rippled. Theo felt it pass through them like wind through wheat—a collective intake, a leaning, a turning toward home. People who had lived beside each other for forty years suddenly saw strangers. The egg-woman clutched her basket to her chest as if the eggs were her son. Old Tam spat and stalked toward the river. The innkeeper's wife smiled at three different people in a way that fooled none of them.

And underneath it all, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, Theo felt something else ripple through the valley—something that was not human fear at all.

The border stones.

He had grown up within sight of them, those gray monoliths strung along the valley's rim like the vertebrae of some buried god, and he had never once felt anything from them. Stone felt nothing. Stone was the whole point: magic died at the border stones, that was the Accord, that was the peace, that was why Vaelmoor was safe and sleepy and poor and why no mage had crossed in living memory. The stones were dead things doing a dead thing's work.

But as the thirteenth bell of rumor rang through the square—as the word *hunters* traveled mouth to mouth like a spark down a fuse—Theo felt, for one clear and terrible instant, a hum. Low. Patient. Like a struck bell that has stopped ringing to the ear but goes on ringing in the teeth.

Then it was gone, and he told himself he had imagined it, and he did not believe himself.

* * *

That evening he sat in his room above the cooper's shed with the shutter cracked, watching the moon.

It hung over the eastern ridge, fat and white and just beginning to be eaten—one shadow-nibble at its lower edge, barely there, the way rot begins in an apple. The thirteenth moon. He counted backward out of habit, the way other men counted coins. Twenty-nine nights until it waned full dark. Twenty-nine nights until the eclipse, though the proclamation hadn't mentioned the eclipse—only the surrender deadline, which fell, he realized with a slow cold settling in his stomach, on the very same night. Someone in the capital had chosen that date deliberately. Dates like that were never accidents. Dates like that were arguments.

His room was small and deliberately forgettable: a cot, a stool, a candle stub, a loose floorboard under which he kept the only things he owned that mattered. He knelt and lifted the board and took them out now, because a man facing the end of his safety wants to count what safety was built from.

Three memories, wrapped in linen like relics, though they needed no wrapping—memory keeps itself. He had learned early that stolen memories could be held outside the mind, coaxed into objects, stored like wine. Maren called them *vintages*. Theo called them what they were: other people's worst days, bottled.

He unwrapped the first. A soldier's dying hour, taken in the market town last autumn—the mud of a border skirmish, the astonishing warmth of blood leaving a body, and threaded through it all a love so specific and unbearable that Theo had wept for a week after selling it, wept for a man he'd never met, for a wife named Liss whose face Theo knew better than his own mother's. He had sold it to Maren for bread and silence. Somewhere beyond the valley, some buyer was paying good silver to feel a stranger die lovingly. He had told himself that was mercy: the soldier's widow would never know her husband's last thoughts had been harvested. He had told himself so many things.

The second memory was older. A merchant's guilt, taken on the south road—the details still made Theo's jaw tighten, and he had carried it two years. The third he did not unwrap. The third was the reason he never let anyone stand behind him. The third was the reason he checked the shutter twice and slept with a knife.

He wrapped them all again, slid them under the floorboard, and sat back on the cot with his elbows on his knees.

Here was the arithmetic of his life, laid bare by one sheet of nailed parchment: he had built his invisibility out of other people's losses. Every secret he'd lifted had been a brick. Every grief he'd carried away had been mortar. He knew which neighbors cheated their taxes, which wives knew which husbands' sins, which farmers had seen what in the marsh fog—and he had used that knowledge the way a spider uses silk, never to harm, only to remain untouched, unremarked, unknown. He had told himself that made him harmless. A leech tells itself the same thing.

But the proclamation changed the arithmetic. Because now the crown was asking the valley a question—*who among you is touched?*—and every answer Theo had ever stolen was suddenly currency in a market where the buyers burned people. If the witch-hunters came to Vaelmoor with soft voices and full purses, how long before the innkeeper's wife remembered that the quiet young man in the loft room always knew things? How long before someone traded a memory of *him*?

He had spent nine years making sure no one in Vaelmoor held anything dangerous about Theo Joubert. But memories leaked. People forgot they knew things; then they remembered. And there was one person in this valley who had seen him work—who had, three weeks ago, watched him lift a smuggler's secret in the alley behind the wool-house with the smooth practiced motion of a pickpocket lifting a purse—

Theo went cold all over.

The girl. The fine-boned girl with the noble accent she couldn't quite bury, who had appeared in Vaelmoor in autumn with no history and too many clothes hidden under plain ones. She had seen him. She had said nothing. She had simply begun, thereafter, to be *near*—at the well when he drew water, at the market when he bartered, on the lane when he walked home—with the patient orbit of someone holding a card and waiting to play it.

She knew what he was. And now the crown was paying for exactly that knowledge.

Theo pinched out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the valley breathe. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing. Somewhere the river worried its ice. And far off, at the rim of the world, he thought he felt the border stones humming again—that low, patient note, like a choir tuning before a service no one had announced.

Twenty-nine nights.

Outside, the thirteenth moon rose a finger's width higher, and its shadow grew, and in the dark of his small forgettable room, Theo Joubert began—for the first time in nine years—to plan how to run.
