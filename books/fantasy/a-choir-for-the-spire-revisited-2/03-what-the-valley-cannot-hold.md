## 3. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The border stones stood a half-day's walk north of Vaelmoor, where the road thinned to a shepherd's track and the pines gave way to grey scree. Theo had never gone there. That was not superstition so much as arithmetic: a man who lived by staying unremarkable did not walk toward the one place in the valley where remarkable things were said to happen. The stones were where magic came to die. Every child in Vaelmoor learned it before they learned their letters—*beyond the stones, the old blood wakes; inside them, it sleeps forever.* Mothers used the phrase like a lullaby and a warning in the same breath.

He went anyway, on a morning so cold his breath hung around him like a second cloak, because he could no longer afford not to know.

It had started three nights ago, small as a splinter working its way to the skin's surface. He had lifted a memory from a tinker at the Drowned Bell—a trivial thing, the taste of river water swallowed on a dare when the man was nine—and felt something he had never felt before: resistance. Not the usual friction of a mind closing against him, which was ordinary and manageable, but a deeper drag, as though the memory itself were reluctant to leave, anchored somewhere below the man's remembering. And when Theo had carried it out of the inn, wrapped and sealed in the hollow behind his ribs where stolen things lived, it had *hummed*. Faintly. Like a struck bell heard through a wall.

Memories did not hum. Memories were quiet cargo. They sat where you put them and waited to be sold, and if they pressed against you at night—if the soldier's dying or the widow's grief leaked through the seals and colored your dreams—that was the cost of the trade, weather to be endured, not a mystery to be solved.

But this hum had not stopped. It had grown. And this morning, dressing in the dark of his room above the cooper's shed, Theo had put his palm flat against his own sternum and felt the vibration answer him, patient and rhythmic, like something breathing in its sleep.

So: the stones. Because there was only one explanation that made sense of it, and he hated the explanation, and he needed to be wrong.

*Magic cannot cross the border stones.* If the humming was magic—if the tinker's memory had somehow come into contact with the gift that stole it, gift and theft touching like flint and steel—then the stones should smother it. He would walk to the border, stand within arm's reach of the standing granite, and feel the hum die. Simple. Clean. The world returning to its proper shape.

He told himself this the whole way up the valley, past the last farmsteads with their frost-silvered fields, past the shrine to the Shepherd of Small Hours where someone had left an offering of bread gone hard as brick. He told himself it as the track climbed and narrowed, as the wind came down off the high passes carrying the smell of stone and old snow, as the trees stopped pretending to be a forest and became scattered, stunted, bent. He told himself it right up until the moment he crested the final rise and saw them.

There were thirteen of them.

He had known that, too, the way every valley child knew it—thirteen stones for thirteen moons—but knowing and seeing were different countries. They stood in a broken arc across the neck of the pass, each one taller than two men, granite gone silver-grey with lichen, their surfaces carved with lines that might have been writing and might have been water-erosion and might have been both, worn so deep and so long ago that no one living could say. Snow lay in the carved channels so that from a distance the words seemed to glow faintly white against the dark stone. Beyond them, the pass climbed away into country Theo had never seen—the true north, the outer world, the place where the exiled mages supposedly still wandered, if they existed at all outside of chapel sermons.

The air between the stones looked exactly like air. That was the unsettling part. No shimmer, no wall, no visible seam in the world. Just a gap in a line of rocks, and beyond it, more mountain.

Theo stood at the top of the rise for a long time, letting the wind push at him, feeling the hum in his chest quicken—not louder, exactly, but *more attentive*, the way a dog lifts its head before the knock comes.

"Right," he said aloud, because the silence up here was total enough to make speech feel necessary. His voice cracked away in the wind and was gone. "You walk down there. You touch nothing. You see if it stops." A pause. "And if it doesn't stop—"

He didn't finish it. He went down.

* * *

Up close, the stones were older than anything had a right to be. He put his hand against the nearest one and the cold of it bit through his glove immediately, a cold with weight behind it, the cold of deep cellars and well bottoms. The carved lines under his palm were smooth as riverbed pebbles. Whatever had been written here had been written by hands that expected to be read for ten thousand years.

Inside the arc—or rather, just at its edge, one foot over the invisible line he had drawn for himself—he stopped and listened inward.

The hum was still there.

Worse: it was *stronger*. Now that he paid attention properly, now that he stood still and let himself actually feel instead of flinching away, he could parse it. It wasn't one note. It was many—dozens, scores, a whole buried choir of them, each one a stolen memory he carried, each one vibrating at its own pitch. The tinker's river-water dare sang high and bright. The soldier's death from two winters ago, the one he'd sold to Maren but somehow still felt the ghost-weight of, droned low and dark. And beneath all of them, beneath everything, a single tone so deep he hadn't recognized it as sound at all until now—more a trembling than a hum, coming from somewhere he did not have a name for. From the floor of himself. From whatever he was built on.

"No," Theo said quietly. "No, that's not how it works."

Because it wasn't. He had built his entire life inside a single sentence: *magic cannot cross the border stones.* It was the sentence that made Vaelmoor safe. It was why the witch-hunters had never come here, why gifted children born in the valley grew up merely strange instead of dead, why a thief of memories could operate for years in plain sight among people who believed the gift couldn't exist inside the stones. The valley was a room with the fire stamped out. Nothing burned here. Nothing *could*.

Except him.

He stepped fully between the stones. Past the arc. Into the outer world, if only by three paces.

The hum didn't falter. If anything it opened, bloomed, the way a chord opens when the last voice joins it—and Theo staggered, caught himself against the thirteenth stone, and stood there gasping while something happened inside him that he had no language for.

It was as if he had spent his whole life hearing music through a closed door, and someone had just opened it. Not loudness—*clarity*. Every stolen memory in him suddenly had edges, texture, depth. The tinker's river water was cold going down, he could feel it, cold and green-tasting, and the boy who'd swallowed it had been dared by a girl with a gap in her teeth whom he'd loved hopelessly for four more years. Theo knew this the way he knew his own name, instantly and totally, details surfacing from a cargo he'd sealed and sold and forgotten. Except he hadn't sold it yet. Hadn't he? Or had Maren's coin simply bought a copy while the original stayed curled inside him like—

Like everything else. Like all of it. Like every memory he had ever lifted, none of them ever truly gone, all of them still singing inside the hollow behind his ribs, and the hollow was not hollow at all.

He was aware, distantly, that he had slid down the stone and was sitting in the snow, and that the snow was melting around him in a slow spreading ring, and that the stone at his back was warm now. Warm. Granite in a mountain pass in late autumn, warm as a sleeping animal.

"Theo Joubert," he said to the empty pass, and his breath shook, "you are in a great deal of trouble."

* * *

He did not go home. Not yet. He made himself stay—made himself sit against the impossible warm stone until his breathing steadied, then made himself do what he did best, which was to think like a thief.

Thieves survived by understanding systems. Who watched what. What was worth taking. Where the exits were. So he catalogued, methodically, sitting in the trampled snow:

Fact: his gift worked inside the valley. Fact: it worked *better* outside it, or at least it felt different here, richer, closer to the skin. Fact: the memories he carried were not copies. This was the fact that made his hands shake, so he set it aside and picked it up again twice before he could hold it still. If the memories were originals—if every sale to Maren had been a theft in the truest sense, leaving some traveler permanently lighter—then he had spent seven years maiming people. The widow whose grief he'd lifted at the spring market: she had wept once afterward, briefly, confused, and then smiled and thanked him for his kindness, and he had walked away with her husband's funeral folded up inside his chest. He had thought he'd done her a mercy. He had thought the mercy was the point.

Fact: the deep tone beneath all the others, the one from the floor of him, had gotten louder the moment he crossed the line. As if it recognized where it was. As if it were *from* here, or *of* here, or—

He stopped. Sat very still.

There was a memory he did not have. He had noticed the absence the way you notice a missing tooth—constantly at first, then only when the tongue goes looking. Everyone had a childhood. Theo had fragments: a kitchen, a woman's hands kneading dough, a song hummed off-key, a door. Then nothing, then the foundling cart, then the orphan house in Vaelmoor, then the long apprenticeship of becoming invisible. Everyone assumed his early years had simply been lost to age, the way early years are. But Theo had lifted hundreds of memories; he knew what memory felt like from the inside. And his own first years did not feel lost. They felt *removed*. Cut clean, the way a surgeon cuts, the way a thief cuts. There were even edges where the cutting had been done.

He had never let himself think about this. Thinking about it led nowhere; there was no one to ask, no ledger to consult, no market where you could buy back your own beginnings.

But sitting against a warm stone at the edge of the world, with a choir of stolen lives humming in his chest and the deep tone rising through them like a root drawing water, Theo let himself think about it for the first time.

The Accord. Everyone knew the word the way they knew the shape of the valley—from the inside, without question. The Moonlit Accord, signed at the border stones at the war's end, the treaty that drew the line: magic stays out, the valley sleeps, everyone lives. The chapel taught that the signing had been a miracle, that the stones themselves had drunk the spell, that the peace was permanent and sacred and the reason Vaelmoor had no witches.

But treaties were signed by people. And the stones had drunk *something*.

What if the line hadn't been drawn in granite? What if it had been drawn in flesh? What if the Accord needed an anchor—something alive, something that straddled the boundary, something born at the signing or bound to it—and what if that anchor was the reason a memory-thief's gift worked fine in the one valley where it should have died?

The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, enormous, and Theo rejected it immediately, completely, with his whole heart.

Then he looked down at the melted ring in the snow, perfect and deliberate, centered on him, and understood that rejection was not the same as refutation.

"I'm not," he said to the stone, to the pass, to whoever had signed whatever had been signed here. "I'm nobody. I'm a cooper's shed and a stolen loaf and a face nobody remembers. I steal *grief*, do you understand? I'm the least magical man in a valley with no magic in it."

The stone said nothing. The stone was warm against his back, and the deep tone in his chest had settled into a rhythm that felt horribly like recognition, like a lock acknowledging a key, and far to the south—so far he could not possibly have heard it, and yet he did, faint as the hum had been three nights ago—a bell began to ring.

Not a chapel bell. Thirteen strokes, unhurried, spaced like footsteps. When it finished, the silence it left behind had a shape, and the shape was a sentence he already knew by heart, the sentence every valley child learned, except that now it seemed to be saying itself back to him from the inside of his own bones:

*Beyond the stones, the old blood wakes.*

Theo got up. Brushed the snow from his coat with hands that almost obeyed him. Took one last look at the pass beyond the stones—at the road he had never traveled, leading to a world he had never touched—and turned south toward Vaelmoor, walking fast, because there was a broker's shop on Candlemaker's Row with a ledger in it, and if anyone in this valley kept records of the old blood and the old war and the things the stones had drunk, it would be the woman who bought and sold other people's souls for a living.

Behind him, the thirteen stones stood in their arc, silvered with lichen, glowing faintly where the snow filled the ancient words.

Warm, all thirteen of them, all the way down.
