## 4. Maren's Ledger

The rain came down on Vaelmoor like a confession nobody wanted to hear—steady, thorough, indifferent. Theo Joubert stood in the alley behind Maren's shop with his hood up and his collar wet against his neck, counting the windows of the memory-broker's house the way a man counts the teeth of a trap he is about to step into.

Three windows lit. One dark: the storeroom above the shop, where the ledgers lived. He had watched that window for eleven nights, and eleven nights it had gone dark at the ninth bell, when Maren climbed to her bed with a candle stub and the heavy tread of a woman who had earned every ache in her knees honestly, or as honestly as anyone earned anything in this valley.

He told himself he was not a thief tonight. That was the joke of it, of course—the whole architecture of his life was a joke with only one listener. He stole memories from the living and sold them to the living, and everyone called it trade. But break into a shop for paper and ink, and suddenly he was a criminal. The distinction mattered to someone, he supposed. It had never mattered to him.

What mattered was the proclamation nailed to the chapel door three days ago, its wax seal the color of old blood, its letters crisp enough to cut:

*Let all persons touched by the gift be surrendered to the crown's hunters before the thirteenth moon turns. Shelter given knowingly shall be judged as gift itself.*

He had read it twice while the crowd around him held its breath, and he had felt their fear the way he felt everything—not by choice, but because fear was the loosest thread on any soul, the first thing to unravel, the easiest thing to lift. He could have taken the whole square's terror in an afternoon and sold it by the pound. Instead he had walked home with his hands in his pockets and his heart going like a drum in a procession, and he had made a list.

Maren's ledger was at the top of it.

Because Maren kept records. That was her religion: ink, columns, dates, sums. Every memory Theo had ever sold her was written there in her cramped, upright hand—the dying soldier's last morning, the miller's guilt over his brother's drowned son, the widow's grief so dense it had taken two visits to lift. Names of sellers. Dates of purchase. Descriptions detailed enough to hang a man. And somewhere in those pages, if she was as thorough as he believed, was a portrait of exactly what the witch-hunters were coming to find: a gifted thief who had been feeding the Spire for six years without once being caught.

The evidence would not burn itself.

Theo pressed his palm flat against the shop's back door and felt for the lock the way he felt for memories—with attention, with patience, with the particular stillness that made people forget he was standing there even when they were looking at him. The lock was iron, new, expensive. Maren had grown careful since the proclamation. Careful, but not careful enough; she still trusted walls more than she trusted people, and walls could not feel a pick sliding into them.

It took him four minutes. His hands were steady. His mouth was dry as flour.

Inside, the shop smelled of cedar and cold ash and something older underneath—something like the inside of a skull, which was what a room full of memories always smelled like to him, though he had never said so aloud and never would. Shelves rose into the dark, rows upon rows of vessels: glass phials stoppered with wax, lead caskets banded shut, jars of river clay sealed with sigils he couldn't read. Each one held somebody's worst day. Each one had been somebody's worst day until they'd paid Theo—or paid someone like him—to carry it away.

He did not look at them. Looking was how you started hearing them.

A single candle, shielded with his hand, took him up the narrow stair. The storeroom door was unlocked. Of course it was; Maren's real lock was the belief that no one dared rob a broker, because brokers knew where everyone's secrets were buried, and secrets made better weapons than knives.

The desk sat beneath the dark window. Three drawers, all locked, all opened under his picks in less time than it takes to recite a psalm. And there, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, the ledger: fat as a Bible, corners worn soft from handling, smelling faintly of tallow and vinegar.

Theo set it on the desk, opened it to the first page, and began to read his own history in another woman's handwriting.

*Autumn, year of the Long Frost. Purchased of T.J., market town: grief of a soldier (dying), one measure. Paid: bread, salt, silence.*

There he was. *T.J.* Six years of him, page after page, in entries as neat as stitches. He turned the pages slowly, and each turn was like turning over a stone to see what had been living underneath it. Here was the ferryman's shame. Here was the peddler's terror of open water. Here, twice, the same entry—a grief sold, bought back, sold again—which meant some poor soul in the valley had tried to reclaim their sorrow and found they couldn't afford it, and had sold it off again rather than live half-hollowed. Theo closed his eyes for a moment. He hadn't known that. He had never asked where his goods went after Maren weighed them.

That was the thing about being a thief of intangibles: you never had to see the fence you built around someone's heart. You just had to count the take.

He found a knife in the desk drawer—he had come intending to bring one—and laid the ledger flat, ready to cut the pages free, burn them in the cold hearth downstairs, scatter the ash in the rain. Simple. Clean. Ten minutes' work and six years erased.

Then he reached the last quarter of the book, and stopped.

The entries changed.

They were still Maren's hand, still neat, but the dates went back further than Theo had been selling—back before he'd ever lifted his first grief off a dying man in the market square, back past the war itself, back to years whose names he only knew from chapel sermons. And the purchases were different. Not griefs. Not guilts. The descriptions were terse, coded, almost frightened:

*Purchased of source unnamed: fragment, sealed, provenance disputed. Buyer: House of Ashfall. Delivered by third hand. No further contact.*

*Purchased: fragment, sealed. Buyer: House of Ashfall.*

*Fragment. Ashfall.*

Page after page of them, spaced out across decades like a pulse too slow to be healthy. Always the same buyer. Always the same word—*fragment*, as if naming it plainly might cost something. And always delivered by third hand, never by Maren herself, as though even she, who traded in other people's worst days for coin, would not put her own fingers on whatever these things were.

Theo knew the name. Everyone in Vaelmoor knew the name, the way children know the names of monsters—vaguely, ritually, without believing. House of Ashfall. The mages who had ended the last war by signing the Moonlit Accord, binding magic behind the border stones, exiling themselves beyond the valley so that the rest of the world could sleep without dreaming of fire. The priests said the Ashfalls had saved mankind. The farmers said they had stolen something from the world and locked it away where no one could reach it. Both stories agreed on one point: the House of Ashfall belonged to the past, sealed outside the stones along with everything else that could work wonders.

And Maren had been selling to them. For forty years. Fragments of something.

His paranoia, which had brought him here with a knife and a plan, shifted shape in his chest—sharpened, cooled, became something with edges. This was not evidence of his crimes. This was evidence of hers, and of something larger, something that made his crimes look like a boy picking pockets outside a burning city.

He should burn the ledger anyway. That was the discipline of survival: leave nothing, explain nothing, be no one. Every instinct he had honed since he was old enough to know he was different said *burn it and walk away and never think of Ashfall again*.

But his hands didn't move. Because there was one more entry, near the end, in fresher ink, and it was different from all the others.

*Purchased of T.J., market town: grief of a soldier (dying), one measure.* — that he'd seen already. But beneath it, squeezed into the margin in a hand smaller than Maren's usual script, hurried, almost furtive:

*T.J.'s goods run strange. Sells clean, sells deep. Asked him nothing. Note for the Spire: the boy may be more than a gleaner. Query re: fragments — do the Houses seek such as him?*

Theo read it three times. The candle flame leaned in the draft and his shadow leaned with it, and the words stayed exactly where they were, patient as scars.

She knew. She had known—how long? Since when? She had weighed his memories in her little brass scales and noticed what he had spent six years making sure no one would notice: that his takings came away *clean*, root and branch, that the people he worked on woke lighter in a way no other gleaner's customers ever did. Copies left the original intact. Theft did not. Maren had looked at her ledger, looked at her scales, and done the arithmetic he had forbidden himself to do.

And then, instead of selling him to the crown, she had written a query. To the Spire. About the Houses. About whether *such as him* were sought.

Which meant Maren was not merely a broker. Which meant there was a market beyond the valley for something Theo had never thought of himself as being—a thing sought, a thing queried, a thing with a price above grief. Which meant the ledger in front of him was worth more unburned than burned, dangerous as it was.

He sat very still in the dark storeroom and listened to the rain, and felt the shape of his life quietly rearrange itself around a new center.

He had come here to erase himself. He was leaving with proof that he could not be erased—that he had never been invisible at all, only unnoticed, and that the difference between those two things was the width of one woman's marginal note.

Footsteps below. Floorboards, the third step from the bottom, the one that always spoke.

Theo's blood went to ice water. The ninth bell had rung long ago. Maren did not rise in the night; he had counted her nights the way he counted everything, and she did not rise. Unless—

Unless she had counted his nights too.

He pinched out the candle. Darkness took the room, total and soft. Below, the footsteps crossed the shop floor, unhurried, and stopped. A match scraped. Light bloomed up through the gap beneath the storeroom door in a thin gold line, and in that line he saw the shadow of Maren's head tilt upward, listening, the way he listened to strangers in the market—reading the air above her for the tremor of another person's presence.

He had perhaps thirty seconds. The window at his back overlooked the alley and a drainpipe he had already measured with his eyes. The ledger lay open on the desk, huge and damning, full of his name and her knowledge and forty years of fragments bound for a house of exiled wizards.

He could not carry it and climb. He could not climb and leave it. So he did the only thing left that was both quick and his: he tore the last quarter of the book free—all the Ashfall entries, all the queries, the whole secret spine of it—folded the pages into his shirt, and left the wounded ledger lying open on the desk like a confession with its throat cut.

The door handle turned as his boots cleared the sill.

He went down the drainpipe in the rain, silent as water, and ran through streets gone silver and blind, and did not stop running until he reached the shrine of the border stones at the valley's edge, where the old monoliths stood black against the storm and the air around them tasted of nothing at all—no magic, no memory, no gift, just rain and granite and the long dead hush of the Accord.

He leaned against the coldest stone, panting, the torn pages soaked against his chest, and by the light of a lightning flash he read the final line of Maren's margin note, the part he had missed in the storeroom, the part that changed the taste of everything:

*If the hunters come asking, I will tell them nothing. Some goods are worth more unsold. But someone should know what walks our market in a grey hood.*

Someone should know.

Maren had been protecting him. Or studying him. With Maren, Theo understood suddenly, those had always been the same activity.

Lightning again, and this time it lit the border stones all down the line, and Theo saw—for the first time, unmistakably, impossibly—that the nearest stone bore a carving worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather: a circle of small figures, hand in hand, and at its center a single figure with its arms spread wide, holding the circle open.

An anchor.

The thunder rolled over him, and Theo Joubert, thief of memories, keeper of no secrets but his own fatal one, stood in the rain with a stranger's ledger against his heart and understood that he had just become the most dangerous kind of man in Vaelmoor:

A man with a purpose.

* * *

He did not go home. Home was a rented room above the cooper's, with a loose shutter and a hiding place under the floorboard that now seemed laughably shallow. Instead he walked the drowned lanes toward the inn where the runaway noble girl had been drinking alone for a week, paying her tab with coins that rang too true for a servant's wages, watching the door the way soldiers watch doors.

Ivy. She had caught him mid-theft two nights ago—had seen the stillness settle over him, had seen the traveler's face go slack and peaceful as a child's—and instead of screaming she had smiled like a card player showing her ace, and named her price: partnership, or exposure. Blackmail wore a friendly face in Vaelmoor that season. Everyone was trading in something.

He had resented her. He resented her still. But she was the only person alive who knew what he was and wanted something other than his surrender, and tonight, with Maren's torn pages drying against his ribs and an anchor carved in lightning-light behind him, resentful company was better than none.

The inn's windows glowed through the rain. Through them he could see her at the corner table, awake far too late, a cup of wine untouched before her, her sharp face bent over something she was writing—and writing quickly, furtively, glancing up between lines like a girl doing something she had already decided to be ashamed of.

Theo stopped in the street.

He had spent his whole life reading people at a glance—the flicker of guilt, the weight of grief, the exact shade of a lie. And what he read now, through rain-streaked glass, in the set of Ivy's shoulders and the way she shielded the page with her arm, was the oldest fear in the world, the one he knew best because he wore it daily:

The fear of a letter that will betray someone.

He stood in the rain a moment longer, the Ashfall pages against his chest, the anchor's image burning behind his eyes, and made the second decision of the night—one he did not yet understand, and would pay for, and would not regret.

He pushed open the inn door, dripping, hood back, visible for the first time in six years.

"Ivy," he said. "We need to talk about what you're writing."
