## 5. The Girl Who Sold Her Name

Ivy Ashvale had been many people in her nineteen years, but she had never been a liar until she came to Vaelmoor.

That was not quite true, and she knew it, and the knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She had been a liar in the capital—she had lied beautifully, effortlessly, in silk and candlelight, lying the way other girls danced, because it was expected of her and cost her nothing. *What a lovely gown, Lady Mereth.* *Of course I'll write to my father about the harvest tariffs.* *No, I've never seen that man before in my life.* Lies as currency. Lies as manners.

But those lies had been hollow things, sugar shells with nothing inside. What she had refused to do—the thing that had put her on a night barge down the Serein with nothing but her mother's ring sewn into her hem—was not lie at all. They had asked her for names. Her father's friends in the Chancellery, men with soft voices and hard eyes, asking over brandy which of her companions at the Lyceum were *touched*, which ones hummed when they thought no one listened, which ones could find lost coins without looking. And Ivy, who could have lied so easily, who could have invented three false names and sent the hunters chasing shadows while her friends slipped away—had said nothing at all.

Silence was not a lie. Silence was the one thing they couldn't make her give.

So she had run instead, and now she lived above a dyer's shop in Vaelmoor under the name Ivy Fern, and she lied constantly, cheaply, about everything—where she was from (south), why she'd come (work), whether she had family (dead, all dead, poor thing)—and each small lie felt like paying out rope from her own body, thread by thread, until someday there would be nothing left to hold.

The valley smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke in the last week of autumn, and Ivy had learned its rhythms the way she'd once learned court dances: thoroughly, resentfully, and better than anyone suspected. She knew that Maren the broker opened her shutters at dawn to count the light. She knew that Old Tam watered his onions with tears he pretended were rainwater. She knew that the proclamation nailed to the chapel door two weeks past had changed the way people walked—not faster, exactly, but closer to the walls, the way animals move when something hunts at the treeline.

And she knew, though she could not have said how she knew, that Theo Joubert was not what he pretended to be.

* * *

She had been watching him for eleven days.

Not because she wanted to. Because watching was what kept her alive. In the capital she had survived by noticing—the flicker in a chamberlain's eye that meant a purge was coming, the way her father's steward counted silver twice when certain guests visited. Noticing was the only inheritance her family had ever given her that proved useful.

Theo Joubert noticed nothing, apparently. That was his art. He moved through the market like a man made of fog, apologizing his way through crowds, dropping things, picking them up, laughing at his own clumsiness. He was handsome in an unremarkable way—brown hair, brown eyes, a face built to be forgotten—and he wore his forgetfulness like armor. Ivy had watched him bump into a carter, stammer apologies, help gather spilled apples, and walk away with the man's grief gone from his shoulders like rain off oiled cloth. She had watched him sit with a widow whose son had drowned in the spring floods, saying nothing, just sitting, and leave an hour later while the woman wept without knowing why and also without knowing why she suddenly felt lighter, cleaner, emptied like a house after a funeral.

He took things. She was almost sure of it. Almost—because certainty would require proof, and proof would require catching him, and catching him would require—

Well. It would require what she was doing now: following him through the dusk-lit lanes behind the tannery, her breath smoking, her boots silent on ground she'd learned to cross silently months ago.

He stopped at the well at the end of Cooper's Lane, where an old man named Aldous sat every evening with a cup of thin wine and the memory of his wife, dead these four years, worn smooth as river stone from handling. Ivy melted into the shadow of the cooper's eaves and watched.

Theo sat beside Aldous. Said something she couldn't hear. Laughed. Put his hand briefly, casually, on the old man's shoulder—a touch so natural it might have been nothing, a gesture between neighbors, a pat, a steadying.

Aldous's face changed. It was subtle—like watching a lamp gutter and steady—but Ivy saw it. Something went out of him. His shoulders, which had been hunched around his wine cup like a shell around a soft body, straightened. He looked at the sky. He looked, for a moment, like a man who had set down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

And Theo stood, wished him good evening, and walked away with his hand curled loosely at his side, and even across forty feet of darkening lane, Ivy could see it: his fingers were trembling.

*Got you,* she thought, and felt no triumph at all, only a cold bright clarity, the same feeling she'd had standing in her father's study refusing to speak. *I know what you are.*

* * *

She gave him three days. Long enough for the wariness of near-discovery to fade—he was careful, she'd grant him that, checking sightlines, varying his routes, never taking from the same person twice in a week. But careful men grew careless in familiar places, and Vaelmoor had only so many lanes.

On the third evening she caught him in the tithe barn behind the chapel, of all places, crouched over a sleeping hired girl named Bett who'd come in from Lowfield with a wagonload of barley and a head full of nightmares. Ivy had been waiting in the loft since noon, among the dusty rafters where swallows used to nest, and she came down the ladder without a sound because she had spent a childhood learning to descend staircases unheard.

Theo had his palm flat against Bett's brow. His eyes were closed. His lips moved slightly, shaping words or maybe just breathing, and between his fingers—between his skin and the sleeping girl's skin—Ivy saw it.

Light. A faint gold shimmer, like sun through honey, threading from the girl's temple to his palm. It pulsed once, twice, slow as a sleeper's heart. And then it flowed, and Ivy watched a nightmare leave a sleeping girl's face the way frost leaves a window in the morning, and Theo Joubert breathed in, and shivered, and opened his eyes.

"Hello," Ivy said.

He spun. For a big moment neither of them moved. Bett slept on, her breathing deep and even, her brow smooth as a child's. The barn smelled of barley dust and old timber and, beneath it, something else—something clean and strange, like the air after lightning.

"You should be asleep," Theo said finally. His voice was steady. His hands were not; he'd tucked them behind his back, but she could see the tremor in his wrists. "It's late for—"

"For watching you steal?" Ivy stepped forward, out of the rafter-shadow, letting him see her fully: plain wool dress, dyer's stains on her knuckles, hair bound back like any working girl's. Let him look for the noble and fail to find her. "Because that's what this is. Isn't it? You take them. You don't copy them—you *take* them. That girl won't remember her nightmare tomorrow. Or whatever else you just pulled out of her."

Something flickered behind his eyes—fear first, quick as a fish, then calculation, then a kind of exhausted resignation that told her more than either of the others. He wasn't surprised enough. He'd known someone might catch him someday. He'd simply hoped for later.

"You don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"I know exactly what I'm talking about." She let a beat pass. Let him watch her watch him. "I grew up around gifted people, Theo Joubert. Real ones. Ones who could light candles with a word and read the weather in their bones. And every one of them, when they used their gift, *glowed*. Just like that." She nodded at his hand. "Just like that."

His jaw tightened. "Then you know what happens to people like me now. There's a proclamation on the chapel door with your name on it if you keep talking."

"My name isn't on anything," Ivy said pleasantly. "My name is Ivy Fern, dyer's assistant, orphan, no family, no history, no one who'd miss me if I vanished. Isn't that convenient? No one to ask questions if I disappear." She tilted her head. "Funny, though. That's your trick too, isn't it? Being no one. We should compare notes."

For a long moment the only sound was Bett's breathing and the wind working at the barn's loose shutter, a small rhythmic knocking like a polite visitor.

"What do you want?" Theo asked at last. Flat. Tired. A man setting down a heavy load because his arms had finally given out.

"Partnership," Ivy said.

* * *

They talked in the loft, above the sleeping girl, voices low, the shutter knocking its patient rhythm. Ivy sat on a grain sack with her knees drawn up; Theo stood near the ladder, always near the ladder, she noticed, a man who liked his exits.

"I watched you take from Aldous," she said. "The old man at the well. Four years he's carried his wife's death like a stone in his chest, and you lifted it out of him in an evening, and he'll wake tomorrow lighter and not know why, and somewhere in your head is everything he ever felt about her. Every memory. Every—" She stopped. "Do you sell them?"

"Yes."

"To Maren."

"Maren buys. I don't ask where they go after." His voice had gone carefully blank, and Ivy recognized the craftsmanship of it, because she'd built the same wall in her own voice a hundred times. Blankness was a skill. Blankness took practice.

"And the nightmares? Bett's nightmare—does Maren buy those too?"

"Nightmares are cheap. Grief sells better. Guilt best of all." He said it like reciting prices at market, but his eyes were somewhere else, somewhere far away and cold. "Why do you care? You followed me for days. You waited in a hayloft half the day. People don't do that out of concern for old men's feelings."

"No," Ivy agreed. "They don't."

She had rehearsed this part on the walk over, and still it caught in her throat like a fishbone. She made herself say it anyway, because a lie of omission was still a lie, and she had promised herself—one promise, just one—that with this man she would trade only in true coin.

"My name is Ivy Ashvale. My father is Chancellor Edmun Ashvale. Three months ago his colleagues asked me to name the gifted students at the Lyceum. Friends of mine. I refused. I ran before they could decide what to do with a daughter who wouldn't talk." She held his gaze. "So yes. I know what hunters do. I know what proclamations mean. I've sat at tables where men planned them over dessert."

Theo stared at her. Whatever he'd expected—blackmail for coin, threats, a demand to be left alone—it wasn't this. She watched him rebuild his understanding of her piece by piece, the way she'd watched masons rebuild a wall after a flood.

"Ashvale," he repeated. "The Chancellor's daughter is hiding in a dyer's loft in Vaelmoor."

"The Chancellor's daughter is dead," Ivy said. "Ivy Fern is very much alive, and very much interested in staying that way. Which brings me to the partnership."

"I'm listening."

"Captain Serah Voss arrived nine days ago. You've seen her—red cloak, grey eyes, smiles too easily. She's not here for show. She's a charm-sense; my father mentioned her once. She can feel the residue a gift leaves. Like smelling smoke on someone who swears they haven't been near a fire." Ivy leaned forward. "You've been thieving in this valley for years, Theo. Every theft leaves smoke. And now there's a woman in town whose whole talent is smelling it."

"I know," Theo said quietly. "I've been careful."

"You've been lucky. Careful runs out." She let that settle. "Here's my offer. I notice things—you may have gathered that. I can tell you which travelers are worth lifting from and which are traps. I can read a room, a ledger, a lie. In exchange, you teach me how your gift works—not to use it, I don't want it, I want to *understand* it—because the more I understand what the hunters are hunting, the better I hide. And when Voss gets close—" she met his eyes, "—we get close together. Two sets of eyes. Two exits. One story."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I go to Captain Voss and describe, in detail, the glowing I saw in this barn tonight." She said it gently, which somehow made it worse, and she knew it, and hated herself a little for using it anyway. "I don't want to. I've had enough of giving names to hunters to last several lifetimes. But I didn't survive my family to die of your stubbornness."

Theo looked at her for a long time. The wind knocked the shutter. Below them, Bett turned in her sleep and murmured something—*mother*, maybe, or *more*—and settled again, dreamless now, swept clean.

"You're a liar," Theo said at last, and there was something almost like admiration in it. "You've been lying since you sat down. Half of what you've told me tonight is shaped wrong. Stretched. You're telling me truths bent into the shape you need."

Ivy felt her face flush hot and forced it still. "And you'd know," she said. "Being such an honest man yourself."

To her surprise, he laughed—a short, real laugh, quickly strangled, as if it had escaped before he could stop it. He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he lowered them he looked older and younger at once, a boy wearing a man's exhaustion.

"Fine," he said. "Partnership. But we do it my way. Nobody gets hurt. Nobody gets taken who isn't offering pain they want rid of. And you never—" his voice roughened, "—you never point me at someone just because it's profitable. Understood?"

"Understood." She spat in her palm and held it out, an absurd peasant gesture she'd learned from the dyer's apprentices, and after a pause he clasped it, grimacing, and she grinned at him, and for one unguarded moment they were just two liars shaking on a truth.

"One more thing," Theo said as she turned to climb down. His voice had changed—careful again, probing. "Ashvale. Your father's house. There was a name in a ledger I saw once. A buyer, from the old war days. House of Ashfall." He watched her closely. "Ring any bells?"

Ivy went very still on the ladder.

"No," she said, and it was the smoothest lie she'd ever told, because it was almost true: she had never heard of any House of Ashfall.

But the name landed in her like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known her body contained, and somewhere behind her ribs, something old and sealed began, very softly, to open.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. She lay in her loft above the dye-works listening to the vats tick as they cooled, turning the name over and over. *Ashfall. Ashfall.* It tasted like a word from a song she'd heard as a child and forgotten—her nurse singing something about moonlight and borders, about sisters parted, about a price paid in names—

Below her window, in the blue hour before dawn, a bell began to ring.

Not the chapel bell. Not any bell of Vaelmoor's thirteen. This one came from beyond the valley walls, far off, thin as a wire, tolling a pattern she did not know and yet understood the way one understands a scream.

Thirteen strokes. Then silence. Then, faint and terrible, a second bell answering from the opposite horizon.

In the market square below, doors began to open. Voices rose, frightened, questioning. And Ivy Ashvale, who had fled a capital full of bells and proclamations, pressed her forehead against the cold sill and whispered:

"They've started counting."
