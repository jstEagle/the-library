## 6. Hunters at the Inn

The witch-hunters came to Vaelmoor on a Tuesday, which Theo thought was almost funny. Tuesdays were market days, and market days were when he did his best work—when the town was full of strangers with heavy hearts and no time to guard them.

He was lifting a tanner's regret off him at the cheese stall—a small, sour thing, the memory of a brother not written to in nine years—when he felt it first: a coldness moving through the crowd like a fish through warm water. Not magic. Magic couldn't cross the border stones. But something that *remembered* magic, the way a scar remembers the knife.

Theo let the tanner's regret dissolve into his own keeping—he'd sell it to Maren later, or bury it under the willow if it tasted too bitter—and turned slowly, casually, the way a man turns to admire the weather.

Three riders at the far end of the square. Grey cloaks, silver buckles shaped like open eyes. The crown's sigil stitched at their shoulders in thread so dark red it looked like dried blood. And leading them, on a dun mare that moved like she resented every step, a woman with iron-grey hair cropped close to her skull and a face that seemed carved for the specific purpose of disappointing people.

Captain Serah Voss. He knew her by reputation only—the way you know a storm by the color of the sky before it breaks. She had burned the gifted out of three counties south of here. They said she could smell a charm the way hounds smelled fear.

*They can't touch me,* Theo told himself, the old litany, worn smooth as a river stone. *The stones hold. Nothing crosses. I am just a man buying bread.*

But the coldness in the crowd kept moving, and it was moving toward him.

---

**Serah Voss counted lies the way other officers counted soldiers.**

She had learned the trade young, in the ash-country below the Spire, where her mother had been taken for lighting candles with a word instead of a match. Her mother had been innocent, probably. Serah had spent thirty years making sure the word *probably* never again decided who lived and who burned. Innocence was not an absence of guilt. It was a thing you proved, the way you proved a bridge would hold—by walking across it, again and again, until either it held or you fell.

The valley of Vaelmoor should have been easy. The border stones drank magic like sand drank rain; nothing gifted could live inside them for more than a season without withering. That was the whole point of the Accord, signed in moonlight by men and women who had grown tired of burying children. The valley was supposed to be clean.

It wasn't.

She had felt it the moment her company crossed the stones—a wrongness, faint as a hair across the tongue. Like a room where someone had smoked years ago and painted over the wall. Something in Vaelmoor carried the shape of magic without carrying magic itself. A hollow mold. A keyhole waiting.

Her charm-sense was not a gift—she thanked every god she didn't believe in for that—but a discipline, trained into her by the Order until it lived in her bones. It read residue. It read the places where power had been, the way a tracker read disturbed leaves.

And in this market square, among two hundred honest-smelling farmers and one cheese merchant cheating on his weights, something had left a print.

She reined in before the inn—the Drowned Bell, its sign swinging on a rusted hook—and dismounted. Her sergeants, Brannoc and the twins, flanked her without being told. Good dogs, both pairs.

"Captain," said the innkeeper's wife in the doorway, wiping flour from her hands, "we've no rooms till the festival—"

"We're not here for rooms." Serah looked past her into the common room, all low beams and hearth-smoke and faces trying very hard to be ordinary. "We're here for supper. And conversation."

She smiled. It was important to smile. Fear made people stupid, and stupid people ran, and running people were hard to question. She wanted them calm. Calm people told you things they didn't know they knew.

"Spiced wine," she said. "For four. And whatever the pot is."

The innkeeper's wife's hands had stopped moving on the cloth. Serah noted it, filed it, let it go. Everyone's hands stopped moving when the hunters came. That meant nothing by itself.

What meant something was the boy by the window.

Young. Dark-haired, forgettable face, the kind of face you lost in a crowd on purpose. He sat alone with a cup of small beer and a book he wasn't reading, because his eyes hadn't moved across a line of it since she'd walked in. His stillness was wrong. Guilty men fidgeted. Innocent men relaxed. This one had gone *smooth*, like water flattening after a stone sank—and beneath the smoothness, when her sense brushed against him—

There. Faint. Cold. The taste of a keyhole.

Serah sat down at the long table nearest the hearth and began, very gently, to ask questions.

---

Theo kept his eyes on his book and his breathing slow and counted the ways out of the Drowned Bell. Front door: watched. Kitchen door: blocked by the innkeeper's wife, who was standing in it like a hen defending one egg. Back stair to the rooms: creaked on the third step; everyone in the valley knew it; so did anyone who'd asked around for ten minutes.

So. No way out. Which meant the only direction left was through.

The captain was doing what hunters always did, he realized—she wasn't interrogating anyone. She was *chatting*. Asking about the harvest, about the road conditions north, about whether the miller's daughter had married. Small questions, soft as wool, each one a finger testing a lock. Brannoc, the big sergeant, had drifted to stand near the kitchen door with the casual weight of furniture. One of the twins leaned against the stairwell.

They'd done this before. Many times.

Theo turned a page he hadn't read. Around him the common room performed normalcy badly: dice clicked too fast, laughter came a half-beat late, and Old Tam by the fire had begun telling the story of the ghost of Harrow Ford in a voice pitched to carry, as if a haunting were somehow more respectable than what the grey cloaks had come for.

The captain laughed at something the miller said. It was a good laugh—warm, unforced, the laugh of a woman who genuinely liked bad jokes. Theo hated how much it unsettled him. Monsters were easy. This woman was having a pleasant evening.

Then she stood, cup in hand, and said, "I'll thank you all for the welcome," loud enough for the room, "and I'll say plainly what I'm sure you're all wondering. We're looking for the gifted. Anyone touched by the old blood, anyone who carries a charm or works a cantrip or hears what shouldn't be heard. The proclamation on your chapel door says they must be surrendered before the thirteenth moon turns." She paused, letting the fire pop. "I know what you're thinking. *Not here. Not us.* Every valley thinks that. Every valley is right, until it isn't."

Her gaze swept the room—miller, smith, the widow Crane, Old Tam mid-story—and snagged, briefly, on Theo.

Briefly. But it snagged.

"And here's the thing," she went on, softer now, walking toward the window tables with her cup held loose and friendly. "Magic leaves traces. Even here. Even behind your stones. Residue doesn't need the gift to exist—it needs only the gift to have *passed through*. So if any of you have noticed anything odd lately—candles relighting, dreams shared between neighbors, griefs lifting off people like fog burning away—" she smiled at the widow Crane, who had gone pale, "—I'd take it as a kindness if you mentioned it. Discreetly. Tonight, or tomorrow, or whenever your courage finds you. I'm easier to talk to than my reputation suggests."

*I'd bet my life on that,* Theo thought. *Which is exactly what she wants.*

She stopped at his table.

"Good book?" she asked.

---

**The boy's pulse was visible in his throat, which meant he was good, because everywhere else he was perfectly calm.**

"History," he said. "Local. The founding of the valley."

"Any good?"

"It lies," he said, and something flickered behind his eyes—humor, maybe, or its disguise. "Says the stones were raised to keep war out. Doesn't mention what else they keep out."

"Or in," Serah said, and sat down across from him without asking, because asking gave people the chance to refuse and refusal told you less than surprise. "Serah Voss. Crown Witch-Hunter, Third Order."

"Theo." No surname offered. Interesting. Either he had none worth giving or none he wanted traced. "Just a clerk, Captain. Passing through."

"A clerk." She sipped her wine. Up close, the residue on him was stronger—not the wild tang of true talent but something stranger, something *folded*, like a letter written and then erased, the impression of the pen still pressed into the paper. "Passing clerks don't usually sit with their backs to the wall and their eyes on both doors."

"Passing clerks who grew up in port towns do."

"Which port?"

"The kind with ships."

Someone down the table snorted into their ale. Even Brannoc's mouth twitched. Serah let the smile come and go. Clever, then. Quick. She'd known clever ones before—they lasted longest, and burned brightest when they went, and she had buried three of them herself and remembered all their names, which was more than the crown ever bothered to do.

"You know," she said, conversational, turning her cup slowly on the wood, "most people, when I sit down across from them, do one of two things. They talk too much, or they stop talking entirely. You're doing neither. You're answering exactly, precisely, the question asked. It's very well done." She leaned forward slightly. "Where did you learn it?"

"My mother taught me never to waste words."

"Is she living?"

"I don't know." Flat. True, or true-shaped. "I was young when we parted."

Something moved behind his face when he said it—grief, real grief, sudden as a bird striking glass—and Serah's sense flared against it, and there, *there*, underneath the sorrow, was the cold keyhole-taste again, stronger now, and threaded through it something that made the hairs on her arms rise:

An echo. Faint, half-gone, like a bell rung in another room. The signature of a working. Not recent. Old. Buried deep, the way you'd bury a body you didn't want found.

*You've done magic,* she thought, staring into his ordinary brown eyes. *Long ago. Or something was done to you. And you've hidden it so well even you've forgotten where the grave is.*

"Your wine's going warm," Theo said politely.

"So it is." She rose, unhurried, and set a coin on his table—too much, deliberately, a small public generosity that would make him memorable to the room and therefore safe-seeming and therefore careless. "For the book, when you finish lying through it."

She walked back toward the hearth, feeling his attention on her spine like sun through a lens, and allowed herself one moment of professional satisfaction.

She couldn't prove anything yet. But proof was a harvest, and harvests took patience, and she had planted her coin and her name and her smile in this valley's soil, and by tomorrow morning half the town would have talked about the hunter who'd been kind to the stranger, and one of those someones would remember something, or invent something, or envy something.

That was how it always worked. People surrendered each other far more willingly than they ever surrendered themselves.

She just needed time.

---

Time was the one thing Theo didn't have.

He sat with her overpaid coin sweating in his palm and watched her work the room—watched her learn it, really, the way he learned travelers, with patience and warmth and a hundred small hooks—and understood with sickening clarity that she was better at this than he was. He stole memories one at a time, in passing, from strangers who'd never miss them. She was stealing a whole town, a piece at a time, in front of everyone, and the town was *helping*.

By morning there'd be a list. Names in her ledger. Widow Crane, who'd gone pale. Old Tam, whose ghost stories were a little too detailed. The miller, who'd laughed too loudly. And somewhere on that list, in handwriting she hadn't even needed yet: *the clerk.*

Unless.

Theo looked at the coin. Then at the captain's back, straight and patient by the fire. Then at the stairs he couldn't take, the doors he couldn't use.

There was another way out of a locked room. He'd never used it on a hunter—never dared—but he'd used it on customs men and suspicious innkeepers and once, memorably, a bridegroom who'd caught him lifting the groom's doubts on the wedding morning. It was dangerous, because it required touching skin, and it required taking something *and leaving something*, and the leaving was the part that could go wrong.

He had a memory of innocence. Bought cheap off a novitiate in the spring—a girl's last summer before her vows, an afternoon of stolen apples and river-swimming and absolutely nothing in her heart darker than a sunburn. It was the purest thing in his collection. He'd been saving it for a day he needed to believe the world was simple.

Apparently today was that day.

He stood, tucked the book under his arm, crossed the room with the unhurried gait of a man going to refill his cup, and—stumbling, artfully, on the uneven flagstone—caught himself on the captain's shoulder.

"Forgive me," he said. "Clumsy."

Their hands met on her arm. Skin to skin, one heartbeat, two.

He reached into himself for the apple-tree afternoon and *pushed*.

---

**Warmth.**

Sunlight through leaves, green-gold, dappled on bare shoulders. The shock of river-cold climbing her ribs. An apple, tart as a slap, bitten while laughing. Someone shouting her name from the far bank—her *real* name, the one she'd had before the Order, before the grey cloak—and the pure animal joy of not answering, of staying hidden in the shallows where nobody could find her, where she owed nothing and hunted nothing and was nobody's instrument, just a girl on a summer afternoon with wet hair and sticky fingers and the whole enormous day still ahead of her—

Serah Voss blinked.

The common room swam back: smoke, spiced wine, the boy steadying her elbow with an apologetic grip.

"—Captain? You went somewhere, just then."

"Did I." Her voice came out strange. Soft. There was sunlight behind her teeth. There was an apple's taste in her mouth, impossibly, absurdly, and her eyes were stinging and she did not know why, and she found that she was smiling at this clerk, this stranger, with an openness she hadn't felt since—

Since when?

Since a summer she couldn't place. Since a river she'd never visited. Since a name shouted from a bank that she suddenly, desperately, wanted to remember, and couldn't, and the wanting hurt like a bruise.

"I'm fine," she heard herself say. "A moment of dizziness. The wine, perhaps." She withdrew her arm from his grip, gently, and the loss of contact felt like setting down something precious on a high shelf. "You should sit down before you fall down, clerk. And watch yourself on these flagstones. They're treacherous."

"They are that," Theo agreed solemnly.

She returned to the hearth. She sat. She drank her wine and listened to Old Tam's ghost story and laughed in the right places, and all the while a green-gold light burned quietly behind her ribs, a memory of an afternoon that had never happened to her, and she could not for the life of her understand why she felt both healed and robbed, both warmed and grieving, as though someone had handed her a gift and pickpocketed her in the same motion.

*The boy,* she thought, once, briefly, from very far away. *Something about the boy.*

But the thought slid off the sunlight in her chest like rain off oiled cloth, and she poured another cup, and the evening went on.

---

Theo walked up the creaking stairs—third step and all—on legs that shook only after he was out of sight.

In his narrow rented room he sat on the bed's edge and put his head in his hands and waited for the nausea, because there was always nausea after a push. Taking was easy; the memories came away like ripe fruit. Leaving was violence. He'd just planted a whole afternoon in a witch-hunter's heart, and the recoil of it tore through him now, cold sweat, copper taste, hands that wouldn't steady.

And worse than the sickness was the knowing.

Because he hadn't just given her the novitiate's summer. Pushing a memory into someone meant anchoring it to something already there, and the nearest anchor had been her own buried longing—the girl she'd been before the Order, the name shouted from a bank that she'd spent thirty years not answering. He'd woven his stolen apples into her real grief, and now she'd walk around forever with a sweetness in her chest that ached every time she touched it, and she'd never know why, and neither would he be able to undo it.

*I just made her a little more broken,* he thought, *to buy myself one night.*

Below, through the floorboards, he heard her voice rise once—clear, commanding, ordering the twins to ride at dawn for the chapel records, for the tithe rolls, for the list of every soul born in Vaelmoor in the last twenty years.

Every soul born. Including, eventually, a record of a birth twenty-three years ago, on the night of a signing, at the border stones, to a woman with no surname who had died before her son could remember her face.

Theo lay back on the narrow bed and stared at the beams and did the arithmetic of his remaining safety, and the number came up short.

Dawn was coming. And with it, the ledgers.
