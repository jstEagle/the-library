## 7. Ashes in the Ledger

I had been in Vaelmoor for four months before I learned that the memory-broker kept her true ledger in the floor.

Not under the floor — *in* it. The boards of Maren's back room had been cut and rejoined so many times they read like a confession, each seam a different decade of small dishonesties. I found it the way I found most things: by being a nuisance until the world gave up its secrets. I'd come to sell her three memories I no longer wanted — a wedding, a whipping, a winter — and while she weighed them like a fishwife weighing eels, I let my gaze drift, lazy as a cat's, across every corner of her shop. Brokers are vain about their trade. Vanity is a door left unlatched.

The loose board was beneath the drying rack where she hung the glass vials of extracted grief, each one fogged from within like a breath trapped against a window. When she turned to her scales, I pressed my thumbnail into the seam and felt the board give — hollow, shallow, lined with something soft. Wax, maybe. Or wool. Something meant to keep what slept below it quiet.

I didn't lift it then. A thief who looks twice at a hiding place might as well sign her name to it. But I told Theo that night, sitting on the cold hearthstone of the empty house we'd begun to share out of mutual distrust, and I watched his face do something I had never seen it do in all our weeks of wary partnership.

It went white.

"The ledger," he said. "Her real ledger."

"You knew she kept one."

"I knew she kept *a* ledger. The pretty one, with the neat columns, the one she shows the wardens when they come sniffing about taxes." He was standing now, pacing the length of the hearth, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, a man wearing a groove into his own anxiety. "The pretty one lists maybe a tenth of what moves through her hands. The rest goes somewhere the crown can't find it — because most of it shouldn't be moving at all. Grief lifted from nobles' bastards. Secrets sold twice. And—" he stopped pacing, "—names of buyers outside the valley."

"Outside." I sat up. Magic died at the border stones; everyone in Vaelmoor learned that lesson as children learn not to touch hot stoves. Whatever moved beyond those stones belonged to the exiled houses, to the war that ended twenty-three years ago and was spoken of only in the flattened voice people use for plague pits. "Who buys memories from beyond the border?"

Theo looked at me for a long moment, and I saw him deciding — the same calculation he made every time I asked about his past, weighing how much of himself to spend against how much suspicion my silence cost. He spent less than I wanted and more than I expected.

"I don't know," he said. "But Maren does. And if there's a list of names out there, then there's a list with my name near it, and yours, and every soul we've ever touched this trade with. When the hunters finish reading the proclamation on the chapel door, they'll want ledgers. Ledgers hang people."

"So we burn it."

"We *read* it first." His jaw set in that way I would come to know too well, the stubbornness of a man who hoards knowledge the way other men hoard coin. "Ash tells you nothing. Ash just smells."

* * *

We went on the night the thirteenth moon rose fat and wrong-colored over the Spire, its light the yellow of old ivory, so that everything it touched looked slightly embalmed. Theo had planned it for weeks — or so he claimed. In truth I think he had been circling the idea the way a tongue circles a broken tooth, unable to leave it alone and unable to bite down.

Maren kept late hours during market weeks, and market week it was: traders from the low country sleeping off their wine at the inn, carters snoring in their wagons, the whole town breathing slow and stupid. We came through the tannery yard where the smell of lime and rot covered any lesser scent, crossed the ditch on the plank Theo had laid days before, and entered her shop through the cellar hatch he'd shown me, its lock so old it surrendered to a bent nail.

Inside, the shop breathed. That is the only word for it. Hundreds of vials hung in their racks and shelves and ceiling-cradles, and each held a little weather of its own — a grief fogging its glass blue-grey, a joy warming it amber, a terror shivering it with a light like distant lightning. The air tasted of copper and lavender, the two scents brokers use to keep volatile memories docile. My skin prickled. Even giftless, even ordinary as ditchwater, I could feel the room attending to us, the way a stable of horses attends to strangers.

Theo moved through it like a man wading through his own history. He did not look at the vials. That was the strange thing — he kept his eyes fixed on the floorboards, on Maren's desk, on anything but the racked lives around him, as though looking too long might catch him on one of their hooks.

"Back room," he murmured. "The board under the drying rack. Third seam from the wall."

"How do you—"

"I've sold here since I was fourteen." He knelt, worked his knife into the seam, and lifted.

The cavity was lined with oiled leather, and in it lay a book bound in grey calfskin, unmarked by title or sigil, its pages swollen with use. Beside it, wrapped in waxcloth, a stub of black candle and a ring of keys, and — this made my breath catch — a small tin box that rattled.

Theo opened the ledger across his knees. There was no candle lit yet, but the moonlight through the rear shutter fell across the page, and I watched his eyes move down the columns and stop moving.

"What?" I whispered.

He didn't answer. So I leaned over his shoulder and read.

Maren's hand was small and exact, an accountant's hand, and the ledger was organized the way fear organizes things: by risk. The earliest pages were innocuous — dates, goods, buyers within the valley, prices in coppers. But deeper in, past five years, past ten, the entries changed character. Buyers appeared whose names were followed by small marks — a circle, a slash, a star. Some entries were written in a different ink, brown-black, and the handwriting shifted too, looser, older.

And then, eleven years back, a name that made the hair rise along my arms.

*House of Ashfall.*

I knew the name the way every child of the capital knows it — from the histories they made us recite until the words wore smooth as river stones. Ashfall, the mage-house that had held the western marches. Ashfall, whose seat was broken in the last year of the war. Ashfall, whose mages were named traitors in the royal proclamation that ended it, the proclamation that established the border stones and the valley of Vaelmoor as the place where magic went to die. Exterminated, the histories said. Rooted out. Every one.

Yet here was Maren's neat hand, dated eleven years ago: *Delivered, per arrangement, to the House of Ashfall — twelve vials, war-grade grief, sealed in salt. Payment received in old coin.*

"They're alive," I said. My voice came out strange, flat, too loud for the room. "The Ashfalls. Someone's buying from them — or they're buying from us — either way they're *alive*, Theo, and Maren's been trading across the border for—"

"Eleven years at least." He had gone very still. Stillness, in Theo, was never calm; it was a held breath before deep water. "Look at the entries after that one."

I scanned down the page. The Ashfall purchases continued, irregular but persistent, always the same pattern — deliveries of vials going *out*, and coming back, occasionally, marked with a symbol I didn't recognize: a crescent drawn open like a cupped hand.

Then, three years ago, the entries stopped. All of them. And in their place, a single line in that older, browner ink:

*Hold the last of it. Do not sell. Do not open. The drop at the millstone will be collected before the thirteenth turn.*

"The millstone," Theo repeated softly. "There's an old quarry road above the town. Fallen millstone by the third milestone — I've walked past it a thousand times. It has a hollow underneath where children hide sweets." His laugh came out cracked. "Someone's been using a children's hiding place to store — what? What's 'the last of it'?"

The tin box rattled again as he set it aside, and we both looked at it, and neither of us touched it yet. Some instinct older than sense said *not here*. Not among the hanging vials, not inside walls that might have ears, not under a moon that watched like a witness.

"We take everything," I said. "Ledger, box, keys. We put the boards back. And we go look at your millstone."

For once, Theo didn't argue about caution. That frightened me more than the ledger had.

* * *

The quarry road climbed north out of Vaelmoor through birch scrub gone silver in the moonlight, and the cold up there had teeth. We spoke little. Theo carried the ledger inside his coat against his chest like a second heartbeat; I carried the tin. The fallen millstone sat beside the third milestone exactly as promised — a great grey disc taller than a man, half-sunk in earth and moss, its central hole staring up at the sky like the eye of something buried.

Theo circled it twice, running his fingers along the underside where generations of children had worn the stone smooth. Then he stopped.

"There's a seam here. Fresh wax." He glanced back at me, and in the ivory moonlight his face looked carved, all planes and shadow. "Whatever's in there hasn't been collected. Whoever wrote that entry — they're waiting for the thirteenth turn. Which means—" he worked his knife into the wax, "—we're early. We're stealing from thieves who haven't finished their own theft."

"That's the best kind of theft," I said. "No one misses what was never delivered."

The wax parted. Beneath it, fitted into the stone's hollow as precisely as a heart in a chest, lay a case of dark wood banded in lead, and the moment Theo's fingers closed on it, the night changed.

I felt it before I understood it — a pressure behind my eyes, a taste of ozone and cold iron, the sudden certainty that the moonlight had thickened into something almost liquid. Theo made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life: not a cry, nothing so simple, but a low involuntary note like the hum of a struck bell, and the case *answered* it. I swear this. The lead bands shivered. From inside the wood came a resonance, a rising tone, thin and sweet and terrible, the sound a wineglass makes just before it shatters — except it wasn't breaking. It was *recognizing*.

"Theo," I said. "Theo, let go of it."

He couldn't. I saw that. His hands had locked around the case, knuckles bloodless, and the hum was climbing, and the vial-fog of his breath hung in the freezing air, and every hair on my arms stood up and pointed at the thing between us like iron filings finding north. The birches around the clearing began, very faintly, to glow — no, not the trees; the *moonlight* on the trees, gathering, pooling toward the stone as though the night itself were leaning in to listen.

And then I saw what I had refused to see for four months.

Magic was supposed to die at the border stones. That was the whole covenant of Vaelmoor — the reason fugitives like me hid here, the reason the crown tolerated the valley, the reason the war stayed ended. Nothing gifted survived inside these bounds. Everyone knew it. Everyone built their lives on it.

The humming thing in Theo's locked hands sang magic into the air like a struck chord fills a chapel, and the air of Vaelmoor *held* it.

"It shouldn't work here," I heard myself say, and my voice shook. "Nothing should work here. Theo. *Theo.* Why does it work on you?"

The hum crested — and broke. The case went silent. Theo sagged against the millstone, dragging air, and when he raised his head his eyes were wet and wide and full of a horror that had nothing to do with witch-hunters.

"It knows me," he said. His voice was ruined, scraped raw. "Ivy, it — when I touched it, I *saw* — there were towers, and a moon, and people singing in rows like a choir, and someone was holding a child, and—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "The case is sealed. Lead and wax and wards, whatever's in there should be mute as a grave. But it rang against me like a tuning fork. Like it was *looking* for something and found it."

I crouched beside him. Up close I could see the fine tremor in him, the sweat freezing at his temple despite the cold. I am not, by nature, a tender person; tenderness was beaten out of me young in the capital, along with honesty. But I put my hand on his shoulder, because some moments do not allow craft.

"Open it," I said quietly. "Out here. No witnesses but the moon and me, and I already know too much to matter."

He laughed — a broken scrap of a laugh — and turned the case over. The lead bands bore stamps worn nearly smooth: a crescent, a tower, a row of small figures linked hand to hand. The lock answered to the oldest key on Maren's ring, which told me she had never once opened this herself, only kept it, kept it the way one keeps a debt.

Inside, nested in rotted velvet, lay a shard of crystal the size of a man's thumb, wrapped in silk gone brittle with age. It gave off no light. It hummed no note now. It sat in its wrappings like a dead coal.

But the moment Theo's bare skin met it, the clearing *sang*.

This time I felt it in my own bones, giftless Ivy, ordinary Ivy, and that frightened me worst of all — that whatever slept in that fragment reached past him, past his gift, into anyone standing close enough to its keeper. Images poured through me like water through a burst dam: a hall of white pillars under a huge low moon; ranks of singers, hundreds, their mouths open in a harmony I could almost hear and would spend years trying to forget; a table where figures in grey robes bent over a document that glowed faintly silver; and across it all, written in light like frost on glass, words I could not read in a script I somehow *almost* knew—

Then it cut off. Theo had torn his hand away. The shard lay in its silk, inert again, innocent as sea-glass.

Neither of us spoke for a long time. Somewhere downslope, a fox screamed and was answered, and the ordinary sounds of the night came creeping back in, and still we knelt there in the birch-silver like two people praying to the same frightening god.

"Moonlit Accord," Theo said at last. His voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a man handling live coals. "That's what the choir was singing. The signing. That's what's in the shard — a piece of it. A *memory* of it. The Accord didn't get written down and locked in a vault, Ivy. It got *remembered*. Sung into memory and split apart and hidden, so no one power could hold all of it." He stared at the fragment. "And Maren has been keeping a piece of the peace treaty that ended the war in a hole under a millstone, waiting to hand it to the House of Ashfall before the eclipse."

"Why?" It came out of me barely above a whisper. "Why would the exiles want it? What happens if they don't get it?"

"I don't know." But his face said otherwise, said *I suspect, I dread, I know more than I'm saying even now* — and I realized, with a lurch, that the tremor in him hadn't started tonight. It had started at the ledger, at the name Ashfall, as if that name were a hook already sunk deep in his flesh and the ledger had merely pulled the line taut.

"Theo." I took the shard, wrapped it, boxed it, my hands steadier than his — someone had to be. "When it rang against you. When it *sang*. You said it was looking for something and found it." I made myself hold his eyes. "What did it find?"

The silence stretched. Above us the thirteenth moon stood at the top of the sky, swollen, ivory, one night closer to its eclipse, and I thought: whatever he says next will change what I am willing to do, and possibly what I am willing to become.

"It found a memory," he said finally, "that I don't remember stealing. A woman's face. She was singing." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "Ivy — I think she was singing to *me*."

He picked up the case and started down the quarry road toward the valley, toward the town where hunters now walked the streets with the proclamation folded in their coats, and I followed, and the shard rode against my ribs like a second heart beating out of rhythm with my own — patient, ancient, and waiting for the thirteenth turn.
