## 8. The Memory That Isn't Mine

The shard did not sleep.

Theo had told himself it would. He had wrapped it in three layers of wool and buried it beneath the loose floorboard under his cot, the same place he kept the coin he didn't trust himself to spend and the letter he'd never sent to anyone. He had told himself that a thing sealed in wax and silver, cold as riverstone when Ivy pressed it into his hands at the dead drop, was a thing that could be ignored until morning.

But all through the small hours of the night, lying on his cot with the blanket pulled to his chin like a boy afraid of the dark, Theo felt it humming against the soles of his feet. Not sound exactly. Not warmth either. Something between a held note and a held breath—the feeling of standing in a chapel where someone has just stopped singing, and the air still remembers.

He got up twice to check the floorboard. Both times the boards were undisturbed, the dust unbroken, and both times the hum grew louder as he knelt there, as if the shard knew he'd come and was pleased.

By the third hour past midnight he gave up pretending. He dressed in the dark, pried up the board, and unwrapped the shard by the light of a single tallow candle, because some part of him—some old, animal part—understood that whatever lived inside this thing deserved better than darkness, even if it also deserved worse than his curiosity.

Up close, it was smaller than he remembered. A sliver of something glassy and grey-blue, no longer than his thumb, veined with lines of silver that caught the candleflame and moved with it, slow as fish under ice. The wax seal had been broken at the dead drop—Ivy's doing, her knife-work neat as her lies—and along the broken edge Theo could see where the silver veins ran deepest, converging toward the center of the shard like streams toward a lake.

He knew what memory looked like from the inside. He had carried hundreds of them, lifted them out of strangers' heads like splinters, felt their textures: grief like wet wool, guilt like a hook behind the eye, love like a hand cupped around a candleflame. Every stolen memory had a weight and a temperature and a taste. That was how he sorted them for Maren's ledger, how he knew which ones fetched bread and which fetched silence.

The shard tasted like none of them.

It tasted like snowmelt. Like the first cold breath of a morning in late winter, when the world is still dark but the light is already deciding to come. And underneath that, threaded through it the way the silver veins threaded through the glass, was something older and stranger—a texture he had no word for, because he had never touched its like. It felt the way he imagined the border stones must feel from the other side. It felt like law made liquid.

"Right," Theo said aloud, because the silence of the room had grown too attentive, and his voice broke it the way a stone breaks the skin of a pond. "Let's see what you are."

He had rules for this. Rules he'd written for himself over ten years of theft, carved into habit deeper than any ledger: never take more than the edge of a memory; never look at the faces; never follow a grief down to its root, because roots hold. He had broken every rule exactly once, early, with a soldier's dying memory of a burning orchard, and he had spent three days after unable to tell whether the smoke in his throat was borrowed or his own.

But the shard was not a head. There was no skull here, no warm wet dark to reach into. The shard was sealed, and sealed things wanted opening, and Theo had spent his whole life being very good at opening things that wanted to stay shut.

He turned it over once in his palm. The silver veins brightened at his touch—not flaring, not warning, but *recognizing*, the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar footstep. His gift rose in him without being called, that familiar loosening behind his sternum, the sense of doors everywhere, all of them unlocked.

He touched the largest vein with one fingertip.

The room went away.

* * *

Snow. Not falling—*held*, each flake suspended mid-air around him like a congregation frozen mid-prayer, and Theo understood with a lurch that this was not weather but memory, memory rendered so vast it had swallowed the sky. He stood in a courtyard of pale stone he had never seen and yet somehow knew, stone worn smooth at its edges the way things get smooth only from centuries of hands. Lanterns hung from iron hooks, their flames motionless, sculpted light. Beyond an archway stood figures—dozens of them, robed in white and grey, arranged in a crescent, mouths open in song that made no sound, because sound was the one thing this memory had been stripped of, or perhaps the one thing it was saving.

And at the center of the crescent, facing away from him, stood a woman with her sleeves pushed back and her dark hair coming loose from its braid, holding something small and shining in both hands.

Theo knew her before she turned around.

That was the horror of it. Not recognition—recognition he could have survived, could have filed and priced and sold. This was worse. This was the knowledge that lives in the body and not the mind: the way his chest tightened at the set of her shoulders, the way his throat closed around a name he had never learned, the way his feet, in the memory that wasn't a place, took one step toward her without his permission, the way a child's feet move toward a mother across a crowded room.

She turned.

Her face—

He had no face for her. That was the second horror, arriving close behind the first: the memory was damaged, or guarded, or *choosing*, and where her features should have been there was only a soft blur, like a portrait left out in rain. But her eyes came through the blur anyway, two points of focus in the fog, and they were brown, and they were looking directly at him, and they were amused, and they were tired, and they were kind in the specific way of someone who has run out of time to be anything else.

"There you are," she said—or the memory said, in a voice like a door opening into a warm room. "I wondered which of you it would be."

"I don't—" Theo's voice worked, barely. In the frozen courtyard his breath hung motionless beside him, another held prayer. "I don't know you."

"No," she agreed, and the amusement deepened, folding into something that hurt to look at even blurred. "That's rather the point of you, isn't it? They took such care. Wiped the mirrors clean, burned the letters, taught the neighbors to say you were a foundling off the north road." Her blurred head tilted. "And still you walk like me. Did you know that? All these years, and you walk like me."

The shard pulsed against his real palm, far away, and the courtyard flickered like a candle in wind. Theo felt the edges of the memory straining—felt, with a thief's precision, that this was not a complete thing. It was a fragment of a fragment, a shard of the Accord that had swallowed a moment of its own making, and the woman at its center had been standing there when the sealing happened, singing, holding the piece of it that would one day be hidden in a valley where magic couldn't follow.

"You're the anchor's mother," he said, because the words arrived in him fully formed, the way stolen memories always arrived, wearing someone else's certainty. "You're—"

"I'm the one who signed first," she said. "Someone has to sign first. Someone has to stand at the stone and give it a voice to bind itself to." She glanced down at the shining thing in her hands—the shard, this shard, unborn—and her voice gentled in a way that undid him more than anything else, because it was gentle the way voices are gentle with children. "And someone has to be born on the border while the ink is still wet, so the binding has somewhere to live. So peace has a body."

The courtyard shuddered. Somewhere beyond the archway, one of the frozen singers' robes stirred, though nothing else moved, and Theo understood dimly that the memory was ending, or being taken back, or deciding how much he was allowed.

"Wait," he said. "Wait—your name. Your name, I don't—"

"You'll hear it," she said. "From someone who loved me longer than I loved myself. Go east when the moon thins. Knock loudly; they've let the ivy grow over the bell." The blur of her face turned fully toward him one last time, and though he could not see her eyes anymore, he felt them, felt them the way he felt every memory he'd ever stolen—warming his hands over someone else's fire. "And Theo."

His heart stopped. He had never told her his name. He had never told anyone in this memory anything at all.

"How do you—"

"You're my son," she said, simply, terribly, the way people say things that have been true for twenty years while the person they're true about wasn't listening. "Mothers keep what thieves can't find."

* * *

He came back to himself on the floor.

He didn't remember falling. The candle had guttered to a stub; the room was grey with the first suggestion of dawn, the light that isn't light yet, only the world clearing its throat. The shard lay a foot away from his hand, and he had no memory of letting go of it, and his fingers were curled as if they still held it, and they were shaking.

Theo lay on his back on the cold boards and stared at the ceiling beam above him, the one with the water stain shaped like a running hare, the stain he had looked at every night for eleven years in a room he rented with money earned from other people's worst days.

He tried to remember his mother.

This was a thing he had done perhaps a hundred times in his life, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. He had been told—he had *always known*, the way you know things that were put into you rather than learned—that he was a foundling, left at the shrine of Saint Edrys in Vaelmoor one thaw-month, wrapped in a blanket of good wool that suggested money and abandoned with nothing else that suggested anything. Sister Brigit had raised him until the fever year. After that, the valley. After that, the work. He had never questioned it. Questioning required a shape to question, and there had never been a shape. Just the blanket, just the shrine, just the north road that supposedly produced him.

Now he reached for the memory the way he'd reached for a thousand others, with that practiced loosening behind the sternum, that careful thief's touch.

And found, where a mother should be, a hole.

Not emptiness—emptiness he knew, emptiness was the natural state behind most faces. This was a *cut*. Clean-edged. Surgical. He could feel the borders of it the way his tongue could feel the border of the missing tooth: a precise absence, shaped exactly like a person, sized exactly for a life. Someone had gone into him—into a child, into a child lying in a shrine or a border-stone circle or wherever he had actually been born—and had lifted out everything, every lullaby, every face, every ordinary morning, and had done it *well*, had done it with a skill that made Theo's own gift look like a pickpocket's sleight-of-hand, and had left the blanket story behind like a coin on a dead man's eyes.

They hadn't stolen from him. He understood that now, lying in the grey light with his heart going hard and strange. You stole from strangers. Whoever had emptied him had *protected* him—had built the invisibility he'd spent his whole life believing was his own cleverness. He had thought himself a ghost haunting Vaelmoor, and he had been a child hidden in a wall, and the wall had been made of his own missing years.

He laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, closer to the sound the Lowfield child might have made, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his mouth until it stopped.

The shard sat on the floorboards, catching the first true light of dawn, its silver veins faintly luminous, patient as water.

*I wondered which of you it would be.*

Which of—you. Which of *us*. She had known there would be others, or had feared it, or had counted on it. And she had known his name. Mothers keep what thieves can't find—but who had been the thief, then, and who had been keeping him, and why had the keeping required erasing her?

He thought of Ivy asleep two streets over, behind the dyer's shop, with a forged name and a nobleman's stubborn jaw and a letter to the crown she hadn't confessed to yet—though he'd seen the truth of it in her face last night at the drop, the way you see weather coming. He thought of Maren's ledger and the House of Ashfall written in faded ink, east, beyond the stones, in a country the valley pretended was a story.

*Go east when the moon thins.*

The moon was thinning. Four nights of waning left before the thirteenth turned, and somewhere in the capital, Captain Serah Voss was sharpening her certainty against the whetstone of three arrested innocents, and the valley's mood was turning ugly as milk in heat.

Theo sat up. He picked up the shard, and this time it lay quiet in his palm, warm as a held hand, and he looked at it for a long time—at the fragment of a treaty that had eaten a moment of its own signing, at the blurred face of a woman who walked like him, at the cut-out hollow inside himself shaped exactly like her.

Then he got up, dressed properly, tucked the shard into the lining of his coat where his knife used to go, and went out into the blue hour to find Ivy—because there was a house east of the stones with ivy growing over its bell, and a lady there who had lost a sister, and Theo Joubert, who had spent ten years selling other people's memories, had just discovered the largest theft of all had been committed against him.

He wanted his childhood back.

And he intended to learn, from whoever had loved his mother longest, exactly who had taken it—and what else, in the wiping of the mirrors, they had taken with it.
