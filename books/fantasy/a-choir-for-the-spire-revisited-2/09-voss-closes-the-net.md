## 9. Voss Closes the Net

Captain Serah Voss had learned long ago that cruelty done reluctantly was still cruelty, and that the only mercy worth offering was speed. She held that thought like a coin bitten between her teeth as she stood in the market square of Vaelmoor and watched her soldiers bind three strangers to the posts.

The first was a tanner's widow named Odile Ferran, sixty years old, whose crime—according to the charm-sense of Corporal Bevan, who had never once in his career been right—was a "flicker" he'd felt near her stall. The second was a drover boy of perhaps sixteen, taken off the road at dawn because he'd flinched when asked his name. The third was a miller who had once, years ago, been seen talking to a peddler from beyond the stones. None of them were gifted. Voss knew it the way she knew rain coming: in her back teeth, in the old scar along her ribs where a warlock's fire had kissed her and left a coldness that never warmed. Her sense reached for them and found nothing but the ordinary static of human fear, which felt, from the inside, almost identical to magic if you wanted it to.

She did not want it to. That was the discipline. That had always been the discipline.

"Captain." Bevan hovered at her shoulder, young and eager, his gloves too new. "The crowd's gathering."

"I can hear it."

They could all hear it. The square filled the way water fills a hull—not all at once, but with a terrible patience. Bakers still in their flour. Children hoisted onto shoulders so they could see better, because their parents had not yet decided whether this was a thing to hide them from or a thing to teach them. An old man crossed himself with the wrong hand, the old way, the way people had prayed before the crown told them how, and then corrected himself, glancing around to see who had noticed.

Voss noticed everything. It was her gift, the only one she'd ever been given, and it had been ground into her by twenty years of service until it shone like a parade boot.

The proclamation hung on the chapel door behind her, its wax seal fat and red as a wound. *All persons touched by the gift shall be surrendered to the crown before the thirteenth moon turns.* Below it, someone had already scratched a word into the wood. She'd read it that morning before the sun cleared the ridge: *liars*. Small letters, cut quick and shallow, the kind of thing done in the dark by someone who would deny it in the light. She had not ordered it sanded out. Let them see she read what they wrote. Let them know she was watching even their small rebellions.

"Read it again," she said to Bevan.

He unrolled the second copy, the one meant for voices instead of doors, and read the proclamation aloud in his parade-ground baritone while the square went quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet—like a held breath, like snow before an avalanche. Voss let her gaze travel the faces. This was the true work, not the arrests. The arrests were theater; the watching was the war.

A woman near the well whose hands wouldn't stop moving, pleating and unpleating her apron. A man who looked at the bound prisoners with something worse than hatred—with relief, the ugly gratitude of a man glad it isn't him, already rehearsing how he'll tell the story tonight. A girl of maybe nineteen standing too still among the shifting crowd, dark-haired, watchful, her eyes moving over the soldiers with the flat assessment of someone counting exits. Voss marked her. Not a flicker of magic about her—nothing—but stillness like that in a crowd like this was its own kind of tell. People who stood that still were either very brave or very guilty, and in Voss's experience the two wore the same face.

Bevan finished reading. Silence. Then the tanner's widow began to weep, quietly, the way women weep when they have practiced it all their lives, and something in the square shifted—the crowd's breath going out, the first mutterings starting up like flies.

"You're mistaken," Odile Ferran said, and her voice cracked on the second word. "I've lived here forty years. I buried my husband in your war. I'm no witch."

"No," Voss said, and heard her own voice come out level and cold and hated it, and made herself continue anyway. "I don't believe you are."

Confusion rippled through the nearest listeners. Bevan shot her a look. She kept her eyes on the widow.

"Then why—" The old woman's chin came up, wet-eyed fury replacing terror for one bright moment. "Then why am I tied to a post?"

"Because the law requires suspects," Voss said, "and I have none. Because the last time the gift hid in a valley like this one, it grew there for eleven years before anyone noticed, and when it finally showed itself, it showed itself as fire, and I carried the survivors out in my arms until my arms stopped working." She let the silence sit. She did not raise her voice. She never raised her voice; the story did the work. "I am not here because I enjoy binding grandmothers to posts, madam. I am here because every day I wait is a day the real ones grow bolder, and every person in this square who shields them is a plank in the floor they're standing on."

"The miller talked to a *peddler*," someone called from the crowd—a man's voice, aggrieved. "That's your evidence? We all talk to peddlers."

"We do," Voss agreed, turning toward the voice. "And most of us are not hiding anything. But guilt hides, and hiding looks like ordinary life, and ordinary life is exactly where it will be." She paused, and then gave them the thing she'd come to give, the thing she'd rehearsed on the road, the thing that tasted like ash even as she said it. "Surrender the gifted to me, and these three walk free within the hour. That is the bargain. It does not change. It will not change. Bring me what I came for, and no one else need stand where they're standing."

She watched the bargain land. Watched it move through the crowd like a current under ice—you couldn't see it, but you could see where it went: the little glances between neighbors, the recalculations, the way a woman looked at the house next door and then quickly away. Somewhere in that crowd, Voss knew, someone was already deciding. Someone was remembering a cousin's strange luck, a lodger who paid too promptly, a child born during the wrong season with eyes the wrong color. The crown's arithmetic was simple and it always worked: you did not need to find the guilty. You needed only to make innocence expensive enough that people started selling each other to afford it.

It was monstrous. She knew it was monstrous. She had known it since her second year of service, since the village of Harrow's Bend, since the woman she'd arrested who had turned out to be nothing but a healer with herb-knowledge and a stutter, since Voss had signed the transfer order that sent her to the Spire's cells anyway because the paperwork was already stamped and refusing would have meant an inquiry, and inquiries ended careers, and her career was the only wall between men like Bevan and the people in their keeping.

If I don't hold the leash, she told herself, the same telling she did every morning, a rosary without a god, someone worse will take it. If I do the arithmetic, at least I round down. At least I count the children twice.

The drover boy was crying now, silently, tears cutting tracks through road dust. The miller had gone past fear into a gray absence, staring at nothing. And the widow Odile Ferran had stopped weeping and was looking at Voss with an expression the captain had seen before and would see again until she died: not hatred. Pity. That was the worst one. Hatred you could carry; pity got inside you and nested.

* * *

By evening the mood of the valley had turned, and Voss felt it turn the way sailors feel weather.

Her soldiers had put the three prisoners in the tithe barn at the edge of the square rather than the cells below the inn—there wasn't room, and besides, visible suffering worked better than hidden suffering; that was not cynicism, it was doctrine, written in the crown's own handbooks. From the inn's common room, where she took her supper apart from her men, she could see the barn's lit window, and she could hear the valley thinking.

They thought in the doorway clusters. They thought in the taproom, where conversation died whenever a soldier passed and resumed in a lower key afterward, like water finding a new level. They thought loudest of all in the silences—the missing greetings, the shutters closed an hour early, the way the innkeeper's wife set down Voss's plate without meeting her eyes and then, catching herself, smiled too much.

"They're saying the widow Ferran's granddaughter was born with a caul," the innkeeper told her that night, unprompted, wiping a table that was already clean. He was a broad, careful man named Aldous Pell, and he had the tired competence of someone who survived every regime by being useful to all of them. "Old story. Twenty years back. Nobody thought anything of it at the time."

"And now?"

"And now they're thinking of it." He set the rag down. Looked at her, finally, and there was no fear in his face, which was rarer than magic and more dangerous. "You want to know what I think, Captain? I think you tied three innocent people to posts today and called it mercy, and tomorrow morning this valley is going to start bringing you names. And the day after that it'll bring you neighbors. And by week's end some man will hand over his brother-in-law over a fence line or a debt, and he'll sleep fine after, and you'll have taught him that he can." He picked the rag back up. "I've seen crowns come and go. The valleys remember longer."

Voss finished her wine. "Is that sedition, Master Pell?"

"That's a man talking to the only person in this valley with the power to stop it."

"It's already stopped," she said. "It stopped the day the Accord was signed. That's what the Accord *is*—the border stones, the exile, the peace. Magic stays outside, we stay inside, nobody burns. All I'm doing is making sure it holds."

"Is it?" He nodded toward the window, toward the dark hills where the border stones stood somewhere past sight. "Holding? Then why send hunters? Why the thirteenth moon? Why you?" He shook his head before she could answer. "You don't send soldiers to guard a door that's locked. You send them when you think somebody's found the key."

She lay awake that night in the inn's best room—which was to say the least bad room—and listened to the valley breathe. Wind in the eaves. A dog somewhere, barking at nothing with the steady rhythm of boredom. And beneath it, fainter, the sound she'd been trained to listen for and had spent twenty years pretending she didn't dread: the sound of a community organizing itself around a secret. Not the guilty ones. The innocent ones. Fear did that—it welded people together faster than love ever managed, because love required trust and fear required only company.

Somewhere in Vaelmoor, she thought, someone knows. Someone has always known. There was always one—the quiet nephew, the friend who covered, the lover who lied beautifully. Her job was to make that person's loyalty cost more than they could pay.

She turned her face to the wall. On the other side of the valley, in a tithe barn, three innocent people waited for a bargain she had no intention of breaking and every intention of using.

*Forgive me*, she thought, in the direction of no one, of everyone, of the woman from Harrow's Bend whose name she still refused to remember. *This is what the wall costs. Someone has to be the wall.*

* * *

On the third morning, the names began.

Not many. Never many—that was the trick of it, the slow poison drip of it. A fisherman reported that the ferryman's daughter "had a way with nets." A grandmother, trembling, gave up the name of a midwife in Lowfield who had delivered too many healthy babies during the fever years. A boy of twelve, pushed forward by his father's hand heavy on his neck, whispered that the reeve's son could make birds land on his finger.

Voss wrote them all down in her ledger in her small, square hand. She questioned no one roughly. She promised nothing she wouldn't honor. And at noon she released Odile Ferran, the drover boy, and the miller, exactly as sworn, and watched them stumble out of the tithe barn into a crowd that did not cheer them and did not comfort them and mostly did not look at them—because looking at them might mean owing them, and the valley had begun learning what things cost.

The widow stopped in front of Voss on her way across the square. Up close she smelled of straw and old tears, and her wrists were ringed raw where the ropes had been.

"You'll get your witches," she said quietly. "You'll get them. And you won't know them from us, and that's the curse you've bought, Captain. You've made this whole valley lie. And liars—" her voice caught, steadied, "—liars are so hard to catch."

Then she walked away, straight-backed, and Voss stood in the cold sun and understood that she had just been cursed more thoroughly than any hex could manage.

That night, reviewing her ledger by candlelight, she came to the last name of the day's harvest, given by a carter who'd come in sweating and wouldn't sit down: a memory-broker. Maren, of the shop by the chapel lane. The carter claimed Maren dealt in stolen things—grief lifted from travelers, secrets peeled from their owners like fruit from the rind. The carter claimed he'd sold his dead wife's last words to her himself, and woken the next morning unable to remember her voice, and only then understood what he'd traded, and had been dying of it slowly ever since.

Voss read the entry twice. Gift-work, if true. Theft of the mind, which was the worst kind—the kind that didn't leave marks, the kind the crown's laws barely had words for yet. And a broker meant buyers, and buyers meant a network, and a network meant the gift was not hiding in Vaelmoor but *trading* there, comfortable, established, woven into the town's commerce like rot through a beam.

She closed the ledger. Downstairs, the taproom noise rose and fell, and through the window the thirteenth moon was rising over the ridge, one phase short of full, one phase short of eclipse, hanging over the valley like a coin someone was deciding whether to spend.

"Corporal Bevan," she said.

He appeared in the doorway, fastening his sword belt. "Captain?"

"At first light, we visit a broker." She snuffed the candle. In the dark, the moonlight lay across the floorboards like a blade laid down but not yet picked up. "And afterward, I want the girl found. The one who stands too still in crowds. Put word out—I'll pay for her name."

She did not yet know why the girl mattered. She only knew that in twenty years of hunting, she had learned that the ones who watched the watchers were always closer to the center than anyone guessed—and that somewhere in this lying valley, the thread had already begun to pull.
