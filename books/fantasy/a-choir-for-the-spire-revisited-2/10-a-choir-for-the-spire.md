## 10. A Choir for the Spire

Ivy had learned, in the capital, that the most dangerous conversations were the ones held in beautiful rooms.

The memory-brokers of Vaelmoor kept their shops like shrines—candles guttering behind smoked glass, shelves of little lead-lined boxes each labeled in a careful hand: *a first kiss, a mother's voice, the smell of rain on a specific street in a city that no longer existed.* Maren's shop was the finest of them, all dark wood and hushed lamplight, and it was there, three nights after the dead drop, that Ivy laid the shard on the counter between them and said, "We have to get this out of the valley."

Theo went very still.

He had a way of going still that most people would mistake for calm. Ivy had spent enough weeks watching him work the market crowds to know better. Stillness was his armor. When Theo Joubert stopped moving, it was because something inside him had started screaming.

"No," he said.

"You haven't even heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. The answer is no." He reached for the shard.

Ivy put her hand flat over it first. The metal was cold under her palm, colder than the shop's air justified, and she felt—she was almost sure she felt—a faint pulse against her skin, like a second heartbeat keeping time slightly out of step with her own. She had carried it from the drop site wrapped in three layers of wool and she had dreamed of singing every night since. Not her own songs. Old ones. Round songs, the kind sung by many voices weaving in and out of each other, in a language she didn't speak but somehow knew the shape of.

She hadn't told Theo about the dreams. She wasn't sure why. Perhaps because telling him would make it real, and real things had a way of getting people burned in Vaelmoor these days.

"Maren," she said instead, "you told me once you'd moved memories for buyers beyond the stones."

Maren, who had been pretending to inventory a shelf of griefs with the absorbed attention of a woman hoping to be elsewhere, sighed through her nose. "I told you that when I was drunk on plum wine and flattered by your attention, girl, which I now regret."

"Then tell me sober. Please." Ivy kept her hand on the shard. "There are singers at the Spire. The memory-choir. You know them."

Maren's eyes flicked to the window—to the street beyond, where the proclamation still hung nailed to the chapel door, its ink refreshed twice since the thirteenth moon began to wane. *All gifted persons must be surrendered.* Below it, someone had chalked a crude drawing of a hanging man, and below that, in a different hand, someone else had scrubbed at it with a wet rag until only a gray ghost remained. The whole town looked like that lately, Ivy thought. A thing written, a thing crossed out, neither quite gone.

"I know of them," Maren said finally. "Every broker knows of them. They're how truth travels past the border stones, if you've the coin and the nerve. Singers who take a memory into themselves and carry it in harmony with others, so no single throat holds the whole of it. Tear one singer out of the choir and the song survives in the rest. Clever. Old magic. Older than the Accord, some say." Her gaze dropped to Ivy's hand, to the shape beneath it. "And you're thinking to give *that* to a choir."

"It can't stay here."

"It can't *travel*, either. Do you imagine the hunters don't watch the roads? Captain Voss has eyes on every cart leaving this valley, and that woman smells a lie the way I smell cheap incense." Maren's voice dropped. "Whatever you two are into—and I don't want to know, I have a ledger full of reasons not to want to know—you're playing with fire that doesn't burn wood. It burns *names*. Whole houses. There are people alive today only because the world forgot they existed. You start moving war-secrets across borders and the forgetting stops working."

"The forgetting is exactly what's killing people," Ivy said. "Lowfield hasn't happened yet. Maybe it doesn't have to."

The words came out harder than she intended, and she saw them land. Maren turned away, busied herself with the candles, snuffed two of them so the shop grew dimmer, more private. Theo hadn't spoken again. He stood by the door with his hood back and his hands loose at his sides, and he watched Ivy with an expression she was beginning to catalog the way she'd once cataloged the faces at court—notices of mood, small shifts of weather. Right now the weather was storm-still.

* * *

They argued the whole walk home, which is to say, Ivy argued and Theo walked.

Vaelmoor at night was a town holding its breath. Lamplight leaked yellow through shutters and died a foot from the doors. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse and was silenced by a thrown boot. The mountains stood black against a sky scoured down to its bones—the thirteenth moon hung low and wrong-colored, a coin rubbed thin, shedding light like a wound weeps. Every night it was thinner. Every night the whole valley seemed to lean toward the eclipse the way a crowd leans toward a scaffold.

"We take it to the Spire," Ivy said. "The choir sings it to the exiles. Ashfall gets their fragment back, the Accord renews or breaks or whatever it does, and none of it happens here. No hunters burning hamlets. No children in cellars. Theo. It's the only plan that keeps everyone safe."

"And how do we get it to the Spire?" His voice was flat. Tired. "It's a month's ride to the coast, then a ship, then the Spire sits on a rock in the sea that you can only reach if the mages *want* you there. And that's before we discuss the fact that I can't cross the border stones."

"You could try."

He stopped walking.

They were on the lane above the tannery, where the smell of lime and hides rose up thick enough to taste, and the moonlight lay along the mud like spilled milk. Theo turned and looked at her, and for a moment she saw past the stillness to the thing underneath, and it frightened her more than any shout could have. He looked like a man being asked to cut off his own hand and call it generosity.

"You don't understand what the stones are," he said quietly. "You've lived inside them your whole life. Everything you are fits here because here is the only place your kind of life is allowed. Out there—" He flexed his fingers, staring at them as though they belonged to a stranger. "My gift works inside the valley. Inside the place where magic goes to die. Do you understand what that means? It means I'm not carrying magic. I *am* something the stones can't touch. And I don't know what happens to a thing like that when it crosses over. I don't know if I come apart. I don't know if I become—" He stopped himself. Swallowed. "I don't know anything except that everyone I've ever protected is behind those stones, and you're asking me to walk away from them with a war-talisman in my pocket."

"Not away. *Through.* You come back."

"Do I?" Something cracked in his voice, just a hairline. "Ivy, I steal memories. That's what I am. It's ugly and it's small and I've made my peace with it because it keeps bread on tables and keeps Voss from looking twice at the people I love. But the moment I step past those stones, I stop being a thief and become a courier. A piece on a board. And pieces don't come home. Pieces get spent."

"That's not—"

"Ondine Ashfall," he said. "House of Ashfall. You read the ledger same as I did. Those names aren't customers, Ivy, they're *creditors*. Someone in the exile city has been waiting for that shard for twenty years, and when it arrives, do you honestly believe they'll let the man who carried it just stroll back across the border? The Accord holds because nothing crosses. If I cross carrying the one thing that can end it—" He shook his head. "They'll never open the door again. Either direction."

The mud sucked at Ivy's boots. She wrapped her arms around herself against the cold that came off the mountain, and hated, suddenly and completely, how much she loved this stupid, stubborn, careful man who had taught her that invisibility was a craft and then made her complicit in it.

"Then we don't send you," she said. "That's the whole point of the choir, Theo. We don't need a courier. We need voices."

* * *

She'd found the reference in Maren's back room, in a ledger older than the others—a slim volume bound in gray leather, its pages soft as cloth, recording sales from before Ivy was born. Most of it was the usual inventory. But near the end, in fading ink:

*Sold to the Choir of the Spire, via the blind ferryman at Coldwater: one memory, sealed, contents unknown to seller. Payment deferred until delivery. Note — the choir takes nothing that is a copy. They will know.*

"They will know," Ivy repeated now, sitting on the floor of the loft above the cooper's yard where she lodged, the old ledger open across her knees, the shard wrapped in wool in her lap pulsing its slow off-beat heart. Theo sat across from her on an upturned barrel, elbows on his knees, and the candle between them threw his shadow huge against the rafters.

"So we bring them a copy," he said. "A forgery. You're good at those."

"The choir takes nothing that is a copy. They'll know." She tapped the page. "And even if we fooled them—which we won't—what then? The exiles get a fake, the Accord fails, the war comes back, and it comes back *here* first, because this valley is where everyone decided to hide instead of fight. Theo, listen to me." She leaned forward. "I fled the capital because they wanted me to name my friends and I wouldn't. I've been running from that choice ever since—hiding in a valley where nobody asks questions because nobody wants answers. But the questions found us anyway. Voss found us. Lowfield is coming whether we move or not. The only variable left is *when*, and *who pays*."

"And the answer is me. It's always me."

"No." She heard her own voice sharpen, and let it. "The answer is *everyone*, shared out thin, like the choir does it. One singer carries a phrase, another carries the harmony, and no single throat holds enough truth to hang. That's the design. That's the whole genius of it. So we break the shard's burden into pieces too. I'll carry part. Maren carries part, if we can buy her nerve. Half the choir of Vaelmoor's underworld, each holding a scrap of a song none of us understands alone. And the singers at the Spire reassemble it on the far side, where Ashfall can hear it, and the anchor—" she gestured at him, at all of him, "—never has to leave home."

For a long moment Theo said nothing. The candle flame leaned and righted. Down in the yard, the cooper's dog turned three times and lay down with a groan.

"It's clever," he admitted at last, and the word sounded like it cost him. "It's the kind of clever that gets people killed slowly instead of quickly. You'd be handing pieces of a war-secret to brokers, Ivy. Brokers sell. That's what they do. What happens when Voss offers Maren enough gold—or enough silence—for one little scrap?"

"Maren hates the crown."

"Everyone hates the crown until the crown knocks."

"And everyone betrays everyone until they don't!" It burst out of her louder than she meant, echoing off the rafters, and she pressed on before the silence could close over it. "I wrote a letter, Theo. Back in the capital, before I ran—I drafted a letter trading names for my family's pardon. I never sent it. I burned it. But I *wrote* it, and I know exactly how heavy a pen gets when you're scared enough. So don't stand there telling me people can't be trusted with dangerous truths. I'm trusting *you* with mine every day. You're trusting me with yours. That's how it starts. That's the only way anything has ever started."

Theo looked at her for a long time. In the candlelight his face seemed younger and older at once, worn smooth in strange places, rough in stranger ones. A man built out of other people's losses, standing in a room with a girl built out of other people's names.

"If it fails," he said finally, "if one scrap reaches the wrong ears—if Voss so much as *hears* the word 'choir'—"

"Then we're no worse off than we are tonight, with a hunter in the valley and a countdown in the sky." Ivy closed the ledger. Under her hands, the shard pulsed, and for a moment—just a moment—she thought she heard it. Faintly. Many voices, far away, singing in round, patient as tide. Waiting to be carried.

Theo must have seen something change in her face, because he frowned. "What?"

"Nothing." She tucked the shard deeper into the wool. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow I talk to Maren about Coldwater, and the blind ferryman, and what a deferred payment buys these days."

"Ivy."

She paused at the ladder.

"I'm not saying yes," Theo said. "I'm saying I haven't said no."

From him, she knew, that was a landslide. She climbed down into the dark feeling lighter than she had since the dead drop—and it was only halfway home, boots splashing through the frozen ruts, that she realized the singing hadn't stopped. It was still there, faint under the wind, under her own heartbeat, and it was closer than it had been in the loft.

As if the shard were learning her rhythm.

As if it were already choosing which voice to hide inside.
