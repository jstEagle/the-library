## 12. Confession at the Border Stones

The frost came early that year, silvering the border stones like a congregation of penitents bowed in a row. Theo had walked this line since he was old enough to walk at all—past the first stone with its crown of lichen, past the fourth where someone long dead had carved a bird that no longer looked like anything—and never once had he brought another soul.

Now Ivy stood beside him in the blue hour before dawn, her breath smoking, her hands jammed into the pockets of a coat she had stolen from him without asking. She had earned it, he supposed. You could not blackmail a man into partnership and then complain about the terms.

"You've been strange," she said, "even for you."

"I'm always strange."

"You've been *wrong*-strange." She turned to face him. In the half-light her eyes were the color of river ice, and they did not waver. "Since the shard. Since you touched it and your hands shook for two days. I've watched you lie to brokers, lie to hunters, lie to me with a smile so smooth it could butter bread. This isn't lying. This is something chewing on you from inside."

Theo looked out past the stones, to where the valley ended and the world beyond began—a fold of grey hills, a smear of forest, nothing remarkable at all except that everything remarkable was forbidden there. Magic died at these stones. Every child in Vaelmoor learned it before they learned their letters: cross the line and whatever gift you carried would gutter out like a candle dropped in a well. The Accord had seen to that. The Accord, signed in moonlight by kings too tired to keep killing each other, had drawn a circle around the valley and said *nothing crosses here*.

And his gift worked here anyway. Had always worked here. He had told himself a hundred comfortable lies about why—the stones were old, maybe they'd weakened; maybe his talent was small enough to slip under the Accord's notice like a mouse beneath a door. He had built his whole careful invisible life on top of those lies the way you build a house on frozen ground, never once looking down.

Until the shard had sung against his palm like a struck bell finding its twin.

"Ivy," he said. His voice came out rusted. He cleared his throat and tried again. "I need to tell you something, and when I'm done you're either going to understand why I've never told anyone, or you're going to run back to the inn and decide you never knew me. There's no middle."

"Try me. I grew up in a house full of courtiers. I can hold almost any truth without dropping it."

"Not this one."

She folded her arms. "Then stop performing and say it."

He laughed despite himself—a short, unhappy sound that the frost swallowed whole. Then he walked to the nearest border stone and laid his bare hand flat against its cold shoulder, the way a man might touch the wall of a house he was about to confess he'd burned.

"Do you know what the Accord actually is?" he asked. "Not what the priests say. Not the pretty version with the doves and the bells. The real one."

"A treaty. The mages lost, the crown won, everyone agreed magic stays outside the valley and soldiers stay inside it. The Moonlit Accord. It's the reason there's been no war for sixty years." She paused. "It's also the reason there's a Spire full of exiles who hate us, so I'm aware the pretty version has cracks."

"It's not a treaty." He kept his hand on the stone. Beneath his palm he could feel it—that faint hum he had known all his life and mistaken for imagination, the way you mistake the pulse in your own ears for the sound of the sea. "Treaties are written on paper. Paper burns. Kings die, heirs forget, borders drift. No treaty lasts sixty years untouched, Ivy. But the Accord hasn't drifted an inch. Magic still dies exactly here, exactly along this line, on every day of every year. Do you know why?"

She didn't answer. Her breath had gone shallow; he could see it in the quickening of her chest, the way a liar's body betrays her before her mouth does. He pressed on, because if he stopped now he never would begin again.

"Because it's not paper. It's a memory. The largest memory ever taken—the signing itself, witnessed by hundreds, bound into a single fragment so the vow could never be forgotten or forged or quietly amended. That's the shard we found. That's what Maren sold to strangers across the ledger. The Accord is a remembered promise, held outside every mind, so that no one alive can unremember it." He turned to look at her fully. "But a memory needs somewhere to live while it holds the line. A vessel. An anchor. Something set into the border itself, at the moment of the signing, to keep the vow rooted in place."

The wind moved through the dead bracken behind them, a dry whispering like a congregation shuffling in pews.

"Theo," Ivy said carefully, "what are you telling me?"

"My mother went into labor on the night the Accord was signed. Right here. On this exact stretch of ground—my father told me once we were camped near the stones, refugees from the fighting, too late to move on. She gave birth as the mages sang the vow into the shard and drove it into the border." His voice cracked and he hated it, hated the nakedness of it, but there was no way through except straight ahead. "The singers needed an anchor born under the vow. A life begun at the same instant as the promise, so the two would be braided together forever. They didn't ask her permission. There wasn't time. And so—" He made himself finish it, made himself say the words aloud for the first time in twenty-six years. "So I am the anchor. The Accord lives in me. That's why my gift works inside the valley. I don't cross the rule because I *am* the rule."

* * *

For a long moment Ivy said nothing at all, and Theo discovered that silence, after a confession, is the loudest thing in the world.

He heard in it everything he had spent his life dreading: the recalculation, the re-pricing, the moment a person who thought she knew your worth discovers the figure was wrong all along. He had stolen enough memories of betrayal to know its texture from the inside—the cold spreading bloom of it, the way the betrayer watches the betrayed's face and sees their own reflection change there. He stood with his hand on the stone and waited to become a stranger to her.

"That's why you shake near the shard," she said at last. Her voice was very quiet. "It's not resonating with your gift. It's resonating with *you*. You're two halves of the same vow."

"Yes."

"And Voss." Ivy's head came up sharply, ice-eyes suddenly fierce. "If the hunters ever understood what you are—if the crown learned the Accord has an anchor walking around in a stolen coat eating stolen bread—they wouldn't arrest you, Theo. They'd *use* you. Or they'd kill you and let the Accord rot, because a dead anchor means magic dies everywhere eventually, which is what half the court has wanted since the war." She grabbed his sleeve. "You have to run. Tonight. Over the stones, out of the valley—"

"And go where?" He gently freed his arm. "Ivy. Think about what I just said. If I leave, does the Accord weaken? Does it break? I don't know. Nobody knows. I've spent my whole life afraid to find out, because testing it means either abandoning the only thing standing between this valley and the last war—or discovering I can't leave at all, that I'm less a man than a nail hammered into the world." He laughed again, and this time there was nothing in it but exhaustion. "Do you understand? I've never even crossed the line. Not once. Twenty-six years, and I've stood at this border more times than I can count, and I've never been able to make myself step over, because some part of me has always known that I'm not visiting these stones."

"No," Ivy said slowly. "You're guarding them."

"I'm *made* of them."

* * *

They walked the line together, north along the row of stones, because Theo found he could not stand still while his skin still crawled with the feeling of being seen—truly seen—for the first time. Frost crunched under their boots. Somewhere behind them a crow complained about the dawn. The ordinary sounds of it nearly undid him; the world insisting on continuing, indifferent to the fact that he had just handed over the only secret he had.

"You stole memories," Ivy said as they walked. "All those years. Grief, guilt, secrets—you said yourself you took them clean, left the donors hollowed but functional."

"I did."

"But you couldn't steal your own childhood, could you?" She stopped and faced him. "Because it was already gone. Taken at birth to make room for the Accord. That's the trade nobody told your mother about. They didn't just use you, Theo—they *emptied* you first. That's why you take memories. Why you can't stop taking them. You're a man with a hole where a life should be, filling it with other people's leftovers."

The words landed like a blade slid between ribs—precise, surgical, true. Theo stood very still. The frost on the stones gleamed. Far off, the first chimney-smoke of Vaelmoor rose thin and grey against the brightening sky, and he thought of the inn, and Maren's shop, and Lowfield, and every face that had ever handed him their worst hour for coin, and he felt the full weight of what Ivy had just said settle onto him like snow.

"I remember fragments," he managed. "A smell of woodsmoke and wet wool. Someone singing—not a song, just humming, low, the way you do when you think no one hears. I used to think it was a dream I kept having. Now I think it's her. What was left over after they took the rest." He swallowed. "There was a memory in the shard, Ivy. My mother's. I saw her face for the first time in my life inside someone else's sealed keepsake. She was laughing at something my father said, and she had a scar through one eyebrow, and I have spent every night since trying to grieve a woman I cannot remember ever meeting. That's what they did. That's what I am. Not a man with a gift. A wound wearing a man's shape, holding up a treaty."

Ivy reached out and took both his hands. Hers were cold as his own, chapped from the road, steady as bedrock.

"Listen to me," she said. "I fled the capital rather than name my friends to men who burn children. I know what it costs to be made into an instrument by people with power. So hear this clearly: they used you. They did not *make* you. Whatever the singers intended when they anchored their vow to a newborn, you have spent twenty-six years being something they never planned for—a thief, yes, and a liar, and a coward on your worst days—but also the man who went into Lowfield's ashes and took a dying child's last moments so she wouldn't die screaming. The Accord didn't do that. *You* did that. Don't you dare confuse the two."

Theo's vision blurred. He blinked hard and pretended it was the wind.

"There's more," he said hoarsely. "Of course there's more. There's the part that matters."

"Of course there is. Go on."

"The shard. We found it, but Maren already listed buyers—the House of Ashfall wants it, and God knows who else. And now I know what it is, and what I am, and I understand something I wish I didn't." He drew a breath that hurt all the way down. "The Accord can be renewed. The choir could sing it fresh, bind it new, if the anchor consents. That's the rite. That's why the thirteenth moon matters, why the eclipse matters—it's the window when the vow can be rewritten. But it requires me, Ivy. Freely. Willingly. Standing at these stones with the fragment in my hand and giving myself to it all over again. Another lifetime. Another generation of valley children taught that magic dies at the line, and exiles teaching their children that the valley is the enemy. Another sixty years of hunters and burnings and girls running away from capitals because they wouldn't betray their friends." His hands trembled in hers. "Or the Accord breaks. And I don't know what happens then. Maybe nothing. Maybe everything. Maybe the war comes back with interest."

"So choose renewal," Ivy said. "It's peace, Theo. It's the safe road."

"Is it?" He pulled his hands free and gestured at the valley behind them—at the smoke, the fields, the chapel spire catching the first light. "Renewal means Voss keeps her commission. Renewal means the next gifted child born in Lowfield gets burned anyway, because the hunt doesn't stop just because the border holds. Renewal means I stand here and *consent* to all of it, forever, with my eyes open this time instead of a newborn's shut ones." His voice broke entirely now, and he let it. "I don't know how to choose that. I don't know if I can. And I don't know what happens to any of us if I refuse."

Ivy was silent again, but it was a different silence now—not judgment, but weight-bearing. He watched her look at the stones, at the hills beyond them, at the smoke of home, and he realized she was doing the same arithmetic he had been doing alone his entire life, and that having her do it beside him changed the sum somehow. Not the answer. Just the carrying of it.

"The eclipse," she said finally. "How many days?"

"Nineteen."

"And Ashfall wants the shard. And Voss wants you, though she doesn't yet know why she wants you." Ivy turned back to him, and there was something in her face he had never seen there before—not the sharp marketplace cunning, not the nobleman's daughter's armored amusement, but plain, uncovered fear, offered to him as a gift because he had offered her his own first. "Nineteen days, then. We'd better decide what kind of anchor you're going to be."

Theo opened his mouth to answer—

and stopped, because down the line of stones, perhaps three hundred yards south, a figure had appeared in the frost-grey light. Small at this distance. Unhurried. Walking the border with the easy stride of someone who knew exactly where the line ran, and had come specifically to stand upon it.

Even at that distance, even through the cold morning air, Theo knew the coat. Knew the posture. Knew the way Captain Serah Voss paused at each stone and rested her gloved hand on it, lightly, almost tenderly, as though greeting an old acquaintance.

She had not seen them yet. The light was wrong; the bracken was high.

"She's mapping the border," Ivy breathed. "Why would a witch-hunter map the border?"

Theo watched Voss lay her palm against the seventh stone—the one with the ruined bird—and felt, with sickening certainty, the faint hum of the Accord pass between hunter and stone like a whisper passed between conspirators.

"Because somebody told her," he said, "that the Accord has an anchor."
