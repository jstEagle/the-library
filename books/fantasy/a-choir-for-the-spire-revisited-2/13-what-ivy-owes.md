## 13. What Ivy Owes

The confession had been sitting in Ivy's chest for eleven days, and it weighed exactly as much as a letter.

She knew because she'd written it. She knew the weight of paper, the weight of ink, the weight of a name pressed into wax by a signet ring she no longer wore. She had carried that letter through three towns in her mind before she ever carried it in her satchel, and even now—now that Theo had given her his mother's face, now that he had told her about the border stones, about being born on the seam of two worlds, about being the Accord's living anchor—even now it sat inside her like a swallowed stone, working its way down.

He was asleep by the fire in the ruined mill above the river, the shard wrapped in wool beside his heart, and Ivy sat with her back against the millstone and watched the embers breathe and told herself: *tonight*. She had been telling herself *tonight* for eleven nights.

The trouble with lying, she had learned early, was not the fear of being caught. The trouble was that every truth you withheld grew heavier, and there was only so much a person could carry before her spine learned to curve around the weight like a question mark. Ivy had been a question mark since she left the capital. She was tired of it.

She thought about how she would say it. There were a hundred ways, and she rehearsed them all the way you rehearse anything you dread: standing at the chapel door of your own conscience, hand raised to knock, unable to choose between honesty's blunt instruments and cowardice's silk. *Theo, I have something to tell you.* Too gentle; he'd brace himself. *Theo, I betrayed you before I ever met you.* Too cruel; he'd flinch, and she couldn't bear the flinch. *It doesn't matter anymore, the letter never arrived* — except she didn't know that, did she? She didn't know anything except that Captain Serah Voss had come to Vaelmoor asking questions that fit Ivy's letter like a key fits the lock it was cut for.

Because Voss had known things. That was the thing that kept Ivy awake while Theo slept, that turned her stomach sour whenever she let herself think it: Voss had known that a girl of noble blood was hiding in the valley. Had known to look for someone who could name gifted friends. Had asked at the inn, casually, over spiced wine, whether anyone new had come through from the east road in the last season — *a lady's daughter, perhaps, traveling light*. And Ivy had sat there with her hands around a cup of small beer, wearing a dyer's apron and a lie, and had felt the words of her own letter recited back to her across the table like scripture.

She had written: *I am willing to provide the names of persons within the valley who possess the gift, in exchange for a full pardon and the restoration of my family's standing.* She had written it in the third week of hiding, when hunger had hollowed her out and homesickness had done the rest, when her father's face came to her nightly — not angry, which she could have fought, but *disappointed*, which she could not. She had written it and walked to the crossroads post and dropped it into the crown's courier box with hands that shook so badly the letter rattled against the iron slot like a small trapped bird.

And then, three days later, she had caught a thin young man in the market lifting a dying soldier's grief out of the air like a wasp in a jar, and everything had changed.

That was the cruelty of it. Not that she had betrayed strangers — strangers were abstract, and she had told herself they were abstract, gifted people she'd never met whose names she hadn't actually supplied yet, only *offered* to supply, which was almost innocent, which was nearly a victimless crime. No. The cruelty was that she had offered to sell the valley's secrets on Tuesday and fallen into partnership with its best keeper of secrets on Friday, and she had never once, in all the weeks since, found the courage to unsay what she had said.

*Before I knew you,* she practiced silently, watching Theo's chest rise and fall. *I wrote to them before I knew you. It wasn't about you. It was about me. It was about my father's disappointment and my empty stomach and my stupid, stupid pride.*

But it *was* about him, now. That was what made it unforgivable in her own accounting. A letter written in ignorance was a sin; the same letter, left unrecalled once knowledge arrived, became a choice. Every day she had stayed silent was another day she had chosen to let the offer stand. And Voss had come. Voss had come with her charm-sense and her gentle voice and her list of questions, and somewhere in the capital or on the road behind her, Ivy's handwriting was being read aloud in a room lit by tallow candles, and men were nodding, and dispatches were being written, and the thirteenth moon was turning toward its eclipse with terrible punctuality.

The fire settled. A log collapsed into itself and sent up a fistful of sparks, and Theo stirred without waking, turning his cheek toward the warmth the way a plant turns toward light. He looked younger asleep. He looked like someone who had never stolen anything in his life, which was the great joke of him, because he had stolen more than any man in Vaelmoor and carried it more quietly than any thief she'd ever imagined becoming.

*I'm going to tell him,* Ivy thought. *Tonight. Before Voss finds us. Because if he hears it from her first — if he learns it in an interrogation room with his wrists bound and her voice soft as oil — then I will have killed the only honest thing I've ever built.*

She stood. Her knees cracked. Outside, the river ran dark and constant, and the moon hung low over the ridge, one sliver shaved off its edge already, beginning its long surrender to the earth's shadow.

* * *

She woke him gently, with a hand on his shoulder, and he came up out of sleep the way he did everything — quietly, watchfully, a knife of attention already open in his eyes before recognition softened it.

"Nightmare?" he murmured.

"No." She fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "Sit up. I need to tell you something and I need you to hear all of it before you say anything."

He sat up. He pulled the blanket around his shoulders like a cloak, and in the ember-light he suddenly looked less like a thief and more like a penitent at a vigil, and the symmetry of that — *he* the penitent, *she* the confessor — struck her so hard she almost laughed, a small broken sound that escaped before she could stop it.

"That bad?" he said.

"Worse." She wrapped her arms around herself. "You remember the night Voss came to the inn. You remember what she asked me."

"You told me after. She asked about newcomers. About the east road." His eyes narrowed slightly. "Why?"

"Because I wrote to the crown." The words came out flat, dead, like coins dropped on stone. "Six weeks ago. Maybe seven. From the crossroads post. A formal petition under my true name — Ivy Ashcombe, daughter of Lord Edmun Ashcombe of the capital — offering information." Her throat closed and she forced it open. "Offering the names of gifted people in this valley. In exchange for a pardon. For my family and for me."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, an owl called and got no answer.

Theo did not move. That was the worst part, Ivy thought later — not anger, not the flinch she'd dreaded, but this absolute stillness, as though her words had struck him somewhere too deep for the body to know how to bleed from. He just looked at her across the fire with those grey eyes that saw everything, the eyes of a boy who had made invisibility his trade, and waited, because he was fair even when he was breaking, and fairness demanded he let her finish.

"I never sent any names," she said quickly, the words tumbling now, the dam cracked and useless. "I want you to understand that. I never got that far. I offered, and I told myself offering wasn't doing, and then—" her voice wavered and she hated it, hated the wetness climbing into it, "—and then I met you. Three days later. Three days, Theo. And I should have gone back to the post and written a retraction, I should have spent every coin I had buying the letter back from the courier, and instead I told myself the post was gone, the letter was gone, what's done is done, and I let it stand. Every day since. I let it *stand*."

"Why are you telling me now?"

The question was quiet. Not cold — Theo didn't do cold, not really, not with her — but careful, the way a man walks on ice he has decided to trust.

"Because Voss knew about me." Ivy hugged herself tighter. "A lady's daughter, traveling light, from the east road. Those weren't guesses, Theo. Those were details. Details from my letter. Which means it reached someone with authority, which means someone read it and dispatched a witch-hunter captain to a valley of four hundred souls to find one runaway girl who could name names. And every person she's questioned, every door she's kicked in Lowfield, every innocent she's dragged to the chapel square—" her breath hitched, "—that's mine. My letter bought those arrests. My handwriting signed for them. You've been carrying the Accord on your back like Atlas, and I handed the crown the map to find you."

Silence. The river spoke its one long syllable beyond the wall. Theo looked into the fire, and Ivy watched him do the thing she had seen him do in the market a hundred times — that inward turning, that reading of a memory not his own, except this time the memory was hers, laid out on the flagstones between them like a gutted fish.

"Ivy," he said at last.

"Don't." Her voice cracked clean in half. "Don't be kind yet. I haven't finished paying. There's more."

His eyebrows lifted.

"The night you went to Maren's shop — the ledger, the House of Ashfall, all of it — I wasn't keeping watch on the street. Well. I was. But before you came, I went through her desk." Ivy knelt, putting herself level with him across the coals, because this next part deserved eye contact even if it cost her everything. "Maren keeps copies of correspondence. Brokers do — provenance, authentication, whatever excuse brokers use. And there was a letter there, weeks old, unsigned, describing a *girl matching my description* seen asking questions about the Spire's choir in the market town. Someone had reported me, Theo. Before I ever did anything worth reporting. And my first thought — my actual first thought, God forgive me — was not *I'm in danger*. It was *if they're already watching me, my letter can't hurt me anymore*. I felt *relieved*. I used their suspicion as a shield and kept right on lying, and I let you walk into Maren's shop alone believing I was covering the door, and I was, but I was also covering myself."

She ran out of sins. That was the strange thing about confession — you rehearsed it for eleven nights and when the moment came it took four minutes, and afterward there was just silence and the smell of woodsmoke and the terrible ordinary fact of another human being sitting across from you, deciding.

"So," Ivy said. Her voice came out small. "Now you know what I owe. Say something. Curse me. Tell me to go to Voss and turn myself in and take what's coming. Tell me you'll leave tonight without me. Anything but nothing, Theo, please—"

"I'm trying to work out when," he said slowly, "you think I'm going to be surprised."

She blinked. "What?"

He leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving his face into planes and shadow. "Ivy. You're a lord's daughter who fled the capital rather than name her friends to the crown's inquisitors. You survived alone in a strange valley for months on wit and performance. You caught *me* — the invisible man — mid-theft, in a crowded market, on your third day here." He shook his head, and something that was almost a smile touched his mouth, though it never arrived. "People like us don't get found by accident. We get found because we're looking for leverage. I knew you'd come to Vaelmoor carrying something heavy. I just assumed it was guilt. Turns out I was right twice."

"That's it?" Her voice rose despite herself. "That's your response? I sold this valley to save my family's reputation and you're making *jokes*?"

"I'm making the point that I'm not surprised, because surprising isn't the same as forgiving, and I need you to know the difference before I say the rest." He held her gaze. "I'm angry, Ivy. I woke up ten minutes ago and you've already rearranged the entire shape of my life, and I'm furious — at you, at the letter, at the version of events where we might have had a season of peace if you'd burned it in your first week instead of feeding it to the crown. I'm allowed to be. But I also know what a letter written at the bottom of despair looks like, because I've lived in that country my whole life. You didn't betray me. You betrayed a valley full of strangers to save people you loved, and then you fell in love with the strangers, and now you're the one holding the debt. That's not villainy. That's just what being a person costs."

Ivy discovered she was crying. It annoyed her intensely. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand like a child. "My father used to say forgiveness is just arithmetic. You forgive when the ledger balances."

"Your father sounds like a broker."

"He is, essentially." A wet, unwilling laugh escaped her. "That's rather the whole tragedy of my life."

Theo was quiet a moment, turning something over. Then: "The letter. Do you know if it named anyone? Actually named anyone?"

"No. I swear it. I gave my identity and my intent. The names were to come 'upon assurance of terms.' I hadn't—" she steadied herself. "I hadn't chosen them yet. I told myself I'd decide when I knew who was worth selling."

"And now?"

"Now there's no price high enough," she said simply. "That's what I couldn't put in a retraction letter. They wouldn't believe it. Words are cheap; everyone in the capital knows that better than I do. But I can show it." She lifted her chin. "Voss is hunting an anchor. She thinks the anchor is a stranger, some exile agent, maybe a mage sympathizer. She has no idea it's the dyer's quiet nephew who fixes fences. If I go to her—"

"No." It came out fast and hard, and Ivy realized it was the first truly sharp emotion she'd ever heard from him. "Absolutely not."

"—if I go to her," Ivy continued over him, "and give her a false trail — feed her the story she expects, point her at a ghost, buy us time until the eclipse—"

"*No*, Ivy." He was on his feet now, blanket falling, shard-bundle clutched to his chest, and the firelight made his shadow enormous on the mill wall. "You don't get to purchase your absolution with another transaction. That's how you ended up writing that letter in the first place — trading one debt for another, always moving the weight around, never setting it down. You don't owe me a scheme. You owe the truth, and you've paid it. We figure out the rest together, or not at all."

They stared at each other across the dying fire, and Ivy felt something shift in her chest — the stone finally settling into place, not lighter but *rightly placed*, a cornerstone instead of a burden.

"Together," she repeated.

"Together." He exhaled, long and unsteady, and sank back down. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the river filled the silence, and Ivy thought: *this is what honesty buys. Not peace. Just company in the wreckage.*

Then Theo went very still.

Not the stillness of thought. The stillness of listening. His head tilted, the way it did when his gift brushed against something, and his eyes fixed on the black rectangle of the mill doorway, and Ivy felt the temperature of the night change.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"Riders," he breathed. "On the road below. Four of them, maybe five. And one of them—" his jaw tightened, and Ivy saw the color drain from his face in the emberlight, "—one of them is carrying something that feels like the shard. Like a piece of the same song."

Hoofbeats reached them then, muffled by distance, deliberate, unhurried. Coming up the river road toward the mill.

Toward them.

Ivy reached for her boots, and the last thing she saw before the dark swallowed the doorway was Theo wrapping the shard tighter against his heart, his lips moving around a single word she couldn't hear — and she was almost certain it wasn't a prayer.

It was a name.
