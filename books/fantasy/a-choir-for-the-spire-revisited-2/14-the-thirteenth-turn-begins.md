## 14. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The thirteenth moon rose over Vaelmoor like a coin held up to a dying man's eye—thin, silvered, and worth nothing unless you were desperate enough to bite it.

Theo felt the turn coming in his teeth before he saw it anywhere in the sky. For as long as he could remember—and his memory was a house with too many doors, most of them borrowed—the moons had been a kind of arithmetic. Twelve of them, and the year turned clean. The thirteenth was the one that didn't fit, the one that hung in the winter sky like a debt no one had agreed to pay. The old women at the well said the thirteenth moon was when the gods counted what was owed. The brokers in the Spire said it was when memory ran thinnest, easiest to lift, like cream off milk left out overnight.

Theo had always thought both explanations were probably true.

He was counting coins behind the tannery when Ivy found him—not walking toward him, never that; she simply appeared beside him the way she did, as if she'd been leaning against the wall for an hour waiting for him to notice. Her hood was down despite the cold. She'd stopped hiding her face since Lowfield burned, and there was something in that which frightened him more than any proclamation nailed to a chapel door.

"You need to see this," she said, and put a letter in his hand.

It wasn't hers. It was a copy—he could tell by the ink, by the way the crown's clerks pressed their seals twice, once in wax and once in cruelty. It bore the arms of Captain Serah Voss, and beneath the arms, in a clerk's careful hand:

*Received from one Ivy Ashcombe, of the House of Corvane, written in her own blood-oath: that a gifted man dwells in Vaelmoor who trades in stolen memories. Description follows.*

His own description followed. Tall. Dark-haired. A scar on the left thumb from a bread knife, aged eleven. He read it twice because the first time the words refused to mean anything. Someone had told Voss about the scar. Someone had sat across from her in a room that smelled of lamp oil and confessed the shape of him in detail, and the someone had signed it *Ivy Ashcombe*, and the date at the bottom was three weeks old.

Three weeks. Before Lowfield. Before the child. Before everything.

"I wrote it," Ivy said quietly, when he looked up. "Before I knew you. Before any of it. I told you I owed the crown a letter—I didn't tell you I'd already sent it." Her chin was up but her hands weren't steady. "I traded your existence for my family's pardon. That's what I am, Theo. That's what I've been since the day we met."

The tannery yard smelled of lime and rot, and somewhere beyond the wall a dog was barking at the rising moon, and Theo stood very still and discovered that betrayal, when it finally arrives, feels less like a knife than like a door you realize has been open the whole time.

"You let me trust you," he said.

"Yes."

"You let me—" He stopped. He had been about to say *love you*, or something adjacent to it, something they had never said aloud because saying things aloud was how people died in Vaelmoor that season. "You watched me give back the child's memory to her mother. You watched me weep in the ash. And you knew."

"I knew," Ivy said. "And then I spent two weeks trying to steal the original back from Voss's strongbox, if you want the whole ugly truth of it. I couldn't. It's under seal. Blood-oath documents can't be destroyed by the oath-breaker—that's rather the point of them." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the tannery dust. "So instead I'm here. Warning you. Which makes me either the worst traitor in history or the best one, depending on whether you run fast enough."

Above the wall, the thirteenth moon cleared the ridge of the Spire, and every shadow in the yard leaned a little, as if the world had tilted.

"How long do I have?" Theo asked.

"Voss rides for the tannery district tonight. I heard the order given at the garrison—I still have friends among the clerks, God forgive me, I seem to collect them." Ivy took his sleeve, and her grip was fierce. "Theo. Listen to me. There is nowhere inside the stones she cannot reach eventually. You know what's outside them. You know what it will cost."

He knew. Everyone in the valley knew, the way everyone knows fire is hot without ever having touched it. Beyond the border stones lay the exile lands, where magic lived unchained—the drowned valleys of Ashfall, the singing towers of the Spire's far cousins, all the broken country the Accord had amputated from the world forty years ago so that the rest of it could sleep. No gifted soul crossed willingly. The stones didn't just stop magic. They stopped *you*, if you carried too much of it, the way a dam stops water—with pressure, and violence, and a sound like the sky clearing its throat.

But he had tested the stones once, in another life, in chapter three of his own quiet catastrophe. His gift worked inside the valley when it should not have. He was the flaw in the arithmetic. The anchor. The living knot at the center of the Accord, born on the border on the night it was tied.

Which meant, though he had never said it aloud even to himself, that the stones might not stop him at all.

Or they might unmake him. There was no way to know except to touch them.

"Theo," Ivy said again, softer. "Whatever you choose—"

"Don't," he said. "Don't apologize again. Apologies are memories too, and I'm not taking yours." He folded the clerk's copy into quarters, into eighths, until it was a small hard star of paper in his fist. "Go home. Write to your family honestly this time. Tell them their daughter is a coward and a hero in the same breath, and that both halves of her saved my life tonight."

Ivy's eyes were bright and furious and wet. "That's a terrible thing to make me carry."

"I know," Theo said. "Welcome to my entire profession."

* * *

He went home first. Not because it was wise—because it was the only direction his feet understood.

The room above the cooper's shop was barely a room: a pallet, a candle stub, a loose floorboard beneath which lived the whole architecture of his survival. He knelt and pried it up and looked down at what he had spent eleven years assembling, the way other men assemble gold.

Shards. Dozens of them. Slivers of moonlit crystal, each one holding a memory he had lifted and never sold—the ones too dangerous to broker, too heavy to keep in his own head, too true to destroy. A soldier's last sunrise. A mother's voice reading a psalm in a language the valley had forgotten. The child from Lowfield, laughing, before the smoke—because he had taken her screaming and left her laughing, and her mother deserved the laughter back someday, and someday had better come.

And beneath them all, wrapped in wool gone soft with age, the fragment of the Moonlit Accord itself. The shard that hummed against his gift like a struck bell against a struck bell. The thing half the world would burn for.

He gathered them into a satchel with hands that shook only once, and then only briefly, and he blew out the candle because a lit window is a confession.

Outside, the valley lay silver and silent under the thirteenth moon, and it looked so much like peace that his chest hurt. The chapel. The well. The crooked lane where Maren's shop stood dark, its ledger long since emptied of his name. Somewhere in the garrison, torches were being lit and horses saddled, and Captain Serah Voss—a woman who believed, with the terrible sincerity of the truly certain, that she was saving the world—was reading his description aloud to men with ropes.

*I'm sorry,* he thought at the valley, at all of it, at the well and the chapel and the graves on the hill. *I stole from you for years and now I'm stealing myself away.*

Then he ran.

* * *

The border stones stood in the high pasture above Lowfield's ruins, and Theo had avoided that pasture for a month, and now he crossed the ashes' edge at a dead sprint with his lungs full of cold and his satchel banging against his hip.

They were older than anything else in the valley. Older than the chapel, older than the Spire, older than names. Seven standing stones in a broken ring, each taller than three men, each wearing the centuries like lichen wears rock. Between them the air looked wrong—not empty, not full, but *tense*, the way a rope looks just before it parts. The grass grew right up to an invisible line and then lay flat, combed flat, as if something vast had passed over it nightly for forty years and the grass had learned to bow.

Beyond the line: darkness, and distance, and the drowned country of Ashfall somewhere past the hills, and war.

Behind him, faint on the wind, hoofbeats. More than one horse. Coming up the valley road, and the road bent around the millpond, which bought him perhaps four minutes, perhaps three.

Theo walked to the line where the grass lay flat and stopped, and for a moment he simply breathed.

This was the edge of everything he was. Everything he had ever been happened on the near side of these stones—the thefts, the hunger, the careful invisible years, the hollow comfort of a life built out of other people's losses. On the near side he was a thief with a system. On the far side he was, according to Lady Ondine Ashfall's letters smuggled through the choir, *the anchor*, *the living Accord*, *the son of our lost sister*—words that fit him like a coat cut for a larger man.

The hoofbeats rounded the millpond.

He thought of Ivy's face in the tannery yard. He thought of the Lowfield child's laughter going home to her mother. He thought of a woman he could not remember, whose memory lived in a shard against his ribs, who had stood on this exact line forty years ago—or so the shard suggested, or so his gift insisted, humming through him like a second heartbeat—and chosen to bear him on the border between worlds.

"Mother," he said, to the stones, to the dark, to no one. "If you can hear anything anymore. Open the door."

He stepped across.

* * *

The world broke.

Not gently. Not like crossing a threshold. Like diving into deep water after a lifetime of believing yourself unable to swim—except the water was everything, was light and sound and gravity and time, and it came at him from all directions at once with the weight of an ocean deciding to be a single drop.

Magic.

He had spent his whole life handling it in thimbles—one memory lifted here, one grief set down there, small transactions in a currency he'd assumed was scarce. Now the currency was the air. Now it was the ground under his boots and the sky overhead and his own blood, and all of it *sang*. He could hear the stones behind him, hear the Accord itself, a chord sustained for forty years by seven pillars and one living boy, and he could feel his own note in it, the keystone tone, and he understood with a clarity that felt like drowning that he had never once been separate from this. Every theft he'd ever made had been a ripple in a pond he was standing in. Every memory he'd lifted had traveled along threads that ended here, at him, anchored here, *anchored BY him*—

He went to his knees in the wet grass of the far side and was sick, and the sickness tasted of starlight, which should have been impossible and was merely the least strange thing happening to him.

Get up. Get up. They're coming.

He got up.

The far country spread below him: black hills stitched with rivers that glimmered faintly, wrongly, as if the water remembered being enchanted and hadn't fully forgotten. Far off, maybe a day's ride, a light burned on a headland—not a farm light, not a watchfire. A beacon, tall and steady, burning some color that wasn't quite flame. Toward it, he knew, lay the House of Ashfall, and Lady Ondine, and answers shaped like his mother's face.

Behind him, the border stones flared.

He turned in time to see the ring ignite with pale light, and through it—like figures seen through ice—came riders. Four of them. Five. At their head a rider in a captain's cloak who did not slow at the line, who did not hesitate, who drove her horse straight at the boundary of the world as if orders were stronger than physics.

Voss.

The stones rejected her. He felt it happen, felt the Accord flex and refuse, and her horse screamed and reared and threw her backward onto safe grass, onto valley grass, and even from thirty yards he could hear her shouting—not in pain, in fury, in a voice cracked open with something that was not entirely hatred. She gained her feet. She stared across the boundary at him, and the thirteenth moon lit her face, and what he saw there was not the hunter's mask. It was a woman looking at proof that her whole life's arithmetic was wrong.

"Joubert!" she called, and the wind carried it thin and clear. "You're running to them! You're running to the ones who started the war!"

"They didn't start it!" he called back, and his voice came out strange, resonant, doubled, as if the stones themselves spoke with him. "Neither did we! Come and see! Break your orders and come and SEE!"

She stood a long moment. Then she turned and shouted to her men, words he couldn't catch, and one by one they tried the line and one by one the stones threw them back, and Voss stood last, watching him, her hand pressed flat against air that would not admit her, and Theo understood that he had just planted a seed in the hunter that would grow or kill her, and that he could not stay to learn which.

He turned his back on Vaelmoor—on the chapel and the well and the graves on the hill, on Ivy writing her honest letter by candlelight, on every version of himself he had ever been—and started down through the dark grass toward the beacon on the headland.

With every step, the magic sank deeper into him, terrifying and sweet, a flood that did not drown so much as *rename*. By the time the valley was a silver smudge behind him, he could feel the shard of the Moonlit Accord against his ribs not as an object but as a voice, patient and ancient, keeping time with his heartbeat.

By the time the stars wheeled past midnight, he could hear it speaking.

*"Come home, little anchor,"* it seemed to say, in a cadence almost like a woman's. *"Your aunt is waiting. And she has been waiting a very long time."*

Ahead, the beacon burned its impossible color, and the thirteenth moon waned above it, one turn from eclipse, and Theo Joubert walked into the war with a satchel of returned promises and nothing else, feeling—for the first time in his life—completely, catastrophically awake.
