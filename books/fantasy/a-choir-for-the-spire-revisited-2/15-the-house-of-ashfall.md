## 15. The House of Ashfall

The road beyond the stones did not feel like a road. It felt like a held breath.

Theo walked it for three days, and for three days the world refused to settle into the shapes he had always trusted. Trees grew wrong here—or rather, they grew *right*, in ways the valley's stunted pines never dared, their crowns braided with pale lichen that glowed faintly after dark, as if the forest itself remembered a moon that had set long ago. Rivers ran uphill in slow silver ribbons where the ground remembered being tilted by some old working. Once, crossing a meadow of blue grass, he watched a shepherd's flock drift six inches above the turf, their hooves stirring nothing, and the shepherd—a broad woman with a crook of woven star-iron—nodded at him as though floating sheep were no stranger than rain.

Magic soaked everything here. It soaked *him*.

He had thought crossing the border stones would be like stepping out of cold water into air. Instead it was the opposite: drowning. Every step beyond Vaelmoor pressed against his skin like a second atmosphere, thick with voices that were not voices—residue, echo, the sediment of centuries of workings laid down like silt. His gift, which inside the valley had been a quiet instrument, a thief's pick made of nerve and longing, was now a struck bell that would not stop ringing. He could not stop hearing memories. They clung to the hedgerows, pooled in the ruts of the cart road, seeped from the stones themselves: a wedding celebrated two hundred years ago, still warm; a funeral whose grief had soaked so deep into an oak that the tree grew its rings around it like a pearl.

He learned to walk with his hands fisted in his cloak and his teeth clenched, a man wading through other people's yesterdays.

And through all of it, the shard rode against his chest in its wrapping of Ivy's scarf, cold as a coin left overnight in snow, and it hummed. Not constantly. It hummed when he thought of her—the mother he had never known, the woman who existed for him only as a face glimpsed inside a stolen thing, dark-haired, laughing at something out of frame, one hand pressed to her belly in a way that, now that he knew what to look for, might have meant she was carrying him.

*You are the Accord's living anchor.* His own words to Ivy, spoken on the last night inside the valley, when saying them aloud had felt like pulling a splinter from the bone of the world. He still did not fully believe them. But the shard believed. The closer he came to the mountains, the louder it sang against his sternum, a tuning fork finding its note.

On the fourth morning, the road ended at a wall of ash-grey stone that climbed out of a gorge like something grown rather than built. No gate broke its face. Only a carving, weathered nearly smooth: a tree with thirteen branches, and beneath it two figures holding hands across a line scored deep between them.

The shard went silent. Then it sang—one clear, high note that Theo felt in his molars—and the carved tree *bloomed*. Light ran along the grooves of the stone like dawn running along a mountain ridge, and a doorway opened where there had been none, breathing out warmth and woodsmoke and, underneath it, a smell that stopped Theo's heart mid-beat: rosemary bread. Someone was baking rosemary bread, and somewhere in the deep places of him, in the vaults where his stolen goods were kept, a memory that was not his own turned over in its sleep and whispered, *home.*

He stepped through before he could lose his nerve, which was, he admitted, the only way he had ever done anything brave.

* * *

The city—if city it was—had been built inside the gorge and up its walls, terraces of grey stone stitched together with bridges of living root, windows glowing amber against the morning mist. People moved along the terraces doing ordinary things: carrying baskets, arguing about the price of wool, teaching children letters on slates. But the ordinary here wore its strangeness openly. A woman mended a fishing net with threads that knotted themselves. An old man swept his steps and the leaves obeyed the broom from three feet away, herding themselves into the pan. Two boys raced paper birds down the gorge, and the birds beat wings of folded air.

No one hid anything. That was what undid him. In Vaelmoor he had spent twenty years reading people the way a gambler reads cards—the flinch, the swallowed word, the secret carried like a stone in the shoe. Here everyone walked lightly, unburdened, and Theo stood in the middle of the market terrace feeling like a man made entirely of contraband.

They saw him anyway. Of course they did. Word traveled ahead of him in murmurs; heads turned; a hush moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. A child pointed—not at his face but at his chest, where the shard's humming must have been audible to more ears than his.

"The anchor," someone said, and the word passed hand to hand until the whole terrace breathed it.

Then the crowd parted, and Lady Ondine Ashfall came down the steps.

She was older than he expected and younger than she should have been—silver at the temples, but a face that time seemed to have negotiated with rather than conquered. She wore mourning grey shot through with a single thread of white at the collar, and her eyes were the color of the valley sky just before rain, and when they fixed on Theo, he understood with terrible clarity that he had never once in his life been truly *seen* until this moment. Maren had looked at him and seen inventory. Ivy had looked at him and seen a partner, a lever, eventually a friend. Voss had looked at him and seen prey.

This woman looked at him and saw—

"Say your name," she said. Her voice was low and roughened, a voice used to command and grief in equal measure.

"Theo," he said. "Theo Joubert." It sounded small here, a beggar's coin offered at an altar.

"Joubert." She tasted it. Shook her head slowly. "A name given by strangers. Say the name you woke with, the first one, before anyone told you who you were."

"I don't—" His throat closed. "I don't have one. I was found. I was told I was found."

"No," said Lady Ashfall, and crossed the remaining distance between them, and took his face in both her hands the way one handles something already broken, already grieving, "you were not found. You were *hidden*. And we have spent twenty years searching every shadow in the world for where."

Her thumbs brushed his cheekbones, and it was nothing, it was an old woman's gesture, and yet something behind his ribs tore open like ice going out in spring. Because no one had touched him like that. No one had touched him like that in twenty years, since—since—

Since a kitchen that smelled of rosemary bread. Since hands lifting him onto a counter. Since laughter he had no right to remember because he had never been given it, only—

"You," he managed. "You knew her."

"I raised her," Ondine said. "From the day she was born to the day the world ended. Come inside, child of my sister's body. You are twenty years late for supper, and the table has never once been cleared."

* * *

The hall of House Ashfall was long and low-ceilinged, built of the same ash-grey stone, hung with tapestries that moved—gently, subtly, their woven rivers flowing, their woven seasons turning—as if the threads could not bear to be still. A fire burned in a hearth big enough to stand in. And down the length of the hall, portraits lined the walls, dozens of them, generations of faces, and Theo made himself look at each one because looking away would have meant admitting how badly his hands were shaking.

Near the hearth, smaller than the rest, hung a portrait of a young woman with dark hair and a laugh caught mid-motion, one hand pressed flat below her ribs.

He stopped walking. He could not help it. His body simply quit.

"That's—" His voice came out in pieces. "That's her. That's the face from—I stole a memory once, off a dying soldier, a man who'd been fed lies about the war, and there was a propaganda print in it, a wanted poster, and her face was on it, and I've carried that face for years without knowing why I couldn't put it down—"

"Séraphine," Ondine said softly, coming to stand beside him. Up close, the resemblance was a physical blow: the same jaw, the same rain-colored eyes. "My sister. The finest memory-wright our house ever produced. She could lift a grief so cleanly the mourner never knew it was gone, and carry it herself without breaking—which is more than most of us can say." A pause. A dry, broken almost-smile. "It seems the gift ran true in her son."

"I'm not—" Theo started the denial out of pure habit, then heard how hollow it rang even to his own ears, here in a hall where nets tied themselves and leaves obeyed brooms. He let it die. "I steal memories. Or—I stole them. I didn't know they were thefts. I was told—I sold myself to a broker as a boy, I was told I *copied*, that the grief stayed with its owner and I just took a reading, an impression. Twenty years I told myself that. It was the only way I could sleep."

"And is it true?"

He closed his eyes. Behind them: the soldier in chapter one of his life's ledger, the dying man in the market town, the light going out of him as Theo lifted his last hour clean away. *So that's what peace feels like,* the man had said, smiling, empty. And Theo had traded the dead man's final hour to Maren for bread and silence, and slept soundly, because the theft had been invisible to everyone but the victim.

"It isn't true," he said. "I take them. Whole. The grief goes with me and leaves nothing behind but a smooth place where it used to be." His hands curled. "I've spent twenty years telling myself I was a comfort. A taker-away of pain. And I was just a thief with a good excuse."

"Yes," Ondine said, without cruelty and without mercy. "You are your mother's son. She lied to herself the same way, at the end. She thought she could hide the shard inside her own unborn child and the Accord would never find him." She touched the portrait's frame. "She was wrong. The Accord found her instead. And she died buying you twenty years of invisibility, and it cost her everything, and it very nearly cost the world."

"The shard." He pulled it from his shirt, unwound the scarf. In the hearthlight the fragment threw back no reflection at all—it drank light the way he drank memories—and its hum filled the hall now, resonating in the stone, in the tapestries, in the portraits' painted eyes. "What is it? Truly. Not what the brokers said, not what the hunters want it for. What is it?"

Ondine did not answer at once. She gestured, and a servant—no, not a servant, a scholar, robes ink-stained at the cuffs—brought a cushioned case and opened it, and Ondine lifted the shard from Theo's palms with a care that made his throat ache, as though he had been carrying a sleeping infant all this way and hadn't known it.

"When the war ended," she said, "there were those on both sides who wanted the fighting sealed away forever, and those who wanted magic restored to every soul in creation. The Accord was the compromise: magic confined beyond the border stones, the gifted exiled, the valley and the crown's lands kept clean and quiet and safe. And the terms of the Accord—every word, every clause, every escape written into it—were sung into a single vessel so that no king or council could ever amend them by forgery. A memory of the truth, kept outside any human mind, where no thief could reach it." Her mouth curved. "Or so we believed."

"But my mother—"

"Séraphine was the vessel's keeper. When the crown turned on the houses, when the hunt began and exile became slaughter, she took the shard and ran, because as long as the shard survived, the Accord's true terms survived—and so did the possibility of *changing* them. Renewal or breaking. Peace forever, or the world remade. Both futures live in that fragment, waiting for a voice to choose." Ondine's gaze lifted from the shard to Theo's face. "But the shard cannot be sung by just anyone. It answers only to its keeper's blood. And its keeper's blood—" she pressed the fragment gently back into his hands, folding his fingers over it, hers over his, "—is standing in front of me."

The hum rose through his palms, up his arms, into the hollows where twenty years of stolen lives lay shelved like jars in a pantry, and for one vertiginous moment Theo felt every theft he had ever committed tremble on its shelf, as if called to attention, as if *counted*.

"There is a price," he said. He didn't know how he knew it; the shard told him the way a fever tells the body. "When it's sung. There's a price, and it has to do with me. With what I'm carrying."

Ondine studied him for a long moment, and in her rain-colored eyes he saw her arrive at the edge of the same understanding, and flinch from it, the way mothers flinch.

"How much have you taken?" she asked quietly. "In twenty years. How many lives do you carry?"

"Hundreds." The word fell out of him like a stone down a well. "Griefs. Guilts. Last hours. Secrets people would have died to keep. I took them to survive, and I sold them, and some of them are gone past recall, and—" He stopped. Breathed. The hearth cracked; somewhere in the hall a tapestry river flowed on, indifferent. "Some of them I mean to give back. All of them, if that's the price. I decided on the road. Whatever it costs, whatever it empties."

For the first time, Lady Ondine Ashfall smiled—not the careful smile of a matriarch, but something rawer, wetter, younger. She looked, suddenly, unbearably, like the woman in the portrait.

"Séraphine used to say exactly that," she whispered. "*Whatever it empties.*" Her hands tightened over his. "You come from thieves and keepers, child, but tonight you sit at her table, under her roof, among people who have mourned you since before you could walk. Eat. Rest. Tomorrow we begin teaching you what the shard will ask of you—and I warn you now, it will ask for everything you have stolen, everything you have hidden, and the childhood those thieves in the valley took from you before you ever stole from anyone else."

Theo's breath caught. "My childhood. You know where it is?"

"We have always known where it is." Ondine's voice gentled into something like pity, something like fury. "It was never lost, Theo Joubert. It was confiscated—by the men who pulled you from your mother's arms and buried your first five years so deep you'd never dig for them, because a child who remembered his mother's face might someday ask why she died. It sits in a vault beneath the Spire, catalogued and priced, next to the ledgers of every broker in Vaelmoor."

Outside, far above the gorge, the thirteenth moon slid thin and sharp from behind the clouds, one turn from eclipse, and its light fell through the high window and lay across the portrait of Séraphine like a blade laid across a table.

"And tomorrow," said Lady Ashfall, "we are going to teach you how to make the Spire give it back."
