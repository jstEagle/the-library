## 16. Two Ways to End a War

Ondine Ashfall had learned, in the long years of exile, that truth was best carried the way water was carried in the desert: carefully, in small measures, and never all at once. She had spent the morning preparing the chamber for it anyway—the high room at the top of the Ashfall tower, where the walls were hung with banners of the old house, silver moons on ash-grey silk, faded now to the color of bone left out in weather. She had lit the lamps early, though the light through the windows was still strong, because she wanted the boy to see that this room had been kept warm. That someone had waited.

He was coming up the stair now. She could hear him—could hear, with the small gift of attention that exile had honed in her sharper than any magic, the particular hesitation in his step. He paused on the seventh landing. She had counted the landings herself, years ago, when she first came here broken and furious, and she knew that the seventh was the one where the stair turned and you could see the whole of the valley through the eastern window: the border stones in their long grey line, the fields beyond, the smoke of Vaelmoor rising thin and ordinary as any town's smoke.

He was looking at it. She let him.

*Let him look as long as he needs,* she thought, turning the page of the ledger before her—a ledger of a different kind than the broker Maren's, though she did not yet know that name. This one held the genealogy of the Ashfall line, seven generations written in her mother's hand, and in the third generation, in ink gone brown with age, a name she had spent forty years not speaking aloud.

*Sereth. Sereth Ashfall, who walked out of the valley and never came home.*

The door opened.

He was thinner than he had been three days ago, when her riders had brought him in from the border road, half-drowned in magic and shaking like a man pulled from a river in winter. The gift had gotten into him out there, past the stones, and it had not been gentle. Magic was never gentle with those who had lived without it. It came in like floodwater into a dry cellar, finding every crack, filling every hollow, and Theo Joubert had been hollow in more places than he knew.

"You've been staring at that ledger since I came in," he said. His voice had recovered its dryness. She liked that about him. Grief that could still be dry was grief that had not yet finished its work.

"I've been reading it," Ondine said. "There's a difference. Staring is what people do when they're afraid of a thing. Reading is what they do when they've decided to learn it." She closed the book and set her hand flat upon its cover, as if it might otherwise fly open of its own accord. "Sit, Theo. What I have to tell you is better taken sitting down."

He sat, in the chair by the window that she had placed there for him—she had placed it there the first night, without letting herself think about why—and for a moment neither of them spoke. Below them the exile city went about its evening: the bells of the low quarter, the smell of bread and lamp-oil and, faintly, the ozone tang of workings done openly in the streets, which had stopped startling her years ago and still, some evenings, made her want to weep.

"You said you'd tell me about my mother," he said at last.

"I said I'd tell you about my sister." Ondine watched his face as she said it, and saw the word land the way she had known it would—not like a gift but like a stone dropped into still water, the ripples going out and out into places he had not known he had. "Sereth Ashfall. Born in this tower, in this room, in the year the old king died. She had our mother's black hair and our father's temper, and she could carry a tune so sweet that birds would land on the sill to listen. She was the finest of us with the gift, and the least patient with it. She thought magic should belong to everyone. She thought the war was being fought over a treasure that should never have been locked in a box."

"The war," Theo said. "The one the Accord ended."

"The one the Accord *ended*," Ondine agreed, "the way a surgeon ends a fever. By cutting. You know the story they tell in the valley, I expect. That the mages went mad with power, that the crown rose up to stop us, that wise people on both sides drew the border stones and made a peace." She smiled, and knew it was not a kind smile. "The story they tell out here is different. In this story, the crown wanted the gift for itself. Wanted it conscripted, licensed, owned. And when the mage-houses refused to hand over their children to be weapons, the crown called it treason and called the war that followed a rebellion. Both stories are true. Neither is whole. That is what war is: two whole truths cut in half and sharpened."

"And my mother was on which side?"

"My sister was on the side of the valley," Ondine said. "She crossed the stones in the last year of the war, under a flag of parley, to sing the first draft of the Accord to the crown's own negotiators. She believed it could work. She believed that a border was a wound that could be allowed to heal over, and that peace was worth the price of never going home." Her hand tightened on the ledger. "She was right about the peace. She was wrong about the price. The Accord needed an anchor, Theo. A thing of both worlds, born on the line itself, at the moment of the singing, to hold the two sides of the spell together. The negotiators found such a child. A baby, born that very night in a cart on the border road, to a woman of the valley and a man of the houses. They took the child, and Sereth—"

She stopped. Not for effect. She had rehearsed this part forty years and it still would not come out whole.

"Theo," she said instead, "what do you remember of your childhood?"

He was quiet a long moment. The light had gone amber in the window; the valley below was turning blue.

"Nothing," he said finally. "That's the truth of it, whatever else I've told people. I have a story I tell about myself—that I was born in Vaelmoor, that my parents died of fever, that I learned to steal memories because hunger teaches fast. But when I go looking for the memory of learning it, there's nothing there. Just the story, worn smooth like a stone I've carried in my pocket so long I've mistaken it for a bone." He looked at her, and his eyes were very steady, and very frightened. "You're going to tell me why."

"Because you gave it away," Ondine said. "Or it was taken, or you spent it—I don't know which, and neither, I think, do you. But I know this. The Accord was sung by my sister and sealed by an anchor-child, and that anchor was meant to be a vessel that held nothing of its own. An empty vessel holds the spell. A full one holds only itself. So the child's first years were lifted—every memory, every grief, every name—and stored in the shard that Sereth carried. The fragment you found. The fragment you've been carrying against your ribs like a second heart since the dead drop."

His hand had gone, without his seeming to notice, to his chest. She watched him notice, and watched him take the hand away, and watched him put it back.

"And the woman in the shard," he said. "The one singing. The one I thought might be—"

"Is my sister," Ondine said. "Singing the Accord into the anchor. And the child in her arms, Theo—the child she was singing *to*—was you."

* * *

The lamps had been lit some time before he spoke again. Ondine let the silence do its work. She had learned that from the choir: silence was not the absence of the song. It was the space between the notes that made the notes mean anything.

"The choir," he said at last. "You said the choir could sing the Accord again. Renew it. Or break it."

"The choir can sing anything," Ondine said. "That is their gift and their curse. Twelve voices, carrying truths in harmony, each voice holding a piece of the working so that no one singer bears the whole of it. They sang the first Accord. They can sing its renewal, or its ending. What they cannot do—" and here she rose, and went to the window, because it was easier to say the next part looking at the valley and not at his face— "what they cannot do is choose."

"Can't they? They sang the first one."

"They sang what the anchor held," Ondine said. "The first anchor was an infant. An infant cannot choose, so the negotiators chose for it, and the price of that choice has been paid for forty years by everyone on both sides of those stones. Exiles who cannot go home. Valley-folk who cannot be whole. A crown that hunts its own gifted children like vermin because the Accord taught it that magic is a thing to be caged." She turned back to him. "The spell is old now, and tired, and it has learned. It will not be sung again over an unchoosing vessel. The renewal—or the breaking—must be chosen by the anchor, freely, knowing the whole of both fates. That is you, Theo. That is the unbearable shape of what you are."

"Then tell me the fates." His voice was flat, and she recognized the flatness—it was the voice of a man standing at the edge of a high place, making himself look down. "Tell me both of them. All of it. Don't soften it."

"I won't." She sat again, facing him, and folded her hands in her lap like a woman about to hear a confession, because in a way she was. "The first fate. Renewal. The choir sings the Accord as it was written, renewed for another age. The border stones stand. Magic stays beyond them; the valley stays as it has always been. The exiled houses remain in exile—my house, and the others, forever, our children's children born and dying on this side of the line, never seeing the valley our mothers came from. And the crown's hunt ends. The witch-hunters go home. No more burnings. No more Lowfields." She saw him flinch at the name, and did not stop. "That is renewal. Peace, bought with a wall. Safety, bought with exile. Everyone in the valley lives, and everyone beyond the stones goes on living the life I have lived for forty years, which is a good life, Theo, I will not lie to you about that either. It is a good life. It is just not *home*."

"And the other fate."

"Breaking." The word came out of her like a splinter. "The choir sings the Accord undone. The stones dissolve. Magic returns to the world—everywhere, all at once, after forty years of sleeping. And the world is not ready for it, Theo. The valley-folk have forgotten what the gift looks like when it walks abroad. The crown has spent forty years teaching its people that the gifted are monsters. The moment the stones fall, every fear the crown ever fed gets a body and a face. There will be those who reach out across the line with open hands. And there will be those who reach across with fire. I will not pretend to know which would win. I only know that the last time magic and the crown met in the field, it took a war and a million dead to stop it, and the stopping cost us everything we had." She held his gaze. "But the stones fall, Theo. The wall comes down. No more exile. No more hiding. The wound is allowed to be a wound, and to heal in the open air, or to fester. That is breaking. Freedom, bought with fire."

He sat with it. She watched him sit with it, and she did not help him, because no one could. That was the whole of the cruelty of a free choice: that it could not be carried by anyone but the one choosing. She had carried her sister's memory for forty years, and it had weighed nothing like this.

"If I choose renewal," he said slowly, "the valley's safe. Ivy, the innkeeper's family, everyone who ever fed me or lied for me or didn't ask the questions they should have asked. But the hunt just—moves? Or ends?"

"Ends. The Accord renewed includes the amnesty. It was always in the first singing; the crown has simply chosen to forget it. Renew the Accord, and the gifted inside the valley are protected by the same spell that walls the magic out. The hunt dies with the hunt's purpose."

"And if I break it—"

"The gifted in the valley are free, and in danger, on the same day. The stones fall and every hunter in the crown's service is a day's ride from every gifted child, and what happens after that is not in the song. It is in the hearts of a million people, and I have read hearts, Theo, and I do not trust them. Not theirs. Not mine. Not yours." She leaned forward. "And there is one thing more. The crueler thing. Will you hear it?"

He almost laughed. "I've heard crueler things this week than I've heard in my whole life. Say it."

"The anchor must choose freely," Ondine said. "Freely means unforced—not by threat, not by bribe, not by love. If you choose renewal because Ivy is in the valley, the song fails. If you choose breaking because my face looks like your mother's, the song fails. The spell will know. The spell has had forty years to learn the difference between a choice and a surrender." She watched the understanding come into his face, and hated herself for putting it there, and knew that not putting it there would have been the deeper cruelty. "You must choose as if no one you love stood on either side of the line. And the moment you choose, everyone you love will live inside the choice. That is the weight of it. That is what my sister understood, at the end, and why she gave you up—why she let them lift your childhood and seal it in the shard and send you into the valley to be raised by strangers. Because she loved you, and love would have ruined the choosing. She made herself a stranger to you so that you could one day stand free."

The silence after that was very long. Down in the city the evening bells rang, twelve of them, and somewhere below Ondine could hear the choir rehearsing—fragments of harmony drifting up through the window, a phrase rising and falling, rising and falling, like something practicing for a birth or a funeral.

"The eclipse," Theo said at last. "The thirteenth moon. That's when the song has to be sung."

"At the border stones, under the eclipse, when the Accord is thinnest. Crown forces will be there. So will ours. Both sides armed, both sides afraid, and one thief standing between them with the whole of two fates in his hands." She rose and went to him, and this time she did take his face in her hands, because he was her sister's son and she had forty years of not-touching to make up for, and because some truths are better carried with the hands than with the voice. "I will not tell you what to choose. Do you understand me? I have wanted the stones to fall for forty years, and I want it still, and because I want it so badly my wanting is poison, and you must not drink it. Whatever you choose, I will stand behind it. That is the only vow I can make you that is worth anything."

"And if I choose wrong?"

"There is no wrong," Ondine said. "There is only the weight. And the weight is yours." She stepped back, and gathered herself, and became again the Lady of Ashfall, because he would need that from her too in the days to come—a wall to lean against, not just a sister's ghost. "The choir sings in eleven days. Voss is coming—I've had word from my riders; the crown's banners are on the north road, moving faster than orders should allow. And the boy—your friend Ivy's letter has reached the crown, Theo. Whatever she wrote, whatever she meant by it, the hunters are using it. You have less time than you think."

He stood. At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at her with his mother's eyes—Sereth's eyes, the temper and the tune both—and said, quietly:

"When I was a child—when I was *your* sister's child—did she sing to me? Before they took it all?"

"Every night," Ondine said. "The same song. The one the choir still sings. She said you'd forget the words, but the words would not forget you."

He nodded once, and was gone down the stair, and Ondine Ashfall stood alone in the high room with her ledger and her lamps and the blue dark coming down over the valley, and let herself, at last and briefly and where no one could see, weep for her sister's son, who had eleven days to choose the shape of the world, and who did not yet know that the crown's riders were closer than she had told him—close enough, by her reckoning, to reach the border stones before the moon did.
