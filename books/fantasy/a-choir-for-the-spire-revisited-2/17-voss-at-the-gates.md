## 17. Voss at the Gates

Serah Voss had crossed forty borders in her service to the crown, and every one of them had been a line drawn by men—surveyors' stakes, river fords, the painted posts of customs yards. The border of Vaelmoor was the first she had ever felt.

She stood before it at dusk with three soldiers who had volunteered out of loyalty and two who had been ordered along because loyalty could be faked, and she laid her bare palm against the nearest stone. It was taller than a man, older than any kingdom she served, its surface worn smooth as sea glass and faintly warm, like the shoulder of a sleeping animal. Beneath her hand something hummed—not sound, not quite, but a pressure in the bones of her wrist, in the roots of her teeth.

*The gift dies here,* said the training manuals. *Magic cannot cross.* That was the whole architecture of the realm's peace: a valley where the touched could hide but never work their arts, a wall made not of mortar but of law older than law.

And yet the stone was singing to her charm-sense like a struck bell.

She had felt Theo Joubert through three weeks of interrogation, through spiced wine and careful questions—a flicker at the edge of her sense, there and gone, like a candle carried past a window. She had told herself it was nerves. She had told herself many things on the road north from Lowfield, riding past the black skeleton of that hamlet, the ash still sifting down like gray snow onto the shoulders of the survivors who would not look at her. She had told herself that burning a village to catch one child was arithmetic. She had told herself so firmly, so often, that she had almost believed it.

The stone under her palm did not believe it either.

"Captain?" said Corporal Ehren, her oldest soldier, his voice pitched low. "Orders were to hold at the boundary. The writ stops here. Beyond this is—" He gestured at the stones, at the darkening forest beyond them, and did not finish, because beyond this was nothing the crown admitted to. Beyond this was a country that officially did not exist, populated by mages who had officially died twenty years ago in a war that had officially ended when the last of them was driven out.

"Orders," Serah said, "were written by men who have never stood here."

She took her hand off the stone. The hum faded, leaving an ache like a pulled tooth.

She thought of the interrogations. Three innocents in the Vaelmoor jail, chosen not because they were guilty but because their absence would itch at the valley like a splinter, and things that itch get dug at, and what gets dug up can be examined. It had been clean doctrine. It had been *effective*. And one of them, the miller's wife, had wept and said, *I don't know anyone gifted, I swear it on my children,* and Serah had believed her—had known, in the marrow-deep way her gift knew things, that the woman was telling the truth—and had kept her locked up anyway, because the splinter needed to itch.

She thought of Lowfield. Of the child they had dragged out of a root cellar, small enough that the soldier carrying her had held her the way you'd carry a lamb. The girl had looked at Serah with no fear at all, only a kind of puzzled courtesy, as if trying to understand why the lady in the gray coat wanted her to burn.

Serah had watched the pyre from horseback. She had not turned away. Turning away was for people without convictions.

Her conviction had been simple enough to carry in one hand: magic had started the last war. The Moonlit War, they'd called it, though no text she was permitted to read explained why. Mages had razed cities; mages had turned neighbor against neighbor; the crown had ended it by sealing them behind the border stones, and every gifted person left inside the realm was a spark looking for its fire. Her whole career—the interrogations, the lists, the pyres—was built on that single load-bearing truth.

She had never once been asked to look beneath it.

Until a thief named Theo Joubert had fed her a memory of innocence in a smoky inn, and instead of anger she had felt—for one disorienting heartbeat—what it was to be a person with nothing to answer for. She had spent the following days furious, and then sleepless, and then quiet in a way she distrusted. Because the stolen feeling had not faded the way stolen things fade. It had sat in her chest like a seed.

"The writ," Ehren said again, gently.

"The writ governs the valley." She swung back into the saddle. "We are no longer in the valley."

* * *

They crossed at moonrise, and the crossing was like being born.

There was no barrier to push through—that was the horror of it. One moment she rode through old forest, and the next the air changed texture, thickened, went sweet and strange as water going into the lungs, and her charm-sense, which had been a narrow lamp all her life, opened like a cathedral door. Magic was everywhere out here. Not hidden, not hoarded—in the ground, in the leaves, in the breath of her own horse, whose eyes rolled white until a passing farmer, if farmer was the word for a man with light cupped in his palms like water, murmured to it and calmed it with a touch.

Serah sat very still on her horse and let the fullness of the world pour into her, and understood for the first time what the valley's silence must feel like to someone born deaf who suddenly hears: grief and wonder in the same instant, indistinguishable.

"Captain." Ehren's voice, thin. "Your hands."

She looked down. Faint light moved beneath her skin, following the veins of her wrists, answering the world's abundance the way iron answers a lodestone. Her own modest charm-sense—the hunter's tool she'd trained for thirty years—was blooming in this air into something vast and unmanageable. She could feel her soldiers' hearts beating. She could feel the trees dreaming.

She clenched her fists until the light dimmed. It did not go out. Nothing, she understood now, ever fully went out here. The valley wasn't where magic died. The valley was where magic was *held*—pressed flat under those ancient stones, sealed like a letter no one was allowed to open.

Which meant the stones were not the crown's wall at all. They were someone else's seal. And seals are made by the people with something to contain—or something to protect.

"Move," she said hoarsely. "Before I think too much."

* * *

They found the city on the second day, though found was the wrong word—it permitted itself to be found, unfolding out of a fold of hills the way a face emerges from shadow, all at once and then impossible to unsee.

Ashfall.

Even from the ridge she knew it, though no map in the royal archive showed it, though the war histories described the House of Ashfall as a burned ruin, its line extinguished, its name a cautionary tale. Below her lay terraces of pale stone descending toward a lake that held the evening sky like a cupped hand. Towers rose slender as candles. Gardens climbed the walls in green rivers. Windows glowed with a light that had no flame in it, and on the wind, faint and impossible, came music—many voices, braided, singing something wordless that made her chest hurt.

A city of the exiled, alive and lit and singing, twenty years after the crown declared them ash.

"We should report this," said Lieutenant Casse, the younger of her ordered soldiers, but his voice shook, because reporting meant returning, and returning meant telling the crown that everything in its histories was a lie, and some part of him already knew what crowns did with bearers of inconvenient truths.

"Yes," Serah said. "We should."

She did not move. Neither did they.

It was Ehren who finally said, quietly, "What did they actually do, Captain? In the war. Nobody I know ever met a mage. My grandfather fought in it and he wouldn't speak of it except to drink." He paused. "You've read the closed archives. What did he see?"

And Serah, who had indeed read the closed archives—who had read them twice, hunting ammunition, and found them strangely thin, strangely *curated*, full of atrocity accounts that contradicted one another and supply ledgers that told a more honest story than any chronicle—heard herself say:

"He saw us lose."

The words came out of her like a splinter leaving flesh.

"Read the ledgers sometime," she went on, and her voice sounded far away to herself, calm and terrible. "Chronicles say the mages sacked Callowmere. The ledgers show Callowmere's granaries emptied *before* the siege—by royal order, to feed the front. Chronicles say the mages poisoned the wells at Harrowgate. The ledgers show the crown's own engineers doing it, to deny the enemy water, and the enemy was—Ehren, the enemy was *farmers*. Gifted farmers. Healers. People who could sing blight out of wheat." She stared down at the singing city. "We didn't win a war against monsters. We starved out our own neighbors and called it righteousness, and then we built a wall around the ones who survived and called it peace, and then we taught children—taught *me*—that the wall was mercy."

"Captain—" Casse started.

"I'm not finished." But she was, nearly, because there was only one thing left and it would not stay down: "Lowfield. There was a child. There was a child, and we burned her, and I stood there believing I was holding shut a door that keeps out the dark." Her hand found the stone wall beside the road, ordinary fieldstone, and gripped it. "I think we may have been keeping a door shut that leads to *this*."

Below them, the gates of Ashfall stood open.

That was the detail that undid her, more than the towers, more than the singing. No fortress in the realm would leave its gates open with armed riders on the ridge. They had seen her coming—of course they had seen her coming, probably from the moment she crossed the stones—and they had left the gates open anyway, the way you leave a door open for someone you expect to arrive cold and hungry and lost.

Or the way you set a trap. Both possibilities stood in her mind, side by side, and for the first time in her life she could not tell which one she was walking into, and for the first time in her life she found she did not much care.

"Dismount," she said. "Leave the horses. If we're to be hanged either way, I'd rather meet it on foot, like a person."

* * *

The woman waiting in the gate court wore gray travel clothes and a circlet of twisted silver, and her hair was white though her face was not old, and when her eyes found Serah across the courtyard, Serah felt her charm-sense flare like a struck match.

Not hostility. *Recognition.*

"Lady Ondine Ashfall," Serah said, because there was no use pretending otherwise; the woman's power wrapped the courtyard like weather.

"And you are the witch-hunter who followed a thief across the world's edge," the Lady said. Her voice was level, almost kind. "Captain Voss. You burned Lowfield."

It was not a question, and there was nowhere to put it down. Serah did not try.

"Yes."

"The child's name was Wren. She was seven. She could make flowers open out of season." Ondine came closer, and up close her eyes were the color of the lake at dusk. "I am not going to curse you, Captain, though every person in this city has the right to ask me to. I'm going to do something worse. I'm going to show you the truth your archives were written to bury—and then I am going to let you live inside knowing it."

"You could just kill me," Serah said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "It would be simpler."

"It would be easier," Ondine corrected. "Simpler is different. Simpler is what the crown calls everything it doesn't want to understand." She turned, robes catching the lamplight that lived in the walls themselves. "Come and walk with me. There is a young man here you've been hunting—you know him as a thief. He carries something that belonged to my family. And he is about to learn what it costs to give back what you've taken." A pause, and over her shoulder, softly: "Perhaps you and he will recognize each other, Captain. You are both, I think, in the business of unmaking yourselves."

Serah Voss, who had crossed the world's edge breaking her own orders, who had watched her certainties crack one after another like river ice in spring, drew a breath that tasted of lake water and woodsmoke and song, and stepped through the open gates of the city that did not exist—

—and somewhere above the towers, behind the hills, the thirteenth moon, waning now toward its final turn, slid thin and sharp as a blade out of the clouds.
