## 18. The Price of Every Theft

The House of Ashfall kept its dead the way other houses kept wine: in cool dark rooms, labeled by year, brought out only on occasions of grief.

Theo learned this in the third hour after dawn, when Lady Ondine led him down a stair that had not existed until she touched the wall and asked it, politely, to open. The stair was cut from grey stone that hummed faintly under his palm—the same hum he had felt in the border stones all his life, though here it did not die at his fingertips. Here it rose to meet him like a dog hearing its name.

"The vaults," Ondine said. Her voice in the dark was low and even, the voice of someone who had spent thirty years saying terrible things gently. "Every memory the choir has ever carried for us. Every truth too dangerous to speak aloud, too precious to lose. We do not write them down. Writing can be burned." She glanced back at him, her face half-lit by the pale lamps that grew—actually grew, like fruit—from niches in the walls. "A memory carried in a living mind cannot be burned unless the mind is."

"And yours are all carried," Theo said. "In singers."

"In volunteers. The choir is an honor and a burden both. My brother carried the last words of our father for nineteen years before he could set them down at his grave." She stopped at a door of black wood banded with silver, and laid her hand flat against it. "You carry more than any of them. Do you understand what I am about to ask of you?"

"I think so." His mouth was dry. "You said the renewal needs an anchor who chooses freely. And you said—" He made himself say it. "You said freely means empty. That I can't choose while I'm holding stolen things, because stolen things are leverage, and leverage is not freedom."

"Close enough." She pushed the door open.

The room beyond was round and windowless and utterly silent, so silent he could hear his own blood moving. In the center stood a basin of white stone, shallow as a birdbath, filled with water so clear it looked like a hole cut in the floor into morning sky.

"This is a giving-basin," Ondine said. "It is how we return what the war took. Names. Lands. Memories. Anything held wrongly can be given back through it, if the giver means the giving." She turned to face him fully, and in the lamplight her resemblance to the woman in the shard—the woman in the memory that was not his—was so strong that Theo's chest hurt. "There is no spell in this room, Theo Joubert. There is only you, and what you have taken, and whether you can bear to let it go."

He thought of Maren's shop. Of the ledger with its neat columns, dates and prices and buyers, each line a life someone else had lived and lost without knowing it was gone. He had told himself for eleven years that he was a scavenger, not a thief—that grief abandoned on a roadside was fair salvage, that secrets hoarded past their usefulness were waste he merely recycled. He had been very good at telling himself that. It was, he realized now, the longest memory he had ever stolen: the one where he was innocent.

"How many?" he asked.

Ondine's eyes were kind and merciless. "How many do you think?"

He closed his eyes and let himself count—not the sales, the ones Maren had recorded, but the takings. Eleven years of market days and caravan stops and inn corners. A soldier's last battle. A bride's cold feet. A smuggler's route. A mother's face at a sickbed. A child's first lie. Hundreds. Perhaps a thousand. He had never once written them down either, because writing could be burned, and because some part of him had always known that a list would look like what it was.

"A thousand," he said. "Give or take."

"Then you will be here some time." Ondine moved toward the door. "The water remembers how this works. Touch it, hold the memory in your mind, and mean the return. If the owner lives, it goes home. If they are dead, it goes to their heir, or their grave, or—if there is neither—to the choir, who will keep it until someone claims it. You cannot choose wrong, Theo. You can only refuse to choose."

She paused at the threshold.

"One thing more. Your own childhood. The one you traded away when you were nine years old, to buy your first winter's bread." She said it without flinching, and he understood she had read it in him the way he read fear in travelers—as plainly as weather. "It belongs to you. But you sold it, and what is sold must be bought back, or returned by the buyer. Maren holds it still, if she lives. Or whoever bought it from her."

"Maren's ledger," Theo said slowly. "There was a buyer. Someone paid for a childhood whole. I saw the entry years ago and never let myself wonder about it." He laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "I've been carrying everyone else's past around my neck, and I sold my own for bread."

"Yes," Ondine said. "That is what the valley does to people. That is why the Accord must change." And she left him alone with the basin and the silence and the thousand small thefts of his life.

* * *

He started with the soldier.

It was the oldest taking he had, the one from Chapter One of his guilt, as Ivy would have put it—a dying man in a market gutter outside Vaelmoor, gasping out his last battle because Theo's gift had opened him like a door and everything had poured through. Theo knelt at the basin, cupped the water in both hands—it was warm, blood-warm, which he had not expected—and let the memory rise.

The battle came with it. Mud and trumpet-sound and the specific weight of a spear in hands that were not his, the smell of a horse's terror, a comrade named Aldric laughing a second before the arrow took him. Theo had carried this for eleven years and had never once looked at it straight; he had kept it wrapped and sealed, merchandise, not experience. Now he unwrapped it, and it hurt exactly as much as it should have, and he understood that this was the point.

"You're Aldric's friend," he whispered to the empty air, to the man behind the memory. "Your name was Corven Hale. You were twenty-six. You didn't want to be in that war. You went because your brother went."

He had never known those details before. They surfaced now, rising out of the memory like fish coming up to feed, because he was finally looking at it as a life instead of a coin. He pressed the water between his palms and meant the giving—meant it the way he had never meant anything, with his whole chest, with the part of him that had watched a man die and taken the death for pay.

The water glowed faintly, moon-colored, and went still.

The memory was gone. Not dulled, not faded—gone, the way a tooth is gone, a clean absence with a tender place around it. And in the absence was something he had not anticipated: relief. Grief, yes, sharp and appropriate, grief for Corven Hale who had died alone in a town that wasn't his—but grief that belonged to him honestly, earned rather than lifted. It sat differently in him than stolen sorrow ever had. Stolen grief had always fit badly, like another man's coat. This fit.

"Oh," he said aloud, to no one. "Oh, that's what it's supposed to feel like."

He gave back the bride's cold feet to a granddaughter in the capital—he felt the memory find its road, a tugging sensation behind his sternum, south and east, gone. He gave back the smuggler's route to the sea, where it dissolved over salt water because every soul who'd used it was dead. He gave back a lullaby to a cradle that no longer existed, and the choir took it, three voices somewhere above him beginning softly to sing a melody he had carried for six years without knowing whose it was, and hearing it sung made him weep for the second time that day.

Hours passed. The lamps in the walls dimmed and brightened with their own slow rhythm, a tide of light. Ondine came once with bread and watered wine and left it untouched by the door, and he ate it kneeling at the basin with wet hands, and kept working.

Some returns were easy. Some were not.

The mother's face at the sickbed—he had taken that from a boy of fifteen outside the chapel at Vaelmoor, lifted the whole scene because the boy had begged him to, *take it, please, I can't carry it, I'll give you anything.* Theo had told himself that one was mercy. Standing at the basin now, holding it, he saw the truth: the boy had needed to grieve, and Theo had stolen the grieving along with the memory, and the boy had walked away hollow and called it healed. He sent it back anyway, because it was not his to keep even as a kindness, and he added to it, deliberately, everything he knew of what had come after—the boy grown, the boy surviving, the boy's own children—so that whoever received it would know the story had continued. Restitution, he was learning, was not just giving back. It was giving back *whole*, with the interest of everything you'd learned while holding it wrong.

By the time the lamps dimmed to their night-tide, the basin had drunk four hundred memories, and Theo Joubert sat on the cold floor with his back against the wall and discovered what emptiness actually felt like.

It was not numbness. He had expected numbness. It was the opposite: every honest thing he had ever felt stood out in relief now that the dishonest things were going, the way stars come out when a fire dies down. He was lighter and more raw. He could feel the stone under him, the lamp-light on his face, the ghost-ache where each memory had been. He was becoming, memory by returned memory, a man with nothing left to hide—and the strange thing, the thing he would remember later, was that it did not feel like being emptied.

It felt like being *counted*. Like every true thing in him was being weighed, and found sufficient.

Ivy found him there near midnight. She came down the impossible stair with a candle she didn't need—the lamps gave plenty of light—and stopped when she saw him, and her face did something complicated.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I feel fantastic," he said, and mostly meant it, and she laughed, and then crossed the room and sat down beside him against the wall, shoulder to shoulder, the way people sit when words would only get in the way.

"How many left?"

"Six hundred. Give or take." He turned the cup of wine in his fingers. "Some of them don't have anywhere to go. Whole villages burned in the war. The choir keeps those."

"Is that bad? Keeping them?"

"It's a promise," Theo said. "Someone might come looking someday. A grandchild. Someone who wants to know what their grandmother's kitchen smelled like." He shrugged, and the shrug hurt, everything hurt a little now, honestly. "I spent eleven years being a warehouse for other people's lives. Turns out the job isn't done just because the thief quits."

Ivy was quiet a while. Then, carefully, in the voice she used when she was about to say something she'd rehearsed: "The childhood, Theo. Yours. Have you found where it went?"

He had known she would ask. He had been avoiding asking himself since dawn.

"Maren's ledger," he said. "Entry dated the winter I was nine. 'One childhood, complete, unsorted. Sold to a private buyer, capital, name withheld.'" He drank the wine down. "Maren wouldn't withhold a name for free. Which means whoever bought it paid her enough to make silence worth more than the sale. And there's exactly one kind of buyer who pays that way."

"The crown," Ivy said quietly.

"Or the Spire. Or the House of Ashfall." He looked at her. "Ondine says the choir keeps a registry of everything it carries. If my childhood is in a singer's throat somewhere in this house, I'll know by tomorrow. And if it's not—"

"Then it's in the capital, in some archivist's vault, in a jar with a wax seal." Ivy's jaw set. "And the eclipse is in five days, and Voss is somewhere between here and the border with three hundred men, and you want to walk into the capital to buy back your own life."

"I don't *want* to," Theo said. "That's rather the definition of the price."

* * *

The registry was kept in a round chamber at the top of the House, beneath a ceiling painted with thirteen moons in their thirteen phases, and it was not paper. It was people.

They sat in a ring, forty of them, old and young, robed in undyed wool, and when Ondine spoke a name into the quiet, the one who carried it stood and sang it—not the memory itself, but a brief phrase of melody that meant it, the way a bell means noon. Theo stood among them feeling like a man reading his own indictment.

"...Corven Hale, fallen at Redford field, carried by Sister Amet..." A woman rose, sang four notes, sat.

"...the Lowfield child, carried by the full choir since the burning..." Every singer in the room rose together, and the sound they made was small and terrible and perfect, and Theo had to close his eyes. He had taken that child's final memory so it would not die screaming. The choir had taken the screaming itself, apparently, and kept it, so that no one would ever forget. Two halves of one mercy, performed by strangers.

Then Ondine said: "A childhood, complete, unsorted, sold in Vaelmoor in the year of the frost fair. Does the choir carry it?"

Silence.

Theo waited through it with his heart going like a hammer. One by one the singers shook their heads. The registry was complete; the choir carried nothing it had not declared. His childhood was not here.

"It was bought," Ondine said at last, into the hush, "before the choir's founding. Before the exile, even. There was a broker in the capital then, in the years just after the Accord, who dealt in memories the crown confiscated from gifted families. Most of what he held was destroyed when the purges came." Her eyes found Theo's, and held. "But the crown kept certain acquisitions. Sealed. Catalogued. In the Undercroft of the Citadel itself, behind wards that only a royal key will open."

"The royal key," Ivy said flatly, "being on the belt of whichever witch-hunter captain happens to be carrying it. Say, for example, a Captain Serah Voss. Who is currently camped at our border with three hundred men and a warrant with your name on it, Theo."

The chamber was very quiet. Thirteen painted moons looked down on them, and the twelfth was painted waning, and the thirteenth was painted as a circle of pure black.

"So there it is," Theo said. He heard his own voice as if from a distance, calm and slightly amazed. "To sing the renewal, I have to be empty. To be empty, I have to buy back the one thing I ever sold. And the only key to it hangs from the neck of the woman hunting me." He laughed once, short and disbelieving. "The Accord really does insist on choosing freely, doesn't it. No shortcuts. No doors left unlocked. If I want my childhood back, I have to go and ask Serah Voss for it."

"Not ask," Ondine said. Her voice was gentle and absolutely without comfort. "*Take* would be the old way. *Ask* is the new one. That is rather the entire point of everything we are about to attempt." She stepped closer, and for a moment the lamplight made her look startlingly like the mother in the shard-memory, the sister she had lost, and Theo's throat closed. "Five days, anchor. The eclipse waits for no one. Decide how you mean to knock on that door."

Outside the painted moons, through a high slit of window, the real thirteenth moon hung over the exile city, waning, thinning, a nail-paring of light—and below it, far off across the dark hills, Theo could just see the fires of the crown's encampment at the border stones, hundreds of them, patient as stars.

Between the two lights lay Serah Voss, and his childhood, and the last piece of the price.
