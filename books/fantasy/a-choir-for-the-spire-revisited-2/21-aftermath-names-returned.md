## 21. Aftermath: Names Returned

The stones had been archways for eleven days when Ivy finally wrote the letter.

She sat at the inn's long table with a sheet of good paper—the kind she'd once taken for granted in her father's house, cream-colored and heavy as a held breath—and found that her hand had forgotten how to lie. That was the strange inheritance of the eclipse. Not magic, exactly, though magic moved through Vaelmoor now like water through a thawed riverbed, pooling in doorways, humming in the well-ropes, making the bread rise higher than it had any right to. No, what Ivy carried was simpler and crueler: she had stood between two armies and told the truth to both of them at once, and now every smaller truth came out of her sounding thin.

*Dear Father,* she wrote, and crossed it out.

*To Lord Aldous Fenwick of the capital,* she wrote, and crossed that out too, because even the salutation was a performance—a title worn like armor over the girl who had run away with nothing but a stolen name and a talent for not being seen.

In the end she wrote: *I don't know how to begin this honestly, so I will simply begin.*

* * *

The valley was learning its new shape the way a body learns a healed limb—cautiously, testing weight, flinching at old pains. Where the border stones had stood for two hundred years there were now seven archways of pale granite, their surfaces smooth as river ice, and through them Ivy could see the exile lands: terraced orchards climbing hills that had no business being so green in autumn, rooftops of slate and copper, the distant glint of the Spire's memory-choir tower catching light like a struck bell. People walked through in both directions now. Some wept. Some turned back immediately, changed their minds on the far side, and came home again within the hour, which everyone agreed was the true miracle—that coming home was still possible.

Maren had reopened her shop under new terms. The brokers' guild had dissolved itself in a single embarrassed afternoon; there was no more selling of memories when the mages beyond the arches taught anyone willing to carry their own past without flinching. Maren kept the ledger, though. She said someone ought to remember what had been bought and sold here, and Ivy suspected she meant herself most of all.

It was to Maren's shop that Theo went each morning, and it was there that Ivy found him on the day the child's memory came home.

* * *

Lowfield had rebuilt almost nothing yet. The hamlet was a field of black foundations and one surviving barn, and the survivors—forty-one souls out of sixty—lived in tents and lean-tos among the ruins, stubborn as weeds through cobblestones. They had refused the crown's relocation offer with a unanimity that had frightened the king's surveyor, Ivy heard, and they had refused the exiles' offer of rebuilding-magic too, at least at first. "We'll raise our own walls," old Tam Weller had said. "But you can tell your lady we won't say no twice."

The child's name had been Merrin. She had been nine years old. She had hidden in a root cellar while Lowfield burned, and Captain Serah Voss's soldiers had found her anyway, and what happened after that was the reason Voss now lived in a rented room above the chapel and would not accept wine from anyone in the valley, because she did not trust herself to be offered forgiveness she hadn't earned.

Theo had reached Lowfield too late that night. He had taken the child's final memory so it would not die screaming—and then he had kept it, because there had been no one left alive to give it back to.

Until now.

Ivy arrived at Maren's shop just after noon and found the door open and the smell of beeswax and old paper thick in the air. Theo sat at the counter with his hands flat on the wood, palms down, the way he always did when he was emptying himself—as if holding himself to the table against a current. Across from him sat Tam Weller's daughter, Bess, who had pulled Merrin from a burning house once before, years ago, when the girl fell into the millrace. Bess was maybe fifty, weathered as fence posts, and she was crying without any noise at all, tears simply falling off her chin onto the counter.

"Go on," Theo was saying gently. "It's yours to keep or refuse. Either way is right."

"I want it." Bess's voice cracked like green wood. "Whatever it is. I want her last thing to have somewhere to live."

Ivy watched Theo close his eyes. She had seen him return memories perhaps thirty times in these weeks, and it never looked like much from outside—a stillness, a small exhale, sometimes a tremor in his fingers. But she knew now what it cost. Every return took something with it. Not memories of his own—he'd given those up too, all of them, including the childhood he'd stolen from himself without ever knowing it was stolen—but something adjacent. A little warmth. A little certainty. He came away from each restitution slightly more transparent, slightly less sure of where he ended and other people began.

Bess made a sound like a bird startled off a branch. Her hand flew to her mouth.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, she was singing. She was singing the whole time, wasn't she? In the dark. She wasn't afraid, not really, she was—" The tears came faster. "She thought it was a game at first. Hide-and-seek. God forgive us all, she thought it was hide-and-seek."

"I'm sorry," Theo said. "I couldn't change what it was. Only that it didn't die alone."

"You gave it a lantern," Bess said fiercely, gripping his wrist. "You hear me? You put a lantern in the dark with her. Don't you dare apologize for that."

Ivy slipped back out before either of them saw her crying too. Some griefs deserved privacy, and some joys wore grief's clothes, and this was both.

* * *

That evening she climbed the hill behind the inn where the seventh archway stood alone among gorse, and she brought her letter with her, unfinished, and she sat against the warm stone and read it over for the dozenth time.

*I don't know how to begin this honestly, so I will simply begin.*

*I ran away because you asked me to name my friends. You remember them—I know you do, even though we never spoke of it. Mira who could make plants bloom out of season. Joss who could find anything lost. Little Annet who talked to birds, God help her, who was six years old. You asked me to write down their names for the crown's men, and I burned the paper instead and took the coach south and became nobody, because becoming nobody was the only way I knew to keep from becoming yours.*

*I am not writing to ask pardon anymore. I withdrew the request. Whatever I owe the crown, I'll pay it here, in the open, where I chose to stand.*

*I am writing to tell you who I became instead. Her name is Ivy. She steals nothing and lies badly now and lives in a valley where the bread rises too high and the border stones are doors. She loves a man who spent his whole life carrying other people's worst days so they wouldn't have to, and who has finally set them all down, and who laughs more easily than any person I have ever known—which I think is what happens when you stop hiding.*

*Come and see us. The archways are open. There is no hunt anymore, no Accord to break, no names to surrender. Just come, and knock, and use my real name at the door. It's the only one I have left and I find I'm not ashamed of it.*

*Your daughter,
Ivy*

She read it three times. Then she folded it, and sealed it with plain wax—no crest, no signet, just the pad of her thumb pressed into the cooling drop—and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched since she was sixteen years old.

Footsteps on the path. Theo came up through the gorse with two cups of cider steaming in the cold, and settled beside her against the archway's shoulder without asking, which was another thing the weeks had taught them: how to share silence like a coat.

"How many left?" she asked.

He counted on his fingers, an old habit. "Four. Two travelers whose kin are in the capital—one of those goes by post now, can you believe it, Maren figured out how to fold a memory into wax seal. One soldier from Voss's company who deserted rather than burn Lowfield; I'll walk that one through the arches myself next week. And—" he paused, and Ivy watched him decide to finish the sentence, which was also new, this man who had once buried truths so deep he'd lost the shovel— "and mine. My childhood. The last piece."

"You're afraid of it."

"Aren't you supposed to be bad at lying now?" He smiled, but it didn't reach all the way. "Yes. I'm afraid of it. Everyone else got their worst days back and survived them. Mine might be the best day I never had. What if it doesn't fit? What if I put it on and I'm someone else entirely?"

Ivy leaned her head against his shoulder. Beyond the archway, the exile lands were lighting their lamps one by one, and behind them Vaelmoor was doing the same, and for a moment the two valleys looked like a single conversation conducted in firelight.

"Then you'll be someone who was loved first," she said. "Before anything else. Before the thefts and the hiding and the war. That's what a childhood is, Theo. It's proof you started out loved. Everything after is just what happened to the proof."

He was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "When?"

"Whenever you're ready. But—" she nudged him, "—Ondine Ashfall arrives tomorrow. And she claims to remember your mother better than anyone living. If you're going to meet your own four-year-old self, I'd want an aunt standing by."

* * *

Serah Voss resigned her commission on a Tuesday, in the chapel, in front of the whole valley, because she was a woman who had never learned to do things quietly and had decided, at last, that this was not entirely a flaw.

Ivy stood at the back and watched her lay the captain's insignia on the altar rail like setting down something hot. Voss had grown thinner since the eclipse; the certainty that had armored her for twenty years had cracked along the fault line of one burned hamlet and one living anchor, and what showed through the crack was not weakness but something rawer—a woman seeing clearly for the first time and hating the view.

"I was told the gifted caused the war," she said to the assembled valley, her voice carrying the way voices do in chapels, whether you want them to or not. "I was told it by my teachers, my officers, my king. I built my life on being told. Then I walked through an archway and met people my orders called monsters, and they fed me dinner." A ragged half-laugh went through the pews. "I have resigned. I will not leave—I know I am not owed a place here, and I will earn one anyway if it takes me the rest of my life. I have one skill. I can find people. From today I will use it only to find the lost, never to hunt the found. That is my whole confession. Judge it as you like."

Old Tam Weller stood up. The whole chapel held its breath—everyone knew what Lowfield meant, everyone knew what this woman's soldiers had done. Tam walked the length of the aisle slowly, stopped in front of Voss, and held out a hammer.

"Barn-raising starts Saturday," he said. "You've got strong arms. Use them."

Voss took the hammer with both hands, the way you take communion, or a verdict.

Afterward, in the churchyard, Ivy found her turning the insignia over and over in her pocket—she hadn't been able to leave it on the rail after all. Ivy understood. Some things you couldn't set down until you'd found somewhere to put them.

"My letter to my father went out this morning," Ivy said. "First honest one in six years. I fully expect it to kill me."

Voss almost smiled. "What did you tell him?"

"That I'm not sorry. That I love him anyway. Both at once." Ivy watched a crow cross the pale sky. "Turns out that's allowed now. Nobody's making anybody choose."

"No," Voss said quietly. "Nobody's making anybody choose." She looked at the hammer in her hands. "Do you know what I keep thinking about? The night at the inn. Spiced wine. I sat across from him and I *knew*, Ivy, I knew something was wrong with that boy, and he handed me a memory of innocence and I drank it like a fool, and the terrible thing—the truly terrible thing—is that it worked. Because he was innocent. That's what I couldn't prove and couldn't stop suspecting. The guilt I was hunting in him was never his. It belonged to everyone who'd ever handed him their worst day and walked away lighter." She closed her fist around the insignia. "I hunted a man for carrying what we refused to hold."

"He doesn't hate you," Ivy said. "I've asked. He says hating you would mean keeping you, and he's done keeping things."

Voss breathed out slowly, and something in her shoulders let go—an inch, no more, but Ivy had learned to measure salvation in inches.

* * *

Three days later, Lady Ondine Ashfall came through the seventh archway wearing traveling gray and carrying a wrapped bundle against her chest the way you carry an infant, or a wound.

Ivy met her at the foot of the hill. The lady of House Ashfall was older than Ivy had expected and younger than her reputation, silver-streaked hair braided with copper wire, eyes the color of the moon two nights before it fills. She looked at Ivy for a long moment and then inclined her head with the precise courtesy of someone raised in a court that no longer existed.

"You're the one who wrote to the crown," she said. "And then unsent it."

"Withdrew it. There's a difference. Unsent means pretending it never existed. Withdrawing means owning that it did."

Something warmed in the lady's face. "My sister would have liked you." She shifted the bundle in her arms. "Is he ready?"

"He says yes. His hands say he isn't sure. I told him ready isn't a feeling, it's a decision you make with shaking hands."

Ondine laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and for a moment Ivy saw the family resemblance to a man she'd never met: Theo's mother, alive in the angle of that smile.

They climbed together through the gorse. Theo was waiting beneath the archway, and Ivy saw that he had dressed carefully, in the good shirt Ivy had bullied him into buying, and that his hands were indeed shaking, and that he had come anyway.

Ondine unwrapped the bundle. Inside, nested in wool, lay a memory—not a shard this time but a small sphere of moon-pale glass, and inside the glass, faint as a figure behind frosted panes, a woman's silhouette rocked a child. Humming. Even at ten paces Ivy could almost hear it, a lullaby in a language the valley had forgotten.

"She sang it to me too," Ondine said softly. "Different words. Same tune. Sisters borrow." She held it out. "This is yours, nephew. It was always yours. We only kept it safe."

Theo took the sphere in both hands. Ivy watched his thumb trace the glass the way you'd touch a sleeping face. The wind moved through the gorse. Somewhere below, the valley bells rang the hour, thirteen strokes now, and no one flinched at the number anymore.

"When I give the others back," he said, "they go to their owners and I feel them leave, like setting down weights. What happens with this one?"

Ondine's voice gentled further. "With this one, you don't give it away. You take it in. It's the only return that flows the other direction, Theo. This is the one that fills you instead of emptying you." She stepped closer. "It will hurt. Truth usually does. And then, I promise you—it will fit."

Theo looked at Ivy. She nodded. *Shaking hands,* she mouthed. *Decide anyway.*

He lifted the sphere to his forehead, closed his eyes—

—and Ivy saw the light of it move through him like dawn moving through a window, slow and total, and heard him make a sound she had never heard him make in all their months of knowing each other: small, and wordless, and unmistakably the sound of a four-year-old being sung to.

The lullaby rose around them all, spilling out of him into the evening air, and the archway stone hummed in harmony, and far below in the valley and far beyond in the exile lands, lamps flickered brighter for no reason anyone could name.

When Theo opened his eyes, he was crying and laughing at once, and he said, in a voice Ivy would remember for the rest of her life:

"I know her name. I know *my* name. I've had it this whole time—I just couldn't hear it over everything I was carrying."

Below them, on the road from the capital, impossibly soon, impossibly hopeful, a travel coach crested the hill with the Fenwick arms on its door.
