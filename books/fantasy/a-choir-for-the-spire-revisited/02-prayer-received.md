## 2. Prayer Received

The bells of the Spire had not rung for anything but funerals in Odile Oyelaran's lifetime, so when she heard them pealing at dawn — bright, reckless, unpracticed, as though the ringers themselves had forgotten how joy was done — her first instinct was professional: someone had died badly enough to require celebration.

Her second instinct was to count the strokes. Old habit. The Vellum Guild taught its clerks to count everything, because everything in the city of Coriel was eventually a line item, and the guild's ledgers did not tolerate approximation. She stood at her window above the Saltmarket with her hands still smelling faintly of last night's candle smoke and ledger ink, and she counted eleven strokes before the pattern dissolved into exuberant chaos, and she thought: *eleven. Eleven is an answer number. Eleven is what you get when you ask the old gods for a sign and they are feeling generous.*

Then she told herself she was being foolish, dressed quickly, and went down into a city that had already lost its mind.

* * *

The Saltmarket on any ordinary morning was a negotiation between fish and salt and the people who could afford neither. This morning it was a river running the wrong direction — everyone flowing toward the Spire, toward the well at its foot, and Odile let herself be carried along with the crowd because a clerk who does not witness events cannot price them, and because, if she were honest with herself, which she made a practice of never being before noon, she wanted to see it with her own eyes.

She heard the story three times before she reached the square, each telling already growing ornaments.

A beggar named Aldous Wren — *no, a lamplighter, no, a beggar, he's been at the Spire steps for twenty years* — had gone to the well at the hour before dawn, as he did every morning, and prayed. Not a grand prayer. Not a prayer anyone would have bothered to write down. He had asked, as he asked every morning, to hear his daughter's voice again. His daughter had died in the fever years, when she was nine, and every morning since he had knelt at the well's lip and said good morning to her and told her one small thing about his day, and every morning the well had answered with silence, because that was what wells did, that was what gods did, which was to say nothing at all.

Except this morning the water had spoken back.

In her daughter's voice.

The third telling added that the whole well had glowed like a lamp underwater, which Odile privately doubted, because by the time she arrived there was no glow, only a crowd six deep around a stone lip worn smooth by three centuries of thirsty hands, and Aldous Wren himself sitting on the bottom step of the Spire with a look on his face that Odile recognized from the guild's intake rooms — the look of a man holding something precious and breakable and entirely beyond his understanding, waiting for someone to tell him what it was worth.

Someone had put a blanket around his shoulders. Someone else had brought him bread he wasn't eating. A woman was weeping into her apron two steps below him, and a boy of perhaps twelve was crouched at the well itself, cupping water in both hands and shouting into it — *"Hello? Hello? Is anyone there?"* — with the terrible hopefulness of children, and getting back nothing but his own echo, and laughing anyway.

Odile worked her way to the front. She was small and gray-cloaked and carried the particular authority of a woman with ink-stained fingers, and the crowd parted around her the way crowds do for people who seem to have business.

The well water was clear. That was the first thing she noticed, and it mattered more than she could yet say why: it was *clear*, not dark, not deep-shadowed the way the Spire well always was, but lit from somewhere beneath as though the morning sun had found a way in past all reason. And the water moved. Not rippling — moving with intention, turning slowly in the shaft like a page being turned.

"Say something to it," someone whispered, and a dozen voices hushed the whisperer, because you don't command a miracle, everyone knows that, you wait for it.

Odile leaned over the lip. Her reflection looked up at her, thin-faced, sharp-eyed, thirty-one years old and composed as a balanced column of figures. Beneath her reflection the water turned its slow pages.

And she heard it.

Not words, at first. A timbre. A quality of voice, if voice it was — something that lived underneath sound the way grain lives underneath wood, a resonance she felt in her teeth and in the flat bone behind her sternum. It was low and vast and patient and *grieving*, grief stretched over such depth and duration that it had stopped being an emotion and become a place, a country you could live in, and Odile knew that country. She had visited it every night for nine years. She kept it in a jar on the seventh floor behind a false wall in Vault Nine, labeled in her own handwriting with nothing but a date, and she had never once opened it, and she knew its voice the way you know a house you grew up in even after the house is gone.

The water spoke.

*"Little one."*

Two words. They rose through the well not as echo but as arrival, and they were spoken in the voice of a nine-year-old girl, Aldous Wren's dead daughter, and they were also — beneath, beneath, *beneath* — spoken in the other voice, the jar-voice, the vast grieving patience, the way a single violin note contains the whole orchestra's tuning. The little girl's voice was a mask worn by something enormous, or perhaps a window cut into something enormous, and through it Odile could see — could *hear* — the shape of the thing behind.

Around her, the crowd came apart with joy. People fell to their knees. The woman in the apron wailed aloud. The boy shouted *"I knew it! I KNEW IT!"* until his mother clapped a hand over his mouth, half in reverence, half in embarrassment. Somewhere a priest of the New Concord was pushing through the throng with his vestments half-fastened, face shining with a terror he was trying very hard to experience as ecstasy.

Odile did not kneel. Odile stood very still with her hand pressed flat against the cold stone of the well's lip, and felt wonder curdle in her chest like milk left out in summer, because she alone in that square understood what the miracle meant, and it was not *the gods have returned.*

It was *something has touched my jar.*

* * *

She stayed until midday. She was a professional; she took notes; she did it with her body angled so no one could see the paper.

Facts, as gathered:

One. The voice belonged to Maren Wren, deceased nine years, age nine at death, confirmed by three independent witnesses including the parish death-roll (which Odile consulted within the hour, because a miracle without documentation is merely a rumor). Two. The voice answered questions — simple ones, childlike ones. What is it like where you are. Do you remember the song. Did you get the flowers Papa leaves. Each answer came up through the water in that sweet doubled voice, and each answer was correct, and each answer was also, Odile noted with a clerk's cold precision, *vague*. What is it like where you are: *"Warm. You can run here."* Do you remember the song: *"All of it. Every note."* No specifics. No new information. Nothing that could not have been known already, remembered already, reconstructed already from a grieving father's twenty years of one-sided conversations at the well's lip.

Three. The voice did not say anything Maren Wren had ever been recorded as saying. It said things in her *manner*. And under the manner ran the other thing, the deep thing, the thing Odile had bought for four coppers' worth of candle wax and a forged provenance slip eleven years ago from a dying smuggler who had stolen it from a ruin that predated the Exile, the thing she had hidden off the books ever since because every instinct she owned had screamed at her that this was not merchandise, this was not inventory, this was a *relic*, and relics of the old gods got entire guilds burned to their foundations.

Four. At noon, the voice stopped. Mid-sentence, almost — Aldous Wren had been asking whether she was cold at night, and the water had gone still and dark and ordinary, and the silence that fell over the square was the loudest sound Odile had ever heard.

Five. In that final moment before the silence, the deep voice beneath the child's voice had risen close to the surface, close enough that for one heartbeat the whole square heard it — not words, just a tone, a vast slow sorrow like a bell struck underwater — and several people fainted, and one old woman began singing a hymn that hadn't been sung in three hundred years, a hymn to the god of thresholds and small mercies, and she sang it perfectly, though she claimed afterward she didn't know how she knew it.

Odile wrote that down too. Her hand was steady. Her hand was always steady. Inside, she was doing arithmetic that would not resolve.

Because here was the arithmetic: the memory in her jar was the final memory of a dying god. She had never opened it — opening a stored memory releases it, degrades it, spends it like coin — but she had held the sealed jar against her ear once, eleven years ago, alone in the vault, in a moment of weakness she had never repeated and never forgiven. And through the glass she had heard a hum. A resonance. A vast, patient, grieving tone.

It was the tone from the well. Note for note.

Which meant one of two things. Either the god whose death-memory she hoarded was not as dead as the Exile had made them, and had woken, and was reaching out through whatever water lay near — in which case her hidden theft was now the most dangerous object in the city, and every returning miracle would pull the searchers closer to the seventh floor.

Or — and this was the thought she could not finish, the sum that broke her ledger — the god was still exactly as dead as it had been, and something else was answering prayers. Something wearing the voices of the dead like the child's voice had been worn. Something that had noticed, across whatever distance separated the living world from the drowned, the small warm fact of a stolen memory kept warm in a mortal vault for eleven years.

Something was looking for it.

* * *

She walked home the long way, past the shrine-row, and the city was transforming around her as she went, and she watched it happen with the detachment of a woman watching weather she has already decided not to prepare for.

By evening there were candles at every threshold. The New Concord's priests — the tidy, reasonable faith that had spent three centuries explaining that the old gods were gone, comfortably gone, mercifully gone, and prayer was a private exercise best conducted inwardly — were holding emergency services, and the sermons had the strained brightness of men improvising theology at speed. Street preachers of the older sort, the ragged ones who had never stopped praying to the Exiled names, were being carried on shoulders through the Warrens, weeping vindication. And everywhere, everywhere, people were praying. Audibly. Openly. A cobbler stood in his doorway asking aloud for his wife's rheumatism to ease. A dockhand knelt at the harbor edge asking after a brother lost at sea. Children had turned it into a game already — pray-and-listen, pray-and-listen — because children metabolize miracles faster than adults, and are less afraid of them.

And Odile saw the other thing too, because she was Vellum Guild to the marrow and could not help seeing it: she saw the market open.

At the mouth of the Warrens, a guild factor she knew slightly — Pellam, a soft man with soft hands — had set up a folding table and was taking deposits. Not selling anything yet. Just taking names. *Register your prayer with us, friend, and when the guild establishes terms for intercession, registered clients receive priority consideration.* People were queuing. People were giving him their names and their coppers and, she noticed, in three cases out of ten, small vials and lockets and folded papers — memories, offered freely, as surety. As *payment*.

Pellam caught her eye across the street. He smiled the smile of a man who has just watched the tide come in over dry land, and raised two fingers to his brow in mock salute.

Odile did not return it. She walked on, and the guilt arrived then, finally, fully, the way floodwater arrives — not gradually but all at once, finding every low place.

Because she understood, with terrible clarity, what was happening. The gods — or whatever wore their voices — had answered one beggar's small prayer, free, unasked-for, a gift. And within a single day the city had begun building machinery to make sure no gift ever happened again. Machinery of queues and fees and registrations and sureties. The guild's oldest lesson, written in her own hand ten thousand times: *nothing given freely survives contact with a ledger.* And she had helped write that lesson. She had priced the griefs of this city for nine years. She had weighed widows' memories of wedding days and sold them by the ounce to buyers who wanted to know what love felt like without paying love's price. And now heaven had cracked open over a well, and the first thing her city — her guild, her craft, her own soft complicit hands — had built at the crack was a tollbooth.

And beneath the guilt, quieter and worse, sat the fear.

They would come looking. Whoever had spoken through the water, whoever had sent that doubled voice up into the daylight — they had brushed against her secret the way a hand brushes a wall in the dark, and they would come back along the touch. She thought of the seventh floor. Of Vault Nine, the false wall, the jar with its blank label in her own handwriting. She had told herself for eleven years that she kept it hidden out of prudence. Standing in the Warrens with candles blooming on every sill around her, she admitted, for the first time, the truer thing: she had kept it because some part of her had always known it was not hers to keep and not hers to sell and not hers to destroy either — that she was not the owner of that memory at all.

She was only the vessel it had been poured into. Or the vault it had been locked inside. She genuinely did not know which, and the not-knowing had begun, tonight, to feel like a debt accruing interest.

Behind her, the Spire bells rang again — not eleven strokes now, but continuous, celebratory, unhinged. Ahead of her, the guildhall windows glowed with late lamplight. Guildmaster Vane did not sleep on nights when the market moved, and the market had just moved more in one day than it had in Vane's entire tenure.

Odile turned up the guild steps, and paused at the door with her hand on the iron ring, and allowed herself one breath of the old professional calm, the calm that was itself a kind of forgery, stitched like everything else about her over something she could no longer name.

Then she went in, to find out what Vane knew — and to lie, beautifully, about the seventh floor.
