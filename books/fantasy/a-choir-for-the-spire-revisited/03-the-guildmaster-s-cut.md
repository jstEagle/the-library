## 3. The Guildmaster's Cut

The prayer reached Guildmaster Corvus Vane before the celebration did. That was his habit: news arrived at his desk the way blood arrived at a leech — pulled, not pushed. He kept six runners on the Spire's stairwells, two priests of declining faith in his pocket, and a deaf boy's sister among the well-drawers, and by the time the beggar Tam Wicke had finished weeping into the water, Vane already knew what the water had said.

He sat now behind a desk of black walnut older than the guild itself, turning a letter knife across his knuckles and listening to the city make noise. Bells. Not the alarm bells, which he knew intimately, but the other kind — the ones that hadn't rung since his grandfather's grandfather had been a rumor in his mother's belly. Celebration bells. The sound of an economy changing hands.

"Say it again," he told Pell, who stood sweating by the door with his cap crushed between his fists.

"The well on Candlemaker's Row, sir. Tam Wicke drew water for his supper like any night, and there was a voice in it. His daughter's voice. She's been dead eleven years — drowned, as it happens, though not in that well." Pell swallowed. "She sang him the lullaby he used to sing her. Two verses. He's been at the well ever since, they can't move him, and half the Row is down there listening to the bucket come up empty because nobody else gets a voice."

"Nobody else," Vane repeated softly. "Yet."

"They're saying it's the gods, sir. Saying the old ones are waking. There's a woman on the Row selling cups of well water for three coppers and she's sold out twice."

Vane set the letter knife down with great care, the way he did everything that mattered. Three coppers. A woman with a ladle and a bucket, undercutting the Vellum Guild by eleven centuries of infrastructure. He almost admired her.

"And the timbre," he said. "Describe the timbre."

Pell blinked. "Sir?"

"The voice in the water. Was it thin? Sweet? Did it echo strangely, as if heard through cloth?" Vane laced his fingers. "Every memory has a grain, Pell, like wood. You've handled enough jars in the receiving room to know that much. So tell me about the grain of this miracle."

Pell's brow furrowed with the effort of thought, a labor Vane rarely asked of him. "It was... warm, I'd say. Like standing near an oven. And it had a — a weight to it. People said it felt *true*, more true than remembering. Like the thing remembered you back."

Vane closed his eyes.

He knew that warmth. He knew that grain. He had held it once, twenty-two years ago, in a jar labeled with handwriting that wasn't his, in a vault that officially did not exist, on a floor whose stairs were struck from the guild's own plans. He had been a younger man then, newly risen, still capable of being afraid, and Odile Oyelaran's predecessor — a soft, superstitious auditor named Brannick who had died conveniently of a fever — had shown him the ledger line that wasn't there. *One item, unweighed. One item, unpriced. Held in trust against a debt no one living could collect.*

A god's last memory. The death of one of the old ones, folded small enough to fit in a mortal skull, hidden down a mortal bloodline the way a knife is hidden in a body.

And now the old ones were answering prayers, and somewhere below his feet, in a candlelit vault where his best auditor was probably this very moment pretending her hands didn't shake, sat the reason why.

"Pell," Vane said pleasantly, "you may go. On your way out, send word to every floor-warden: full inventory audit, all pre-Exile holdings, effective tonight. Nothing leaves the building. Nothing is bought, nothing is sold, nothing is pawned until I say otherwise. Anyone who objects may bring their objections to me personally, and I will explain the economics to them slowly."

Pell went, visibly relieved to be dismissed from a conversation he understood only a tenth of.

Vane remained at his desk while the bells rang themselves hoarse, and permitted himself, for exactly one hundred heartbeats, to feel the thing he actually felt.

Fear. Clean, cold, clarifying fear — the same feeling he imagined a man might have upon learning the river under his house had changed course in the night. For three hundred years the Vellum Guild had built its fortune on a simple truth: the gods were dead, and grief was the only miracle left, and grief could be weighed, priced, and pawned. Every contract in his archives rested on that truth like a house on pilings. And now the pilings were singing lullabies up through the water table.

But fear, properly cut and polished, became opportunity. It always had. That was the whole of Vane's creed, learned at fourteen in a debtor's cellar when he'd traded away his memory of his mother's face and discovered, in the hollow afterward, how much lighter a man could travel. Everything was currency. Even terror. Especially terror — it was the one commodity every customer possessed and none wished to keep.

The gods were waking. Very well. Then someone would have to stand between the waking and the world, holding out his hand, saying: *everything has a toll.*

* * *

He found the seventh-floor ledger where he always found it — or rather, where he always pretended to find it, since he had known its location for two decades and simply lacked the key that opened what it described. The audit summons went up to Odile Oyelaran by ordinary messenger, unsigned, routine, the sort of paper she processed forty of a day. But he wrote one line at the bottom in his own hand, in the old guild cipher that only wardens of her rank were taught: **Bring your discrepancies. All of them.**

Then he waited, which was the hardest work he did, and the only kind he never delegated.

* * *

She came at the hour when the tower windows went the color of tarnished copper, and she came composed, which he had expected; Odile's composure was the finest instrument in his collection, better than any memory in any vault. She stood before his desk without being offered a chair, ledger hugged to her chest like a shield she was too proud to admit was a shield, and her eyes went briefly — very briefly, a professional's glance, half a heartbeat — to the window, to the bells, to the noise of a city drunk on hope.

"You received my instruction," Vane said.

"I received an instruction to audit pre-Exile artifacts," she said. "I've begun. It will take some weeks. The cataloguing conventions of the founding era are inconsistent—"

"Odile." He said her name the way he opened a lock: precisely, with pressure applied at the single correct point. "Sit down."

She sat. Good. Chairs mattered with Odile; a seated auditor was a person conducting business, and business was the language she trusted above all others. He had watched her use that language for years, fluently, coldly, beautifully — and he had watched, these past years, the way her hand lingered sometimes over certain entries. Long entries. Old entries. Entries with gaps in them shaped exactly like a jar.

"I'll be plain with you," Vane said, "because you value plainness the way other people value piety. This morning a dead girl sang to her father through a bucket of well water. By noon the temples were full for the first time since the Exile. By evening, I expect, the first of the desperate will be trying to sell me their memories *of* the old gods — prayers said in childhood, grandmothers' rites, fragments of songs — hoping to catch the divine ear through the collateral. Business will be extraordinary. And beneath all of it, underneath the bells and the buckets and the coppers changing hands, something has woken that I have known about for twenty-two years, and so have you."

Her face did not move. Her thumb, resting on the ledger's spine, pressed white.

"There is a discrepancy in your care," Vane continued, "that has never appeared in any ledger. An unweighed item. Unpriced. Held in trust against a debt no one living can collect — although, as of this morning, I begin to wonder whether *living* remains the operative word." He leaned forward. "You will note that I am not asking you whether it exists. You will note that I am not threatening you. I want you to observe carefully what I am *not* doing, Odile, because it should tell you exactly how much I know, and exactly how long I have known it."

For a moment the only sound was the bells, fainter now, winding down toward the exhausted silence that follows any city's first good cry.

"Brannick told you," Odile said at last. Her voice was level. Her knuckles were not. "Before he died."

"Brannick told me a story. You are the story's current custodian. I have let it lie, year after year, because a sleeping debt accrues no interest and demands no payment. I am a patient man. Patience is simply greed with better posture." Vane rose and went to the window, where the whole of the lower city lay spread out below him, lamplights kindling street by street like a fever chart. "But the debt has woken up, Odile. It is singing in wells. And I find that my patience has matured into something far more profitable."

"What do you want?"

"*There* it is." He turned, smiling, and gestured her to the window beside him. Below, on Candlemaker's Row, even at this distance he could see the crowd around the well, a dark clot of bodies with torchlight moving through it, pilgrims already, on the first day. "Look at them. Look what one answered prayer does. Now imagine a thousand. Imagine the temples reopening, the old rites remembered — and remember, Odile, *who remembers things for this city.* Who holds its past in jars and sells it back by the dram. When people pray to gods they barely recall, they will need their memories of those gods restored first. And when the gods answer, they will answer according to what the supplicant carries. Which means the Vellum Guild does not merely profit from the return of the divine." He let the silence stretch. "The Vellum Guild *mediates* it. We become the bridge. And every bridge charges toll."

"That's monstrous," Odile said quietly.

"It's *bookkeeping*," Vane said, with genuine feeling. "The difference between the two has never once been demonstrated to me. Monstrous is what the poor call any price they cannot pay."

* * *

He made the offer over wine he did not drink and she did not touch, because neither of them trusted the other's hospitality and both respected the other's caution — a mutual courtesy he found almost tender.

"A partnership," he said. "Equal, or near enough. You hold the asset. I hold the guild, the clients, the runners, the priests, the muscle, and the patience. Together we control the single most valuable object in the world: the death of a god, carried in the mind of a woman who has spent her life deciding what memories are worth." He spread his hands. "The kin will come for it, of course. The old ones left alive always hunt what they abandoned — guilt makes them predictable, and predictable things can be scheduled. When they arrive, they will not go to the temples. They will go to us. Because we are the ones who deal in what they need, and they will know it the way sharks know blood through a hull. And when they sit where you are sitting, wanting their sibling's last breath back — we will name our price. Peace, perhaps. Worship, codified and rationed. Or simply their absence, guaranteed by covenant, witnessed by ledger. Whatever we ask. Because whoever holds that memory holds the only thing the gods still grieve."

"And if I refuse?"

Vane studied her across the desk — the sharp face, the steady eyes, the composure worn like armor over whatever it is that auditors bury under their arithmetic. He liked her. He genuinely did, in the way a collector likes the rarest piece, which is to say he would sell her in a heartbeat and mourn her honestly afterward.

"If you refuse," he said gently, "nothing happens tonight. Nothing happens tomorrow. I am not a man who burns his ledgers. But you will do the arithmetic yourself — you're better at it than I am. A secret shared by two is a partnership. A secret shared by two when the whole sky has started answering prayers is a countdown. The kin are coming regardless, Odile. The only question is whether they find us divided, or find us *expensive*."

She rose. She gathered her ledger. At the door she paused, and did not turn around, and said in the flat clear voice she used for closing accounts:

"You should know that if it comes to a choice between your partnership and that jar, Guildmaster, the jar doesn't weigh anything at all. That's the trouble with it. It's the only entry I've never been able to price."

"Which is why," Vane said, smiling at her back, "it will finally be worth everything."

The door closed. He stood alone with the last light and listened to the city below — the bells done, the crowds murmuring, the well on Candlemaker's Row surely still crowded, the water still holding its warm and weighted grain of a dead girl's song.

Tomorrow the audits would begin, floor by floor, and every warden would report to him, and every report would pass through Odile's office first, and she would understand — she already understood — that the audit was not for finding artifacts.

It was for finding out what she would hide.

Vane poured himself the untouched second cup of wine at last, and drank to the waking gods, and began, in the old cipher, in his own careful hand, to draft the terms of a treaty the world did not yet know it would be signing.
