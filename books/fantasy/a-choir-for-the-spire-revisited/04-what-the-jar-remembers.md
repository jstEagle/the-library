## 4. What the Jar Remembers

The guild had rules for everything, and Odile had written half of them.

Rule the eleventh: no keeper opens a sealed vessel alone. Rule the twelfth: no keeper opens a sealed vessel at all, save in the weighing room, with two witnesses and a notarized intent. She had drafted both in her third year, after old Master Corvel cracked a jar of a drowned man's last afternoon and spent four days believing he was someone's wife. They had found him standing in the rain on the guild steps, holding an umbrella over nothing, saying *there, there* to the air.

So she knew better. She had always known better.

And yet here she was, past midnight, alone in her office above the weighing room, with the jar cradled in a sling of wool against her chest like something newborn, and the candle turned down to a bead of flame because — though she would never have admitted this to anyone, least of all herself — some animal part of her believed that what lived inside might be less likely to notice her in the dark.

The beggar's voice was still in her ears. It had been three days since the well spoke with a dead girl's timbre, and the city had not stopped singing since, but Odile had stopped sleeping. Every night she lay awake cataloguing the miracle the way she'd catalogue any acquisition: provenance, resonance, weight. And every night the same conclusion arrived, patient as interest accruing: *that voice and the voice in your jar are cut from the same cloth.* Not similar. Identical. The way two pages from the same printing are identical — down to the flaw in the type.

She had told herself she only wanted to verify. Verification was a professional act. Verification could be entered in a ledger under a legitimate heading. But her hands, wrapping the jar in its wool, had not moved like a professional's hands. They had moved like a woman's hands going to a letter she already knew was bad news.

She set the jar on her desk, on top of the ledger of small debts, which seemed fitting and also blasphemous, and she looked at it for a long time.

It was a modest thing. Guild-standard glass, the kind they bought by the gross, blown thick so that clumsy apprentices couldn't shatter a man's wedding day. The stopper was waxed linen over cork, sealed twice, and around the neck ran a strip of vellum bearing a single line in her own handwriting:

*Do not open. Not inventory. — O.O.*

Her handwriting. Her exact hand — the slight forward lean of it, the way her *O*s came out narrow as coin slots. She had written that label. She must have written that label. And yet she had no memory of writing it, which should have been impossible, because Odile Oyelaran forgot nothing. That was her gift and her curse and the reason Vane kept her: a memory that held other memories without bleeding into them, each one filed clean, each one retrievable whole. She could recite the guild's founding charter backward. She could produce, on request, the smell of her mother's kitchen — tallow and cardamom and river-mud off the washing.

Except.

Except that when she reached for the memory of writing this label, she found the shelf where it should stand, and the shelf was empty, and the empty shelf had a thin skin of dust on it, and dust did not belong inside a mind kept as carefully as hers.

Odile sat down slowly, the way you sit when your knees have made the decision without consulting you.

"Right," she said aloud, to the candle, because saying things aloud made them real and real things could be audited. "We'll do this properly."

Properly meant procedure. Procedure meant control. She took out her knife — the little bone-handled one she used for trimming vellum — and she did not break the seal. Breaking a seal was destruction, and destruction was entry, and entry went in the ledger. Instead she worked the wax loose with the blade's flat, softening it over the candle until it lifted away in one piece, intact, a little cap of amber she set aside like evidence. Underneath, the cork. She gripped it and pulled, gently, a quarter-turn, no more — enough to open a seam between cork and glass, a crack no wider than a hair, a keyhole rather than a door.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. Memories were not alive; they were goods. They sat in their jars the way salt sits in a cellar, inert until weighed, inert until sold, inert until some poor debtor drank his own childhood back and wept in the alley behind the guildhall because he'd forgotten he'd ever been happy. That was the trade. That was the whole trade. You could not catch a memory like a fever through a crack in a cork.

She leaned close anyway. Close enough that her breath fogged the glass, and in the fogging she saw — or thought she saw — the contents shift. Not move. *Settle*, the way sand settles when a footfall shakes the table. A slow slide of pale light within the dark of the jar, like a body turning over in sleep.

And then the smell came up through the crack.

Tallow and cardamom and river-mud.

Odile went very still. The candleflame stood straight as a nail. Somewhere below her, in the vaults, seven hundred jars sat in their niches, and none of them smelled of anything through the glass — that was the point of the glass, that was why glass and not clay or iron, because glass kept the contents *in* and the world *out*. But this smell was in the room now, unmistakable, warm, domestic, the smell of her mother's kitchen on a winter evening when the stew had gone wrong and her mother laughed about it —

— except her mother had died when Odile was six, and who remembers a kitchen at six? Who keeps, at six years old, the precise architecture of a room, the height of the mantel, the particular note of a laugh gone wrong with grief in it? Children remember fragments. Odile remembered *everything*, and she had never once, in forty years of keeping perfect records inside her own skull, asked herself the question that any auditor worth her salt would ask first:

*Where did the inventory come from?*

The crack in the cork breathed again. Or she breathed, and imagined it breathing; by then she could no longer have said which.

She should stop it down. She should press the cork home, re-wax it, write in the ledger — in a different hand, a false hand, she'd done it before, everyone had done it before — *vessel damaged during audit, contents compromised, disposed per rule nineteen.* She should do all of that, and instead she heard herself say, quietly, in the voice she used for clients who needed telling the truth:

"Show me."

The crack widened. She had not touched it.

Later — much later, when she tried to reconstruct the sequence, the way she reconstructed everything — she could not say whether the jar opened itself or whether she opened it, whether her hands moved or the memory simply poured upward through the seam like water finding its level, filling her the way water fills a bell, from the bottom, displacing everything lighter than itself. What she knew was this: one moment she was sitting at her desk with a bead of candlelight and a quarter-turned cork, and the next moment she was not in her office at all.

She was somewhere high, and cold, and ending.

* * *

The god did not know it was dying, at first, because gods do not die the way mortals die. Gods end the way cathedrals end — by degrees, by neglect, by the slow withdrawal of the people who once called them necessary — and so the being whose perception Odile now wore understood its own dissolution as weather. Something was leaving it. Something had been leaving it for a long time. Prayers arrived thinner each century, like letters from friends who had moved away, and the god answered them anyway, faithfully, spending itself to do so, drawing down its own substance the way a household burns furniture in a hard winter.

It was at war. That part came through without translation, raw as a wound: a war fought not with armies but with *belief*, god against god, sibling against sibling, each trying to be remembered more than the others, and the mortals below caught in the geometry of it, their cities burning whenever two great attentions crossed overhead. The god — she could not get its name; names were the first thing a dying thing spends — the god had grown tired of winning. Winning meant surviving, and surviving meant continuing to spend, and there was almost nothing left in the house to burn.

So it did the one thing left. It took its final memory — not a memory *of* something, but the memory that was most essentially *itself*, the last unspent coin of its treasury, the thing that would prove, if anything proved, that it had existed and had loved and had been loved — and it looked down through the smoke of the war for a place to hide it.

Not in stone. Stone got broken. Not in gold; gold got melted. Not in temples; temples were the first things burned in wars of belief. It looked for something mortals never looted, never melted, never broke open, something that renewed itself generation after generation and carried its cargo forward without knowing it carried anything at all.

A bloodline. A family. Small, obscure, unimportant — the kind of family that survives every purge precisely because no one thinks to purge them. A woman in a city by a river, a tallow-chandler's wife, pregnant with her second child.

The god gathered what remained of itself — and Odile felt that gathering from the inside, felt the terrible economy of it, the way a starving man counts coins — and it pressed its last memory down, down, through cloud and prayer and distance, into the small dark warmth of an unborn child.

And the child already had memories. Of course she did; even unborn, even newborn, a soul arrives carrying its own small seed-corn of self, the first faint impressions: warmth, heartbeat, a voice muffled through flesh, the taste of the world before the world. The god saw these — saw them the way a thief casing a house sees the household's own treasures — and it understood that its hiding place must be seamless. A foreign memory sitting beside native ones would be found. Would be dug out. Would be destroyed, and the war would flare again over its corpse.

So it did what any careful accountant does with inconvenient entries.

It struck them from the book.

Odile felt it happen. That was the worst of it — that she felt it from both sides at once, felt the god's cold necessity and felt, underneath that, faint and fading, the small native memories of the child being lifted out like stitches pulled from cloth: warmth, heartbeat, a voice muffled through flesh. Gone. Replaced. The god laid its own death down in the space where a childhood should have been, smoothed the surface, stitched the edges neat, and whispered over its work the way any craftsman whispers: *no one will ever know.*

No one ever knew. For three hundred years, no one ever knew.

* * *

She came back to herself on the floor.

She did not remember falling. She was on her side against the boards, one arm flung out, the candle guttering low, and the jar stood on the desk above her with its cork fully out, and the smell of tallow and cardamom filled the room like a congregation.

For a long time she lay there and did not move, because moving required a self to do the moving, and the question of what self remained was suddenly, catastrophically open.

Her childhood was not hers.

She tested it the way you test a tooth that has gone bad, pressing and pressing despite the pain. The kitchen: forged. The mantel's height: forged. Her mother's laugh, gone wrong with grief — that laugh had belonged to a dying god, had been the god's own memory of some other laughter, some other grief, worn like a borrowed coat and passed down to her as authentic. The stew gone wrong. The river-mud off the washing. Cardamom. All of it, every treasured detail she had kept polished through forty years of careful keeping, was a dead stranger's property arranged to look like hers.

And worse — worse, because it was professional and the professional part of her was the last part still standing — worse was the arithmetic underneath. If the god had replaced her infant memories wholesale, then the person those memories built was a structure raised on stolen foundations. Her patience, her precision, her habit of keeping accounts — where had those come from? A chandler's bloodline, or a god's long habit of tallying prayers received and answered? Was her love of ledgers *hers*, or was it residue, sediment, the dead god's nature seeping up through the forgery like damp through plaster?

She thought of the label in her own handwriting. *Do not open. Not inventory.*

She had written it. She must have written it. And now she understood, with a lurch that had nothing to do with the floorboards, that she could not have written it — that the hand on that vellum was her hand the way her childhood was her childhood: accurate, convincing, and possibly authored by something else entirely. Perhaps the god had written it, through her, on some night she had no record of. Perhaps the god had left instructions inside its own hiding place, the way smugglers leave maps sewn into coats. *Do not open. Not inventory.* Not because opening was dangerous.

Because opening was *working*. Because the seal was meant to hold until — until what? Until the war was truly over? Until the kin were dead or scattered beyond finding?

Until tonight. Until a beggar's prayer got answered at the Spire and the well spoke in a dead girl's voice, and the timbre of that miracle matched the timbre in the jar, and Odile Oyelaran, keeper of ledgers, auditor of souls, had walked alone past midnight to a sealed vessel and said *show me* in the voice she used for telling truths.

The kin. The thought arrived cold and complete, the way the worst thoughts do. The god had had siblings. The war had been fought among siblings. And if prayers were being answered again — really answered, after three hundred years of silence — then the silence was over, and the ones who survived the war were waking, and waking things go looking for what was lost.

They would come looking for the jar. And the jar, she understood now, was not in the vaults, was not anywhere the guild's books could name, because she had hidden it from her own ledgers — or it had hidden itself through her — and the only trail to it ran through the one person alive who carried its contents stitched into her childhood.

They would come looking for *her*.

Odile got up off the floor. It took three attempts and she noted each one distantly, the way she noted everything now, from a strange remove, as though she were auditing someone else's collapse and would file a report later. She righted the chair. She retrieved the cork from where it had rolled. She looked at the open jar for a long moment — at the pale light settling in its depths, quiet now, sated, having shown her exactly as much as it wished — and then she did the only thing her hands still knew how to do.

She sealed it. Cork, wax, the little amber cap of the old seal pressed back over the new. And beneath the jar, on the ledger of small debts, she wrote a fresh line in her own hand — her true hand, she insisted to herself, her *own* — and the line read:

*One (1) item, provenance disputed. Owner unknown. Custodian: myself. Status: no longer certain I am not the item.*

She stared at the words. Then, because the alternative was to sit down again and not get up, she blew out the candle and stood in the dark listening to the building breathe — the vaults below, seven hundred sleeping strangers, the whole guild full of other people's yesterdays stacked to the rafters — and she thought about the empty shelf in her mind where the memory of writing the first label should have been.

Dust did not belong in her head. Dust meant time passing unrecorded. Time passing unrecorded meant gaps, and gaps meant that other things might be missing too — other nights, other labels, other instructions written through her by a patient dead hand.

Somewhere below, faint through the floorboards, she heard the front bell of the guild ring.

Once. Twice. A third time, unhurried, courteous — the ring of someone who had all night and knew exactly whom they'd come to see.

At this hour, the guild received no honest visitors.
