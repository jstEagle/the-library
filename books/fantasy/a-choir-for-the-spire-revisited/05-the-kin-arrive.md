## 5. The Kin Arrive

The body Ashen wore had been a customs clerk named Pellam Ostrick for thirty-one years before Ashen arrived in it, and it still smelled of ink and cheap tallow and the particular staleness of a man who ate his lunch at his desk. Ashen found the smell useful. It was a disguise of a second kind — not merely a borrowed face but a borrowed *ordinariness* — and ordinary things were never stopped at doors.

He had chosen the body carefully, as one chooses a coat from a rack. Pellam had been small, gray, forgettable; he had died at his desk in the Customs House on a Tuesday with a pen still in his hand, and no one had noticed for six hours, which told Ashen everything he needed to know about how well this shape would serve. Grief had hollowed the man's face into something apologetic. His hands trembled slightly, always, and people read that trembling as weakness, or age, or nerves — never as what it actually was: a mortal frame straining to hold something that remembered when mountains were soft.

Ashen walked through the morning streets of Vhalcrest and felt the resonance like a hand pressed flat against the back of his neck.

It was stronger today. Three days since the beggar's prayer had been answered at the Spire — three days since a dead girl's voice had risen out of well water and spoken her father's name — and the city had gone strange with joy. Ashen heard it everywhere: in the taverns where men toasted "the gods' return," in the temple squares where priests who had spent generations maintaining beautiful empty rituals suddenly found their incense *meaning* something, in the laughter of children who did not know they were laughing at a funeral.

Because that was what it was. That was what none of them understood.

A god answering prayer was not a dawn. It was a wound reopening. And Ashen knew the wound's exact shape, because he had helped make it.

* * *

He had been born — if *born* was the word, and Ashen had never settled on one — in the last century of the war, when the sky over the Shattered Marches burned in colors that had no names left, because the languages that held those names had been eaten along with everyone who spoke them. He had been young, as his kind measured youth. Young enough to believe the war could be won by retreat. When the tide turned against them — when the mortals' new gods of iron and ledger and law began unmaking the old ones one by one — Ashen's generation had done the arithmetic every dying thing does, and chosen survival.

They had run. Not all at once. Not honestly. They had run in stages, each withdrawal dressed as strategy, each abandonment called a regrouping. And in the final stage there had been one of theirs — Ashen would not write the name even in the privacy of his own mind; names had weight, and this name had been buried under three centuries of deliberate silence — one of theirs who would not run. Who stayed behind at the Spire of the old faith to hold the door, so to speak, while the others escaped into whatever thin refuges remained: deep water, old stone, the bodies of the willing and sometimes the unwilling.

That one had died. Of course that one had died. But death, for their kind, was not an ending so much as a *dispersal*, and somewhere in that dispersal — in that last burning moment — the dying god had done something no one had thought to do. It had taken its final memory, the memory of its own death, the memory of *them*, of who abandoned whom, and hidden it. Folded it small. Sealed it in clay and blood and set it inside a mortal bloodline like a coal banked in ash.

They had searched. For three hundred years, in whatever fragmentary ways remnants can search, they had searched. And then, three days ago, a beggar named Coram Whist had knelt at a dry well in Vhalcrest and prayed to a god three centuries dead, and the well had answered him in his daughter's voice — and Ashen, sitting in a dead clerk's body in a rented room above a chandler's shop, had felt the answer pass through the world like a bell struck in another room, and had known two things at once.

First: the hidden memory had moved. Had woken. Had *spoken*.

Second: it was here. In this city. Close enough that its voice carried.

* * *

The Vellum Guild occupied a wedge of buildings between Coin Street and the old canal, its façade of pale stone so scrupulously clean it looked less maintained than *chastened*. Ashen stood across the street for most of an afternoon, leaning on the rail of the fishmonger's stall (the fishmonger had long ago stopped trying to move him along), and watched the traffic of debtors and clerks flow in and out beneath the carved lintel.

The lintel was what decided him. Most passersby saw only decorative scrollwork, the kind of flourishes masons added to justify their fees. Ashen saw script. Old script. The kind of script that predated the Exile by a thousand years and had been forbidden since, and someone had carved it into the front of a memory-broker's office and let moss soften it until no living scholar could read it.

Ashen could read it.

*We keep what is given,* said the lintel. *We weigh what is kept.*

And beneath, smaller, worn nearly to nothing, in a hand that shook as it cut:

*And we hold what must not be found.*

Ashen smiled with Pellam Ostrick's mouth, which was a disquieting thing to do, and crossed the street.

* * *

The Guild's reception hall smelled of vellum, lamp oil, and under it all — faint, deliberate, masked with citrus — the cold mineral scent of the vaults below. A junior clerk with ink-stained cuffs looked up as Ashen approached the counter, and his face performed the small civic struggle between suspicion and servility that faces performed in such places.

"Good morning," Ashen said. His voice came out mild, slightly nasal, apologetic. Pellam's voice. "I wonder if you might help me. I'm with the Harbor Revenue Office — customs assessment, third division." He produced papers. He had made the papers himself, and they were perfect, because he had copied them from genuine ones and then improved them; mortals never checked the details that mattered, only the ones that didn't. "We're conducting a valuation survey of commercial premises along the canal district. Standard procedure. I'll need to see your storage levels, your inventory manifests, and speak briefly with whoever manages your holdings."

The clerk's eyes flicked to the papers, to the trembling hands holding them, to the gray apologetic face above them. "Valuation survey," he repeated. "I haven't heard of—"

"Third division rarely announces itself," Ashen said gently. "That's rather the point of a third division."

It took four minutes. Mortal institutions were wonderful that way: built entirely of doors, and every door opened by the fear of having refused someone important. By the end of it the clerk was escorting him personally down a corridor lined with framed certificates of guild charter, talking nervously about the audit schedule, and Ashen was listening with half his attention while the other half lay flat against the building itself, reading it the way a blind man reads a face.

Stone remembered pressure. Wood remembered weight. And this building — this building remembered *containment*. Something below had been held here a long time, held so long the walls had grown around the holding the way a tree grows around a nail. Seventh floor, the architecture murmured, in the wordless way architecture does. Or beneath it. Something kept apart from the other keeping.

Something that had spoken three days ago.

* * *

They gave him a small room and a succession of staff, as he had requested — "just brief interviews, quarter hour each, standard questionnaire" — and Ashen conducted them with the patience of a man who had once waited out a siege of ninety years.

The first was an appraiser named Mistress Halloway, a narrow woman with spectacles and the particular pride of someone very good at an unpleasant job. She described the weighing process in loving detail: how memories were decanted into lead-glass jars, how the jar's weight against a counterweight of blessed tin gave the memory's *density* — grief ran heavy, joy ran light, and shame, she said with a thin smile, ran heavier than either. She showed him sample ledgers with entries in a small, precise hand.

Ashen turned the pages slowly. "You handle many pre-Exile items?"

"Rarely. Pre-Exile memories are mostly government-sealed. Why? Looking to buy?" Her smile sharpened. "Everyone asks eventually."

"Only cataloguing," Ashen said mildly, and asked her about the seventh floor, and watched her face do something interesting — a flicker, a smoothing-over, the professional blankness of a woman who had learned which questions were not questions. "Storage overflow," she said. "Nothing assessed. Nothing of interest."

"Of course," Ashen agreed, and wrote a note he did not need to write, because writing notes was part of being Pellam Ostrick, and Pellam Ostrick was proving to be an excellent coat.

* * *

The second interview was a vault-tender, a big slow man named Brakka who sweated through his shirt and answered in monosyllables until Ashen asked him, off the questionnaire, whether he'd ever heard anything from the sealed floors.

Brakka's sweat changed character. "Sealed floors don't talk."

"No," Ashen agreed. "Though I've heard the strangest story. About a well. On the Spire square."

"Everybody's heard it." Brakka shifted. "Girl's voice in the water. Miracle, they're saying. Priesthood's saying it's the old gods come back." He laughed, uncertainly. "Old gods. What'd they ever do for anyone?"

"What indeed," Ashen said softly, and Brakka, who had spent his life around contained things and had developed the animal sense such work gives, suddenly would not meet his eyes. "Thank you," Ashen said. "You've been very helpful."

"I haven't told you anything."

"No," Ashen said pleasantly. "But you wanted to. That's better than telling. Telling can be denied later. Wanting can't."

He let Brakka leave first. Through the little room's high window he watched the man cross the yard below, walking quickly, and pause once — just once — to look back up at the window. Not afraid, exactly. *Recognizing.* Some animal part of Brakka had understood what sat across the desk from him, and would spend the rest of the evening deciding whether to say so to anyone, and Ashen was content to let him wrestle with it. Fear traveled. Fear was a courier that always delivered.

* * *

The third interview was a boy.

He came in sideways, the way deaf people often entered rooms — checking sightlines first, reading the space before committing to it. Young, perhaps sixteen, with quick clever hands and hair cropped short, and when Ashen greeted him aloud the boy tilted his head with the frank impatience of someone who had already read Ashen's lips and found the greeting wanting.

"You're the revenue man," the boy signed-and-mouthed, in the fluid hybrid speech of the profoundly deaf. "Halloway says you ask weird questions. She says watch out for you." He grinned, entirely without fear. "So. Weird questions. Go."

"And you are?"

"Tovi. Apprentice, second class. I do the running and the carrying and the reading of lips, which is most of the actual work around here, though nobody puts it in the ledgers." He dropped into the chair backwards, arms folded on its back. "Ask, then."

Ashen studied him. The boy was a distraction, an irrelevance, a mote — and yet. There was a quality to him. Deafness in a house full of whispers made a person valuable in ways the guild probably hadn't fully priced, and Ashen had lived long enough to know that the smallest creatures in any structure were usually the ones who knew where all the cracks were.

"Tovi," Ashen said, shaping each word clearly for the boy's reading eyes. "If something were hidden in this building — something old, something that didn't want to be found — who would know?"

Tovi's grin faded by degrees, not into fear but into consideration, which was more interesting. "Depends who hid it," he said. "If the guild hid it, the Guildmaster knows, and maybe the head archivist, and nobody else, and everybody else pretends harder than you've ever seen anybody pretend." He shrugged. "If somebody *else* hid it — hid it *inside* the guild, using us like a wall to hide a hole in — then maybe nobody knows. Maybe just one person. Maybe somebody who's very good at pretending too." He cocked his head. "Why? You think there's something like that here?"

"Why would you think I think that?"

"'Cause you walk like the building owes you money," Tovi said, "and you look at the floor seams like you're counting them. Seventh floor's got different seams. Older. You noticed." He stood, slinging his satchel. "I didn't tell you that. Halloway'd skin me." At the door he paused, and looked back, and for a moment the flippancy fell away and something older looked out of the young face — something that had spent a lifetime watching mouths and learning what people concealed. "You're not revenue," Tovi said quietly. "I don't know what you are. But you shake like Pellam Ostrick used to shake, and Pellam Ostrick's dead, 'cause I carried flowers to his sister when he died. So whatever you're wearing—" he tapped his own throat, "—wear it kinder. People here get hurt easy."

He left. Ashen sat for a long time in the small room, listening to the building breathe around its secret, feeling the resonance hum against the back of his borrowed neck like a struck bell fading toward its next strike.

The child knew. Not everything — perhaps not even much — but the shape of it. And the child had told him anyway, out of some reckless integrity or some instinct that honesty offered protection, and either way it meant the household around the secret was thinner-walled than its keepers believed.

* * *

Evening came down over Vhalcrest in the color of tarnished copper, and Ashen walked out through the guild's courtyard past the canal, where the water carried lamplight in long shivering ribbons. He paused at the rail. Below, among the reflections, his borrowed face looked back at him — gray, tired, human — and beneath it, for one unguarded instant, he let the other face surface: the one made of the light that had no names left.

Three hundred years, he thought. Three hundred years of exile thinned into rumor, of worship curdling into folklore, of his kind scattered through deep water and old stone and stolen flesh, hoarding what divinity remained like misers counting coins that bought nothing anymore. And now a well had spoken. Now prayers were landing somewhere. Now the thing they had all fled from — the truth of that last day, the door held open, the sibling who stayed behind so the rest could be cowards — was awake in a jar in a broker's vault, humming its grief into the walls.

His kin would feel it soon, if they did not already. They would come the way he had come: politely, terribly, wearing whatever they could wear. And they would want what Ashen wanted — except that Ashen had already decided what he wanted, and it was not what a kin should want.

They would want it *recovered*. Restored. Raised up again, whole, with all its memory intact — including the memory of them, of the abandoning, of the door and who held it and who walked away. Their guilt, resurrected alongside their sibling, immortal now, permanent, a wound made divine.

Ashen wanted it destroyed. Burned out of its vessel cleanly, before it woke fully, before it could speak their names. Let their sibling rest unremembered. Let the guilty stay forgotten. Mercy, he told himself, though he knew the word was load-bearing and the floor beneath it rotten.

Which meant that before his kin arrived, he needed the jar. And between him and the jar stood a guild full of professional liars, a Guildmaster whose ambition he could smell from the street, a deaf child who saw too much —

— and, somewhere in the ledgers, someone whose handwriting he had seen that afternoon in Mistress Halloway's sample books, in an entry dated eleven years back, recording the intake of an item with no provenance, no seller, no price. An entry labeled only: *held, not owned. Do not assess.*

Someone had written that entry in a hand that shook.

Ashen smiled Pellam Ostrick's apologetic smile at the dark water, and turned back toward the guild, where tomorrow he would request — courteously, terribly — an interview with the keeper of the ledgers herself.
