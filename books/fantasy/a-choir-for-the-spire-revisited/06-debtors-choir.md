## 6. Debtors' Choir

The guild kept a list of everyone it had ever unmade, and Odile had spent eleven years pretending she never read past the first column.

It was called the Redemption Roll — not redemption in any merciful sense, but the record of debts redeemed: memories pawned, priced, and paid out against, with the terms of repayment inked beneath each name in the guild's iron-gall brown. Most entries ended the same way. *Terms lapsed. Retention permanent.* Which was a clerk's way of saying the guild owned that person's wedding day now, or their mother's face, or the smell of bread in their childhood kitchen, and would keep it until someone paid — which no one ever did, because the poor did not become less poor by selling pieces of themselves.

Odile copied the roll by hand every quarter. It was part of her office. She knew its rhythms the way a tide-pilot knows a coastline.

And so, on the morning after the well sang with a dead girl's voice, she took down the roll and turned to the pages no one consulted: the old entries. The pre-Exile pawns. The ones who had sold not weddings and kitchens but *rites* — incantations, processions, the names of gods spoken at the correct hours — back when the guild still bought such things as curiosities, before the war made them contraband and then, when the gods died, made them worthless.

There were nine names. Six were crossed through with the small neat strokes that meant *deceased*. Of the three remaining, one lived in the Almshouse Ward, one had gone deaf and silent years ago according to the margin notes, and one —

One was Maren Ostrova, chandler's widow, Candle Street, debt retained since the year of the Ash Frost.

Odile dressed plainly, told Tovi she was making a collection round, and went out into a city that was still drunk on its own miracle.

* * *

Candle Street smelled of tallow and river mud, as it always had, but today there were shrines on every third doorstep. Little things, hasty things: a cup of oil with a wick floating in it, a child's shoe filled with salt, chalk circles scuffed half away by passing feet. A woman knelt at her threshold with her palms pressed flat to the stone, murmuring, and when Odile slowed — only for a moment, only from habit, the way one slows at any accident — the woman looked up with an expression Odile had never seen on a praying face before.

Hope was supposed to be thin. This woman's hope was fat and certain and terrible, like a goose fattened for slaughter.

"They're answering," the woman said, to Odile or to the street or to no one. "You heard about the well? They're *answering*. My sister's been dead six years and I asked — I just asked, I didn't even know how anymore, nobody taught us how — and last night my lamp burned green. Green! That's a sign. That's *his* sign." She said *his* the way you'd say a lover's name.

"Whose sign?" Odile asked, because her profession demanded it, and because some cold clerk's voice in her chest wanted the answer written down.

The woman blinked at her as if she'd asked whose face was on the coin. "The Kindly One's. The one who kept the thresholds." Then, softer, almost embarrassed: "I don't actually remember much about him. Nobody does. But you feel it, don't you? You feel him *coming back*."

Odile felt something coming back, yes. She had felt it since yesterday, sitting in her audit room with a jar labeled in her own handwriting, feeling the glass hum against her palms like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing.

She thanked the woman, gave her a copper she could not justify in any ledger, and walked on toward Maren Ostrova's shop.

* * *

Maren Ostrova had been beautiful once; you could see the architecture of it under everything else, the way you can see a fine building's bones through ivy and rot. She was perhaps seventy. She sat in the doorway of her candle shop because the light was better there, or because the shop itself had stopped mattering to her, and she was doing nothing at all — no work, no mending, no prayer. Just sitting, watching the street with eyes like water left standing too long.

"Mistress Ostrova," Odile said. "I'm Odile Oyelaran, from the Vellum Guild."

Something moved behind those standing-water eyes. Not fear. Recognition of a different kind — the way a debtor flinches at a creditor's knock, yes, but also something older and stranger, as if Odile's guild badge were a relic from a religion Maren had been told was extinct.

"I know what you are," Maren said. Her voice was flat, unhurried. "Come in if you're coming. The step's uneven. It's been uneven forty years and they've never fixed it."

Inside, the shop was dim and smelled of honey and smoke. Candles hung in ranks from the ceiling beams, hundreds of them, most gray with dust. Only a few near the door looked recently made, and those were wrong — Odile saw it immediately, the way a ledger-keeper sees a transposed digit. They were votive candles, god-candles, poured in the old shapes: the taper-with-seven-knots, the wide flat disc for the hearth-mother, the twisted pair for twins. Shapes that belonged in museums. Shapes the guild itself had bought up and locked away decades ago.

"You still make them," Odile said.

"I make them," Maren agreed. "I don't remember why."

She said it without self-pity, the way you'd report the weather. Odile stood in the doorway and felt the floor tilt very slightly under her professional calm.

"Your account," she began, and stopped, because Maren was already shaking her head.

"I know what I sold. Sit down, girl, you'll want to sit down. Everyone does, by the end."

* * *

What Maren Ostrova remembered, she explained, was this: that she had been a rite-singer. That her mother had been a rite-singer before her, and her grandmother, back to a grandmother whose name was one of the things Maren had sold. That in the winter of the Ash Frost, when her children were sick and the chandler she would marry was dying of the cough, a man from the Vellum Guild had come to their tenement with scales and jars and a price list, and had weighed her inheritance out of her in front of her own eyes.

"The songs themselves I kept," she said. "Isn't that the joke of it? The guild didn't want the songs. Songs are just noise unless you know what they're *for*. What they bought was the knowing. The meanings. Why the seven knots, why the disc is poured at dawn and not dusk, what words you don't say inside the circle, which god answers fast and which one makes you wait. All of that. Gone." She tapped her temple with one knuckle, twice, like knocking on a door no one answered. "I can sing the whole liturgy of the turning year, mistress clerk. Every note. And I haven't the faintest idea what any of it means. It's like being a lock with the key thrown in the river."

"Do you regret it?" Odile asked. "Selling?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment.

"My children lived," she said. "My husband lived twenty-two more years. So no. And yes. Both, all the time, in the same breath, forever. That's what they don't put in your ledgers." She leaned forward. "When they took it, it didn't hurt. That's the horror no one believes. It doesn't hurt at all. You wake up the next morning and the hole where it was is so smooth and comfortable you can't even find it with your tongue. You have to be *told* what you lost. I have a receipt, girl. Forty years I've kept the receipt, because it's the only proof I own that there was ever anything in me worth taking."

She got up, joints cracking, and fetched it from a tin on the mantel: a slip of vellum gone amber, the guild's seal still sharp, the itemized loss listed in a dead clerk's hand. *Rite-knowledge, seasonal cycle, complete. Valuation: 4 crowns, 11 shillings.*

Four crowns and eleven shillings for a thousand years of her mothers' voices.

Odile had written valuations herself. She had written thousands. She had never once watched a debtor read one back.

"Why are you here?" Maren asked suddenly, sharp as a young woman. "The guild doesn't do house calls after the sale. The guild is done with you the moment the wax cools. So why?"

Because prayers are being answered, Odile did not say. Because the thing in my vault is humming louder every hour. Because I think the war you sold me the memory of is standing up out of its grave, and I need to know what it looked like while it was still lying down.

"I'm auditing the old accounts," she said instead. "Pre-Exile items. There may be... repurchases. Buybacks. If the guild's holdings appreciate, some debts may be renegotiated."

It was a lie so smooth she almost believed it, and Maren Ostrova — who had been lied to by professionals for forty years — smiled the smile of someone who recognizes the craft.

"Buybacks," she repeated. "After all this time. And I suppose you'd like to know what else I remember. What wasn't in the jars." She sat back down heavily. "Sit, I told you. This will take telling."

* * *

What hadn't been in the jars was this: that in the last year of the old rites, before the Exile, before the war, the gods had begun to go quiet in a way the rite-singers noticed and the priests denied. That the offerings still smoked and the wells still reflected and the festivals still ran on schedule, but underneath it all, the rite-singers — the women who sang to the gods daily, who knew their temperaments the way farmers know weather — had begun to feel like servants calling to a master who has stopped answering from another room.

"And we argued about what it meant," Maren said. Her voice had changed; she was reciting now, or channeling, the cadence of a story told to her so often by her own mother that it survived the selling the way a footprint survives the foot. "Some said the gods were sleeping off the long centuries. Some said they were leaving us, going wherever gods go. And some — my grandmother's line, our line — said the quietest truth. That the gods were *afraid*. That something was coming for them, and they had decided to die quietly rather than loudly, and the rites would be the last thing to go because the rites were the last thing they loved."

"What came for them?" Odile asked. Her mouth was dry.

"That's the part we sold first," Maren said, almost gently. "That's the part they paid best for. Whatever it was — the name of it, the shape of it, whether it came from outside the world or up out of it — the guild bought that knowledge out of every singing family in the city within five years. Bought it cheap, mind, because by then everyone was desperate to forget it. You can't sell a nightmare to someone who wants to keep it. But you can buy it for pennies from someone who'd pay to lose it." She looked at Odile with those standing-water eyes. "Your guild didn't collect memories, mistress clerk. It collected *amnesia*. Warehouse-fuls of it. And now the gods are waking up, and everyone's lighting candles, and nobody — not one soul in this city — remembers what they woke up *from*."

The dust-gray candles swayed slightly in the draft from the door, though there was no wind.

Except there was. Odile realized, with a lurch, that the draft was coming from the candles themselves — that the nearest rank of them, the old-shaped votives, the seven-knots and the dawn-discs, were all leaning their flames toward her. Toward her satchel. Toward the false bottom of the satchel, where she had — stupidly, superstitiously, unable to leave it alone another night — brought the jar.

Maren followed her gaze to the flames. And then, slowly, to the satchel.

Her face did something complicated. Fear and hunger and grief, braided.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor thing. You're carrying one."

"Mistress Ostrova—"

"It's humming. Can't you hear it? No — of course you can't, you're guild-trained, they beat the hearing out of you along with everything else." Maren rose, and her age fell away from her for a moment like a dropped cloak; for one instant she was a rite-singer's daughter standing in a doorway, and her voice came out in a register Odile felt in her teeth. "Put it on the table. Not open. Just set it down. Let me stand near it. Forty years I've been a lock with the key in the river. Let me stand next to the key."

Every protocol Odile owned screamed at her. Unregistered artifact, unauthorized exposure, civilian contact, breach of containment — the guild's whole grammar of ownership. And underneath the grammar, quieter and older, something in her blood said: *let her.*

She set the jar on the table.

Nothing visible happened. The flames straightened. The hum — which Odile could now, horribly, hear, a tone below sound, a bell rung in the marrow — steadied. And Maren Ostrova closed her eyes and stood over the jar of a dead god's memory with tears running freely down her face, smiling.

"There you are," she whispered. "There you are. I don't know you. I sold not-knowing you. But my hands know you. Look — look, they're doing the shape—" Her hands rose without her permission and folded into a gesture, thumbs touching, fingers interlaced in a pattern Odile had never seen and yet recognized with a jolt that went through her like ice water: because she had done that same gesture as a child. In a childhood she had no record of. Before the records began.

Her mother's hands, making that shape. Over a cradle. Over *her* cradle.

"Maren," Odile said, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word. "Who does this memory belong to?"

Maren opened her eyes. The tears kept falling but the smile remained, serene and ruined.

"Belong?" she said. "Child, it doesn't belong to anyone. That's the whole point of it. That's what the quiet ones figured out, at the end. A god isn't a king on a throne. A god is a *song*, and songs don't die, they just wait for throats." She touched the jar's lid with one finger, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Someone put a whole god in here. And someone put *you* around it, like a second jar. And now the song's waking up and it's rubbing against the sides of you, and you came all the way across town to ask widows what you are."

The bell-tone swelled. For a moment — half a heartbeat, no more — Odile heard it resolve into something almost like a word. Almost like a name. Almost like hers.

Then it passed, and the shop was only a shop, and Maren was only an old woman wiping her face with her apron, embarrassed, ordinary, hollowed-out and grateful and strange.

"Go home, mistress clerk," Maren said. "Or don't. But whatever you do with that jar — and you'll do something, people like you always do — remember what I told you about the receipts." She tapped her temple again, that soft double knock. "Keep yours. However bad the truth is, it's better than a smooth hole."

* * *

Odile visited the other two names on the list that day, because she was thorough, and thoroughness was the last professional virtue she had left.

The almshouse client, a former bell-ringer named Corvin Hale, had sold his memory of the Spire's consecration and now could not enter the building at all — his body refused it, sweating and shaking at the threshold, though he could not say why. He sat outside the almshouse window facing the Spire all day, every day, looking at the tower he had once climbed with a god's blessing in his ears, and he told Odile, cheerfully, that he considered himself lucky: "Most men grieve what they lost. I don't even get that. I just get the *shape* of the missing, like a coat hook with no coat. Isn't that tidy?"

The third name, the deaf one, was not deaf at all. Sister Anselm of the Perpetual Lamp had sold her memory of the gods' funeral rites — the actual rites, performed once, at the end — and had then taken vows of silence so that no one could ever ask her what she'd given up, and she wouldn't be tempted to describe the hole. When Odile showed her the guild's seal, the old nun wrote a single line on her slate and pushed it across the table:

*Ask the jar what it forgives. That's the only question that matters now.*

Odile walked home through streets full of new shrines and old hunger, through crowds of the poor lining up — she could see it already, the queue forming outside the guild's public counter, three deep and growing — to sell whatever was left of themselves to whoever was listening, mortal or divine, whichever paid first.

The city had prayed, and something had answered, and now the price of survival was being weighed out by the pound on the guild's old scales, and every soul in the queue was grateful.

At the guild's doors, Tovi was waiting for her in the lamplight, signing urgently before Odile had even reached the steps: *He's asking for you. The Guildmaster. He says come up tonight. He says — * and here Tovi's quick fingers hesitated, and the boy's clever lying face went carefully blank — *he says he knows what you keep.*

Behind the guild's windows, on the seventh floor, a light was burning that Odile had never seen lit before.

She climbed the stairs.
