## 7. Vane's Bargain

Guildmaster Corvus Vane had three rules for negotiation, and he had learned them all in the same afternoon, at the age of eleven, when his mother sold him to the Vellum Guild to settle a debt of forty-one marks.

The first rule: never let them see the price you paid. The second: always know what they cannot afford to lose. The third — the one that had carried him from debt-boy to ledger-keeper to master of the most feared institution in the city of Spirefall — was this: *the man who owns the middle of any transaction owns everything.*

He was thinking about rule three as he dressed for the meeting, choosing a coat the color of wet slate with silver thread at the cuffs. Not too fine. Fine enough to say *I am your equal*, plain enough to say *I have nothing to hide*. Both statements were lies, and lies wore best when tailored.

His clerk had laid out the room an hour before dawn: the long table of black vellumwood, polished until it drank the candlelight; two chairs, one slightly lower than the other, because gods and their creatures responded poorly to looking down at anyone; and in the center, on a stand of pale bone, a single sheet of paper under glass.

Not the real map. Never the real map. The real map lived in Vane's head, where it had lived for thirty years, and it would live there until it lived nowhere at all.

* * *

Ashen arrived precisely on time, which told Vane more than any interrogation could have. Gods did not keep appointments. Mortals kept appointments. Whatever wore the customs clerk's body — a soft-bellied little man named Pellworth, with ink-stained fingers and a nervous habit of touching his collar — had been worn long enough that its habits were becoming habits of its own. That was useful. Things that grew attached to their disguises could be threatened with losing them.

"Guildmaster." Ashen's voice came out of Pellworth's mouth with a strange doubled quality, like a bell rung inside a well. "You received my letter."

"I read it twice." Vane gestured to the chair. "Sit. Drink?"

"I don't—"

"Then I will." He poured himself a measure of plum brandy, unhurried, and let the silence do its work. Silence was the cheapest tool in his kit and the sharpest. Most men filled it with concessions.

Ashen sat. Folded Pellworth's soft hands on the table. Looked at the covered stand in the center of the room and then, deliberately, away from it. Good. Curiosity disciplined was still curiosity, and discipline could be broken on schedule.

"You've been interviewing my staff," Vane said pleasantly.

"I've been *introducing myself* to your staff."

"You made my wax-sealer cry. She's worked here nineteen years and she cried into her sealing wax, which is now useless, so I'm billing you for it."

Something moved behind Pellworth's face — not quite amusement, not quite anger. A god learning what a face felt like from the inside. "I apologize. We are not practiced at gentleness. We were not built for it."

"No," Vane agreed. "You were built for worship. And then the worship stopped, and here we are, three centuries later, having brandy." He raised his glass. "To the Exile."

"Do not toast that," Ashen said quietly, and the candles guttered, all nine of them, in perfect unison. The temperature of the room dropped by a degree that Vane felt in his teeth. "We did not exile ourselves, Guildmaster. Your ancestors' war did."

"And yet." Vane set down the glass, unbothered, though he noted the cold and filed it away. Fear was data, not instruction. "Here we are. You want something in my building. I have something in my building. That is not a tragedy, Master Ashen. That is a market."

* * *

He rose and walked to the covered stand, letting his hand rest on the glass but not lifting it. Theater. All of it theater, and every step rehearsed against the mirror in his private office that morning, twice.

"Three hundred years ago," he said, "when the old gods fell, someone had to decide what happened to the debris. Prayers unanswered. Relics cooling. Memories of divinity sitting in mortal heads like coins in dead men's pockets. My predecessors decided it should be *collected*. Catalogued. Priced." He tapped the glass. "The Vellum Guild is not a memory broker because we are thieves, Master Ashen, whatever the street says. We are memory brokers because grief needs a warehouse, and no one else volunteered."

"What is under the cover?"

"A map." Vane lifted it away.

The sheet beneath was beautiful — he'd paid a cartographer's widow a scandalous sum for it — showing the guildhall in section, floor by floor, like a cake cut open. Six floors rendered in fine ink and wash: the counting rooms, the weighing hall, the vaults of common memories, the deep archives, the reliquary levels where pre-Exile artifacts slept in salt and silence.

"The first six floors," Vane said. "Every vault. Every artifact. Every scrap of pre-Exile material we hold, indexed and annotated in my own hand. Locations, wards, access rites, the names of every keeper who can open them." He watched Ashen look. Watched the clerk's breathing change — Pellworth's body responding to divine attention the way a sail responds to wind. "Yours. For a price."

Ashen looked up. "And the seventh floor?"

"There is no seventh floor."

"Your building has seven stories. I counted the windows from the street. I counted them again from the roof of the mint across the lane."

"Then you counted windows," Vane said mildly, "and windows are not floors. Some of what we keep doesn't fit in architecture. The seventh story of this building is a *ledger entry*, Master Ashen, not a room. It exists between the lines." He smiled, thin and professional. "But if it helps: the map covers everything that exists. What doesn't exist isn't for sale, because there's nothing to sell."

It was a good lie. He had spent twenty years making it true in every way that could be audited — falsified plans, bribed inspectors, a stairwell that ended in brick and a door that opened onto nothing. Odile Oyelaran thought she was the only one who knew about the seventh floor. She was almost right. Almost was the entire art of his life.

* * *

Ashen was quiet for a long moment. When Pellworth spoke again, the doubled quality had gone out of the voice, which was worse; it meant the god had stopped performing civility.

"You know what we seek."

"I know prayers are being answered for the first time since my grandmother's grandmother was ash. I know the poor are lining up at the Spire to trade whole childhoods for a loaf and a miracle. I know something of yours died here once, and left something behind, and you want it back or you want it gone — I genuinely don't know which, and I've found it profitable not to guess." Vane returned to his chair and sat, folding his hands. "What I know is that when beings like you come hunting through a city, cities burn. And I am in the business of this city, Master Ashen. Its fear, its grief, its hope — all of it flows through my ledgers. War is terrible for business. Peace, properly structured, is a monopoly."

"So you sell us the map to prevent a war."

"I sell you the map to *own* the peace." Vane let that sit. "Understand me. I am not a patriot. I am not a believer. I have met gods exactly once before today, and my opinion of the experience is that it was insufficiently itemized. But I am the only creature in Spirefall who has spent thirty years learning how mortals and divinity can transact without one side devouring the other. That knowledge is worth more than gold. It is worth more than worship. It is worth a seat at whatever covenant gets written when this ends — and it will end at a table, not a battlefield, because battlefields produce ashes and tables produce *terms*, and I only deal in terms."

"You speak of covenants," Ashen said. "My kin remember the last covenant mortals wrote for us. It was written in spear-points."

"Yes, and look how that turned out for everyone. Extinction on your side, three centuries of silence on ours, and a generation of children who grew up praying to weather." Vane spread his hands. "I'm offering you something neither side has ever had in this arrangement: a neutral party who profits from the deal holding together. Kings break treaties because treaties cost them. I will never break this one, because breaking it bankrupts me. Self-interest, Master Ashen, is the only honesty left in the world. Everything else is branding."

For the first time, Pellworth's face did something human — a twitch at the corner of the mouth that might have been the clerk's own ghost, laughing somewhere far beneath the god wearing him. "You are offering to be a toll-keeper on the road between heaven and earth."

"I prefer 'custodian of the crossing.' But yes."

"And your price?"

Vane named it. He had named it in the mirror too, and heard how it sounded, and adjusted it downward by exactly one degree so it would sound like restraint rather than appetite.

"One percent. Of every answered prayer, in perpetuity, measured however your kind measures such things — I'll leave the unit to you, you're better at metaphysics than I am. In exchange: exclusive brokerage rights. No god answers a mortal prayer except through a gift registered in my ledgers. No mortal surrenders a memory except at my scale. Every miracle weighed, priced, stamped, and taxed." He leaned forward. "You get your sibling's remains and a lawful road back into the world. Mortals get protection from indiscriminate miracles. I get the tollbooth. Everyone eats."

"That is not a covenant," Ashen said slowly. "That is a *shop*."

"All covenants are shops. The old ones just didn't print their prices." Vane stood, crossed to the window, and looked out over the morning city — the smoke, the spire, the queues already forming at the wells where the water sometimes remembered voices. "You think I'm a parasite. Fine. Consider instead: without a toll, what happens? Your kin answer every desperate prayer in the city directly. The poor give away everything they are, gladly, because hunger makes excellent negotiators. Within a decade half of Spirefall is hollow people walking around full of borrowed grace, and the other half is at your kin's feet, and the moment one of your siblings disagrees with another — and they will, they always did, I've read the accounts — the war starts again, here, on top of my warehouse." He turned. "A toll slows the traffic down. Slowed traffic can be *counted*. Counted things can be governed. I am not selling you greed, Master Ashen. I am selling you time."

* * *

They negotiated for four hours. Vane enjoyed nearly all of it.

Ashen drove a hard bargain for a grieving creature — demanded exemption clauses for prayers of genuine desperation, refused the perpetuity term, countered with a century renewable on review, insisted on the right to audit the ledgers quarterly. Vane gave ground gracefully on the audits (knowing he could bury anything necessary in double-entry), held firm on exclusivity, and conceded the desperation clause with visible reluctance because he had already planned to concede it. By noon they had terms drafted in Ashen's own hand, in ink that shimmered faintly like oil on water, and Vane signed with the plain black wax of the Guild, no seal, because seals were for men who needed witnesses.

The map changed hands. Ashen unrolled it across the black vellumwood table and studied the six floors with an attention that made the candle flames lean toward the paper like listeners.

"You have excluded something," Ashen said at last, without looking up.

"I've excluded nothing. There's nothing to exclude."

"The seventh floor."

"There is no seventh floor."

Ashen straightened. For a moment the customs clerk's body seemed to strain at its seams, the way a glove strains when the hand inside remembers it once held lightning. "Guildmaster. I will find what my sibling left behind. If it is in your building, your map will show me. If it is not in your building—" the doubled voice came back, quiet as frost forming, "—then your building is merely where you are standing when I find it."

"Then I'll want the toll adjusted for hazard," Vane said, and poured them both a drink, and this time Ashen took it.

* * *

After the god-remnant had gone — out through the counting room, past the wax-sealer, who did not cry this time but stared at Pellworth's retreating back as if memorizing it — Vane stood alone in the negotiation room and permitted himself, briefly, the luxury of satisfaction.

It lasted the length of one breath.

Because standing in the doorway, a ledger tucked under her arm and her dark eyes doing that thing they did — reading him the way he read markets — was Odile Oyelaran.

"How much of that did you hear?" he asked.

"Enough to know you sold six floors and lied about a seventh." She came in without invitation, which was one of the thousand small reasons he had never fully trusted her and never fully dismissed her either. "Enough to know you just made yourself the richest toll-keeper in history on the strength of a secret that isn't yours."

"It's mine because I keep it," Vane said. "Possession is nine-tenths of law, Odile. The tenth part is paperwork, and I *am* the paperwork."

"And when they find it anyway?" She set the ledger on the table, next to the faint ring of moisture where the god's drink had sat. "When your new partners come back with their audits and their quarterly reviews and their frost-forming voices, and they discover the one thing you withheld? You haven't bought yourself a seat at the covenant, Guildmaster. You've bought yourself a countdown."

Vane looked at her for a long moment — his best auditor, his coldest mind, the woman whose handwriting appeared on jars she claimed not to remember labeling — and felt the familiar pleasure of recognizing a problem that was also, potentially, an asset.

"Then I suppose," he said pleasantly, "we'd both better make sure they find exactly what I intend them to find. Close the door, Odile. Let's discuss the seventh floor."

She closed the door. Outside, far below, the bells of the Spire began to ring — the evening peal, or perhaps, given the season the city had entered, something closer to a summons.
