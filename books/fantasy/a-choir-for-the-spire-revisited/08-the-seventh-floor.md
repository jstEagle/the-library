## 8. The Seventh Floor

The Vellum Guild had seven floors, and everyone knew the seventh was empty.

That was the point of it. The guild's architecture was a lie told in load-bearing stone: six floors of vaults and counting rooms and the Guildmaster's smoked-glass office, and above them a floor that appeared on no visitor's map, reachable by a stairwell whose door was painted to look like plastered-over brick. Odile had learned its existence the way she learned most things — from a ledger discrepancy. Fourteen years ago, a line item for *candle wax, upper storage* had never stopped appearing, though no upper storage existed on any inventory she could find. She had followed the candles the way an auditor follows anything: patiently, in small increments, until the truth was too large to ignore.

Now she stood at the bottom of that hidden stair with a jar wrapped in wool inside her coat, and her heart was doing arithmetic it had no business doing.

*Tovi will meet you at the second landing,* she had written, because Tovi did not hear whispers and Odile did not trust her own voice not to shake. *Wear the grey. Bring nothing you can't drop.*

Tovi had answered by holding up two fingers — their private signal for *understood*, borrowed from the old tally-clerks who'd counted debts on their hands before paper was cheap — and then, because Tovi was Tovi, had added a third finger and waggled it meaningfully until Odile realized she was being asked whether there would be food involved. There would not be food involved. This was, in Tovi's considered opinion, further evidence that adults had ruined secrecy as a concept.

Odile began to climb.

The stair smelled of tallow and cold dust, of decades of candles burned by people whose names were struck from every record. Her boots found each step by memory. Behind her, below her, the guild went about its evening business: clerks locking the strongroom, apprentices scrubbing memory-residue off the weighing pans — the faint iridescent film that collected where recollections had been poured, like the sheen inside a shell. Somewhere on the fourth floor, Guildmaster Vane was almost certainly still awake, still smiling at some problem he intended to solve expensively.

And somewhere in the city, wearing a customs clerk's body like a borrowed coat, something patient was asking questions.

Ashen. That was what it had called itself when it introduced itself to the door-warden three days ago — *Ashen, come about tariff irregularities* — and the horrifying thing was how well the name fit. It moved through the guild's corridors with a customs clerk's stoop and a customs clerk's ink-stained fingers, and it asked questions the way customs men asked them: politely, exhaustively, with the unspoken promise that refusal was merely a delay. It had interviewed eleven staff members so far. It took notes. Its handwriting, according to the door-warden, was beautiful.

It had not yet asked about the seventh floor, because the seventh floor did not exist, and even gods presumably audited only what was on the books.

That was Odile's whole plan, such as it was. Not courage. Bookkeeping.

She reached the second landing and found Tovi already there, folded into the shadow beneath the stair's iron rail, grey-cloaked as instructed, looking less like an apprentice than like a piece of the architecture that had learned to breathe. Tovi signed without preamble, quick and economical:

*You're late. I counted forty-one heartbeats' worth of late.*

*I don't want to know how you know my heartbeats.*

*You breathe loudly when you're frightened. You've been breathing loudly for three days.* Tovi's hands paused, and then, softer: *Is it true what they're saying? About the thing downstairs asking questions.*

Odile hesitated. Lying to Tovi had always been difficult, not because Tovi caught lies — Tovi caught everything, that was the trouble — but because Tovi treated being lied to as a fascinating professional failure on the liar's part, and would dissect the lie afterward with the same cheerful rigor applied to faulty ledger entries. *You said the shipment came from Marrow Street. But Marrow Street carts unload Tuesdays and this invoice is dated Thursday. Would you like to try again, or shall I write down both versions and let the Guildmaster pick?*

So Odile gave her the truth, or the shape of it. *Something old is looking for something I have. If it finds it here, people get hurt. I need to move it tonight, through the blind spots, and I need someone who can stand in a corridor and look like nothing while I do it.*

Tovi read her hands the way other people listened to music — wholly, with the face going quiet and attentive. Then Tovi smiled, which was the most alarming thing Tovi did.

*You're stealing from the guild,* Tovi signed. *I've been waiting four years for you to steal from the guild. What's in the jar?*

*Nothing you should carry. That's why your hands stay empty.*

For a moment Tovi's face did something complicated — the flicker of a younger child's hurt, quickly filed away under *later*, a debt to be raised at a more advantageous hour. Then the smile returned, smaller and sharper. *Fine. But when we survive this, you owe me the whole story. Interest compounded nightly.*

*Agreed. Now listen—*

And Odile laid out the guild's blind spots the way she knew them, which was better than anyone alive, including Vane. Every institution keeps honest records of what it values and dishonest silence around what it fears; the Vellum Guild was no different, except that its silences were architectural. There was the gap between the fifth-floor vaults and the sixth-floor counting rooms, a service shaft where the building's bones showed through, wide enough for a thin person carrying nothing heavier than guilt. There was the hour between the night-bell and the wardens' first round, ninety minutes when the corridors belonged to candle-drafts and mice. And there was the fact that Ashen, whatever else it was, worked from records — Odile was nearly certain of it. It had asked the door-warden for the staff roster. It had asked the weighing-master for the intake logs. It built its picture of the world the way she built hers: out of paper.

Which meant the safest path for the jar was the one that existed nowhere but in candle-wax receipts and a dead woman's muscle memory.

*We go up the hidden stair to seven,* Odile signed. *Then across, through the roof-gap into the east chimney housing, down the outside of the building on the ledge — the one the glaziers use — and into the cistern room, which has a door to Candlemakers' Row and no lock worth the name. From there, the Undercroft. Mother Verel owes me.*

*Mother Verel hates you.*

*Mother Verel owes me. Hatred is just interest accruing.*

Tovi laughed — a silent laugh, shoulders shaking, breath huffed through the nose, the way deaf laughter always looked to Odile like joy being kept safely indoors — and then sobered, and pointed up the dark stair with her chin.

They climbed.

* * *

The seventh floor was not empty. The seventh floor was a held breath.

Dust lay over everything in a smooth grey tide, undisturbed except for a single walked path from the stairwell door to a low chamber at the floor's center — a path worn shiny by generations of feet, all of them erased from record. Along the walls stood racks of empty jars, hundreds of them, older than any glasswork Odile knew how to price, their stoppers sealed with wax the color of dried blood. And in the center chamber, on a plinth of black stone, sat a socket shaped exactly like the jar beneath her coat.

She had known, in the abstract, that the seventh floor was where the guild had once kept its founding treasure. She had not understood, until standing here, that the room was built *around* an absence. The plinth, the socket, the racks sized to hold companions for it — the whole floor was a reliquary for something removed, and the removal had happened long enough ago that even the guild's oldest ledgers called the floor simply *unassigned*.

Her mother had walked this path. Her mother, whose face Odile could summon only in fragments — a smell of tallow, a hand on her hair, a voice saying *count it twice* — had carried the jar up these stairs, or guarded it, or been its lock. The thought arrived and sat down heavily in her chest.

Tovi touched her elbow and signed: *Your face is doing the thing. The loud-breathing thing, but with your face.*

*Later,* Odile signed back. *Interest compounded nightly, remember.*

She knelt at the plinth and did not put the jar in the socket. She had considered it — the socket was clearly made for it, and hiding a thing in its intended place had a certain terrible elegance — but elegance was for people who weren't being hunted by something that read ledgers. Instead she unwrapped the jar just far enough to work, and performed the small ritual she had invented three nights ago and hated herself for needing: she decanted a measure of the god-memory into a lesser vessel. A thumb's depth of the amber light, poured into a plain guild-standard jar, stoppered, labeled in her own flawless hand: *Residue, misc., 4th flr., awaiting weigh.*

A decoy. A false entry in the world's ledger. If Ashen followed the resonance, let it find a jar that said *nothing important* in the guild's own boring handwriting.

What remained in the true jar she rewrapped, and the wrapping felt suddenly like dressing a wound, and her hands were steady in the way they only were when steadiness mattered professionally, and she despised herself a little for that too.

Then the candle nearest the stairwell door guttered.

Not from draft. The flame bent sideways, slowly, deliberately, like a finger beckoning — and the temperature of the enormous dusty room dropped by the width of a season. Tovi's hand closed on Odile's wrist, hard, and pointed: down the walked path, at the stairwell door, which stood open.

It had been closed. Odile had closed it. She always closed it; closing doors behind her was the first rule of a life built on concealment.

In the doorway, slightly stooped, ink-stained, patient, stood the customs clerk.

He did not enter. He stood precisely at the threshold, hat in his hands — a courteous man observing a property line — and his eyes, ordinary brown eyes in an ordinary tired face, went past them both to the plinth, to the socket, to the racks of empty jars, and something moved behind the ordinariness like grief moving behind frosted glass.

*Don't run,* Odile told herself, and was astonished to find she meant it. Running confirmed value. Running was an admission entered into evidence. She rose instead, slowly, keeping her coat closed with one hand, and inclined her head the precise degree owed to a senior inspector.

Tovi, beside her, had gone magnificently blank — the face of a girl sent up to fetch candles, finding the upper floor dusty and strange, understanding nothing, caring less. It was the best acting Odile had ever seen and she made a note, in the ledger of her terror, to tell Tovi so if they lived.

The clerk spoke. His voice was mild, a little hoarse, a workingman's voice with something underneath it that resonated in the teeth rather than the ears.

"You're the auditor," he said. "Odile Oyelaran. They speak well of your columns."

"They're kind," Odile said. "We're inventorying the upper storage. Candle wax." She gestured at the racks. "Old stock. Mostly dregs."

"Mm." He turned his head, unhurried, and regarded the socket on the plinth. The empty socket. He looked at it for a long time — long enough that Odile's heartbeat became audible to her, long enough that she understood he was letting her watch him look, that this too was a courtesy, or a message, or a hook baited with dread. When he spoke again his voice had not changed at all, and that was the worst thing yet.

"Interesting," he said, "how often the most valuable things in a building are recorded as *residue*."

Then he put his hat back on, wished them a good evening in the tone of a man concluding a routine inspection, and descended the stairs, and the candle flames straightened one by one behind him like soldiers recovering from a bow.

Tovi waited until the last echo died. Then her hands came up, shaking only slightly, and signed with savage precision:

*He knows.*

*He suspects,* Odile signed back. *There's a difference, and the difference is paperwork. Come. We have a ledge to walk.*

* * *

The outside of the Vellum Guild at midnight was a country Odile visited perhaps twice a decade and trusted completely, because it kept no records at all. The glaziers' ledge ran along the eastern face, a hand's width of ancient stone above a drop that ended in the cistern yard, and the wind off the Spire's district came up the wall smelling of well water and celebration. Three floors below, the city was still singing. The answered prayer had made the beggar Tam Wicke famous; crowds still gathered at the well each dusk to cup their ears to the water and hear, faintly, a dead girl humming. Someone had strung lanterns across Candlemakers' Row. The light of them gilded the ledge and made the drop below look almost friendly.

Odile went first, back to the wall, the jar strapped flat against her chest beneath her coat, moving the way she moved through audits: one verified hold at a time, no leaps, no faith. Tovi came behind her, and Tovi — Tovi, who had grown up in a silent world and learned the city by reading its faces from rooftops — moved along the ledge like it was a corridor in her own home, and twice reached forward to steady Odile without being asked, and said nothing about it either time, which was its own kindness.

Halfway down, Tovi tapped her shoulder and signed against the stone where Odile could see it, one-handed, the other hand gripping the wall:

*Question. While we hang here. Since apparently this is where you do your thinking.*

*Ask.*

*The thing downstairs. The clerk. When it looked at the empty socket — before it left — it did something with its face. I read faces. Everyone's faces. And I couldn't read that one. Do you understand? I can read the Guildmaster, I can read the wardens, I can read drunk merchants across a market. That face was like a page in a language I almost know.* Her fingers hesitated. *It wasn't angry that the jar was gone. I'd know anger. It was — relieved? No. Sadder than relieved. Like a man who finds the grave he's been digging for is already empty, and doesn't know whether that's mercy or a worse thing.*

Odile hung on the wall of the guild her family had helped build, and thought about a god's kin coming to collect a dead sibling's last fragment, and wondered — for the first time, with the wind pulling at her coat and the whole city singing below — whether the kin wanted the memory back at all, or wanted it *ended*, and which of those she could survive.

*Keep that thought,* she signed finally. *File it. We may need it.*

*Everything gets filed with you.*

*Everything survives by being filed.*

They gained the cistern room, and the unlocked door, and the lantern-lit noise of Candlemakers' Row, and Odile felt the specific exhaustion of a body that has spent three hours pretending to be calm and now presents the bill. Tovi bought roasted chestnuts from a stall with money she absolutely had not been given for this purpose, and pressed a twist of paper into Odile's hand, and signed: *You said no food. You didn't say no food after.*

They ate walking. Around them the city celebrated its miracle, and the lantern-light lay warm on everything, and Odile held a paper cone of chestnuts in one hand and, beneath her coat, the last memory of a murdered god against her heart, and felt the two weights refuse to reconcile.

They were passing the mouth of Marrow Street when Tovi stopped.

Odile turned. Tovi was staring down the street's throat, where the lamplight failed, and her face had gone carefully, professionally blank — the face she wore for the Guildmaster, the face that meant *I am lying with my whole body, watch me do it*. Odile followed her gaze.

At the corner of Marrow Street and the Row, beneath a guttering lamp, stood the customs clerk. He was not watching them. He was watching the well-water sellers packing up their stalls, and he was listening — head tilted, eyes half-closed — to the sound of the crowd still singing the dead girl's name. And as Odile watched, the clerk lifted one ink-stained hand and touched his own throat, gently, the way a man touches a scar he is still deciding whether to be proud of.

Then he turned, and across forty feet of lantern-lit street he looked directly at Odile, and he did not smile, and he did not approach, and he did not call out. He simply raised two fingers — the old tally-clerks' signal, *understood*, the signal she and Tovi had traded on a dark staircase an hour ago — and touched them to his hat brim, and walked away into Marrow Street, whistling something very old and very slow that the crowd's singing swallowed whole.

Tovi's hands came up slowly.

*Two fingers,* she signed. *Where did he learn our—*

"He didn't," Odile said aloud, and heard her own voice come out steady, which frightened her more than shaking would have. "He learned it from watching us. On the stairs. Which means he was close enough to see."

She looked down at the paper cone in her hand, at the chestnuts going cold, at the ordinary blessed noise of a city that believed its prayers were finally being answered by something benevolent.

"Come on," she said. "Mother Verel's door closes at second bell. And Tovi — tomorrow, when the guild opens, I want you nowhere near the seventh floor. Whatever he left up there, I want it found by me first."

Above them, at the top of the guild's dark eastern face, in the window of the empty seventh floor, a single candle had begun to burn where no candle had been lit.
