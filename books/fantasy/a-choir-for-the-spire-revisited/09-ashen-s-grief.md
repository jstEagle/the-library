## 9. Ashen's Grief

The customs clerk's body had a habit Ashen could not train out of it: at the end of each day, it sighed. A long, wet, human sound, drawn up from lungs that wanted nothing more than a pipe by a cold hearth and a wife who had died two winters past. Ashen had worn the body for eleven days now. The sigh came every evening at dusk, punctual as tide, and every evening Ashen let it happen, because fighting it made the borrowed throat ache in ways that reminded him — uncomfortably, intimately — that flesh kept its own ledgers.

He sat now in the rented room above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, in the hour when the tallow smell sank deepest into the walls, and he did what he had done every night since arriving in this city of steeples and smoke: he remembered.

Mortals thought remembering was simple. They thought it was like opening a drawer. Ashen knew better. Remembering was an act of will so costly that his kind had once built entire religions around avoiding it. To remember was to be present in a moment that no longer existed, and presence, for beings made of attention and worship, was a kind of bleeding.

But he remembered anyway. He had crossed three centuries of silence to do nothing else.

* * *

The god who died had been called Vessia in the last of her names, though she had worn others before that — a name of riverbanks, a name of thresholds, a name so old it predated the concept of doors. She had been the youngest of them. That mattered. In the long arithmetic of the divine, age was not seniority but surface area: the older gods had spread themselves across so much belief, so many altars, so many centuries of fear and gratitude, that they had become diffuse, patient, nearly indifferent to harm. Vessia had still been concentrated. Still *dense* with self. When the war came, she burned like a struck match.

And they had let her.

That was the sentence Ashen carried, the one no prayer had ever been able to absolve because none of them had ever dared pray it aloud. *They let her die.* Not failed her — there was honor in failure, in reaching and missing. They had calculated. They had weighed her survival against their own positions in the war's collapsing geometry and found hers the lighter burden, and they had withdrawn, one by one, in the manner of guests leaving a party that has turned tedious, each assuming another would stay, each knowing none would.

Ashen had been the last to go. He told himself that sometimes, in the dark behind the clerk's eyes. He had stayed nine minutes longer than any of his kin. Nine minutes, measured in the time of that dying field, where the sky itself was sloughing off in sheets and the armies of the unmaking were singing their flat, hungry chord. Nine minutes in which Vessia had looked at him — not with accusation, which he could have borne, but with *understanding*, which he could not — and had begun, deliberately, deliberately, to hide.

She hid her death first. Folded it small, the way a miser folds a coin, and pressed it down into the mortal world through cracks the war had opened between the planes. Then she hid something else, something Ashen had only understood much later, standing in the customs house of a foreign port reading a shipping manifest with dead eyes while his whole nature howled: she had hidden not only her ending but her *witness*. The memory of what they had done. The record of nine gods turning their faces from a tenth, entered into evidence, signed in her final light.

A ledger. She had made of her death a ledger entry, and hidden it in the bloodline of a mortal family whose name Ashen had spent eleven days and three hundred years trying to find.

*Oyelaran.*

* * *

The kin had argued about it, in the way of mountains arguing — slowly, seismically, over decades that mortals experienced as weather. Some wanted the memory recovered and enshrined: a relic of penance, a shrine of shared shame at which they could kneel until the guilt wore smooth as river stone. Others wanted it never found at all; better an unknown sin than a known one. And a few — Ashen among them, though he had said it quietly, almost inaudibly, the way one confesses — wanted it destroyed.

Because it was not only a record. It was alive. Vessia had poured too much of herself into the hiding; the memory breathed. It was the last living fragment of her anywhere in creation, the final place where her voice still sounded, her attention still fell warm as sun through glass. And it sat, all this time, inside a mortal line, braided into the childhoods of people who never asked for it, feeding on their lives the way a lamp feeds on oil.

Every answered prayer since the well water spoke with a dead girl's voice — every miracle that had lit the city like a festival — traced back to that fragment stirring in its jar. Ashen had felt it the moment it woke: a resonance like a finger run round the rim of a glass, and beneath it, unmistakable, the timbre of a voice he had abandoned in a burning field.

The others wanted to bring it home. Ashen understood why. He even pitied them for it. But he had stood close enough to Vessia at the end to see what she saw as she folded herself away, and he knew what the memory contained, and he knew — with the terrible clarity available only to those who have done an unforgivable thing and had three centuries to examine it — that bringing it home would be the second abandonment.

To recover it would be to take the last of her out of the world and lock her in their company again. Their company. The company that left her. She had hidden the memory from *them* as much as from the war; that was the part the others refused to read in the shape of her hiding. She had not buried it deep by accident. She had chosen a bloodline, chosen obscurity, chosen mortality's great mercy — forgetting — because she did not want to be found by her own kind ever again.

Destroying it would end her truly. Ashen knew that too. It would snuff the last warm room in the house of her, and the universe would close over the space like water. But it would also end the guilt — not erase it, nothing erased guilt, but end its *cultivation*. As long as the memory existed, it grew. Every year it ripened, sweetened, became more unbearable, and every year the temptation to retrieve it grew with it, and retrieval meant resurrection, and resurrection meant the old questions waking with it: *who owed whom, who left whom, who started the war that killed a generation of gods.* The memory was not a wound. It was a seed.

He had watched mortals enough, these eleven days, to know how seeds behaved here. This city ran on them. He had seen the guild's work in glimpses — the jars, the weighing, the soft-voiced clerks with their prices per ounce of yesterday — and he had thought, the first time he understood what the Vellum Guild actually traded in, that he might weep if the body allowed it. Mortals selling their own pasts to survive the winter. And somewhere in that vast gray archive, filed under a handwriting he was closing in on day by day, sat the death of his sister, breathing.

* * *

On the twelfth morning, Ashen interviewed the guild's junior registrar, a nervous man named Pellwick who smelled of ink and fear-sweat, and who confirmed what the shipping manifests had already whispered: the seventh floor of the vaults was not on any audit list. Not omitted. *Excluded.* There was a difference, and the difference had a keeper, and the keeper's surname was stitched through the guild's founding records like a thread through cloth.

Ashen thanked Pellwick with the clerk's mild smile and walked out into a morning of thin gold light, and felt the body sigh again — not at dusk this time, but at nothing, at everything, at the sheer weight of having ribs.

He passed the Spire on his way back. A crowd had gathered at the well, as crowds did now, thickening daily like cream. A woman knelt at the rim with her hands cupped, drinking, and whatever she heard in the water made her laugh and sob in the same breath. Around her, vendors sold little tin cups blessed by proximity. A child held up a ribbon for the water to touch.

Ashen stopped at the edge of the crowd and listened with senses the crowd could not imagine, and beneath the water's babble he heard it again: her. Faint. Warped through mortal plumbing and mortal faith, but hers. Vessia's voice, answering prayers in a city that had forgotten her name, because a girl with a ledger-stained soul was keeping her death in a jar on a floor that did not exist.

*You hid well, little sister,* he thought. *You hid so well you hid yourself into their hands.*

And that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the hook the guilt had set three centuries ago and never worked loose. If he destroyed the memory, he destroyed her twice. If he recovered it, he delivered her back to the ones who fled. Either way, the kin won something, and she lost, and Ashen — who had been the last to leave, who had stayed nine whole minutes — would be the instrument of it either way.

Unless.

He looked at the kneeling woman, the tin cups, the child with the ribbon. Mortals, trading scraps of themselves for scraps of heaven, and being cheated at both ends of the scale. The guild fattened. The gods circled. And in the middle, a keeper of ledgers who did not yet know she was a lock.

There was a third door. Ashen had begun, in the last two nights, to see its outline. Not recovery. Not destruction alone. Something negotiated — and for that, he needed the keeper, not as prey but as counterpart. Which meant he needed her afraid enough to listen and unharmed enough to speak. Which meant, tomorrow or the day after, he must stop interviewing clerks and go, politely, terribly, to find Odile Oyelaran — and ask her, in the mild voice of a customs clerk, what she remembered of her mother.

The body sighed. Ashen let it.

Somewhere below the city, in a vault on a floor that was not listed, a dead god's memory turned over in its sleep — and dreamed, he was almost certain, of fire.
