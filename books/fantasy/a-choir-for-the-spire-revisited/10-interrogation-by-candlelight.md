## 10. Interrogation by Candlelight

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when the knock came — three soft raps, spaced like a heartbeat that had learned patience.

Odile did not answer it. She sat very still at her desk in the counting room, one hand resting flat on the closed ledger, and listened to her own blood move. No one knocked on her door at this hour. The guild kept night clerks for a reason; whatever business was urgent after candle-out went to them, and whatever business was *truly* urgent went through Vane's door and never touched hers.

Three more raps. Softer than the first set, if such a thing were possible.

"It's open," she said, and heard how steady she made it sound. Years of practice. Years of saying *the vault is sealed until morning* and *your deposit is safe* and *I'm sorry, we don't buy memories of the dead* with a face like still water.

The door opened without a sound. She'd oiled those hinges herself two winters ago, tired of their complaint, and now she regretted the craftsmanship bitterly, because a creak would have given her another breath to prepare.

Ashen stepped inside carrying his own candle, unlit, and leaned it against hers to catch the flame as though this were the most natural courtesy in the world. In the customs clerk's borrowed body he moved like a man who had worn it long enough to stop noticing it — the slightly too-careful placement of each foot, the way his eyes took in the room in a single unhurried sweep: ledger, ledger, the locked cabinet, the cold hearth, her.

"Ledger-keeper Oyelaran," he said. His voice was mild. It was always mild. That was the terrible thing about him — that everything terrible about him arrived dressed in mildness, wrapped in the tone of a man asking after your mother's health.

"You're out of hours," Odile said. "The guild receives petitioners from ninth bell to—"

"I know the hours." He smiled. He sat down across the desk from her without being asked, folding himself into the client's chair with the ease of someone who had sat in ten thousand chairs across ten thousand years and found them all briefly adequate. "I've been keeping them myself, these last weeks. Hours. They're strange little rooms, hours. You walk into one and you can't remember what was outside it."

She knew what he was doing. She had watched him do it to Marla in receiving, to old Hesketh in weighing, to the boy who tended the furnace. He opened a conversation sideways and let you walk into it, and by the time you understood where you stood you were already deep enough in that turning around would cost more than going forward. Every interview ended with the subject confessing something they hadn't known they carried.

Odile put both hands on the desk where he could see them. Steady hands. A ledger-keeper's hands.

"How can I help you, then? Within hours or without them."

He studied her the way she studied an entry that refused to balance — not with suspicion exactly, but with the particular attention of someone certain the error is there, somewhere, waiting to be found.

"You keep beautiful books," he said. "I've been through them. All twenty-two years of them, every floor but one." A pause, delicate as a knife laid down rather than drawn. "Your hand is remarkable. Consistent across decades. Most people's handwriting drifts — grief changes it, joy changes it, even a bad winter leaves its mark. Yours hasn't drifted since you were, what, nineteen? Twenty?" He tilted his head. "It's as if the hand that writes them isn't aging. As if it learned to write all at once, fully formed, from a source that doesn't change."

The candle flame leaned. There was no draft. Odile watched it lean anyway, the way her heart was leaning now, and made herself breathe.

"Guild training," she said. "We're taught a standard script hand. It prevents forgery."

"Oh, I know. I read the training manuals. Wonderful things." He laced his fingers together on the desk, mirroring her posture so precisely that it looked almost like mockery, almost like tenderness. "Here is what interests me, though. Here is my question tonight, and it's only one question, and then I'll leave you to your candle."

She waited.

"What do you remember of your mother?"

The room went very quiet. Outside, three floors down, the city muttered its late noises — a cart, a drunk singing half a hymn, the well-chain clanking as some sleepless soul drew water. Inside, the candle did its small work, and Odile Oyelaran sat in the circle of its light with a god wearing a customs clerk's body looking at her with what she could only describe, in that first awful instant, as *kindness*.

Her mind offered the memory up instantly, eagerly, like a clerk producing documents at a knock. Her mother — tall, dark-skinned, laugh lines first. The smell of lamp oil and dried lavender. Hands that smelled of soap and ink. A voice that hummed while it worked, off-key, always off-key, a song about a spire and a choir and—

*Careful.*

"She died when I was small," Odile said. "Fever. I don't have much. Fragments." And here she did something she hated herself for even as she did it, something she had done a hundred times across a client's desk to draw out a hesitant seller: she let her voice roughen. Not much. A hairline crack, barely audible. "A kitchen. Lavender. She hummed when she worked. I couldn't tell you her face anymore. Isn't that the way of it? You lose the face first and keep the smell, like the smell is what mattered most and no one told you."

Ashen didn't move.

"The humming," he said softly. "What song?"

"A lullaby. About the Spire. Everyone knows it." Her mouth was dry. "It's a common song."

"Mm," he said, and it was not agreement. It was the sound a man makes over an entry that has finally shown him where the column went wrong.

*It's a common song. It's a common song. Half the city hums it, it's woven into the bells, it's nothing, it's—*

But even as she built the defense, brick by practiced brick, she felt it failing under her, because she suddenly could not recall *where* she had learned the song's second verse. She knew the second verse perfectly — knew it the way she knew her own handwriting, whole and entire, fully formed — and she could not remember ever learning it. It was simply *there*, the way the lavender-smell was there, the way the laugh-lines were there, complete and sourceless, like furniture delivered to a house before the house existed.

And Ashen watched her realize it. She saw it land in his eyes, the exact moment her lie finished betraying her — not because he caught it, but because *she* caught it, in front of him, live, the seam splitting along its whole length.

"There it is," he said quietly. Almost gently. "There's the flicker. Do you know, in three centuries of listening to mortals lie to me — and mortals have lied to me beautifully, Odile, they've lied to me with dying breaths and wedding vows and ledgers just like yours — I have never once needed to catch a lie. I only ever need to wait for the liar to catch it herself. Truth doesn't flinch when you examine it. Only stolen things do."

"I don't know what you think—"

"Yes you do." He raised one finger, not sharply — the gesture of a teacher pausing a student who is about to ruin a nearly correct answer. "You know exactly what I think. You've known since the well water sang to the beggar. You've known since before that, since you wrote that jar's label in your own perfect unchanging hand and shelved it where no audit reaches. You have been knowing it for years, and you are so tired, aren't you? Tired in a way sleep doesn't touch."

The candle guttered. Odile found that her hands were no longer steady. She looked down at them as if they belonged to a client, as if she might weigh them, price them, enter them in a column marked *surrendered*.

"Tell me what you remember of the fever," Ashen said. "The one that killed her. Which fever. What season. Who sat with you after. What the funeral smelled like."

"Lavender—" she began, and stopped, because lavender was the *kitchen*, and funerals in this city smelled of tallow and wet stone, everyone knew that, she knew that professionally, she had appraised a thousand funeral memories and not one of them smelled of lavender—

"Funerals smell of tallow," Ashen agreed, watching her. "And your mother's death smells of lavender, because it isn't a death at all. It's a shelf in a pantry. It's a jar's worth of borrowed warmth, laid down beside the real cargo so the vessel wouldn't rattle." He leaned forward, and for the first time since he'd entered, the mildness thinned, and she glimpsed underneath it something vast and weathered and grieving, the way you glimpse bedrock through a cracked road. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, ledger-keeper? Your childhood is inventory. Someone stocked you with it so you'd hold together. And whoever did the stocking used *his* memories to do it — my sibling's memories, cut into strips and stitched over the hollow place where a life should be. You didn't steal only from a dead god. A dead god was spent on you. You are the receipt."

The word *receipt* went through her like a nail through vellum.

She thought of chapter four — no. She thought of the night weeks ago, alone, when she'd cracked the jar and fallen through a stranger's dying into her own forged beginning, and how she had resealed it and gone back to work the next morning because that was what she did, that was all she had ever known how to do: reseal, rebalance, carry on. She thought of Tovi asking nothing, Tovi reading her lips through glass, Tovi's fierce silent loyalty. She thought of the song's second verse, sourceless and perfect.

"If you know all this," Odile said, and her voice came out lower than she intended, scraped raw at the bottom, "then why ask me what I remember of my mother? Why sit through my lying to you?"

And here Ashen did something she had not seen him do in any of his interviews, not with Marla, not with Hesketh, not with the trembling furnace-boy. He looked away from her. He looked at the candle flame, and his borrowed face — a plain customs-clerk's face, forty-ish, forgettable, chosen precisely for its forgettability — did something complicated and small.

"Because in a few days," he said, "my kin and I are going to come for what's in that jar. Properly. Not interviews — not candles and questions. What sleeps in your seventh-floor dark is the last of my brother, and it is also the worst thing any of us ever did, and when we take it — *if* we take it intact — it will tear loose everything stitched to it. Everything he spent on you. The lavender. The humming. The laugh lines you can no longer see. All of it goes when the memory goes, Odile, because none of it was ever yours. You will be left standing in a house with the furniture gone, trying to remember why you ever called it home." His jaw tightened. "I wanted you to hear it from me first, sitting down, by candlelight, instead of finding it mid-siege with the sky full of wings. That's the only reason I came. Courtesy." A pause, and then, quieter: "The others wouldn't have bothered. The others say you're furniture too."

Odile discovered she was crying. Not dramatically — no sobs, no performance, nothing she could have used across a client's desk. Just heat sliding down her face while she sat perfectly upright, hands flat on the desk, a professional to the last, weeping silently over an entry she could not reconcile.

"Is that mercy?" she asked. "Telling me what you're going to take?"

"No," Ashen said honestly. "Mercy would be leaving you ignorant. Ignorance is the kindest thing we have and I've never been able to afford it." He rose. The chair didn't scrape; even his weight was courteous. At the door he paused with his hand on the frame, and without turning around he said:

"For what it's worth — the song wasn't common. He wrote it. For a choir that never got to sing it. He gave you the only copy." Now he turned, and the mildness was back, and behind it the grief, layered like varnish. "When you decide — and you will decide, you're the deciding kind, it's written all over your beautiful unchanging hand — know that destroying it isn't the only door. My siblings will tell you it is. My siblings are frightened, and frightened gods offer only two prices." He tapped the doorframe once, softly, like a knocker. "There's a third price. It's older than the war. Ask your ledgers what happens to a debt when the debtor offers payment *willingly*. Look up the oldest entries. The ones from before the guild forgot what it was for."

Then he was gone, taking his candle with him, pinching her wick between wet fingers as he passed so that her flame died with his — and she was left in the dark with the smell of smoke and lavender that wasn't real, listening to the well-chain clank below, three floors down, where a dead daughter's voice still sang to a beggar in the water.

In the darkness Odile put her hand flat on the ledger, her true companion, the one thing about herself she had never doubted — and realized with a cold, slow, total certainty what Ashen had just done.

He hadn't warned her out of courtesy.

He'd sent her to the archives. To the founding records. To the oldest entries, the pre-Exile folios no one had opened in living memory — because he knew, the way he knew everything, that a ledger-keeper who has just been told she is a receipt will go looking for the invoice. And buried in those founding pages, in the guild's first covenant, was something about her bloodline that Ashen had chosen *not* to say aloud.

Something he wanted her to find herself.

She lit the candle again with hands that shook, and reached beneath the desk for the key to the restricted stacks.

* * *

The restricted stacks breathed cold. Three floors below the counting room, past the cage of the night clerk (empty, bribed earlier that evening with a story about flooded gutters), the oldest folios slept in their iron cabinets, and Odile walked between them with her candle held high like a woman entering a throat.

The founding records were catalogued, absurdly, under *miscellaneous*. Whoever had hidden them had relied on boredom as a lock, and it had worked for two centuries. She found the folio where the index promised it should be, broke its dust seal with her thumbnail, and turned crackling pages by candlelight until she reached the first covenant — the guild's charter, written in a hand so old the letters had gone brown as dried blood.

She read the terms once. Then again, slower. Then a third time with her lips moving, because her mind kept refusing the sentence the way a tongue refuses a spoiled taste.

*The Vault shall hold the Fragment against all claimants. The Lock shall be mortal, of the Founder's line, and shall not know itself, for a Lock that knows is a Lock that opens.*

Below it, a single name in the founder's hand. Not a signature.

An entry. A debt recorded, the oldest in the book, the first line ever written in what would become the Vellum Guild's ledgers:

*Received in trust: one vessel, aged four years. Payment deferred unto the third generation.*

Odile Oyelaran stood alone in the cold among sleeping debts, holding the record of her own acquisition, and understood at last why Ashen had let her lie beautifully, why he had waited for her to catch herself — because the truth was worse than anything he could have said, and kinder too:

She had never been the keeper of the guild's secret.

She *was* the guild's secret. Filed, shelved, and due.

And the third generation — she counted backward through her forged childhood, through the founder's line, through the columns of dead names — the third generation was her.
