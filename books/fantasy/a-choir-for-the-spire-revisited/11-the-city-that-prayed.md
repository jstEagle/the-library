## 11. The City That Prayed

The queue outside the Vellum Guild began before dawn and by noon had grown a second spine, curling down Candlemaker's Row and into the fish market, where it mingled with the smell of brine and the cries of gulls. Odile Oyelaran watched it from the counting-room window, a cup of cold chicory forgotten in her hand, and tried to count the queue the way she counted everything else — in lots, in grades, in projected yield.

She could not finish the arithmetic. Her hand kept stopping at the faces.

There was a woman three places from the front who had wrapped her feet in sacking because her shoes had gone to the pawnbroker months ago. There was a boy no older than Tovi holding a birdcage with nothing in it, swinging it like a lantern. There was an old man who had clearly walked from the tannery districts, his apron still on, as if he had heard the news mid-shift and run. All of them had heard. All of them knew. The gods were answering, and the guild was the only door.

*First memory pawned free of charge,* the criers had been shouting for six days now. *First memory pawned free of charge, and the gods will know your name.*

It was not charity. Odile had written the policy herself, at Vane's dictation, and she had made sure of that. The first memory was free the way the first taste of poppy was free.

She set down the cup and went down to the floor, because someone had to, and because watching from a height had begun to feel like a kind of lie.

* * *

Marta Quill had sold her wedding before she sold anything else.

That was how she thought of it afterward, though the clerk had made her sign for it as *one (1) episodic memory, nuptial, mid-grade, minor sensory degradation acceptable*. The clerk had been kind about it. They were all kind now; kindness was cheaper than it had been in the spring, when the guild's business had been ledgers and quiet theft and no one wept in the waiting room. Kindness was what you gave people when you were taking the last of something.

She sat in the extraction chair and the needle-scribe touched her temple, and the wedding went out of her the way water goes out of a basin — all at once and then, for a long time, in drips.

She kept the fact of it. She could say: *I was married. His name was Aldous. There were lilies, I am told.* She had written it on her hand in ink before the procedure, in case the *told* part went too. What she lost was the weight of his hand, the specific gray of the rain that day, the moment when the priest stumbled over her name and Aldous laughed and she had loved him so much her ribs had hurt. Those were gone the way a room is gone when the candle is carried out of it. She knew the room existed. She stood in its dark.

What she bought with it was her son's fever broken.

She had prayed at the Spire well on the first night of the answering, with the crowd, with the beggar Tam weeping beside her as his dead daughter's voice rose up out of the water like steam. She had prayed every night since. On the fifth night, a voice — not her son's, not yet, the gods were careful that way, they answered in the voices of strangers until you proved your need — had said: *The child lives if the debt is paid. Bring what you love to the house of vellum.*

So she had brought the wedding. And on the sixth night, kneeling at the well with the crowd's torches guttering around her, she had heard her son cough — actually cough, a small wet miserable sound, the sound of a living boy — echo up from forty feet of old stone.

She laughed until the neighbors thought she had gone mad, and perhaps the neighbors were right, and perhaps it did not matter, because Tammas slept that night without the rattle in his chest, and in the morning he asked for porridge, and porridge was something she could give him with her own two hands.

She went back to the guild the next week. There was more that needed answering. There was always more.

* * *

The clerk who took Marta Quill's wedding was named Pell, and he had worked the extraction floor for nine years, and he had never once gone home crying until the week the gods came back.

It was not the weeping. He had heard weeping for nine years; it ran off him like rain off wax. It was the *prices*. In the old days the guild had bought secrets, shames, first loves, the memories rich people wanted rid of or poor people couldn't afford to keep. Now the queue sold *necessity*. A cooper sold the memory of his father's face to get his daughter's stutter healed. A widow sold her grief — grief, the one thing she had thought was hers, the one thing no one could repossess — and walked out lighter and wrong, patting her own chest as if looking for something she'd mislaid. A fishwife sold the taste of her mother's cooking and stood in the doorway afterward saying, *It was eel pie, wasn't it? Tell me it was eel pie,* and Pell had told her it was eel pie because he did not know, because the memory was already in a jar on a shelf, and her face when he said it —

Her face when he said it had been *grateful*.

That was the part that made him cry. Not the loss. The gratitude. They thanked him. They pressed his hand. A man with no shoes had kissed Pell's ring finger like he was a bishop, and Pell had gone into the back room and put his head between his knees and thought: *I am the toll on the only bridge out of drowning, and the bridge is charging by the pound of self.*

He did not stop coming to work. That was the thing Odile understood later, when she read the floor reports and saw the extraction numbers and knew that every clerk had done the same arithmetic Pell had done: if I refuse, the queue does not shrink. It simply moves to a crueler clerk.

Complicity, Odile had learned, rarely announced itself. It arrived as a shift schedule.

* * *

Ashen walked the fish market in the customs clerk's body, which had begun, in small ways, to fit him better — the body had stopped flinching at loud noises, had learned to want salt, had developed an opinion about the quality of the morning bread — and he watched the queue with the expression of a man reading a bill of lading for a shipment he had hoped would never sail.

*They are selling themselves,* he said to the fishwife beside him, who was not listening, who was arguing about herring. *Piece by piece. To buy the attention of beings who once drowned a coastline for a slight.*

The fishwife told him to buy a herring or move along.

He bought a herring. He did not know why. The body had wanted it, and lately he had begun to honor such small petitions, the way one feeds a dog that guards one's house. He carried it through the streets wrapped in paper and thought about his sibling — the dead one, the one whose death was hidden somewhere in this city of vellum and candle smoke — and thought: *You hid your last thought inside a mortal line, and look what your mortals have become. Look what our answering has made of them.*

Because that was the truth Ashen carried under the customs clerk's gray coat, the truth he could not say aloud in the guild's hearing: the miracles were real, but they were not *kind*. The gods — what was left of them, the scattered kin, the remnants that had heard the well open like a door left ajar after three hundred years — the gods answered prayers the way a starved man answers a knock: too fast, too hard, taking whatever is offered. They did not ask for memories. The memories were the guild's invention, Vane's toll, the toll-keeper's cut on every transaction between the desperate and the divine. But the gods accepted them. They drank what was poured. A memory freely surrendered — or surrendered *believing* it was free, which the old covenants had never distinguished, a loophole Vane had found in texts he should never have been allowed to read — a surrendered memory was sustenance, and the remnants were so hungry.

*We are eating them,* Ashen thought, standing in the fish market with a herring going warm in his hand. *We are eating the poor of this city one childhood at a time, and they are lining up, and they are thanking us.*

Somewhere in the guild's vaults was the memory that would end it — or begin it again. Ashen turned toward the spire, and the spire's shadow lay across the queue like a long gray ledger-line, and every soul in that queue stood inside it.

* * *

Guildmaster Corvan Vane received the week's tallies on a tray, the way other men received breakfast.

He read them by the window of his office on the sixth floor, where the light was best and the view of the queue was uninterrupted. Extraction volume: up eleven hundred percent. Vault occupancy: at capacity, overflow contracted to the salt cellars beneath the tannery. Revenue: he paused over this one with the pleasure a miser takes in a coin of unusual weight, and read it again, and permitted himself, alone, unobserved, a small sound that in a lesser man would have been called a laugh.

The city had never loved him. He had made his peace with that decades ago; love was a currency that inflated too easily. But the city *needed* him now, needed him the way a bleeding man needs a tourniquet, and need was a currency that only ever appreciated. Every prayer answered at the well passed through his ledgers. Every memory that fed the returning gods was weighed on his scales, priced by his clerks, taxed at his rates. When the last of the god-kin came out of hiding — and they were coming, his sources in the temple ruins confirmed it, they were converging on the city like moths who had finally found a flame worth burning for — they would find a mortal already standing at the center of the new faith, holding the only complete map of what had been given and what had been gained.

He would not be a priest. Priests were interchangeable. He would be the *exchange rate*.

There was only one flaw in the architecture, and it was shaped like his chief auditor. Odile Oyelaran knew the vaults better than he did — he had made sure of that, years ago, the better to use her — and there was one item in those vaults that appeared on no ledger, that sat outside his map, and Vane had built his life on the principle that anything outside the map was either worthless or the most valuable thing in the room.

He had offered her partnership. She had looked at him with those ledger-gray eyes and said she would consider it, which in Odile's language meant *no, but I am too intelligent to say so yet.* Fine. There were other ways to bring an auditor into alignment. There were audits of auditors. There were debts. There was, if it came to it, the simple fact that a woman built from stolen goods could be unmade by a single well-placed question about her mother.

He poured a finger of plum brandy and raised the glass to the queue below, to the fishwife and the cooper and the widow, to all the small sellers of themselves.

*To supply,* he said, and drank.

* * *

Odile walked the queue at closing, because the ledgers were done and she could not sit still, and because she had told herself it was to look for Tovi, who was late, and because she knew that was not the reason.

The queue had not dispersed. The queue *never* dispersed now; it simply changed composition, the way a river changes water. People had begun sleeping in it, holding places for relatives. A soup woman worked the line at a copper a bowl and had never been busier. A child was teaching another child a clapping song, and the song had new words already, words Odile did not know, and she caught a fragment — *bring what you love, bring what you love, the well knows what you love* — and had to stand very still for a moment, hand flat against a wall, until the vertigo passed.

Because that was the horror of it, the thing she could not put in any report: the line was *happy*. The people in it were not desperate the way the starving are desperate, gray and silent. They were hopeful. They had seen miracles with their own eyes — Tam's daughter in the well water, the fevered children sleeping, the lame cooper's son who had walked up Candlemaker's Row on legs that had never held him — and they had done the arithmetic that hope does, which is not arithmetic at all. *I have memories to spare,* they said. *I have thirty years of ordinary days in here; who needs all of them? I will sell the bad ones. I will sell the dull ones. I will keep what matters.*

And the guild would take what mattered, eventually, because the gods' hunger did not distinguish, and Vane's scales did not distinguish, and by the time a seller learned the difference between the memories she meant to keep and the memories that were keeping *her*, the transaction was already signed in her own hand.

Odile had watched a man that afternoon sell the memory of his own name — not the name, the *knowing* of it, the way it felt in the mouth — to buy his wife's sight back from a cataract the gods had promised to lift. He had walked out of the guild asking everyone he passed, politely, urgently, *What is my name? What is my name?* And when someone told him, he had repeated it like a foreign word, and thanked them, and Odile had understood that he would spend the rest of his life wearing that name like a borrowed coat, checking the pockets.

She had signed off on the transaction. Her stamp was on the chit. Her stamp was on all of them.

*Tovi is late,* she thought, and then, because she was honest with herself in the way of people who lie professionally: *You came down here to see the line. You come down every evening to see the line. You tell yourself it is conscience, but it is the same thing the faithful do at the well — you come to look at the thing that is feeding on you.*

Because it was feeding on her too. Every memory the guild extracted, every jar that crossed her audit bench, tightened the knot between her and the thing in the seventh-floor jar, the thing she had carried off the books for nine years, the dead god's last thought wearing her childhood like a mask. The city was selling itself to the gods, and she had been sold before any of them, sold before she could consent, made into a vessel before she had a self to lose — and she still had not decided. Return it and unmake herself. Keep it and feed the war.

The lamps along the queue were being lit, one by one, by a lamplighter working his way down the line, and each small flame threw each waiting face into brief gold and then let it fall back to dusk.

Then she saw Tovi — finally, at the far end of the queue, standing very still, talking with his hands to a woman Odile did not know, a thin woman in a temple-ruin cloak, and the woman was shaking her head, and Tovi's hands had stopped moving, and even at forty paces Odile could see that whatever was passing between them had the shape of a warning.

Tovi looked up. Found her. And mouthed, with the terrible fluency of a boy who reads lips and lies for a living, three words she had never seen him sign before:

*They know where.*
