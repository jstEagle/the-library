## 12. Tovi's Question

Tovi counted the day's takings by touch.

The coins told her everything the ledgers did not — which clients had wept when they paid, which had laughed, which had pressed their palms flat against the counter as though the memory they were selling might still be pulled back out of the scales if only they held on long enough. She could feel all of it in the way silver warmed or didn't warm, in the little hesitations pressed into the metal like fingerprints in wax. The hearing world thought deafness was a wall. Tovi had learned it was a window: everyone assumed she couldn't see them, and so she saw everything.

She was sorting the evening's tray into stacks by denomination when Odile came down from the seventh floor with that particular set to her shoulders that meant she'd been up among the jars again. Tovi watched her cross the counting room in the greenish light of the ledger-lamps, watched her pause at the door of the vault and rest one hand against the frame the way a person touches a wound to check whether it still hurts.

Odile had been doing that for eleven days now. Since the fire on the archive levels, since the breach, since the night a hundred strangers in the street below had stopped mid-step and spoken with one voice — a voice Tovi hadn't heard, obviously, but had *felt*, through the soles of her feet, through the iron railing she'd been gripping, a vibration like a bell rung inside her own bones. She'd looked up and seen Odile standing at the guild's front steps with her face turned toward the crowd, and every stranger's mouth moving in unison, and Odile's mouth not moving at all, just opening slightly, as if the name being spoken were hers.

Tovi had read lips since she was four years old. She had read the shape of that name on a hundred mouths at once, and she had never seen it before, and she had known — the way you know a lie is coming before the liar does — that it belonged to Odile.

*Oyelaran* was the name Odile signed. This other name had been older than ink.

Since then, Odile had been careful. Careful in the specific way guilty people are careful: answering questions a half-beat too quickly, smiling a half-beat too warmly, touching Tovi's shoulder when she passed as though affection were a payment due. Tovi kept her own count of these small debts. It was what she was for. The guild had taken her on at thirteen because she noticed things, because she remembered things, because a girl who lived entirely in the visible world learned to treat every flicker of a face as a sentence worth transcribing.

Tonight, when Odile reached the bottom stair, Tovi put down the coins.

"Sit," she said, and made sure Odile saw her mouth form it clearly, no ambiguity, no escape into the blur of casual speech.

Odile sat. Of course she sat; Odile always gave people what they asked for, which was itself a kind of tell. She folded herself onto the bench across the counting table, and the lamplight caught the fine lines around her eyes that Tovi had never noticed until this month, because this month Odile had begun to look like someone carrying something heavier than jars.

"I wanted to ask you something," Tovi said, "and I want you to answer it slowly. I don't want the fast answer. The fast answers are the ones you've been giving me for eleven days."

Odile's hands settled flat on the table. A negotiator's hands. Even now, even here, they arranged themselves as though the conversation were a contract and she'd already drafted terms. "Ask."

Tovi had rehearsed this. That was the shameful part — she'd stood in the washroom that morning mouthing the words to herself in the spotted mirror, arranging and rearranging them, trying to find a version that wouldn't sound like an accusation. There wasn't one. She'd finally understood that the accusation *was* the question, and any version that softened it would be a lie, and she was so tired of how easily lies came to her now, how fluently, how well-paid.

"You remember Marla," she said. "The dyer's widow. Third counter, Tuesdays."

Odile nodded. "Marla Osei. She pawned her wedding week. Four years ago."

"She came back Tuesday." Tovi let the silence do some work. "She wants to buy it back. She's saved for four years — I checked the arithmetic myself, she's been skipping meat, she's been mending instead of replacing, she has *exactly* enough." Tovi's hands moved as she spoke, the way they always did when she was angry, big declarative shapes that she knew Odile could follow without effort. "And Vane's new schedule says repurchases are suspended. Indefinitely. Because of the breach. Because loose memories are 'unstable inventory.' So Marla stood at my counter today and asked me — me, because I'm the one who doesn't make her feel stupid — whether there was any way at all. And I had to tell her no. I had to watch her understand that her own wedding is sitting in a jar two floors above us and she can't have it back, and she thanked me. She *thanked me* for telling her honestly."

"Tovi—"

"No." Tovi held up one finger, and Odile, to her credit, went quiet. "Here's my question. You can take all the time you need. But I want the true answer, not the kind answer, and I will know the difference, because reading faces is the only language I've ever been fluent in."

She leaned forward. The lamps hissed. Somewhere above them, on the seventh floor, the vault's old lock ticked as the building cooled — a sound Tovi felt more than perceived, a small mechanical heartbeat in the walls.

"Why do you get to keep yours?"

* * *

The question hung there, and Tovi watched it land.

She watched Odile's face the way a scribe watches a page: the micro-flinch at the outer eye, the swallow that moved the throat's column, the instant reflexive gathering of an answer — Tovi could see the answer being *drafted*, could see the professional machinery spin up, the guild-trained smoothness that could sell a grieving woman her own mother's face at a fair price. She watched all of it happen in the space of a breath.

And then she watched it fail.

That was the thing Tovi hadn't expected. She'd braced for the beautiful lie, the polished deflection, the *it's complicated, Tovi, the covenant negotiations depend on it*. She'd prepared counters for each. What she got instead was Odile's face simply — stopping. Like a clock running down. Like a woman who had been lying so long and so hard, even to herself, that when the moment came to perform honesty she found the shelf empty.

"I don't know," Odile said.

It came out rough. Unrehearsed. Tovi felt it in her chest more than heard it, that rawness, the way a cracked bell sounds wrong even to the deaf.

"That's not an answer," Tovi said, but gently now, because cruelty had never been the point. "You have a whole life up there in a jar. A god's death, Ashen says. Your mother's face stitched out of stolen thread. And every day I stand at a counter and hand people receipts for their childhoods, and they walk out lighter and poorer and they don't even know what they've lost until years later, when they reach for it and find the shape of the hole." Her hands were shaking. She pressed them against her skirt. "I help do that. I write the tickets. I smile at them. I'm good at it, Odile, I'm *so good at it*, and I tell myself it's just work, it's just the guild, everyone needs a trade. And then there's you. You, who took the worst thing in the building and hid it off the books for years. Years! You broke every rule we have, and the reason you broke them was to keep something that was never yours. And it worked. You got to have a childhood. You got to have a *mother*. Marla's daughter has a mother who sold her wedding week to buy medicine, and Marla can't remember why the smell of marigolds makes her cry."

The words ran out. Tovi sat back, breathing hard, surprised at herself — she almost never spoke this much, almost never spent this much of herself in a single sitting. Speaking, for her, was expensive. Every word had to be shaped twice, once in the mouth and once in the watching, and she had just spent a fortune.

Odile was crying. Not dramatically. Just the eyes filling, the jaw tightening against it, the tears held in suspension like water in a jar with the lid barely on. Tovi knew about holding things in barely-sealed jars. Everyone in this building did.

"Say something," Tovi said.

"I've been trying to find the honest answer for eleven days," Odile said quietly. Her voice had that scraped quality. "I've laid it out every way I know. I told myself I stole it before I understood what it was. I told myself I was keeping it safe, not keeping it. I told myself the covenant needs it, the gods need it, the city needs it. And underneath all of those—" she exhaled, and the tears spilled, and she didn't wipe them, which Tovi had never seen her allow— "underneath all of them is the truth, which is that I wanted it. I wanted a childhood. I wanted a mother who sang. And I had the jar, and I had the access, and nobody was watching the seventh floor except me. So I took it. The same way any client takes. The same way the rich ones take, the ones who come in with lawyers and buy whole decades from desperate families. I was just better positioned than most thieves, so I never had to see the counter. I never had to sit where you sit."

The lamps hissed. Above them the vault ticked its slow heartbeat.

"So you admit it," Tovi said. "You're a thief."

"I'm the guild," Odile said. "That's worse. The guild steals and calls it commerce. I stole and called it survival, and then called it duty, and then called it destiny, and every name I gave it was just another entry in a ledger I refused to audit." She laughed, wet and terrible. "You know what the first chapter of my own bookkeeping rules says? *No debt forgiven by renaming.* I wrote that. I was twenty-two and very pleased with myself."

Tovi should have felt victorious. She had asked her question; she had gotten the true answer; the innocent's reckoning had landed clean. Instead she felt the floor of something shift under her, because the true answer was worse than the lie. The lie would have let her stay angry. The truth made her complicit too — she had known about the jar for months, had helped move it through the blind spots, had lied fluently to Ashen's polite terrible face while her heart hammered silent in her ears. She had told herself she was protecting Odile. She had told herself it was friendship.

But friendship, she was beginning to understand, was also a ledger. And hers had entries in it she hadn't read closely.

"I helped you," Tovi said. "On the stairs. With Ashen. I looked him in the eye and lied about a delivery schedule and I was *good at it*, and afterward I felt proud." Her throat worked. "Why did I feel proud?"

"Because he frightened you, and lying was the tool you had."

"No." Tovi shook her head slowly. "Because it was *ours*. The secret. You and me against the building. I liked being the one who knew. Do you know what that's like, for someone like me? Everyone talks over my head my whole life — literally over my head, conversations happening above me in air I can't reach. And then you handed me the biggest secret in the city and suddenly I was inside the conversation. I mattered." She wiped her nose with the back of her hand, graceless, young. "I think I helped you hide a god's corpse-memory partly because it made me important. So don't put me on your side of the ledger, Odile. I earned my column too."

Odile reached across the table — paused, asked with her eyes — and Tovi nodded, and Odile took her hand. Her grip was cold. Seventh-floor cold. Jar-cold.

"What do I do?" Odile asked. And it was the first time in all the years Tovi had known her that Odile Oyelaran had asked anyone that question and meant it.

"You already know," Tovi said. "You've known since the strangers said your name in the street. You're going to give it back. At the Spire, in front of everyone, with the covenant — I've read your drafts, I dust the desk you hide them under, I'm deaf, not blind." She managed something that was almost a smile. "And it's going to cost you everything you built out of it. The mother. The singing. The whole forged childhood. And you're going to do it anyway, because it's the only entry in your books that balances."

"And you?" Odile's voice was very small. "Will you still—"

"I don't know," Tovi said, and found, strangely, that it was the kindest thing she'd ever said to anyone. "That's my honest answer. Ask me again after the Spire. If we're both still—"

The lamps went out.

Not dimmed — *out*, all six of them at once, and the counting room dropped into a darkness so total that even Tovi, who feared darkness less than most, felt her stomach drop. In the same instant she felt it through the floorboards: footsteps. Many. Descending. Not the guild's rhythm, not the shuffling tread of clerks ending their shifts — this was coordinated, booted, deliberate, and it was coming up from the sub-vaults, the direction no one went, the direction of the burned archives.

Then light again — a single flame blooming in the doorway, held by a figure whose face Tovi couldn't yet read, and behind it, filling the stairwell, the sound — the *vibration* — of many voices speaking one word in unison.

She grabbed Odile's wrist and spelled it fast against her skin, letter by letter, the old alphabet they'd invented for whispering across crowded floors:

*R-i-o-t.*

"The ledgers," Odile breathed, already on her feet. "They've come for the ledgers."
