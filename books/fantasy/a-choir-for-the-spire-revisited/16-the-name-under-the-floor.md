## 16. The Name Under the Floor

The founding records lived in the cellar beneath the counting room, in a room so old it had no name on any floor plan. Odile had worked for the Vellum Guild for eleven years and had never once been sent down there. She had never once asked to go. That was the thing about institutions built on secrets: they did not need to forbid you. They only needed to make sure you were never curious in the right direction.

She went down at dusk, when the counting room emptied and the clerks above her feet thinned to a few tired footsteps. She carried a candle, a crowbar she had taken from the repair bench, and a lie she had rehearsed for Tovi's benefit — *inventory of the deep stacks, don't wait up* — though Tovi had read her lips through the lie and simply nodded, which was worse than an argument. Tovi had not forgiven her. Tovi was helping her anyway. Odile did not know what to do with that except keep moving.

The stair down was stone worn into a shallow trough by centuries of feet, and at the bottom the air changed. It smelled of tallow and cold iron and, faintly, of the same green-water sweetness that clung to the jar in her satchel. Her skin prickled before her mind understood why. The jar knew this place. Or this place knew the jar.

* * *

The records room was smaller than she expected — a single vaulted chamber, its shelves cut directly into the bedrock, no wood anywhere, as if the founders had feared even the furniture might burn or rot or be carried off. Every other archive in the guild was vellum and oak, replaceable, insurable. This room was carved from the city's bones.

The ledgers here were not like her ledgers. She had spent her life among the guild's books, and she knew their grammar the way she knew her own pulse: columns of weight, of clarity, of provenance; prices struck through and restated; the small marginal shorthand that meant *damaged*, *faded*, *sold below reserve*. These books spoke a different language entirely. There were no prices. There were no weights. There were names — long lists of names, hundreds of them, each followed by a word she needed several pages to decode because it appeared so rarely in modern guild script:

*Oathbound.*

Not debtors. Not clients. Not sellers. Oathbound. People who had given something without payment, without ledger entry, without expectation of return. In eleven years of keeping the guild's accounts, Odile had never recorded a single gift. The guild did not deal in gifts. The guild dealt in hunger, in grief priced by the ounce, in the desperate arithmetic of people who needed coin more than they needed their wedding day or their mother's voice or the name of the street they grew up on.

And yet here were three hundred names, entered in a hand so fine and disciplined it looked less like writing than like stitching, each one marked *Oathbound*, each one followed by a second notation she could not at first read — a symbol like a spire seen through rain, a vertical line crossed by falling strokes.

She found the first familiar name on the fourth page. *Oyelaran.*

Her own name. Not hers — a woman named Sefa Oyelaran, dead two hundred years before Odile's birth, but the name, the exact name, in the exact old orthography her grandmother had used for the family graves. Odile stood very still in the candlelight and felt the cellar get colder, or felt herself get colder, which amounted to the same thing.

She read the entry twice. Then a third time, moving her lips, the way Tovi read.

*Sefa Oyelaran, oathbound. Keeper-line. Vessel-warden, third generation. Surrendered: all memory of the war entire, per covenant. Retained: the lock. Term: until the vessel opens or the line fails.*

Odile set the candle down because her hands had begun to shake, and she did not trust herself to hold flame near three-hundred-year-old vellum.

*Vessel-warden.* She said the words aloud and heard how absurd they sounded in the stone room, and then heard how they sounded again in the silence after, where they did not sound absurd at all. They sounded like a job description. They sounded like a sentence — both meanings at once, the grammatical kind and the punitive kind, and she suspected the founders had enjoyed that.

* * *

It took her most of the night to assemble the shape of it, working backward through the ledgers the way she would work backward through any fraudulent account: find the earliest entry, find the discrepancy, follow the discrepancy to whatever someone had wanted hidden.

The earliest entries predated the guild's charter by six years. That alone was damning — every clerk learned in their first month that the Vellum Guild was founded in the Year of Ash, chartered by the Provisional Council to "preserve and equitably distribute the mnemonic heritage of the city." A respectable origin. A civic origin. But these books began six years earlier, in the last year of the war itself, and the handwriting of those first pages belonged to people who wrote by candlelight in rooms that shook.

She pieced together what they had done, and the piecing-together was the worst part, because it was not mysterious. It was bookkeeping. It was exactly the kind of bookkeeping she did every day, applied to an atrocity.

The gods had been dying. Everyone knew the outline — the war between the old powers, the Exile, the century of silence afterward — but the ledgers held the interior of it, the unglamorous interior, the part no hymn covered. The surviving kin had not all fought to the end. Some had fled. And one — the one whose death lived in her jar, whose final memory had been pressed into a mortal bloodline like a flower into a book — had not died cleanly. Had died *slowly*, hiding pieces of itself in the world, and the largest piece, the keystone piece, the memory of its own death and everything that death contained —

— contained *them*. Their kin. Their abandonment. Their guilt.

That was why the founders had not destroyed it. She saw it now, laid out across four ledgers like a diagram. They could not destroy it, because destroying it meant destroying the evidence of what they had done — no. No, that was too simple, too flattering. She made herself read further, made herself be honest, because honesty was the one professional discipline she had never allowed herself to abandon.

They could not destroy it because the memory was anchored. A god's memory did not sit in a jar the way a baker's memory sat in a jar. It rooted. It had been pressed into a bloodline, woven through generations like a warp thread, and cutting it would have unraveled every life it passed through — killed the line outright, perhaps, or worse, left the memory loose in the world, masterless, seeking any open mind the way floodwater seeks a low door. The founders had faced a choice: burn the thread and risk the flood, or build a cage around it and call the cage a guild.

So they had built the cage. The Vellum Guild — vaults, weighing rooms, pricing tables, the whole vast apparatus of commerce — was a shell around a single prisoner. Every stolen memory bought and sold in three centuries of trade had been, whether the traders knew it or not, camouflage. Noise. A thousand thousand mortal memories packed around one divine fragment, so that anything hunting it by resonance would drown in the static of ordinary human grief. The guild had not been founded to preserve the city's memories.

It had been founded to hide one memory inside everyone else's.

And the lock — the lock was her.

* * *

She found Sefa Oyelaran's descendants listed generation after generation, and the pattern was so consistent it stopped being a pattern and became a mechanism. Each generation, one Oyelaran entered the guild's service. Never the eldest, interestingly — always a middle child, a spare, someone the family could spare. Each one marked *vessel-warden*. Each one assigned, by the internal rosters she cross-referenced against the modern personnel files upstairs, to the same post: keeper of the deep ledgers. Auditor of the vaults. The position Odile had held for eleven years.

The position, she realized, she had been *given*. She remembered the interview — she remembered being nineteen and desperate and astonishingly good at the aptitude trials, better than candidates with ten years' experience, and she remembered the Guildmaster of that time looking at her results with an expression she had taken for admiration and now recognized, with a lurch, as recognition. He had known what she was before she walked in the room. They had all known, every warden back through the generations. Hired not for talent but for blood. Her whole career — the one thing she had believed was hers, earned, *authentic* — was a hereditary appointment dressed up as merit.

Her childhood flickered. She had learned, weeks ago, opening the jar a crack, that her early years were forgery — stitched from the god's memory, a false past laid over the true one. But there was a difference between knowing a wall is painted and finding the brick underneath. Now she found the bricks. The village by the sea she remembered loving had no record of ever housing an Oyelaran. The mother whose hands she remembered — flour-dusted, warm, humming — matched no grave, no census, no parish book. The memory of learning letters at age five sat precisely where the god's memory of learning *names* — the true names of things, the old power — had needed a child-shaped container to live in.

She had not been raised. She had been *installed*.

And the installation had instructions. She found them in the last ledger, the thinnest one, kept apart from the others on a shelf of its own at chest height — placed, she understood suddenly, at the height of a kneeling person reading. Written for her. For whoever came after her. The founders had written it plainly, almost gently, in the same fine stitching-hand:

*To the warden who reads this: you were not chosen. You were made. The memory sleeps in you because it has slept in your mothers and fathers since the war, and it will sleep in your children after, if there are children. You will feel it as longing. You will feel it as homesickness for a place you have never been. Do not mistake this for your own heart. Do not open the vessel. Do not answer it when it calls your name —*

She stopped reading.

Because the next line was a name. Not an instruction. A name, written out in full, in that careful stitching hand, and it was not Odile. It was not any name anyone had ever called her.

It was the name the hundred strangers had spoken through the flooded streets, the night the vaults breached — the name she had told no one, the name she had tried to bury under work and candlelight and the comfortable rhythm of audit, the name that had followed her into sleep ever since like a dog that knows its owner.

There it was. On the page. Two hundred years old. Written by a woman named Sefa Oyelaran who had surrendered every memory of the war except the lock, and who had nevertheless, in her last lucid year, written down the one thing the lock whispered to her in the dark.

*It will tell you this is your name,* the entry continued, beneath it. *It is lying. It is not lying either. We could not decide, in the end, and neither will you.*

Odile laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, and echoed off the bedrock, and she pressed her hand over her mouth and stood in the oldest room in the guild and shook — not weeping, exactly, something drier and more structural than weeping, the shaking of a building whose foundation has been discovered to be hollow.

* * *

She did not know how long she stood there. Long enough for the candle to gutter twice. When she finally moved, it was because she heard footsteps on the stair — slow, deliberate, unhurried in the way that only one visitor to this building ever was.

Ashen descended into the lamplight wearing his customs clerk's body like a borrowed coat, hands folded, head tilted with that terrible courtesy he never abandoned, even now, even here. His eyes went first to the open ledgers, then to her face, and something in them shifted — not triumph. Something wearier.

"You found the founding," he said. "Most wardens never do. Most wardens live their whole term and die without ever coming down these stairs." He paused. "You came down on your own. That's either courage or the vessel pulling you, and I confess I no longer know how to tell those apart."

"Say what you came to say."

"What I came to say," Ashen said, "is that my kin have finished their interviews. Every clerk, every debtor, every client of record. And tomorrow, Vane intends to sell them the seventh floor." He let that settle. "He believes he can broker your surrender. He believes the memory is a thing that can be handed over at a price, like a jar of summer." His gaze dropped, briefly, to the satchel at her hip — to the faint green warmth bleeding through the leather. "He may even be right. But I have read these same ledgers, Odile Oyelaran, in another body, in another century, and there is one thing Vane does not know, and one thing you have just learned, and they are the same thing."

"The guild isn't a business," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "It's a cell. And I'm not the jailer."

"No," Ashen said softly. "You're the door. And doors, little warden, are the one thing in any prison that gets opened from both sides." He stepped aside, gesturing toward the stair with a clerk's polite economy. "Your apprentice is waiting above. She has been arguing with my colleagues for an hour about the proper tariff on grief, and she is winning, and I think you should see it before either of us does something historic."

Odile closed the founding ledger. Beneath her palm, the vellum was warm — or her palm was, or the thing threaded through her bloodline was, and the distinction had finally, irrevocably, stopped mattering.

She climbed toward the light, carrying the name under the floor up into the world that was about to learn it.
