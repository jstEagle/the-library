## 17. Vane Ascendant

The smoke had not finished rising when Vane began to profit from it.

He stood at the window of his private office on the ninth floor — the one office in the Vellum Guild with a view of the harbor rather than the city, because Vane had always believed a man should watch what he intended to own — and watched the ash of three centuries of ledgers drift down over the rooftops like grey snow. It settled on the awnings of the fishmongers. It settled on the shoulders of the penitents already gathering at the Spire. It settled, with a certain poetry he intended to charge someone for later, on the guild's own doorstep.

Below, in the courtyard, clerks with scorched sleeves were carrying out armfuls of half-burned vellum and weeping over them. One of the senior appraisers, a woman named Corvel who had spent forty years weighing other people's childhoods to the dram, sat on the steps with a charred ledger in her lap, turning pages that crumbled at her touch, and Vane found he could not summon even a flicker of sympathy. Grief over archives was a luxury of people who believed the past was irreplaceable.

Vane had never believed any such thing. The past, in his experience, was inventory. And inventory, however it was lost, always left a margin somewhere.

The fire had started in the east stacks at the hour of second candle, set — according to the two survivors of the night watch, who had told him everything with the eager terror of men who knew they would be blamed — by a delegation from the Saltmere branch, three of them, wearing guild colors and carrying guild seals. The Saltmere faction. His rivals within his own house. They had learned what he had spent months learning: that somewhere in the guild's keeping sat a memory that did not belong to any mortal ledger, that prayers were being answered again, and that whoever controlled the last unaccounted fragment of a dead god controlled, in a very real sense, the terms of the new world.

So they had burned the books. All the debts. Every record of who had pawned what, who owed whom, which families had traded away their grandmothers' faces and their sons' first words. Three centuries of obligation, gone in a night.

Amateurs, Vane thought, watching a sheet of burning vellum spiral past his window like a dying bird. They had destroyed the debts because they feared the debts. They had not understood that the debts were never the treasure. The treasure was the one debt that had never been written down at all.

He turned from the window and crossed to his desk, where a single object sat upon green baize: a brass lamp, unlit, and beside it a folded map drawn in his own hand over the past eleven sleepless days. The map showed the Vellum Guild as it truly was — not the public floors, not the vaults the Saltmere fools had burned, but the architecture beneath the architecture. The blind corridors. The sealed stairwells. The seventh floor, which appeared on no plan and in no ledger, and which had been, until recently, the resting place of a jar with Odile Oyelaran's handwriting on its label.

He had known about the jar for years. That was the thing Odile had never understood about him, the thing she would perhaps understand too late: Vane did not need ledgers. Ledgers were for men who could not hold a house in their heads. He had watched her carry that jar down from the seventh floor on the night the fire began — had watched from the shadows of the sixth-floor landing, saying nothing, helping nothing, because a man does not interrupt the moment his fortune begins to move. She had taken it somewhere. He did not yet know where. But he knew the guild's every blind spot, because he had built half of them, and he knew Odile Oyelaran's habits better than she knew them herself, because he had spent nine years cultivating her exactly the way one cultivates a vine: patiently, in the dark, toward fruit.

Let her run. A jar in motion was a jar that could be intercepted. A jar in motion was, more importantly, a jar whose existence could now be *advertised*.

Vane lit the lamp, unfolded the map, and made one final annotation — a small circle on the seventh floor, and beside it, in his small, immaculate hand: *allegedly relocated*. The word *allegedly* was the whole of his genius. He did not know where the memory was. He did not need to know where the memory was. He needed only to be the man standing between everyone who wanted it and everyone who claimed to know.

Scarcity, Vane had told a hundred debtors across a hundred negotiating tables, is not a property of things. It is a property of doors.

* * *

They came to him at dusk, in the Hall of Weights, because Vane had insisted on neutral ground and then, with the same breath, insisted that neutral ground be his.

There were seven of them. He had expected fewer; he had hoped for more. The god-remnants came in borrowed bodies the way wealthy travelers came in borrowed coats — carelessly, expensively, with an air of having better things at home. The one who had called itself Ashen wore the customs clerk again, a grey, forgettable man with ink-stained fingers, and stood slightly apart from the others in a way Vane noted and intended to exploit. The rest were strangers wearing strangers: a dockhand with river-light moving behind her eyes, an old priest whose borrowed face kept forgetting its own expressions, a child — or something wearing a child — who sat on the edge of the appraisal dais and swung its legs and looked at Vane with the patience of geology.

They did not speak first. That was the old etiquette, and Vane had done his reading; he had spent three weeks in the burned ruins of the pre-Exile archives — the one fireproof case, ironically, that Saltmere's arsonists had missed — teaching himself the forms of address, the protocols of parley, the grammar in which one negotiated with things that had watched empires as briefly as empires watched weather.

"Guildmaster Vane," he said, bowing — not deeply, never deeply; a man who bowed deeply to a god was a man who had already named his price too low. "On behalf of the Vellum Guild, and in my capacity as its sole surviving officer with knowledge of its holdings, I welcome the kin of the Spire."

"The guild burned," said the dockhand, in a voice like water over stones.

"The guild's *records* burned," Vane corrected pleasantly. "The guild is its obligations, and its obligations survive in me. Every debt the guild ever held, I can reconstruct from memory — that is, I confess, why the house made me Guildmaster. I am, in a sense you may appreciate, a ledger myself." He let the smile come and go. "Which brings us to the matter of the seventh floor."

The child stopped swinging its legs.

"There is a memory in my keeping," Vane said. "You know this. You would not have come otherwise. It is the last of your sibling — the one whose death the city is currently celebrating as a resurrection, though I suspect you know better than the crowds do what actually answered those prayers. I hold the fragment. I hold it absolutely. And unlike my late rivals in the Saltmere branch, I have not the slightest intention of destroying it, selling it cheaply, or letting it leak into the streets to possess fishwives and frighten children." He folded his hands. "I intend to *broker* it."

"Broker," said Ashen. The clerk's voice was flat, but Vane heard the pressure beneath it, the way ice hears the river. "You would sell our dead to us."

"I would *price* your dead," Vane said, "which is the only honest thing anyone has done with it in three hundred years. Consider what the alternatives are. You can tear the city apart searching for it — and you may, in time, find it, though the guild's blind spots were designed by people who expected to be hunted by gods, and I will sell that knowledge to no one. Or you can burn the city, as you burned the world once before, and take your chances in the ash. Or—" and here he let the pause do its work, let it stretch until even the child leaned forward— "you can treat with me, as one power treats with another, and have what you want by week's end, on terms."

"On *your* terms," said the old priest.

"Every covenant has terms," Vane said. "Yours had them too, once — the old rites, the sacrifices, the whole apparatus of worship. That was a covenant, honored ones. A contract between the divine and the mortal, with the divine setting the price. I am merely proposing to correct the historical imbalance." He gestured, and a clerk — a real one, hired that morning, ignorant and therefore safe — wheeled in a table bearing wine, water, and a single sheet of fresh vellum, deliberately blank. "War or worship. Those are the options I am authorized to offer — and I am, I remind you, authorized by no one, which makes me the most flexible negotiator on this continent. War, and I release the fragment into the streets and let your sibling's last memory scatter through ten thousand mortal heads, and you may spend the next century hunting echoes. Worship — by which I mean structured, exclusive, *mediated* access — and I deliver the memory intact, to whom among you you collectively designate, with the guild as the standing instrument of the new covenant. Prayers answered through the ledger. Gifts weighed at the guild's scales. Every miracle in the city passing through one door." He spread his hands. "My door."

Silence. The river-light behind the dockhand's eyes brightened and dimmed, brightened and dimmed, like something thinking in a language older than tides.

Vane had prepared for many responses. Rage. Contempt. The casual erasure of his body, which he had insured against in three separate and mutually contradictory ways. What he had not fully prepared for — though he adapted to it in the moment, as he adapted to everything — was the thing that actually happened, which was that the child on the dais began to laugh. It was a child's laugh, bright and awful, and the other six did not join it, but none of them stopped it, and Vane understood suddenly that he was watching something older than disagreement: a family that had forgotten how to speak to itself except through strangers.

"You smell of the vaults," the child said. "You have been *weighing* us. Since we walked in. You have been weighing us and writing prices on the inside of your skull."

"I weigh everything," Vane said. "It is not disrespect. It is my religion."

"And what do you weigh?" said Ashen, and there it was at last, the pressure cracking through, the grief under the grey clerk's skin. "What is your price, Guildmaster? Name it honestly, for once. Not a percentage. Not a toll. What do *you* want?"

And Vane, who had lied beautifully to debtors and gods and himself for forty years, found — to his own mild surprise — that he had an honest answer, and that the honest answer was more seductive than any lie.

"Relevance," he said. "The old world ended and the guild endured by being *necessary*. The new world is being born in this room, tonight, and I intend the guild to endure it the same way. I do not want to own your sibling's memory. I want to own the *terms on which it changes hands* — forever. Kings will come and go. Gods, I am given to understand, come and go. But there will always be a debt, and always a door, and I intend that the door be mine." He picked up the pen, dipped it, and held it out across the table — not to Ashen, not to the child, but to the space between them, to the family as a whole. "Sign, and the age of unanswered prayers ends tonight, on terms. Refuse, and it ends anyway, in fire, on none."

The pen hung in the lamplight between them.

It was Ashen who moved first — but not toward the pen. The grey clerk stepped back, away from the table, away from the others, and Vane watched the family fracture in real time, watched the dockhand's river-light flare with something like contempt, watched the child's laughter die into stillness. There was a quarrel here that predated the war, that predated the burning, and Vane felt the exquisite vertigo of a man who has thrown a stone into a well and heard, instead of a splash, an argument.

"The fragment is not yours to sell," Ashen said quietly, to the others, not to Vane. "And it is not theirs to buy. You know what is in it. You know what *we* did."

"We know what is *claimed* to be in it," said the old priest.

"You would let this—" Ashen's borrowed hand gestured at Vane, and the clerk's face could not hold the expression it needed, and for one moment something vast and wounded looked out through the ink-stained eyes— "you would let this *toll-keeper* auction our guilt—"

"I would let him auction *leverage*," said the child, "which is what guilt is, little brother, once the grieving is done. It is the only currency that survives its own spending."

And Vane stood very still behind his table with his pen extended, saying nothing at all, because he had learned long ago that the most profitable thing a negotiator can do when his clients begin to devour one another is to be the quiet, reasonable, *available* surface they eventually crawl back to.

The dockhand spoke at last, and her voice was the voice of a verdict. "We will treat. Provisionally. Through you." Her river-eyes fixed on him. "But hear the terms of *our* side, ledger-man. If you have lied about what you hold — if the fragment is damaged, or scattered, or already loose in your burning city — then the negotiation is over, and what follows will not be war, because war is between armies. It will be weather."

"Of course," said Vane, bowing, warm, untroubled, already revising the map in his head. "You shall have proof of the fragment's condition within the week."

* * *

He dismissed them at moonrise and stood alone in the Hall of Weights for a long time, drinking the guild's good wine, listening to the building settle around him like an old animal.

Provisionally. He turned the word over. Provisionally was fine. Provisional was how all empires began. He had seven signatures pending, a city in religious hysteria, a burned archive that made him the last man alive who knew where anything was — or who could convincingly pretend to be — and a rival faction of arsonists who had, in their stupidity, done his consolidation for him. By month's end the Saltmere branch would be a memory, and memories, as the guild had always known, could be priced, pawned, and sold.

There remained the matter of Odile Oyelaran, and the jar, and the seventh floor's empty cradle.

He had sent no one after her yet. That was deliberate. A hunted courier runs; an unwatched one *settles*, and settled things could be found. He had instead done something subtler: he had let one sentence slip, in the hearing of a clerk he knew to be loose-tongued, to the effect that the Guildmaster believed the Oyelaran woman had fled the city entirely. Let her hear, through the guild's endless gossiping veins, that she was believed gone. Believed-gone people grew careless. Believed-gone people wrote letters.

And in the meantime, there was a smaller matter, a matter of architecture. Because Vane had gone down to the seventh floor that morning, before the parley, and stood in the chamber where the jar had rested for three hundred years, and he had noticed something that troubled him more than he showed — a matter of dust, of disturbed stone, of a floor that had been opened *recently*, and not by Odile.

Someone else knew about the lock in the bloodline. Someone else had been reading the founding records. Someone else was moving through his guild in the dark, and the founding records were very clear about who the lock had been designed to hold — and Vane, who weighed everything, had weighed this too, and had arrived at a figure he did not like at all.

It was possible, he admitted to the empty hall, swirling his wine, that Odile Oyelaran was not running *away* with the memory at all.

It was possible she was running *toward* something. Toward the debtors, perhaps — those hollow, grateful, strange clients she had spent the last weeks visiting, one after another, like a woman collecting pieces of something. Toward the burned and the cheated and the memoryless. Toward, Vane realized, setting down his glass with a small sharp click, the only army in this city he had never thought to price, because he had assumed they had nothing left to give.

He smiled at the dark window, at the ash still falling, and reached for a fresh sheet of vellum.

"Note for the ledger," he said aloud, to no one, in the manner of a man beginning a very long entry. "New asset class discovered. Liabilities, self-identified, in large numbers. Valuation: pending."

Somewhere below, in the city, a bell began to ring — not the guild's bell, not any bell he had licensed. He listened to it, and frowned, and began, for the first time in his life, to feel the faint cold draft of a door he had not built.
