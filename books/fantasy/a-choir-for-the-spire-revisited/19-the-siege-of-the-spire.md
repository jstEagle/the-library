## 19. The Siege of the Spire

* * *

Vane watched the city from the Spire's highest gallery and thought it looked like a ledger about to be balanced.

Below, the streets had emptied into a single river of bodies flowing toward the tower — debtors, beggars, hollow-eyed clients with their pawned pasts showing through their faces like watermarks. They carried candles. Of course they carried candles. The poor always believed fire was theirs.

He turned from the window to his hired blades, forty of them arrayed on the marble in oiled leather and quiet confidence. He had paid for the best the fractured guilds could offer: the Kettle Street Company, two brothers out of the Saltmarsh wars, a woman named Brakka who was said to have once killed a man through a closed door.

"The girl will read from the belfry," Vane said. "She will be given every chance to stop. If she does not stop—" he let the pause do its work, "—she will not read."

"And if the gods come?" asked the elder Saltmarsh brother. He said *gods* the way other men said *plague*.

"Then you will learn," Vane said pleasantly, "why I am worth what I cost."

It was not entirely a lie. He had spent three days in negotiation with things that wore borrowed flesh, trading terms like a merchant trading futures. He held the only complete map of the vaults. He alone knew which memories had survived the fire, which had fled into the streets, and which still sat sealed in Odile Oyelaran's keeping. War or worship — he had offered both, priced both, and the kin had agreed to hear him out before they heard her.

That was all he needed. An hour. An hour in which his voice would be the only one the gods trusted.

He straightened his cuffs. Outside, the first horns sounded from the harbor quarter, where Ashen's kin were coming up from the water, walking calmly through the crowd the way weather walks through a field.

"Positions," Vane said. "And gentlemen — if anything falls from the sky tonight that used to belong to someone's childhood, do not touch it."

Brakka almost smiled. "What happens if we do?"

"You become someone else," Vane said, "and I do not pay people who are someone else."

* * *

Ashen came up through the crowd wearing the customs clerk's body like a coat that no longer fit.

The kin moved around him — six remnants, seven, he had stopped counting precisely, because counting required admitting how few remained. They had crossed the harbor at dusk, and the sea had gone flat and attentive beneath them, the way it did when old things passed over old places. Mortals pressed back from them without knowing why, the way animals press back from a change in pressure.

They were not going to negotiate. Ashen had made that clear on the walk from the well. Whatever bargain the Guildmaster imagined himself brokering, it would end the way all bargains with mortals ended: with something of theirs in mortal hands, accruing interest.

But grief is patient, and Ashen had learned patience across three centuries of being less than he was. So he walked, and the crowd parted, and above them the Spire rose black against a sky that had begun, very softly, to snow.

Except it was not snow.

The flakes came down slow and silvered, catching lamplight, catching candlelight, and where they touched the ground they did not melt but sank, and where they sank, people stopped mid-stride and wept without knowing why. A baker's wife caught one on her palm and suddenly remembered her mother's hands, though her mother had died forty years ago and she herself had sold those memories to the Vellum Guild to pay a fever debt. She stood in the street holding a falling memory like a snowflake and sobbed with gratitude and horror in equal measure.

The breach below the guildhall was still open. The burned archives were still bleeding.

"They're frightened," said Sef, the youngest of them, a remnant wearing a fishwife's shoulders. "The loose ones. They can feel us coming."

"Let them feel us," said Korren, who had been a god of thresholds once and now had a limp. "Let everything feel us. We are owed."

"We are *owed nothing*," Ashen said, more sharply than he intended, and the street went quiet around him as though the air itself had flinched. He softened. That was the discipline. That was the whole discipline of being this small. "We are owed nothing. We came for our sibling's last breath. Nothing else. Say it until you believe it, because if we go to war over a jar, we will lose, and this time there will be no one left even to remember losing."

Sef looked up at the snowfall of stolen lives. "And the mortals? What they've taken—"

"Was given to them by us," Ashen said. "Every covenant we broke, every prayer we answered cheaply, every miracle we sold like wine. Their thieving learned from ours." He began walking again. "Come. The girl is at the top of that tower, and she has been carrying my brother's death in her chest since before she could speak. I want to know what it sounds like from inside her."

* * *

Odile climbed the Spire's inner stair with the jar against her heart and the ledger under her arm, and Tovi climbed beside her, and behind them came everyone the guild had ever wronged.

It was not an army. It was a procession of damage. Old Marta, who had pawned her wedding day and could no longer remember why she grieved each spring. The twins from Rope Lane who had sold their father's face between them and now shared half a memory like half a blanket. Debtors with tally-scars on their forearms, clients with the pale look of people whose interiors had been gutted and repainted by strangers. Two hundred souls, perhaps. Three hundred. Odile had stopped counting too, because what she was counting was not numbers but weight, and the weight was beyond arithmetic.

The stair wound upward through the dark, and their candles made a moving constellation against the walls.

"You're doing the breathing wrong," Tovi signed. Tovi's voice lived in those quick hands; the fever had taken sound from Tovi's world at age four, and Odile had long ago learned to read the signing while climbing, while crying, while lying awake.

"I'm breathing fine."

"You're breathing like someone about to lie to gods." Tovi's hands were steady, but Tovi's eyes kept going to the ledger under Odile's arm. To the book. Not the jar. Odile had noticed this three flights ago and had not yet found the courage to ask why.

"It's not a lie," Odile said aloud, because some things deserved voice even if Tovi couldn't hear them. "It's the opposite of a lie. It's the first true thing I'll ever have said."

Tovi signed something small then, almost private: *Then say it loud enough for the deaf.*

They reached the belfry gallery as the bells began — not rung by any hand, but humming, resonating, the whole bronze throat of the Spire vibrating with the pressure of what approached. Through the arched openings Odile could see the city: the river of candles, the silver snowfall of escaped memories drifting down through the smoke, and at the edges, converging, the shapes of Vane's blades moving through the crowd with the terrible politeness of professionals, and beyond them, walking up from the water, the kin.

Between her and the sky stood nothing but bells.

"Odile." Tovi caught her sleeve. Below, on the spiral stair, the procession was still coming, still singing — they had begun to sing somewhere around the fourth floor, the debtors' choir, wordless, cracked, beautiful. "If this works. If they take it back. You understand what you lose?"

"I lose a childhood I stole," Odile said.

"You lose *yourself*. Whoever you were before the jar — you don't come back to her. There's no her. There's just the hole where twenty years should be, and then whatever you build after." Tovi's hands trembled now, and the trembling traveled up the arms and into the set of the shoulders, and Odile saw that Tovi had understood the mathematics better and sooner than she had. "I'm asking if you've actually done that sum."

"No," Odile admitted. "I've been avoiding the sum. But I've audited enough ledgers to know when the numbers can't be argued with." She lifted the jar. Inside, the god's last memory turned slowly, like sediment in wine, and faintly, faintly, it glowed. "Someone died so this could exist. Someone died *hiding* it. And a child was hollowed out to hold it. I don't get to keep the arrangement just because I'm fond of the tenant."

Tovi looked at her for a long moment. Then Tovi took the ledger out of her arms.

"I'll carry the book," Tovi signed. "You carry the truth. That way neither of us drops what we're worst at holding."

It was such a small kindness, offered at the edge of the end of the world, that Odile nearly wept. She had time to notice that. She would always have time to notice that, afterward. She noticed the bells humming, and Tovi tucking the ledger inside a coat lined with wax-cloth against fire, and the first crossbow bolt coming through the western arch—

—and then the night tore open, and the siege began.

* * *

Brakka took the belfry stair with eight of hers and lost four of them on the way up.

Not to blades. That was the thing none of the contracts had covered. The stairwell was full of loose memories — the breach below had pumped the tower full of them like smoke — and they came off the walls and out of the air and took whoever touched them. A Kettle Street man brushed a drifting wisp in the dark and sat down on the stairs and began to live, visibly, the life of a shepherd he had never been, weeping and laughing, his sword clattering down the steps. Another caught a memory of drowning and drowned standing up, thrashing against nothing, until Brakka knocked him cold to save him.

"Up!" she roared. "Don't breathe deep! Don't touch the walls! Up!"

They burst onto the gallery into bell-hum and candlelight and there she was — the ledger-keeper, the little auditor, standing at the rail with a sealed jar raised in both hands like an offering or a weapon, and beside her a deaf apprentice holding a book.

"Odile Oyelaran," Brakka called over the bells. "Guildmaster's terms. Put it down, walk down, live rich. Or put it down anyway, because we're taking it either way, and the difference is whether you're alive to see it go."

Odile looked at her with eyes that were far too calm. "Do you know what's in this jar?"

"Don't care."

"A god's death. The last unbroken piece of the war. And if your blades break it, everything loose in this tower tonight will look like a gentle dream." She raised it higher. "I'm not putting it down. I'm giving it back. Those aren't the same thing, and the difference is going to matter to you personally in about a minute."

Brakka had killed men through doors. She was not a woman given to hesitation. But something in the air had changed — the bells had stopped humming and started *listening* — and the professional instinct that had kept her alive through three wars told her, clearly and coldly, that she was standing in a room where the price of everything was about to be renegotiated.

"Take the book," she said instead. "The Guildmaster wants the ledgers. Leave the girl."

And that was when Tovi stepped in front of the ledger, spread both arms wide, and lied fluently in the oldest language there is: the body saying *no*.

* * *

Ashen felt the moment the memory rose toward the sky.

It was like hearing a chord struck on an instrument three streets away — every remnant on the Spire square went still at once, six borrowed bodies turning their borrowed faces upward. High above, through the belfry arches, a light was kindling that did not behave like light. It behaved like recognition.

"There," Sef breathed.

Korren was already moving, limping through the crowd, and the crowd let him, and Ashen followed, and the last of the kin came after, and above them Vane's men were swarming the tower like ants on a corpse, and between the two ascents the debtors' choir was still singing, ragged and unafraid.

A blade found the choir first. Ashen never learned whose — one of Vane's professionals, cutting a path, and an old debtor went down with his song still in his mouth, and the song did not stop, it simply continued in the mouths around him, and Ashen understood with a lurch that these mortals had already surrendered something, freely, to be here, and that free surrender was armor no mercenary's steel had ever been forged to pierce.

"Stop them," Ashen said quietly, and the kin stopped the mercenaries — not with fire, not with wrath, but with presence, with the old pressure of divinity pressing down like deep water, and swords drooped, and men knelt without deciding to, and Brakka's company on the high gallery found their arms suddenly too heavy to raise against the sky.

But mortals are inventive, and fear is fuel, and somewhere below a fire-pot arced up through the dark trailing flame, thrown by a hand that had decided kneeling was worse than dying. It burst against the belfry's wooden shingling, and the ancient timber caught, and burning memories fell like brand-new stars.

* * *

Later, Odile would remember it in fragments, the way the vaults stored what they stored: in pieces, priced by weight.

Fire on the roof. Bells groaning. Tovi's arms spread before the ledger like a gate. The jar blazing in her hands, warmer now, answering the flames, answering the kin, answering something older than all of it. Brakka's soldiers frozen mid-violence by the weight of watching gods. And through the smoke, climbing the outside of the tower itself — because of course they did not need stairs — the kin, ascending with terrible gentleness, their borrowed faces lit orange and silver.

"ODILE OYELARAN." The voice came from everywhere, from the bells, from the fire, from the snowfall of memories still sifting down through the smoke. It was a voice like a door opening onto a country she had never visited and had always missed. Her sibling's voice. The dead god's kin, speaking with one throat. "GIVE US WHAT WAS HIDDEN IN YOUR BLOOD."

"I intend to," she called back, "but not to you! Not like this!"

The fire reached the gallery. Heat licked along the floorboards. Somewhere below, the choir faltered as the tower shook, and resumed.

Then a crossbow bolt — fired blind through smoke by some terrified man on the stair — took Tovi in the side.

Odile saw it happen the way she had once seen entries post themselves in a ledger: instantly, irreversibly, in ink that could not be recalled. Tovi folded around the ledger, around the book, curling over it as the body dropped, shielding it with ribs and blood and stubbornness, and the wax-cloth lining smoked, and the book did not burn, and Tovi did not rise.

"Tovi—"

"*Read,*" said the voice of the dead god's kin, closer now, at the arch, filling the firelight with shapes like grief given posture. "*Or bury another of us unfinished.*"

Odile knelt. Blood, warm through her skirt. Tovi's eyes open, fixed on her face, and Tovi's hands — oh, Tovi's hands — lifting, shaking, signing one last message in a language the gods could not hear and the fire could not burn:

*Say it loud enough for the deaf.*

Odile picked up the jar. She stood. The roof burned above her and the gods waited below her and the ledger lay under her dying friend's body, and she opened her mouth to speak the covenant —

— and Vane's voice cut through everything, from the stair, from the dark, smooth as a closing contract:

"That will be quite enough. The lady has no authority here. I do."
