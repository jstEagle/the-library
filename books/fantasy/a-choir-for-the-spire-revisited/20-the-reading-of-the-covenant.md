## 20. The Reading of the Covenant

The spire had no doors left. The siege had taken them, along with the windows, half the bell tower, and the great rose window that had shown the Vellum Guild's founders — in colored glass — weighing sins on brass scales. Odile stood where the doors had been and felt the wind of the city come through the wound like breath into a lung.

Below her, in the shattered nave, the mob and the gods had arrived at the same place from opposite ends of the street.

The mob came up from the poor quarter: debtors with their hollow eyes, clients who had pawned their mothers' faces, the weeping strangers still leaking fugues from the burned vaults, Tovi carried on a door taken from a house that no longer had need of doors. The gods came down from the sky and the wells and the cracks in the world, wearing bodies the way beggars wear borrowed coats — Ashen in the customs clerk's flesh, patient and terrible; a tall figure made of river-light that had once been worshipped in three drowned cities; a small god that looked like a child and had not been a child in four thousand years. And above them all, everywhere and nowhere, the dead one — the voice in the water, the name in the walls — pressing against the membrane of the world, wanting in.

Between the mob and the gods, on a dais of broken pews, stood Guildmaster Vane with his map and his price and his hired blades, and for one long moment it seemed the day would be decided by whoever reached him first.

Then Odile stepped forward, and the jar was in her hands, and the whole spire went quiet the way a room goes quiet when a debt is named aloud.

She had not planned to speak. She had planned, in the way she planned everything — in columns, in margins, in the careful hand that had kept the ledgers of small debts for nine years — to hand the jar to Ashen and be done, to let the gods settle their own accounts and take whatever was left of her when the false childhood was reclaimed. It was Tovi who had changed it. Tovi, gray-lipped on the door, fingers bleeding where the blade had gone through the hand that shielded the ledger, who had looked up at her in the doorway of the spire and said, with the flat clarity of someone who reads lips for a living and therefore knows exactly what people say when they think no one is watching:

*You're going to give it back like a thief returning stolen goods. Don't. Give it back like a keeper returning what she kept. There's a difference. Write the difference down.*

So Odile stood at the broken doors with the jar in her hands, and she did what she had been made — forged, bred, locked into a bloodline for three hundred years — to do.

She opened the ledger.

It was the master book, the one from the seventh floor, the one Tovi had bled for. The pages were smoke-stained at the edges and one corner had been singed to lace, but the columns held. She had written the new entries in the cart on the way up the hill, by the light of the burning archives, and her hand had not shaken once. Her hands shook now, holding it up before gods and mob alike, but the ink was dry, and dry ink does not tremble.

"My name," she said, and her voice carried strangely in the gutted nave, the way voices do in places built for prayer, "is Odile Oyelaran. For nine years I kept the ledgers of the Vellum Guild. For nine years I recorded what was taken from the desperate and sold to the comfortable, and for nine years I kept one entry off the books — a theft of my own, or so I believed." She lifted the jar. Inside it, the memory turned like weather, gray-gold, slow, the color of the moment before rain. "I believed I had stolen it. I learned last week that I was stolen *for* it. That my childhood was stitched around this fragment the way a glove is stitched around a hand. That my bloodline was a lock, and the guild was a box, and I was the hinge that kept it shut."

She let that sit. Let the debtors hear it. Let the hollowed clients hear it. Let Vane hear it — Vane, whose face was doing the arithmetic of a man watching his monopoly dissolve in real time, whose hired blades were already glancing at each other, recalculating whose coin would be worth more by sundown.

"The old covenant," Odile said, "was this: the gods gave, and mortals took, and when the taking grew too great, the gods went to war with their own children, and everyone lost, and the gods died, and we forgot, and we built a guild on the forgetting." She turned a page. "I am not here to restore the old covenant. I am here to read the new one. It has only one term. It is very short. I wrote it in the space of a burning building, and I have had no time to make it longer, which I consider a mercy, because covenants grow long only when someone intends to hide something in the length."

Ashen, at the edge of the dais, made a sound that might have been a laugh, quickly strangled. *Civilized menace*, Odile thought, *and underneath it, a sibling's grief with nowhere to go.* She did not look at Ashen. If she looked at Ashen she would remember chapter ten — the candle, the question about her mother, the courtesy warning — and her voice would crack, and this could not crack. Not here. Not now.

"The new covenant," she read, "is as follows.

"First: no prayer shall be answered except from willing gift. No god shall take. No god shall reach into a well or a womb or a memory uninvited. What the divine receives, it receives as the mortal gives — freely, knowingly, and entered into the record.

"Second: the record is the treaty. The ledgers of the Vellum Guild — rebuilt, jointly kept, open to any mortal who can read and any god who can wait — shall stand as the covenant itself. What is written there is binding. What is not written there was never given, and shall never be taken.

"Third: no price shall be set on necessity. The poor shall not be made to buy miracles with their mothers' faces. A gift given under compulsion — of hunger, of fear, of debt — is not a gift, and the gods shall refuse it, and the guild shall refuse to record it, and any god or man who circumvents this clause shall be answered by all the others together.

"Fourth—" and here Odile paused, because the fourth term was the one that cost her, the one she had written with Tovi's blood drying on the page beside her, "—fourth: what is returned is returned whole. The gods shall reclaim nothing by halves. If a mortal gives back a borrowed life, the mortal shall be named anew — by the one who receives — and the name given shall be true, and the mortal shall be free to keep it or refuse it."

She looked up.

The nave was absolutely silent. Even the wind had stopped coming through the broken doors, as if the city itself were leaning in.

Vane moved first. Of course he did. "This is nonsense," he said, smooth as ledger-leather. "This girl is a clerk. A bookkeeper. She holds one fragment of one dead god and she proposes to speak for all of them—" He gestured at the river-light figure, at the child-shaped god, at Ashen. "I hold the map. I hold the vaults. I hold the only working knowledge of what remains of the divine, and I am prepared to offer reasonable terms to—"

"You hold a map of a burned building," Odile said, and was surprised at how steady it came out. "I hold the ledger. You taught me the difference yourself, Guildmaster. You said it the day I was apprenticed: *anyone can own a vault. The one who keeps the books owns what's in them.*" She turned the page. "I am keeping the books."

And the dead god — the voice in the water, the fragment in the jar, the sibling these three remnants had abandoned four thousand years ago and had been hunting ever since, not to recover but to bury — spoke.

It did not speak through the jar. It spoke through everything. Through the well water in the poor quarter, through the mouths of the hundred strangers still leaking fugues in the nave, through the cracked bell above them, through the wind that had stopped and now started again all at once, and the sound it made was not thunder and was not a voice and was not music but was somehow all three, and every person in the spire — mortal and remnant alike — felt it in the place where their own name lived.

It said: *I accept.*

The jar in Odile's hands grew warm.

Not hot — warm, the way a hand is warm, the way a mother's hand was warm, except that Odile's mother had never existed and the warmth had no memory to attach itself to and therefore attached itself to Odile, simply, wholly, like a debt being paid to the only creditor in the room.

"It's time," Ashen said quietly. The customs clerk's body looked very small against the broken rose window. "You understand what happens. When it takes the memory back, it takes the childhood stitched around it. Everything you remember before the age of — before the guild. It goes. You will stand there and not know why you are standing there." Ashen's voice did something Odile had never heard it do: it gentled. "I want you to know that I would not have asked this of you a month ago. I would have taken it and let the pieces fall. I am asking now. That is the only mercy I have, and I am spending all of it."

"I know," Odile said.

And she found that she did know, and that the knowing did not make it smaller.

She thought of her childhood — the false one, the stitched one, the glove. The kitchen with the blue door in a house that had never stood anywhere. Her mother's hands, which were the guild's hands, which were a forger's hands, which had held her and taught her numbers and had been, all along, a lock teaching itself to be a woman. The first ledger she had ever kept, at seven years old, recording which neighbor children owed her marbles — already, at seven, already keeping accounts, because that was what she had been *made* to be, a hinge, a keeper, a vessel with excellent penmanship.

It was false. All of it. And it was hers. And both of those things were true, and the covenant's fourth term existed precisely because both of those things were true.

"Before I open it," Odile said, "I have one entry to make."

She set the jar on the broken pew that served as her desk, took out her pen, and wrote, in the master book, in the careful hand that had not shaken on the cart and shook now only a little:

*Received back, this day, from the undersigned: one memory, being the death of a god and the last true thing its kin abandoned. Given freely. Entered willingly. No price asked. No price paid.*

*Note to the record: the giver does not know what she will be when this entry is complete. The record asks that she be treated well.*

She signed it. Then, because it was her ledger and her covenant and her one act of authorship in a life that had been authored by others, she added a line beneath her name — a line no guild rule had ever required, a line that would have gotten her dismissed from the Vellum Guild of nine years ago:

*Countersigned by Tovi, witness, who bled for this book and is owed more than this page can hold.*

Tovi, gray and swaying on the door, lifted the unbandaged hand and made the guild's oldest sign — the one that meant *entered, true, and binding* — and the debtors in the nave, every one of them who had ever been on the wrong side of a Vellum entry, made it back.

Odile picked up the jar.

"Last words," she said to Ashen, and her voice finally cracked, and she let it. "When you name me — make it a good one. I was Odile Oyelaran, and I kept the books, and I kept them honestly in the end. Whatever I am after — let it be someone who would have done that."

"I promise," Ashen said, and gods, Odile thought, gods keep their promises, that is the one thing they have never learned not to do.

She broke the seal.

The memory did not rush out. It rose, the way water rises in a well when you have finally, after years, lowered the bucket all the way down. It rose through her hands, her wrists, the stitched seams of her — and Odile felt it going, felt the blue door go first, then the kitchen, then the marbles, then the mother's hands, then the mother, then the word *mother* itself, peeling away layer by layer like a forgery being unmade, and underneath the forgery there was not nothing, there was not a blank, there was—

There was a name.

Not *Odile*. Not Oyelaran. Not vessel, not hinge, not lock. A name that had been waiting under the floor of her the way the covenant had waited under the floor of the guild, written by no one, chosen by no one, simply *true* — and the dead god's voice, pouring out of her and into the world and into its waiting siblings, said it aloud.

The nave heard it. The mob heard it. Vane heard it and took an involuntary step backward, because a true name, spoken by a god, is the one currency the Vellum Guild never learned to trade.

And Odile — who was no longer entirely Odile, who was standing in the space between the self that had been stitched and the self that had just been named, holding a ledger with one hand and nothing at all with the other — heard it too, and found that she knew it the way you know your own heartbeat, and found that it fit, and found that she was still standing, still herself, somehow, in the way that a river is still the river after it has been renamed.

The jar was empty. The god was whole. Its siblings stood around it in the broken light, and the tall river-light figure was weeping, and the child-shaped god had its face turned away, and Ashen — Ashen was on both knees on the stone, and the customs clerk's body was shaking, and what came out of it was not menace and not civilization but four thousand years of a sibling's death finally, finally remembered by everyone it had happened to.

Odile looked down at the ledger, at the last entry, at the note she had written to the record: *the giver does not know what she will be.*

She knew now.

She picked up the pen.

* * *

The mob did not disperse. That was the first miracle of the new covenant, and it was performed entirely by mortals.

They had come up the hill expecting a war, or a miracle, or both, and instead they had watched a bookkeeper give back a stolen childhood and get a name in return, and they had heard the terms read aloud — *no prayer answered except from willing gift; no price set on necessity; the record is the treaty* — and they had understood, with the speed of people who have spent their whole lives on the wrong side of ledgers, exactly what they had been given.

They began to line up. Not to beg. To *give*.

An old woman who had pawned the memory of her wedding and wanted, now, to gift it — freely, to the river god, in thanks for a flood that had spared her street. A man who had carried guilt for thirty years and wanted to lay it down where something could finally hold it. A child with a jar of fireflies, offering the memory of watching them, because the child-shaped god had looked at the fireflies with an expression no one in the spire would ever forget.

And Odile sat at the broken pew with the master book open, recording each gift in the careful hand, entering each into the covenant, and the line grew, and the light in the nave changed from siege-light to something older, and beside her — moved there by two debtors without asking — Tovi sat propped against the pew, watching her write.

Tovi signed the witnesses' column for the first gift, and the second, and the third. Then Tovi touched Odile's arm and signed, slowly, so there could be no mistake:

*That's not your old handwriting.*

Odile looked at the page. The entries were in her hand and not in her hand — the same careful letters, the same accountant's discipline, but looser somehow, warmer, a hand that had never been taught to be a lock.

"No," she agreed, and signed the next entry, and the next, and the line of givers stretched out through the broken doors and down the hill into the city, and the gods moved along it like light moving along water.

It was nearly dusk when Vane came to the pew.

He had lost. Odile could see it in the set of his shoulders — the map burned, the vaults breached, the blades unpaid and drifting away, the monopoly dissolved into a commons. But Vane had been the Guildmaster of the Vellum Guild for twenty-two years, and a man like that does not come to surrender; he comes to renegotiate.

"Oyelaran," he said. Then, correcting himself, with the precision of a man who had heard a true name spoken and understood that names were now the whole game: "Or whatever you are called now."

Odile did not look up from the ledger. "The record is open to any mortal who can read," she said. "That was the second term. You can read."

"I want to discuss the guild's charter. The rebuilding. There are vaults that survived the fire, assets that—"

"Bring them to the line," Odile said. "Gift them, like everyone else. Freely, knowingly, entered into the record." She turned the page. "No price set on necessity, Guildmaster. That includes yours."

For a long moment Vane stood there, and Odile thought — hoped — feared — that she saw something move behind his face that was not arithmetic. Then he turned and walked away down the nave, toward the line of givers, and Odile did not see whether he joined it, because at that moment Ashen reached the pew.

The god-remnant looked emptied. Washed. The customs clerk's body seemed to fit differently now, or perhaps Ashen simply wore it differently, the way a coat fits differently once you have stopped pretending it is yours.

"The kin are withdrawing," Ashen said. "The covenant binds us. It binds *me*." A pause. "I have a proposal to make, and a debt to pay, and I would rather make the proposal to the keeper of the record than to the crowd."

Odile set down the pen.

"A debt," she said.

"You wrote a note in your ledger," Ashen said. "*The giver does not know what she will be. The record asks that she be treated well.*" The god's eyes — the clerk's eyes, gray and ordinary and bottomless — held hers. "I intend to see that note honored. But honoring it will require telling you what we found, my siblings and I, when the memory came home. What was in our sibling's last moments besides the dying. What it saw in the bloodline it chose. What it made you *for* — because vessel was only the half of it, keeper only the half of it, and the other half—" Ashen stopped. For the first time since the customs office, the god-remnant looked afraid. "The other half is the reason the war started. And it is not finished. And it will want its keeper."

The line of givers stretched down the hill. The lamps of the poor quarter were coming on, one by one, in the gathering dark. In Odile's hands, the master book lay open to a blank page, and the blank page waited the way the spire had waited for three hundred years — for someone willing to write on it.
