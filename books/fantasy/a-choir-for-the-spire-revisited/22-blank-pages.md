## 22. Blank Pages

The candle had been Tovi's idea.

*You audit by candlelight,* she had signed, her hands moving in the quick, precise grammar they had built together over the winter — half guild-sign, half invention, wholly theirs. *It's tradition. And it makes you look solemn.*

*I am solemn,* Odile had signed back.

*You are vain about looking solemn. There's a difference.*

So the candle burned now on the counting table of the Vellum Guild, reopened under charter, its flame steady in the draftless room, and Odile Oyelaran bent over a ledger that weighed nothing and everything, and found that her hands did not shake at all.

The ledger was new. That was the first thing any visitor noticed, if they were permitted past the door at all: the ledgers of the Vellum Guild had always been old, bound in vellum scraped from calfskin that had itself been blessed twice and sold once, their pages soft as the inside of an eyelid, their ink brown with age before it ever touched them. These were white. Rag paper, cotton-fibered, unbleached, sewn with linen thread by a bindery in the Weavers' Quarter that charged honestly and tipped its cap to no one. The pages were so blank they seemed to hum with it, the way a struck bell hums after the note has gone.

On the first page, in three hands, was written the covenant.

The gods' hand came first, though "hand" was a generous word for it; the script had arrived one dawn in dew on the parchment, each letter formed of condensation that refused to dry, cool to the touch months later. *What is given freely is kept freely. What is taken is not ours to hold.* Beneath it, in Ashen's careful clerk's hand — borrowed from a customs clerk whose body still fit him like a coat slightly too large in the shoulders — the same terms in the trade-tongue, with clauses. Ashen loved clauses the way other men loved wine. Beneath Ashen's, in her own ink, her own hand, the mortal countersignature: *Odile Oyelaran, Keeper of Gifts, formerly Keeper of Debts.*

Formerly. She turned the phrase over sometimes like a coin from a country that no longer existed.

She ran her thumb down the column of entries for the day. This was the work now, and she had come to love it the way one loves a wound that has finally decided to heal: not because it stopped hurting, but because the hurt had become legible.

*A memory of a wedding, given by Maren Sault, age sixty-one, who wished her late husband to know he had been forgiven. Received into the well. Weight: joyous. No payment rendered; none requested.*

*A memory of a mother's kitchen, given by an unnamed debtor of the old regime, who asked only that it be held somewhere safe until his daughter was old enough to want it back. Held in trust. Not answered. Not sold. Waiting.*

*A memory of drowning, given willingly by a fisherman of the Salt Stair, who said the sea had earned the right to remember him. Answered within the hour: the sea sent back his father's boat, patched, seaworthy, with a new rope coiled on the thwart that no one in the city could account for.*

Odile read each entry twice, as she had read every entry of the old ledgers twice — but where the double-reading had once been suspicion, checking for fraud, for smuggling, for the small dishonesties that compound like interest, now it was something closer to reverence. Every line was a gift someone had chosen to give. She was not weighing what people had lost. She was recording what they had offered.

And nothing — she checked again, the way she would check forever, the way a woman who had hidden one jar for nine years checks — nothing was off the books.

* * *

Tovi arrived at the fourth bell, shaking rain from her shoulders in the doorway like a dog, which she knew annoyed Odile and therefore performed with flourish. She was seventeen now, or claimed to be; she had stopped correcting people who guessed older, because being underestimated remained, in her professional opinion, the single most valuable asset in commerce.

She crossed the counting floor — the vaults below were open now, their jars emptied or surrendered, the shelves repurposed to hold seed grain for the river districts, which Odile considered the finest joke the building had ever told — and dropped onto the stool across the table.

*Well?* she signed.

*Well what?*

*You've been sitting there since second bell looking like a woman about to confess something. Out with it.*

Odile hesitated. Then she turned the ledger around and slid it across.

Tovi read. Her lips moved — she had never fully outgrown mouthing words even when reading sign, a habit from the years before anyone taught her that language could live in hands — and her eyebrows climbed slowly toward her hairline. When she reached the bottom of the page she looked up, and her expression was one Odile had learned to dread and treasure in equal measure: the look of Tovi about to be devastatingly reasonable.

*You're offering me partnership,* Tovi signed. *Of the whole guild. Joint keepership. Charter rights.*

*I am.*

*Me. Who forged your signature on a requisition slip eleven times during the siege.*

*Twelve. You missed one.*

*That one was practice.* Tovi's hands stilled. The play drained out of her face, and what remained underneath was younger than seventeen and older than the city. *Why?*

Because you carried the ledger through falling blades, Odile thought. Because you asked me the question no one else dared ask — *why do you get to keep a stolen life when everyone else loses theirs* — and then stayed anyway, after I gave you no answer worth having. Because when my childhood turned out to be forgery, when everything I remembered of my mother dissolved into a dead god's hiding place, you sat with me in the ash of the archives and said, *Fine. Then write a better one,* as though identity were a document that could simply be amended, and perhaps — this was the thing that undid her — perhaps it could.

But sign-language is honest in ways speech is not; you cannot mumble with your hands. So she signed the true thing:

*Because you're the only person I know who has never once wanted anything from me except the truth. And this place runs on truth now. I need a partner who will notice if I start lying again.*

Tovi studied her for a long moment. Rain ticked against the high windows. Somewhere below, in the old vault levels, the seed grain breathed its faint dry sweetness into the stone.

Then Tovi reached across the table, took Odile's pen, and wrote — aloud, in ink, in the ledger itself, in a hand far too flamboyant for a keeper of gifts — *Tovi Ansel, Partner. Terms: I get the corner office. And we never audit on fast days again.*

*The corner office has no roof. It burned.*

*Then I'll have the sky. Very fashionable.* But her eyes were bright, and wet, and she pretended they weren't, and Odile let her pretend, because some mercies are best administered silently.

* * *

Ashen came at dusk, as Ashen always did, announced by no sound at all — the customs clerk's body moved through rooms like weather through an open window — and stood dripping gently at the edge of the candlelight until Odile looked up and nodded him into a chair.

He had grown into the body over the months, or the body into him. The face was still wrong in a way that took newcomers a moment to place: too symmetrical, too patient, the face of someone listening to a song no one else could hear. But he had learned to slouch. He had learned to eat bread in the Weavers' Quarter and complain about the price of it. Last month he had laughed — actually laughed, a rusty, astonished sound, as though the mechanism had been packed away in peat for three centuries — at one of Tovi's impressions of Guildmaster Vane, may his name be filed under closed accounts.

"The well," Ashen said, without preamble. He set a small clay cup on the table between them. Water moved inside it, though the room was still. "It sang last night. Did you feel it?"

"I felt the floors hum. I assumed it was the river."

"It was the river." He smiled faintly. "The river is learning the song. Everything that flows is learning it. My kin argued about that, in the early centuries — whether memory should stay in flesh or seep outward into water, stone, wind. We thought it was a question of doctrine." He turned the cup slowly. "It was a question of generosity. We were very bad at it."

"You're better now."

"We have excellent teachers." He glanced at the ledger, at the day's entries, and something in his ancient face eased. "Maren Sault's wedding. The husband heard it at the well this morning. He walked home singing a song he'd forgotten he knew." A pause. "He wept on the Salt Stair for an hour afterward. The neighbors brought him soup. It was—" Ashen searched, visibly, among the vast vocabulary of a god-remnant, for the word. "—it was correct. All of it. The grief and the soup. That is what we were for, I think, before we forgot. Not miracles. Correctness."

"Will you stay?" Odile asked, though she knew the answer; she asked anyway, because some questions are gifts, freely given, and deserve to be received. "In the clerk's body. In the city."

Ashen looked at his own hands — the ink-stained, blunt, mortal hands he had worn through interrogation and siege and treaty alike — and flexed them once, like a man testing a rope bridge he has decided to trust.

"My sibling's last memory lives in the well now," he said quietly. "Given back. Given *willingly*, by a woman who built her whole self around it and handed it over anyway. Do you understand what that did to us? To all of us, the scattered kin?" He shook his head slowly. "We spent three hundred years hunting that fragment because we could not bear what it contained — our abandonment, our guilt, our sibling dying alone while we hid. And your guild kept it. Not kindly. Not cleanly. But *kept*, when we would have destroyed. And then you returned it, and instead of the guilt devouring us, the well sang." He met her eyes. "I will tend that well until the body fails and the water forgets my name. That is not penance, Odile. Penance implies debt. There are no debts anymore. It is only work, and I find I love work."

He rose, paused at the edge of the light.

"There is one more entry coming. Tomorrow, perhaps. A gift from my kin — collectively. They wish to surrender the memory of the war's final day. All of it. The whole of it, into the well, so that no god and no mortal carries it alone again." His voice went very quiet. "It will be heavy. When it arrives, the floors will shake, and the candle flames will bend east, and you will be afraid. Don't be. Just write it down."

*Just write it down.* Odile almost laughed. Three centuries of divine terror, and the instruction was bookkeeping.

"We are what you made us," she said.

"No," Ashen said, and for a moment the symmetrical face was young, and sad, and grateful beyond all measure. "We are what you *forgave*. Write carefully."

* * *

He left. The candle burned lower. Tovi had gone home to the corner office with its fashionable sky, and the counting house settled into the particular silence of a building that has survived its own burning and knows exactly how lucky that is.

Odile sat alone with the ledger, and did the thing she did every night now, the ritual that had replaced the old one.

Once, in another life she could not remember, she had ended each audit by walking the seventh floor, past the shelf where a jar sat labeled in her own handwriting, and touching the glass, and feeling the terrible comfort of the secret — the way a splinter comforts the flesh that grew around it, proof of something, evidence of depth. She had believed the secret made her real. She had built a self the way a pearl builds around a wound, layer upon patient layer of invented childhood, invented mother, invented reasons, and she had called the ache of it *identity* and been proud of the ache.

Now she opened the ledger to the last written page and read backward, entry by entry, all the way to the covenant on the first page, and then — this was the ritual — she turned past the covenant to the blank pages that followed it.

They waited there, dozens of them, white as snowfall, humming softly with everything that had not yet been given.

Her own page was in there somewhere. She had started it, tentatively, weeks ago, in the manner the charter prescribed: *Gifts of the Keeper.* So far it held only three entries, small ones, true ones.

*A memory of rain on the counting-house roof, given to the well, because it was beautiful and beauty hoarded spoils.*

*A memory of Tovi's face the day she became partner, kept — not hoarded, kept, there is a difference, the charter says so — to be returned to her on some gray morning when she needs proof she is loved.*

*A memory of a mother I never had, released. It was never mine. It was never anyone's. Let the water have the shape of her, so that some other orphan, someday, might dream of a kitchen.*

There would be more. That was the discovery that had remade her, the one no god had taught her and no ledger had predicted: that a self built on a secret is a prison with excellent acoustics, but a self built on *gifts* is a garden — it does not hide its root; it spends them. She was writing herself now, entry by entry, in ink that could not be forged because it was given away as fast as it was made. Whatever Odile Oyelaran had been before — vessel, lock, thief, keeper — whatever the dead god had hidden in her bloodline, whatever false childhood had been stitched over the wound — all of it had gone into the well, and the well sang, and what remained was hers. Chosen. Amended, as Tovi would say, like a document. Blank pages, filling.

Outside, the city murmured its evening murmur: the Salt Stair fishermen arguing about tides that now occasionally arrived with messages in them; the Weavers' Quarter children playing a game called *well-whisper* that consisted entirely of telling each other secrets and then, solemnly, forgiving them; somewhere, faint and unmistakable, Maren Sault's husband still singing a song he had forgotten he knew.

Odile trimmed the candle, dipped her pen, and began tomorrow's page in advance, leaving the entry lines empty, ready to receive.

Then, because the night was quiet and the habit was old and the old habits deserved gentler work now, she performed the audit. Down the columns, line by line, thumb on every entry, checking for fraud, for smuggling, for the small dishonesties that compound like interest — checking, above all, for anything unlabeled, anything hidden, anything sitting off the books in a dark corner wearing her own handwriting.

Every gift accounted for. Every weight recorded. Every page open to whoever asked.

She blew out the candle, and in the dark she signed the closing of the day into the empty air, the way Tovi had taught her, the way deaf hands say a thing so it cannot be misheard:

*Nothing hidden. Nothing owed. All of it given.*

And the well, three streets away and flowing through everything, sang the audit back to her — balanced.
