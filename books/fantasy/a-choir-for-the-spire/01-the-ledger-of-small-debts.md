## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The Guild house at Vellum Street kept its own hours, and none of them were kind. By the time the last lamp in the common room guttered out — Master Corvane's, always first, because Master Corvane believed sleep was a debt owed to no one — Ivo Montrose was still awake at his desk on the third floor, carving a dead woman's knucklebone by candlelight.

He had been at it for two hours. The bone was small, no bigger than a hazelnut, yellowed with age and a little soft at the joints where sixty years of living had worn it down. It had come to the Guild that morning in a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, sent by a granddaughter who wanted, per the enclosed note, "to know if Grandmother said anything before she went." The granddaughter had paid the standard fee: three crowns, plus one for expedience.

Ivo set his burin against the bone and made the first cut.

It is difficult to explain what happens next to anyone who has not felt it. The bone did not speak, exactly. Speaking implies a voice, and what came through was more like weather — a pressure behind Ivo's eyes, a warmth in his fingertips, and then, faint as a song through a wall, a woman's presence settling into the room like someone taking off her coat.

*Oh,* she said. *Oh, it's dark in here.*

"They all say that," Ivo murmured. He kept carving. Small, precise cuts along the grain, following the pattern every bone-carver learned and only three ever mastered: the lattice that let the dead lean their weight against the living world without falling through it.

*Are you the one? The one they send things to?*

"I'm the one."

*I expected someone taller.*

Ivo smiled despite himself. He was not tall. He was thirty-one years old, narrow-shouldered, with ink-stained fingers and a face that people tended to describe as "unremarkable" to his actual face, which he found honest of them. He lived alone in a room above the ledgers, ate alone at a corner table, and had not been touched by another human being in — he did the arithmetic and decided against finishing it.

"What's your name?" he asked the bone.

*Marta. Marta Ellery-Voss.* A pause, and then, with the particular indignation of the recently dead: *Ellery-Voss. Not just Voss. My husband's family can write whatever they like on the tombstone, but I was an Ellery for twenty-two years before I was ever a Voss, and I will be one after, thank you very much.*

"Noted," Ivo said, and wrote it in the margin of the ledger. This was, technically, against procedure. Procedure said the carver recorded the confession and nothing else — no names beyond what the client required, no opinions, no marginalia. But Ivo had come to believe that the dead deserved better bookkeeping than the living gave them, and since he kept the ledgers, the ledgers kept his beliefs.

*Now then.* Marta gathered herself, the way they always did before the true confession, like a swimmer at the edge of cold water. *There are three things. Write them properly.*

"I'm writing."

*First: the silver service under the floorboard in the pantry is not my husband's. It was my mother's, and he knows it, and he has told everyone it was his grandmother's, which is a lie, and I want it written down somewhere that it is a lie.*

Ivo wrote it down. Somewhere, presumably, a future carver would receive a knucklebone from old Voss and hear him insist otherwise, and the two entries would sit in the archive disagreeing with each other forever. There was something almost comforting about that. The ledgers as a long argument between the dead, going on past everyone involved.

*Second:* and here her voice wavered, the way voices do when they pass over something sharp, *the letter in my writing desk, the one addressed to Tomas Greenfield of Harrow Lane — he was not my cousin. Whatever the family says. He was not my cousin.*

"Shall I say what he was?"

*No.* A pause. *Yes. No. Write that he was loved. That's enough. That's the whole of it, really. He was loved, and I never said so while I had a mouth that worked, and now I have this instead.* The bone warmed slightly in Ivo's palm, which was either her gratitude or her embarrassment; after eleven years of this work, he still couldn't tell the difference.

"And third?"

Marta Ellery-Voss laughed. It was a good laugh, unguarded, the laugh of a woman who had clearly spent a lifetime being told to quiet down and had recently stopped listening.

*Third: tell my granddaughter Bess that the jam in the blue crock is not spoiled. Her grandfather says it's spoiled because he wants the crock. It is plum, and it is excellent, and I made it myself in September, and there is nothing wrong with it whatsoever.*

Ivo put down his pen and pressed his fingers briefly to his eyes.

"You understand," he said, "that I am a licensed carver of the Guild of Impossible Things, sworn keeper of its ledgers, and that my fee schedule covers confessions of guilt, love, hidden treasure, and unresolved murder. Jam is outside my jurisdiction."

*Then expand your jurisdiction,* said Marta, with the serene authority of the dead, *or I shall haunt this bone until you do.*

"You're already haunting the bone. That's how this works."

*Then we understand each other.*

He wrote it down. Plum jam, blue crock, not spoiled, grandfather covetous. And beneath it, because he was tired and no one would ever read it, he added: *She seems like she was good company. Someone should have listened to her sooner.*

That was the trouble with the work, he thought, as he finished the last cut and sealed the lattice with a drop of wax. Everyone assumed the hard part was hearing the dead. It wasn't. The dead were easy. The dead were grateful for any attention at all. The hard part was the living — the clients who handed over their grandmothers' bones the way you'd hand over a coat to be brushed, who never asked what the dead sounded like, who took their receipt and left. Eleven years, four hundred and six confessions, and Ivo could count on one hand the number of times anyone had asked him, *What were they like?*

Nobody asked. He was a tool, the way the ledger was a tool, the way the burin was a tool. Tools didn't get asked questions. Tools got oiled occasionally — Master Corvane's idea of oil being a nod in the corridor and an extra ration of wine at midwinter — and otherwise they were expected to function.

*You're lonely,* observed Marta, from the bone in his hand.

"That's not in the confession."

*I've been dead nine days. I have perspective. You're lonely, and you talk to us more kindly than anyone talked to me in forty years of marriage, and I'd wager you don't charge enough.*

"The fee schedule is fixed by the Guild."

*Then the Guild is a fool.* She sighed, a sound like wind moving through a house at night. *Well. I suppose you'll put me away now.*

"In the archive. Shelf forty-one, V section. You'll be near a man named Aldous Crane who talks exclusively about his horses. I apologize in advance."

*Ha! Good.* And then, softer, as the wax cooled and her presence began to thin: *Thank you, carver. For writing it all down. Even the jam.*

"Especially the jam."

And she was gone, the way they always went — not dramatically, but like a lamp turned low, until the room was just a room again, and Ivo was just a man alone in it with a cooling candle and four hundred and seven entries.

* * *

He carried the bone down to the archive himself, because he always did. The other carvers had theirs delivered by the vault boys, but Ivo liked the walk: three flights down, past the common room where tomorrow's commissions were pinned to a corkboard like fish on a line, past the door of Master Corvane's office (dark, mercifully), down into the archive proper, where the air smelled of wax and dust and the particular mineral patience of ten thousand small bones waiting their turn to speak.

Shelf forty-one. V section. He slotted Marta in beside Aldous Crane and stood there a moment longer than necessary, listening to the silence of the shelves.

It was not, strictly speaking, empty silence. Ivo had long ago stopped mentioning this to anyone, but the archive hummed. Faintly. Like a hive seen through a wall. Four hundred and six carved bones, each one holding the residue of a voice, and together they made a sound below sound, a murmur at the bottom of hearing. On bad nights it felt like being watched by everyone he'd ever failed. On good nights — nights like this — it felt like company.

"Talking to the inventory again, Montrose?"

He turned. Tamsin Vale leaned in the archive doorway, arms crossed, looking as though she'd come directly from somewhere better and resented every minute since. She was the Guild's runner and occasional smuggler-of-last-resort, and she was the closest thing Ivo had to a friend, which was a sentence that said more about his life than he liked examined in daylight.

"I was shelving."

"You were lingering. There's a difference. Lingering is what people do when the shelves are better company than the colleagues." She pushed off the doorframe. "Corvane wants you at dawn. Big commission coming in — some noble house upriver, whole box of ancestors, pays double if it's done discreetly."

"Discreetly meaning the ancestors say embarrassing things and he doesn't want them entered in the main ledger."

"Meaning exactly that. Congratulations, you're our discretion." Tamsin paused at the stairs, glancing back. "There's supper left. Bread and that grey soup Odile makes. She saved you some even though you ignored her twice today, which I mention purely to make you feel guilty."

"It worked. Tell her I'm coming."

Tamsin went up. Ivo lingered — there was that word again — one moment more among the shelves, one breath of the humming dark, and then climbed toward the smell of soup and the small, insufficient warmth of the Guild's idea of family.

Outside, the weather had turned. He noticed it at the window of the common room while Odile ladled soup and scolded him gently for skipping meals: rain coming in off the river, hard and sideways, the kind of storm that bent trees and emptied streets. The lamps along Vellum Street swung on their brackets like men losing their footing.

"Ugly night," Odile said, following his gaze. "Nobody sane will be out in that."

"Mm," said Ivo, and ate his soup, and thought of shelf forty-one, and of the fee schedule, and of how many crowns stood between him and nothing at all.

The knock came at the Guild door close to midnight, when the storm was at its worst and every soul in the house was abed. Three knocks, small ones — a child's knocks, if a child's fists could make themselves heard over that wind. Ivo was the only one who heard them, lying awake as he always did, listening to the archive hum through three floors of stone.

By the time he got the door open, drenched and half-frozen on the step, mud to the waist and eyes too old for her face, the child was already speaking — a language Ivo had never heard, older than the ledgers, older than the Guild, older, some traitorous instinct whispered, than the kingdoms that paid the Guild's fees.

And around her neck, on a cord, hung a small carved amulet that made every bone in Ivo's body turn toward it like iron to a lodestone, humming a name he did not yet know.
