## 2. A Word Older Than Kingdoms

The storm came up the river valley an hour past midnight, and Ivo Montrose knew it before he heard it, because the dead in his workshop had begun to complain.

They always did, before weather. The knucklebone of a drowned fisherman hummed against the shelf like a struck tuning fork. A soldier's tooth — bought fair at a widow's estate sale, whatever the rumors said about bone-carvers and graveyards — chattered softly in its velvet box. Even the great elk antler mounted above his workbench, older than the Guild house itself, seemed to lean into the wind.

"Go on," Ivo told them, flexing his stiff fingers. "Grumble. See if I care. You're not the ones who have to finish a commission by dawn."

He was carving a dead woman's promise ring when the knocking started.

Not on the front door — that would have been sensible, and storms rarely brought sensible things to the Guild of Impossible Things. This was the tradesman's entrance, the low door in the cellar wall that led down to the bone-market docks, and whoever knocked did so with the patience of someone who had been knocking for a very long time and expected to knock for a very long time more.

Ivo set down his graver. The woman's ring — her last words to a daughter she'd abandoned thirty years ago, which Ivo was being paid to carve into a band of her own finger-bone so the daughter might finally hear them — sat half-finished under the lamp. *Forgive me* was done. *I kept your letters* remained.

He went down the stairs with a candle and the particular wariness of a man whose profession attracted every crank, fraud, and desperate soul in the city.

The child on the step was perhaps eight years old, mud to the eyebrows, soaked through three layers of somebody's cast-off clothing. She stood perfectly still in the rain, which fell around her as though it were falling on something else entirely — as though she were a hole cut in the night and the storm simply hadn't noticed.

"You'll want the poorhouse," Ivo said. "Two streets east. Tell them Sable sent— no, tell them nothing, just go."

The child looked at him. Then she spoke.

It was not any language Ivo knew, and Ivo knew languages the way other men knew drinking songs. He'd carved relics from a dozen nations and heard their dead mutter in all of them: the trade-tongue, high court speech, the clipped island dialects, the throat-singing of the far north. He knew what language sounded like — that rhythm of meaning moving underneath sound like a current under ice.

This had no current. It had *depths*. The sounds were simple, almost birdlike — three notes, four, rising and falling — but underneath them was something vast turning over slowly, the way a whale turns beneath a rowboat.

"I don't understand you," Ivo said honestly.

The child repeated herself. Patiently. As though she had been saying this same thing for a long time, to many people, and none of them had understood either.

Then she held out her hand.

On a cord of braided hair — human hair, Ivo noted, and old, gone brown as pond water — hung a small carved amulet. Bone or ivory or something that was neither, worn smooth as a river stone, worked with a pattern that hurt his eyes to follow: spirals inside spirals, a shape like a wing folded into a shape like a flame folded into a shape like a word.

Every instinct Ivo owned told him not to touch it. Every instinct he owned had been overruled by curiosity since roughly the age of six.

He took the amulet in his palm.

The world went silent.

Not quiet — silent, the way the bottom of a lake is silent, pressure and depth and no air at all. And then, out of that silence, voices. Not one voice. A *choir*, if a choir could be made of things that had never needed breath — vast, slow, harmonies stacked on harmonies going down further than he could listen, and threaded through all of it, unmistakable even across the gulf between living and dead:

Grief.

They were dead. Whatever these voices were, they were dead — he knew the texture of death in a voice the way a midwife knows a cry of pain from a cry of effort — and they were singing, and they were grieving, and one of them, closer than the others, older and hoarser than the rest, was saying something over and over in that same bird-simple tongue the child spoke, and Ivo realized with a lurch that he could almost —

"*Ash-keeper,*" he said, without deciding to. His mouth shaped it wrong; his tongue didn't fit the word. "You're — you're saying *ash-keeper*. Or... *ember-holder*. Something kept. Something kept burning."

The child's eyes went wide. They were ordinary brown eyes, mud-rimmed, red-rimmed, a child's eyes entirely — except that when she looked at him just then, Ivo felt like a man holding a candle at the mouth of a cavern, seeing by its light only how much dark there was.

She said something else, quick and urgent, and pressed the amulet back into her shirt, and the choir cut off like a door closing, and the rain came rushing back in, loud and ordinary, and Ivo stood on his own doorstep shaking like a man who'd touched a lightning rod during a storm.

"Well," he said faintly. "Come in out of the wet, then."

* * *

Getting her past the common room required strategy, misdirection, and a stolen meat pie, in that order. The Guild house at night was a den of half-drunk relic-traders and oath-brokers playing cards by tallow light, and Ivo had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being unremarkable among them. He wrapped the child in his own dry coat, which made her look like a bundle of laundry that had learned to walk, and steered her up the back stairs toward the scriptorium, where the ledgers lived and where, at this hour, only Odile Voss would be awake.

Odile was awake. Odile was always awake; the Guild's senior archivist treated sleep as a bad habit she'd given up decades ago along with patience for fools. She looked up from her ledger, took in the coat, the bundle, the small muddy face inside it, and raised one grey eyebrow with the precision of a surgeon lifting skin.

"Ivo."

"Odile."

"That is a child."

"It is possible," Ivo admitted, "that I have acquired a child."

"Possible." Odile set down her pen. "Ivo. We are a *trade guild*. Our charter covers relics, oaths, and debts. There is no line item for children."

"There's no line item for most of what comes through that door. Remember the man who sold us his own shadow? That got filed under 'miscellaneous ephemera.'"

"The man who sold his shadow," Odile said, "was eighteen years old and doing it for a bet. This—" she leaned forward, and Ivo watched forty years of professional detachment develop a crack straight down the middle "—this child has been walking a long time. Look at her feet."

Ivo looked. Under the mud, the girl's boots were worn through entirely, and her feet beneath them were cracked and blistered and healed and cracked again, layer upon layer, months of walking written in skin.

"She came to my door," Ivo said quietly. "Out of every door in this city. And she was carrying this." He described the amulet as best he could without touching it again — the spiral-work, the hair cord, the way his gift had reached into it and found not a single dead voice but a *depth* of them. He watched Odile's face as he talked, and watched the crack widen.

"Dragons," she said at last, flatly. "You're telling me you heard dragons."

"I'm telling you I heard something dead, enormous, and sad, and the child speaks its language. Draw your own conclusions. You're better at conclusions than I am."

"I'm better at filing than you are. That's different." But Odile was already pulling a fresh ledger toward her, already writing — date, hour, description of acquisition — and Ivo felt something cold slide down his spine as he recognized what she was doing, because it was what she always did, because it was what the Guild *did*, because everything that crossed that threshold became, sooner or later, an entry.

"Odile. Don't."

"Don't what? Don't do my job?" Her pen paused. "Ivo. Listen to yourself. A child arrives speaking a dead language, carrying a relic that answers to your gift alone, on the same night the river fog smells of ash though nothing's burned. If we don't document her, someone worse will. At least in our ledgers she's *ours*."

"She's not an *ours*. She's a *she*."

"And what," Odile asked gently, "is the difference, in this building? In this *trade*?"

Ivo had no answer. He'd been asking himself some version of it for eleven years, ever since the Guild had bought his apprenticeship off his starving family and entered him in a ledger as an asset, value: one (1) bone-carver, gift confirmed. He belonged to the ledgers. He had made his peace with belonging to the ledgers. Looking at the child — who had fallen asleep sitting upright, mid-sentence, her hand closed around the amulet through the fabric of his coat — he found his peace had developed a hole in it exactly her size.

Which was, naturally, when Master Corvane arrived.

The Guild master never hurried anywhere; hurrying implied events could surprise him. He filled the scriptorium doorway in his grey brocade, hands folded, and regarded the sleeping child with the expression of a man appraising a shipment.

"A word, Master Montrose. Both words, actually. The watchman reports a disturbance at the water door. And Mistress Voss appears to be opening a new ledger." His eyes moved to the entry, and something in them sharpened, pleasant as a blade going into a sheath. "*One item, animate, provenance unknown.* How thorough of you, Odile."

"She's a child, Corvane," Ivo said. "Not an item."

"Everything is an item. That is what makes a guild a guild and not a charity." Corvane circled the table slowly. "A child bearing a relic of pre-Calamity workmanship, resonant with draconic echo, arriving unannounced during the first ash-fog in twenty years. Do you know what I call that, Master Montrose?"

"Impossible?" Ivo offered. "It's rather our brand."

"I call it *inventory*." Corvane smiled. "And inventory of this quality draws buyers. Very dangerous buyers. Crown buyers." He turned for the door, robes whispering. "Lock her in the east vault. Ward the amulet separately. And Montrose — if the inquisitor's people come asking, and they will, we will need our records to be *precise*. See that they are."

His footsteps faded down the stairs. The rain hammered on. The child slept on, her fist tight around the thing that sang.

Odile finished the ledger entry in silence, then looked up at Ivo with an expression he had never seen on her face before, and would spend the rest of his life trying to name.

"Ivo," she said. "Whatever you're going to do — do it before morning."
