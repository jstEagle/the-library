## 3. Inventory

The vault smelled of iron and old decisions.

Wren had decided, in the way children decide things — absolutely and without evidence — that the door would not open again. She had tested this theory twice by pushing against it until her palms ached, and the door had agreed with her both times. So she sat down on a crate of somebody's grandmothers (the Guild kept its relics in labeled boxes, and this one said *ASSORTED MATRIARCHS, UNPAID*) and waited to find out what happened to inventory.

She knew what inventory was. The tall man with the ink-stained fingers had used the word in front of her like a lock of his own. *"We'll keep her in the vault until we know what she is."* Not *who*. Wren had learned, in her short and soggy career as a person, that *what* was a very different word, and it usually came with a cage attached.

The lamp on the shelf guttered. Somewhere above, rain worked at the roof like a patient carpenter.

And then the door did open — not all the way, just enough — and the tall man came in carrying two cups of something steaming, and he looked so awkward about it, hunched under the low ceiling like a heron in a pantry, that Wren almost laughed.

He set one cup near her. He didn't touch her. He'd learned that already too; she'd bitten him earlier, gently, on principle.

"Ivo," he said, tapping his own chest. Then he tapped hers, questioningly.

Wren considered. Names were dangerous things to give away. But he had brought warm milk with honey in it, which was either kindness or a trap, and she was cold enough not to care which.

"Wren," she said. Her voice came out rusty. She hadn't spoken since the burning place, since the running, since the mud took her shoes and nearly took her too.

Ivo repeated it, turning it over. "Wren." He smiled — a small, crooked smile, like a door left ajar. "A small bird with a loud song."

She didn't understand his words. But she understood the shape of them, the way you understand music through a wall, and she understood that he had said her name kindly, and that mattered more than grammar.

Then he reached out — slowly, asking permission with his whole hand — toward the amulet at her throat.

* * *

Here is what happened when Ivo Montrose touched the carved bone at Wren's neck, told the only way it can be told: through her, because it was her memory, and memories do not belong to the people who carry them so much as they belong to whoever is brave enough to listen.

He heard fire first.

Not the friendly fire of hearths and candles, but fire with intention — fire that had been *asked for*, called up from deep stone by voices in an older tongue, and had come gladly, the way a dog comes when its name is sung. Wren remembered — no. That was the wrong word. Wren *held* a memory that was not hers, the way a cup holds water that fell as rain long before the cup was made. Spires going up like torches. A city of white towers folding into orange dark. People running with their hands full of nothing, because there was nothing left to carry.

And underneath the fire, a voice. Vast and slow and grieving, speaking the language Wren spoke, saying over and over:

*You promised. You promised, little brothers, you promised.*

Ivo pulled his hand back as if the amulet had bitten *him*. His face did something complicated — Wren watched it happen, fascinated, the way you watch weather. Grief and wonder fighting over the same ground.

"You saw it," he whispered. "You weren't there. You can't have been there. But you *saw* it."

He didn't expect an answer, which was fortunate, because Wren could not have given one he understood. Instead she did what she always did with the memory: she opened her hands and let it pour.

* * *

What Ivo received, translated now from the old tongue into the plain speech of the ledgers, went roughly like this:

There was a covenant once. This much the Guild half-knew, though it kept such knowledge in the back drawers of its back drawers, filed under *MYTHOLOGY, DISPUTED*. Dragons had sealed the borders of the human realms with their fire — not conquered them, *sealed* them, the way a wax seal closes a letter — and in exchange the humans had sworn a price. Not gold. Not land. Something dearer. The exact terms lived in the memory Wren carried, and they were written in a script of flame that hurt to look at even secondhand.

For nine hundred years the price was paid. Kings grumbled about it. Priests built churches with very high ceilings and pretended the dragons were metaphors. But the price was paid, and the seal held, and the realms knew a peace so old they mistook it for the natural order of things.

Then came a king — Wren's memory did not give his name, or perhaps the name had burned out of it — who looked at the annual cost of the covenant and did what kings do with costs. He cut it.

He sent soldiers to the Spire where the great flame lived. He sent scholars to argue it into being merely a large fire. And when argument failed, he sent men with chains and cold-iron pikes, and they put out the first flame the way you'd douse a hearth before bed.

The dragons did not rage. That was the terrible part, the part that made Ivo set down his cup because his hands had gone unsteady. They *grieved*. One by one, across the span of a generation, they folded themselves into stone, or flew past the edge of every map, or simply stopped — and the covenant, which had never been a contract so much as a conversation, lost its speakers one voice at a time.

Until the last flame guttered alone in the high dark of the Spire, waiting for someone to speak to it.

Until a child was born in the burning place, carrying the last living word of a dead pact, and ran barefoot through the mud to find anyone at all who might still be listening.

* * *

When the vision released him, Ivo sat down hard on an opposite crate, next to a box marked *TEETH, MISCELLANEOUS, PROVENANCE UNCLEAR*.

"That's why the Guild master wants you locked up," he said quietly. "Not because you're strange. Because you're *evidence*."

Wren tilted her head. She offered him, from her limited stock of human gestures, a shrug that meant *obviously*.

He laughed — a startled, rusty sound, as if laughter were another relic he'd forgotten how to handle. "Right. Obviously."

He looked at her then, really looked, and Wren looked back, and something passed between them that needed no translation at all. Two creatures in a locked room. One of them had spent her whole short life being a remainder, a leftover, a *what*. The other had spent his whole adult life being a tool — the man the Guild pointed at bones when it wanted to hear confessions, useful as a key and kept as carefully and loved as little.

*I belong to the ledgers,* he had told her once already, in the tone people use for wounds they've stopped dressing. Now he looked around the vault — at the matriarchs and the teeth and the shelves of unpaid debts — and Wren saw him understand, all at once, that she belonged to these boxes too. That *inventory* was simply the word the world used when it wanted to keep something without owing it anything.

She reached out and touched his hand. Deliberately. No biting this time.

Through her skin, faint as a pulse, he felt the old language moving — and this time it wasn't grief. It was a question, shaped like a small bird with a loud song:

*Will you listen? Will you keep listening?*

"Yes," Ivo said, before his caution could catch up with his mouth. "Yes, I will."

It was, though neither of them knew it yet, the first entry in a new ledger entirely.

* * *

They were interrupted by boots.

Not the soft, apologetic boots of Guild clerks. These were military boots, polished to a shine that suggested a man who believed the world should reflect well on him, and they came down the vault stairs with the confidence of someone who had never once been told *no* by a door.

Ivo stood so fast he knocked over the honey-milk. Wren pressed herself back among the crates of grandmothers, small and silent, doing the thing she did best, which was becoming furniture.

The man who filled the doorway wore grey — grey coat, grey gloves, grey eyes that moved across the vault the way a ledger clerk's pen moves down a column, ticking things off. Everything in the room became, visibly, slightly less real to itself. Even the lamplight seemed to dim out of professional courtesy.

"Montrose," the man said. His voice was pleasant the way frost is pretty. "Still playing with the inventory, I see."

"Inquisitor Halvard." Ivo's spine had gone straight and his voice had gone flat, and Wren, watching from her crate, recognized both postures: they were the postures of prey deciding to be very still. "This wing of the house is Guild property. Crown writs don't reach past that door."

"Crown writs," Halvard said mildly, producing a document sealed in red, "reach wherever the crown says they reach. Which lately is everywhere." He stepped into the vault. His gaze slid across the boxes, the shelves, the spilled milk — and stopped on Wren's hiding place exactly as if she had been standing in the middle of the floor waving.

"The Guild master wrote to us," he continued, drifting closer, "about an anomaly. A child found in the mud, speaking a dead language. He was quite eager to be rid of the problem. The crown, you'll be pleased to know, shares his enthusiasm." He smiled. It did not involve the rest of his face. "Dead languages are my specialty, Montrose. I collect them. Hand her over."

"She's not here," Ivo said.

"No?" Halvard looked at the crate. At the small muddy shoes just visible beneath it. At Wren, who was looking back at him with the fierce, hopeless defiance of a sparrow facing a cat the size of a cathedral.

"No," said Ivo Montrose, bone-carver, keeper of ledgers, liar for the first time in his careful life.

Halvard sighed, the way one sighs at a slow student, and drew off one grey glove finger by finger.

"Well then," he said. "Let's see what your bones have to say about that."
