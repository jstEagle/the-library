## 4. The Price of Survival

The Guild of Impossible Things kept three kinds of ledgers, and Ivo Montrose had spent eleven years learning that the third kind was the only one that mattered.

The first ledger recorded what came in: relics, oaths, teeth, promises, the occasional saint's finger that turned out to be a pig's toe with good provenance paperwork. The second recorded what went out. The third — the Ledger of Small Debts, bound in grey calfskin and chained to Master Corvane's desk like a guilty dog — recorded what was *owed*. Not in coin. Coin was simple. The third ledger recorded the other currency: favors, secrets, silences, the small treasons everyone committed and everyone pretended not to see.

Ivo had heard, through a dead archivist's thumb-bone he'd carved two winters past, exactly what happened to the last person who'd tried to amend the third ledger without authorization. The archivist's ghost had been very thorough on the subject, mostly because his hands had been the first part of him taken. Hanging was the official penalty. The Guild, being traditionalists, preferred to keep the body available for carving afterward. Waste nothing; that was the motto under the seal.

So it was with a steady hand that did not match his heartbeat at all that Ivo sat at the long table in the scriptorium at half past midnight, holding a pen knife over an entry he had made himself, three days ago, in his own careful hand.

*Item 4471. One (1) child, female, approx. eight years, mute, recovered from doorstep during storm of St. Vellum's Eve. Speaking artifact: amulet, bone, unknown provenance. Classification: anomaly. Disposition: vault, pending assessment.*

Pending assessment. That was the phrase doing all the work, and it was about to stop working entirely, because Inquisitor Halvard had sent word ahead by courier hawk, and the courier hawk's message had been read aloud in the common room that evening with the particular relish guildsmen reserved for bad news about someone else.

*His Excellency arrives at dawn. He will inspect all anomalies. He will require the ledgers.*

"He will require the child," Ivo said quietly, to no one, because Wren wasn't there — she was in the vault, where Master Corvane had ordered her locked as inventory — and because saying it louder would have made it true in a way he couldn't survive.

He looked down at the entry. Ink dried fast in the scriptorium's heat. There were ways around ink, though. Every carver knew that bone remembered, but paper only pretended to. He had learned erasure from Odile years ago, back when she'd taught him everything else — how to hold a knife, how to listen to the dead, how to drink the cheap wine at Midwinter without grimacing. She'd called the lesson "unwriting," and she'd demonstrated it on a receipt for a love potion some hapless journeyman had tried to return.

"You don't scrape," she'd told him then. "Scraping shouts. Anyone can read a scrape from across the room. You *dissolve*. Ox-gall and a wet brush, worked slow, until the ink forgets itself. Then you wait. Then you write something so boring on top that God himself wouldn't look twice."

Ivo fetched the ox-gall from the mending chest. He cut a brush down to three hairs. And then, because he was Ivo Montrose and had never once in his life done anything halfway, he thought about what boring thing to write in place of a child.

A mislabeled entry. The safest lie is a small administrative one. His knife hovered, and his mind did what it always did when his hands were nervous — it wandered into the bones.

He hadn't touched Wren's amulet since the night she arrived. He'd been avoiding it, actually, the way you avoid a door you know leads somewhere deep water does. But fragments stayed with him anyway, lodged like splinters: fire seen through eyes too large for any skull he knew, a spire going up like a struck match, voices agreeing to something enormous and then — silence, the specific silence of a promise dying mid-word. She wasn't just mute. She was what remained after a whole conversation between species had burned down to one witness.

And Halvard wanted her on a table in the Bastion under glass, or worse, wanted her *provenance* — wanted to prove the covenant dead beyond resurrection so no one would ever go looking for embers.

Ivo pressed the wet brush to the page.

The gall bit into the ink. He worked in small circles, breath held, the lamp flame steady, his own reflection doubled faintly in the polished wood beside the ledger like a second, more honest clerk watching him commit the crime. *Dissolve*, Odile had said. The letters softened. *4471* became a grey bloom. *Child* became weather. *Anomaly* became nothing at all, which felt briefly, terribly appropriate.

Then he waited, counting his heartbeats because he had nothing better to count and they came quickly. At forty he took up the pen and wrote, in his own hand, in his own style, the dullest sentence ever composed by man:

*Item 4471. Misentry, corrected. Storm-damage inventory, cellar B: twelve (12) candlesticks, pewter, water-stained. No further note required.*

No further note required. He almost laughed. Somewhere below them, locked behind iron, a girl older than kingdoms was currently worth less paperwork than candlesticks.

He blotted the page. He closed the ledger. His hands, now that they were permitted to shake, shook thoroughly, and he let them, alone in the lamplight, feeling the specific vertigo of a man who has stepped off a ledge and discovered the ground is much farther away than it looked.

"You're doing it wrong."

Odile stood in the doorway with two cups and no expression whatsoever, which from Odile meant she was either furious or delighted, and he genuinely could not tell which.

"The correction," she said, crossing the room. "You wrote 'no further note required.' Nobody writes that unless there's a further note required. It's like a sign saying *nothing to hide here*. You've hung a lantern on it."

"I hung a—"

"A lantern." She set the cups down. "On your own crime." She pulled out the chair opposite, sat, and studied him across the ledger the way she used to study his first clumsy carvings, back when his gift was raw and his hands bled. She was sixty-some years old, built like a dock crane, with silver in her braids and a scar across her knuckles from a relic that had bitten her in 4-something-or-other. She had taught him everything. She had also, he understood suddenly and completely, been waiting up for this.

"How long have you known?" he asked.

"Since the child arrived and you started sleeping four hours instead of six and checking the vault door twice a night like a new father." She pushed one cup toward him. Wine. The cheap Midwinter vintage. "Also since you asked Tamsin Vetch last month whether her smuggling schedule ran south in winter. You're a terrible conspirator, Ivo. You're a wonderful carver and a terrible conspirator. Fortunately for you, conspiracies need a wonderful carver."

"Odile—"

"Don't." She held up the scarred hand. "Don't thank me yet and don't confess to me yet. Both are dangerous. Listen instead." She leaned forward. "Halvard will ask for the ledgers at dawn. He'll bring clerks. Guild clerks work fast, but crown clerks work faster and sloppier, and sloppy readers miss things. Your candlesticks will hold — barely, if we help them. So here is what we do." 

What they did, in the small hours, was bookkeeping.

Odile unpicked three stitches in the ledger's binding and shifted a folio so the corrected page fell in the shadow of a longer, duller run of cellar inventories. She coached him through adding two more corrections nearby — a mislabeled reliquary, a transposed date — harmless errors that made his forgery look like habit rather than desperation. She showed him how crown clerks read: top-left corner, summary column, never the body of the page unless summoned to it. "We are not hiding the child," she said, brushing dust from her fingers. "We are hiding the *idea* of her. Ideas need paper to live. Starve the paper."

It was nearly three in the morning when the work was done and the ledger re-stitched and returned to its chain, looking exactly as tedious as it had ever looked, which Ivo now understood to be its greatest defense.

They sat with their wine. Odile drank hers slowly, looking at the banked hearth.

"When I was young," she said, "I unwrote an entry too. A boy the Guild sold north to a collector who liked young bones. I erased him, walked him out the coal gate, and gave him to a river family upcountry." She turned the cup. "Corvane found out within the year. He didn't hang me. Do you know why?"

"Because you're terrifying?"

"Because the Guild doesn't punish debtors, Ivo. It *collects*. Everything you take from the ledger, the ledger takes back, with interest, at a time of its choosing. I have spent thirty years paying mine off in small installments. Tonight you've opened an account." She looked at him then, level and warm and sorrowful all at once, the way only Odile could manage. "I'm covering your margin. Understand that. But someday — maybe soon, maybe years from now, maybe at the worst possible moment — the Guild will present its bill. And you'll pay it. We always do."

Ivo thought about the vault, and the small figure inside it who spoke a language made of fire and grief, who had fallen asleep that evening with her forehead against the iron bars while he sat on the wrong side and read her the shipping manifests aloud in a soothing voice, because it was the only thing he had to offer and because, absurdly, she seemed to find them funny.

"It'll be worth it," he said.

"That," said Odile, draining her cup, "is what makes it a debt and not a theft. Worth it means you'd do it again. Debts collect best from people who'd do it again." She rose, cracked her back like a ship changing tack, and paused at the doorway. "Sleep if you can. Dawn's coming, and with it a man who smells of cold iron and clean paper."

She was right about dawn, and she was right about the smell.

Halvard arrived as the light went grey, ahead of schedule, with six clerks and twelve soldiers, and the first thing his sharp eyes found — before the ledgers, before the vault, before anything — was the empty perch where the courier hawk should have been, and the scrap of parchment beneath it that had fluttered down in the night:

*The child is awake,* it read, in Wren's careful copied letters, the first words anyone at the Guild had ever gotten her to write in the common tongue. *She says the fire remembers your name.*
