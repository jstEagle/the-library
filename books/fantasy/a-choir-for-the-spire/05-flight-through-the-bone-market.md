## 5. Flight Through the Bone Market

**IVO**

The knock came at the third bell, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict.

Three blows, evenly spaced, delivered with the flat of a gauntlet. Ivo had heard that particular percussion exactly once before, ten years ago, when the Guild of Impossible Things had been audited by the Crown's Office of Inquiry and two carvers had left in chains. He was at his workbench, Odile's shawl over his shoulders because she'd draped it there an hour ago without a word and he hadn't dared remove it, and Wren was asleep in the ledger room with her head pillowed on the *Inventory of Unresolved Debts*.

He touched the knucklebone at his ear — habit, comfort, the dead woman's confession still lodged there like a splinter — and heard nothing from below except boots. Many boots. The Guild house had never held many boots.

"Odile," he whispered.

"I know." She was already moving, already pulling the false panel off the back of the ledger shelf with the ease of a woman who had hidden things from kings for forty years. "Take the girl. Take the amulet. Take nothing else — do you hear me, Ivo? Nothing else. Grief is heavy and you'll want to carry all of it."

"Odile—"

"The east stair," she said, as if he hadn't spoken. "Down through the reliquary, out past the tithe-room. Tamsin Vael keeps a boat at Coalstep Stairs; tell her the favor is called due. Tell her—" and here the old woman smiled, quick and crooked, the smile she saved for ledgers she intended to cook, "—tell her Odile says the debt has interest."

Below, the front doors went in with a sound like a rib cracking.

Ivo woke Wren with a hand over her mouth before she could make a sound, which was its own small cruelty, because the child had spent her whole life unable to speak and here he was taking even her breath. But her eyes understood. Children who have burned before know what smoke means.

They went down the east stair in the dark, Ivo counting steps by touch, Wren's fist knotted in his sleeve. Behind them, above them, the Guild house filled with noise — Halvard's voice, smooth as poured wax, saying *the anomaly, only the anomaly, harm no one who cooperates* — and then Odile's voice, dry as August, rising to meet it.

He could not make out the words. He would spend the rest of his life not making out the words.

**WREN**

*(as Ivo translated it later, sitting in the bottom of a boat, her hands shaping the shapes of it)*

There were too many shoes. That was the first thing. The world of the Guild-house had been mostly slippers and soft boots and the master's ridiculous velvet things, and now the halls were full of hard shoes, and hard shoes meant men who did not live there, and men who did not live there meant the fire-remembering was close again.

The old woman stood on the stairs like a door. Wren knew doors. Doors were what you put between yourself and the burning. The old woman put herself there and said words in the shoe-language, slow words, and the man with the white gloves said words back that were smooth like water over stones, and Wren understood none of it but understood all of it, because some sentences are the same in every tongue: *give me the thing, or I will take more than the thing.*

Then the old woman did something with her hand — a gesture, sharp, downward, the same gesture the bone-man made when he closed a book — and the men moved, and the old woman moved, and there was a sound Wren had heard once before, in the memory that lived behind her eyes where the spires burned.

She did not see it. She was being carried down dark stairs, pressed against the bone-man's shoulder, his heart going fast under her ear. But the bone in his pocket heard it. The bone always heard. And the bone told her, in the way bones tell things, wordless and total:

*The door has fallen.*

* * *

**IVO**

He felt it through the knucklebone the way you feel a blow to your own body — a white shock, then cold, then a terrible quieting, as if a room full of voices had all stopped talking at once.

Odile.

He was three steps from the coal cellar when the bone spoke, and he stopped, and Wren, feeling him stop, looked up at him with those eyes that had seen spires burn, and he shook his head because there was nothing else in him to give. No time. No tears yet. Tears were a luxury item and the Guild had just repossessed everything he owned.

Later, he promised the bone silently. Later, when she is safe, I will sit with you and hear every last word of how she died, and I will carve it into marble, and it will be true, and it will be the only true thing left of her.

The bone said nothing. Bones are patient. It was one of the first lessons of his gift, and the cruelest.

The reliquary passage let out behind the tithe-room, and the tithe-room let out onto Coalstep Stairs, and Coalstep Stairs stank of fish and tar and the particular despair of the river district at four in the morning. Fog lay on the water like something drowned. And tied at the bottom step, riding the black water with the insolent grace of a knife floating in a washbasin, was a longboat with a lantern at its prow turned low, and a woman leaning against the mast eating an apple.

Tamsin Vael. Smuggler, blasphemer, and — though she would deny it under torture and had, once, cheerfully — the closest thing Ivo Montrose had ever had to a friend who wasn't dead.

"You're late," she said.

"Odile says the debt has interest," Ivo said.

Tamsin stopped chewing. For a moment her face did something complicated and then put itself away, the way sailors' faces do. "Interest," she repeated. "On what?"

"On a favor from nine years ago. On a coffin she paid for that was never used. On—" His voice cracked, traitor, and Tamsin looked at him, and then at the child, and then up the stairs toward the sounds of the raid, and her face finished its arithmetic.

"They got her." Not a question.

"She stayed behind as a door."

"That stupid, magnificent old fraud." Tamsin threw the apple core into the river with unnecessary violence. "Get in the boat. Both of you. Down, keep the child's head down, we're going through the Bone Market and the Market does not love the Guild."

"Why are you helping us?" Wren asked — no, Wren couldn't ask; but she looked at Tamsin with a question in her whole body, and Ivo, translating without thinking, found himself answering aloud for both of them, which was becoming a habit he didn't examine.

"Because nine years ago a bone-carver falsified a manifest for me," Tamsin said, pushing off from the stairs, "and swore on his own name that a crate of 'relics' was actually medicine, and when the harbor-master opened it anyway, the crate *was* medicine, because Ivo Montrose had spent three nights carving fever-charms into the lid until the lie became true. The Guild calls that impossible. I call it the only reason my crew survived the grey fever." She snapped the tiller around. "So yes, carver. The debt has interest. Sit down and hold the child."

* * *

**WREN**

*(translated)*

The water-world moved beneath them, black and breathing. Wren had never been on water. In the memory-behind-her-eyes there had been no water, only stone and fire and the great falling of wings, and so the river frightened her in a way that was almost pleasant — a new thing to fear, after so many old ones.

The bone-woman — the loud one, the one whose mouth moved constantly — steered them into a place where the buildings leaned together overhead like conspirators. Lanterns hung everywhere, green and red, and under the lanterns were stalls, and under the stalls were—

Bones. All kinds. Wren felt them before she saw them, a hum like a hive, thousands of small voices murmuring their small deaths. A market of the dead, awake and gossiping. She pressed her hands over her ears even though the voices weren't in her ears, they were in the place behind her eyes, and the bone-man saw her do it and covered her hands with his own.

*I know,* he said, in the language of hands and face that needed no translation. *It's loud. Breathe.*

The bone-woman bargained with a stall-keeper while the bone-man bought cloaks, and Wren watched the market watch them. Everyone watched everyone here. A man with a scale weighed something that wriggled. A woman sold teeth by the string. And everywhere, threaded through the murmur of the dead, Wren began to hear it — faint, far off, coming from everywhere and nowhere, from the direction of the tall broken thing on the horizon that even this lawless place did not look at directly.

A word.

One word, older than kingdoms, spoken in a voice like a mountain clearing its throat.

*Come.*

She grabbed the bone-man's sleeve and pointed, and pointed, and made the shape of the word with her hands the way he had taught her to shape simple things — *food*, *water*, *danger*, *home* — and this word had no shape yet, so she invented one: both fists pressed together, then opened, like wings.

The bone-man went very still.

Then he took out his coin-purse and gave it, all of it, to the bone-woman, and said something short, and the bone-woman swore in three languages and turned the boat toward the tall broken thing, and Wren sat in the bow with the fog beading on her eyelashes and let the word pour through her like warmth.

Behind them, somewhere upstream, bells began to ring — wrong bells, alarm bells, the crown's bells — and the bone-woman said a word that Wren's memory recognized across all languages, because dragons had said it too, at the end.

But ahead of them the Spire stood against the grey sky, guttering, waiting, and inside her chest the last living word of a broken covenant turned over in its sleep and began, very quietly, to learn the names of the people carrying it home.
