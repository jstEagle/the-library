## 6. What the Dead Remember

Wren counted the people in the world now. There was Ivo, who had hands like careful birds. There was Tamsin, who smelled of tar and oranges and said *damn* in eleven different ways. And there was the horse, whose name was Soup, which Wren had decided was a very good name for anything.

Three people and one Soup. Before, there had been only the burning and then the not-burning and then the long gray walking where she forgot how to make sounds come out of her mouth because everyone who could have heard them was ash. Three people and one Soup was so many that some nights she lay awake just feeling the size of it, like a coat that was too big and warm.

They were three days out from the city now, following a river that Tamsin swore would take them to the Spire if they didn't drown first, "which is a real possibility with you two," Tamsin said, "because I've seen Ivo swim exactly once and it looked like a sack of angry cats."

"I was stabbed at the time," Ivo said.

"That's no excuse. Soup swims better than you."

Soup, hearing her name, looked up with an expression of profound indifference and went back to eating reeds.

* * *

The teaching started by accident, on the second morning, when Wren burned the breakfast.

She hadn't meant to. The fire in their little camp pit had gotten hungry — she could always feel when fire got hungry, it was like a dog leaning against your leg — and she'd leaned toward it without thinking and made the sound, the old sound, the one that meant *be patient, little brother*, and the fire had leapt up joyful and taken the porridge pot with it.

Tamsin stared at the smoking pot. Then at Wren. Then at Ivo. "Right," she said. "So we're doing *this* now. Good to know. I'll add it to the list of things I pretend are normal."

Ivo crouched beside the pit. He wasn't laughing, exactly, but his eyes did the thing they did — the thing where they got soft around the edges, like he was looking at something he wanted to carve and keep forever. "Do that again," he said quietly. "The sound. Please."

Wren hesitated. People had asked her to do the sound before. Soldiers had asked, with knives. But Ivo asked the way you ask someone to share bread.

"*Ashketh*," she said. *Be patient.*

And Ivo shuddered all over, the way he did when the bones spoke to him, and he put his hand flat against the cold ground as though steadying himself against the whole turning world.

"It's not just words," he whispered. "There's something underneath them. Something enormous." He looked up at her, and his face was doing several things at once, most of them frightened. "Wren. What am I hearing?"

She didn't know how to explain in their tongue, which was a small tongue, a bread-and-weather tongue. So she took his hand instead, turned it palm-up, and traced one finger across it while she spoke, slow, the way the last dragon-mother had once traced words on *her* skin when everything was ending:

"*Ashketh* means be-patient. But underneath, it means: *I remember you were small once, and I loved you even then.*"

Ivo made a sound like a man who has been struck gently by something very large.

* * *

That was how the lessons began. Not with ink or slate, but with Wren speaking into Ivo's skin, word by word, and each word opening like a door.

Because that was the thing about her language — the true thing, the heavy thing. It wasn't made of letters. It was made of memories. Every word carried what it had been used for, all the way down, the way a river carries every rain that ever fell into it. When she taught Ivo the word for *fire*, he didn't just learn *fire*. He learned the first time fire had ever been spoken to kindly.

He learned it that evening, sitting cross-legged by the river with his boots off and his eyes shut, and afterward he couldn't speak for a long while, and when he finally could, his voice came out cracked.

"There was a dragon," he said. "Young. She was showing a human child how to hold a coal so it wouldn't burn. That's the word. That's what *vess* means — not just 'fire.' It means *fire, held carefully, between friends.*" He opened his eyes. They were wet. "How old is that memory, Wren?"

She held up fingers until she ran out of fingers, then gave up and shrugged, which made Tamsin snort soup — actual Soup, the horse, no — snort *cider*, and say, "Wonderful. We're traveling with a library older than dirt and neither of you can tell me how old."

But Wren saw that Tamsin kept glancing north after that, toward the smudge on the horizon that was the Spire, and that her hand kept finding the little wooden charm at her throat, rubbing it smooth, smooth, smooth.

* * *

More words. More doors.

*Senneth* — *oath*. Underneath: two dragons and two kings standing in a circle of their own mixed blood, promising. Wren spoke it into Ivo's wrist and he gasped and gripped the grass and said, "I can hear them agreeing. I can hear how much they *wanted* it to work."

*Kirrith* — *price*. Underneath: the same circle, years later, and the kings' grandsons saying, *why should we pay? Fire is fire. You owe us warmth. You owe us light. We built our granaries on the promise of your breath.*

And underneath *that*: the silence of dragons deciding, together, whether to break the ones they loved rather than let themselves be broken slowly.

"The covenant broke," Ivo said slowly, "because humans stopped paying. They treated the flame like a well — something you draw from because it's *there*." His jaw tightened. "The Guild does the same thing. With relics. With the dead. Draw and draw and never give back." He laughed, but it was a bad laugh, a stone-in-the-shoe laugh. "I keep ledgers for thieves and I never once wrote down what *we* owed."

"You're writing it down now," Tamsin said, nodding at his hands, which had been scratching shapes in the dirt all evening — the words, over and over, learning them the way he carved bone: patiently, lovingly, twice if the first attempt wobbled.

"Yes," Ivo said. "Better late than foreclosed upon."

* * *

It rained on the fourth night, a proper drumming rain, and they huddled under the wagon's tilt-board with Soup steaming contentedly beside them like a large kettle. Wren sat between the two of them, which had become her place, the way a keel is the middle of a boat.

"Tamsin," Ivo said, in the careful voice he used when he'd heard something in a bone that he wasn't sure he should repeat aloud. "You never talk about where you're from. But you knew the road to the Spire before I showed you the map. You knew it before I finished drawing it."

Tamsin was quiet long enough that the rain filled the whole silence up.

"Born in its shadow," she said at last. "Little town called Emberfall, half a day downhill. Whole town existed to serve the Spire — charcoal-burners, ore-haulers, shrine-keepers. My mother fed the pilgrims. My father hauled slag." She turned her cider cup around and around. "When I was six, the crown sent men to close the mountain roads. Said the Spire was unstable. Said the flame was dying anyway, nothing left worth guarding, better to wall it off than waste soldiers." Her mouth did something sharp. "What they actually meant was: nothing left worth *taxing*."

"They cut the towns off," Ivo said softly.

"They cut the towns off. And the flame — " Tamsin stopped. Started again. "My grandmother remembered when the flame still answered prayers. Actual answers, mind, not the echo-nonsense the priests sold. By the time I was born it was down to a flicker. But on certain nights, if the wind was right, the whole valley would hum. Low, like a held note. All of us kids thought it was the mountain singing. We'd lie on the roofs and hum back." She looked at Wren, and her rough face went unguarded, young. "Was that — was that real? Was somebody listening?"

Wren reached out and took Tamsin's calloused hand, the way she took Ivo's, and she gave her the word she'd been saving, the one that hurt too much to teach first:

"*Lorrenneth*." *Listen-back.*

Underneath: dragons on a dying mountain, hearing human children humming on distant roofs, and singing low so the children would know the world still held them.

Underneath *that*: the dragons' grief that soon they would not be able to answer anymore, and their promise — *the last of us will keep trying until the last ember*.

Tamsin cried. She tried very hard to hide it behind gruffness — "Rain," she said. "In my eyes. Shut up, both of you" — but Wren leaned her head against Tamsin's shoulder and stayed there, and eventually Tamsin's arm came around her, heavy and careful, and stayed too.

"Well," Ivo said thickly, staring hard at the fire. "This is either a family or a conspiracy."

"In this economy," said Tamsin, "same thing."

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Wren sat up and let herself remember the rest.

Because the lessons were teaching Ivo the past, but Wren had never told anyone the last part. How the breaking happened. How the humans' demands had grown teeth — armies at the mountain's foot, inquisitors with instruments she still dreamed about, kings demanding fire for war, fire for forges, fire for punishment, *fire, more fire, you owe us, you promised* — and how the dragons, who could not break their own oath-word, had chosen instead to pour almost all of themselves into one final working. A sealing. A covenant folded small enough to survive.

Small enough to fit inside a single living word.

Inside a child born in the last hour, when the spires burned and the great voices went out one by one, each death spending itself into her like coins into a purse. She was not the heir. She was the remainder — the change left over when everything else was spent.

She had never said this aloud. It sat in her chest like swallowed coals.

But tonight, watching Ivo sleep — his hands even in sleep curled slightly, as if still carving, still writing down debts — she felt the coals shift.

Tomorrow, maybe. Tomorrow she would trace it onto his skin and let him carry some of the weight. He seemed to grow stronger under her words instead of weaker, which the dead had told her once was possible, though rare: *some humans can hold grief without being hollowed by it. Find those. They are the only vessels worth pouring into.*

Across the dead fire, Tamsin muttered in her sleep — something about tariffs, probably — and rolled closer, and Soup sighed like a bellows.

Three people and one Soup.

And far to the north, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, Wren felt the last flame gutter — and felt, riding the wind down from the mountain, something else. Hooves. Many hooves. Moving fast along the river road behind them.

She touched Ivo's shoulder, and he woke instantly, silently, the way people wake when they have spent their lives expecting trouble.

"They're coming," she signed, hands clumsy in the dark, borrowing his own new gestures. "*Kirrith.* The price is coming."

Ivo looked north, then south, then at her — and nodded, already reaching for the ledger he'd begun keeping, the new one, the honest one, the one with their names in it.

"Then we'd better decide," he said quietly, "what we're willing to pay."
