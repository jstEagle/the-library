## 7. The Inquisitor's Bargain

The trouble with crossing a country on smuggler's roads, Ivo had learned, was that the smuggler's roads eventually crossed you.

They'd made it as far as the Ashen Vale — a gray fold of hills where nothing grew except regret and a stubborn kind of moss that Tamsin swore was edible if you boiled it twice and hated yourself thoroughly. They had been three days out of the Bone Market, two days since Odile's death had stopped ringing in the knucklebone and settled instead into something heavier that Ivo carried behind his ribs. Wren had spent those days teaching him words.

*Ashen* meant both "burned" and "blessed," depending on how you held your tongue. *Home* was not a place but a direction — the way a flame leaned. And *sorry*, he had discovered to his quiet astonishment, did not exist in her language at all. The closest word translated roughly as *"I will carry this with you."*

He thought about that a great deal.

It was evening when the trap closed, and it closed politely, which was somehow worse. They came around the bend of the vale road and found their path blocked by six riders in crown livery, arranged in a neat crescent like an apology delivered at sword-point. At their center sat Halvard, the king's inquisitor, wrapped in a traveling cloak of dove-gray wool, looking for all the world like a man who had stopped to enjoy the scenery.

"Master Montrose." He inclined his head. "You look terrible."

"You look well-rested," Ivo said. "I hate that about you."

Behind him, he felt Tamsin shift her weight. She had a crossbow under her oilskin, and she had been re-oiling it every night with the tenderness other people reserved for children or lovers. Wren's small hand found his sleeve and gripped it. Through the amulet against his chest, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the dead dragons stirred.

*Small one,* they murmured. *The gray man comes again.*

"Yes," Ivo said softly. "He does."

Halvard dismounted. That was the thing people missed about him — the courtesy. In the Guild house he had always removed his boots before entering the archive. He had once returned a ledger to its shelf with the spine facing precisely outward. Men who were merely cruel were easy to hate. Halvard was *orderly*, and order, Ivo was beginning to understand, could be the most frightening thing in the world.

"I'll come directly to terms," Halvard said, walking toward them with his hands visible, palms open. "I know what you think of me. You think I want the child dead. I want to be clear: I have never wanted that."

"No," Ivo agreed. "You want her *erased*. It's tidier."

"Tidiness saves lives, Master Montrose. Covenants are contracts, and contracts are debts, and debts—" He smiled thinly. "Well. You of all people know what debts do. They collect. They compound. They burn down houses." He stopped ten paces away, close enough to speak without raising his voice, far enough to be respectful of the crossbow he absolutely knew was there. "Here is my bargain. Give me the amulet. Walk away — all three of you. The girl lives. You live. Your smuggler friend lives, though frankly she's cost the crown enough in tariffs to hang twice over."

Tamsin muttered something under her breath that Ivo chose not to translate for himself.

"And what happens to the amulet?" Ivo asked.

"It goes into the deep vaults under the Citadel. Next to the others."

"The others." Ivo's mouth went dry. "There were others."

Something flickered across Halvard's face — not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have been almost refreshing. It was more the expression of a clerk confronted with an error in a column he had already balanced. "You genuinely didn't know," he said. "The bone-carver who hears the dead didn't know there were other anchors." He laughed, short and humorless. "Of course. Why would anyone tell you? You're a tool, Montrose. Useful tools aren't told things. Tools are told *what to carve*."

The words landed harder than he expected, because Odile had said nearly the same thing once, gently, as a warning rather than a wound. *You belong to the ledgers, boy.* He had spent thirty years believing that belonging was a kind of home.

Wren tugged his sleeve. Her eyes were wide, fixed on Halvard, and her hands moved — quick, urgent shapes in the air, the language older than kingdoms spilling out of her fingers because her voice couldn't hold it.

"What is she saying?" Halvard asked.

"She says," Ivo translated, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, "*you burned the first flame on purpose.*"

The vale went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Ah," said Halvard. He looked, for a moment, almost relieved — a man finally permitted to set down a heavy bag. "Yes. Well. Since we're being direct." He clasped his hands behind his back, a lecturer before a disappointing class. "Two hundred years ago, the covenant demanded a price for fire. A living voice, freely given, renewed each generation. Do you know what that means, practically? It means every generation, some family gave up a daughter or a son. Some village lost its best singer, its loudest arguer, its most beloved fool. And in exchange, dragons kept the borders, warmed the winters, sealed the old wounds in the world." He shook his head slowly. "Noble. Expensive. Unsustainable. So the crown made a decision. Better one clean extinguishing than a slow bleeding of our children into a furnace. We put out the first flame ourselves. Deliberately. Humanely. With the last speaker's cooperation — she was promised her family would be spared."

"And?"

"And the flame took her voice as it died. Promises made to a dying fire don't survive the dying." Halvard's tone never changed; that was the horror of it. "The border-wounds reopened. The cold winters came back. But the realm survived, Master Montrose. That is what duty looks like. Not glory. Arithmetic."

Ivo looked down at Wren. She was staring at Halvard with an expression he had no translation for — until he realized he did. *I will carry this with you,* her language said, and she was carrying, visibly, the weight of two hundred years of arithmetic. Every burned spire. Every silenced speaker. All of it stacked on a child small enough to fit inside a flour barrel, because the alternative — the remainder, the leftover, the last living word — had to go somewhere.

"You call it duty," Ivo said. "I keep ledgers. I know rot when I see it written down. This isn't duty. Duty pays its debts. This is a kingdom that set fire to its own contract and then spent two centuries hunting the smoke."

For the first time, Halvard's composure cracked — just a hairline, just enough to show the heat underneath. "You sanctimonious little tradesman. You falsify books and run from your Guild and call it love, and you think that makes you better than me? I have done *terrible arithmetic* so that millions never have to do any at all. Someone has to be the one who signs the ugly page. That someone is me. It has always been me." He drew a breath, and the composure reassembled itself, brick by brick. "Last chance. The amulet. Now."

"No," Ivo said.

It was a small word. He had carved eulogies longer than it. But he felt it leave him like a splinter leaving a wound, and behind it came everything else — the vault, the false entry, Odile's hand on his shoulder, the road, the words Wren had pressed into his palms like seeds. *Sorry* did not exist in her language. There was only carrying. He was done pretending he wasn't carrying anything.

Halvard sighed. "I was afraid you'd say that. Garett — the woman has a crossbow. Take it from her. The rest of you, the carver and the child. Alive, if it can be managed. If it cannot—"

What happened next happened quickly, the way bad things do, without any respect for narrative.

One of the riders spurred forward. Tamsin's crossbow came up from under the oilskin with a speed that would have made her proud, and the bolt took the rider's shoulder and spun him from the saddle — but there were five more, and Halvard moved too, faster than a man in dove-gray wool had any right to, and his blade was already out and already angled, and it was not aimed at Ivo.

It was aimed past him. At Wren.

Ivo turned into it. It was not a decision; decisions take time, and there was none. He simply put his body between the child and the steel the way a door puts itself between a room and a storm, and he saw the blade coming, and he thought, absurdly, *Odile is going to be so insufferable about this.*

He never felt it.

What he felt was Tamsin. She hit him like a landslide with opinions, shoulder-first, and the world became mud and sky and the enormous weight of a smuggler who owed him one favor and had apparently decided to pay it back with interest. The blade that should have opened his throat opened her side instead — a long, ugly line below the ribs — and she made a sound he had only ever heard her make at tax collectors, and then they were both down in the moss and the gray grass, and the riders were closing, and—

And the amulet sang.

Not the way it usually sang — not whispers, not fragments. It *roared*. The dead dragons, two hundred years patient, two hundred years listening through their own bones, came up through the relic and out into the vale like a tide through a broken seawall. Every rider's horse screamed and bolted. Two men fell off entirely. Halvard staggered back with his hand over his ears, his perfect composure shredded at last into something human and afraid.

In the ringing silence after, Wren stood between the inquisitor and her family, small and mud-streaked and absolutely luminous with fury, and the amulet blazed at her throat like a coal refusing to die.

*Run,* the dead said, in Ivo's bones, in Tamsin's blood, in the very moss. *Run now, small ones. The Spire is calling, and it will not call twice.*

Tamsin groaned against Ivo's shoulder. "Tell me," she wheezed, "that you have a plan."

"I have a direction," Ivo said, hauling her arm across his neck. "It's up."

"Up. Wonderful. I'm bleeding out and we're going *up*."

"The Spire doesn't wait," Wren signed, and for once Ivo needed no translation at all.

Behind them, Halvard rose slowly to his feet, blood at his ear, watching them go. He did not shout orders. He did not pursue. He simply watched, with the expression of a man doing arithmetic — and Ivo understood, with a cold settling in his gut, that the inquisitor had not lost tonight.

He had learned exactly where they were going.

* * *

They climbed through the dark with Tamsin's blood marking the trail, and above them, far off through the breaking clouds, the Spire stood against the stars — and its light, for the first time in Ivo's life, guttered.

Once. Twice.

Like a candle deciding whether to stay lit.
