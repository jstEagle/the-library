## 8. Ascent to the Guttering Light

The Spire did not look like a place. It looked like a wound that had healed wrong.

Wren had seen it in the memories the amulet gave her — seen it whole, when it wore a crown of flame and the sky around it bent like water around a stone. Now it stood bare and black against a bruised dawn, and the only fire left was somewhere inside it, guttering like a candle in a dying man's hand.

*Small,* she thought, in her own tongue, which was also the mountain's tongue, which was also — now, after all these weeks — Ivo's tongue, badly pronounced but stubbornly learned. *The last word is so small.*

"You're making that face again," Tamsin said beside her, tightening the straps on her pack. "The one where you look at something enormous and decide you're disappointed in it."

"I am not disappointed," Wren said. Her voice came out rusty; she still rationed words the way other people rationed coin. "I am *accounting*. There is a difference."

"There is not," said Ivo, from behind them, "and as the Guild's former ledger-keeper, I'd know."

He was smiling when she turned, but it was the smile he wore when his hands were shaking — the one he thought fooled people because it moved his mouth correctly. Wren had spent her whole life being read by strangers who wanted things from her. She had gotten very good at reading back.

His hands were shaking badly this morning.

* * *

They climbed through the morning, up switchbacks cut into the Spire's root-mountains by hands long dead. The path was old dragon-road, wide enough for six wagons abreast once, its paving stones fitted so precisely that moss grew in seams thinner than thread. Wren walked with her palm trailing along the stones, and they remembered her touch. They murmured under her fingers: names of masons, songs about mortar, one extremely long complaint about a foreman named Brakka who apparently never once measured anything twice.

"What are they saying?" Ivo asked. He always asked. He could hear the dead through bone and relic, but the road itself was stone, and stone was outside his gift. He climbed half-deaf to the world's oldest conversation, and it clearly tormented him.

"Brakka," Wren said, "was a disgrace to his profession."

"Was he."

"He measured nothing twice. He built a wall crooked on purpose to spite his rival. He stole a chisel." She paused, savoring it. "There is a four-hundred-line poem about the chisel."

Tamsin snorted. "Four hundred lines? About a chisel?"

"The dragons were very thorough," Wren said. "It is why they lost everything. They never let a grievance go unfinished."

She meant it as a joke. It came out true instead, the way most of her truths did, and the laughter died on the path between them. Ahead, the switchbacks narrowed, and the wind coming down off the Spire's shoulder carried a smell none of them mentioned: hot metal and old smoke, the smell of a fire deciding whether to keep living.

* * *

By midday they were above the treeline, and the dead began to climb with them.

Wren felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes — the amulet warming against her chest, waking, reaching. But it wasn't calling to her. It was calling to him.

Ivo stopped at the third bend and put his hand flat against the cliff face, and Wren watched the color leave his knuckles.

"They're louder here," he said. His voice had gone careful, the way it went careful when he was holding something shut. "All of them. Not just — usually it's one bone, one voice, one debt. This is..." He laughed, short and awful. "This is every dragon who ever died on this mountain, and every human who ever swore to them, and they've been waiting, Wren. They've been waiting a very long time to be heard, and I'm the only ear for a hundred miles, and they know it."

"How loud?" Tamsin asked. Practical Tamsin, who asked *how many guards* and *how much food* and never flinched at answers.

Ivo opened his mouth. Closed it. When he spoke again, each word came out placed like a stone across a river, tested before weight went on it. "Imagine a cathedral full of people all confessing at once. Now imagine you love every one of them. Now imagine you cannot help them, and they will not stop, and they do not understand why you have stopped listening, because to them no time has passed at all."

Wren's chest hurt. She knew that sound. She had been that sound — the last word of a broken sentence, shouting into a silence that used to be an entire covenant.

"We rest," she said.

"I can walk."

"We rest." She took his wrist — gently, the way you took hold of something that might bolt — and pulled him down onto a shelf of warm stone. "You cannot pour from an empty ledger. Your Odile taught me that. Well. She shouted it at Tamsin, and I read her lips through a door, but the principle holds."

Tamsin, unpacking bread, pointed a knife at them both. "I liked it better when nobody talked."

But she sat close, shoulder to shoulder with Ivo, and passed the bread without being asked, and Wren understood — not in words, in the older language underneath words — that this was what a family looked like from the inside. Not a covenant. Not a contract with terms and prices. Just three people eating bad bread on a mountain because one of them was breaking and the other two refused to pretend otherwise.

* * *

The confession came out of her at the fourth camp, near dusk, because terror and tenderness had finally squeezed it loose.

They were pressed into a hollow below the last ridge, the Spire's summit a black tooth overhead, and through a crack in the rock Wren could see the light. Not sunlight. The last flame, deep in the Spire's heart, pulsing slow as a sleeping pulse — and each pulse weaker than the last. She counted them the way she counted everything now. Sixty breaths between beats. Then seventy. Then, tonight, ninety-one.

At ninety-one, a candle isn't guttering anymore. A candle is deciding.

"Ivo," she said. "I have to tell you something, and you will want to argue, and I need you to not argue until I finish, because if you interrupt me I will lose my courage, and my courage is very small. It fits in a thimble. Possibly a smaller thimble."

He set down the bone he'd been carving — he carved constantly now, little tokens, little anchors, keeping his hands busy so the voices stayed in the bone and out of his skull — and gave her his whole attention. He was good at that. It was, she suspected, the actual reason she loved him, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before saying the word *love* aloud yet. Some debts you paid slowly, on purpose, to make them last.

"I am not the heir," she said. "Of the covenant."

The wind filled the pause. Far below, a nightbird complained about something.

"Halvard thinks I'm the heir. The Guild thought I was inventory. Everyone who has ever looked at me has decided I am something — a weapon, a relic, a bloodline, a key. And I let them, because being *something* was safer than being nothing." Her hands found the amulet, the carved scale worn smooth by her thumb over these long weeks. "But I listened to the memories. All of them, on the road, every night while you slept. And I know how covenants end, Ivo. I know what happens to the words."

"Wren—"

"You promised not to argue."

"I'm not arguing. I'm bracing."

"Then brace harder." She made herself look at him. Made herself say it plainly, in his language, so there could be no translation softening it. "When the old speakers burned — when the crown put out the first flame and the pact broke — everything bound to the covenant burned with it. Every oath-taker. Every heir. Every word of the old tongue except the ones already carved in stone and bone. The covenant didn't leave me behind, Ivo. It *unraveled*, and I am the knot at the end of the rope. The remainder. What was left over when the sum was taken away." Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she kept going anyway, because a thimbleful of courage poured out is still all the courage there is. "You cannot reforge a chain with a knot. A knot is just a rope remembering it was once straight. That's all I am. A rope remembering."

The silence afterward was very long, and very gentle, which was somehow worse than a bad silence, because it left her nowhere to hide.

Then Ivo said, "May I argue now?"

"...Yes."

"Good. Because I've been holding it since *thimble*." He shifted closer, and the firelight from the crack in the rock moved on his face. "Do you know what I did for twenty years? I held bones and heard dead women confess, and I wrote it all down, and everyone called it a gift. Do you know what it actually was?" He tapped his own chest. "A remainder. Every person whose story ended badly, ending again in my head, every night. I was the knot at the end of a great many ropes, Wren. I know exactly what a knot is worth."

"That's different—"

"It's *precisely* the same, and I'll thank you not to insult us both by pretending otherwise." But his voice had gone soft on the edges. "A knot is where the rope holds. That's the whole point of a knot. Nobody ties a knot in the middle of a beautiful straight length of rope. You tie it at the end, where everything would otherwise come undone." He nodded at the crack in the rock, at the slow failing pulse beyond it. "That thing up there isn't looking for an heir. Heirs are born. It's looking for someone who *remembers* — and you are the only creature alive who remembers from the inside."

Wren discovered she was crying, quietly, in the undramatic way she despised. Tamsin, without opening her eyes or ceasing to pretend to sleep, handed her a rag.

"The dead agree with him, incidentally," Ivo added. "They've been very clear on the subject since the ridge. Loudly clear. I'd tell you to hush them, but honestly—" he smiled, tired and real, "—it's nice, having people in my corner for once. Even if they're all several centuries late."

* * *

They started the final climb in the dark, because the flame's pulse had stretched past two hundred breaths and none of them could bear to watch it slow any longer.

Halfway up the last pitch, Ivo stumbled.

Not a climber's stumble. Wren caught his arm and felt it through her grip — a shudder running through him like current through wire, his jaw clenched, his lips moving around words that weren't his. The amulet blazed hot against her sternum. Below them, all down the mountainside, the old road-stones had begun to sing, faint and terrible, and every bone in Ivo's pack was singing with them.

"They know," he gasped. "They know we're close, they're all — Wren, I can't — there are too many, they're coming through, they want to *help*, they want to carry us, they want their names said, they want—" His knees buckled. Tamsin caught him from the other side, swearing fluently in three languages.

And Wren, holding half a breaking man against the black rock of the Spire, felt the truth settle over her cold and complete:

The flame was not the only thing on this mountain running out of time.

Above them, through the summit arch — ancient, carved with dragons mid-flight, dark for three hundred years — a thin red light flickered on.

Someone was carrying a lantern down to meet them.

Footsteps followed. Unhurried. Familiar.

"No," Tamsin breathed. "No, he's *dead*, we saw the fall—"

The footsteps came closer, and Wren's hand closed around the amulet, and the last word of a broken covenant prepared, at last, to speak.
