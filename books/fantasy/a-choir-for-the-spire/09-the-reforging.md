## 9. The Reforging

The last flame of the world was smaller than a candle.

That was the first thing Ivo thought when they reached the summit of the Spire — not *how beautiful*, not *how terrible*, but simply: *it's so small*. After everything. After Odile's blood on the Guild floor and Tamsin's shoulder stitched with sail-thread in a ditch outside Vell's Crossing, after Wren's small hand in his through six days of climbing stairs that had not been climbed by living feet in two hundred years — the fire that had sealed the covenant between humankind and dragon-kind fit inside a thimble.

It guttered at the center of a bowl of black glass, and it made no heat Ivo could feel, and yet every bone in his body leaned toward it like a flower toward sun.

"It's dying," Wren said. She said it in her own tongue first, then in his, because she had been teaching him and he had been an indifferent student, and she had learned to be patient about it. "It's been dying since the day they put it out in the east. This is just what's left. The last ember of a promise nobody kept."

Ivo knelt at the rim of the glass bowl. The dead were loud up here — louder than he had ever known them, a pressure behind his eyes like a storm trying to get in. Two hundred years of dragons, all of them speaking at once through whatever thin membrane separated their silence from his hearing. He had spent his whole life listening to the dead one confession at a time, one knucklebone at a time, and now they were all talking over each other like merchants in the Bone Market on a feast day, and underneath all of it he could hear the shape of what they wanted.

They didn't want vengeance. That was the thing that surprised him. They wanted *witness*. They wanted the promise said again, out loud, by someone alive who meant it.

"Can you hear them?" Wren asked, watching his face. She always watched his face when the dead spoke; she said it was like watching someone listen to music through a wall.

"I can hear them," Ivo said. His voice came out rough. "They're very rude about taking turns."

Wren laughed — a small, startled sound, the kind of laugh that hadn't had much practice. It was the best thing he'd heard in weeks, and he told her so, and she rolled her eyes the way Odile used to, which hurt and healed in the same breath.

Then the door at the top of the stair burst open, and Halvard came through it with fire in his hand that was not the covenant's kind of fire at all.

* * *

The crown inquisitor looked worse than they did, which Ivo counted as a small victory. Six days of pursuit had stripped him down to sinew and obsession. His grey coat was torn at the hem where Tamsin's knife had caught him on the ferry, and there was a burn scar along his jaw that hadn't been there before — the flame's kind of mark, though Ivo couldn't imagine when he'd gotten close enough to earn it.

"You are a difficult man to kill, Montrose," Halvard said. Almost companionable. "And you've stolen crown property."

"She's not property." Ivo stepped between Halvard and Wren without deciding to. His body had opinions about this child that his mind was still catching up to. "She never was. That's the whole point of her. That's the whole point of *all of it*, and your masters burned it down rather than learn it."

"The covenant was a leash," Halvard said. "On us. Humans paid in fire for two hundred years — villages, harvests, children marked at birth as tribute-speakers. My predecessors ended that debt. I intend to end the interest." He raised his hand, and the false fire in it brightened. "Step aside. You're a bookkeeper. You above all people should understand: some debts are better defaulted."

Behind him, breathing hard from the stairs, Tamsin limped into the light of the little flame. Her shoulder was still bound. In her right hand she held the long gutting-knife she'd carried since the Bone Market, and her grin was the one she wore before doing something spectacularly unwise.

"Halvard," she said cheerfully. "You remember me. Ferry. Knife. Your coat."

"I remember you'll hang for it."

"Everyone keeps saying that." She rolled the knife across her knuckles. "Nobody's managed it yet."

"Tamsin—" Ivo started.

"He's mine," she said, and there was nothing cheerful under it anymore. "You go do your bookkeeping. I'll balance this entry."

What happened next happened fast, the way the worst things always do. Halvard moved — faster than a man that worn-out should move — and Tamsin met him, and the two of them went spinning past the glass bowl in a clash of steel and that ugly orange fire, and Ivo had perhaps ten seconds, maybe fifteen, alone at the edge of the last flame in the world with a child who was the remainder of a broken promise.

Wren grabbed his sleeve. "Ivo. Listen to them. What are they saying? Tell me what they're saying."

He closed his eyes and listened, really listened, letting the storm of the dead pour through him — and beneath the roar he found it, the way you find one true voice in a crowded room. An old voice. A patient one. The last dragon whose echo still clung to this place, or perhaps the sum of all of them, distilled.

And the price was exactly what he'd feared, and exactly what he'd hoped.

"It needs a voice," he said. "A living one. Freely given. Not read from a page — the words have to be *carried* by someone who means them, given over whole. That's why it broke, Wren. The old speakers gave the words but kept themselves back. They recited. Nobody ever *gave*." He opened his eyes. She was looking at him with dawning horror, because she was clever, far too clever, and she had already done the arithmetic.

"No," she said. "No. I'm the speaker. I'm the last word. It should be—"

"You're the *word*," Ivo said gently. "Not the voice. And even if you were—" he crouched to her height, the way he had on the first night, mud and rain and a locked vault, "—I would not hand a child to a fire. I'm sorry. Some things I will be selfish about."

"There has to be another way."

"There isn't. There's only the question of whether I mean it." He smiled, and found, to his genuine surprise, that the smile was real. "Wren. I have spent my whole life belonging to ledgers. To other people's debts, other people's confessions, other people's dead. Do you know what it is, to finally hold something that's *mine* to give?"

Her eyes filled. "It's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Most true things aren't." Behind them, steel rang against stone; Tamsin swore with a smuggler's full vocabulary. "Now help me. Quickly. I need bone, and I need your hands steady."

* * *

He had carried a piece of Odile with him since the Guild house — a sliver of her wrist-bone, taken before they fled, because he could not bear to leave all of her for Halvard's men to catalog. He drew it out now, white and smooth, warm from his pocket.

"What are you doing?" Wren whispered.

"Authorship," Ivo said. "The only kind that matters."

Because here was the truth he had understood kneeling at the glass: the fire would not take a recitation. But words carved in love, in a dead friend's bone, spoken aloud by the one person left who could carry them — words *given*, not merely said — that was a different ledger entry entirely. That was a debt willingly assumed. That was a signature.

He set the bone against his knee and took up his smallest knife, the one he'd used for knucklebones and confessions, and he began to carve.

His hands did not shake. Twenty years of the Guild had seen to that. He carved the old words of the covenant as Wren fed them to him syllable by syllable, her voice trembling and sure at once, and around the old words he carved new ones, his own, the ones he would not be able to say aloud soon and needed the world to keep anyway:

*Ivo Montrose, bone-carver, keeper of impossible accounts. I heard the dead my whole life and never once asked what they wanted. They wanted this. Let the record show I give it gladly: my gift, my speech, my name, my self. Let the record show I was not a tool of the ledgers. Let the record show I was loved, and knew it, at the end.*

"Wren," he said, when the last stroke was done. His throat was already tightening, the fire reaching for him through the carving like a hand through water. "When I can't speak anymore — read it. All of it. Mean it if you can. I think you can."

"Ivo—"

"And tell Tamsin she still owes me eleven crowns from Vell's Crossing, and that I forgive four of them."

She made a sound that was half laugh and half sob and threw her arms around his neck, and he held her with one arm and pressed the carved bone into her hands with the other, and across the summit Tamsin shouted — "Montrose! Whatever you're going to do, *do it*!" — and Halvard tore free of her and came toward them with the false fire rising like a tide.

Ivo stood. He faced the little flame. He opened his mouth to speak the first word himself, to start the sentence he would not finish —

— and found, with a strange calm, that his voice was already leaving. Not painfully. The way a boat leaves a dock. He got one word out — "*Witness*" — and felt the rest go quiet behind it, a door closing softly in a house he had lived in all his life.

Wren lifted the bone in both hands, held it toward the flame, and began to read.

Her voice — the last living word of a broken covenant, clear as a bell in the dark — rose over the sound of Halvard's boots, over Tamsin's cry, over the roaring of two hundred years of the dead. And the little flame stood up.

It stood up like something waking. It stood up the height of a child, then a man, then a spire, gold-white and vast, and every dragon-echo Ivo had ever heard poured into it at once, and the choir began.

Halvard stopped mid-stride. For one moment his face held not rage but naked, boyish awe — the face of a man seeing the thing he had spent his life denying — and then the light took the summit, and took the false fire from his hand like a mother taking a knife from a toddler, and the covenant reforged itself around them with a sound like every bell in every kingdom ringing once.

Ivo tried to say Wren's name.

Nothing came.

But inside the silence, for the first time in his life, he was not listening to the dead.

He was singing with them.
