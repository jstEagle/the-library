## 10. A Choir for the Spire

The flame did not roar when it took him.

That was the first thing Wren understood, watching from the edge of the old altar with her hands pressed over her mouth: fire, in all the stories the dead had given her, was supposed to *announce* itself. It was supposed to be a hunger and a trumpet blast and a wall of gold. But the last dragon-fire at the heart of the Spire took Ivo Montrose's voice the way a mother takes a sleeping child from a cart — carefully, with both hands, as if it had been waiting a very long time to be trusted with something precious.

He had knelt. That was how it began. Not grandly. He had knelt on the cracked stone the way he knelt at his workbench, sleeves pushed up, that little furrow between his brows he got when a piece of bone was being stubborn. And in his hands he held not the amulet but the rib bone he had spent the whole ascent carving, the one Tamsin had carried against her wound like a talisman because she said it made her feel "aggressively calm."

His final words were cut into it in letters small enough to fit inside a swallow's song.

*Take what I have been keeping. It was never mine.*

Wren read them aloud.

She had thought it would be hard — that her throat would close around the words of a language older than any kingdom, that speaking would feel like betrayal, since her whole life had been built out of silence. But the words came easily, easier than anything had ever come out of her, because they were not really hers. They were his. She was only the door.

The flame leaned toward him.

Not surged. *Leaned*, like a listener bending close across a table. The guttering light — which had been dying for three hundred years, which had burned down to a coal the size of a clenched fist, which had flickered so low during Halvard's attack that Wren had honestly believed they were too late — stood up straight. Every tongue of it pointed at Ivo like a compass needle finding north.

And then it drank him.

There was no scream. There was no sound at all, and that was the worst of it, and also the most beautiful. Wren watched his mouth open and nothing come out — watched the voice go out of him the way breath fogs on cold glass, visible for one impossible moment, silver and shivering in the air between his lips — and then the flame caught it, cupped it, *held* it, and everything changed.

The Spire sang.

It started low, under the floor, under the mountain, under the world — a note Wren felt in her back teeth and the roots of her hair. Then another voice joined it, higher, and another, and Wren realized with a jolt that went through her like sunrise that these were not echoes. Echoes repeat. These voices were *answering*. Dragon-voices, hundreds of them, thousands, the whole vast congregation of the dead who had been waiting inside the covenant's broken word for someone foolish and brave enough to pay its price — and every one of them was singing through the silence where Ivo's voice used to live.

Only she could hear it. She knew that even as it happened. Tamsin was shouting something — probably profanity, probably *worried* profanity — and Halvard was gone, dragged down or driven off or simply swallowed by the dark below, and none of them heard what Wren heard, because the choir was not in the air. It was in the space where a voice had been, and now only one person alive could stand in that space and listen.

*Ivo,* the choir said, in a hundred languages and none, in the language of the child and the language of the ledger and the language of fire. *Ivo, we remember you. Ivo, we accept the debt.*

And underneath, threaded through all of it, quieter than the rest and somehow the loudest: the woman whose knucklebone he had carved on a quiet night long ago, still confessing; Odile Vane, who had died buying two strangers an hour of running, saying something dry and warm that sounded suspiciously like *told you the Guild collects*; the dragons themselves, the great slow minds, turning their attention toward one small bone-carver with the gentleness of weather.

The flame set his voice down among theirs like a candle in a window.

And then — this was the part Wren would never be able to explain to anyone, ever, no matter how many languages she learned — the covenant *closed*. She felt it happen. The old broken pact, the one humans had torn open by demanding fire without price, the one her people had died holding shut with their bodies and their tongues, knit itself closed around Ivo's offered voice the way bone knits around a well-set fracture. New. Whole. Sealed.

The flame turned gold. Then white. Then it settled, calm as a hearth, no bigger than before — but steady now. Fed. Willing to keep burning.

Wren stopped reading.

The choir faded to a hum, the way a bell keeps ringing after you stop hearing it. And Ivo Montrose knelt on the altar stone of the Spire with his mouth still open on a silence that would last the rest of his life, and he looked at her, and his eyes were wet, and he was smiling.

He tapped his own chest twice. Then he pointed at her, and at Tamsin, and at the flame.

Wren didn't need words — his or hers or anyone's — to know what he meant.

*Home. All of us. Home.*

*

Coming down the Spire took three days and cost Tamsin exactly one argument about who got to walk behind whom ("I've got the sword, featherbrain, the formation is *not* negotiable"), and by the time they reached the bottom, Wren had learned eleven new ways that a person can speak without a voice.

Ivo had always been quick with his hands — carving taught you that, she supposed, taught you to make matter obey intention — but now his hands had become a whole country she got to explore. Two taps on the sternum meant *I'm here*. A flat palm drawn slowly downward meant *slow down, tell me again*. Fingers laced together and squeezed meant something he refused to demonstrate in public and blushed ferociously about whenever Tamsin guessed at it, which was constantly, because Tamsin was Tamsin.

But the strange thing — the thing that made Wren laugh until her ribs hurt, there on the road with the Spire shrinking behind them — was that losing his voice had not made Ivo quieter. He had been a quiet man when he had one. Now he was *relentless*. His hands never stopped. He narrated everything: the shape of clouds (a sweeping gesture, then a skeptical tilt of the head), the quality of roadside stew (a shudder, then a firm thumbs-down, then, after a pause, a grudging half-thumb), the number of times Tamsin had nearly gotten them killed (he kept count on his fingers, and had run out of fingers twice).

"He's worse than before," Tamsin said one evening, delighted, watching Ivo deliver what was clearly an entire lecture on the structural failures of their current inn's roof using only a bread roll and a candlestick. "I want it noted that I said the sacrifice was worth it and I stand by that."

Wren signed back — clumsily, still learning — *worth it*.

Because here was the truth she carried home in her chest like a coal: he had not lost his gift. The Guild had called it a tool, the way they'd called her inventory, and both of them had believed it, once. But the dead still found him. She saw it on the road, in villages, in the Bone Market they passed through on the way west — people pressing relics into his hands, shy and hopeful, and Ivo closing his eyes and listening with his whole face, and then answering with his hands, translating grief into gesture after gesture after gesture, patient as water.

He couldn't speak anymore. So everyone else's dead spoke *for* him, and he spoke back through his palms, and somewhere in the exchange — Wren was sure of it, sure the way she was sure of the flame — the choir never entirely stopped. It lived in his silence now. It hummed there, low, under everything, the way a bell hums.

On the last night of the road, in a rented room above a tavern that smelled of tallow and rain, Wren sat across from him at the little table while Tamsin snored in the next bed like a landslide, and she taught him the last word.

Not the last word of the language — the language went on forever, word after word after word, each one opening a door in her memory into some vast winged remembering. But the last word *she* had been carrying. The one she'd been born mute around, the one the covenant had left inside her when it burned everything else away, the remainder of the remainder.

She took his hand. Turned it palm-up. And with one finger, slowly, she wrote the word in the oldest script there was, the one that predated kingdoms and crowns and inquisitors:

*Stay.*

Then she folded his fingers closed over it, the way you fold a letter.

Ivo looked at his closed fist for a long moment. Then he opened his other hand — the one that had carved confessions into bone and debts into ledgers and, once, his own self into a flame — and wrote his answer in the air between them, plain and unmistakable, in the simple sign language they'd built together out of nothing, out of necessity, out of love:

*Always.*

Outside, far to the east, the Spire burned steady and gold against the stars, and if either of them listened — really listened, the way only the two of them could — the wind coming off the mountains carried a sound like ten thousand voices humming one held note.

A choir, keeping watch.

A covenant, kept.

Home.
