## 1. The Bone That Remembers

The widow Maren brought me her husband's femur on a Tuesday, wrapped in a flour sack like it was something to be ashamed of, which in this valley it very nearly was.

"You'll do it, then," she said. Not a question. Maren had never asked me a question in forty years of living three fields over; she issued pronouncements the way weather issues rain.

"I'll do it," I said, because I always did, and because refusing would have required an explanation, and explanations were the one thing I had spent my whole life learning not to give.

Here is what you need to understand about Coldwallow Vale, if you are unlucky enough to be born in it: nothing works here. No charms, no blessings, no hexes, no hedge-cunning. A wizard could stand at the border stone and hurl fire until his hair caught light and not so much as scorch a dandelion. The valley eats magic the way sand eats water. The founders chose it for exactly that reason, back when choosing things was fashionable, and their descendants have been smug about it ever since — smug, and safe, and dull as unglazed pottery.

Which is why what I do has to look like carpentry.

I took the femur into my workshop — a shed that smells of tallow and juniper smoke, kept scrupulously boring — and I set it on the bench and let it rest a while before touching it. Bones are patient. They have waited decades inside a body; they can wait an afternoon on a bench. But they remember everything, and memory is heavy, and you want to let them settle before you ask them to speak.

Because that is the thing about the dead in Coldwallow: magic cannot cross our border, but the dead are already inside it. Death walked in long ago and never left. And a bone, carved just so, tuned just so, is a door with no lock.

I won't tell you how the carving is done. Not because it's secret — it isn't, anyone with good knives and better patience could learn it — but because the technique is the least interesting part, like explaining that a letter is made of ink. What matters is listening. You shave away until the bone's own voice comes clear, and then you put your mouth where a mouth used to be, and you play, and the dead man says what he never got around to saying.

Old Tam Joubert had died mid-argument, eleven years back, over whether the south field should go to barley or beans. His widow wanted to know how it ended. So did he, probably.

I worked through the afternoon. Boring work, careful work, the kind of work that keeps a woman invisible: I am Isandro Joubert, bone-carver, maker of flutes and fipple-pipes and the occasional recorder for the rector, who believes I am merely eccentric rather than profane. Let him. Eccentricity is a fine coat. It hangs loose enough to hide almost anything.

By dusk the flute was done — six holes, a warm grain to it, the bone gone honey-colored under the oil. I played it once, quietly, testing the tuning, and Tam's voice came up through it thin and dry as chaff:

*—told her a hundred times, barley, it was always going to be barley—*

And then, softer, the part he'd actually carried to his grave: *—and she was right about the beans, God rot it, she was right—*

I laughed. Dead men are funnier than living ones; they've had time to edit.

Maren came at full dark, as arranged, hood up, glancing down the lane both ways like a woman buying contraband, which in a sense she was. I handed her the flute without ceremony.

"He says you were right about the beans."

She made a sound halfway between a sob and a snort. "The stubborn old mule." She clutched the flute to her chest. "How long will he — will it last?"

"As long as you keep it oiled. Years, if you're kind to it. He'll say less each winter, but he'll say it."

"Isandro." She paused in the doorway, and for a moment her face was young under all those years. "You're a good woman. Whatever else people whisper."

"People whisper I'm odd."

"They whisper worse than that." She pulled her hood up. "But they don't know you. I do. Good night."

She went off down the lane, and I stood in my doorway watching her go, feeling the particular loneliness that comes after giving someone else a conversation. My workshop held thirty-one instruments now. Thirty-one voices. On quiet nights I played them all in turn, a choir of the valley's unfinished business, and every voice belonged to someone else's grief. Mine had burned out of me so gradually I couldn't tell you when. Or perhaps it hadn't burned out at all. Perhaps I'd simply never fed it anything worth keeping.

That was the thought I was chewing when I saw the child.

* * *

She came up the cart-track from the border side, which no one does. The track runs south out of the vale toward the pass, and the pass runs toward the lowlands, and the lowlands run toward kingdoms whose names change faster than the weather. Nobody walks *into* Coldwallow. People are born here or they marry in, and both processes involve more complaining than walking.

She was small — eight, maybe nine, though hunger makes children hard to date — and she moved wrong. Not limping, not staggering. Moving like someone who had learned that being seen was expensive. She hugged the hedgerows. She stopped when a dog barked two farms over and stood absolutely still for the length of ten heartbeats, which I counted, because I am the sort of person who counts.

I should have gone inside. I want that on the record. Every sensible instinct I owned — and I had cultivated them like orchids, rare and delicate and expensive — told me to shut the door, bank the fire, and let whatever crossed the border cross it alone.

Instead I stepped off my stoop and said, "You'll want supper, then."

The child looked at me. She had ash-grey eyes and a face smudged with road dirt, and she did not answer, and after a moment I understood she wasn't being rude. Her lips moved. Nothing came out. She pressed her throat with one hand, hard, as if checking whether the machinery still worked, and then her face did something terrible and brief — a whole sentence of grief crossing it in half a second — before going smooth again.

"Mute," I said. "Or near enough. Well. I talk enough for two; most folk say three."

She followed me inside the way a cat follows you — close enough to bolt, far enough to deny association. I set out bread and dripping and the last of the stewed apples, and she ate like a small polite wolf, and I talked the whole time, about nothing, about the weather and the price of wool and the scandal of the rector's new hat, filling the silence the way you'd fill a hole in a wall, and she watched me with those grey eyes and I got the distinct impression I was being *read*. Assessed. Filed somewhere behind them.

When she finished eating, she looked around the room, and her gaze snagged on the hearth — on the cold grate, where I'd raked out the previous night's fire that morning. Fine wood-ash, pale grey, spread smooth.

She slid off her chair. She crouched at the hearthstone. And with one deliberate finger she began to draw.

Spirals. Rows of them, nested, turning inward on themselves, each one linked to the next by a spine-line running down the ash like a river through country. She drew fast, sure, the way you write your own name — not letters learned, letters *lived* — and the shape of it itched at the back of my skull, familiar as a smell from childhood.

I knew that script. I knew it the way you know a song you've only heard underwater.

Because I had read it before. Carved into ribs. Scored along a jawbone. Written by the dead of a kingdom that does not appear on any map, in any chronicle, in any living memory — a country rubbed out of the world so thoroughly that even its neighbors' histories have a smudge where it used to be. Vessarion. The name arrived in my head unbidden, in a voice like wind through a flute, and I sat down rather heavily on my own chair.

Thirty-one instruments in my workshop, and every Vessarioni bone among them spoke that script. The dead of a nation that had never existed here, in a valley where nothing crosses the border — except, apparently, its ghosts, who had been drifting in for years like leaves on a stream, carrying their spirals and their griefs and their names, which I alone in the living world could read.

"How," I said to the child, and my voice came out strange, "do you know that?"

She looked up at me. Ash on her finger. Ash-grey eyes. And slowly, deliberately, she drew one more spiral in the hearth — larger than the rest, enclosing all the others — and then pointed at it, and then pointed at herself.

*I am this*, the gesture said. *All of it. What's left.*

Outside, far down the valley toward the border stone, a dog began to bark, and then another, and then they did not stop.
