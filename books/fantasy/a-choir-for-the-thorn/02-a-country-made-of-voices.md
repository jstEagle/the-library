## 2. A Country Made of Voices

The hunters arrived on a Tuesday, which I found insulting. Catastrophe ought to have the decency to come at night, or at least in weather.

I saw them first from the ridge above my house: nine figures on the valley's northern rim, standing in the exact places where the mist goes strange and turns back anyone who means to cross. They didn't pace. They didn't confer. They simply stood, spaced like fence posts, and waited with the patience of things that do not need to eat. One of them had removed her hood, and even at that distance I could see she was smiling—not at us, not at anything, just smiling the way a knife smiles when it's being sharpened.

Magic can't cross our border. That's the valley's whole trick, its one modest miracle. Whatever runs in the blood of the world everywhere else runs dry here, like a river losing itself in sand. The dead who come to me don't arrive as ghosts; they arrive as bone that remembers, and I carve the remembering loose. It's slow work and it keeps me fed and it has kept me invisible for forty years, because no one hunts a woman who makes flutes for widows.

But the ones on the rim weren't hunting me. They were hunting what had walked into my kitchen three nights ago and drawn spirals in the ash of my hearth—the script of Vessarion, a kingdom I knew only because my bones knew it, because half my choir came from a country that no longer appears on any map, in any ledger, in any living memory except the ones I keep in a cedar box under my floor.

They couldn't enter. But they could wait. And I had flour enough for perhaps six weeks, if I was honest about it, and I am always honest about bread.

* * *

That night I took down the box.

The choir lives in my cellar in ordered rows: ribs and finger-bones and one entire jaw, each carved with the spirals that let a voice out of the past. Most of them speak the valley dialect—farmers, a ferryman, two sisters who drowned in the millrace and still argue about whose fault it was. But eleven of them are Vessarioni. Eleven bones from a country that was not conquered but *unmade*, unwritten, pulled out of the world like a thread from cloth so thoroughly that the cloth forgot it had ever been woven.

I set them in their crescent on the workbench and lit the lamp and began, as I always begin, with the oldest voice.

The femur belonged to a mason. When I drew the bow across it, his voice came up through the wood of the bench like water through stone: *Ask.*

"I'll ask," I said. "There's a child in my house. She draws your script. Tell me who she is."

A long note, low, the sound a building makes settling. Then: *She is the last. There was a daughter of the house of Aurel. The ritual took the country but could not take the blood while the blood still breathed outside the border.*

"The house of Aurel." I looked at the smallest bone in the crescent, a child's finger bone I'd never been able to coax more than a few words from. "Your princess."

*Our princess,* said the mason, with something that in a living man would have been a smile. *Princess Aurel. Nine years old when we ended. She was visiting her aunt across the mountains. The ritual did not reach that far. We thought—* the bone hummed, a broken chord— *we hoped the world would forget to look for her too.*

"And it didn't."

*It cannot afford to. An erasure is a debt, Isandro Joubert. You do not unmake a kingdom for free. The ones who paid—they must collect the interest. A survivor is a loose thread, and loose threads unravel ledgers.*

I sat with that. Outside, the wind came down off the rim and carried, faintly, the smell of the hunters' cookfire—which meant they had a cookfire, which meant they were prepared to stay until spring, until the girl starved or surrendered or I grew tired and stupid.

"Tell me how it was done," I said. "The erasure. All of it. Spare me nothing."

And the mason told me, and I will write down here only the shape of it, because the details are the kind that follow you into sleep. Nine singers—I counted the figures on the rim twice after I heard this, and yes, there were nine, and I did not enjoy being right. Nine singers standing in a ring around the capital, singing the country's name backward until the name wore thin and then wore through. Every song has a cost; that's the first law of my craft and probably the first law of everything. Their cost was memory. Each verse they sang erased the name from one mind at a time, outward in a wave, until the whole world had forgotten Vessarion—and the world, having forgotten, could no longer hold the place in existence, and the place folded up like a letter.

"But you remember," I said to the mason. "You're dead, and you remember."

*The dead keep what the living spend,* he said. *That is why your craft works at all. Everything forgotten goes somewhere, Isandro. It goes down. It goes into bone and soil and the bottoms of rivers. Your valley sits on a great deal of forgetting.*

I thought of the mist at the border. I thought of how nothing magical crosses it—and wondered, for the first time in forty years, whether the valley wasn't a place where magic failed, but a place where magic had already been spent. Drunk down by the ground like spilt wine. A country-sized debt, paid into our dirt.

"Is there a way back?" I asked. "Into Vessarion. For the girl."

Silence, long enough that I checked the bow-string. Then the mason again, slower, choosing words the way a man chooses footholds:

*There was a door. In the old palace. It opened only from inside, and only to the royal blood, and it led—elsewhere. Somewhere the nine could not follow. But a door needs a frame, and a frame needs a shape, and the shape of Vessarion exists now only in memory. Living memory, none. Dead memory—* the bone sang a small, grim arpeggio— *scattered. Incomplete. We each remember our own street, our own year, our own grief. No single one of us remembers the whole country.*

"But together?"

*Together, perhaps. If someone could gather every testimony. If someone could build the door's true shape from a thousand fragments and then sing it whole.* He paused. *And if the singer were willing to pay what such a song costs. A door that size does not open for coins, Isandro. It opens for a life's worth of remembering.*

I looked at my hands. Carver's hands, scarred, steady. Hands that had spent forty years making other people's dead speak and taking, as payment, small slivers of my own past—a face here, a winter there, the taste of a fruit I can no longer name. I had thought myself frugal. I had thought myself careful.

The mason's voice dropped almost to nothing. *One thing more. The nine on your border—they know about the door. Why do you think they wait? Not to storm you. They cannot enter. They wait because they know the girl will try for the door eventually, and they know the door needs a singer, and they mean to take the singer's payment for themselves. Or failing that—to wait until everyone in that valley who might help her has starved or abandoned her. Erasure is patient work. They are patient people.*

* * *

I climbed back up at dawn and stood in my doorway with the cold coming off the fields, and I looked north at the nine shapes on the rim, unmoved, unhurried, patient as winter.

Behind me, in the kitchen, the child was awake. I could hear her moving—careful, quiet, the movements of someone practicing being small. Three days she'd been with me and she hadn't made a sound. I'd begun to wonder if she could.

Then, through the open door, drifting out into the grey morning air where the hunters might even have heard it, I heard her voice for the first time. Thin, rusty, an instrument long unplayed. She was singing—not a song I knew, but a song with a shape I knew, a shape that matched the spirals in my ash, the spirals on my bones.

A lullaby. From a country that no longer existed, sung by the last person alive who'd been sung to in it.

I closed my eyes and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. Eleven bones, a thousand missing testimonies, a door built of pure remembering, a price measured in everything I am. Against that: one child, one lullaby, nine knives on a hill, and six weeks of flour.

The mason had said the dead keep what the living spend.

Well. I had spent a long life being careful. It seemed the account had finally come due—and the first installment, I understood, would be finding out just how much of Vessarion my little choir could hold before I asked it to hold a kingdom.
