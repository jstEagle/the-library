## 3. What the Valley Cannot Keep

*Isandro*

The winter came early that year, the way it always does when you need time most. By the second week of hiding, my larder had shrunk to a sack of barley, half a wheel of cheese going the color of old ivory, and a jar of pickled things I'd been avoiding since before the widow Maren's flute. The hunters at the valley's edge had not moved. They didn't need to. Patience is a weapon for people who can afford it, and they could.

Aurel ate like a bird and watched me eat like a wolf pretending otherwise, and one evening she pushed her bowl across the table toward mine with the solemnity of a queen surrendering territory.

"Eat," I said. "You're growing."

She shook her head. Then she opened her mouth, and out of it came a word I had only ever heard from the dead.

It was a small word — *bread*, or something near it, bread-adjacent, bread's older sister — and it cracked something open in the room. I put down my spoon. She said it again, watching my face the way I watch bone when it begins to sing: for the tremor.

"You can speak," I said. "You've been able to speak this whole time."

Another word. This one took longer, and her brow furrowed with effort, and when it arrived it was unmistakably *yes*. Then, after a pause, with the air of someone testing whether the floor would hold her weight: *no*.

"Well," I said. "Between those three we could govern a small nation."

She laughed. It was a rusty sound, a hinge unused for years, and I understood then that the silence hadn't been emptiness. It had been a locked room. And she had just decided to give me the key.

* * *

*Aurel*

The woman carved bones. I knew this the way I knew the taste of ash — from before, from the part of before that survived. In Vessarion there had been singers too, but they sang into bells of glass, and the glass remembered everything and forgave nothing. The woman here sang into bone, and bone is kinder. Bone forgets what it wants to forget.

I had not spoken since the night my country ended. Words felt dangerous, like carrying lit candles through a house full of paper. But hunger makes you honest, and the woman was hungry too and pretended not to be, which made her honest in a different way, and I found I wanted to give her something. The tongue was all I had left that was truly mine. So I began teaching her.

We started with names of things. *Water.* *Fire.* *Door* — and when I said *door* she went very still, the way animals go still, and I learned later that door was a word with weight in this valley, though I did not yet know why.

I taught her the word for *mother* on a bad night, when the wind came down off the ridge sounding almost like the choirs of home, and I cried without noise because I had forgotten how to make noise while crying, and she held my hand and repeated the word back to me until it stopped being a lesson and became a comfort. Her mother was dead too. Everyone's mother is dead eventually; this is the one grief no kingdom's erasure can improve upon.

I asked her — slowly, with my hands helping — about her father. She made a face I recognized from mirrors. Some questions you answer with your whole body before your mouth agrees to anything.

I liked her. That frightened me more than the men at the valley's edge. Things you like can be taken. Things you merely survive with cannot.

*Isandro*

The bargain arrived on the third week, delivered by a boy of perhaps sixteen who walked up the road as if he owned it, which in a sense he did, since none of us were fool enough to contest him. He carried no weapon. He didn't need one. He carried an envelope sealed with grey wax, and he stood in my doorway radiating the confidence of someone whose employers have never once lost.

"The gentlemen beyond the border," he said, "are reasonable men."

"They've erased a kingdom," I said. "Reasonable is a strong word."

"They'd prefer not to erase a valley. Erasure is expensive." He smiled. He had been taught to smile the way soldiers are taught to march. "The girl for the valley. You have until the thaw. After that, well." He looked past me, at my thin smoke, my thin fields, my thin face. "Winter does its own negotiating."

I closed the door. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Fear, I've found, lives mostly in anticipation; the thing itself is almost restful.

That night I went down to the choir.

The bone-choir hung in my cellar like a strange orchard — forty-one instruments carved from the dead of Vessarion, smuggled to me over years by rivers and peddlers and one memorable crow. When played together they spoke with the voices of the erased. I had questioned them piecemeal. Now I needed everything, and everything has a price.

I played until dawn. The testimonies braided together: the ritual had unmade Vessarion root and branch, name and grave, but erasure leaves seams, and seams can be sung open. There was a door. Not a door of wood or stone — those had been unmade — but a door of memory, standing where the memory of Vessarion stood complete. To open it required a singer who remembered the country fully. Every street. Every bell. Every name.

And there lay the cruelty of it, elegant as their wax seal: no living soul remembered Vessarion fully. Only the dead, and the dead cannot cross the border. The magicless valley kept them out as surely as it kept the hunters in.

I sat among the hanging bones in the grey light and laughed, quietly, so as not to wake the child upstairs. Because I am a liar by trade — I pass as a carver of trinkets, a quiet woman, nobody — and liars know a loophole when one bites them.

The singer didn't have to remember Vessarion.

The singer had to remember *fully*. The choir didn't care what it was fed. Memory is memory. A country is just a life with more people in it.

I had a life. Forty-one years of it, hoarded like barley against a long winter. A mother who sang off-key. A craft learned finger by finger. A valley I knew down to its stones, its gossip, its grudges, the exact blue of the sky in the month the river floods.

I could build them a country out of myself.

* * *

*Aurel*

She began acting strangely after the boy came. Strangely for her, which meant: warmly. She let me win at the game with the stones. She gave me the last of the cheese and claimed she disliked cheese, which was a lie so poor it was nearly a poem.

I had learned enough of her tongue by then to ask real questions, and one night, over the fire, I asked the one I had been carrying since the ash spirals: *Why are you doing this? You owe me nothing. Your valley owes me less.*

She thought about it. She was a person who actually thought rather than performing thought, which I had not known in anyone since my tutor, may his erased soul rest wherever erased souls go.

"When I was young," she said, "my mother died, and then everyone else who might have claimed me drifted off or died too, and I learned that being alone isn't a wound. It's a room. You furnish it or you freeze." She turned a bone whistle over in her fingers — a little one, a practice piece, shaped like a reed. "You walked into my room. Out of everywhere in the world, you walked into mine. That's not owing. That's—" She searched for the word, and I gave it to her in the old tongue, and she repeated it carefully, like a prayer she wasn't sure she was allowed.

*Sister,* I had said. Half-joking. Mostly not.

Later, when she thought I slept, I heard her downstairs among her hanging bones, talking to them softly, counting them, the way you count the people you love before a journey. I did not understand then what she was preparing. I understood only that the sound of her voice in the dark made the room feel furnished.

* * *

*Isandro*

On the last night of the month I laid it all out on the kitchen table, physically, like a merchant's goods: the plan, spoken aloud to Aurel in her own tongue, badly pronounced, because if I was going to spend my entire self I was going to do it in the language of the person I was spending it for.

The door needed a full memory. Mine would do. The song would take it — all of it, mother, craft, valley, self — the way a fire takes a letter. She would walk through whole. The hunters would scatter when their quarry's country flickered back into being beyond their reach, a kingdom resurrected around its heir like armor.

"And you?" she asked. Her accent in my tongue was better than mine in hers now; children are thieves of language. "*You* will be—"

"A stranger," I said. "To myself. To this place. I'll wake up with empty pockets and a full set of hands. People will tell me who I was. Apparently I'm well-liked." I shrugged, aiming for lightness and hearing it land somewhere nearer to terror. "Worst case, I'll be very good at carving and have no idea why."

She was crying again, silently, and then not silently, and then she said the word she'd taught me first, the one for *mother*, and I corrected her gently — *not that one, little queen* — and she shook her head and said it again, stubborn as winter, insisting, and I found I could not argue, because love is the only argument that wins by losing every point.

So it was settled, the way terrible things settle: quietly, at a kitchen table, over the last of the barley.

Tomorrow we would walk to the border stone. Tomorrow I would play a country into being out of the only materials I had ever truly owned.

Tonight, I sharpened my knives and memorized the color of the sky.
