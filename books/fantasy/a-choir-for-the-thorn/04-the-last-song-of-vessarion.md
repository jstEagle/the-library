## 4. The Last Song of Vessarion

The border stone was older than the valley's oldest grave, which is saying something in a place where even the rocks seemed to have opinions about death. It stood at the eastern pass like a bad tooth—grey, stubborn, and impossible to ignore once you'd noticed it. I had walked past it ten thousand times and never once thought of it as a door.

Doors, after all, require two sides.

Aurel stood beside me in the pre-dawn dark, wearing every coat I owned layered over her small frame, so that she looked less like a princess and more like a laundry pile that had learned to walk. She had insisted on carrying the choir herself. All nine-and-forty instruments, wrapped in sacking and bound with rope, clacking softly against her back with each step like a bag of impatient knuckles.

"You could let me carry some," I said.

She shook her head. In Vessarion's tongue she said something long and formal, and though my grasp of the language had grown from nothing to nearly-something over these hungry weeks, I caught only the shape of it: *the singer does not carry; the singer is carried by the song.*

"Convenient," I said. "I'll remember that excuse next time there's firewood."

Her mouth did the thing it did when she almost laughed—the thing that had undone me entirely, somewhere around the third week, when I had understood that I would burn down anything that came for this child, including, as it turned out, myself.

The hunters were visible on the far ridge. Seven of them, or perhaps seven shapes arranged hunter-wise, patient as winter, exactly as my dead had described them. They had waited out the valley's magiclessness for months. They had offered their bargain—*the girl, and we go*—and the valley elders had spent three terrible nights not quite deciding. Aurel had heard them through the floorboards. Children always hear through floorboards. It is one of their many useless talents.

So here we were, then. My plan, such as it was, fit inside a flute case: a door exists only in memory, therefore a memory can be made into a door, therefore a woman who has spent her life teaching bones to speak might teach one last bone to open.

It sounded better in my head. Most things do.

* * *

We reached the stone as the sky went the colour of a bruise healing. Aurel set down the choir and unwrapped each instrument with the reverence of someone handling relics, which, I suppose, they were—femurs and ribs and one small finger-bone whistle, all of them carved from the dead of Vessarion, all of them holding what the ritual had tried to erase.

I had built the set-piece over six weeks of nights, working from testimony the way other people work from sheet music. A miller who remembered the smell of his own flour. A queen's seamstress who remembered the exact blue of a coronation gown. A soldier who remembered nothing but a gate, and loved the gate anyway. Bone by bone, song by song, I had assembled a country out of secondhand grief.

And now I would have to make it whole. Because testimony, my dead had told me, is mortar—but the keystone must be living memory. Someone who remembered Vessarion fully, completely, without gaps. And the only person left alive who had ever seen it entire was seven years old, and I was not about to take anything from her. I had done the arithmetic in the dark weeks ago and hated the answer, and then, being me, had gone ahead and fallen in love anyway, which made the arithmetic worse.

"Singer," Aurel said, and touched my sleeve. She spoke slowly, formally, the way she did when the words mattered: *"Whatever you give, it will be enough. Whatever you keep, it will be enough."*

"Liar," I said fondly, and picked up the first instrument.

* * *

My mother went first.

Not her death—I kept that, selfishly, the way you keep a splinter because removing it hurts more than leaving it. No, I gave the song her hands. The way she kneaded bread with her thumbs hooked inward. The way she hummed off-key and dared anyone to mention it. The way she said my name—not *Isandro*, but the private diminutive, the one nobody else ever used, the one that existed nowhere now except in my skull and, briefly, in the throat of a carved rib.

The note rose and hung above the border stone like frost forming. The air beyond the stone shivered.

Then my craft. That one cost more than I'd budgeted. Twenty years of apprenticeship, of ruined blanks, of learning how a femur wants to be opened—it poured out of me and into the choir, and I felt it go the way you feel blood leave a cut finger, warm and then cold. I still knew *that* I had been a carver. I no longer knew *how*. My hands were suddenly just hands. Clever strangers attached to my wrists.

"That's fine," I told myself, out loud, because lying to yourself works best when you commit to the performance.

Aurel watched me with wide eyes. "Stop," she said in valley speech—one of her new words, hard-won, precious. "You can stop."

"I can't," I said, which was true, and also "I won't," which was truer. "Keep singing, Your Highness. You're the echo. I'm the shout."

* * *

The valley itself was the worst of it, and I saved it for last, the way you save the worst of it.

I fed the song the road up from the lowlands. The smell of the tanneries in August. The bell that rang wrong and everyone pretended rang right. Widow Maren's kitchen, where I had delivered her husband's femur-flute and drunk tea while she played him back to himself. The particular grey of the morning fog. The way the word *home* had felt in my mouth, which is a thing I had never noticed until I felt it leave.

Each memory went out of me like a lamp going out in a house I was still standing in. Not pain, exactly. Worse: absence discovering its own shape. I knew I was losing things. I could no longer say what they were. I was a woman standing in an emptying room, watching furniture vanish, unable to remember what any of it had been *for*, knowing only that the room had been warm.

The air above the border stone cracked.

Not broke—cracked, the way ice cracks before it gives, a white line splitting the dawn, and through it came a sound I had never heard from any of my instruments: a chord made of everything at once. Flour-smell and gate-iron and coronation-blue and rain on Vessarion tiles, a whole kingdom exhaling after years underwater.

Beyond the stone, the world changed.

Grass grew where grass had never been. A road unrolled itself from the crack like a tongue. Towers assembled themselves out of light and insistence, and a gate—that gate, the soldier's gate—swung open on hinges that had waited a very long time. The erased kingdom flickered back into being, half-transparent, then less so, then real, realer than the ridge behind it, and the seven shapes on the far ridge scattered like crows off a carcass, because you cannot hunt an heir into a country that exists again. Erasure only works on things that stay erased.

Aurel turned to me. Her face was wet. So was mine, though I couldn't have told you when that started.

"Come," she said. Valley speech, then Vessarion, then just her hand, outstretched. "Come *with*."

And oh, I considered it. Let the record show I considered it, standing at the threshold of a country I had built out of my own self, with my name already going strange in my mouth.

But the door was sized to one heir, and I had built it that way, and besides—someone had to stay and be the proof. Someone had to remain in the valley as the living evidence that Vessarion had existed, that its music had passed through human hands. A country needs witnesses on both sides of its walls.

"Go," I said. "Grow. Be insufferable about it."

She threw herself at me, a laundry pile with arms, and held on, and I held on back, memorizing nothing, because there was nothing left in me to memorize with—and then she stepped through, and the door closed behind her soft as a book shutting, and the towers faded to a shimmer, to a rumour, to a story that only I would carry.

Except I wouldn't. That was rather the point.

* * *

I woke in Maren's kitchen.

I knew it was Maren's kitchen the way you know a stranger's face from a description—told, not remembered. There was tea in front of me. There were people around the table calling me friend, and their kindness was genuine and their names slid off me like rain off oilcloth.

"You gave us a fright," the widow said—Maren, apparently; the others used the name like a handle I could grip. "Three days asleep. Do you know your name, love?"

I looked down at my hands. Clever strangers. They flexed when I asked them to, which seemed promising.

"No," I said honestly. Then, because some part of me evidently survived the spending—a part too stubborn to file under any category—"But I think it starts with I."

Maren smiled and squeezed my hand. "Well. That's a start. We'll teach you the rest."

Outside, the valley went on being the valley, indifferent and green, and far to the east—though I had no reason to know east from west, and didn't—something faintly shimmered at the horizon, like heat, like hope, like a door left very slightly ajar.

I picked up the teacup. I taught myself its name.
