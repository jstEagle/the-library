## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The dying woman's house smelled of rosemary and beeswax and the particular sweetness of a life almost finished — like fruit left too long in a bowl. Casper Ishikawa stood in the doorway of the bedroom with her hood down and her hands bare, because hands were the whole of her trade and they worked best unhidden.

Maren Tull lay propped against three pillows, small as a bird in the great featherbed she'd occupied for sixty years. Her eyes were open. They had been open for days now, fixed on the ceiling beams where swallows had nested every summer of her marriage, and Casper knew without asking that behind those eyes was a memory Maren wanted gone before the end came.

"You're the thief," Maren said. Not an accusation. Almost a welcome.

"I'm a collector," Casper said. "Thieves take things people want to keep."

"And what I want to keep, you'll be taking anyway." The old woman's laugh was dry as seed husks. "Sit, girl. My son says you'll do it gentle. He says you did his neighbor's grief last autumn and the man slept like a child after."

Casper sat on the edge of the bed. The mattress dipped; dust motes turned in the light from the window, gold and slow, and somewhere below a kettle was beginning its low complaint. This was the part of the work no one paid for — the sitting, the listening, the small ceremony of it. She had learned from Odell that the taking was only half the craft. The other half was making sure what remained behind was livable.

"Tell me which one," Casper said.

"The day at the weir." Maren's voice went soft and far away, the way voices did when they traveled inward. "Midsummer, forty-one years ago. Tam was alive. The girls were small enough to carry. We ate plums on the stones until our hands were sticky, and Tam said — he said *this is the day I'd choose if I could only keep one.*" Her throat worked. "I've kept it too long. It's all I have left of him, and it's eating me alive, child. Every hour I lie here I'm back on those stones and he's laughing and then he's not, he's forty years dead, and I have to die it all over again every morning."

Casper nodded. She had heard this shape of story before, many times: joy grown heavy from carrying. In the valley of Vell, where magic pooled against the border stones like rain against a roof and could not follow anyone past them, there were no priests who could cut sorrow out cleanly, no charms to soften remembering. There was only Casper, and her hands, and the strange inheritance Odell had found her worthy of.

"Plums," Casper said. "Sticky hands. Your husband laughing. Anything else I should leave you? A piece of it? Most people like to keep the smell."

Maren considered. "Keep the plums," she said at last. "I'd like to remember that fruit existed."

* * *

The taking itself took less time than the talking.

Casper drew off her gloves entirely — she wore them only for walking through town, so that no accidental brush of skin would cost a stranger their wedding day or their mother's face — and laid two fingers against Maren's wrist, over the thin drumbeat of her pulse.

Memory lived close to the surface in the dying. That was a mercy of the work. In the young and healthy, memories burrowed deep, wrapped in years of newer days, and Casper had to go digging through strata of living to reach them. But Maren's happiest day floated near the top like a leaf on still water, worn smooth from handling.

Casper closed her eyes and *pulled*.

It came away the way ripe fruit leaves the branch — with a small, sweet resistance, then release. It poured up through her fingertips and into her, and for a moment Casper stood inside another woman's midsummer: sun hammering the flat stones of the weir, water talking over the sluice-gate, plum juice running down a child's brown arm, and Tam — young Tam, whole Tam, alive Tam — tipping his head back to laugh at the sky, saying *this is the day I'd choose if I could only keep one.*

Casper held it carefully. She always held them carefully. Each stolen memory was a live coal in her keeping, and she had learned early that carelessness burned.

Then she sealed it, the way Odell had taught her: folding the edges of the thing inward, pressing warmth around it until it hardened into something like glass, something that could sit in a person's chest without leaking. She opened her eyes.

Maren was blinking at the ceiling. Her expression was not grief. It was the peculiar blankness of a page with one line erased — the ink gone, but the faint groove of the writing still there, so that anyone reading knew *something* had been written and could not say what.

"Plums," Maren murmured. "I remember plums."

"That's yours to keep," Casper said. "Free of charge."

She pressed her palm once more to the old woman's wrist — lightly, a benediction rather than a taking — and felt the hollow where the memory had been. Smooth. Livable. Maren would sleep now, Casper thought. She would sleep and wake and sleep again, and the day at the weir would trouble her no more than a story someone else had told her long ago.

Downstairs, Maren's son waited by the cold hearth with bread wrapped in cloth and a look on his face that couldn't decide between gratitude and shame. He set the loaf in Casper's arms without meeting her eyes. People never met her eyes after. It was easier to hand a memory-thief bread than thanks.

"Will it hurt her?" he asked. "The forgetting?"

"No," Casper said. "That's the trouble with it. Nothing hurts except for everyone around the empty place."

He didn't understand her. Few did. She tucked the loaf under her arm and let herself out into the blue end of the afternoon.

* * *

Vell in the evening was a bowl of woodsmoke and lamplight, terraced houses climbing the valley walls like barnacles on a ship's hull, every window a small amber eye. Casper walked the crooked main street with her hood up and her gloves on, nodding to neighbors who nodded back — some warmly, some with the careful blankness of people who suspected, correctly, that she might know things about them they'd chosen to lose.

They called her the Quiet Hand behind her back. To her face they called her nothing at all, mostly. A memory-thief was a necessary shame in Vell, like the night-soil man or the debt collector. Everyone needed one eventually. No one invited one to supper.

Casper didn't mind. Or she told herself she didn't mind, which in the valley passed for the same thing.

Her rooms were above the cooper's shop at the street's dead end, two chambers stacked like loaves, and the upper one was where she kept her collection. Not in jars — Odell had broken her of that superstition early. Memories lived fine in the body; they were weightless, odorless, invisible. But Casper kept a ledger, because a ledger made the weightless feel real. Three hundred and eleven entries in her own careful hand, each one a name, a date, a description in as few words as honesty allowed.

*Entry 311: Maren Tull, aged 79. Midsummer at the weir, 41 years past. Husband living, daughters small, plums. Taken freely, gladly. Paid: one loaf, seeded.*

Three hundred and eleven. She ran her thumb down the column sometimes, the way a miser might count coins, and felt the strangest arithmetic of her life: she owned more happiness than anyone in the valley, and none of it was hers.

That was the trade's quiet cruelty, the part Odell never spoke of and she never asked about. When Casper took a memory, she carried it — truly carried it, could walk through it at will, could stand in Tam Tull's laughter whenever the valley fog got into her bones. But carrying was not owning. The memories sat in her like birds in a cage that wasn't quite theirs, singing songs about other people's skies. And her own life — her own before — was a locked room even to herself. She had come to Odell at six years old with no past she could name, and whatever had been written on her before that page was gone, taken or lost or never given, and no thief's hands could steal it back because there was nothing left to steal from.

She owned hundreds of days. She owned none of her own.

Outside, far off at the valley's mouth, the border stones were humming.

Everyone in Vell could hear them if they listened — a note below hearing, more felt than heard, like the moment before a storm when your teeth ache. The stones ringed the valley's single mouth, twelve of them, older than any record, taller than two men, and they did one thing only: they stopped magic at the line. Inside, the valley was ordinary as bread. Outside — well, outside was where the world went strange, where the crown's sorcerers worked their weather and their wars, where gifts like Casper's came from in the first place. Odell said the founders had raised the stones to keep the strangeness out. Some nights, walking home past the last terrace, Casper thought the stones sounded less like a wall and more like a lid. Like something being kept *in*.

Tonight the hum seemed louder than usual. Thicker. She paused at her window with the ledger open on her knees and listened, and the note went through the glass and through her ribs and settled somewhere under her heart like a stone dropped in a well.

Then boots on the stair — quick ones, Odell's, though Odell hadn't climbed these stairs at this hour in years — and the door swung open before she could call out.

Her mentor filled the doorway, gray-bearded, sharp-eyed, breathing hard as if he'd run the length of the terraces. Sixty years old and he moved like a man half that, except tonight. Tonight he looked like a man who had heard something at the stones.

"Pack nothing," Odell said. "Answer nothing. And Casper—" His voice dropped, and beneath the urgency she caught something she had never once heard in it in all her seventeen years: fear. "There's a herald coming up the valley road. Royal colors. He'll read a proclamation at the well tomorrow, and when you hear what it says, whatever you do — don't be standing in the square."
