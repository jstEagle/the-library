## 2. Proclamation at the Well

The herald came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays in Vell were for mending nets and arguing about the price of flour, and the town had a rhythm to it that Casper Ishikawa knew the way she knew the backs of her own hands — better, actually, since her hands spent most of their time learning other people's lives.

She was at the well because everyone was at the well. The square smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke and the particular sourness of a crowd that has been told to gather and resents it. Above them all, on the steps of the tithe-house, stood a man in royal blue so deep it looked like a hole cut in the morning.

He unrolled a scroll with a snap that carried.

"By decree of His Majesty Aldric, King of Vell and the Marches Beyond," he read, and his voice had been trained to fill cathedrals, so that even the pigeons flinched from the rafters, "let it be known throughout the valley: there are among you those who bear gifts. Gifts of sight. Gifts of shaping. Gifts of taking." A pause, measured like coin. "These persons are commanded to present themselves to the crown's officers within one turning of the moon. Those who surrender will be treated with the mercy owed to subjects of the realm."

He let the word *mercy* hang there, gilded and hollow as a festival lantern.

"Those who do not surrender will be taken."

Casper stood near the back with her hood up and her heart doing something quiet and terrible against her ribs. Around her, the crowd murmured — not afraid yet, not quite. Vell was a valley that forgot things; forgetting was practically its trade. She could feel the proclamation already beginning to slide off the town like rain off oiled wool. By supper half of them would be arguing about whether the king's men would really come this far upriver, and by market day it would be a story someone's cousin had heard secondhand.

But Casper did not forget. That was the joke of her life, the cruel little punchline she'd never earned the right to laugh at: the thief who kept everything. Hundreds of memories sat folded inside her like letters in a chest — a fisherman's first storm, a bride's terror walking down the aisle, an old man's last afternoon watching his orchard bloom. None of them hers. All of them hers now, in the way that anything you carry long enough becomes part of your weight.

And every single one of them was evidence.

*Gifts of taking.* The herald hadn't needed to say it twice. She'd heard him fine the first time.

She slipped away before the crowd broke, moving along the shaded side of the chandlery where the stones stayed cool underfoot, and made for Odell's house at the crook of the millrace. Her boots knew the way without her. That was another thing about stolen memories — they taught you other people's shortcuts, other people's habits, until sometimes she wasn't sure which footsteps were originally her own.

Odell was in his garden pretending to prune roses, which meant he already knew. He always knew before she did; it was his oldest trick and she'd never caught him performing it.

"You heard," she said.

"I heard." He didn't look up. His shears clicked through a stem that didn't need cutting. Odell was sixty-some years of weathered oak and careful hands, gray at the temples, gray in the eyes too, a grayness she'd once assumed was age and had lately begun to suspect was something else entirely. "Come inside. Standing in doorways is how people get remembered."

Inside smelled of tea and lamp oil and the faint mineral tang of the border stones, which clung to everything he owned the way river smell clings to a boatman. He poured two cups without asking and set hers down, and only then did he look at her, and his face was calm in a way that frightened her more than shouting would have.

"They'll come tonight," he said. "Not the herald's men. Hunters. They travel light and ask questions that sound like small talk."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've seen a hunt before." He said it simply, the way a man says *I've seen winter before*, and drank his tea.

Casper wrapped both hands around her cup. "What do I do?"

"What you always do." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, and it was warm, and it was perfect, and some cold sliver of her noticed how perfect it was. "You're a girl who lost her parents young and was raised by a fussy old bookbinder who taught her the trade. You know nothing of gifts. You've never stolen so much as an apple. If they ask about gaps — about people waking with holes in their lives — you'll be as puzzled as anyone. Grieved, even. It's a strange thing happening to good neighbors."

"And you?"

"I'll be the fussy old bookbinder." Another smile. Click went the shears, somewhere in memory. "Go home, Casper. Light your stove. Be boring. Boring is armor."

She went. But halfway down the lane she stopped, because the border stones were humming.

They always hummed, faintly, a note below hearing that lived in the teeth rather than the ears — the valley's constant, the sound she'd slept beside her whole life. Magic couldn't cross them. Nothing gifted could leave Vell, and nothing wild could enter. She'd been told as a child that the stones were protection. She'd never asked protection from what.

Tonight the hum was different. Thinner. Like a wire drawn taut.

Like something on the far side, listening.

* * *

They came at the hour when lamps gutter low and honest towns are asleep, three riders whose horses made no more noise than snowfall. Casper heard them anyway — she'd learned to sleep with one ear open years ago, back when she'd first begun taking memories and feared, absurdly, that the dead might come looking for theirs.

She watched from the gap in her shutters as they dismounted in the square. Two were ordinary enough: hard-faced, road-worn, swords worn low. The third was wrong in a way her body understood before her mind did. Tall, still, with a face like a door left slightly ajar — nothing visible behind it, but the sense of a draft coming through. He stood in the empty square and turned his head slowly, like a man tasting air.

Then they knocked on doors. Not hers. Not yet.

Through the walls she heard the ritual repeat itself, house to house. Polite voices. Questions shaped like kindness. *Strange season, isn't it? Anyone in town been ill lately? Anyone forgetful?* And the neighbors, yawning, answering honestly, because what did any of it mean to a netmender or a baker? *Old Marta wakes some mornings not knowing her daughter's name. Poor thing. Old age.*

A thief whose victims wake with gaps in their lives. That was what they called her, out there beyond the stones. Not a name. A symptom.

When the knock finally came at her own door, her heart had already rehearsed this moment so many times that it barely stumbled. She lit the lamp, smoothed her nightdress, and opened the door on the tall one and one of the hard-faced men, lamplight pooling between them like a moat.

"Forgive the hour," said the tall one. His voice was soft and reasonable and completely empty, a voice like an unfurnished room. "We're asking after a practitioner. Someone who takes. Memories, specifically. There may be folk here who've lost pieces of themselves without knowing why."

"Oh," Casper said, and let her brow crease, and it wasn't even acting anymore because the fear underneath was real enough to furnish any expression. "That's — I'm sorry, that sounds dreadful. There's old Marta, down by the tannery, she has her bad mornings. But that's just age, surely. My guardian could tell you more — Odell, the bookbinder, he knows everyone's history in this town. He's forgotten more than most folks ever learn."

The tall one regarded her. For a moment — one long, cold moment — she felt something move across her mind like a hand passing over a shelf, checking for dust. Checking for gaps. She thought of the hundreds of memories sleeping inside her, pressed flat and silent, and willed them to be still, to be nothing, to be a girl's ordinary night.

"Hm," he said. "Your guardian. We'll speak with him."

"He'll be asleep—"

"It's no trouble." The almost-smile he offered was the politest threat she'd ever been shown. "Sleep well, mistress Ishikawa."

Her name. He hadn't asked her name.

She closed the door and stood with her back against it, listening to her own blood, and only after their horses' silence had fully returned did she allow herself to understand what she already knew: they hadn't come to search. They'd come to confirm. Somewhere, someone had written her name in a ledger, and tonight had merely been the ink drying.

* * *

She ran to Odell's through the blue-black hour before dawn, breath smoking, and found him awake, of course, sitting in his chair by the dead fire with a lamp turned low, as if he'd been waiting for exactly this knock at exactly this time.

"They spoke to me last," he said, before she could speak. "Or rather, the tall one did. The others just watched him work." He poured her tea though her hands were shaking too badly to hold it steady. "He asked whether anyone in town had ever shown signs of gift. I told him no. I told him Vell was the emptiest valley in the kingdom — that whatever magic used to live here died out generations ago, and good riddance, and wouldn't the king's men be better spent in the lowlands where the real trouble breeds."

"And he believed you?"

"He wanted to believe me." Odell set down his cup. In the low light his face was all planes and shadow, older than it had been that morning, much older than it had been yesterday. "Belief is easy when you hand it to a man pre-shaped. He'd ridden days to find nothing. Nobody wants to ride back having found something."

"That's it?" Casper whispered. "It's over?"

"No." The word fell like a stone into still water. "It's not over, child. Men like that don't close a ledger on a maybe. They'll leave a watcher behind, or they'll come again in autumn wearing different faces. Which means we have work to do, and not much time to do it."

"What kind of work?"

He was quiet a moment. The lamp flame leaned, righted itself. When he answered, his voice had changed — lower, wearier, carrying something she'd never heard in it before, something that sounded almost like guilt.

"There are things in my cellar you should have seen years ago. Things I swore I'd never show you unless the day came that showing them was kinder than hiding them." He rose, joints cracking, and took the lamp from the table. "I think, Casper, that today is that day. Come downstairs. And bring your courage — you're going to want both hands free."

The cellar door opened onto darkness, and the darkness breathed out cold, and somewhere beneath the smell of paper and glue and forty years of Odell's careful life, Casper caught another scent. Older. Sealed. The smell of glass, and beneath the glass, faint as a heartbeat, the hum of the border stones —

but wrong, somehow. Deeper. As if one of the memories down there in the dark had been waiting a very long time to be found.

As if it had been humming her name.
