## 3. What Odell Kept

The cellar door had always been locked.

That was the thing about Odell — he locked nothing else. Not the bread box, not the coin tin, not the drawer where he kept his razors and his little silver instruments for trimming wicks and cleaning ears. In thirty years of living above his shop, Casper had never once seen him turn a key on anything but that door, and even then it was less a lock than a habit, a small brass click at night like a man buttoning his collar before bed. She'd heard it a thousand times from her cot in the loft. Click. Goodnight, girl. Sleep.

Tonight she heard it and waited until his footsteps crossed the floorboards overhead, paused at the washbasin, crossed again, and settled into the particular creak-and-silence of a man lying down to pretend he was asleep. Then she went down the back stairs on bare feet, with the tallow candle cupped in one hand like a bird she didn't want to startle.

The proclamation had changed everything. *All gifted persons must surrender themselves or be taken.* The herald's voice still hung in the town square like smoke that wouldn't clear, and all day Casper had watched her neighbors look at each other sideways — the miller's son who could quiet dogs with a hum, old Marta who read fortunes in spilled flour — everyone suddenly counting who among them might be counted. And the hunters had come at dusk asking after a thief whose victims woke with gaps in their lives, and Odell had lied for her so smoothly, so *warmly*, that she'd believed him right up until the moment she caught his eyes over the hunters' shoulders: not afraid. Calculating. A man doing arithmetic behind his face.

Odell did not do arithmetic behind his face. Odell wept at funerals for people he hadn't liked. If he was calculating now, then there was something in this house worth calculating about, and everything worth hiding lived behind that little brass click.

The lock yielded to a hairpin and forty seconds of patience. The door swung inward on a breath of cold air that smelled of stone and lamp oil and — beneath it, faint as a ghost's cologne — something older. Dust, yes. But also myrrh. And under the myrrh, something like the inside of a bell.

Casper lifted the candle and went down.

* * *

The stairs were steeper than she remembered, though she couldn't have said when she'd last been down here — childhood, maybe, sent to fetch a jar of pickled plums, back when the cellar was just a cellar. It wasn't a cellar anymore. Her candle found shelves first, ordinary ones, jars of preserves in cloudy rows, sacks of onions sweating in the dark. But past the onion sacks the walls changed. Someone had built new shelving against the old stone, deep and deliberate, and on those shelves stood glass.

Dozens of vessels. Hundreds. Little sealed orbs and cylinders and teardrop flasks, each one stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, each one holding a curl of light that moved when she looked at it — not reflected light, not candlelight bending through glass, but light with *intent*, light that turned toward her the way a cat turns toward a mousehole. Memories. Stolen memories, hundreds of them, arranged by some system she couldn't read, labeled in Odell's cramped hand on tags yellowed with age.

She had known, of course. She had always known what she was, what her hands did, what Odell had taught her hands to do. But knowing your gift and seeing its whole harvest at once are different hungers entirely. She stood in the cold with her candle shaking and understood that she was looking at thirty years of theft, catalogued like a library, kept like a man keeps letters from a dead lover.

And there — on the center shelf, alone, in a stand of black iron — a vessel larger than the rest. A sphere the size of a heart, and inside it not a curl of light but a *scene*, whole and moving, like a snow globe full of summer.

She reached for it before she decided to reach for it. Her fingers closed around the glass, and the memory poured into her the way stolen memories always did — but this one came like a river through a broken dam, because it had never been meant for her hands, because it was *royal*, sealed with a sigil she knew only from coins:

*—a young man kneels in a cavern lit blue-green by veins of light running through the rock, and he is beautiful the way winter is beautiful, sharp and pale, and he is afraid. Aldric. King Aldric, but young, younger than she is now, his crown not yet worn smooth. Before him the mountain opens like a throat, and in the throat something moves that she cannot look at directly; her own stolen eyes slide off it the way rain slides off oilcloth. The young king speaks words in a language that hurts her teeth, and his hands press flat against the stone, and the stone accepts them, takes him to the wrist, to the elbow, and he does not scream, which is worse than screaming. And behind him, half in shadow, holding the book open, reading the binding-words aloud while the king gives himself away —*

A man with grey in his beard. A man with steady hands and ink-stained fingers.

Odell.

The vision snapped shut. Casper stood in the dark holding a king's sacrifice in both hands, and her arms were shaking, and somewhere above her a floorboard creaked.

She didn't put it back. That was the strange part, the part she would think about later — she simply stood there, candle guttering, glass warm against her palms, when the door opened at the top of the stairs and lamplight fell down the steps in a long yellow blade.

"Casper." Odell's voice, very quiet. "Put it down."

"You bound him." Her voice came out wrong, scraped. "You were *there*. You held the book."

"Put it down, girl."

"The herald comes, and the hunters come, and you lie for me with a straight face, and all the while—" She turned, and the candlelight showed her his face, and it was worse than guilt. It was grief, old grief, worn smooth as a river stone. "You've known this hunt was coming. You've known for *years*. Haven't you."

He came down the stairs slowly, one hand on the wall, an old man descending into his own confession. He didn't take the sphere from her. He looked at it the way other men look at graves.

"I bound a boy to a hunger," he said, "to stop a war that would have burned every soul out of three kingdoms. I told myself it was mercy. I have told myself it was mercy for thirty years, and some mornings I almost believe it." His eyes came up to hers. "They're coming for you tomorrow, Casper. Not the hunters. Worse than the hunters. And I needed you to find this tonight, because you're going to need to know how to unbind a king."

* * *

From the loft above the shop, Odell listened to the girl's breathing slow toward sleep, and allowed himself, for the first time in decades, to feel the full weight of what he had done.

He had been young too, once. Young and certain, with a book in his hands and a war at his back, and a boy-king who volunteered — *volunteered*, that was the wound of it, Aldric had knelt willingly, had smiled at him with trust — and Odell had read the words and pressed the seal and told himself that one life, even one life hollowed and fed upon forever, was a fair price for a hundred thousand. Mercy. Sacrifice. The arithmetic of kings and cowards.

He had hidden the memory in glass because he could not bear to keep it in his head, and he had kept it because destroying it felt like murder. He had taught a thief's child to steal memories because her gift was the only counter to his binding he had ever found — every stolen memory returned weakens the seal, the book said, *every debt repaid, every theft undone* — and he had never told her, because telling her would make her a weapon, and weapons get taken, and he could not lose her too.

But the crown had learned of the binding's decay. The thing beneath the mountain grew hungry, and its hunger leaked — cold summers, whispering prophets, a king unseen for a year — and the crown had begun hunting the gifted to feed it, or to silence those who might undo it. Either way, they would come for Casper. They had already come asking their careful questions, and Odell had lied to them beautifully, and the lie had bought exactly one night.

One night to teach her what the book said. One night to tell her the truth he should have told her twenty years ago: that he had helped bind the king, that he had raised her partly as penance and partly as a key, and that if she hated him for it, she would be right to.

He rose quietly and took down the old book from behind the loose brick, and carried it down the cellar stairs to where the girl slept — no. Where the girl lay awake, he realized, her eyes open in the dark, the royal sphere still cradled against her chest like something rescued from a fire.

"You're not sleeping," he said.

"No," Casper said. "I'm deciding whether to hate you."

"That's fair," Odell said, and sat down beside her on the cold step, and opened the book across both their knees. "Let me tell you how a king gets unbound."

Above them, far off toward the valley's edge, the border stones began — very faintly, for the first time in living memory — to hum.
