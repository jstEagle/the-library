## 4. Taken Across the Stones

They came at the hour when the bread ovens first breathed heat into the streets, when the town of Vell smelled of ash and rising dough and no one was awake to see anything.

Casper knew the knock before it finished. Three raps, spaced too evenly — the rhythm of men who had rehearsed their confidence. She had been sleeping in the loft above Odell's shop, curled in the narrow space between his old map chest and the wall, and her body woke before her mind did, the way a thief's body learns to. Her hands went still against her ribs. Her breath went shallow. In the dark she counted: three sets of boots on the stairs below, one set heavier than the others.

"Odell," she whispered through the floorboards, though she knew he was already awake. He was always already awake when trouble came. It was one of the many things he had taught her without ever saying it aloud.

The door opened on its leather hinges with a sound like a held breath released. Lamplight climbed the stairs ahead of the hunters, gold climbing the grain of the wood, and then they were in the room — four of them, gray-cloaked, their badges stamped with a mountain split by a sword. The crown's mark. The mark that had never meant anything in Vell because nothing royal had ever bothered to cross the stones.

"Casper Ishikawa." The heavy-booted one said her name like he was reading it off an inventory. "You'll come with us."

She looked at Odell, standing in his nightshirt with his white hair unbound, looking for all the world like a harmless old man surprised in his bed. He was very good at looking like that. He had been very good at looking like things for as long as she had known him.

"She's my niece," Odell said. "A baker's girl. You've made a mistake."

It was smooth. Too smooth. Casper heard the seam in it — the way his voice didn't crack where a frightened man's voice should have cracked, the way his hands hung at his sides instead of wringing themselves. *He's done this before,* she thought, and the thought went through her colder than the draft under the door. *He's lied to hunters before. He's known this day was coming.*

"You were seen at the well yesterday," said the heavy one. "Both of you. Listening."

"We listen to proclamations like everyone else—"

"The girl's name is written in our book, old man." The hunter drew out a ledger bound in black leather, and even from the loft Casper could feel it — a wrongness radiating off the pages like heat off a stove. Someone had written names into that book with something other than ink. "Names don't lie. Hands do. Show me your hands, girl."

And there it was. The thing she had feared since the herald's voice cracked across the square: not the hunt itself, but the proof. Because her hands were the proof. They always had been. Thin scars webbed across both palms in patterns like frost ferns, marks that every gifted person carried and no honest person did, and she had spent nineteen years keeping them hidden in wool and work, and now a man with a ledger wanted them bared in lamplight.

She could have run. There was a gap in the roof tiles above her, a drop to the neighbor's wash line, a route through three back gardens she had mapped years ago for exactly this kind of morning. But the hunters had spread through the house below — she could hear them now, doors opening, the cellar hatch creaking — and if she ran they would take Odell alone, and whatever was written in that black book about him would be worse than what was written about her. She understood that without knowing how she understood it. Some knowledge you steal so young it feels like memory.

So she came down the ladder slowly, and she held out her hands, palms up, and let the lamplight find the frost-ferns.

The heavy hunter studied them the way a butcher studies meat. "Both of you," he said again, and this time it wasn't a question.

* * *

They bound her wrists with cord woven through with iron thread, and the moment it touched her skin she understood, with a sick lurch, that the iron wasn't for her safety. It was a muzzle. Her gift sat behind her teeth like a shout swallowed, and every step toward the valley's edge made the cord hum faintly against her pulse.

They walked her and Odell out the east road, past the last houses, past the tannery with its sour reek, up the long slope where the fields gave out into gorse and stone. And there they were: the border stones. Nine of them in a broken crescent, taller than a man, older than the town, their surfaces worn smooth as river pebbles except where travelers had pressed their palms over generations. They hummed. They had always hummed — a note so low you heard it in your back teeth rather than your ears, the sound of the valley holding its breath. Every child in Vell learned the rule before they learned letters: *what you carry cannot follow you past the stones.* Magic stayed inside. Magic died at the border like a fish pulled from water.

Casper had stood at the stones a hundred times in her life, close enough to touch, never touching. Everyone in Vell made the pilgrimage eventually — lovers swearing they'd cross together someday, dreamers staring out at the gray hills beyond, all of them knowing they never would, because past the stones a gifted person was just a person, and who would choose to be only that?

The hunters walked them straight through the crescent without pausing.

The world ended and began in the same heartbeat.

It started as cold — a cold that came from *inside*, flooding down her arms like winter water poured into her sleeves. Then the iron cord burned white-hot against her wrists and she screamed, or thought she screamed; she couldn't tell over the roaring. Because the magic hadn't died at the border. It had been *waiting*. All those years in the valley, her gift had been muffled, wrapped in wool, breathing through a straw — and now, on the far side of the stones, it tore its wrappings off and filled her lungs like the first breath after drowning.

She fell to her knees on the wet grass of the outside world and the whole hillside *told her things*. Every footstep the hunters had ever taken was printed in the mud in light only she could read. The grief of the heavy-booted one rose off him like steam — a dead daughter, a name he couldn't say aloud — and Casper flinched from it as if struck. Odell's memories shimmered around him in layers, decades deep, and she saw — one flash, one terrible flash — a throne room, a young man kneeling, glass glinting — before Odell caught her eye and shook his head, once, small.

The gift didn't stop. That was the horror of it. In Vell she had to *reach* for memories, press her palms to skin, coax them loose like splinters. Here they came to *her*, unbidden, the whole loud grieving world pressing against her skin wanting to be touched, wanting to be taken, and the hunger in her hands woke up and stretched and *smiled*.

"Get her up," the heavy hunter said, and his voice wavered, because he could feel it too, whatever the crown's handlers had told him to expect. "Iron holds till the capital. Move."

Casper got up. The world outside was larger than promised — that was the second thing she learned, after the cruelty of her own sharpened gift. The hills rolled on farther than hills should roll. The sky went up and up without the valley walls to hem it, and somewhere in that enormous blue a bird cried out, and the bird's loneliness hit her like a thrown stone. She had spent her whole life in a bowl. She had not known the world was a country of open sky, and she hated it instantly, the way you hate anything vast enough to make you small.

They traveled three days. She remembered them later as a smear of rain and roads and the iron cord singing against her bones, and Odell walking beside her with his hands bound too, silent, watching her with an expression she couldn't read and was afraid to steal.

* * *

The capital announced itself first as a smell — coal smoke and horse and river rot — then as a bruise of light on the horizon at dusk, and then all at once, cresting the last hill, as a city so large Casper's new-sharpened senses simply refused it. Too many heartbeats. Too many lives stacked on lives. She felt the city the way you'd feel a wave breaking over you: ten thousand griefs, ten thousand small joys, all of it pressing in, and the iron cord groaning at its seams to keep it out.

They took her down. Down was the direction of everything here — down from the gate, down from the street, down stairs worn hollow by feet, until the sky was a grate overhead and the air tasted of stone and old fear. The dungeons beneath the crown's seat were not the rough cells of stories. They were clean. White-washed. Quiet. Somehow that was worse.

They put her in a cell alone, replaced the iron cord with an iron cuff bolted to the wall, and left her with a bucket, a blanket that smelled of lye, and the distant dripping of water counting out hours.

She had been counting drips for what might have been a day when the lock turned.

The woman who entered wore the gray cloak and split-mountain badge of the hunters, but she wore them wrong — sleeves rolled, collar open, the cloak slung like a burden rather than a rank. She was perhaps thirty, brown-skinned, with a scar splitting one eyebrow and eyes that moved over the cell the way Casper's own eyes moved over a room: cataloguing exits. She carried a tray of bread and broth, which hunters did not do. Hunters sent servants.

"Eat," the woman said, setting the tray down. "You'll want your strength."

"For what?"

The woman hesitated. Glanced at the barred door. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, pitched under the hearing of any listener there might be, and her eyes — when they met Casper's — held something Casper recognized with a jolt, because she heard it in dying women's memories all the time: the specific frequency of a person who has decided to stop obeying and hasn't yet told anyone.

"They're not taking you to justice," the woman said quietly. "I'm Sera. I'm ordered to deliver you to the throne room tomorrow. And Casper Ishikawa — whatever they've told you about why the crown wants a memory-thief—" her jaw tightened, and for half a heartbeat her carefully flat expression slipped, showing the dread underneath, "—it isn't justice. The king hasn't been seen unmasked in a year. And the snow started falling in summer."

She straightened, mask restored, and stepped back toward the door.

"Sleep," Sera said, in her official voice now, loud enough for listeners. "You'll be presented at court."

But her eyes, one last time, said something else entirely.
