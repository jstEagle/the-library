## 5. The Court of Borrowed Faces

Sera had walked the Marble Gallery a thousand times, and a thousand times it had lied to her.

The court of Aldric the Unforgotten — so the heralds still styled him, though no one could say when they had last heard him speak — was a machine of smiles. They clicked into place as she entered, tooth by tooth, the way portcullis teeth dropped before a gate. Lady Vessa of the Amber Reach, who had wept genuine tears at her husband's funeral two winters past, was smiling as though joy were a uniform she'd been ordered to wear. Lord Callen, whose son had vanished in the first purge, was laughing at a joke no one had told. The candles along the gallery walls burned without flickering, because the air did not move, because the windows had been sealed against a summer that was already turning strange at the edges — frost on the glass at dawn, the gardeners muttering about roses blooming white.

Sera kept her own face still and professional. It was easier than the others. She had never learned to smile on command, and lately she suspected she had forgotten how to do it at all.

The thief walked beside her, wrists bound in iron-threaded cord that Sera had insisted upon and then hated herself for insisting upon. Casper Ishikawa moved like water finding its level — unhurried, observant, her dark eyes cataloguing everything: the guards, the exits, the weight of the candle sconces, the particular way Lord Callen's smile didn't reach the bruised hollows under his eyes. Sera had spent three days in the dungeons below the capital and had learned exactly two things about her prisoner. First, that she counted. Her lips moved sometimes, barely, tallying steps or guards or heartbeats. Second, that she was afraid, and hid it so well that only another person doing the same thing would have noticed.

*Two women pretending not to be afraid,* Sera thought. *In this place, that's practically a friendship.*

"You're staring," Casper murmured, not turning her head.

"I'm assessing."

"You're staring *assessingly*. There's a difference. One of them is rude."

Sera almost smiled. She caught it before it landed. "Keep walking."

The gallery opened onto the Court of Sunfall, the great receiving hall where the king had held audience every seventh day for the first decade of his reign — a young king then, golden-haired and warm-voiced, who had learned the name of every servant in the palace and been known, absurdly, for it. Now the throne stood empty beneath its canopy of white silk, and the court arranged itself around the emptiness the way a body arranges itself around a wound. The nobles bowed to the vacant throne. They spoke to the vacant throne. Twice a year, a proclamation arrived in the king's own hand — the handwriting perfect, the seal unbroken, the words growing stranger each season, all frost and hunger and *the long quiet coming, the long quiet good* — and the court read them aloud in voices like people reciting scripture they did not believe, because not believing it out loud was the one crime the crown still punished swiftly.

The last time anyone had seen the king unmasked was fourteen months ago. Sera had been there. She had seen the thing that looked at the court through Aldric's grey eyes, and she had not slept properly since.

"Is he real?" Casper asked quietly. "The king. The one they keep behind the silk."

Sera's step faltered — half a heartbeat, no more, but the thief caught it the way she seemed to catch everything.

"You've heard the stories," Sera said.

"I've heard a hundred stories. I'm asking the woman with the keys."

Ahead of them, Lady Vessa turned, and her rehearsed warmth swung toward them like a lantern. "Captain Sera. And this must be the gift from the valley." Her eyes moved over Casper the way appraisers' eyes moved over jewelry — weighing, pricing. "The crown is *pleased* with you, Captain. Delivering the memory-thief intact. His Majesty's ministers speak of nothing else."

"His Majesty's ministers," Sera said, "speak of a great many things."

"They do, don't they." The smile held. The eyes did not. Vessa leaned in, lowering her voice to a courtier's murmur, silk over steel: "The audience is at dusk. Not the public one. The other one." Then she swept away, her gown whispering against the marble like a snake crossing stone.

Casper waited until they were moving again. "The gift from the valley," she repeated. "That's what I am to them. A gift."

"You're a tool," Sera said. It came out harsher than she intended, and she did not soften it, because softening it would have been a lie. "So am I. That's the whole of the court, if you want the truth of it. A hall full of tools that have learned to smile so no one melts them down."

They passed beneath the great window — sealed now, but once open to the western light — and Casper looked up at it. "The border stones," she said. "Back home, they hum. You can feel it in your teeth if you stand close enough. Magic can't cross them. Nothing crosses them, really. The valley forgot there was an outside, and the outside forgot there was a valley." She flexed her bound hands, and Sera saw the fine tremor in them that the thief would have hidden if she'd known she could. "But the hunters crossed. They came for me. Which means someone on this side of the stones knew I existed. Someone wanted me badly enough to reach into a place the world itself has decided to abandon."

Sera said nothing. She was thinking of the order that had come down three days ago, written in the king's own perfect hand, delivered to her quarters by a servant whose eyes had been wrong — too still, too patient, like a pond in deep winter. *Deliver the thief to the throne room at dusk. Alone. Do not announce her. Do not remain.*

She had read it four times. She had burned it, as instructed, and watched the ash curl, and felt the frost of it in her chest long after.

* * *

They took the midday meal in a side chamber off the gallery, because Sera had decided — without admitting to herself that she was deciding anything — that the dungeons were no place to keep a girl who might be the only person in the kingdom the crown actually *needed*. Bread, cheese, small beer. Casper ate like someone who had learned never to trust the next meal, and Sera pretended not to watch, and both pretenses failed beautifully.

"My orders were to bring you to the magistrates," Sera said at last.

Casper looked up. "Were."

"Were." Sera set down her cup. The beer was bad; everything in the palace had been going subtly bad for a year — the wine souring in sealed casks, the meat greying, the bread tasting faintly of cold. "Instead I've been ordered to deliver you to the throne room. At dusk. Alone. No announcement. No witnesses. And I'm not to stay." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "There is no lawful proceeding that happens in an empty throne room at dusk, thief. Whatever waits for you up there, it isn't justice."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I spent three days in a dungeon watching you, and you didn't once ask me to let you go. Not a bargain, not a plea, not a tear." Sera met her eyes. "I've escorted forty-one gifted souls to the magistrates in six years. Every one of them begged. You just *counted*. I want to know what a person counts, when she's certain no one is coming."

For a long moment Casper said nothing. Then, quietly: "Exits. Guards. The weight of things I could break." She turned her bound wrists over. "And whether the woman with the keys walks like someone following orders, or someone looking for a reason not to."

"And?"

"And today you walked like the second one." The thief's mouth curved — not quite a smile, something older and warier than a smile. "So. The throne room. What do you know about the thing that sits in it?"

"It doesn't sit," Sera said. "It *waits*. I saw the king once, a year ago, before the silk went up. He was holding court. He was *perfect* — warmer than he'd ever been, funnier, every word placed like a jewel. And then a serving girl dropped a tray, one small crash of silver, and every head in the hall turned to her except his." Sera's hands had gone cold around the cup; she made herself continue. "He was looking at the *window*. At the light coming through it. Like he was hungry, and the sun was food, and he was deciding how much of it he could take before anyone noticed the sky getting dimmer." She looked up. "That winter, the frost came early. This year it's coming in summer. The ministers call it a blessing. The long quiet. I've started calling it what it is. Something up there is eating, and the world is what's on the plate."

Casper had gone very still. Not the stillness of fear — Sera had seen fear on prisoners, knew its shape — but the stillness of recognition, of a lock finding its key.

"That's why they took me," Casper said softly. "Not the thefts. The thefts were the *excuse*. Someone with a king's handwriting reached into a valley the world forgot, for a thief who steals memories with her hands." She laughed once, without any humor in it. "They don't want me punished. They want me *used*. The thing on the throne is starving, and it's heard there's a girl who can carry whole lives in her fingers."

"Dusk is four hours away."

"Then I have four hours to become very brave or very clever." Casper looked at her bound wrists, then up, and there was something naked in her face now, something she had clearly spent a lifetime walling off. "Sera. When you deliver me — and you will, because if you don't, they'll send someone worse, and you know it — I need you to do one thing. Stay near. Not in the room. Near. If what's up there decides I'm more useful emptied out than filled in, I want one person in this palace who knows what I was when I walked in."

Sera should have said no. Every instinct of six years of survival, every rule that had kept her alive while better soldiers vanished, said *no, keep your head down, deliver the tool, sharpen nothing but your own silence.*

She reached across the table and, instead of unlocking the irons, rested two fingers briefly on the thief's bound hands — the closest thing to an oath she could give in a palace where the walls were said to listen.

"Four hours," Sera said. "Then we find out what the crown is really hungry for."

Above them, through the sealed window, the summer light was already going the color of old ice.
