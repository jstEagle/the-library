## 6. An Audience with Winter

They dressed her for the throne room the way one dresses a corpse — carefully, and without asking what she would have wanted.

A woman with cold fingers laced Casper into a gown of grey silk, the color of frost on a windowpane, and pinned her hair back from her face so that nothing obscured it. *So they can see what I am,* Casper thought. *So they can see the thief.* The gown was beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when they are meant to be looked at rather than lived in, and she hated it immediately, and then hated herself for hating it, because there had been years in Vell when she'd have traded a finger for a dress like this.

The corridor to the throne room was long and lit by lamps that burned without flickering. That was the first wrongness she noticed: no draft moved them. No breath of air at all. The palace breathed like something holding its breath.

Sera walked beside her, armored now, her face arranged into the blank courtesy of a woman doing her duty. But twice, as they passed through galleries hung with portraits of dead kings, Sera's hand brushed Casper's elbow — once to slow her before a turning where two guards stood too close, once simply to touch, briefly, as if to say *I am still here* without saying anything at all.

"You'll kneel when the herald calls it," Sera murmured. "You'll speak only when spoken to. And whatever you see—" A pause, measured to the length of a heartbeat. "Whatever you feel, don't let it reach your face."

"And if it does?"

"Then pray he's not looking."

*He,* Casper noted. Not *it*. She filed that away among the small hoard of truths she was accumulating about this place, the way she'd once filed away exits and guard rotations and which merchants kept their strongboxes under the bed instead of behind the false wall.

The doors opened.

The throne room was vast and white. Not pale — *white*, every surface of it, floor and pillar and vaulted ceiling, as though snow had fallen indoors centuries ago and never melted, as though the room itself had been frozen mid-breath. Courtiers lined the walls in furs despite the season being late summer; she could smell their sweat beneath the perfume, fear wearing its finest clothes. And at the far end, on a throne carved from a single block of ice-grey stone, sat the King of Vell's enemies, the King whose name had crossed even the border stones, the King who hunted the gifted.

King Aldric did not look like a monster.

That was the worst of it. He looked like a man in his early thirties, handsome in a worn way, dark-haired, with a face that might once have been quick with laughter. He wore black and silver, and the crown upon his head was old iron worked into the shape of mountain peaks, and his eyes—

His eyes were winter. Not cold exactly. *Empty*, the way a field is empty after harvest, after everything has been taken and carried away and there is nothing left but stubble and frost.

"The gifted thief," he said, and his voice filled the white room without effort, the way cold fills a valley at dusk. "Of Vell. You've traveled far to be disappointing."

Casper knelt when the herald called it, silk pooling around her knees on stone so cold she felt it through the fabric like a brand. "Your Majesty."

"Look at me."

She raised her eyes. The court held its collective breath. Somewhere to her left, a noblewoman's fan stopped mid-motion.

"Odell Marsh taught you," Aldric said. It was not a question. "He taught you to take with your hands. Tell me — when you take a memory, what do you feel?"

Casper's mouth went dry. There were a hundred safe answers and she reached for all of them at once and found none. So she told the truth, because it was the only coin she had left. "Warmth. Like holding a cup of tea someone else poured."

Something moved behind the king's eyes. Something *leaned forward*.

"Rise," said Aldric. "Come here. Give me your hand."

Every instinct she owned screamed against it. Her gift worked by touch; she had spent her whole life controlling what entered and left her skin, building walls of habit and discipline, and here was a king — a *hungry* king, some part of her whispered, though she didn't know yet how she knew — asking her to bare the one door she had. Beside her, almost imperceptibly, Sera stiffened.

But refusal was not among the options offered to thieves in white rooms.

Casper rose. She crossed the frozen floor, each step ringing faintly, and took the king's hand.

* * *

The world went white, then went deep.

She fell through him the way she had once fallen through a frozen lake as a child — no, not as a child; she had no childhood of her own to fall through, and that absence opened beneath her now like a second lake — and she saw.

She saw a young man with quick laughter standing in a chamber beneath a mountain, iron crown heavy on his brow, speaking words in a language that hurt to hear, binding himself — *willingly*, that was the horror of it, *he chose this* — to something vast and patient coiled in the dark below. She saw the bargain struck: strength for the kingdom, an end to war, and payment deferred. She saw the deferred payment begin.

She saw the thing feed.

It ate from the inside out. Memories first — she watched them go, whole years of a life peeled away like fruit skin: a mother's face, a wedding day, the taste of rain, a dog named something warm and forgotten. Each memory it swallowed, the young king grew quieter, and the thing beneath the mountain grew stronger, and its hunger rose up through the stone and into the sky, and the sky answered the way the sky answers any great emptiness —

*with cold.*

She saw the summer shorten. Saw the first frost come earlier each year, then the snows, then the white silence creeping down from the peaks like a tide that had forgotten how to turn. The endless winter wasn't a curse. It was an *appetite*, made visible. The thing wearing Aldric's face was starving the world to fill itself, and it could not be filled, because it was eating the wrong food — it wanted memories, human warmth, the tea someone else poured, and the world outside the king's body was only weather.

And then the hunger turned, and *looked at her.*

It found her gift the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. It found the hundreds of memories she carried, stolen and sealed and treasured — a fisherman's dawn, a bride's terror, a dying woman's happiest hour — and it found the hollow where Casper Ishikawa's own childhood should have been, and it *purred.*

*Oh,* said the winter, in a voice like snow sliding from a roof. *Oh, you lovely hollow girl. You carry a feast and you are yourself the famine.*

She tried to pull her hand back. She couldn't. The grip that held hers was gentle and absolute, a father's grip, a trap's grip.

*I can give you back what Odell took,* it murmured, and the words slid into her like cold water into cracks. *Yes. We know. We know what he keeps in glass, we know what he buried in you, we know why a man who steals memories would leave his own daughter empty. Serve us. Open the vaults of your little hands and pour. Feed us, and we will make you whole — every year of your own returned, every stolen treasure yours to keep forever. And the old liar rots free in the sun, unharmed, unhunted, forgiven.*

*Odell goes free,* it repeated, softer, crueler. *All it costs is everyone else.*

The vision released her.

* * *

Casper came back to herself kneeling on the white floor, though she had no memory of falling. Her hand still lay in the king's. His face was composed, courtly, bored — but beneath the boredom, deep down in the harvested fields of his eyes, something waited. Patient. Certain.

"Well?" said Aldric aloud, for the benefit of the court. "What did you see, thief?"

She should say nothing. She should lie, the way Odell had taught her — *lie smoothly, lie lovingly, lie like a blanket over a birdcage.* She should say she saw ledgers and taxes and the ordinary machinery of crowns.

Instead she heard herself say, quietly, in the silence of the frozen room:

"I saw why the snow comes early."

The fan resumed moving somewhere. A courtier exhaled. Aldric's expression did not change, but the temperature of the room dropped — she felt it physically, felt frost feather across the flagstones near the throne, felt the courtiers' furs suddenly less absurd.

"You presume much," said the king.

"I was given to understand presumption was why I was brought here." Her voice didn't shake. She was distantly proud of that. "You didn't summon me across the border stones for my manners, Majesty. You summoned me because I take memories with my hands — and you are *so hungry*."

The word landed in the white room like a stone in a well. She heard it fall a long way.

For a moment — one suspended, glittering moment — the mask slipped. What looked out through Aldric's face was not a man and not a king but a *famine*, ancient and enormous and pressed up against the inside of its prison like a child against a window, and it looked at Casper with something very like love, which was the most frightening thing she had ever seen.

Then the mask returned, mild and royal.

"Take her back to her cell," Aldric said to Sera. "We will speak again."

As Sera's hand closed on her arm — firm, real, warm, the warmest thing in the entire white room — Casper leaned close to the hunter and whispered, so softly that only the two of them and the hungry thing on the throne could possibly have heard:

"He's not the king anymore. He's the cage. And the thing inside is offering me a bargain."

Sera's step faltered, just once.

"And Casper," the winter called after her, mild as morning frost, using the king's kind, worn voice, "do think about our offer. The old man's freedom weighs so little. Your whole self weighs so little. Empires have been bought for less."

The doors closed behind them, and the corridor lamps burned steady and unflickering, and Casper walked back toward her cell with Sera's grip on her arm and the bargain coiled in her chest like a second heartbeat — *serve it and Odell goes free* — knowing already, with the terrible clarity of the hollow, that she was going to say yes to something.

She only didn't yet know to whom.
