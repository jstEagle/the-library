## 7. The Mentor's Confession

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Odell Vane had slept in worse.

He told himself that first, the way he told himself things now — small, practical lies laid down like paving stones across a chasm. He had slept in ditches during the Border Wars. He had slept in a hay barn with fever chewing through his bones. He had slept, once, in the shadow of the border stones themselves, listening to them hum all night like a hive of patient bees, waiting for him to be foolish enough to try to cross.

He had never been foolish enough. That was the whole of his life, he thought, turning his hands over in the dark. Forty years in the valley, and not one act of foolishness — only acts of cowardice dressed so carefully as wisdom that even he had stopped being able to tell them apart.

The cell was beneath the barracks of the hunting company, three turns down a stair that smelled of tallow and rat. His wrists were raw where the irons bit. They had taken his coat, his knives, the little glass vials sewn into every seam — thirty years of careful work confiscated in eleven minutes by a sergeant who handled each memory like it might bite. Let him wonder what was in them. Let him lie awake at night wondering why an old man carried the sound of someone else's wedding day against his ribs.

Casper would have known how to steal past that sergeant. Casper could have walked out of this cell wearing the warden's own face, if faces were the sort of thing she took.

But Casper was not here. Casper was somewhere above him, or beside him — he had counted the footsteps, mapped the echoes the way she had taught him to map a house before a job — and she was in a cell much like this one, and it was his fault.

That was the truest sentence he owned. Everything else was paving stones.

* * *

He had been young once. It surprised him, sometimes, how thoroughly he had buried that fact. In the valley he was simply old Odell, the fence with the crooked door and the endless tea, the man who taught orphan girls to lift memories out of grieving heads without leaving a scar. The children of Vell thought he had been born old, the way they thought the border stones had always hummed, the way they thought magic had always been a story told about elsewhere.

But he had been young. He had crossed the stones at nineteen with a soldier's pay and a soldier's arrogance, and the world outside had flooded into him the way water floods a lung.

He remembered the war the way you remember a fire you survived: not in images but in heat. Two kingdoms grinding against each other for a mountain pass neither needed, because pride is the only resource men never run out of. He had been a memory-thief then too, but a different kind — the crown's kind. He had walked battlefields lifting terror out of dying boys so their officers could send fresh ones forward unburdened. He had stolen courage from cowards and poured it into heroes until the heroes burned out like cheap candles. He had been very good at it. Goodness, he had learned, was just cruelty with better posture.

And then there was Aldric.

Aldric had been twenty-three, a second son no one expected anything of, with ink-stained fingers and a laugh that made whole rooms turn toward him like flowers toward light. Odell had met him in a field hospital, stealing the screaming out of a boy two cots down, and Aldric had watched him do it — watched, and not flinched, and said afterward: *You take the worst of it. Does anyone ever give anything back?*

No one ever had. No one had ever asked.

They became friends the way soldiers become friends — quickly, completely, under the assumption that one of them would die before it mattered. But Aldric didn't die. Aldric's brother died, and then his father, and the second son with ink-stained fingers became king in the worst year of the war, and came to Odell in the ninth month of his reign looking like a man who had been awake for a decade.

*It talks to me,* Aldric had said. *The thing under the mountain. It's been talking since the coronation. It wants — * and here the young king had laughed, horribly, *— it wants everything. And I am so tired, Odell. I am so tired of holding the door shut.*

The binding had seemed like mercy. That was the thing Odell could never make anyone understand, the thing he had rehearsed explaining to Casper for thirty years and never once said aloud. The binding had seemed like mercy. A ritual older than either of them, half-remembered from a text he'd stolen the knowledge of out of a dead archivist's head: bind the hunger, seal the king's awareness of it behind glass and stone, let him live his life unhaunted. The entity would sleep. The king would forget it existed. The war would end — and it did end, within the season, because a king who no longer heard the whisper beneath his throne found it easy to be generous, and generosity ended wars faster than swords did.

Odell had pressed his hands to Aldric's temples in a candlelit room while snow fell on the capital, and he had taken something out — not a memory exactly, but the *door* to the memory, the hinge, the handle — and sealed it in the deepest vault of the young king's mind, and locked it with a working so old it predated the border stones themselves.

*A price,* the archivist's stolen notes had warned. *All bindings have a price. The sealed thing does not die. It waits. And it feeds on whatever seeps through the lock.*

He had read that warning. He had decided, at twenty-six, with a king's grateful hand on his shoulder, that waiting was not feeding. That a sleeping wolf was not a wolf at all.

Foolishness. After forty years, he finally had the word for it.

* * *

The thing he had not understood — the thing he still barely understood, lying awake in the dark with rats moving in the walls like thoughts — was that a binding does not hold a monster. It holds a *door*, and doors swing both ways, and every year a little more of Aldric leaked through the lock into the dark, and a little more of the dark leaked back. The entity fed on the king's memories the way a taproot feeds on soil, slowly, patiently, from below. And the king forgot. First small things — the name of a childhood dog, the taste of a particular summer — and then larger ones. His mother's face. His brother's funeral. The reason he had ever wanted to be good.

Until the man on the throne smiled with rehearsed warmth and no one could say what was smiling.

Odell had seen it coming for years. That was the unforgivable part — not the binding itself, but the years after, when the reports drifted north with the traders: the king unseen, the king unmasked, the winter starting earlier each season, frost in the high meadows in midsummer. He had sat in his crook-backed house in Vell drinking tea and *knowing*, and he had done nothing, because doing nothing had kept him alive for forty years and he had mistaken survival for virtue.

Then the hunt was declared, and the hunters came to the valley, and he had lied for Casper — smoothly, easily, the way he lied about everything — and understood even as the words left his mouth that he was not protecting her. He was protecting the last version of himself that could pretend to be innocent.

She had found the glass anyway. Of course she had. He had raised her; he should have known better than to hide anything from her anywhere in that house. He had come down the cellar stairs and seen her holding the sealed memory of the binding up to the lamplight, turning it, watching the young king inside it press his hands to his own temples, and the look on her face —

Not betrayal. That was the terrible part. Betrayal he could have borne. What he saw was *recognition*. She looked at the memory the way she looked at a lock she already knew how to open.

* * *

Footsteps on the stair. Not the sergeant's heavy tread — lighter, quicker, a walk he knew better than his own heartbeat, because he had taught it to her, corrected it a hundred times in a hundred dark houses: *heel first, girl, the ball of your foot is a drum.*

Her shadow stopped outside his cell. Through the wall — rough stone, badly mortared, the builders of this place more concerned with keeping people in than keeping secrets apart — he heard her sit down with her back against the bricks.

"Odell." Her voice was low. Careful. She was doing what he had taught her to do before every job: naming the exits, counting the risks. Only now the risk was him.

"Casper." He made his voice steady. An old trick: steadiness borrowed, like everything else he owned.

Silence. Then, through the wall, flat and terrible: "I touched his hand today."

He closed his eyes. "I know."

"You knew. You've known for—" A breath. "How long?"

"Since the frost reports started coming north. Eleven years, perhaps. Truly knowing — since the night I bound him, and I chose not to know, which is its own kind of knowing." He leaned his head back against the stone. "Ask me, girl. Whatever you came to ask. I owe you answers the way other men owe debts, and I have been defaulting my whole life."

Another silence, longer. When she spoke again, her voice had gone quiet in the way that frightened him most — the quiet of someone deciding something.

"Why teach me? All those years. Why take in a thief's orphan and teach her your gift, if you were hiding this?" A pause. "Were you training me for this? For him?"

"No." The truth came out of him ragged. "God forgive me, no. I taught you because teaching you was the only way to keep you. Because a gifted child alone in Vell is a child the stones will eventually swallow, and because—" His voice caught on the old, true thing. "Because you were seven years old and you put your hand on my arm to comfort me when you thought I was asleep, and I felt you *take* the grief out of me without meaning to, gentle as breath, and I thought: this child was born to give back what the rest of us only know how to take. I taught you out of selfishness, Casper. I taught you because I loved you. Every sin I have committed began with love and ended with arithmetic."

"What arithmetic?"

"The kind where you weigh one suffering against many and call the answer mercy." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I bound a kind young king to save a kingdom. I let the binding rot rather than admit my error. I hid in the valley behind stones that keep magic out, which means they also keep *consequence* out, and I called it peace." He turned his ruined wrists in the irons. "And now the winter is coming down off the mountain, and the thing wearing his face has sent hunters into the one place I thought would never be touched, and it did that because of me. Because I built the lock and threw away the key."

"You didn't throw it away," Casper said. "There's a counter-ritual. Sera says the archivists' order kept records. There has to be—"

"There is," Odell said.

The silence that followed had weight. He felt her go still against the wall, the way she went still over an open strongbox.

"I stole the rite itself," he said. "From the archivist, the same night I stole the binding. Out of caution, I told myself. A craftsman keeps his tools." His mouth twisted. "I have carried it in my head for forty years, girl. It is the only memory I own that is truly mine — I paid for it with my soul, so I suppose I earned it. And I will give it to you now, whole, and you will hate every word of it."

"Tell me."

"The binding was sealed with a theft. So the unbinding must be sealed with a return." He spoke slowly, laying each word down like a man crossing ice. "Every stolen memory must go home. Not destroyed — returned. Pressed back into the mind it was taken from, freely given, freely received. The lock was built from taken things; only given things will dissolve it. And Aldric—" Here he stopped, because the next part was the part he had dreaded saying for forty years, and saying it aloud made it real in the air between them, in the cold and the rat-smell and the dark.

"Aldric what?"

"The entity is anchored in what he's lost. Every memory you return to him tears the anchor loose. But the rite requires a thief to perform it — a thief who gives instead of takes — and the final return, the one that breaks the lock entirely, cannot be a stranger's memory." He shut his eyes. "It must be the thief's own. The rite demands a price equal to the binding, and the binding cost a king his self. So the unbinding costs the thief hers. Every stolen memory returned, Casper. Including the ones you stole without knowing you stole them. Including your own childhood. Including—" his voice failed, gathered itself, "—including whatever it was that made you *you*, before you came to my door with snow in your hair."

Through the wall, nothing. Long enough that he thought she had gone. Then her voice, and it was steady in a way that broke him, because he had taught her that steadiness too:

"The woman in the first house I ever robbed. The baker's wife. I gave her memory back to her last spring. Did that count?"

"It counts," he whispered. "You've already begun."

"And if I do this — if I give all of it back — what's left of me?"

"Everything," he said. "That is the joke of it, girl. The cruelest joke I know. Thieves end with nothing because they spend their lives filling a hole that taking only widens. But you — you have spent ten years carrying other people's happiest days. Give them home, and what remains is not emptiness. What remains is the shape of a person who gave. Others will have to fill it. Freely. That is the whole of the rite's last line, the part the archivist wrote in the margin and I never understood until tonight: *what is emptied by giving may be filled by love.*"

He heard her breathing change. He pressed his palm flat against the cold stone between them, the way he had pressed it to a seven-year-old's hair on the night she arrived.

"I am not asking your forgiveness," he said. "I am asking for your hands. They were always better than mine."

For a long moment, silence. Then her hand came to rest against the wall opposite his — he couldn't see it, but he felt the faint warmth of her gift bleed through the mortar, the way it always did when she was afraid and trying not to be. And with it, deliberately, gently, she *gave* him something: a single memory, pressed through the stone like a flower through a page. Her first job. The pickpocket's stall at the autumn market. Him, younger, laughing, telling her *heel first, girl* — and in her version of it, he was not a liar or a binder or a coward. In her version, he was simply the man who had opened his door.

He held it the way you hold something you don't deserve.

"Tomorrow," she said quietly, rising. "Sera moves at dawn. The vault, the throne room, all of it. Rest, old man. You'll want to be awake for the spring."

Her footsteps climbed the stair, heel-first, soft as snowfall.

Odell sat alone in the dark with her gift warm in his chest and the counter-ritual burning a hole where his soul used to be, and he began, at last, to pray — not to any god, but to the girl behind the wall, whom he had wronged beyond mending and trusted beyond reason, to do the one thing he had never dared:

To give everything back.
