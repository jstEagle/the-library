## 8. The Long Winter Falls

The snow began on the morning of the harvest festival, and it did not stop.

Sera stood at her window in the hunters' barracks and watched it fall — fat, unhurried flakes drifting down through a sky the color of old pewter, settling on rooftops that should have been baking in late summer sun. Below, in the market square, a fruit-seller stood beside his cart of ruined plums, his hands hanging at his sides, staring up as though the heavens had made an error and he was waiting for someone to correct it. No one corrected it. By noon the snow lay ankle-deep in the streets, and by evening it had stopped melting at all.

They called it a blessing, at first. The priests of the Hollow Crown processed through the white streets swinging censers, their voices lifted in gladness: *the King's favor falls upon us, soft as mercy.* Sera had walked those processions herself for eleven years. She knew what gladness sounded like when it was rehearsed. This was worse than rehearsed. This was frightened people choosing to sing.

On the third day of snow, the gates closed.

She heard the order read aloud in the great hall, standing among the other hunters with her fists folded behind her back and her face arranged into obedience. No one leaves the city. No one enters. All gifted persons remaining within the walls are to be gathered for the King's protection — and the word *protection* sat in the air like a blade laid flat on a table. In the week that followed, the gathering became a taking. Hunters went out in pairs and came back dragging weavers and hedge-healers and a boy of perhaps nine who could make candles burn blue. They came back with fewer prisoners each time, too. Some of the gifted ran. Some fought. One old woman on Tanner's Row had burned three men to ash before they brought her down, and after that the orders changed again, quietly, the way cruel orders always do: no longer gather. Cull.

Sera signed the reports. That was her punishment, she supposed, for being good at her work — she was given the pen instead of the sword, and told to make the dying legible. *Subject resisted. Subject subdued. Subject did not survive transport.* Her handwriting stayed beautiful. It was the only part of her that did not shake.

At night she lay awake listening to the snow against the shutters, and she thought about Casper Ishikawa in her cell beneath the keep, and about the memory Sera had glimpsed once, accidentally, when the thief's bare fingers had brushed her wrist during transport — a flash of a valley, green and warm, border stones humming like struck bells. A place magic could not follow. A place this winter could not reach.

*There are still places,* she thought. *There is still a way out.*

Then she thought of the order pinned to her door that morning, written in the Lord Marshal's hand: *Deliver the thief to the throne room at moonrise. Unescorted. Unwitnessed.*

And she understood that there were no places left at all.

* * *

Moonrise came silver and cold, and Sera walked the dungeon stairs with a lantern in one hand and treason coiled tight in her chest like a second heartbeat.

She had decided nothing, formally. That was the trick of it — she would never decide, never sign anything, never speak the word aloud even to herself. Treason was not a choice you made; it was a door you noticed standing open, and then you were simply walking through it, and the only sin was in looking back. She had noticed the door three nights ago, when she'd seen the frost creeping up the inside of the throne room windows from the *outside* of the glass. She'd noticed it again reading the cull-lists, finding her own cousin's name among the gifted, struck through in red ink with *resolved* written beside it in a clerk's tidy script. And she noticed it now, most of all, descending into the dark with keys she had stolen from a sleeping sergeant, because whatever waited in the throne room at moonrise did not need witnesses, and Casper Ishikawa was the only person alive who might unmake it.

The cell block exhaled cold when she opened the iron door. Not dungeon-cold. Something older, moving through the stone like breath through teeth.

Casper sat cross-legged on the cot, awake, unsurprised. Of course she was unsurprised. Sera was beginning to understand that the thief lived half her life in other people's heads, and so very little could surprise a woman who had already seen it.

"You're late," Casper said.

"The court sleeps early these days." Sera held up the ring of keys. "Enchanted frost. Half the palace hasn't woken since the snow started. The other half wishes it hadn't."

"Odell?" The name came out careful, stripped of everything.

"Alive. Moved to the tower cells two days ago. I can't reach him tonight." Sera unlocked the shackles, working fast, not letting herself look too long at the raw skin of the thief's wrists. "I can get us to the vault. After that—"

"After that I press a king's nightmares back into his skull and hope it kills the thing wearing him." Casper flexed her fingers, and in the lantern light Sera saw them properly for the first time — scarred, steady, hands built for theft the way a hawk is built for wind. "You know what happens to you if we're caught."

"I've read enough reports to know how it goes." She kept her voice flat. "I'd rather be a line in someone else's ledger than keep writing them in mine."

Something moved across Casper's face — not gratitude; Sera suspected the woman had been robbed of the easy expressions long ago. More like recognition. Two tools, lying side by side in the same drawer, deciding to pick themselves up.

"Vault's below the east wing," Casper said. "I've seen it. In a memory I took off a vault-warden who drank too much and dreamed too loud." She stood, rolled her shoulders, and almost smiled. "Lead on, hunter. Try to look like you're arresting me."

* * *

The crown vault slept under a footfall of enchanted frost, and it was beautiful, and Sera hated it.

Frost had climbed the shelves and the iron cages and the long rows of sealed glass, sheathing everything in white fur and dim rainbows, so that the whole chamber glittered like the inside of some dead saint's reliquary. The wardens at the door had been the hardest part — not hard, exactly, but heavy. Sera had used the sleeper's grip the hunters taught for taking the gifted gently, pressure behind the ear, and lowered them down without a sound, and knelt a moment longer than necessary to check that they breathed. Then the door, then the stairs, then this: hundreds upon hundreds of glass vessels racked in the dark, each one holding a curl of light that pulsed faintly, like something breathing in its sleep.

Memories. Decades of them. Taken from the gifted the crown had culled, and from others besides — Sera recognized the royal seal on dozens of the older cases, wax gone amber with age. The thing on the throne had been eating for a very long time.

"It's all here," Casper whispered. She had gone still in the middle of the aisle, her breath fogging, her eyes moving shelf to shelf with an expression Sera had no name for. Hunger and horror wearing the same face. "Gods. It's all *here.*"

"How many do you need?"

"All of them." Casper reached out — stopped herself, fingers hovering a hair's breadth from the glass. "That's the rite. Every stolen memory returns to its owner or none of it works. Odell was clear." Her voice caught on his name and smoothed itself over. "The binding was woven from theft. A hundred small violences braided into one rope around a man's soul. You can't untie a braid by pulling one strand."

Sera looked down the endless aisles. "Some of these owners are dead. Some of them are *ashes.*"

"Then those go back to the mountain, to the earth, to whatever keeps what we lose." Casper finally touched the nearest vessel, cradling it in both palms the way you'd hold a bird. The light inside leaned toward her skin like a plant toward sun. "Everything that was taken has to be given. That's the whole of it. That's the price."

"And yours?"

Casper didn't answer. She was already moving down the row, lifting vessels two at a time, packing them into the canvas satchel Sera had brought with a gentleness that looked almost painful on her. Sera took the opposite aisle and worked in silence, and the frost ticked and settled around them, and somewhere far above, in the throne room, something ancient turned over in its borrowed sleep.

It was near the end — the satchel heavy, the first grey hint of false dawn pressing at the high vents — when Sera found the case with her own seal on it.

Small. Plain. The wax unbroken. Inside, a thread of light the color of a summer field, and she knew without touching it whose it was: her cousin Maren, who could coax honey from stones, whose name she had struck through herself in red ink because the alternative was letting another clerk do it badly.

Her hands shook. Casper appeared at her shoulder without sound, the way she did everything, and looked at the case, and looked at Sera, and said nothing at all. That was its own kind of mercy.

"Take it," Sera said finally. Her voice came out scraped. "When you do your rite. Take this one back to the earth with the rest."

"I will." And then, quieter, in the voice of a woman who had spent her life watching people lie to themselves: "You know there's no going back up those stairs. Not for either of us."

Sera closed her hand around the little glass case until the edges bit her palm, and found, to her surprise, that the shaking had stopped.

"No," she said. "But there's a way forward. Come on — dawn bells in an hour, and a king to wake."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of frozen stone, the first true thunder of the thaw-to-be rolled like a promise. Or a warning. Neither of them stopped to learn which.
