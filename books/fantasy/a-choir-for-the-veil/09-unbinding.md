## 9. Unbinding

The throne room doors opened without being touched.

They swung inward on hinges that had not been oiled in a year — Sera had told her that much on the stairs, whispering like a woman confessing a crime — and the cold came out to meet them. Not the honest cold of winter, which at least had manners, an end to it, a reason. This was a cold with appetite. It slid across the marble floor toward Casper like something that had been waiting for her name to be spoken.

Snow drifted down inside the hall. It fell from a ceiling lost in grey, slow and deliberate, settling on the banners of Vell's dead kings until they looked like shrouds. The court was gone — fled or frozen or folded into the walls, Casper did not know — and so the room belonged entirely to the throne, and entirely to what sat upon it.

Aldric looked up as she entered. His face was still his face. That was the cruelest part of it, she thought. Whatever wore him wore him well: the hollowed cheeks, the frost-white temples, the eyes like chips of river ice — all of it arranged into something almost gentle. Almost kingly. A mask carved by someone who had studied kindness from a great distance and never once touched it.

"You brought them," it said through his mouth. Its voice was Aldric's voice, but slowed, the way a song sounds when played under water.

Casper lifted the satchel against her chest. Inside, glass vials clinked softly, each one holding a stolen life sealed in silver and breath. Behind her, Sera stood in the doorway with a drawn sword and a face like a closed door of her own.

"I brought them," Casper said.

"Then you have chosen wisely." The thing smiled with Aldric's mouth, and frost crept out along the armrests where his fingers rested. "Return them to me, thief. Every one. Feed me what you carry, and I will let your old man walk out of this city with his bones unbroken. I will let the valley sleep. I am not cruel. I am only *hungry*."

"You're not hungry," Casper said. Her voice surprised her — steady, low, the voice she used over sleeping strangers whose happiest hours she was about to lift away. "Hunger ends when it's fed. You've been fed for thirty years and you're only getting colder. That's not hunger. That's *emptiness*, wearing hunger like a coat."

The temperature dropped. Ice bloomed up the pillars in ferns and feathers. Somewhere above, the snow fell faster.

"Odell taught you to talk," it said. "He should have taught you to kneel."

"He taught me to steal." She walked forward, one step, then another, her boots crunching on the frosted marble. "And the first thing he ever taught me was this: you can't take what someone gives freely. And you can't keep what was never yours."

She stopped before the dais. Up close, Aldric smelled of deep stone and old snow, of the underside of mountains, of places light had never bothered visiting. Beneath all of it — faint, nearly drowned — there was still a man. She could feel him the way she felt memories through glass: a warmth pressed flat under weight.

"I'm not here to feed you," she said quietly. "I'm here to give everything back."

She reached into the satchel and drew out the first vial.

* * *

The first memory belonged to a farmer outside the capital, a man who had once stood in a wheat field at dusk while his daughter ran laughing between the rows, gold light everywhere, gold light in everything. Casper had taken it two years ago for a client who wanted to know what joy felt like. She had carried it since, warm against her ribs like a second heart.

Now she pulled the stopper, pressed her palm flat against the glass, and pushed.

The gift moved through her the way it always did — a tingling, a loosening, the sensation of pouring water from one cup into another — but this time the cup was a king. The memory left her hands and sank into Aldric's skin like rain into cracked earth, and the whole throne room *shuddered*.

The thing beneath the crown screamed.

It was not a sound. It was a pressure, a cold detonation behind Casper's eyes; every candle guttered, the snow reversed itself mid-fall, and for one white instant Casper saw beneath the king — saw the vast coiled darkness knotted around his spine and skull and heart, ancient and eyeless, feeding through threads sunk deep into his living mind. Her stolen memory struck those threads like a spark striking rot. One of them *burned away*.

Aldric gasped. For half a heartbeat, the ice-chip eyes were brown. Human. Terrified.

"*More,*" he whispered — and she could not tell which of them said it.

So she gave more.

She worked through the satchel methodically, the way Odell had drilled into her across twenty years of cellar lessons: one at a time, palm to vessel, intention like a blade. A fisherman's first storm survived. A mother's lullaby sung to a child now grown and gone. A wedding danced barefoot in a kitchen. A boy's first sight of the sea. Each one she poured back into the man on the throne, and each one tore another thread loose, and each time the entity recoiled harder, the winter screaming through the stones, frost racing up the walls in jagged continents.

But it learned. Of course it learned. Halfway through the satchel, when Casper reached for the next vial, the cold found her instead.

* * *

It did not take her hands. It took her *past*.

The throne room vanished and she was six years old again, standing in a doorway in the valley of Vell, and it was raining, and a woman was crouched in front of her saying the words Casper had spent her whole life refusing to remember:

*"You can't come with us, little wren. Not past the stones. Gifted children can't cross — the magic won't let go of you, do you understand? It holds you here. If we take you, it will pull you apart."*

Her mother's face. Her father behind her, not looking, jaw tight, already halfway to gone. The cart loaded with everything they owned. The border stones humming their low grey hum at the edge of town.

*"Someone will look after you,"* her mother said, and her voice broke on it, and Casper — six years old, small hands, small heart — felt the exact shape of the lie as it left her mother's mouth. Because no one had been arranged. No one had promised. They had simply driven to the edge of the valley and set her down beside the road like an unwanted chair, and driven on, and the stones had hummed, and the rain had fallen, and Casper had stood there until dark.

Until a man with ink-stained fingers and tired kind eyes came walking up the road, stopped, crouched down, and said: *"Well. You look like you've got nowhere to be. Neither do I. Come on, then."*

Odell. Odell had found her because her parents hadn't even had the courage to leave her at a door.

The vision held her there in the rain, made her stand in it, made her feel the smallness and the wet and the terrible arithmetic of a child understanding she had not been protected but *discarded*. And through it all, the thing beneath the crown spoke in the voice of her mother, in the voice of her own six-year-old throat, in the voice of every lonely night since:

*This is why you steal, little thief. This is why you fill your hands with other people's happiness. You are trying to build a childhood out of salvage. Keep it. Keep the pain. It is the only thing that is truly yours — and if you give it away too, you will be nothing at all. An empty cup. A girl-shaped room with no one home.*

Casper stood in the rain of her own making and wept, and understood, finally, completely, what the cost would be.

Every stolen memory returned meant every stolen memory *gone from her*. She had known that since Odell whispered the counter-ritual through the cell wall. But she had imagined it as losing a hoard — the farmer's wheat field, the wedding dance, the sea. Things she could grieve and survive.

Not this. This was the anchor. This was the last proof that Casper Ishikawa had ever been a child, had ever been loved badly, had ever been anything at all. Return it, and there would be no wound to explain the woman. No reason. No root.

An empty cup.

In the throne room — somewhere far above the rain inside her head — Sera was shouting her name. The snow was falling upward. Aldric's body arched against its bonds of frost, and the thing beneath the crown laughed through his teeth, certain, triumphant, sure that no soul would choose to unmake its own foundation.

Casper wiped her face with the back of her hand, the way she had done in the rain thirty years ago, and said aloud, to the darkness, to the girl in the doorway, to herself:

"You're right. It's mine."

She reached into the deepest pocket of the satchel, where a single vial of plain grey glass had ridden against her heart since the dungeons — the one memory she had never sold, never shown, never named. Her own. Lifted from her own sleeping mind years ago by her own gifted hands, because some nights the remembering hurt too much to carry awake.

"But it was never meant to be a prison," she said. "It was meant to be a *memory*. And memories aren't for keeping."

She pressed it to Aldric's brow.

* * *

The return of her childhood went through the king like sunrise through fog.

There was no scream this time. There was silence — total, ringing, absolute — and in that silence the last thread binding the ancient thing to the man snapped with a sound like a mountain exhaling. Casper felt it go. Felt the darkness tear free of Aldric's spine and reel backward, howling without sound, shrinking, starving, suddenly *unfed* in a way it had not been in thirty years — and then the winter in the room broke all at once, every icicle crashing down, every banner shaking off its snow, and the entity poured out of the king's open mouth as a breath of black frost that thinned, and thinned, and was gone.

Aldric collapsed. Casper caught him — barely, he was heavy as a felled tree — and lowered him to the thawing marble, and put her ear near his lips, and waited.

He breathed. Once. Twice. Then his eyes opened, brown and clear and drowning in thirty returned years, and King Aldric of Vell looked up at the thief kneeling over him and whispered, in a voice rusted with disuse:

"...I remember my mother's name."

And Casper opened her mouth to answer — and found nothing there.

No mother. No rain. No doorway. No Odell on the road at dusk. She searched inward, frantically, the way she had once searched a stranger's sleeping mind, and found only smooth, shining emptiness — a house swept clean, a cup emptied to the bottom, a girl-shaped room with no one home. She knew facts, still. Words, skills, the counter-ritual's final line. But the *why* of her was gone. The wound, the root, the rain.

She sat back on the cold marble and heard, distantly, running footsteps — Sera's boots hammering up the hall, the doors bursting wide, Sera's voice crying out her name with something raw underneath it.

Casper turned toward the sound, toward the woman sprinting through the melting snow, and realized, with a calm that frightened her more than any winter, that she did not know who Sera was.

But Sera clearly knew her. And she was saying, over and over, as she fell to her knees in the thawing water and took Casper's empty hands in both of hers:

"It's done — it's done — oh gods, Casper, it's actually done—"
