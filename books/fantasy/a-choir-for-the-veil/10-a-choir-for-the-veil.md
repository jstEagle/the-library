## 10. A Choir for the Veil

The last memory left her hands like a bird leaving a cage.

Casper felt it go — the small warm weight of her own childhood, the valley, the border stones humming their low gray song, Odell's voice teaching her the first rule of taking: *be gentle, be quick, be gone.* She pressed her palms to Aldric's temples and gave it all away, and for one long heartbeat she was nothing at all. No name. No history. No thief.

Then the king opened his eyes.

They were brown. Not winter-brown, not the pale glacial color of the thing that had worn him — but warm, human brown, startled and wet, the eyes of a man waking from thirty years of drowning. He looked at her the way people look at the sun after a long illness: as if he could not believe it was still there.

"You," he said, and his voice cracked like ice on a river in March. "You're the one who was *taking*."

"And giving back," Casper said. Her own voice sounded strange to her, unanchored, belonging to no one she remembered being. "Every piece. I kept count."

Around them the throne room groaned. The frost that had sealed the windows for a season was retreating up the glass in silver threads, and where it went, light followed — real light, gold and thick, the light of a morning that should have come months ago. The thing beneath the crown made a sound then. Not a scream. A *sigh*, vast and old and almost relieved, the sound of a hunger finally allowed to end. It poured out of Aldric like smoke from a snuffed candle, rising through the rafters, thinning, unraveling into motes that caught the sunlight and did not fall again.

And with it came the thaw.

Casper heard it before she understood it: water. Everywhere, water. Ice cracking along the eaves of the palace, snow sliding from rooftops in soft avalanches, gutters waking with a sound like applause. The endless winter — the false season the entity had spun around the capital to feed its cold appetite — broke all at once, the way a fever breaks, sudden and total, leaving the world flushed and dripping and astonished.

Aldric sat up on the steps of his own throne. He was thinner than any king should be, wrapped in a blanket Sera had thrown over him, and he was laughing — a ragged, disbelieving laugh, the laugh of a man who had just been handed back three decades of his life and found them mostly intact.

"I remember my mother's kitchen," he said. "I remember the smell of bread. I had forgotten there was such a smell." He turned to Casper, and something in his face folded with grief. "What did it cost you?"

She reached inside herself, out of habit, looking for the shelves where she kept what wasn't hers, the locked room where her own life was stored.

The room was empty. The shelves were bare. She was, she realized, standing in herself for the very first time — and there was nothing in her but the present moment, bright and terrifying and clean.

"I don't know your name," she said honestly. "But I think you're the king."

"Aldric," he said gently. "And you are Casper Ishikawa. You told me that an hour ago, when your hands were shaking so badly you could barely press them flat."

"Tell me again tomorrow," she said. "And the day after. I may need to hear it many times."

He took her hand — carefully, the way one handles something broken and precious — and bowed his head over it. Whatever he meant to say, he didn't say it. Outside, the whole city was shouting, and the sound came through the melting windows like a tide: bells, voices, dogs barking, the enormous collective noise of ten thousand people realizing that summer had returned to the calendar.

* * *

They walked out together, the three of them — the king in his blanket, the hunter with her sword still drawn out of habit, the thief with empty hands — into streets running with meltwater.

The border stones cracked at noon.

Casper felt it even from the capital, leagues away, or perhaps she only imagined she felt it, since imagination was nearly all she had left. But Sera swore afterward that every gifted person in the city felt it too: a deep resonant *note*, like a bell struck underground, rolling out of the mountains where the valley of Vell lay hidden behind its gray humming stones. The stones that had held magic out of the valley for centuries. The stones that had made Casper's gift a small, quiet, parlor-trick thing, good only for lifting a dying woman's happiest hour for a loaf of bread.

The note rolled through the earth, and the stones split, and magic came home.

She would see it later, when she traveled east with the king's caravan — because Aldric insisted, because he said a debt that large deserved to be paid in person. She stood in the pass above Vell and watched the valley change color. It happened slowly, then all at once: the grass greening in visible waves, wildflowers opening in the ditches, the river running bright instead of dull. Old women in the village square lifted their hands and light pooled in their palms like water, tentative, wondering, as if they'd been told all their lives they were holding something forbidden and were only now daring to look at it.

Odell met her at the edge of town. He looked older than the mountains, smaller than she'd ever seen him, a man hollowed out by confession and cell walls. He opened his mouth — to explain, to apologize, to teach, some old reflex — and Casper raised her hand.

"I don't remember why I should hate you," she said. "Everyone tells me I have reason. They tell me you bound a king, and started a hunt, and lied to me my whole life." She studied his face, this stranger who had taught her gentleness and theft in the same breath. "But my hands remember you. When I see you, they want to reach out. So whatever else is true — you loved me once, and I must have known it."

"Casper—"

"Don't tell me the rest yet." Her voice shook. "Give it to me slowly. Like bread. One piece at a time, so I can learn to carry it."

He wept then, the old thief, standing in the new green grass of a valley that magic had finally been allowed to enter, and she let him, and did not touch him, and did not leave.

* * *

She stayed in the capital after that. It surprised everyone, including her.

She had no trade anymore — or rather, her trade was gone, because there was nothing left to take; she had emptied herself completely into the man on the throne. She rented a small room above a bakery that smelled, perpetually, gloriously, of the bread Aldric had spent thirty years forgetting. And slowly, without quite deciding to, she began to collect again.

Not by taking. Never by taking.

It started with Sera, who came by every evening at first and then most evenings, and who had developed the habit of pressing two fingers to Casper's wrist before telling a story — a hunter's gesture repurposed, a deliberate echo of the thief's touch, reversed. *"Here,"* Sera would say. *"This one's yours. Keep it."* And she would give her a memory freely: the first time Sera saw the sea. The taste of her grandmother's plum wine. The exact sound the training yard made at dawn, boots on gravel, a hundred recruits breathing in rhythm. Each gift settled into Casper like a stone dropped into clear water, rippling outward, finding a place.

Then others came. The baker whose mother's last hour Casper had once sold for a loaf — she arrived with her sister and they told Casper everything about the woman, every story, until the memory rebuilt itself in Casper's keeping not as stolen goods but as something shared, something held in common. The old archivist whose wedding day Casper had lifted for a client with a grudge — he brought the wedding portrait and narrated it, laughing, weeping, giving it back twice over. Word spread through the city the way warmth spreads through a house in spring: *the memory-thief has empty hands now. Bring her yours.*

By midsummer, Casper's small room above the bakery had become a strange kind of chapel. People queued on the stairs with offerings that were not bread or coin but hours — first kisses, dead fathers' voices, the smell of a ship's deck, the green flash of a son's first step. They gave her their memories the way other people gave relics to saints: not because she needed them, but because a thing given becomes larger than a thing kept alone.

Her hands learned a new grammar. Where once they had known only how to lift and seal and slip away, now they learned how to receive — palms open, fingers still, patient as cupped water. It hurt less than stealing ever had. It filled more.

One evening in late summer, Sera found her on the roof of the bakery, watching the sun go down over a city that no longer flinched at its own king.

"Do you miss it?" Sera asked. "The valley. The quiet. Being small."

Casper considered. Inside her were hundreds of memories now, none stolen, all given — a choir of other people's lives singing softly in the dark behind her ribs, and threaded among them, growing steadily, a few notes that were hers alone: this roof, this sunset, this woman beside her, this exact feeling of having nothing left to hide.

"No," she said. "For the first time in my life, everything I'm carrying belongs somewhere. Including me."

Below them, the city lit its lamps one by one, and far to the east, where the border stones lay cracked and silent in the mountain pass, magic moved through the valley of Vell like a tide returning to a shore that had forgotten it — and no one, anywhere, was keeping anything out anymore.

* * *

Sera stayed on the roof until the stars came out, and then a while longer, because Casper had fallen asleep against her shoulder — truly asleep, unguarded, the sleep of a woman with no secrets left to defend.

Sera had killed men for the crown. She had broken her oath, committed treason, emptied a vault under enchanted frost, and helped a thief give a king back his soul. By every law she'd sworn to, she should be in a cell. Instead the king had made her Warden of the Veil — a title invented that afternoon, with duties no one fully understood, which seemed mostly to consist of guarding a memoryless woman above a bakery and reporting to the throne each week on whether the world had ended yet.

It hadn't. That was the strange part. The world kept *beginning* instead.

She looked down at Casper's sleeping face, at the hands curled loosely in her lap — those famous hands, empty now, open always — and felt the old doubt that had led her here settle at last into certainty. Some debts can't be repaid. Some can only be carried forward.

In the morning there would be reports: the eastern lords demanding answers about the fallen veil, the first gifted children born outside the valley in three hundred years, rumors from the far north that the entity's dissolution had left *something* in the ice — a seed, a scar, a question. Aldric would need her. The world, wide and magic-soaked and newly dangerous, would need her.

But tonight the bakery below smelled of bread, and the woman who had given everything away slept soundly against her shoulder, and Sera thought: *let the north keep its secrets one more night.*

Tomorrow they would ride to meet them.
