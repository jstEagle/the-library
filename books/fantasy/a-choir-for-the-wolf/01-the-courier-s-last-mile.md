## 1. The Courier's Last Mile

There were three things Odile Voss knew about the Widow's Stair, the smuggler's road that switchbacked down the eastern face of Thornfell like a drunkard descending a ladder: one, nobody official knew it existed; two, the people who used it paid her well to keep it that way; and three, its name was a lie, because the widow in question had died rich, comfortable, and nowhere near these stairs, which Odile considered the only honest thing about the whole enterprise.

She was halfway down the fourth switchback, counting her profits in her head — thirty crowns for the silk route survey, twelve for keeping the toll-collectors' noses pointed elsewhere, minus what she owed Marta at the Waystone Inn for three nights of stew and one night of regrettable conversation — when she smelled ash where there was no fire.

That was wrong. Thornfell in late autumn was all wet stone and pine rot and the particular mineral stink of snow deciding whether to commit. Ash meant travelers. Travelers on the Widow's Stair meant either smugglers she knew or smugglers she didn't, and both kinds were problems before breakfast.

Odile did what she always did when the road offered her something unexpected: she stopped, crouched behind a shelf of granite, and became furniture.

It was a talent. She'd spent twenty-nine years cultivating it, ever since the day in Coppersmith Ward when she'd realized that the surest way to survive was to be the kind of person no one thought to write down. Not beautiful enough to remember, not ugly enough to remark upon, not loud, not silent, not anything. A woman of average height, average coloring, average everything, whose name appeared on no ledger, whose face appeared on no warrant, whose debts were few and promptly paid precisely so that no creditor had reason to describe her to anyone else. Other girls dreamed of being queens. Odile had dreamed of being a footnote, and by every god's grace, she had achieved it.

The smell of ash grew stronger. Then came a sound she liked considerably less: footsteps, uneven, dragging, accompanied by the wet rattle of someone breathing through a throat full of blood.

Around the bend below her came a man.

He had been a courier once — the grey-and-silver livery of the royal post still clung to his shoulders, though it hung off him now like skin off a plucked bird. He walked by leaning on the rock wall more than his own legs, and he left prints in the frost-mud that were too dark. Blood, Odile noted professionally. A lot of it, and old, which raised the interesting question of how he was still upright.

What raised the *un*interesting question — the kind that made the back of her neck prickle — was the state of his wounds. She could see them as he passed beneath her ledge: great weeping gouges across his chest and arms, but no blade had made them. They were too smooth-edged, too deliberate, curling in spirals like something had been *unwriting* him from the outside in. And where blood had dried along their rims, it hadn't gone brown. It had gone grey. Ash-grey.

Odile had survived the Thornfell mountains by knowing every road and trusting no one, and every instinct she owned now stood up in a body and voted to let this man pass by and become somebody else's problem.

Then he looked up. Directly at her hiding place, forty feet above and off the trail, behind granite, in shadow.

"There you are," he said, and his voice was almost pleasant, almost amused, the voice of a man who had made peace with something enormous. "Voss. The smuggler. I was told — " he coughed, and the cough produced a fine drift of grey powder rather than spit — "I was told you'd be hard to find. You're not, actually. You're very easy to find. That's the trouble."

"Congratulations," Odile said, because her mouth had never once waited for her brain's permission. "You've found a woman who is about to be elsewhere."

"He said you'd say that." The courier smiled. It cracked grey at the corners. "Corvane's men said it too. Said, find the woman who isn't anywhere, because she's the only one who can be somewhere in time." He took another dragging step and then, with the terrible economy of the dying, simply folded, knees first, into the frost-mud. "Please. I don't need long. I don't have long. That's rather the point."

Every sensible fiber in Odile screamed at her to keep walking. So naturally, she climbed down.

Later she would tell herself it was curiosity. Later still, when she understood what curiosity cost, she would tell herself it was nothing of the kind — that some things press themselves into your hands whether you reach for them or not. But in the moment, descending the rocks toward a dying stranger who smelled of cold hearths, she felt only irritation, sharp and clean, at the universe for interrupting her perfectly good morning.

"You're bleeding out on my road," she said, kneeling beside him. "My road. I found it. I maintain it. There are rules."

"I know." He fumbled at his chest, at a leather satchel strapped there under the ruined livery. His fingers wouldn't work properly; they moved like they were forgetting how fingers went. "Rules are why it has to be you. You don't work for anyone. You aren't anyone. Do you understand? Everyone else I could have given this to belongs to *something* — a guild, a crown, a cause. Belonging is how he finds you. You—" the grey smile again, "—you belong to absolutely nothing. It's the most beautiful thing about you."

"That's the least flattering compliment I've ever received," Odile said, "and last winter a man told me I had 'a face that grows on you, like mold.'"

The courier laughed, and the laugh cost him; he sagged against the rock, and for a moment Odile saw the ash spreading further, up his jaw, into his hairline, the wounds on his chest widening with terrible patience, as if time itself were erasing him in strokes. Her stomach turned over. She had seen men die — the passes were not gentle — but she had never seen a man die *sideways*, undone instead of ended.

"The map," he rasped. "Take it. Folded. Left pocket — no, other — yes. Don't open it here. Don't open it near anyone. It's not paper, do you hear me? It's not paper. It remembers. Guild-work. Memory-ink. The cartographers didn't vanish, whatever the histories say — they were *taken* — and this is the last true map of the capital, the last true map of the king, and Corvane cannot allow it to exist, and neither, now, can he allow me to."

"Corvane," Odile repeated flatly. "The mage-regent. The one on all the coins next to our beloved king." Everyone knew Corvane's face; it stared out of every tavern in the realm from between the king's shoulder and the mint-mark, tall and patient and smiling like a man waiting for soup to cool. "You want me to get involved in court politics. Me. I once declined involvement in an argument between my own two hands over whose turn it was to hold the lantern."

"I want you to carry something," the courier said. "That's all carrying ever is. You don't have to believe it. You just have to not burn it." His hand closed around hers — cold, lighter than a hand should be, as though he were already partly smoke — and pressed the folded packet into her palm. It was heavier than paper had any right to be, warm, and it seemed to shift against her fingers like a sleeping animal resettling. "His name," the courier whispered. "You'll need the name. When the ink shows you. Remember the name."

"What name?"

But the courier's eyes had gone past her, up through the pine canopy to some sky only he could see, and he whispered it anyway, soft as snowfall:

"*Aldric.* The king's name is Aldric. He's still in there. Under everything. Tell him — tell him the choir hasn't forgotten the third verse."

And then he came apart.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. No scream, no collapse. He simply loosened, all at once, like a knot untied, and where a man had knelt holding her hand there was a drift of fine grey ash settling gently into the frost-mud, already dispersing, already becoming indistinguishable from the ordinary dust of the road. His livery settled on top of the pile like a shed cocoon. The wind took a little of him and gave him back to the mountain.

Odile sat very still for the length of ten heartbeats, which was as long as she allowed herself for any emotion, professional habit.

"Well," she said finally, to the ash, to the pines, to the universe at large. "*That's going in the complaint.*

* * *

She told herself she would burn it.

This was, in fairness, her plan for most objects: strange letters, threatening letters, love letters, tax assessments. Fire solved the problem of information by converting it into warmth, which was the only form information should take. She had the packet half over her little brass tinderbox on a flat stone above the treeline, afternoon light coming gold through the smoke of her cookfire, when she noticed the ink.

The map was folded into eighths, and the visible eighth showed mountains — the Thornfells, unmistakably, drawn with a loving precision she recognized the way one recognizes a rival's handwriting. But the ink of the ridgelines was *moving*. Slowly, languidly, the way a cat moves in sleep. A river she knew shifted its course a finger's width and then shifted back, as if trying on positions. In the margin, tiny letters surfaced and sank like fish rising to feed: dates, names, altitudes, none of them staying legible long enough to read twice.

Odile held the map at arm's length, the way one holds a spider.

"No," she informed it.

The ink rippled. Where her thumb pressed the vellum, the mountains parted — politely, the way curtains part — revealing beneath them a floor plan of chambers she had never seen, corridors threading through solid rock, and at their heart a small stamped seal: a tower crowned with seven stars. The royal seal. The true one, not the mint's version. And around the seal, in that surfacing-sinking script, words assembling themselves letter by letter, patient as tide:

*he does not know he is lost*

Odile dropped the map on the stone, stood up, and paced a full circle around her campfire, rubbing her face with both hands.

"Right," she said. "Options." Talking to herself was another professional habit; silence invited company, and company invited questions. "Option one: burn it. Except it's warm and it *moves* and burning things that move feels like the opening chapter of a much worse story. Option two: sell it. To whom? Every buyer worth robbing works for the crown, and the crown — " she glanced at the map, which had helpfully begun sketching, unbidden, the floor plan of a throne room — "the crown is apparently the *problem*. Option three: bury it in a hole and never think about it again, which is the option my entire personality is voting for."

The map, unprompted, drew a small, precise picture of a hole, and then drew a line through it.

"Oh, don't you *presume*," Odile snapped.

And that was when the pain came — a bright, biting sting at her left wrist, sudden as a nettle. She hissed and shoved back her sleeve. On the soft skin inside her wrist, rising through the surface like a bruise developing in fast-forward, was a mark: a circle of seven stars around a vertical line, ink-dark, intricate, and unmistakably fresh. It did not hurt anymore once formed. It simply *sat* there, on her body, on the one territory she had kept unmapped for twenty-nine years.

In the margin of the fallen map, the tiny letters rose one final time, serene and horrible:

*claimed. property of the crown. return for collection.*

Odile looked at the mark. At the map. At the thin column of her cookfire smoke rising toward a sky that had, until this morning, contained no obligations whatsoever.

"You know what your problem is?" she told the map, rolling it up with the brisk violence of the deeply rattled. "You're a *commitment*."

She kicked dirt over the fire, stuffed the map into her pack, and started down the mountain at a pace just short of running — toward the lowlands, toward Vell's Row and the free cities, toward anywhere the word "collection" couldn't follow.

Behind her, faint on the wind, she could almost swear she heard the ink whispering names.

One of them sounded like hers.
