## 3. The Singer in the Cellar

Emeric Voss had been singing for his supper in the cellar of the Drowned Lantern for three nights running, and on the third night, a woman with a knife sat down at his table and asked him to stop.

"You're flat," she said, "on purpose. Nobody sings that badly by accident."

He set down his lute with the care of a man disarming himself. "It's a technique. The tavern-keeper pays me less if the patrons stay sober enough to count their coin."

"I've watched you for two nights." She turned her cup but didn't drink from it. "You change the words when soldiers come in. Every song about kings becomes a song about weather. *The old king's crown was heavy as rain.* That's not a drinking song. That's a warning dressed as one."

The cellar of the Drowned Lantern was low-ceilinged and lamplit, all smoke-stained beams and barrels of cheap wine, and Emeric had chosen it precisely because no one important ever came here. He looked at the woman properly now — travel-worn leathers, a sword worn at her hip like it had grown there, eyes the grey-green of mountain water over stone. She had the look of someone who mapped exits without noticing she did it.

"And who are you," he said carefully, "that warnings concern you?"

"Nobody." She said it the way other people said their own names.

"Emeric," he offered, because even disgraced choristers had manners. "Formerly of the Royal Choir of Aldric's Court. Currently of nowhere in particular."

Something moved behind her eyes at *Royal Choir* — quick, guarded, gone. "Odile," she said, after a pause long enough to be its own answer. "Also nobody."

She reached into her coat then, and he thought — absurdly, given the knife, given the way she held her shoulders like a drawn bow — that she was going to show him a warrant. Instead she unfolded a square of vellum and laid it flat between them on the scarred table.

The map was beautiful. Even folded soft with handling, even stained dark along one edge with something he refused to name, it was beautiful: roads that seemed to breathe under the lamplight, ranges rendered in strokes so fine they might have been drawn by frost itself. And there, in the lower corner, half-hidden beneath a cartouche of mountains —

The guild-mark. A compass rose whose points were open hands.

Emeric stopped breathing.

He knew that mark. He had sung beneath it every morning of his childhood, painted on the vaulted ceiling of the Cartographers' Hall where the choir rehearsed, because in the old days the singers and the mapmakers had been two halves of one craft: those who fixed the world in ink, and those who fixed it in memory. His brother Tam had crossed the hall every day to his drafting table while Emeric crossed to the rehearsal dais, and they used to joke that between them they'd hold the whole kingdom still.

Tam, who had left the capital eight months ago carrying nothing but a satchel and a promise to send word.

Tam, whose word had never come.

"Where," Emeric heard himself say, very quietly, "did you get this?"

"A courier gave it to me. On the Thornfell road." Her fingers stayed flat on the map's edge, pinning it like something alive. "He was dying. Wounds no blade made. He pressed this into my hands and said a name, and then he—" She hesitated, and for the first time since sitting down, the woman looked uncertain. "He came apart. Like ash off a log. I told myself I imagined it."

"What name?" Emeric's voice came out strange to him. Thin. Young.

"He didn't finish. Just—*tell Em*—and then nothing."

The cellar went on around them — dice rattling upstairs, a barrel being rolled, somebody laughing too loudly — and Emeric sat inside all of it perfectly still, because if he moved he was going to shatter, and if he shattered he was going to do it loudly, and loud men got noticed, and noticed men got reported, and reported men got visited by the inquisitors with their grey gloves and their patient smiles.

*Tell Em.*

Eight months of silence, and Tam's last act in the world had been to spend his final breath on a message to him. Not to the guild. Not to any of the surviving masters. To his little brother, the failure, the disgrace, the man who'd been dragged out of the royal choir in chains of public laughter for singing the wrong verse at the wrong coronation.

"His name was Tam," Emeric said. "Tamasin Voss. He was my brother."

Odile studied him for a long moment. Whatever she was looking for, she must have found some version of it, because she uncurled slightly — not trust, exactly, but the decision to postpone suspicion. "You said you sang for King Aldric."

"Until four years ago."

"Then sing me something now. Quiet. Here."

"Now? Here?" He gestured at the cellar, at the drunkards, at the general indignity of everything.

"Quietly," she repeated, in a voice that suggested the alternative involved her knife and his dignity parting company.

So Emeric leaned close over the map, as though showing her a detail of the coastlines, and he sang — barely above breath, barely more than shaped air — the first line of the Waking Verse. The old harmony. The one written into the choir's founding charter, the one every chorister learned before they learned scales, the one the masters had made them swear never to perform outside the presence of the crown.

The map answered.

The ink moved. It was subtle — a shimmer, like light through water, like heat off summer stone — but the roads shifted, the rivers rethreaded themselves, and for one heartbeat the vellum showed not the kingdom as it was but the kingdom as it had been: whole, unbroken, its borders drawn in confident gold, its capital crowned with a second ring of walls that no longer existed anywhere but in memory.

Then it stilled. Odile snatched her hand back as if the vellum had bitten her.

"It's never done that before," she whispered. "I've touched it, slept on it, nearly burned it twice. It just lies there. What did you *do*?"

"That wasn't me doing anything." Emeric's hands were shaking. He flattened them against his knees. "That was the song. Odile — may I call you Odile? — that map isn't paper. It's memory-ink. Guild-work. The last of it, I'd wager my voice, which is the only thing I own outright. And it doesn't respond to touch or heat or cleverness. It responds to *remembering*. To the songs that were built to carry remembering."

"The courier said the map shows the true face of the king." She said it fast, low, the words of a woman deciding whether to jump. "I thought he was raving. But you sang at court. Four years ago. Tell me what happened four years ago."

And there it was. The question he had spent four years not answering, drowned nightly in whatever wine the road provided, buried under small songs in small rooms across half the free cities.

Emeric took a breath, and found, to his surprise, that grief had burned away the fear. There was nothing left in him tonight but the truth, and the truth wanted out.

"Four years ago," he said, "King Aldric died. Or mostly died. And the man on the throne now wears his face and signs his decrees and hunts with his falcons, but he is not Aldric. He is a vessel. Corvane hollowed him out — the royal mage, the king's oldest friend, the man who wept so beautifully at the funeral-that-wasn't. Corvane devours memory. It's how he's lived three lifetimes. He ate the king's years whole — his childhood, his coronation, his wife's face, his son's laugh — and what's left is a body that walks and talks and rules, animated by whatever scraps Corvane lets it keep."

"Memory-eating mage," Odile said flatly. "Of course. Why not."

"You don't believe me."

"I believe the courier dissolved into ash and the map moves when you sing. My standards have become extremely flexible." She rubbed her wrist absently, and Emeric noticed — noticed and felt his stomach drop — the faint mark there, like a brand drawn in shadow, a seal he recognized from the palace heraldry. A claim-mark. Crown property. Someone had already tagged her like a deer for winter.

"Why were you disgraced?" she asked. "You said wrong verse, wrong time. What verse?"

"The Waking Verse. In full. At the coronation of the vessel." He smiled, and it hurt his face. "You see, the choir wasn't decoration, whatever the histories claim. The guild and the choir together built a safeguard, generations back. The king's true self — his memories, his *selfness* — couldn't be destroyed outright, not even by Corvane. So the guild bound it into song instead. Buried it deep, below where a devourer could reach, and gave the choir the key. As long as the choir sang the holding-songs — daily, faithfully, in the king's hearing — the buried self stayed anchored. Sleeping, but *his*. Waiting."

"And you sang it at the coronation."

"I sang it at the coronation. Loudly. In front of everyone." He turned his cup in circles. "Corvane couldn't kill me — killing the last keeper of the key would risk unraveling the binding, and our usurper is nothing if not careful. So he had me flogged, stripped of rank, and exiled with a decree that my songs were treason and my memory of them would fade 'naturally.' He's been waiting four years for my memory to rot on its own. He sent inquisitors to hurry it along whenever I lingered anywhere too long." He nodded at her wrist. "You've met his patience."

Odile was quiet. Then: "The courier. Your brother. He was guild."

"Draughtsman, third chair. Better with coastlines than people." Emeric's throat tightened. "He must have learned what Corvane intended — maybe the guild discovered the harvesting, maybe that's why the guild *vanished*, why there hasn't been new guild-work in four years, why—" He stopped, because the arithmetic was assembling itself in his chest with terrible clarity. "They weren't scattered. They were *harvested*. Every cartographer who could draw memory-ink, eaten to keep the binding from being renewed. And Tam ran. Ran with the only finished map, the master-map, the one that holds what the guild knew about the king's buried self. And he spent his dying breath trying to get it to me."

"To *you*. Because you're the key."

"Because I'm the fool with the voice," Emeric corrected, but gently, because it was also true.

Outside, somewhere above them, boots on the Lantern's front steps — several pairs, unhurried, the tread of men paid to be thorough. The tavern noise stuttered, resumed, hushed. Odile's hand went to her knife without her face changing at all.

"Inquisitors?" Emeric breathed.

"Or tax collectors. Either way, I'm leaving through the cellar door, and you're welcome to come." She was already standing, folding the map into her coat with the tenderness she'd shown nothing else all evening. "But understand what you're asking, chorister. The capital is nine hundred miles through country that wants us dead, and the thing wearing your king's face will send everything it has once it knows the map exists. I've survived twenty-eight years by being unmapped, unremarkable, and alone. You're proposing I walk into the brightest lantern in the world carrying the one object it's hunting."

"Yes," Emeric said.

"You're not even going to pretend to weigh it?"

"No." He stood too, and slung his battered lute across his back, and found that his hands had gone steady — steadier than they'd been in four years. Grief, he was discovering, made an excellent ballast. "My brother died saying my name. The least I can do is arrive at the throne room singing it. I know the waking-song, Odile. All of it. I'm the only one left alive who does. If Aldric's buried self can be woken — truly woken, whole — Corvane loses his vessel, his throne, and every stolen year he's built his rule on. One song. That's all I'm good for. Let me be good for it."

She looked at him for a long moment, this woman who trusted nothing, and Emeric braced for the dismissal, the knife-point logic, the door.

Instead she said, "Can you run in those boots?"

"...Pardon?"

"Your boots. They're court boots. Soft soles, pretty stitching. Can you run in them?"

He glanced down. "I can run in anything."

"We'll see." She was already moving toward the cellar's far wall, where a delivery hatch sat behind a rack of casks, and her voice drifted back low and dry. "Keep up, chorister. And if anyone asks — you're my husband, you're simple, and you owe me money."

"That's *three* lies."

"Yes," said Odile Voss, shouldering open the hatch onto the cold night of Vell's Row, "but they're memorable ones. Come on — the map just moved again, and I don't think it's waiting for us."

Behind them, up the stairs, the boots reached the taproom. Emeric followed the woman with the knife into the dark, singing under his breath the first line of the Waking Verse, and far away — though neither of them could have said how they knew — something buried beneath a crown stirred in its sleep, and listened.
