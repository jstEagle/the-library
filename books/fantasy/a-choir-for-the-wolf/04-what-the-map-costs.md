## 4. What the Map Costs

The inquisitors reached the pass at dusk, and Odile knew it before she saw them, the way she always knew weather coming—by the way the birds went quiet and the light turned the color of old bruises.

"Down," she hissed, and hauled Emeric off the trail by his sleeve, into the scree and the shadow of a leaning boulder that some ancient flood had left there like a door propped open by a careless god.

Emeric went down with more grace than she expected from a man who had spent ten years indoors singing for his supper. He folded himself against the stone and did not ask questions, which she was beginning to suspect was not cowardice but habit—a chorister learns to be silent when the conductor's hand lifts. She filed that away under *useful* and put her eye to the gap between boulder and hillside.

Three riders on the switchback below, moving wrong. Not like men riding a road—like men reading one. Their heads turned in unison at each bend, and where their gazes lingered, frost bloomed on the rocks out of season. Inquisitors. Corvane's hounds. The mark on her wrist throbbed in time with her heartbeat, a hot little brand shaped like a crown seen through a keyhole.

"They're following the mark," Emeric breathed beside her. "Not you. It."

"I know." She pulled her sleeve down over it as if fabric had ever once in her life stopped anything. "Which means I can't outrun it. It's tied to me like a leash, and they're holding the other end."

"Then we don't run along their road." He said it simply, the way he'd say *the third verse modulates here*, and something in Odile bristled and then, annoyingly, settled.

"And what road would you suggest, chorister? Because I have mapped every pass in Thornfell twice, once for coin and once to prove I could, and every route south from here funnels into the Serpent's Gorge, which is exactly where three men who read roads will be waiting."

Emeric was quiet a moment. Then, carefully, he reached toward the map case at her hip—not touching it, just indicating, the way one indicates a sleeping dog. "May I?"

"You may look. If it bites, it bites me first. That's the arrangement."

"That's a terrible arrangement."

"It's mine, though. That's what makes it terrible."

She drew the map out into the last grey light. It lay across her knees like a living thing pretending to sleep, its ink shifting in slow tides, coastlines breathing, mountain ranges rearranging themselves when she wasn't looking directly at them. Guild-work. Memory-ink. The last of the cartographers' guild, drawn in the only ink that had ever told the truth about King Aldric—and charging for every truth like a moneylender with a poet's soul.

"Show me," Emeric murmured, "what isn't on any other map."

Odile pressed two fingers to the vellum, over the tangle of peaks between them and the gorge.

The ink moved.

It pooled and parted like water around a dropped stone, and beneath the printed mountains another range surfaced—the same mountains, but *more* of them, ridges and hollows no surveyor had ever troubled to record, and threaded through them, fine as a hair, a line. A path. It wound up past the treeline, across a shoulder of stone no goat would claim, and down through a cleft so narrow the ink had to squeeze to fit it.

"There," she whispered. "Saints' teeth. There's a road through the Gray Mantle. Nobody maps the Gray Mantle because nothing lives up there and the rock sheds scree like a dog sheds fur in summer. But if this is true—"

"It's true," Emeric said. "My brother trusted it with his life."

"Your brother trusted it with his death, more's the point." She heard her own voice go flat and practical, the voice she used for price negotiations and other forms of grief management. "Fine. We climb tonight while the hounds are still reading the low road. By morning we'll be above them, and by tomorrow night—"

She stopped.

Because the map had begun to move again, and this time it was moving *toward* her.

A thread of ink lifted off the vellum like a filament of black spider silk, delicate, almost tender, and touched the back of her hand where it rested on the parchment. It did not feel like much. That was the horror of it—it felt like a moth landing. Like nothing. Like a summer evening years ago and someone calling her name from a garden gate.

"Odile." Emeric's voice, sharp now. "What's it doing?"

"Paying itself," she said, and hated how steady she made it sound. "I asked it for a secret. Secrets cost."

"What does it—"

And then her mother's face was gone.

Not the memory of the face—the face itself, the actual thing, the whole warm architecture of it: the crooked smile with the chipped tooth, the laugh lines deep as irrigation channels, the way her eyes crinkled half-shut when she was trying not to laugh at something Odile had said, the smell of bread and lamp oil and the particular roughness of her hands—

Gone. Lifted out of her like a splinter drawn clean, and where it had been there was only a smooth, polite absence. She knew she'd had a mother. Everyone has a mother; it's practically a legal requirement. She knew the woman had raised her in a village whose name she still had, taught her to read stars and strangers, died of a fever when Odile was nine. All the facts remained, tidy as ledger entries.

But when she reached for the face behind the facts, her mind's eye found a blank, a silhouette, a figure cut from dark paper. A mother-shaped hole wearing a dress she couldn't describe.

"Oh," Odile said. Very quietly. Very reasonably.

Then the reasonable part of her did the sensible thing, which was to fold the map along its living creases, stow it, stand up, and say, "We move. Now. While the light lasts."

"Odile—" Emeric caught her arm. His grip was gentle and absolutely immovable, which she would later learn was simply how he did everything. "What did it take? What did you lose?"

"A fact," she said. "It took a fact. I have plenty."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I give for free." She twisted free of his hand. "Come on. Scree doesn't care about our feelings and neither does gravity."

They climbed.

* * *

The Gray Mantle earned its name within the hour. Fog came off the high snowfields in slow grey rivers, and the world shrank to a radius of ten feet: Odile's boots finding the path the map had promised, Emeric's breath ragged behind her, the occasional groan of settling stone. Twice the slope shed sheets of shale around them, hissing down into white nothing, and twice Odile stood perfectly still and waited for the mountain to finish its tantrum.

"You're good at this," Emeric panted, somewhere behind her left shoulder.

"I'm good at everything. It's exhausting."

"No, I mean—you walk like someone who's done this alone a long time. You check the ground ahead and the sky behind without seeming to do either. My brother moved like that."

That landed somewhere she didn't want it to land. "Your brother is dead."

"Yes."

"And you're following his ghost up a mountain with a woman you've known four days."

"Five," Emeric said. "You counted wrong. You were counting while pretending not to count, which throws off the arithmetic."

Odile snorted despite herself, and the fog swallowed the sound, and somehow that made it easier to keep talking. "He pressed this thing into my hands and called my name. Yours, I mean. He said *find Emeric*, and then he came apart like a doused fire. Why you? Why does a courier's dying thought go to a disgraced choirboy in Vell's Row?"

"Because of what I sang." Emeric's voice changed—flattened, the way voices do when they've been rehearsing grief so long it comes out smooth. "In the palace, before. The choir didn't just perform for Aldric. We *held* him. Corvane eats memory, and a king's memory is a kingdom's worth of food, so the guild trained us—certain songs, certain harmonies, sung at the right hours, they anchor a mind. Keep it too heavy to swallow. I was the anchor-lead. When Corvane took the throne, he silenced us one voice at a time. I ran. Tomas stayed, became a courier, carried the guild's last work out of the capital piece by piece." A pause, filled with wind. "He got the map out. He couldn't get himself out."

Odile walked three steps in silence. Four. Five.

"He chose well, then," she said finally. "Sending it to you."

"You think so?"

"I think most people, handed a thing like this, would sell it or burn it or sit down and cry about it. You climbed a mountain with it instead. That's choosing well." She glanced back. The fog had thinned enough to show his face, pale and earnest and absurdly young-looking for a man who'd lost a brother and a career and a king. "Don't let it go to your head. Most people also don't sing at rocks."

"The rocks appreciate it."

"The rocks are being polite."

They gained the shoulder of the ridge as full dark fell, and there—impossibly, exactly where the ink had promised—a cleft in the rock, narrow as a doorway, descending through the mountain's spine. Below it, far below, she could see the Serpent's Gorge glittering with campfires. The inquisitors' fires, patient as spiders. Waiting on a road no one would take.

"We sleep here," Odile decided. "First light, we're through the cleft and down the far side before they've finished their breakfast."

They made a cold camp in the lee of the boulder, sharing a blanket because the alternative was freezing separately, which even Odile's pride could see was stupid. Emeric produced a stub of candle and, instead of lighting it, held it and talked softly into the dark—about Vell's Row, about the cathedral choir's rivalries, about a conductor who threw batons with genuine accuracy. Odile found herself answering. Found herself saying things about Thornfell passes and smuggler's rates and the exact weight of a bribe that opens a border gate. Small things. True things. She noticed, distantly, that she was doing it, and did not stop.

Somewhere in the middle of the night, half-asleep against cold stone, she said: "Her name was Maren."

Emeric didn't move. Didn't ask who. He just waited, the way he waited, and after a while she kept going.

"My mother. I know her name, her dates, the village, the fever. I know everything except—" Her jaw tightened. "Except the part that mattered. The map took her face tonight, and I keep reaching for it and there's just... paper where she should be. A cutout. And the worst part, the truly pathetic part, is I can't even remember whether I looked like her."

"Odile."

"Don't say something kind. I'll bite."

"I'm going to say something useful instead." His voice was low and very steady. "Tomorrow, and every day after, before you touch that map—we decide together what a question costs. We count what you can't afford to lose, and we guard those things out loud, so the ink can't sneak up on either of us. Your mother's name stays spoken every morning. Mine too, if you want it. Memory keeps better when it's shared—that's the first thing the guild teaches, and the last thing they forgot."

Odile stared at the faint shape of him in the starlight. "Why? You barely know me. Every mile we spend of me makes your errand cheaper. A half-erased guide is a disposable guide."

"Because," Emeric said, "when that thread of ink touched you tonight, you went white as the snowfield, and then you stood up and led us up a mountain anyway. And because you counted five days when it was six, which means you've been counting since the cellar, which means you're keeping track of me the way you keep track of things that matter." He paused. "Also because my brother died carrying this, and I intend to see it delivered, and I would rather deliver it alongside someone than in spite of someone. That's all. Take it or leave it."

Odile lay back against the stone and looked at the stars, which were the one map she'd never needed ink to read.

"Maren," she said again, testing it, hanging the name in the air between them like a lantern. "Chipped tooth. Terrible singing. Excellent bread."

"Excellent bread," Emeric repeated solemnly, as if entering it into a ledger. "Spoken and witnessed. It'll keep."

And it did keep—mostly. But at first light, when she unrolled the map to chart the descent, the ink had already shifted to meet her, patient and hungry, and coiled at the edge of the vellum she found new writing that had not been there before. Fine, elegant script, in a hand she was beginning to know too well:

*One face purchased. Balance remaining: considerable.*

Below it, a second line, fresher, the ink still wet:

*The singer's songs will not save her. Bring both to the capital. — C.*
