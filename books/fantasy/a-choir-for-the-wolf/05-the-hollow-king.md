## 5. The Hollow King

Emeric dreamed in the old way, the chorister's way: lying flat on his back on a stone floor that smelled of candlewax and cold, hands folded over his breastbone like a man already laid out for burial, letting the song carry him down.

He had not meant to sleep. They had stopped in a shepherd's bothy above the tree line, and Odile had taken first watch without asking, which was her way of saying she didn't trust him yet and probably never would. He had lain down intending only to rest his eyes. But the map was there in the room, folded shut beside her knee, and it hummed. Faintly. Like a struck bell that hadn't finished deciding whether to stop.

The humming found the shape of the song he knew best, and the song pulled him under.

* * *

Five years ago. The Hall of Voices, beneath the palace of Aldermere, where the royal choir rehearsed at dawn because the king liked to say that a kingdom should wake to singing rather than to bells.

Emeric stood in his place — third from the left, second row, where a voice of middling power but reliable pitch belonged — and watched King Aldric come down the stairs alone.

That was the first wrong thing. The king never came alone. He came with his shadow-tall wife, Queen Isolde, who could not sing a note but attended every rehearsal anyway because she said music was the one honest thing left in a palace. He came with Corvane, the court mage, drifting behind him like a man following his own smell.

Tonight the king came down the stairs by himself, and he came down them wrong. Not stiff — puppets are stiff, and everyone knows to look for stiffness. He came down them *smooth*, too smooth, each step exactly the length of every other step, as though someone had wound him and set him walking.

"Your Majesty?" Emeric's master, Choirmaster Bellamy, a round man with a voice like a landslide and a heart to match, stepped forward. "We weren't told you'd attend."

"I attend," the king said, "every night now."

His voice was right. That was the horror of it. It was Aldric's warm baritone, the voice that had laughed at Emeric's nervous first audition and told him, *sing louder, boy, the rafters won't break.* But nothing moved behind it. Emeric had spent his whole life watching faces while he sang — you had to, to know what a song was doing to a room — and the king's face was a room with no one home.

"Where is the queen?" Bellamy asked.

"The queen is resting." A pause, precise as a metronome. "She will rest for some time."

And behind the king, at the top of the stairs, Corvane appeared. He did not descend. He simply became present at the top of them, the way frost becomes present on a window, and he smiled at the choir with the fondness of a farmer looking at full granaries.

"Gentlemen," Corvane said. "Sing something lovely. His Majesty has forgotten how."

* * *

They sang. What else does a choir do? Bellamy raised his hands, and thirty men opened their mouths, and Emeric sang the waking-song — the great old cycle the guild had written generations ago, the one whose true purpose he had learned at nineteen when Bellamy took him aside after rehearsals and showed him the score's hidden second layer.

"You think we sing to please the king," Bellamy had said. "We do not. We sing to *keep* him. Every crowned head in this line carries two selves — the one the world sees and the deep one, the true one, that sleeps unless woken. The songs hold the deep self in place, like moorings hold a ship. Forget that, and the day comes when someone offers the crown an anchor made of nothing."

Now Emeric sang the mooring-song at a hollow man and felt it go through him like wind through a broken window. Nothing caught. Nothing held. The king sat in his chair with his hands on his knees and listened the way furniture listens.

Afterward, Corvane thanked them personally. He shook each singer's hand. His grip was cool and dry, and where it touched, Emeric felt a small *tug*, like a page being turned somewhere inside him.

Three days later, Bassus, the oldest bass, forgot his own wedding anniversary. A week after that, he forgot his wife. A month after that, he forgot how to swallow, and they buried him quietly, and Corvane sent flowers.

One singer at a time. Never all at once — attention draws eyes. Tenor by tenor, bass by bass, the choir thinned, and each man who faded was replaced by a new hire who sang beautifully and asked no questions, and within two years Emeric stood in a hall full of strangers singing the mooring-songs into a king who was no longer there to be moored.

Bellamy lasted longest. He lasted until the night he confronted Corvane in the Hall of Voices with the original guild score in his fists, shouting about anchors and ships and what had been done to his singers. Emeric heard it from the corridor. He heard Bellamy's voice rise, and crack, and then — this was the part that woke Emeric screaming for years afterward — he heard Bellamy's voice continue, calm and pleasant, mid-sentence, saying words Bellamy would never have said, in a cadence that belonged to no one.

By morning, Choirmaster Bellamy remembered only that he loved the court mage very much and had always, always been loyal.

Emeric ran that night. He took nothing but the stolen rehearsal copy of the score and his own voice, and he had hated himself for five years for running, and he had told himself a hundred times that one man's voice against Corvane was a candle against a glacier.

Then his brother Tam had died on a mountain road with a map pressed into a stranger's hands, and the glacier, it turned out, had cracks in it after all.

* * *

The dream-song turned, as dreams do, and Emeric stood in the throne room he had fled, except it was flooded with light the color of memory-ink, and the map hung in the air before him unrolled and vast as a sky. And across its face walked figures — hundreds of them, thousands, small and precise, drawn in that moving ink — and each figure carried a rolled vellum on their back, and each vellum was leaking.

Leaking upward. Into a shape at the center of the map that Emeric recognized with a lurch: the palace at Aldermere, rendered in perfect detail, drinking.

He tried to read the names on the little figures. The dream would not let him. It only let him count.

He counted past a thousand before the song released him.

* * *

He woke gasping on the bothy floor with Odile's boot prodding his ribs.

"You were singing," she said. Not accusing. Worse — curious. "In your sleep. Badly."

"That's the traditional way," Emeric croaked. "All the great composers snored in key."

"It wasn't snoring. It was—" She hesitated, and Odile hesitating was rarer than Odile apologizing, which was itself rarer than snow in high summer. "It sounded like the map."

He sat up. Gray dawn light through the smoke-hole. She had the map open across her knees, and she was staring at the margins — the narrow borders of decorated text that ringed the cartography proper, the flourishes every guild map carried, the little scrolls and vines and marginalia that nobody ever read.

"Emeric," she said. "These aren't decorations."

He came to kneel beside her. The morning was cold enough that their breath mingled over the vellum, hers sharp and quick, his still ragged from the dream.

"What I took for vines," she said, tapping one border with a fingernail, "is script. Guild shorthand — my mother used it, smugglers' cartography, compressed notation. You can fit a whole ledger into what looks like a flourish if you know the hand." Her finger moved along the margin. "It's a census."

"A what?"

"A census. Names. Look." She turned the map toward the light, angling it so the ink shifted, and the shifting revealed what stillness hid: rows upon rows of tiny names woven into the borders, each name followed by a date and a number. "Here — 'Maren Ostell, guild-second, harvested 4th of Embermonth, yield: forty-one years.' And here—"

She stopped. Her finger rested on a line, and Emeric watched her face do something he had never seen it do. Not fear. Fear he'd seen plenty of, worn like a working coat. This was smaller and worse.

"'Tam Vell,'" she read quietly. "'Courier, guild-sworn. Harvested—'" She looked up at him. "Your brother isn't just dead. He's *in here*. What Corvane took out of him — the map kept it. All of them did. Every courier, every guildsman, every—" She turned pages of margin that shouldn't have been pages, the vellum folding in ways vellum doesn't. "Emeric, the guild didn't vanish. Everyone says the cartographer-guild vanished forty years ago, scattered, extinct. They didn't scatter. They were *harvested*. One at a time. Slowly. The same way your choir was."

The bothy was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a shepherd's dog barked twice at nothing and thought better of it.

"Forty years," Emeric said. His voice came out strange. "Corvane has been doing this for forty years. Not since he took the throne. Since *before*."

"Since long before. Your king was just the largest vessel. The final cask." Odile's finger traced the central figure on the map — the palace, drinking — and her jaw tightened. "Every year, a few names. A few memories. Nobody notices a trickle. You can empty a lake with a trickle, if you're patient and no one watches the shoreline." She looked up, and for once there was no armor in her expression at all. "I've spent my life staying off maps because maps mean someone owns the territory. I never once wondered who owned the *mapmakers*."

Emeric looked down at the margins, at the thousands of tiny names, at Tam's name among them, and the grief he had been carrying since Thornfell changed shape in his chest. It had been a private grief, a brother-sized grief. Now it was a cathedral.

"He has an army," Emeric said slowly, thinking aloud. "Everyone talks about his army, his inquisitors, his passes closed with soldiers. But that's not the weapon. That's the fence around it. The weapon is—" he touched the drinking palace at the map's heart "—this. Forty years of stolen lives, all of it stored, all of it *his*. That's why no rebellion holds. That's why the free cities argue and stall and do nothing. Half the councilors arguing in Vell's Row are probably running on borrowed years themselves and don't know it."

Odile went very still.

"Emeric," she said. "When the council demanded proof. When I offered the map." Her hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to her own wrist — to the pale ghost of the inquisitor's mark burned there weeks ago. "What if the reason the council wouldn't act isn't cowardice? What if it's *hunger*? What if Corvane's reach is already inside those walls, already sipping?"

Outside, far below the bothy, faint as a thought, a bell began to toll from the valley chapel. Then a second bell, from a different village. Then a third, arrhythmic, urgent — the pattern refugees used, the pattern that meant *move, they're coming.*

Odile was on her feet before the third bell finished, map snapping shut, and the look she gave Emeric over the top of it was the old look again, armored and sharp — but she tucked the map inside her coat, against her heart, rather than into her pack.

"They'll have sealed the southern passes by noon," she said. "Which means the map gets to choose our road again. And Emeric—" she paused at the door, dawn behind her, "—whatever it charges me next, don't let me agree to pay it without knowing what I'm buying."

She went out into the cold. Emeric stayed a moment longer, kneeling by the dead fire, looking at the place where the map had lain.

One thousand names he had counted in the dream. The margins held ten times that.

And the road south was the only road left, and it ran straight through the drinker's throat.
