## 6. Free Cities, Chained Hands

The Council of Vell's Row met in a hall built to look humble and failed at it. The ceiling was plain timber, the benches were honest oak, and every single one of them had cost more than Odile would earn in ten years of mapping smuggler's roads. She'd sat in enough back rooms to know expensive modesty when she saw it. It was the political equivalent of a knife worn openly: *we could afford to threaten you, but we're choosing not to, aren't you grateful.*

Nine councilors sat behind the long table. Eight wore merchant gray or guild blue. The ninth wore the deep green of the Cartographers' Compact, which was either an insult or a joke, given what was rolled up on the table between them.

"Madam Voss," said the chairman, a soft-bellied man named Halvard whose rings caught the lamplight like small fish, "the Compact is a *trade* organization. We broker routes. We certify harbors. We do not—"

"Depose kings," Odile finished. "I heard you the first four times."

"Then I'm not sure why we're still discussing it."

"Because your harbor masters are already discussing it." She let that sit. "Three of your grain ships turned back from Corvane's coast last month. Your toll roads through the eastern passes are closed. You can pretend this isn't your war, Master Halvard, but it's already eating your margins, and merchants notice hunger before anyone else does."

A murmur ran down the table. Emeric, standing beside her with his chorister's hands folded and his face arranged into something patient, shifted his weight almost imperceptibly — his way of saying *you're enjoying this too much*. She probably was. It was better than being afraid, and she was doing both.

"The map," Halvard said, and here his voice went careful, the way men's voices go when they approach something they want very badly, "is a remarkable artifact. Guild work. Pre-Disappearance. If its claims are true—"

"Its claims are true."

"—if its claims are true, then it belongs in a vault. Under seal. Studied by qualified hands." He spread his palms. "Surrender it to the Council's keeping, Madam Voss, and we will act. Armies take time to raise, but they raise faster with certainty behind them. Give us certainty."

Odile looked at the map.

It lay closed on the table, a plain leather rectangle, unremarkable as a hymnal. But she could feel it the way you feel a hand resting on the back of your neck. For weeks it had been taking from her in small installments — her mother's face, her first winter alone, the name of the shepherd who'd taught her the high passes. Each secret it yielded, it collected its fee. And now these nine comfortable people wanted her to hand over the ledger itself.

"You understand what it costs to read it," she said.

"We understand it takes memories. A regrettable price—"

"Not *takes*. Trades. And the trade runs both directions along the same thread." She kept her voice level, though her wrist — marked, claimed, branded with the crown's inquisitor's sigil that no amount of washing had faded — throbbed as if it knew it was being discussed. "If I surrender it, whoever opens it next pays in their own past. Or worse — if it's opened by someone Corvane owns, he doesn't need to hunt me anymore. He just reads everything I've spent, and everything I am, out of your nice secure vault."

Halvard smiled the smile of a man who has already counted someone else's money. "The Council would ensure it was opened by *qualified* hands."

"Whose?"

Silence, of the expensive kind.

Emeric stepped forward then, and Odile felt the room recalibrate around him the way rooms did — he had that quality, the trained stillness of a man who had once held two thousand voices in tune with a lifted finger. "Master Halvard. May I ask what the Council knows of the Disappearance? Of how the guild actually ended?"

"The guild failed, Master—"

"Emeric. Formerly First Voice of the Royal Choir." He let the title land; several councilors flinched, because a disgraced royal chorister was a walking scandal and everyone present knew exactly why he'd been disgraced. "The guild didn't fail. It was harvested. Every cartographer who drew in memory-ink was taken, and their craft was taken with them, and the man who took them sits on a throne made of other people's years. I sang for that throne. I know its appetite." He paused. "You want proof? The map *is* the proof. But proof you lock away is just treasure with extra steps."

"Master Emeric," said the councilor in green, the Compact woman, dry as old vellum, "with respect, you were stripped of office for claiming precisely this. Sedition, the writ said."

"I was stripped of office for singing a song the king asked me never to stop singing." His voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse for them. "I've been wrong about many things in my life. Not about that."

Odile watched him and thought, not for the first time, that grief had rebuilt him into something more useful than ambition ever had. His brother had died on a mountain road so this map could reach free hands. He wasn't going to let it end up in a drawer.

Halvard cleared his throat. "The Council's position remains. Surrender the artifact, receive our support—"

"No," Odile said.

"Madam Voss—"

"No." She put her hand flat on the leather, and the map warmed under her palm like a living thing, because it was one. "Here's my counteroffer, and it's generous, so listen closely. I'll open the map *here*, in front of all of you, and show you what your king looks like under his skin. You pay nothing. I pay. That's the whole arrangement, and it's already ruinous, so don't haggle."

She didn't wait for permission. She unfolded it.

The ink moved. It always moved, but in the council chamber, under nine pairs of eyes, it seemed to wake fully — coastlines breathing, borders shifting like sleeping animals, and there, in the heart of the realm, the capital rendered not as streets but as anatomy. And above the palace, drawn in strokes that hurt to look at directly, the true face of King Aldric: young, tired, *present*, sealed beneath a second figure that crouched over him like smoke poured into a bottle. Corvane. Feeding.

Someone gasped. The Compact woman stood up so fast her chair fell.

"That's—" Halvard's voice cracked. "That's impossible. That's forgery—"

"It's memory-ink," Odile said. "It can't forge. It can only remember. Ask your Compact colleague what that means."

But the Compact woman wasn't listening to Halvard. She was staring at the map with an expression Odile recognized from mirrors — the look of a professional watching her entire discipline resurrected in front of her. "The binding," she whispered. "The guild's master-binding. I've only seen fragments. This is—"

Doors at the far end of the hall opened.

Not slammed. Opened — quietly, courteously, which was worse. Six figures entered in travel-stained gray, hoods back, faces pleasant, and Odile's marked wrist went cold enough to ache. Inquisitors. Three days' ride from the nearest pass, and here they were, strolling into a council session like tax assessors.

"Apologies for the interruption," said the lead one, a woman with a kind, forgettable face. "We're seeking a fugitive carrying stolen crown property. We won't be a moment." Her gaze found Odile's wrist without hunting for it. Of course it did. The mark called to its own.

The councilors looked at Odile.

And here was the thing she'd known was coming since the pass, since the cellar, since the courier dissolved into ash in her hands: they looked at her the way people look at a lit fuse. Halvard's eyes went to the inquisitors, then to the map, then to the trade routes he was imagining closing forever, and Odile watched him do the arithmetic and arrive at the answer she'd have arrived at herself, honestly, in his place.

"Master Halvard," she said quietly. "Don't."

"Madam Voss, the Council must consider—"

"*Don't.*"

It was Emeric who moved. Not toward the door — toward the center of the floor, where every eye already was. And then he did the last thing anyone in that room expected, the thing that ended whatever remained of his old life with a clean snap:

He began to sing.

No accompaniment. No choir. Just his voice, unaccompanied, filling the vaulted humility of the council hall — and Odile felt it land somewhere beneath her ribs, in the place where the map lived, in the place where her spent memories had left hollows. It was the waking-song, or the seed of it, the phrase he'd sung once in a cellar to prove who he was. The inquisitors stopped. Even they, it seemed, had instincts older than their orders.

"This voice," Emeric said, letting the last note decay naturally, "swore oaths to the crown of Aldric. I hereby unsay them. Every title, every pension, every protection." He looked at Halvard, and his gentleness was gone, replaced by something that had walked out of a burning palace and kept walking. "You wanted to hand her to them to keep your roads open. Understand what you'd be handing them. Not a fugitive. The only proof in the world that your king is alive and screaming."

The lead inquisitor sighed, the way one sighs at children. "This is treason, Master Emeric."

"Yes," he said. "Finally."

What happened next happened fast. The Compact woman overturned her chair getting to the side door — shouting, of all things, for the harbor guard. Halvard discovered an urgent need to be elsewhere. And Odile grabbed the map, grabbed Emeric's sleeve, and ran, because she was Odile Voss, and running was the one art she'd never surrendered to anyone.

They came out into the fish-market lane with gulls screaming overhead and the harbor wind smelling of salt and rope, and Emeric was laughing — actually laughing, breathless, a sound she'd heard from him maybe twice.

"Treason," he said. "I've wanted to commit that for six years."

"Don't celebrate yet." She nodded back at the council hall, where gray figures were emerging with terrible patience. "Six of them. One map. And every road out of this city except—"

She stopped.

Because the map, warm against her chest inside her coat, had begun to unfold on its own — a corner lifting, ink crawling, drawing a line she hadn't asked for. A road. Out of the city, south, through country she knew and hated: straight into the heartland of the usurper himself.

And beneath the road, in fresh-drawn script, the map wrote its price.

* * *

Odile read it twice. Then she folded the map shut, hard, as if it were a mouth.

"What?" Emeric said. "What does it want?"

"Nothing yet." She started walking, fast, toward the docks. "It's offering. There's a difference, and the difference is that I haven't said yes."

Behind them, unhurried as tide, the inquisitors came out into the light.
