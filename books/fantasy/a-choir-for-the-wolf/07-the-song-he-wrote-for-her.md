## 7. The Song He Wrote for Her

The waystation at Greyfen Hollow had been abandoned for a decade, which by Odile's standards made it a palace. It had a roof—mostly—and a hearth, and walls thick enough to muffle the wind coming down off the Serrated Teeth. It did not have a lock, but Odile had wedged a chair under the door handle and declared it defensible, the way she declared everything defensible: briskly, as if daring the world to argue.

Emeric had learned not to argue. Mostly.

"Sleep," she said now, feeding the last of their dry wood to the fire. "You sing tomorrow whether your voice is ready or not."

"I'm always ready."

"You snored like a landslide last night. That is not the sound of readiness."

"That was *harmonizing*," he said, with dignity, "in my sleep. Some choristers pay good coin for that discipline."

She gave him the look—the one that said she was cataloguing his nonsense in some internal ledger of things she would never admit to finding endearing—and rolled into her bedroll with her back to the fire, the map wrapped against her chest like a swaddled infant. She'd taken to sleeping with it there. He understood why. It was the only thing in the world that couldn't be stolen from her without taking her with it.

He was three verses into a lullaby he half-remembered from the cathedral choir when the first rider's horn sounded on the ridge.

* * *

There were six of them, maybe seven—inquisitors moved in sevens, Corvane liked the symmetry of it—and they came down the pass with torches, which meant they weren't trying to be quiet, which meant they didn't think quiet was necessary.

Odile was awake before the second horn note. She was already at the window slit, counting, when Emeric got his boots on.

"Back way?" he asked.

"There is no back way. There's a woodshed, a privy, and a cliff." She paused. "I'm open to suggestions regarding the privy."

"The privy it is."

But the inquisitors were faster than privies. The first torch arced over the roof and landed in the dry bracken beside the woodshed, and the wind did the rest, and within four minutes the old waystation was less a building than a bellows.

They retreated to the stone-floored root cellar beneath it, because Odile had scouted it hours earlier—"always know where the ground keeps its secrets," she'd said, and he was beginning to understand that this was her entire philosophy of existence—and now they crouched in the dark among turnips while the floorboards overhead began to glow.

And then the map woke up.

It happened the way it always happened when danger came close: the ink stirred, restless, hungry, sensing opportunity. Emeric felt it through Odile's coat where she clutched it—a warmth, a pulse, like a cat pressing against a hand it intends to bite.

"No," Odile whispered. "Not now. Not *here*."

But the map had found something. He could see it even in the near-darkness: lines surfacing along the vellum, silver-bright, a route out of Greyfen Hollow that no surveyor had ever walked. An escape. The map was offering her a way out.

And the map never gave anything away.

"It wants payment," Emeric said quietly.

"I know what it wants." Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice she used when terror was being filed neatly somewhere out of sight. "It can wait until we're clear."

"It won't wait. It never waits once it starts showing you something. You told me—it takes while it gives. That's how the guild built it. Payment on delivery."

Overhead, a beam groaned and split. Smoke poured down the cellar stairs in a slow grey tongue. Somewhere above, an inquisitor's voice chanted the finding-verse, the one that smelled out memory like a hound smells blood.

Odile looked at the map. Then at the burning ceiling. Then at the map again.

"If I take the route," she said, "it'll take something big. Small secrets cost small. A way out of a burning building in the middle of a manhunt—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

"Then don't take it," Emeric said. "We dig. There's a root wall here, earth behind it. We can—"

"With our hands? In the time it takes this roof to come down?" She laughed, short and terrible. "Emeric. I've spent my whole life making sure nothing about me could be traced. No records, no debts, no names in any book. Do you know why?"

"You told me. Invisibility is survival."

"I told you the *conclusion*." The smoke was thicker now; her eyes were wet, and she'd have denied it was smoke alone. "I've never told anyone the reason."

The map pulsed in her arms, patient as a moneylender.

And Emeric—who had spent his life learning exactly one skill, the skill of holding a self together with sound—did the only thing he knew how to do.

He began to sing.

* * *

It wasn't the waking-song. It wasn't anything from the royal repertoire. It was a thing he'd been carrying unfinished in his chest since Vell's Row, since the night Odile had counted inquisitors through a window slit and he'd realized, with the helpless clarity of a man falling, that he was watching the bravest person he would ever know pretend very hard not to be afraid.

He'd written eleven bars of it. He'd never found the twelfth.

He sang the eleven he had.

The song was simple—that was the craft of it, simplicity, a melody a frightened mind could hold onto like a rail in floodwater. It went up where panic goes up, and came down where breath comes down, and it repeated, and repeated, and each repetition it gathered a little more of the room into itself: the crackle of the burning floor, the hiss of the turnips roasting in their dark, Odile's ragged breathing, all of it pulled into the rhythm until the cellar was less a place where people were about to die than a place where music was happening, incidentally, amid other events.

And Odile opened the map.

Later, Emeric would try to reconstruct what he saw, and fail, the way you fail to describe a dream by daylight. The ink rose off the vellum like a flock of black birds. And it *fed*—he watched it happen, watched the transaction occur, watched years leave her the way heat leaves a room when a door opens in winter.

Her apprenticeship went first. He saw it go: the sudden blankness in her face where a master cartographer's name used to live, the way her hands briefly forgot the cleverness of them. Then her first map—the pride of it, the girl she'd been who drew it, gone, gone, gone. Then something deeper, something structural, and he understood with horror that the map had found the load-bearing wall: the reason itself. Why Odile Voss had chosen to be no one. The wound underneath the armor, lifted out whole, and swallowed.

She stayed anchored. That was the thing he would remember, if he remembered anything: she stayed *anchored*, eyes fixed on him, following the melody through the smoke while her own history was eaten out of her, and when the eleventh bar came around again she hummed the harmony without knowing she was doing it, because the song was the last fixed point left in her shifting world, and a drowning soul will grab any rail.

The twelfth bar arrived then. He didn't write it so much as discover it, fully formed, the way you discover the last step of a staircase in the dark. It resolved everything the other eleven had left hanging. It sounded, he thought distantly, like a door opening.

The floor above them collapsed in a bloom of sparks—but the map's silver route had already shown her where to stand, and the burning joists fell around them like a cage with a person-shaped hole in it, and the root wall behind the turnips, weakened by fire, came apart at a single kick.

They came up out of the earth into snow and starlight, coughing, filthy, alive, while the waystation burned like a festival behind them and the inquisitors' horns called confusion to each other across the ridge, hunting ashes for two people who were already a mile into the dark.

* * *

They walked until the horns faded, and then until the adrenaline faded, and then a little further, because Odile didn't know how to stop and Emeric didn't dare suggest it. They made camp in the hollow of a rockfall, and he built a small fire, and she sat staring into it with the map lying closed in her lap like a dead bird.

"Emeric," she said at last.

"Here."

"Why are you here?"

He turned. Her face in the firelight was composed, familiar, and utterly wrong—like a portrait of someone he loved hung slightly crooked. The sharpness was all still there. The humor was still there, buried. But the foundations had been sold, and she didn't know what was missing, which was the cruelest part: grief requires a shape, and hers had none.

"You could have run," she said. "In Vell's Row. When I told you what the journey would cost. Any sane person would have run. So either you're insane, or—" She stopped. Frowned, as if the next word had been removed from her vocabulary along with everything else. "Or there's a reason I can't see anymore."

The truth sat in his chest, enormous, obvious, terrifying. He thought about wrapping it in something smaller. He thought about kindness as concealment.

But she had followed his song through a burning cellar. She deserved a straight line.

"Because I love you," he said. "That's the reason. I wrote you a song. You heard most of it tonight. The rest of it I'll teach you when you're ready, or when I am. And I know—" His voice cracked, and he let it. "I know you can't keep it. I know that by morning this conversation may be ash too. But I'd rather tell you a hundred times than lie to you once. That's the whole of it, Odile. That's why I stayed."

She looked at him for a long moment. The fire popped. Snow ticked down through the branches.

"Huh," she said finally. And then, in a smaller voice, with the bewildered honesty of a woman reading someone else's diary: "That feels true. Like a word I knew and lost. I wish—" Her hand drifted toward the map and stopped. "I wish I remembered why that matters to me."

"You will," Emeric said. "Or you won't, and I'll tell you again. Either way, I'll be here."

She nodded slowly, and lay back, and slept—with the map at her chest, and, though she'd have denied it, her bedroll closer to his than it had ever been.

Emeric stayed awake and watched the road behind them.

Because far off, down the pass they'd escaped, he could see lights moving. Not inquisitors' torches—these burned white and cold, in formation, thousands strong. Corvane's army, marching out of the heartland, closing the passes ahead of them like a fist.

Every road east was sealing. Every road south. Which meant that tomorrow he would have to show Odile the map's margins, and ask her to read the one route left—the one drawn straight through the usurper's own country.

And he would have to do it knowing that every secret it yielded would cost her another piece of herself, and that the largest piece of all might be the one she'd just lost, and lost, and would lose again:

him.
