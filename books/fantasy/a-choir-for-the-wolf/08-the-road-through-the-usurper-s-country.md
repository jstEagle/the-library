## 8. The Road Through the Usurper's Country

The map showed her the road at midnight, and Odile hated it on sight.

She had spread it across the floor of an abandoned shepherd's hut, weighted the corners with stones, and asked it—politely, the way you ask a wolf to move aside—for a way east. The passes were sealed. She had watched that happen from a ridge two days earlier: Corvane's engineers firing the snow-sheds, collapsing the high tunnels, closing the mountains like a fist. The free cities were behind them now, their council's cowardice still sour in her mouth. Ahead lay only the usurper's country.

The ink stirred, pooled, and drew.

A single line unspooled from the western edge, threading between the mountain ranges where no cartographer had ever bothered to draw anything, because there was nothing there worth drawing—or so every map she'd ever owned had claimed. The line ran straight as a spear through the heartland of Corvane's power, past garrison towns and tithe-roads and the blackened fields where the memory-harvests burned, and ended, with a small flourish of gold leaf, at the gates of the capital.

"Of course," Odile said flatly. "Straight through his front yard."

Emeric looked up from tuning his lute—he tuned it constantly, the way other men chewed their nails—and read the route over her shoulder. She felt him go still behind her.

"That's the King's Road," he said quietly. "Or it was. Before Corvane renamed it. They call it the Tithe Road now, because everything that travels it pays."

"Everything," Odile repeated. "Meaning?"

"Meaning I don't know. Meaning nobody who's traveled it has come back to say." He crouched beside her, close enough that she could smell woodsmoke and lute-string oil on him. "There will be another way. There's always another way. Give me until morning; I'll think."

"You think in songs, Emeric. Songs take time we don't have." She traced the line with one finger, not quite touching it. The ink leaned toward her skin anyway, eager, the way a dog leans toward a hand it has learned might feed it or strike it. "How much for this one? That's what you're really asking, isn't it. What will it cost me this time."

He didn't answer, which was answer enough.

Her mother's face was already gone. Her apprenticeship. The first map she'd ever drawn, of the smuggler's roads above Thornfell, drawn at fourteen with stolen ink and unbearable pride. The reason she'd chosen invisibility—she knew she had chosen it, knew it had mattered desperately once, but the *why* of it was a room in her mind with the furniture removed. She kept walking past the doorway anyway, out of habit.

"I'm not calculating it," she said. "That's the trick. If I calculate, I'll flinch. So I've decided not to do arithmetic anymore."

"That's not courage, Odile. That's just refusing to count."

"Yes," she said. "I know. It's the same thing, if you hold your breath."

* * *

They went down into the low country with the refugees, because a lone pair of travelers on the Tithe Road was a question, and questions got answered by inquisitors. But a river of displaced people—thousands of them, flowing east ahead of the sealed passes, pushed toward the capital like water toward a drain—was a crowd, and crowds were anonymous, and anonymity was the one country Odile had ever been a citizen of.

Except that she wasn't, anymore. The mark on her wrist saw to that. It ached when inquisitors came near, a hot wire under the skin, and so she learned to read its ache the way she'd once read weather. *Two hours out,* it said. *Ride wide. Keep your sleeve down.*

The refugees moved slowly, because refugees always do—they carry their whole lives on their backs and their dead in their chests, and neither travels light. Odile found herself falling into their pace without meaning to. Emeric played for the children in the evenings, small bright songs that made even the mothers stop weeping for a quarter hour, and the children gave him things in return: a bent nail, a crow feather, half a honeycake gone hard as slate. He accepted each gift with the gravity of a man being knighted.

"You're collecting," Odile observed one night, watching him arrange his treasures on a blanket with the care of a jeweler.

"I'm being paid," he said. "In the only currency that hasn't been debased."

She snorted. "You were a royal chorister. You used to be paid in gold."

"I used to be paid in gold to sing lies to a hollow man while his thief counted my brothers' memories into jars." He set the crow feather carefully at the top of the arrangement. "This is better money."

* * *

It was somewhere in the third week, on a stretch of road where the harvest-burns stained the horizon a bruised orange day and night, that Odile caught herself doing something unforgivable.

She was mapping faces.

Not deliberately. That was the horror of it. She had spent her life memorizing terrain—the fall of a ridge, the width of a ford, the exact angle at which a cliff could be climbed—and now, without deciding to, she was doing it to people. The miller with the burn scar shaped like a river delta. The old woman who walked with a stick and told fortunes that were all identical: *you will outlive this.* The boy with the missing front tooth who had appointed himself Emeric's bodyguard and took the job more seriously than most kings took theirs. She knew the precise curve of the fortune-teller's spine, the exact shade of the miller's stubbornness, the geography of the boy's grin with its gap like a broken wall.

And then one evening she realized she was drawing them. Actually drawing them, in charcoal on the back of a scrap of vellum, the miller's delta-scar rendered with a cartographer's precision, and the boy standing over her shoulder saying, "You got my tooth wrong, it's bigger than that," and the fortune-teller peering at it and saying, "Make me taller, I've earned it," and Odile laughing—a sound that startled her, coming out of her own chest, rusty as an unused gate.

"What are you doing?" Emeric asked, sitting down beside her. He did that now. Sat beside her. When had that started? She couldn't remember, which should have frightened her, given what else she couldn't remember, but instead it felt like a door left open in winter: cold, but letting light in.

"Mapping," she said, before she could stop herself. Then, defensively: "People. Apparently."

He looked at the scrap of vellum, at the little crowd she'd gathered on it, and something crossed his face that she couldn't read—grief and tenderness tangled together, the way they always seemed to be in him lately.

"Do you know why the guild made memory-ink?" he asked.

"To sell secrets to kings."

"No. That's what it became. But the first maps—" he touched the edge of her great map, sleeping in its case, "—the first ones weren't for finding places. They were for keeping them. A guild-cartographer would walk a village and draw it true, and the drawing would hold the village safe. Floods went around the mapped houses. Fires forgot the mapped names. The old stories say a well-drawn village could survive a war that erased everything around it." He paused. "Then Corvane learned the ink worked both ways. That a map could hold a place—or harvest it."

Odile looked down at her charcoal faces. The miller. The boy. The fortune-teller who would outlive this.

"So if I draw them," she said slowly, "they're—"

"Held. Maybe. The ink remembers what the hand loves." He said it lightly, but his ears had gone red, and she decided, generously, not to notice. "It's probably nonsense. Old chorister talk."

"It's not nonsense." She looked at the little map of faces, and felt something turn over in her chest, vast and slow, like a continent shifting. All her life she had believed that to map a thing was to own it, and to own it was to endanger it, and so she had mapped nothing and no one, including herself. But Emeric was telling her the opposite: that to map a thing was to keep it. To carry it safe through the fire.

"The map takes from me," she said quietly. "Every secret. Every road. And I let it, because the king is worth more than my mother's face, or I decided he was, back when I could still do arithmetic." She turned the vellum over. "But nobody takes these. These aren't payment. These are mine."

* * *

The inquisitors found the column of refugees four days from the capital.

There was no warning. The mark on Odile's wrist simply went from ache to shriek, and then the sky over the road filled with grey cloaks dropping from the ridgeline like ash falling upward, and the screaming started.

What happened next happened fast, and Odile's hands remembered what her mind sometimes didn't. She had Emeric by the collar and the map-case strap in her teeth and she was running, hauling him off the road into the gully, into the tangle of bramble and abandoned carts, while around them the inquisitors moved through the refugee column with terrible efficiency—not killing, never killing, because corpses had no memories worth taking. They were harvesting. She heard the sounds the extraction-rigs made, a wet humming like a hive, and she heard the silence that followed each one.

The boy with the missing tooth found them at dusk, three miles downstream, filthy and shaking and alone. He didn't cry. He stood in front of Emeric with his fists clenched and said, in the voice of someone reporting a military matter: "They took my mum's face. Not off her. Out of her. She looked at me and she didn't—" His jaw worked. "She looked at me like I was a stranger's house."

Odile knelt. She took out the scrap of vellum with the charcoal faces, and she added one: the boy, drawn quick and true, gap-toothed and furious and brave, and beneath it she wrote his name, which was Tam, which he had never told anyone because names got you harvested, and she said, "There. Now your mother's face is in two places. One of them is hers again, if she looks. And one of them is here, and nothing gets this one. Not him, not the map, not the king himself."

Tam stared at the drawing. Then at her. Then he sat down abruptly in the mud, the way small boys do when bravery runs out, and Emeric put a hand on his head, and Odile looked up at the darkening sky over the capital, forty miles away, where the towers were already lit for the memory-tithe ceremony.

"We're close enough now," she said. "Close enough that the map can feel it pulling." The case at her hip was warm, warmer than body-warm, the ink inside moving like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice. "One more road. One more payment. And then it's done, whatever done costs."

Emeric helped Tam up. In the distance, faint on the wind, came a sound Odile had never heard before and recognized instantly anyway: thousands of voices, singing in unison, the tithe-hymn, rising from the capital like smoke.

"They're singing it early," Emeric said, and his face had gone the color of the harvest-fires. "Odile—the ceremony isn't in three days. It's tomorrow. Corvane's moved it up. He knows we're coming."

The map, against her hip, pulsed once—hungry, eager, and afraid.
