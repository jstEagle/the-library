## 9. Throne of Borrowed Years

The tithe-wagons came into the capital at dusk, and Emeric came in with them, folded into the straw beneath a tarp that smelled of other people's memories.

That was the cruelty of it, he thought as the wagon rattled through the gates of Corvane's city. Every soul in those wagons had paid something to be here. The memory-tithe was voluntary in the way a drowning is voluntary — you gave Corvane your sweetest years or you starved, and the city ate well either way. Emeric could hear it from inside the straw: a low, contented hum under the cobblestones, like a belly digesting. Ten years of court life had taught him what that sound meant. The palace itself was full.

Odile lay beside him, close enough that her shoulder pressed his, and did not sleep.

"You're humming," she murmured. "Stop humming."

"I'm not humming."

"You're always humming. You hum when you're frightened and you hum when you're pleased and I can't tell which this is."

"Neither," Emeric said honestly. "It's the third thing. The one where tomorrow we walk into the hungriest room in the world and ask it to spit out a king."

She was quiet for a moment. In the dark under the tarp her hand found his — not tenderly, exactly; Odile did nothing exactly — but with the grip of someone checking that a rope still held.

"When we're in there," she said, "and I open the map — you'll sing whether you can see me or not. Promise me that part. Don't stop for anything. Not even—" Her voice did something small. "Not even if I look at you like I don't know you."

"I know that look already," Emeric said. "I've been getting it for weeks."

"That's different. That's you being annoying on purpose."

The wagon stopped. Voices outside; the wet snuffling of one of Corvane's scent-hounds, bred noseless but never quite, and Emeric held his breath until the animal moved on, unsatisfied by straw and fear and two heartbeats pretending to be cargo.

* * *

They went up through the kitchens, because Odile had spent three nights memorizing the map's rendering of the palace service ways and because, as she put it, "no guard in history has ever truly believed anyone would *choose* the kitchens." The map rode against Odile's chest now, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a sleeping bird. It had grown heavier since Vell's Row. Emeric tried not to think about why. He failed most nights.

The throne room doors stood open for the ceremony, and they slipped into the gallery above among the chorus-stalls — the old choir loft, sealed since Emeric's disgrace, its benches still bearing the scratched initials of dead singers. He ran his thumb over one. *Tamsin, who sang the low parts.* Tamsin who had been silenced third.

Below, the tithe was underway.

King Aldric sat on the Ironrose Throne and smiled at his people, and the smile was wrong in the specific way Emeric had spent years learning to see. It reached the eyes a half-second late, like an echo arriving after its shout. Around the dais burned the seven braziers of Corvane's order, each fed not with coal but with little glass vials that cracked and flared gold when they were thrown in — vials of memory, harvested and rendered, burning the past for light.

And between the braziers, thin as smoke poured upward instead of down, stood Corvane himself.

He had no body worth the name anymore. He was a tall absence, a man-shaped hunger wearing the suggestion of grey robes, and when he turned his attention along the rows of kneeling tithe-takers, people visibly aged — not much, a season apiece, a bad winter — as he skimmed the cream off their lives and left them the milk.

"There." Odile's breath was against his ear. "There's the king. Is he in there? Really?"

"He's in there," Emeric said. "Deep. Corvane keeps him the way a miser keeps coin — buried, counted, touched every night to make sure it's still his." His mouth was dry. "The song goes in where the counting happens."

"And the inquisitors?"

Emeric looked. Six of them, ranked at the hall's corners in their bone-pale masks, hands empty, which was worse than hands full. "Ready. Which is flattering, actually. They only post six when they're expecting trouble."

"We *want* them to expect us," Odile said. "Every eye on us is an eye off him." She pulled the map from its wrappings, and even here, even now, Emeric caught his breath at it — ink moving across vellum like fish under ice, mountains rearranging themselves, the whole world redrawn in a guild-hand that had been extinct for a decade. "On my mark. And Emeric—"

"Mm?"

"If this works, the first thing he remembers will be the last thing he lost. Make sure the last thing he lost wasn't the war."

"It wasn't," Emeric said softly. "It was the music. He loved the music. That's why I'm here and not some braver man."

She almost smiled. Then she stood, stepped onto the choir-loft rail — *of course* she did, balance like a cat on a fence, no fear in her body anywhere except her eyes — and cut the map's binding cord with one motion of her knife.

"*Now,*" she said, and opened it wide, and the world changed.

Light came off the map like dawn coming off water. The ink rose — Emeric had seen it move before but never *leave*, never rise off the page in ribbons and rivers, guild-script unspooling into the air, ten vanished cartographers' worth of stolen knowledge pouring upward toward the vaulted dark. The kneeling crowds cried out. The braziers guttered. And in the center of it all, on the Ironrose Throne, the king's borrowed smile faltered like a reflection in a pond when a stone goes in.

Emeric began to sing.

It was the waking-song, the true one, the one he'd written in a cell in Vell's Row with his brother's name still fresh as a wound — and it was built from the old court melodies, the ones Aldric had loved, braided together with a harmony Emeric had composed for someone else entirely, someone who mapped roads and trusted none of them. He gave the song everything. The loft had been built to carry a choir of forty voices, and one voice alone sounded thin in it, so he made himself forty. He sang the low parts Tamsin used to hold. He sang the descant. He sang the part the king used to hum, badly, during long councils, thinking no one heard.

Below, the hollow face twitched.

"Inquisitors!" Corvane's voice was not a voice; it was the sound a fire makes eating a house. The masked figures moved, fast, silent, converging on the loft stairs — and on Odile, who had come down off the rail and was walking straight into the open floor, map held high, ink streaming from it like a banner in wind she was the only one standing in.

"Emeric!" she called back up, without turning around. "Whatever happens down here — *sing.*"

So he sang.

Aldric stirred. Emeric saw it happen the way you see a sleeper turn toward a window at dawn — the head lifting, the shoulders squaring, the eyes coming off their leash. The hollow puppetry shuddered. Somewhere deep under ten stolen years, a real man heard his favorite melody and *remembered having liked it*, and liking became knowing, and knowing became *mine*—

"No," said Corvane, and the word bent the air.

The usurper-mage turned from the tithe-lines all at once, all his hunger swinging like a lighthouse beam, and fixed on the map. On Odile. Emeric saw the moment she understood what was looking at her — saw her go very still, the stillness of prey deciding to be something else — and then Corvane flowed toward her across the marble, robes trailing into nothing, reaching, and the inquisitors broke formation to follow him, and the crowd screamed and scattered, and Emeric sang louder, throat raw, holding the note like a hand holds a door.

Aldric rose from the throne. One step. Two. A king-shaped man waking in a king-shaped body, gold light leaking from the seams of him where the waking-song found purchase.

Corvane reached Odile.

She did not run. Of course she didn't run. She opened the map wider — wider than vellum should allow, wider than the world should allow — and the last hidden chambers bloomed across its face, and Emeric understood with a sick drop of his stomach what she was doing. Everything it held. Every secret it hadn't shown yet. Every payment it hadn't taken yet.

She was showing it all to the thing that eats memories. She was setting the table.

"Come take it, then," Odile said, clear across the whole roaring hall. "But you have to come *into* it to read it."

Corvane hesitated — hunger does hesitate, at a trap dressed as a feast — and in that half-breath of hesitation, Aldric arrived.

The restored king moved like weather. There was no sword in his hand and he needed none; he simply laid both palms on the smoky shape of Corvane's shoulders, the way a chorister steadies a shaking novice, and spoke a single word that Emeric felt rather than heard, a word made of every year the mage had swallowed coming back up all at once —

—and the light went white, and the singing in Emeric's own ears became the only sound left in the world, and he kept singing, eyes shut, because he had promised her he would sing whether he could see her or not.

When the whiteness drained away, the hall was silent.

Corvane was gone — unraveled, unmade, a knot untied. The braziers' flames burned steady and ordinary. Aldric stood whole at the center of the marble, breathing, weeping without shame, a man returned from a decade underwater.

And Odile lay on the floor beside a sheet of blank vellum, eyes closed, chest rising, alive.

Alive, Emeric told himself, sliding down the loft stair two at a time, alive, *alive* — and then he knelt beside her and took her hand and she opened her eyes, and they were clear, and calm, and entirely, perfectly strange.

She looked at him the way you look at a stranger who has caught you falling.

"Hello," said Odile Voss politely. "Where am I?"
