## 10. A Choir for the Wolf

The throne room smelled of ozone and old parchment when the king opened his eyes.

Odile did not see it happen—not exactly. She was too busy being unmade herself.

She had opened the map completely. That had been her choice, made in the space of a heartbeat while Corvane's hunger came down on them like an avalanche: she had spread the vellum wide across the marble floor and pressed both palms flat against it and said, *Take everything. Take all of it.* And the map, which had been eating her memories in careful spoonfuls for months—her mother's face, her apprenticeship, her first map, the reason she'd chosen to be no one—took the rest at once.

It took the mountains. It took Vell's Row. It took a burning waystation and a man singing in the smoke. It took the passes and the refugees and the faces she had drawn in her mind because they deserved better than to be forgotten. It took the courier's name, whispered with his last breath into ash. It took Emeric.

It took Emeric.

And what it gave back—

Aldric rose from the throne like a man surfacing from deep water. He was not the hollow thing that had sat there for years, nodding at ceremonies with borrowed eyes. He was whole. Every stolen year came home to him at once—the guild cartographers who had been harvested to make the memory-ink, the choir silenced one voice at a time, the night Corvane had poured him out like last week's wine and worn his body as a coat. All of it returned, and Aldric stood in the middle of his own restored self and looked at the usurper-mage with something worse than rage.

He looked at Corvane with recognition.

"You were my friend," the king said, and his voice filled the throne room the way dawn fills a valley. "I remember now. I remember *everything*."

Corvane screamed. It was not a kingly sound. It was the sound of a man who has spent decades devouring other people's lives and discovers, at the end, that he never kept any of his own—that there is nothing inside him but appetite, and appetite cannot fight memory. The stolen years rose out of him like birds off a winter field. Every life he had taken, every mind he had hollowed, every song silenced—they came home too, and they went through him on their way, and there was not enough left of Corvane afterward to bury.

Odile remembered none of this clearly. She remembered it the way you remember a fever: heat, and light, and someone holding her hand, and then—

Then blank vellum.

* * *

She woke in a bed with clean linen, in a room with a window, and outside the window a city was rebuilding itself with a noise like applause.

Her wrists were bare. No mark. Her hands were her own, scarred from rope work and cold, familiar, and yet when she tried to remember how she had come here, her mind met a smooth white wall, like snowfall over a road.

"Easy," said a woman in healer's grey, noticing her sit up too fast. "You've been asleep two days. You're in the capital. You're free—everyone keeps saying it, so I'll say it too: you're free."

"Free," Odile repeated. The word fit strangely, like a boot for the other foot.

"The king asked after you himself." The healer smiled. "King Aldric. He remembers everyone who helped. He says—" she hesitated, "—he says he owes you more than a kingdom can repay, though he intends to try."

"I don't remember helping anyone."

"No," the healer said gently. "We know."

* * *

Weeks passed. Odile stayed, because she had nowhere else to be and, for the first time in her life, nowhere else she needed to hide from either. The capital was learning to walk again after its long possession; scaffolding climbed the palace walls like ivy, and in the squares people argued cheerfully about everything, which Odile gathered was what freedom sounded like up close.

She found work, of all things, drawing maps. A guild clerk looked at the sureness of her hand and hired her on the spot, never asking where she'd learned it, because the answer wasn't in her head anymore either. She drew coastlines and trade roads and the rebuilt districts, and her pen moved with a confidence she couldn't account for, and sometimes, drawing a mountain range, her chest would ache without reason, the way a room aches after furniture is carried out.

She was good at it. Everyone said so. *You draw like someone who's been everywhere,* the clerk told her, and Odile laughed and said she'd never been farther than the capital's north gate, and believed it, mostly.

At night she dreamed of ink that moved.

* * *

She was walking through the Lanternmarket on a bright autumn morning, carrying a roll of fresh maps under one arm and a honey-bun in the other hand, when she heard the singer.

He was set up near the fountain, as street singers were, with a lute that had seen honest wear and a voice that had seen worse. The crowd around him was loose and half-attentive, the way crowds are. He was singing something new—she could tell it was new, because the market had already learned every old song by heart and sang along with those, badly, joyfully.

This one nobody knew. This one he sang alone.

And Odile stopped.

Not decided to stop. Stopped, the way water stops at a ledge. The honey-bun hung forgotten. The song was simple—a walking rhythm, a road-song, the kind of tune meant to keep feet moving when feet wanted to quit—and the words were about a woman who knew every road and trusted no one, who had spent her whole life staying unmapped, and how the singer had loved her anyway, and how love, unlike memory, does not need to be remembered to remain.

Odile had never heard the song. She was certain of it. Certain the way you are certain of your own name.

And then her mouth opened, and she hummed the harmony.

Perfectly. Without hesitation. The line wove under his melody like a second thread through the same cloth, rising where his fell, falling where his rose, and the singer's voice caught—actually caught, mid-word—and the crowd turned to look at the woman with the maps and the honey-bun who was harmonizing with a stranger's heart.

He stared at her across the fountain. He was younger than his voice suggested, or older than his face; it was hard to say. His eyes were red-rimmed, and full, and fixed on her with an expression she had no word for, though somewhere beneath the snowfall in her mind, something shifted its weight.

"Do you—" he began, and stopped, and started again, carefully, the way a man steps onto ice. "Do you know that song?"

"No," Odile said honestly. "Should I?"

"It's new. I wrote it recently." His knuckles were white on the lute neck. "For someone. She hasn't heard it yet."

"That's a shame," Odile said. "It's a good song. Whoever she is, she should hear it." She frowned at herself, at the wetness arriving without permission at the corners of her eyes. "Sorry. I don't know why I'm—I don't even know you."

"No," he said softly. "But you know the harmony."

"I suppose I do." She laughed, embarrassed, wiping her eyes with the back of the hand that held the honey-bun, which was a mistake, and made him laugh too—a broken, wonderful sound. "Where did I learn it? I've never been anywhere. I've lived here all my life."

Something crossed his face—grief and hope wrestling for the same chair—and lost, and won, and lost again. "Maybe some things," he said, "don't live in the head."

"What, the chest?" She tapped her sternum. "My mother used to say that. I think. Someone used to say that."

"Someone did," he agreed, and his voice cracked on it, and he smiled through it, because he was the kind of man who sang through things. "Would you—if it's not strange, if it isn't too strange—would you sing it with me? Once through? So I can hear how it sounds with the harmony in it. I wrote the harmony for a particular voice, and I've been wondering ever since whether I got it right."

Odile considered the roll of maps under her arm. Considered the guild clerk who expected her by noon. Considered the ache in her chest that had followed her for weeks like a dog she couldn't see.

"It's strange," she said. "But sing it again. From the top."

He sang it from the top.

And she hummed the harmony perfectly, all the way through, and when it ended the little crowd applauded, and the singer looked at her the way men look at shorelines, and Odile Voss—who did not remember Thornfell, or the courier, or the price, or the throne room, or him—heard herself say:

"I'm Odile, by the way. I don't know why that felt important to tell you."

"Emeric," he said. "I don't know why that felt important either."

They stood a moment in the bright noise of the market, two strangers sharing a song neither could explain, and above them the scaffolding on the palace gleamed like the ribs of a ship being built, and somewhere in the city a bell rang the hour, and Odile thought—idly, lightly, the way you think a thing before it becomes your whole life:

*I have the feeling I've finally arrived somewhere.*

"Well," she said, shifting her maps. "Do you know any others?"

"Every one I've ever written," said Emeric. "And I'll write more. As many as it takes."

"As many as what takes?"

He smiled, and began to play.
