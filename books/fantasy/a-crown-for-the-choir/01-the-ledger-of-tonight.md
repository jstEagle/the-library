## 1. The Ledger of Tonight

The candle had burned down to its last thumb's width when Lena Sandoval found the error.

She noticed it the way she noticed everything in the counting room: without looking up, without breaking rhythm. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts the way a surgeon keeps instruments—each one clean, each one in its place, each one accounted for at the end of the day. Lena had been keeper of those accounts for eleven years, since Master Corvane himself had lifted her ink-stained hand off an orphanage bench and said, *This one counts true.* She was twenty-six years old and she could balance a ledger in her sleep, which was fortunate, because she often did.

Tonight's work was ordinary. That was the first thing she would remember afterward—not the shock, not the cold that walked up her spine like a finger, but the ordinariness. The guild had taken delivery that morning of three crates of bottled thunder from the storm-farmers of the Grey Reach, two vials of yesterday (a rare vintage, always in demand among the grieving), and one live shadow, crated in ironwood and breathing softly through the slats. All of it had to be entered. All of it had to be priced. Impossible things were still things, and things had costs.

*Three crates bottled thunder, grade two, forty-one flasks intact, six cracked in transit—*

Her pen scratched. The candle guttered and steadied. Somewhere above her, in the dormers of the guildhall, an apprentice snored. The building settled around her with the small creaks of old timber and older stone, sounds she knew as intimately as her own pulse. This room, this desk, this candle: hers. The ledger lay open beneath her hands, columns marching down the page in disciplined ranks, and for another hour everything in the world would be exactly where it belonged.

That was what order meant, Lena had learned early. Not safety—she'd stopped believing in safety the night the fever took her parents—but *placement*. A thing in its right place could be trusted. A number in its right column told no lies. She had built her whole life on that quiet faith, stone by patient stone, and if anyone had asked her—and no one ever had—she would have said the foundation was sound.

The prophecy book sat on the corner shelf where it had sat for as long as she'd been allowed in the counting room. It was called the *Vellum Oracle*, though no one called it anything anymore; it was simply *the old book*, a fat, cracked-leather volume chained to its stand by a chain so corroded it looked more ceremonial than functional. A century ago, some guildmaster or other had commissioned it—a record of omens, portents, the guild's founding superstitions. Lena dusted it on the first of every month. She had never once read it. Prophecy was not a tradeable commodity, and therefore, by the logic that governed her life, it was not her business.

But the candle was dying, and the last entry needed checking against the intake manifest, and the manifest lived on the shelf behind the oracle's stand. So Lena rose, stretched her cramped back until it popped, and reached past the old book to pull the manifest free.

As she drew it out, the stand rocked. Just slightly. Just enough.

The *Vellum Oracle* slid forward on its shelf, caught the edge of the manifest, and fell open on the desk beside her ledger with a slap of old pages that sounded, in the silent room, like a door closing.

Lena stared at it. Then, because she was who she was—because disorder offended her before danger ever did—she bent to pick it up.

The page it had fallen open to was headed, in faded brown ink, with a date.

She knew the date. Of course she knew the date. It was written at the top of tonight's ledger in her own neat hand, the same date she had written every morning for years, the date that was now, at this precise moment, ticking toward midnight somewhere above the candle flame.

*The ninth of Emberfall.*

A century ago, someone had written that date into a book of prophecies. Not a year—just the day and month, as if the writer had known the day mattered but not which year it would finally arrive. Lena frowned. That was odd. Odd enough to hold her attention the way a misaligned column held it: a small wrongness that demanded righting.

She read the entry beneath the date.

*On the ninth of Emberfall, the gate will hunger. What was sealed in patience will be opened in haste. The seam between the realms will thin to a thread, and thread will become knife.*

Well, thought Lena, with the detached amusement of a practical woman. A hundred years of waiting and the great omen was that the gate might get hungry. She reached to close the book.

Her eye caught the next line, and her hand stopped moving.

*Beneath the gate that night stands the keeper of accounts, and her name is written here:*

And there, in ink that had dried a hundred years before Lena Sandoval drew her first breath, in a careful antique script nothing like her own, was written:

*Lena Sandoval.*

The counting room went very quiet. Quiet in a new way—not the comfortable hush of routine but a held-breath silence, the silence of a room listening. The candle flame stood perfectly still, though Lena could feel, or thought she could feel, a draft moving across the back of her neck, cold as well-water, coming from the direction of the sealed gate at the far end of the hall.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, because saying it made it smaller. Her voice sounded strange to her, flattened, as if the room had swallowed its echo.

Names could be coincidence. Names were common things; Sandovals had lived in this city for generations. Someone a century dead might have known a Sandoval, might even have—her mind scrambled for purchase—might have recorded a family name, a future name, some scribal habit she didn't understand. There were explanations. There were always explanations. Order insisted on them.

But the handwriting bothered her worse than the name. Because the longer she looked at it, the more familiar it became—not like her own hand, no, but like something she had seen somewhere, sometime, in some margin or flyleaf, and the not-knowing-where itched at her like a splinter driven under the nail.

She touched the page. The ink did not smudge. Ink a century old should have browned, should have feathered into the fibers of the paper the way all the other entries had. This ink sat black and sharp on the surface, wet-looking, as if the words had been written minutes ago. As if they were still drying.

As if they were still being written.

Lena pulled her hand back. The candle flame leaned hard to the left, though no door had opened. Down the length of the guildhall, past the shelves of catalogued wonders and the crates of impossible inventory, the great gate stood where it had always stood: double-sealed, iron-banded, warded with sigils older than the guild itself. Corvane had ordered it sealed before Lena was born. No one living had seen it open. It was, in fact, the only thing in the entire guild that had never been traded, priced, or entered in any ledger—which had always struck Lena as a small professional scandal, though she'd never said so.

Now she looked at it across the dark hall, and the gate looked back.

Not literally. Of course not literally. But the shadows along its iron bands seemed deeper than they'd been an hour ago, and the air around it seemed to bend slightly, the way air bends over a hot road in summer. And beneath the silence, so faint she might have invented it, came a sound.

A hum. Low, sustained, patient. Like a struck bell heard through a mountain. Like something enormous clearing its throat after a very long sleep.

Lena closed the prophecy book. Her hands were steady—that surprised her; she'd expected them to shake—but her heart was going like a drummer boy's, quick and light and wrong. She set the book back on its shelf, squared it precisely with the shelf's edge, and stood there a moment with her palm flat against the cracked leather, feeling foolish and frightened in equal measure.

"Scribal error," she told the empty room. "A hundred-year-old scribal error."

The room declined to agree.

She gathered her ledgers, snuffed the candle, and climbed the stairs to her narrow room beneath the eaves, where she did not sleep. She lay staring at the rafters, running explanations like beads along a string, and every bead slipped loose. Scribal error. Family name. Coincidence. But the fresh ink. But the hum. But the way the date had fallen open, of all the pages in a fat old book, to the one bearing tonight—

*What was sealed in patience will be opened in haste.*

Near midnight, she gave up on sleep and lit her lamp again. If she couldn't rest, she would work; work had never yet failed her. She would re-examine the entry in full daylight-equivalent, with a clear head, and find the rational seam in it, and pick it open, and be done with this nonsense before morning bells.

She was halfway down the stairs when she realized she wasn't going to the counting room at all.

She was going to the gate.

* * *

The guildhall at midnight was a country she had never visited despite living in it all her life. By day it was corridors and duties, apprentices trotting with trays, masters murmuring over contracts. By night it was vast and breathing, moonlight falling through the high clerestory windows in pale blue slabs, dust hanging in the beams like suspended snow. Lena's footsteps rang on the flagstones louder than she liked. The hum grew as she approached the far end of the hall—not louder, exactly, but fuller, the way a chord grows when its final note is added. It wasn't unpleasant. That was the shameful part, the part she would examine later and turn over and refuse to look at directly: the sound was beautiful, and some buried part of her responded to it the way a tuning fork responds, helplessly, gratefully, as if it had been waiting all her life for exactly this pitch.

The gate filled the end wall of the hall like a held breath. Twice her height, banded in iron gone black with age, its surface carved with interlocking sigils that predated the guild's own founding charter. Two seals crossed it—one of wax and guild-stamp, one of woven silver wire that Master Corvane renewed each solstice. Both intact. Both, as far as any ledger recorded, unbroken since before living memory.

Lena stopped ten paces away, lamp raised.

"Hello?" she said, and felt immediately absurd.

The hum answered. It rose, swelled, and shaped itself—and Lena's blood went cold, then colder, because the shaping had structure, and the structure was language, and the language was a single word repeated over and over in a voice like wind through a canyon:

*Sandoval. Sandoval. Sandoval.*

Her name. Spoken by a sealed gate in a voice older than the guild, in the dead middle of the night, on the date written in a book a century old.

Every sane instinct she possessed told her to run—to wake Corvane, to fetch the enforcers, to do anything but stand here alone with a singing door. She took one step backward. Her heel scraped stone.

The seals peeled open.

There was no violence in it. That was somehow the worst part. The silver wire unwound itself like a flower opening in fast growth, petal by patient petal, and the wax seal split along its own lines and curled away, and neither showed any resistance at all—as if they had only ever been holding the door shut against strangers, and the stranger had just introduced herself. The iron bands did not move. The gate did not swing. But between the bands, in the spaces where there had been only darkness and carving, something else appeared: a shimmer, a standing ripple, like heat-haze given depth. Like a doorway drawn in water.

Beyond it, impossibly, she could see starlight. Different stars. Stars arranged in patterns no cartographer of her city had ever charted, wheeling slowly around a horizon that glowed the deep violet of dusk that never ends.

The hum resolved into something almost tender. Almost pleading.

*Sandoval.*

"I'm not—" Lena's voice cracked. "I'm not who you think I am. I keep accounts. I count things. I'm nobody's—"

The pull took her the way a river takes a leaf: gently, totally, without any possibility of argument. One moment her boots were on guildhall flagstones, the lamp swinging wild in her grip; the next, the floor was gone, the hall was stretching away from her like a reflection in disturbed water, and she was falling forward into starlight and violet dusk and the smell of rain on a world she had never seen.

The last thing she saw, as the shimmer closed behind her, was the counting room's distant window—her window, her candle, her tidy life—and the last thing she heard was her own voice, thin and receding, saying the only orderly thing left to say:

"This is not in the ledger."

Then Vael took her, and the gate stood shut again, seals whole, hum stilled, as if nothing had happened at all.

Except that on the desk in the dark counting room, the *Vellum Oracle* lay open to the ninth of Emberfall, and beneath the prophecy, in ink that gleamed wet and black and new, a second line had appeared while no one watched:

*The keeper has been claimed.*
