## 2. What the Guild Trades

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Lena climbed the stairs to the Master's study, and she carried it with her because the guildhall's upper corridors went dark an hour after midnight, when the last of the night-tenders banked their lamps. The flame threw her shadow long against the stone, a thin dark sister walking beside her up the spiral stair, and she found herself glad of the company.

She had not slept. She had tried—she was a woman of habits, and habits were a kind of ledger too, each hour entered and balanced—but every time she closed her eyes she saw the ink. *The seventeenth of Emberfall.* Her own small, careful hand had written that date at the top of tonight's page not six hours ago, in the ordinary course of things, and there it had been already waiting in the prophecy book: the same date, the same words, and beneath them, in a script like frost forming on glass—

*The keeper comes to the door. The door remembers her name.*

*Lena Sandoval.*

A century old. A century old, and written in a hand no scribe of the guild would have dared to fake, because the prophecy book lived behind three locks and a warding that blistered dishonest fingers. She had checked the locks herself. She had checked them twice.

The stair ended at a door of black oak banded with iron, and from behind it came the smell she associated with all her safest memories: tallow, cedar shavings, and the faint mineral tang of fresh ink. Master Corvane worked late. Master Corvane always worked late. In nineteen years, since the night a guildsman had carried a mud-smeared child of five through these very doors and Master Corvane had looked down at her over his spectacles and said, *Well. The ledgers were short one keeper*, she could count on one hand the nights he had not been awake past midnight, bent over accounts.

She knocked.

"Enter." His voice, as always, made her think of a drawer closing smoothly.

She stepped into the study. It was a room built for order: shelves ranked like soldiers, ledgers spine-aligned in strict descending years, a single window whose shutters were closed even now, though the guildhall stood above the fog line and the harbor lights would have been worth the view. Corvane sat at his desk, gray-robed, gray-bearded, his pen moving in small precise strokes. He did not look up.

"You're out of bed," he said. "That's unlike you."

"Master, I need to show you something."

"Show me, then."

She crossed the room and laid the prophecy book on his desk, open to the page. She had wrapped it in her own scarf for the climb, absurdly, as if it were cold, and unwrapped it now with hands that were steadier than she'd expected them to be. The candlelight caught the frost-script and made it glimmer.

Corvane set down his pen. He read. She watched his face the way she watched the tide-gauge, and what she saw there lasted less than a breath—a flicker, a tightening at the corner of the eye, gone before it could be named—and then his expression settled into the mild, weary patience he wore for apprentices who miscounted columns.

"Scribal error," he said.

"Master—"

"Lena." He took off his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve, unhurried. "This book is a hundred and four years old. It has passed through the hands of forty copyists, two floods, one fire, and a decade in which Brother Aldous kept it in a damp cellar because he believed prophecies preferred humidity. Errors accumulate. Dates blur. Names—" he tapped the page once, lightly, "—names are the most common corruption of all. Ink blooms. A stain becomes a letter; a letter becomes a name. You know this. You've restored damaged pages yourself."

"I have," Lena said. "And I've never seen ink bloom into a full name in a hand I didn't recognize. And never—" she turned the book toward herself, toward the date at the top of the page, written in her own brown-black ink, "—never have I seen tomorrow's date already entered. That's not corruption, Master. That's *correspondence*."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not fear. She knew his fears; she had catalogued them over nineteen years the way she catalogued everything—the way his left hand trembled when the harbor bells rang off-pattern, the way he refused fish at table, the way he touched the iron band at his wrist when he thought no one watched. This was not fear. This was closer to recognition, and recognition frightened her more.

"The gate is double-sealed tonight," he said. "Both doors. Iron and salt and the spoken wards, all of it, before you leave this room. I'll do it myself."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that matters." He rose, and she saw how tired he was, how the gray of his robe matched the gray of him, and for a moment she felt the old pull of love, dull and deep as an anchor chain. He had fed her. Taught her numbers. Given her a place where everything balanced. "You are the best keeper this guild has produced in fifty years, Lena Sandoval, and I say that knowing what it cost the others. You see patterns. But a pattern is not a prophecy. Go to bed. Seal the gate first. Sleep."

"And if the gate opens anyway?"

He paused with his hand on the prophecy book, and for just a moment—he would deny it later, she would tell herself later, she would try to believe it later—his thumb moved across the page, over her name, the way a man touches a scar.

"Then we'll know the error wasn't yours," he said quietly, "or mine."

* * *

The gate of the Guild of Impossible Things stood at the heart of the guildhall, which stood at the heart of Saltmere, which stood at the edge of the world—or so the apprentices were told, and so Lena had believed for nineteen years.

It was not a grand thing. That was the first lesson every novice learned, standing in the Gatehouse on their first morning: greatness is a costume the world wears to keep you from looking closely. The gate was a double door of black wood in a frame of black stone, tall as three men, set into a wall with no other openings, in a round chamber lit by a single hanging lamp. No hinges showed. No keyhole showed. The wood was older than the guildhall around it, older than Saltmere, older, the old masters claimed, than the idea of doors.

On its face, inlaid in metal gone green with age, ran nine lines of script in no alphabet Lena had ever been able to match to any language in the guild's translation rooms. Nine lines, like nine staves. Like nine lines of music.

She reached the Gatehouse a little past the second bell. Two of the night-tenders were already there, waiting for her, and they fell silent when she entered—not the easy silence of colleagues but the held-breath silence of people who had heard something. Word traveled fast in a building of forty souls. *The prophecy book has tonight's date. The keeper's name is in it.*

"Master Corvane's orders," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat and official, and hated it a little. "Double seal. Both doors. Iron, salt, spoken wards. Let's begin."

They worked well together, the three of them; grief and fear make people efficient. They brought the great iron braces from their brackets on the wall and set them across both doors, horizontal and vertical, six bars in all, and Lena drove the wedges home with the copper mallet while the tenders poured the salt line in its unbroken circle around the frame. Then the wards. These were hers alone—the keeper's portion—and she spoke them in the old cadence, the one that made her back teeth ache, watching the syllables take hold along the salt line as a faint blue shimmer, like heat rising off cold stone.

When it was done they stood back. The gate looked the same as it had looked every night of her life: shut, dumb, patient. Six bars of iron. A ring of salt. A keeper's wards humming so low she felt them in her fillings.

But she had inspected this gate every night for eleven years, and she knew its moods the way sailors know a ship. And the gate was *listening*. She couldn't have said how she knew. The wood hadn't changed. The lamp hadn't flickered. But the chamber felt like a room where someone had just stopped speaking.

"Go on," she told the tenders. "I'll finish the log."

They left gratefully. She stayed, and wrote the entry by lamplight—*double seal applied, 2nd bell, L.S., keeper*—and then she did not leave. She pulled a stool to the middle of the chamber, sat down facing the gate, and told herself she was performing a routine extended inspection.

She was lying, and the gate knew it, and maybe that was why it waited until she was alone.

* * *

She must have drifted. The lamp burned steady; the wards hummed; and somewhere in the space between one breath and the next, the quality of the silence changed.

It began below hearing, the way a storm begins below the horizon. A pressure. A lean. Then—faint, threaded through the hum of her own wards like a voice through a wall—sound. Not words. Not yet. A tone, sustained, patient, coming from the other side of three inches of wood older than language, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard, and that was the moment true fear arrived, because beauty on the wrong side of a sealed door is not a visitor. It is a summons.

The tone gathered itself. Shaped itself. Became, unmistakably, a voice singing, and the voice sang a name.

Her name.

Not shouted. Sung—low, tender, the way you sing a child awake rather than call them, each syllable placed like a stone in a stream, and the sound of it went through her wards as if they were cobwebs, through the salt as if it were sugar, through the iron as if iron were a rumor. The bars did not rattle. They *leaned*. She watched, nailed to her stool, as the great braces bowed outward by the width of a hair, then a finger's width, the wedges creaking, and the salt line began to move—grain by grain, crawling inward, erasing itself, the circle unmaking itself in perfect obedience to the song.

Get up, she told herself. Ring the alarm. Run.

She did not get up. That was the shameful part, the part she would carry afterward like a stone in the shoe: she sat there, heart slamming, and some traitorous chamber of her chest was not afraid at all. Nineteen years of ledgers, of balancing other people's impossible trades, of recording what the guild took in and paid out without ever being permitted to ask *what any of it was for*—and here, at last, was a voice that knew her name. Had known it for a hundred years. Had written it down and waited.

The seals peeled open like petals.

There was no violence in it. The iron bars lifted free of their brackets and hung in the air, quivering, humming in harmony with the voice. The salt rose in a slow spiral, glittering, and drifted apart. The black wood swung inward without a sound, and beyond it was not the Gatehouse wall, not stone, not anything the word *beyond* had ever meant—and light came through, twilight-colored, smelling of rain and something green and impossibly far away, and the pull began.

It was gentle. That was the horror of it. It did not seize her; it *invited*, the way the tide invites the sand, and she felt her body rise from the stool, weightless, her candle-stub still clutched stupidly in one fist, and the last thing she saw as the chamber swung away was the open logbook on the floor, her own handwriting staring up at her:

*Double seal applied. 2nd bell. L.S., keeper.*

The ink was not dry yet.

The gate closed behind her like a mouth finishing a word.
