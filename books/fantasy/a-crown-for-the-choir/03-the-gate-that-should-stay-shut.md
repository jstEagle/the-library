## 3. The Gate That Should Stay Shut

The guildhall slept the way old buildings do—not all at once, but in layers, like sediment settling. First the apprentices' wing went quiet, its candle-smoke thinning to nothing by the ninth bell. Then the counting rooms, where the day-ledgers were locked away and their keepers climbed the narrow stairs to their cells, yawning over unfinished sums they would regret in the morning. Last of all came the deep quiet of the vault levels, where even the mice seemed to understand that certain things were not to be disturbed.

Lena Sandoval walked those lower corridors every night at the eleventh bell, and she had done so for eleven years, since Master Corvane had first pressed the inspection ring into her palm—a cold iron band set with a sliver of gate-glass—and said, *You have a steady hand and an unsteady heart, girl. The second will ruin you if the first doesn't hold it.* She had never been sure whether it was a compliment.

Her boots made no sound on the stairs. That was part of the discipline: wool-wrapped heels, breath measured, lantern shuttered to a blade of light no wider than a finger. A gatekeeper's inspection was meant to be invisible. You checked the seals, you logged the readings, you touched nothing, and above all—*above all*—you told no one what the gate felt like under your hand on the nights it felt like anything at all.

Because some nights it did feel like something. A warmth through the iron, faint as sun through winter glass. A tremor in the hinge-bolts, so slight she'd once spent three hours convincing herself she'd imagined it. The older keepers called these *moods of the door* and wrote them off as settling stone or river-water in the foundations. Lena had learned, early and thoroughly, that curiosity about the gate was a disease the guild cured with extra duties and colder cells.

She had gotten very good at not being curious.

Tonight, though, the ledger was still in her satchel—the prophecy book she should have returned to the archive, the one with tonight's date inked in a dead man's careful hand beside her own name. She had told herself she would put it back before inspection. She had told herself that three times since supper. It rode against her hip now like a coal that wouldn't cool, and every step down toward the vault level, she could feel it thinking at her.

The corridor to the gate chamber was lined with the guild's founding plaques, brass gone green at the edges, each one recording an impossible trade in language so formal it had worn smooth as river stone:

*For the mending of the eastern sky, one song unsung.*
*For the keeping of the border-stones, one name unspoken.*
*For the peace between the shores, one memory surrendered gladly.*

She had read them ten thousand times. Children recited them the way children in other cities recited harvest prayers. They were wallpaper. They were nothing.

Tonight she stopped at the third plaque and read it twice, and hated herself for the small cold thrill that moved through her when she did.

*Steady hand,* she reminded herself. *Unsteady heart.*

The gate chamber opened around her like the inside of a held breath.

It was a round room, older than the guildhall built around it, older than the city, if the masons were to be believed. No windows. The walls curved inward as they rose, meeting in a ceiling shaped like the underside of a bell, and every surface was carved with script in no alphabet the guild used—deep grooves worn soft by centuries of air, filled now with candle-shadow. And there, filling the far wall from floor to vaulted height, stood the Gate.

It was not a door. Doors opened onto places. This was a seam—a vertical wound in the world, thirty feet of it, framed in black iron bolted with bands of silver that no smith would claim. Through the seam itself there was no light and no dark, only a kind of *depth*, a color that wasn't a color, the way the space behind your own eyes isn't a color. The guild's oldest records called it the Veil-Split. The newer ones, embarrassed by poetry, called it Boundary Installation One.

Seven seals held it shut. Lena knew each one the way she knew her own teeth.

Three were wax-and-iron sigils, renewed monthly, stamped with the guild's mark: a key crossed with a quill. Two were chains of cold-forged silver, anchored into the living rock of the wall, each link inscribed with a word of holding. The sixth was a lattice of gate-glass, grown rather than made, humming faintly always with the note the guild called *the floor of the world*. And the seventh—

The seventh was not a thing but a silence. A pocket of air directly before the seam where sound simply declined to exist. You could stand in it and hear your own heartbeat stop. Apprentices dared each other to do it. Lena had done it exactly once, at fifteen, and had come out of it shaking, with the absolute conviction that something on the other side had heard her heart stutter and turned, slowly, to listen.

She lit the four standing candles, logged the time in her inspection book, and began.

Sigil one: intact, wax unbroken, stamp true. Sigil two: intact. Sigil three—she ran her thumb along its edge and felt the fine grain of the seal, uncracked, unwarmed. Chain one: taut, inscriptions legible, no frost, no bloom of corrosion. Chain two: the same. She wrote the entries in her small precise hand, and her hand was steady, and she was grateful for it.

Then she reached the gate-glass lattice, and the night changed.

The hum was wrong.

Not louder—that was the first thing she noticed, and the most frightening. The lattice always hummed; it was supposed to; a silent lattice would mean a crack, a breach, catastrophe. But tonight the note had *moved*. For eleven years it had sat at the bottom of her hearing like a cellar tone, and tonight it had climbed. It was thinner now, higher, straining upward the way a voice strains when someone is trying to reach a note just past their range.

Lena stood very still. Her lantern-blade of light lay across the lattice and the glass threw it back in fragments, and in the fragments she thought—for one vertiginous moment—that she saw movement. Not reflection. *Behind.*

*Settling stone,* said the sensible voice in her head, Corvane's voice, the guild's voice. *River-water in the foundations. Log it and move on.*

Her hand did not move on. Her hand took out the inspection book and wrote, in letters slightly less steady than the last entry: *Lattice pitch elevated. Possible resonance drift. Will re-check at close.*

And then, while she watched her own handwriting dry, the gate began to sing her name.

There is no honest way to describe the sound except the way it arrived: not through the ears first. It came up through the soles of her boots, through the iron ring on her finger, through the fillings in her teeth, a vibration that assembled itself into pitch somewhere inside her skull. And then it was in her ears too, everywhere, the whole bell-shaped room become the throat of an instrument, and the note it sang was two syllables long.

*"Le-na."*

Soft. Patient. The way you say a name when you have said it many times before to someone who keeps not waking.

Every hair on her arms rose. Her body knew what her mind refused to: that this was the sound of the impossible, and the impossible was not supposed to know her name. Names were currency here. The plaques traded in them—*one name unspoken*—and the guild kept its ledgers precisely because names, once given, could not be taken back.

"Who's there?" she said, and heard how small her voice was in the great bell of the room, and hated it.

The singing did not pause. It did not answer. It simply continued, unhurried, circling her name the way water circles a stone it intends to wear away: *"Le-na. Le-na."* And beneath the name, so low she felt it more than heard it, another layer of the song—older, vaster, in no language at all, the sound a glacier would make if a glacier could grieve.

She should run. Every protocol, every drilled instruction, every story the guild told its children about the gatekeeper who listened too long—everything said *run, ring the alarm, wake Corvane, seal the stairwell.* Her legs understood this. Her legs were braced to run.

She did not run.

That was the shameful thing, the thing she would carry afterward like a stone in the shoe of her soul: in the middle of her terror, threaded through it so tightly she couldn't cut it free, was a feeling she had no right to. Because the voice knew her name. In thirty-one years—nineteen of them spent in this guildhall, useful and punctual and quietly, invisibly alone—no door had ever opened because it wanted *her* on the other side. She was the keeper of thresholds. She stood on the near side of every door in her life and recorded who passed through. And now something vast and patient and impossibly old was saying her name the way you call someone home.

*Terror,* she thought, gripping the inspection book until its spine creaked. *This is terror. Name it truly and it loses its power.*

But it didn't lose its power, because it wasn't only terror, and she couldn't lie to herself in her own ledger-hand.

The first seal broke with a sound like a sigh.

Not a crack—a sigh, a long exhalation of wax and iron, and the guild's stamped sigil curled away from the gate-frame like a leaf letting go of its branch in autumn. It drifted down, spinning, and landed at her feet without a sound. The wax was warm. She knew without touching that it would be warm as skin.

"No," Lena said aloud. "No, no—you're sealed, you're *sealed*, I checked—" and even as she spoke she was moving, not away but *toward*, hands rising to the second sigil as though she could hold the gate shut with her body, her palms, her stubborn ordinary weight. "Corvane! *Corvane!*"

Her voice hit the seventh seal—the silence-pocket—and died. The room ate it. The room ate everything now except the singing, which swelled gently, tenderly, *Le-na, Le-na*, and the second sigil sighed loose under her palms and drifted past her shoulder like a moth.

The chains went next. They didn't break; they *unfastened*, link by link, each inscribed word of holding going dark as a snuffed lamp, and the silver pooled on the flagstones in gleaming coils like shed skins. Lena stumbled backward, snatched up her lantern, and the blade of light shook wildly across the carven walls—and in that shaking light she saw, for the first time in eleven years of inspections, that the ancient script was *moving*. Not the carvings themselves. Something behind them, reading them, or writing them, or—her mind flinched from the thought—*singing them*, the grooves lighting faintly gold in waves that traveled upward toward the bell of the ceiling and faded there like ripples reaching shore.

The gate-glass lattice bloomed.

That was the only word for it. The grown panes of glass, which had hummed their single floor-of-the-world note for as long as anyone remembered, peeled outward from the center like the petals of a flower opening in fast motion, each pane folding back with a chime, a high sweet note, and where they folded away the depth behind the gate came forward—not spilling, not pouring, but *arriving*, the way dawn arrives, everywhere at once, patient and total.

Lena backed against the far wall. The inspection book was still in her hand. Absurdly, terribly, some clerk-deep reflex made her clutch it tighter, as if the record mattered, as if someone would want the entry: *Eleventh bell. All seals failed. Cause unknown. Keeper present.*

The last seal was the silence, and the silence broke last, and when it broke the full song came through.

It was not loud. That was the horror and the beauty of it—it was never loud. It was simply *everywhere*, a chord made of voices, hundreds, thousands, men's and women's and voices that belonged to neither, braided so tightly they became one weather-system of sound, and woven through the braid, clear as a thread of silver, her name. Not sung at her now. Sung *to* her. An invitation has a shape, and she could feel the shape of this one pressing against her like wind against a sail.

"Don't," she whispered, to the gate, to the song, to whatever was listening on the other side of the color-that-wasn't-a-color. "I'm not—I'm nobody. I balance accounts. I check seals. You've got the wrong—"

The pull took her mid-sentence.

It did not drag. Dragging implies resistance overcome, and the terrible truth was that her body offered almost none. It was like stepping into a current she had been standing beside her whole life without knowing it was there—one moment the flagstones were under her boots, the lantern tumbling from her hand, and the next the world had tilted and she was moving, flowing, *falling sideways* through the open petals of the gate, and the last things she registered of her own world came in fragments, in the order they left her:

The warmth of the candles she would never blow out.

The green-brass plaques, flashing past, *one memory surrendered gladly*.

The inspection book tearing from her fingers, pages fanning white as startled birds.

And underneath everything, holding her like a hand under a falling child, the song—her name in it still, but changed now, completed, the way a chord completes when the last voice finally joins.

The gate did not slam shut behind her. It closed the way a flower closes at dusk: softly, petal by petal, with a patience that suggested it had all the time in the world, and all the worlds beyond it.

In the empty gate chamber, the candles burned on. On the flagstones lay a torn page from an inspection log, and by the guttering light it was just possible to read the last line of the last entry ever made by Lena Sandoval, keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things:

*Lattice pitch elevated. Possible resonance drift. Will re-check at close.*

* * *

Falling between worlds takes longer than a scream and shorter than a thought, and it smells like rain about to happen.

Lena fell through a darkness that was not empty—it was *full*, crowded with a pressure like the moment before lightning, and the song was with her, wrapped around her, and she had stopped screaming somewhere in the first endless instant because screaming required breath and the between-places did not seem to traffic in breath. She thought of absurd things. The kettle she'd left on her cell shelf. The prophecy book, still in her satchel—still *with her*, she realized, its weight knocking against her hip, the only piece of her old life to make the crossing. She thought: *Corvane will find my lantern. He'll know I'm gone. He'll double the seals. He'll write it in his ledger with his steady hand.*

He'll know, she thought, and did not finish the thought, because finishing it meant asking why a master who ordered the gate double-sealed had not seemed surprised by her name in a century-old book.

Then light.

It came up at her like a tide coming in—gray-gold, diffuse, sourceless—and with it came ground, sudden and merciful, and Lena Sandoval landed face-down in wet grass that smelled of a season she couldn't name, in a twilight that was neither evening nor morning, under a sky the color of the inside of a shell.

For a long time she did not move. The grass bent around her. Somewhere nearby, water was running—she could hear it clearly, a bright conversational trickle—but the sound was wrong, and it took her dazed mind several attempts to identify why: it was getting *quieter* as it approached and louder as it receded, as though the stream were running uphill, as though this place had never agreed to the ordinary rules.

*Get up,* she told herself. *Keepers get up. Keepers take inventory.*

She got up. She took inventory: one gatekeeper, upright, uninjured, shaking so hard her teeth chattered. One satchel containing one forbidden prophecy book with her name in it. One iron inspection ring on one steady, steady hand.

And the song was gone. That was the strangest thing—the vast chord, the patient voice, all of it vanished the moment she crossed, leaving a silence so complete she could hear her own pulse and, beneath it, far off, that uphill water, and beneath *that*—footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. Approaching through the twilight grass with the confidence of someone arriving at an appointment made long ago.

Lena turned, fists raised in a guard stance she'd learned from a dockside brawler and never mentioned to the guild.

A figure resolved out of the gray-gold light: a man, tall, travel-worn, a courier's satchel slung across a coat scarred at the shoulder by something that had left marks no needle could mend. His hair was more gray than brown, though his face was young-ish underneath it, the way faces get when they've been tired for a decade. He stopped six paces away, looked at her—looked *through* her, briefly, the way you check a delivered letter against its address—and then something in his weathered expression cracked open with a relief so naked it was almost embarrassing to witness.

"You're late," he said, in the tone of a man greeting a friend across a market square. "Eleven years, four months. But you're here."

The twilight held its breath. The uphill stream murmured its backwards song. And Lena, keeper of thresholds, standing on the wrong side of the only door that had ever wanted her, found her voice and spent it on the only question she had left:

"*Late?* Late for *what*?"

The man smiled, and it was a tired smile, and a hopeful one, and it frightened her more than the singing had.

"For the war," he said. "Come on. There's a long road, and you'd best hear it walking."
