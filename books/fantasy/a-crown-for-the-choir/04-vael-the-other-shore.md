## 4. Vael, the Other Shore

The first thing Lena noticed was the light.

It was not dawn, though it had the pale, tentative quality of dawn. It was not dusk, though it carried dusk's long amber shadows. It was both at once, held in suspension, as if the sky could not decide which way to turn and had simply stopped trying. The sun—if it was a sun—hung low and swollen at the horizon, and it did not move. Lena lay on her back in wet grass and watched it for a long time, the way one watches a candle flame, and it did not flicker, did not sink, did not rise. It simply was.

Her ledger was still clutched against her chest.

She became aware of it before she became aware of anything else—the hard rectangle of it pressing through her coat, the corner of a page creased against her collarbone. She had been holding it when the gate opened. She was holding it now. Some small, stubborn part of her mind had carried it across whatever distance she had crossed, the way a drowning woman carries a stone, and she almost laughed at the absurdity of it: *I have brought the accounts. Wherever this is, the accounts have come with me.*

Then the rest of her arrived, all at once.

The grass was wet, but not with dew. It was warm, faintly sweet-smelling, and when she lifted her hand from it her palm came away silvered, as though she had been lying in a shallow pool of mercury. The blades of grass themselves were wrong—too long, too narrow, each one tipped with a bead of light that pulsed gently, in and out, like breathing. The meadow rolled away from her in soft swells toward a line of trees whose leaves were the color of old pewter. Above the trees, the sky was not the sky she knew. It was deeper, richer, a blue so dark it was nearly violet, scattered with stars that were visible even in this half-light, as though the heavens had never fully committed to being day.

And the rivers ran uphill.

There were three of them, or perhaps three branches of one, threading down through the meadow in bright ribbons—and *down* was not the word, because the water was climbing. Lena sat up too fast and the world tilted, her stomach lurching, and she had to press both hands into the warm grass and breathe until the meadow stopped spinning. When she looked again, the rivers were still climbing. They moved without sound, without hurry, sliding up the gentle slopes toward the pewter trees, pooling briefly in the hollows before rising out of them like smoke given the patience to be liquid.

"That's not possible," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange here—thinner, as if the air were less dense, as if the world were listening and had turned its attention down a notch to hear her better.

She said it again, louder, and the nearest beads of light on the grass-tips flared briefly in answer, and she decided not to say anything else for a while.

*Ledger,* she told herself. *Inventory. You are Lena Sandoval, gatekeeper and ledger-keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, and you are going to do what you always do, which is write down what is true.*

She patted her coat. Her inkhorn was there. Her pen was there. Her knife was there—small, practical, more useful for trimming wicks than for anything else, but a blade was a blade. The prophecy book was gone. That thought landed in her like a cold coin dropping through her ribs: the book with tonight's date and her own name inked a century before her birth had not crossed over with her. Either it had been left behind, or it had never needed to come. Either possibility was worse than the other.

She got to her feet, slowly, one hand pressed to her sternum as if she could hold her heartbeat steady through her ribs. The meadow stretched in every direction. No walls. No guildhall. No smell of tallow and ink and the particular staleness of the corridor outside the gate chamber. No Master Corvane's voice telling her it was a scribal error, go home, seal the door.

The gate.

She turned in a slow circle, looking for it—looking for *any* door, any arch, any seam in the air. The meadow offered her pewter trees, climbing rivers, the motionless sun, and stars that watched like patient eyes. No way back. The realization did not arrive as panic. It arrived as a kind of professional horror, the horror of a woman who kept accounts and understood, better than anyone alive, what it meant when the numbers did not balance. She had been entered into a ledger she had never opened. Someone, or something, had recorded her on this side of the page.

She was still standing there, inkhorn in hand like a talisman, when she heard the bell.

It was not a guild bell. It was smaller, brighter, a sound like a struck glass, and it came from the tree line—once, twice, a little rhythm, almost casual. Then a voice, human and rough-edged and unmistakably annoyed:

"Down. Get *down*—you great luminous idiot, she's not going to eat you—"

A horse emerged from the pewter trees.

Lena had grown up around the guild's cart-horses, heavy, sensible animals that smelled of straw and complained about hills. This was not that. This creature was narrow-boned and tall, its coat a deep slate grey that shimmered faintly, and along its neck ran a line of the same breathing lights that tipped the grass, a ruff of small silver flames that flickered when it tossed its head. It regarded Lena with large, dark, frankly judgmental eyes.

The man on its back swung down with the ease of someone who had done it ten thousand times, and Lena's hand went to her knife before her mind caught up with her body.

He was young—perhaps her age, perhaps a little older—and built like someone who had been lean once and then hardened, whipcord drawn tight over bone. His travel clothes had been good once and were now patched so many times the patches outnumbered the original cloth. A courier's satchel hung across his chest, scarred leather stamped with a crest she did not recognize: a circle broken by a single vertical line. And his face—

His face stopped her. Not the features themselves, which were ordinary enough, weathered and wind-burnt, with dark hair cut short and practical. It was the scar. It ran from his left temple down across his cheek to the corner of his jaw, old and white and deliberate-looking, the kind of scar that came from a blade drawn slowly rather than a fall taken quickly. It pulled his mouth slightly at one corner, so that even at rest he looked half-smiling, which Lena suspected was a lie the scar told about him.

He was staring at her. Not rudely—*searchingly*, the way she herself stared at a ledger line that refused to add up, looking for the entry that would make it true.

"You're her," he said.

Lena did not answer. She shifted her weight so that her back foot was set, the way the enforcers had taught the gatekeepers during the year of the dock riots—a small thing, a readiness no one was meant to notice. He noticed. Something flickered across his face that might have been approval.

"You're the ledger-keeper," he said again, and now there was something in his voice that was not searchiness at all. It was relief, and it was so naked, so unguarded, that it disarmed her more than any weapon could have. "The one from the other shore. You're—gates and seals and *ink*, you're the one who keeps the accounts." He laughed, a short, disbelieving sound, and dragged a hand down the scarred side of his face. "Nine years. Nine years I've been running the wayshrines, and every one of them cold, every one of them dead, and I told them—I told the Council, I told anyone who'd listen—the ledger-keeper will come. The book will pull her. It pulled the others' names, it'll pull hers." He stopped. Breathe, said the set of his shoulders, the discipline of it. "I'm Dain. Dain Ash. I'm a courier of the free cities. And you, mistress, are standing in the Meadow of the Unspent Hour, which is the single worst place in Vael to be standing after dark, and it is always, always after dark here, so we need to move."

"Stop," Lena said.

He stopped. He was good at stopping, she noted. A courier's discipline.

"I'm not going anywhere," she said, "until you answer some questions, and you're going to answer them in order, because I keep accounts and accounts have an order. First: where am I? Second: how do you know my—"

"You're in Vael," he said.

"Third: how do you know who I am? I hadn't finished. Fourth: what is this place, the meadow, why does the grass *glow*, why do the rivers run *upward*—"

"Because water remembers where it's been and wants to go back," Dain said, as if this were an answer and not a riddle. "The grass glows because the Hour here was never spent—it's the last hour of a day that ended a hundred years ago and never quite finished. And I know who you are because I have spent nine years waiting for you, and a man who waits nine years learns the face he's waiting for." He said it simply, without drama, and somehow that made it worse. "Lena Sandoval. Gatekeeper. The book named you tonight. I felt the wayshrines wake at moonrise—all of them, every dead shrine from Errenfell to the border-stones, singing like someone had struck a chord that hadn't been struck in a century. I've been riding since."

Lena's mouth had gone dry. "The book named me," she repeated. Not a question. She hated that it had come out not a question.

"Didn't it name you on your side too?" Dain asked, and when she didn't answer, something in his expression shifted—sobered, gentled. "It did. I can see it did. That's how it works, mistress. That's how it always worked. The covenant book writes both shores at once. A hundred years ago it named the singers, and they came, and they sang, and the two worlds held together. The book has been silent since the payments stopped." He looked up at the motionless sun, at the violet dark behind it, and his jaw tightened. "Tonight it stopped being silent."

"The payments," Lena said. The word snagged on something—a splinter of memory, Master Corvane's voice in the ledger hall, *the impossible trades, girl, the guild's whole purpose*—and she set it aside, because it would not sit still long enough to examine, and because Dain had said *after dark* in a meadow where dark had never finished falling, and some animal part of her brain had begun, quietly, to count that as a debt coming due.

"You said this is the worst place to be standing," she said. "Why?"

Dain looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the tree line, and Lena followed his gaze, and saw what she had not seen before: that the pewter trees at the meadow's edge were not all still. Here and there, among the silvered branches, something moved—not wind. The movement was *wrong*, low and jointed, a suggestion of limbs folded up like a closed hand.

"The Unspent Hour belongs to things that were never paid for," Dain said quietly. "When the covenant broke, everything owed on this side came due, and nothing came to settle it, and the debts—well. Debts don't die, mistress. You of all people should know that. They wait. And they've gotten *hungry*." He whistled softly; the slate-grey horse stepped back toward the trees with the unhurried grace of a creature that had done this retreat many times. "I have food, I have a waystation two hours' ride from here under old wards, and I have nine years of answers. But I can't give you any of them if something with too many joints eats us in the next quarter hour. So." He extended one hand, palm up—not to take hers, she noticed, but open, an offer, a courier's gesture, *I carry what's entrusted to me*. "Questions in order. I swear it. But in the saddle."

Lena looked at the hand. She looked at the moving shapes in the trees. She looked at the climbing rivers, at the breathing grass, at the sun that would not set and the stars that would not wait, at all of it, this whole impossible shore where her name had been written before she was born.

Every instinct the guild had built into her said: *seal the door, balance the page, trust the masters, go home.* But the gate had opened *for* her. The book had written *her*. And Master Corvane had lied to her face with a chained ledger on his wrist, and she had known it was a lie, and she had let him tell it anyway because the alternative was a world with no order in it at all.

The alternative, she was beginning to understand, was *this*.

She did not take his hand. She took the horse's mane instead—her own choice, her own grip—and swung up behind the saddle with a gracelessness that made the scarred corner of Dain's mouth twitch, though he had the wisdom not to comment.

"Questions in order," she said, low, close to his ear as the horse turned toward the pewter trees. "And Dain Ash—if I find out you've been waiting nine years for a reason that isn't the one you've told me, I will enter it in my ledger, and I will make you read the entry aloud."

"Fair terms," Dain said, and clicked his tongue, and the horse stepped into the silvered dark of the trees just as the folded things at the meadow's edge began, softly and all together, to unfold.

Behind them, in the grass of the Unspent Hour, the beads of light flared once, all at once, like a held breath released—

—and far behind them, at the meadow's far edge where no gate had been, the air split open with a sound like tearing silk, and something else came through.
