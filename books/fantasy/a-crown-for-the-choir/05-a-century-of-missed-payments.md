## 5. A Century of Missed Payments

Dain Ash had carried the ledger-keeper across three miles of meadow that ran uphill toward the sky, and she had not once asked him to put her down.

He noticed things like that. Twenty years as a courier through a world gone strange had taught him to read people the way he read weather—the set of a jaw before a storm, the silence that meant a decision was being made. Lena Sandoval weighed less than he expected and held herself tighter than she needed to, arms crossed over her chest even in his grip, as though she could carry herself if only her body would agree to it. Her eyes never stopped moving. Cataloguing. The rivers running wrong. The twin moons hanging like lamps over a table no one had set for a hundred years. The grass that chimed faintly when the wind moved through it, each blade tipped with a seed of pale glass.

She was frightened. She was also, he realized with something that felt dangerously like hope, taking notes in her head.

He set her down at last where the meadow crested and the land below opened like a hand unclenching: a valley stitched with terraces gone wild, orchards swallowed in flowering vine, and at its heart the grey bones of a town whose rooftops had bloomed into gardens no one tended. Smoke rose from a handful of chimneys—only a handful. Once there had been hundreds.

"What is this place?" she asked. Her voice was steadier than her hands.

"Errenfell," Dain said. "Or what's left of it. In your guild's books—assuming they kept honest ones—it was called the First Shore." He watched the word *guild* land on her, watched her flinch and refuse to show it. "Welcome to Vael, Keeper Sandoval."

* * *

He made tea from dried firethorn leaves on a stove that burned without wood—a small mercy of a dying world, heat remembered into a hearthstone by someone long dead—and while the water muttered, he told her everything.

Not because he trusted her. He didn't, yet. But because he had waited eleven years for her to fall out of that gate, and he had learned that waiting curdles into bitterness unless you spend it preparing what you'll say. He'd rehearsed this telling in shepherd's huts and smugglers' attics, in the pauses between storms. He had imagined saying it to someone tall and terrible, a warrior with prophecy written on her face. Instead there was this: a woman with ink stains on her fingers and candle-wax freckling one sleeve, who looked at the impossible the way other people looked at arithmetic errors—with an itch to correct them.

"The covenant," he began, "is older than any kingdom on either shore. Before your cities had walls, before our valleys had names, there was the seam—the place where your world and ours press against each other like two hands pressed palm to palm. You live on one side of the hands. We live on the other."

Lena sat forward, elbows on knees, and did not interrupt. A ledger-keeper's discipline. Good.

"Two worlds pressed together will grind, given time. Magic bleeds through the seam like water through a bad roof. On our side it made wonders. On yours—" he shrugged, "—it made stories. Children born knowing songs no one taught them. Crops that ripened overnight. Things your people couldn't explain, so eventually you stopped explaining and started trading."

"We trade in impossibilities," she said slowly. "The Guild of Impossible Things. Miracles brokered, wonders delivered. I balance those accounts. I've seen the ledgers—payments going *out*, always out, and I never knew to whom."

"Now you do." The tea boiled. He poured two cups and pushed one across the table toward her, watching her hesitate over whether to drink anything offered by a stranger in a strange world, and watching her decide—deliberately, visibly—to trust him one inch. "Every impossible thing your guild delivered was tribute. Every wonder sent through the seam was a payment on the covenant. Nine singers forged it, back at the beginning—nine voices braided into one song that holds the two worlds gently together. Not sealed apart. Not merged. Held. Like a chord held under a breath. And the song needs feeding, Keeper. Voices wear out. Notes fade. So the covenant was written so that the debt renews: on your side, the guild pays in wonders. On ours, the shrines pay in song. As long as both sides keep their accounts, the seam stays quiet, the magic flows where it should, and neither world bleeds into the other."

"And if the accounts stop?"

Dain looked out the window at Errenfell's flowering ruin, and found he couldn't answer right away. Eleven years, and the grief still caught in his throat like a fishbone.

"Then you get this," he said finally. "You get a world where the magic has nowhere to go, so it goes everywhere. Rivers run uphill because nothing tells them which way is down anymore. Grass grows glass seeds. Old shrines wake up and start singing hymns in languages dead a thousand years, and children are born remembering floods that haven't happened yet. It sounds beautiful." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "Tourists would love it, if we still had tourists. If we still had roads safe enough to walk. That's the cruelty of it, Keeper. The sickness looks like splendor right up until it kills you."

She turned her cup in her hands, not drinking. "When did the payments stop?"

"Fifty-three years ago." He said it without checking, because he'd said it ten thousand times, to councils that wouldn't listen and taverns that wouldn't care and one usurper's tribunal that had cared very much indeed. "Your guild's ledgers just—end. One season the wonders cross the seam regular as tides. The next, silence. No letter. No explanation. The shrines waited, at first. They sang louder, thinking maybe your side had simply forgotten the tune. Then they started failing. Then the first border-stone cracked."

"And your people blamed us."

"Our people *starved*, and then blamed us, and then someone stood up in the wreckage and said: the fault isn't ours, it isn't the singers', it's theirs—the other shore, the guild, the world that took our magic for a century and then closed its purse. And people were hungry enough to believe him." Dain wrapped his scarred hands around his own cup. He'd earned these scars hauling cargo through the borderlands after the roads went lawless; he'd earned worse ones since. "His name was Malvane. He wasn't a lord or a general. He was a shrine-keeper's apprentice. Clever. Charismatic. And angry in a way that doesn't burn out—anger like a coal at the center of a man, that heats everything around it without ever consuming itself. He said the covenant was slavery dressed up as harmony. He said Vael had spent a thousand years paying rent to a world that wouldn't even send a courtesy note explaining why the payments stopped. And when the old order tried to quiet him, he had already taught half the valley to sing his version of events."

Lena's eyes had gone distant, calculating. He could nearly see the columns adding themselves behind them. "Fifty-three years," she murmured. "I've read ledgers older than that. The guildhall archive goes back six centuries. If the payments stopped, there'd be a record. A last entry. An account closed." Her jaw tightened. "There isn't. I balance those books every night. The impossible trades continue—they've *never* stopped. Wonders go out through the gate every quarter, same as always."

Something cold walked down Dain's spine. "Then where do they go?"

"That's what I intend to find out." She said it quietly, but there was iron under the quiet. "Someone on my side has been taking payment for goods never delivered. For fifty-three years. Someone has been letting your world bleed dry and pocketing the difference."

* * *

They walked out at dusk, because Dain wanted her to see it—not the prettiness of the ruined valley, but the truth underneath the prettiness. The glass-seeded grass had gone amber in the low light and rang softly underfoot like wind chimes buried in the earth. Overhead, the twin moons were rising, one silver and one a bruised, unhealthy red that hadn't been there in his grandfather's day.

"That moon's new," Lena said.

"Not new. *Arrived.* It came through the seam thirty years ago, or a piece of something did, and the sky here grew around it like flesh around a splinter." He gestured eastward, where the hills darkened against the last light. "The shrines used to hold all this. Nine great shrines, one for each singer, strung along the seam like beads on a thread. When the payments stopped, the singers' heirs tried to keep the song alive alone. They managed it for a generation—barely. But a braid of nine strands can be held by eight for a while. By seven, longer. By four—" He stopped walking. Below them, in the flooded ruins of what had once been Errenfell's temple quarter, water lay mirror-still among the broken columns, and in that water, faintly, lights were moving. Not reflections. Lights with intentions. "By four, you get *that*. Wild magic pooling in the low places, growing teeth. The villagers call them the drowned choir. Sometimes on quiet nights they sing. Sometimes what they sing comes true, and nobody wants it to."

Lena stood very still beside him. He watched her cataloguing again—the drowned lights, the wrong moons, the singing grass—and watched her do the thing he'd already begun to hope she'd do: refuse to look away.

"You said Malvane rose in the vacuum," she said. "Rose to what?"

"Everything." Dain's voice flattened. "He started as a voice in the market square. Within five years he had the river valleys. Within ten, an army—not soldiers at first, just the desperate and the grieving, which is the rawest ore there is for forging armies. Now he holds the eastern marches and calls himself the Regent of the True Shore. He claims the covenant enslaved us. He claims your guild stole fifty years of tribute. And every year the wild magic spreads further west, and every year more towns decide that a strong hand—even a cruel one—is better than drowning in miracles." He paused. "In three days, his vanguard reaches the border-stones. The last markers between his territory and the free cities. If the stones fall, the seam destabilizes completely, and both our worlds come apart at the pressing-point."

"And the prophecy." She said the word carefully, the way you handle a knife by the flat of the blade. "You knew I was coming. You said you'd waited years. There's a book in my guildhall—a century old—that has tonight's date in it, and my name, next to an omen of war. How does your world know mine?"

For a long moment Dain said nothing. The drowned choir flickered below, and somewhere far off, a shrine bell tolled once, though he knew there was no bell left in that tower to toll.

"Because the ninth singer wrote it down," he said at last. "Before she vanished. Before your guild's masters scrubbed her name from every mural and every record on this side of the seam. She saw what was coming—the missed payments, the war, the keeper who'd have to choose—and she wrote it in a book and hid it where only her own bloodline would ever find it." He turned to face Lena fully, and saw the color leave her face in the moonlight, and hated himself a little for what he was about to take from her. "Your guild raised you, didn't it? Foundling. Brought in young. Given the ledgers because you had a gift for numbers no one could explain."

"Yes," she whispered. "How did you—"

"Because that's how she planned it. The ninth singer fled to your world fifty-three years ago, the very season the payments stopped, carrying the final verse of the covenant in her keeping. And she left behind a granddaughter who would grow up inside the guild's own walls, balancing its books, close enough to see exactly where the accounts went wrong." He let out a breath he felt he'd been holding for eleven years. "The prophecy didn't choose you at random, Keeper Sandoval. You weren't pulled through that gate by accident. You're the inheritance. You're the verse coming home."

* * *

Later, wrapped in a blanket by the woodless fire while he pretended to sleep, Lena spoke into the dark, thinking he couldn't hear her. He heard every word.

"They lied to me," she said softly, to no one. To the fire, maybe. To herself. "My whole life. Every master, every ledger line, every ceremony where they thanked the founders for their generosity. Fifty-three years of stolen tribute, and they let a whole world bleed." A pause, long enough that he almost thought she'd finished. Then, quieter, with something in it that made his chest ache: "I balanced their books. Every night. I kept their lies tidy."

Dain stared at the ceiling beams and said nothing, because there was nothing kind to say yet, and he had learned that some griefs need silence more than comfort.

But beneath his pity, carefully banked where he didn't have to look at it directly, something else was burning. Hope. Real hope, dangerous hope, the kind he'd sworn off years ago after too many councils and too many failed shrines. Because the woman lying awake by his fire had not wept, and had not raged uselessly, and had not asked *why me?*

She had asked *where do the payments go?*—like a ledger-keeper hunting a discrepancy.

And when she slept at last, breath evening out, the glass-seeded grass outside chimed softly in the night wind, and far to the east, past the sleeping hills, the horizon glowed a faint bruised red that was not any moon. Malvane's war-fires, already marching toward the border-stones.

Three days, Dain thought. Three days to find a way to stop an army with a song, using a keeper who didn't yet know what she was carrying, in a world that had forgotten how to listen.

He closed his eyes. Tomorrow they would walk north, toward the drowned shrine at Errenfell's heart, where—if the old tales held—one of the nine might still be living: a fisherwoman, ancient now, whose lullabies could bend the tide.

Tomorrow he would introduce her to the first singer.

Tonight, for the first time in eleven years, he let himself hope—and found it hurt more than despair ever had.
