## 6. The Usurper's Shadow

Dain had watched the road from Cindrel Pass a hundred times in his life, and it had never once frightened him the way it frightened him now.

He crouched in the heather at the lip of the ridge, one hand flat against the cold of the stone beneath him, and let the wind carry the sound up to him. Not drums—not yet. What came first was the ground itself: a low, patient tremor, like the heartbeat of something buried and waking. Then the dust, a brown-gold haze rising above the tree line in a long unbroken banner. Then, at last, the columns.

They came out of the pass like ink poured into water.

Lena Sandoval stood beside him at the ridge's edge, her ledger still slung across her shoulder—she would not put it down, he had noticed, not to sleep, not to eat, as if the weight of it were the only proof that her old life had existed at all. Her knuckles had gone white around the strap.

"How many?" she asked. Her voice was steady. Dain gave her credit for that. Most people who had crossed the seam only days ago would have been weeping by now, or screaming, or both.

"Four thousand on this road," he said. "There'll be as many again on the coast road, and more behind them that we can't see. Malvane doesn't march with armies, keeper. He marches with *weather*."

The columns moved with an awful discipline, no banners snapping, no war-cries, only the soft iron rhythm of boots on the old paved road—the same road, Dain knew, that had carried pilgrims and singers in the years before the payments stopped, when the two realms had still traded in impossible things and neither had thought to make a weapon of it. The soldiers wore ash-grey, and above their helmets they carried no sigil at all. That was the worst of it. An army with a sigil wanted something. An army without one had already been told what it wanted, and had stopped asking.

"Where are they going?" Lena asked, though he could see her working it out even as she spoke, her eyes following the road's long curve down from the pass, past the drowned orchards, past the shrine at the ford, to where the land rose again into a line of standing stones that caught the dying light like a row of yellowed teeth.

"The border-stones," Dain said. "The seam of the worlds. Your gate is one of them—or rather, your gate is what the stones look like from your side. From this side they're older. Rougher. They were standing before either realm had a name for itself."

"And if he takes them?"

Dain didn't answer at once. Below them, the column reached the ford and began to cross, the river running uphill as it always did in Vael, water climbing the stones in bright ropes, and the soldiers wading through the impossible current without a flicker of wonder. That was what Malvane's long war had done. It had taught a whole generation to stop seeing.

"If he takes them," Dain said finally, "he doesn't need to break the covenant anymore. He just needs to stand where it used to be. A covenant is a door, keeper. And a man standing in a doorway can decide who walks through it for as long as he likes."

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow beneath the ridge, in the lee of a fallen wayshrine whose carved face had been worn smooth by centuries of rain. Dain built a small fire—small, always small, smoke was a signature—and shared out the hard black bread and the river-fish he'd taken at dusk, fish that had tasted of honey and salt in the old days and now tasted mostly of mud, because the rivers were forgetting what they were for.

Lena ate in silence, her eyes on the fire. Then, without looking up, she said: "You said you'd waited years for the ledger-keeper to arrive. Back in the meadow. You said it like a man quoting something."

Dain turned the fish over on his stone. The fire crackled. Somewhere far off, too far to be anything but imagination, he thought he heard the tremor of the marching column, the ground remembering boots.

"There's a book," he said. "In Bellhollow. The archive-city. You'll see it soon enough—I mean to take you there, if the roads hold. It's the oldest thing we have. Older than the guild, older than the stones. A prophecy book, written a century ago by nine hands."

He watched her go very still. He'd learned to watch for it, the way people froze when a story came near the wound in them.

"I know the book," she said quietly. "Or—I know *a* book. The guild keeps one. A century old. I balance its shelf against the ledger every season." A pause. "It has tonight's date in it. And my name."

"I know," Dain said.

Her head came up sharply. "You *know*? How could you possibly—"

"Because the book in Bellhollow says the same thing, keeper. Same date. Same name. Two books, two realms, one hundred years apart, and they agree." He fed a twig to the fire. "The guild's copy is a daughter of ours, or ours is a daughter of theirs. The singers who wrote the original made copies before the covenant was sealed. It was meant as a mercy, I think. So that both worlds would know what was coming."

"And what does yours say?" Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading an account she suspected was padded. "About me. Exactly."

Dain looked into the fire for a long moment. He had rehearsed this. On the long walk from the meadow, while she slept, he had turned the sentence over and over, trying to find a shape for it that would not frighten her, and had failed every time, because there was no shape for it that was not frightening. So in the end he simply gave it to her straight, the way you hand someone a hot iron.

"It names a keeper," he said. "*When the ledgers fall silent and the ninth voice is lost, there shall come a keeper who chooses, out of the world of walls and wages, and at her choosing the door shall stand open or be shut forever.*" He met her eyes. "That's the line. *A keeper who chooses.* It's been read aloud in Bellhollow every season for a hundred years. There are old men in that city who've built their whole lives around waiting for you."

The fire popped. Lena did not move.

"I was *pulled*," she said at last. "Not chosen. Pulled. The gate tore open and it took me the way a flood takes a cart. I didn't choose anything. I was balancing accounts by candlelight and the world reached through a door and *took* me." Her voice rose, and she pressed it back down with visible effort, the ledger-keeper in her refusing to let the account run wild. "If your book says *chooses*, then your book is wrong, or it's been translated wrong, or—" She stopped. He watched her arrive at the thought before she said it. "Or it's convenient."

"Convenient," Dain repeated.

"For whoever's been waiting. For whoever *needs* a keeper to show up and do the choosing for them." Her jaw was set. "I've seen prophecy used as a ledger before, Dain. The masters of my guild keep a book a century old with my name in it, and when I ask them about it they call it a scribal error and seal the door twice as fast. Now I cross into a world where another book has my name, and there are old men who've built their lives around it, and an army marching on the door I came through. Forgive me if I don't fall to my knees with gratitude at being *expected*."

Dain nodded slowly. He deserved that. More than that, he understood it, in a way he could never explain to her without explaining everything else, the things he'd done, the people he'd failed, the years he'd spent carrying the word *chosen* like a stone in his boot.

"May I tell you what the waiting does to people?" he said. "Not to argue. Just so you know what you've walked into."

She said nothing, which he took for yes.

"In Bellhollow there's a woman named Ivetta who has kept the prophecy hall swept for forty years. She was born after the payments stopped, but her grandmother remembered them, and her grandmother told her: *a keeper will come, and she will choose, and it will be well.* So Ivetta sweeps. Every day. She has swept the same floor ten thousand times, waiting for a woman out of the world of walls and wages to walk in and make the world well again." Dain turned his hands toward the fire. "And there are a hundred like her. A thousand. People who have hung their whole endurance on the idea that someone is coming who will *choose* rightly, because they have lost all faith in their own choosing. The prophecy is a mercy for them, keeper. It is the only thing that has kept them from lying down in the road and letting Malvane's weather roll over them."

"And that's the trap," Lena said softly.

He looked at her.

"That's the trap," she said again. "I've watched it from the other side. The guild's masters hoarded the tribute because people *needed* the impossible things to keep coming and never asked where they came from. Need makes blind. And if a thousand people in Bellhollow need me to be their chosen keeper—" she shook her head slowly— "then I'm not a person to them anymore. I'm a payment. The last impossible thing on the last outstanding account." Her mouth twisted. "I've been a ledger-entry my whole life, Dain. I know what it's like to be a line in someone else's book. I will not be a line in a prophecy's."

The fire burned low. Dain fed it the last of the dry heather and watched the flames lean away from the wind coming off the pass, the wind that smelled, faintly, of ash. He thought about telling her. He thought about the eighth singer, and the hunters, and the road, and his own name in a different book—a debt-book, not a prophecy—and the way *chosen* had sounded in his mouth the day he'd still believed he was on the right side of it.

He told her a piece. Only a piece. The piece that was hers to hear.

"You're right," he said. "It can be a trap. Prophecy is a door the same as any other door, and a man can stand in it and decide who walks through." He looked up at her, and let her see, for one unguarded moment, how tired he was. "But here is the other thing I know, keeper. Ivetta's sweeping is not the trap. The old men's waiting is not the trap. The trap is *Malvane*. He read the same book. He read *a keeper who chooses* and he understood it perfectly—understood that if no keeper comes, or if the keeper chooses wrong, or if he can make the choosing impossible, then the door shuts forever, and whatever stands in the doorway owns both worlds." He poked the fire apart with a stick, watching the sparks climb. "The prophecy doesn't trap you. It just tells you where the trap is already waiting, and that you'll be the one standing in front of it."

Lena was quiet for a long time. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—cooler, harder, the voice he imagined she used on merchants who tried to pad their accounts.

"Then hear my account, and enter it fairly," she said. "I was pulled, not chosen. I didn't ask for this book, this war, or this seam. But I'm here, and the gate only opens from my side now, and I can't go home, and there is an army four thousand strong marching toward the door of my—" she faltered, just slightly, on the word— "of the world that raised me. So I will walk to Bellhollow. I will read your book with my own eyes. And then I will decide—not because a century of sweeping says I must, and not because Malvane's weather says I must, but because I am the keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, and I do not leave accounts unbalanced." She looked at him across the fire, and her eyes were the grey of the ridge-stone. "But if anyone—anyone—tries to *spend* me, Dain Ash, they will learn what a ledger-keeper does to a false entry."

Dain felt something in his chest loosen that had been clenched for years. Not hope, exactly. Something older and more stubborn than hope. The feeling of a debt, long unpayable, finally finding a debtor who intended to honor it.

"That," he said, "is the first thing anyone has said in this world in a hundred years that sounds like choosing."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then her gaze went past his shoulder, up the ridge, and every line of her body went taut as wire.

"Dain," she said. "The fires."

He turned. Along the whole dark shoulder of the pass, where the army had made its camp, a thousand watch-fires had bloomed at once—silent, orderly, arranged not in scattered rings but in a pattern. He knew the pattern. Every child in Vael had once known it. It was the opening figure of the covenant-song, the shape the nine singers' fires had made on the night the worlds were bound: the *seam-star*, the sign of the door held open between realms.

Malvane had made his army sleep inside the shape of the covenant. Wearing it. Mocking it. Telling everyone who could read fires what he intended: that the door would be his.

And in the center of the pattern, one fire burned higher than all the rest, and around it, faint on the wind across four miles of dark, came a sound that made Dain's blood turn to ice water.

Singing. A single voice, raised in the first line of the covenant-song—*the* first line, the one no living throat should have known—and singing it *wrong*, bent a half-tone flat, the way a lock sounds when the wrong key turns in it.

Lena's hand found the ledger at her shoulder. "What is that?" she whispered. "Who could know that song?"

Dain stood, his knees stiff, his eyes fixed on the distant fire.

"Only the nine," he said. "And the nine are dead." He swallowed. "Or taught by the nine. Come, keeper. We walk for Bellhollow tonight, and we don't stop—and pray to whatever your world prays to that the thing singing down there hasn't learned the second verse."
