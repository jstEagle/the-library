## 7. Return Refused

The wayshrine stood on a hill above the meadow where Lena had woken, and she had been climbing toward it since before dawn.

She had told herself it was only reconnaissance—a ledger-keeper's habit, cataloguing what lay around her before committing to anything. But her boots knew better than her mouth did. They had found the pilgrim path within an hour of Dain leaving her to fetch water, and they had not stopped since. The path was old, older than anything in the free cities, its stones worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet that had come here hoping for exactly what she hoped for now.

Home.

The shrine itself was smaller than she'd imagined. In the guild's oldest maps—the ones Corvane kept locked behind glass and called *speculative*—wayshrines were drawn as towers, as cathedrals of standing stone with doors tall enough for giants. This one was barely taller than Lena herself: a ring of gray stones no bigger than hearthstones, set in a circle of silvered grass, with a single lintel across the top like the frame of a door that led nowhere. Frost clung to the stones even though the morning was mild. Frost, and something else—a faint shimmer, like heat-haze in reverse, like cold made visible.

Lena stopped at the edge of the circle and made herself breathe.

*It's just a door,* she told herself. *You've walked through the guild gate ten thousand times. You've sealed it, oiled its hinges, argued with it when it stuck in wet weather.* Doors were her trade. She understood them the way other people understood weather or bread—intimately, without thinking, down to the grain of them.

But this door hummed.

Not the way the guild gate hummed—that low mechanical complaint of old iron bearing weight. This was a note, sustained and patient, so low she felt it in her back teeth more than heard it. It was the same note she'd felt in the gate the night it sang her name, and her skin remembered that night whether she wanted it to or not: the seals peeling open like petals, the pull behind her sternum, the terrible and wonderful certainty of being *chosen*.

She stepped into the circle.

The shimmer thickened around her ankles like wading into fog off cold water. The hum deepened. And in the space between the two upright stones, where there had been nothing but hillside and morning light, something began to gather—an image, faint as breath on glass. She could almost see through it. A suggestion of candlelight. A suggestion of shelves. The particular amber glow of the guildhall at night, when the lamps burned low and the ledgers lay open and everything in the world was accounted for.

Her chest went tight enough to hurt.

"Please," she said, and hated how small her voice came out. She cleared her throat and tried again, firmer, the voice she used with merchants who tried to dispute their entries. "Wayshrine. I am Lena Sandoval, keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, gatekeeper and ledger-keeper both. I request passage home."

The image sharpened. For one glorious heartbeat she could see the ledger room itself—the third shelf, the one with the warped board she'd been meaning to report for six years, the ink stain shaped like a bird on the corner of the master desk. Home. So close she could smell the tallow and the vellum.

She reached out.

The shrine rejected her the way ice rejects a warm hand—not violently, but completely, with a kind of absolute indifference. The moment her fingers touched the space between the stones, the image shattered into motes of light, the hum climbed to a whine of refusal, and something pushed back against her palm with the gentle, immovable pressure of a closed door held by someone much stronger than she was. She stumbled backward out of the circle and sat down hard in the frost-silvered grass.

For a long moment she simply sat there, breathing, the hillside quiet around her except for the shrine's patient, unbothered note.

Then she got up and tried again.

She tried with words first—formal requests, informal ones, the guild's own liturgy of passage, which she'd recited a thousand times at the closing of each day. *By ledger and by oath, by seal and by key.* Nothing. The image would gather, flicker, show her a sliver of candlelight, and collapse the instant she reached for it.

She tried with objects. Her guild seal, pressed flat against the lintel. The copper token from her first year, worn smooth by her thumb. A strand of her own hair, laid across the stones like an offering. The shrine accepted all of it and gave nothing back, humming its one low note, holding its door shut with the serene confidence of a thing that had never once been argued into opening.

She tried force.

That was the hour she was least proud of. She found a fallen branch half again as long as she was and swung it against the stones until her shoulders screamed and the branch splintered and the shrine didn't so much as crack its frost. She kicked it. She shouted at it—words she hadn't said aloud since she was a child crying in the coal-chutes, words about fairness and promises and being left. The hillside took her rage and gave it back as echo, thin and embarrassing in the wide morning air.

And through all of it, the shrine hummed. Patient. Unmoved. Almost—she thought wildly, leaning against the splintered branch, sweat freezing on her spine—almost *sorry*. There was a quality to the refusal that wasn't cruelty. It was the refusal of a lock that knows it is doing its job.

"It's not broken," she said finally, and the sound of her own voice saying it made it true. "You're not broken. You're *closed*."

She slid down against the largest stone until she was sitting in the grass, and she let herself think it properly, because she had been refusing to think it since dawn: the gate only opened from her side now. Whatever had pulled her through—prophecy, covenant, the hungry need of a dying world—it had spent its strength getting her here. The door home was a door with the handle on the far side. Someone would have to open it from within.

Someone would have to notice she was gone.

Her mind turned, traitorously, to the guildhall. To the evening shift that would find her office empty, her lamp still burning, her cup of tea gone cold beside the open prophecy book with tonight's date inked in a century-old hand. To Master Corvane reading that entry and going very still, the way he went still when numbers refused to balance. He would order the gate triple-sealed. He would tell the apprentices she'd been sent on an errand. He would keep his voice level and his face smooth, because Master Corvane's face was always smooth, and he would—

He would know exactly what had happened. Because he'd read the same entry she had. Because he'd chained a second ledger to his wrist rather than let anyone see what was written in it.

Lena looked at her hands. They were shaking—from the climbing, from the swinging, from the hour of shouting—but underneath the shaking they were steady in the way that mattered. Ledger-steady. The hands of a girl who had learned, at eight years old, that the world only made sense if you wrote down what actually happened instead of what you wished had happened.

*What actually happened.*

She had been taken. Not chosen—taken, whatever the prophecy wanted to pretend, whatever Dain's hopeful eyes suggested when he looked at her like an answer arriving late. She had been pulled through a sealed gate by forces she hadn't consented to, into a realm bleeding toward war, because a book written before her grandmother was born had put her name beside an omen.

And the people who raised her—the guild, her masters, the family that had fed her and taught her her letters and given her a trade and a title—they had known. Or suspected. Or arranged. One of those three, and every hour she spent in Vael narrowed the field.

The fury came up in her again, hot and useless, and she let it—and then, deliberately, deliberately the way she balanced a column that wouldn't sum, she let it cool. Fury was an expense. Fury cost warmth and sleep and judgment, and she had limited reserves of all three. What was left when fury cooled was colder and far more useful.

Purpose.

"If I can't leave," she said to the shrine, quietly, conversationally, the way she talked to stubborn locks and stuck drawers, "then I'll finish what brought me here."

The shrine hummed. If it had opinions, it kept them behind its frost.

She stood, brushed the grass from her skirt, and took inventory—because inventory was prayer, for her; inventory was how she steadied herself when the world tilted. One ledger-keeper, slightly bruised, uninjured. One guild seal, one copper token, one stub of pencil, one small knife good for apples and little else. One coat that smelled of candle smoke and home and was therefore both comfort and wound. No food. No map. One ally, scarred and strange, who claimed he'd waited years for her and who had, at least, not yet lied to her in any way she could prove.

One name, inked in a book a century old, beside an omen of war.

Hers.

* * *

Dain found her coming down the hill an hour past noon, and she watched him read her in a single sweep—the torn sleeve, the split knuckles, the set of her jaw—and wisely decide not to ask about any of it.

"No luck," he said. Not a question.

"The shrine's shut." She kept her voice level, ledger-level. "It shows me the hall sometimes. Candlelight, the third shelf, my desk. Then it pushes me away. It's not damaged. It's *closed*." She paused, and made herself say the rest, because saying things plainly was the whole discipline of her life. "The gate opens from your side now. From mine. Someone has to let me through."

Something moved behind Dain's eyes—relief, quickly buried, and she caught it and filed it and chose not to resent it. Of course he was relieved. The prophecy needed a keeper who chose, and a keeper who could simply walk home was a keeper who might choose the wrong door.

"Don't look at me like that," she said.

"Like what?"

"Like I'm staying because I want to."

"I don't think you're staying because you want to." He shouldered the water skins and started down the path, matching his pace to hers. "I think you're staying because you're the sort of person who finishes what she starts. Which is worse, for you. Wanting passes. Duty doesn't."

She almost smiled at that, and hated that she almost smiled. "You talk like a man who's read a lot of prophecies and believed very few of them."

"My mother was a shrine-tender." He said it lightly, but his hand drifted to the scars on his forearm, the old habit she'd noticed twice already. "I grew up watching people walk into circles of stone asking for miracles. Most of them got answers they didn't want. The wise ones learned to want better things."

"And the unwise ones?"

"They kept kicking the shrines." His eyes flicked to her knuckles. "Until they figured out the shrine wasn't the locked thing."

Lena stopped walking.

The wind moved through the twilight grass—Vael's grass, silvered and faintly luminous, beautiful in a way that felt like being watched—and she stood in it and let his sentence turn over in her mind the way she'd turn a suspicious entry, looking for the falseness in it, finding none.

"The shrine isn't the locked thing," she repeated.

"Never is."

"So what is?" But she was already answering herself, because the answer had been sitting in her chest since dawn, cold and clean as a balanced column. "The covenant. The payments. Whatever broke between our worlds—that's the lock. The shrines are just... hinges." She looked south, toward the horizon where Dain had pointed out the border-stones two days ago, invisible from here but present in every strained muscle of his face. "Malvane's armies are marching on the seam. Every battle weakens it further. And somewhere behind all of it is the reason a hundred-year-old book wrote my name in advance."

"Now you're thinking like a keeper," Dain said.

"I'm thinking like a ledger-keeper." She started walking again, faster now, the cold purpose settling into her stride. "Someone kept accounts on this, Dain. Covenants, tribute, payments missed, debts accrued—someone wrote it all down, because power like that doesn't move without a record. My masters have a second ledger chained to Corvane's wrist. Your usurper has an army and a grievance. Somewhere in between there's a true account of what happened a century ago, and I am going to find it, read every line of it, and then—" she heard her own voice drop into the register she used for closing out a fraudulent account, quiet and final, "—then I'm going to correct it."

They walked in silence for a while. The twilight deepened the way it seemed to do permanently in Vael, never quite night, never quite day, a world lit by perpetual dusk as though the sun itself had agreed to the covenant and was keeping its end of it.

"Lena." Dain's voice was careful. "What the correction costs—I told you the payments stopped decades ago. I haven't told you what the payments *were*. Not fully. When you know, you may wish the shrine had opened."

"Then tell me on the way."

"It's a long way. Bellhollow's archive-city, five days east, if the roads hold."

"Then you have five days to frighten me." She pulled her coat tighter and did not look back at the hill, at the little ring of stones with its patient frost and its closed door. If she looked, she would see candlelight again. The third shelf. The warped board. The bird-shaped stain. She would feel the pull of everything ordinary and safe and hers, and she could not afford it—not now, not with the cold thing in her chest that was, she was beginning to understand, the most valuable thing she owned.

Because here was the truth she'd carved out of the morning's wreckage, the entry she'd write if she dared: homesickness didn't have to be a weight. Carried rightly, it was ballast. It kept her upright in the wind. Every step she took away from that hillside was a step she took *for* the guildhall, for the free cities, for the world whose door was shut to her—so that someday it wouldn't be.

"You said the prophecy names a keeper who chooses," she said.

Dain glanced at her, wary. "I said that. You said you were pulled, not chosen."

"I was pulled." The road bent east, toward the distant smudge of Bellhollow's towers, and Lena Sandoval, gatekeeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, turned onto it with her split knuckles curled into fists and her grief converted, gram by gram, into iron. "But nobody asked the gatekeeper what happens to a door once it's been forced open. It doesn't close itself. Somebody has to stand in it." She lifted her chin toward the horizon, toward the war, toward the account waiting to be corrected. "So I'm choosing to be pulled. Let's go find out what my name is doing in that book."

Behind them, on the hill, the wayshrine hummed its single patient note—and if either of them had been listening closely, they might have heard the note change. Just slightly. Just for a moment.

Almost like approval.
