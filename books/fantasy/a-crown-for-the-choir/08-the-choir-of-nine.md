## 8. The Choir of Nine

Bellhollow announced itself before they saw it.

They had walked since dawn through country that made no sense to Lena's ledger-trained eye—orchards whose fruit ripened and rotted in the same hour, a stone bridge that arched downward into a valley instead of over it—but as the afternoon light thickened into honey, she heard it: a low, continuous murmur on the wind, like a city humming in its sleep.

"What is that?" she asked.

"Bellhollow," Dain said. "The bells never stop. They haven't in four hundred years." He walked with his scarred hand resting on the strap of his courier's satchel, and though his stride was easy, his eyes kept moving—ridge line, tree line, the empty road behind them. "The founders believed a city should never fall silent. That silence was how Malvane's kind first crept in."

"His kind?"

"People who think a quiet world is an obedient one."

The road crested a hill, and Bellhollow opened below them like something out of the prophecy book she'd left burning on her desk a world away. It was built in tiers down the bowl of a valley, and every tier bristled with bell towers—slender ones like needles, squat ones like fists, one enormous tower at the center crowned with a bell so vast that Lena could see its mouth from here, dark as a well. The murmur she'd heard was the sum of ten thousand small bells stirring in the wind, each tuned a fraction apart from its neighbors, so that the whole city sounded like a held breath about to become a chord.

Lena stopped walking. She hadn't meant to. Her body simply refused another step until it had taken all of it in.

"It's beautiful," she said, and heard the wonder in her own voice, unguarded, and hated how easily this world could still surprise her into honesty.

"It was," Dain said quietly. "Come on. We want to be inside before the evening watch. And there's someone you need to see first—or something, rather. The person can wait."

* * *

The archive stood at the heart of the middle tier, a building so old it seemed less constructed than grown, its walls of grey stone furred with pale lichen that glimmered faintly when the bells rang. Inside, the air changed. Lena felt it against her skin like stepping from sunlight into cellar-cool shade—the particular hush of a place where paper was treated as something sacred. After the ceaseless murmur of the city, the silence inside the archive was not empty but attentive, the way a room listens when many people are reading.

Dain spoke to a gatekeeper—a thin woman with ink-stained fingers and a courier's token on a cord—and after a long look at Lena, longer than courtesy required, the woman led them down stairs that spiraled below ground, past shelves that became walls, walls that became cliff faces of books. Lanterns burned in niches without anyone tending them. Lena counted the turns out of habit, the way she counted everything, and lost count somewhere past thirty.

"The Hall of Origins," the gatekeeper said at last, and unlocked a door of black wood with a key she wore around her neck like a relic. "Touch nothing. The paint remembers hands."

"The paint *remembers*—" Lena began, but Dain touched her elbow, and they went through.

The hall beyond was round and high-domed, lit by a ring of lanterns, and every inch of its curved wall was covered in mural.

Lena had seen guild frescoes before—stiff processions of masters, allegorical figures holding scales and keys, all of it painted to instruct rather than to move. This was nothing like that. This was a wall alive with color that seemed still wet after centuries: a night sky scattered with two moons, a seam of light running across it like a crack in glass, and beneath the seam, nine figures standing in a crescent, their faces upturned, their mouths open in song.

Light poured from them. Not painted light—Lena kept looking for the pigment, the gold leaf, the trick of it—but light rendered so convincingly that the shadows of the nine fell across the floor toward her feet, long and real, as if the figures were singing in the room right now and the wall were merely a window onto them.

Above each figure ran a line of script in no alphabet she knew, curling like smoke. Below them, painted smaller, was a world: cities, rivers, forests, ships—all of it stitched to the seam of light by threads of sound, visible ribbons of music rising from rooftops and fields and joining the great chord overhead.

"The covenant," Dain said. His voice had gone rough. "As it was. As it's supposed to be."

Lena stepped closer. The nine singers were rendered individually, lovingly, each distinct. A broad-shouldered woman with braids threaded with river pearls, her song drawn as water flowing upward. An old man leaning on a staff carved like a bell clapper, his music a deep gold. A young man whose ribbon of sound split into dozens of strands, one reaching toward each corner of the painted world. A girl no older than Lena whose song rose straight and white as a pillar of frost.

She read the script beneath them by instinct, tracing the shapes, and found—to her complete lack of surprise anymore—that she could almost read it. Almost. The letters swam at the edge of meaning like fish under ice. Names, she thought. Nine names.

"Dain," she said slowly. "Who are they?"

"The Choir of Nine. The founders." He came to stand beside her, and up close she saw that he wasn't looking at the mural the way a man looks at art. He was looking at it the way a man looks at a photograph of people he has failed. "When the realms were young and the seam between them was raw and bleeding, nine voices rose together and sang it whole. That's the story every child in Vael learns before they learn their own name. The covenant isn't a treaty, Lena. It's a *song*. One that has to keep being sung, or the seam starts to tear."

"And these are the original nine? From a thousand years ago?"

"No." His jaw tightened. "That's what everyone believes, because it's easier. But the choir isn't nine people from one age. It's nine *voices*—nine roles in the song. When a singer dies, the voice passes. Sometimes to a child born with the gift. Sometimes it lies dormant for a generation, waiting. The mural shows the last full choir—the nine who sang the covenant's renewal, ninety years ago. The last time all nine stood together."

Lena studied the faces again with new attention. Ninety years. People, then. People with names and histories, and somewhere in Vael, perhaps, descendants.

"Where are they now?"

Dain didn't answer at once. He walked the curve of the wall, slow, and Lena followed, and one by one he told her, his voice dropping lower with each figure as though the hall itself might grieve aloud.

"Maren of the Tidesong." He touched the pearl-braided woman. "Her voice bends water. Rivers, rain, the sea. She walked out of the public record forty years ago. Some say dead. I say hiding." A ghost of a smile. "I intend to prove which."

He moved on. "Orlin Vael-Keeper of Bells—no relation to the city, despite the name. He rang the mountain shrines, the deep anchors of the song. He was the loudest and the gentlest man I ever met." His hand hovered over the old man with the clapper-staff. "He stopped ringing twenty years ago. They say he drank himself deaf in a mining town called Greyfall. They also say he did it on purpose, because a deaf man can't hear what the world has become."

The young man with the branching song: "Tovan. Killed. Fifteen years ago, in the first of Malvane's purges. They burned him in his own village and called it a fever." Dain's voice flattened. "There were witnesses. There are always witnesses. None of them sang at his pyre."

The frost-pillar girl: "Iselle. She fled across the seam itself, into your world, when she was nineteen. The gatekeepers of your guild took her in—or so the story goes." He glanced at Lena sideways. "Your guild has a long memory for some things and a convenient hole for others."

One by one: a singer drowned, a singer mad, a singer vanished into the eastern wastes, a singer who renounced her voice and took vows of silence in a cloister that had since burned. Eight stories, and each one landed on Lena like a stone dropped down a well—she heard it fall and fall and never heard it land.

Eight. And then they came to the ninth.

* * *

The ninth figure stood at the center of the crescent, and even scratched out, even ruined, she dominated the mural.

Someone had taken a blade or a scraper to the plaster—not carelessly, but thoroughly, methodically, gouging away the face entirely and with it the throat, the hands, the ribbon of song that had flowed from her mouth. The destruction was older than the rest of the damage in the hall; the edges of the wound had been repaired and repainted around, generations of archivists preserving the absence as carefully as they preserved the presence. Where the other eight shone, the ninth was a silhouette of raw plaster shaped exactly like a person, and somehow that was worse than any face would have been. Lena's eyes kept trying to finish her. Kept trying to give her back her eyes, her mouth, her voice.

And failing. Because the erasure had been done by someone who understood exactly which strokes carried a face.

"She was the first among them," Dain said. "The lead voice. The one the others tuned themselves to. The script above her—" he pointed to the curling smoke-letters, more ornate than the rest, "—names her. In the old tongue it reads roughly as *the Verse-Bearer*. The one who carried the final verse. The keystone of the whole song."

"Who destroyed her face?"

"No one knows. Or no one admits to knowing. The scratching happened within a decade of the renewal—archivists' notes from that period call it 'the Unmaking,' and note only that it was done *by authority*. Whose authority, the notes don't say." He turned to face her fully. "But think about what it means, Lena. Someone wanted her forgotten. Not remembered as a traitor, not remembered as a martyr—*forgotten*. You don't scrape a face off a wall out of hatred. Hatred keeps a face. You do it so that no one looking for her will ever know what to look for."

Lena stared at the hollow shape. The lantern-light moved on the raw plaster, and in the shifting glow she had the strangest sensation—that the figure wasn't erased at all, but *waiting*, a door painted shut, holding something behind it.

"Why show me this?" she asked. "Why now?"

"Because of what you said on the ridge." His eyes were steady and very tired. "You said you weren't chosen. That the gate pulled you. I believe you. But the prophecy doesn't say *chosen*, does it? It says *keeper who chooses*. And a keeper—" he nodded at the ruined figure, "—is what she was. The Verse-Bearer was a keeper of the old gate, back when the gate had keepers on both sides. Your title, Lena. Your office. It existed here first."

The cold that went through her then had nothing to do with the archive's cellar air.

"I'm a ledger-keeper," she said. "I balance accounts. I stamp import chits. I—" But even as she said it, she was remembering the prophecy book, the century-old ink, tonight's date beside her name, and the way the seals had peeled open like petals at the sound of her name sung by a door. *Chosen* was a word for heroes. *Inheritance* was a word for debts. And she had spent her whole life among people who taught her that a debt unpaid does not disappear. It accrues.

"There's more," Dain said gently, and she realized he'd been watching her arrive at it piece by piece, letting her get there herself. "The renewal ninety years ago—it worked, but the archivists recorded that it was incomplete. The final verse was never sung in full. The Verse-Bearer withheld it. Within five years, the payments from your side began to falter. Within twenty, they stopped. Whatever she withheld, whatever she knew—" he tapped the black door of the mural with one finger, "—it's connected to why your guild went silent, and why Malvane could rise. Find her story, and we find the rot at the root of everything."

"And if her story leads back to my guild?" Lena asked. "If the people who raised me, who fed me and taught me my letters and gave me a trade—if they're the reason this world is dying?"

Dain was quiet a moment. Outside, muffled by yards of ancient stone, the bells of Bellhollow murmured on, patient as tide.

"Then you'll have to decide," he said, "which family you belong to. The one that raised you, or the truth. Most people never have to choose. The ones who do either break, or become exactly what the times require." He almost smiled. "You struck me as the second kind. From the moment you fell out of the sky swearing at a wayshrine."

"I did not swear at it."

"You swore *near* it. Close enough."

Despite everything—the hollow face, the eight dead and scattered voices, the cold spreading through her chest—Lena laughed. It came out cracked and surprised her, and for a moment the hall felt less like a tomb.

* * *

They stayed until the lanterns burned low. Lena walked the wall again alone, committing each singer to memory the way she committed ledger entries—not just the images but the *roles*: water, bell, branch, frost, depth, breath, root, wind. Eight instruments of a single song. She caught herself humming as she moved, some tune she didn't know she knew, and stopped, unsettled, when she realized the hum was trying to fit itself into the shape of a ninth part that wasn't there.

It was on her third circuit that she noticed what she'd missed.

The script above the ninth figure—the Verse-Bearer's name—had been scraped too, but scraping removes ink faster than it removes the impression ink leaves in plaster. Up close, with the lantern held at an angle, the pressure-marks of the old letters surfaced like writing on the back of a page. Lena tilted the light. Traced with her eyes. The letters were the swimming, half-readable kind, but one thing emerged clearly, because it was short, and repeated, and pressed deepest of all:

A name. Or the start of one. Two syllables, the first shaped like a closed door, the second like—

"*Sandoval.*"

She said it aloud before she understood why, and the syllables fell into the hush of the hall and lay there, and nothing answered, and everything answered.

Because Sandoval was her name. Her grandmother's name, the one the guild records listed as *origin unknown, arrived through the gate, deceased*. The grandmother who had raised her on songs in a language no one else in the guildhall spoke. The grandmother who had told her, once, half-asleep by the kitchen fire, that doors were just mouths that had learned patience.

Lena stood very still. The lantern trembled slightly in her hand, and the pressure-marks of the scraped letters shifted with it, and she could not make them resolve into anything else no matter how she angled the light.

Two syllables. A closed door. A name.

Behind her, Dain asked what she'd found, and she heard herself say, "Nothing. Dust," in the flat, careful voice she used for balancing columns that wouldn't balance—and hated herself for it even as the lie left her mouth, because she had learned that voice in the guildhall, from masters who smiled while they hid things, and now here it was, living in her own throat.

They climbed back up through the cliffs of books into the evening, and the bells of Bellhollow rang the hour, ten thousand small voices tuning themselves toward a chord no one had completed in ninety years.

Lena walked beside Dain and said nothing. But in the ledger of her mind—an account she had always believed perfectly balanced—a new entry had opened itself, in ink that would not dry:

*Nine voices. Eight scattered. The ninth erased by authority. The ninth named Sandoval.*

*Who in the guildhall knew? Who ordered the Unmaking?*

*And what else is Master Corvane keeping behind his smile?*

That night, unable to sleep in the bell-hung guesthouse, she took out the scrap of prophecy page she'd torn free and carried across worlds, and held it to the candle, and read her own name again in century-old ink.

The candle guttered. Somewhere far below, faint through the bones of the city, she could have sworn one bell—one single bell, deep in the archive hill—rang once, softly, like an answer.

Like a door, learning patience.
