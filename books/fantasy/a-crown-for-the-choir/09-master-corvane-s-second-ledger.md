## 9. Master Corvane's Second Ledger

The guildhall at night smelled of tallow and old paper and, beneath both, something faintly burnt—like the ghost of a fire that had been put out too late to matter. Tam had lived in that smell for eleven years, ever since Master Corvane had found him, a scrawny orphan of nine, copying punishment verses in the courtyard with a stolen quill. *You hold the letters too tightly,* Corvane had said, plucking the quill from his fingers. *But you hold them. That's rarer than you'd think.* He had taken Tam inside, fed him, taught him, and never once—not in eleven years—let him see the second ledger.

Tam knew it existed the way one knows a house has a cellar. Everyone in the guild knew. It was the thing apprentices whispered about on the stair landings when the candles guttered low: the ledger chained to the Master's wrist, the one that recorded what the guild really traded, the one no junior scribe had ever been permitted to touch, to see, or in some versions of the story, to survive having seen. Tam had filed it away with the other myths—the gate that sang, the choir of nine, the tribute that kept the worlds stitched together. Children's stories. The guild dealt in impossible things, yes, but as *brokerage*. As accounting. Someone had to keep the ledgers balanced between what was and what was wished for, and Tam had believed, with the whole flat faith of his heart, that the ledgers were honest.

Then Lena Sandoval had walked into the prophecy archive three nights ago and found her own name inked beside tonight's date in a book a century old. Then Master Corvane had ordered the gate double-sealed and had not slept since. Then, tonight, Tam had been summoned to the Master's study at the hour when even the night-porters dozed, and had been given an instruction that did not sound like accounting at all.

"Copy these," Corvane said. "Then we burn the originals."

* * *

The study was the warmest room in the guildhall, and Tam had always loved it—the walls of ledgers bound in green and grey, the brass scales on the mantel that weighed nothing anyone could see, the low fire that Corvane kept burning even in summer because, he said, ink dried truest in warmth. Tonight the fire seemed less like a comfort and more like an accomplice. Corvane sat behind his desk with the second ledger open before him, and Tam saw for the first time that the chain at his wrist was not ornament. It was a shackle, old and polished smooth, and the ledger was bound to it the way a prisoner is bound to a sentence.

"Page by page," Corvane said. "You will copy exactly what is written. No interpretation. No questions. When you finish a page, you hand it to me, and I—" he lifted a page from the ledger, and the firelight came through it like light through a moth's wing— "I dispose of it."

"Dispose," Tam repeated. The word felt wrong in his mouth. Scribes did not dispose. Scribes *preserved*. The first law of the guild, taught before arithmetic, before the trade-tongues, before anything: *What is written is kept. What is kept is true. What is true cannot be unmade.*

"Dispose," Corvane said again, gently, the way he said things he would not repeat.

Tam took the first page.

The hand was not Corvane's. It was older, cramped, slanting hard to the left, and it was dated—Tam checked twice, because his eyes refused it the first time—forty-one years ago. The entry was not a trade record. There were no weights, no measures, no exchange rates between the possible and the impossible. It was a list of names, and beside each name, in a column Tam's training itched to total, a single word: *paid*.

He copied it. His pen moved the way it always moved, steady and small and obedient, and all the while some quiet part of him stood apart and watched his own hand and thought: *you are copying something that is about to stop existing. You are the last witness these words will ever have.*

Page by page, the night unspooled. The fire ate. Corvane fed it. And Tam copied.

* * *

It was on the eleventh page that the phrase appeared.

He had settled into a rhythm by then—the cramped left-slanting hand transcribed into his own neat book-hand, the names and the word *paid* and the occasional marginal notation he did not let himself read twice—and so the phrase arrived the way a stone arrives at the bottom of a well: suddenly, after a long fall.

*The ninth voice has been located. The price has already been paid. What remains is the collection.*

Tam's pen stopped. A bead of ink gathered at the nib and fell, and he blotted it fast, heart hammering, because Master Corvane was watching—he was always watching—and a scribe who hesitated was a scribe who had read something he should have skimmed.

"Ninth voice," Corvane said.

Tam's blood went cold and then hot. He had been caught. He had hesitated, and the Master had seen, and now—

But Corvane did not look angry. He looked tired. He looked, Tam thought with a lurch that had nothing to do with fear, *old*—old in a way he had never permitted himself to look in front of an apprentice, his grey eyes fixed on the fire where the eleventh page was curling, its edges going brown, then black, then gone.

"The choir of nine," Tam said, before he could stop himself. "That's a story. A founding myth. The murals in the old archive—"

"Are older than you think, and more accurate than I wish." Corvane turned a page of the ledger and held it out. "Copy."

Tam took it. His fingers were not quite steady, and he hated that they weren't, because steadiness was the thing he was proudest of, the thing Corvane had praised him for since he was small: *Tam's hands never shake. Tam's numbers never lie.*

The new page was in a different hand—fresher, sharper, Corvane's own. The date was recent. Three weeks recent.

*The Sandoval girl has found the prophecy entry. She was always going to; it is her inheritance, and inheritance finds its heirs. The question is whether she reaches the gate before we can seal it, and whether the seal holds against what calls her. The ninth voice must not be permitted to sing. The price was paid by another. The covenant will not be reforged. It will be *collected*.*

Tam copied it. Word for word. His book-hand did not falter, because he would not let it, because if his hand faltered then his face would follow, and if his face followed then Corvane would know that Tam understood—and Tam was not yet sure what he understood, only that it was terrible, only that the words *the price was paid by another* had settled into him like a splinter working toward the heart.

He handed the page over. Corvane fed it to the fire. Tam watched his own transcription's original curl and blacken and become a small drifting scrap of ash that rose on the heat and vanished up the flue, and he thought: *I am the only place in the world where those words still exist.*

It should have felt like an honor. For eleven years, being trusted with the guild's secrets had felt like being loved. Tonight it felt like being buried alive with a lamp.

* * *

They worked until the third bell of the night watch. Page after page. Names paid, names owed, and threaded through all of it, growing more frequent as the decades marched forward in the ledger's cramped chronology, a single recurring phrase in Corvane's own hand, appearing year after year like a heartbeat:

*Payment deferred.*

*Payment deferred.*

*Payment suspended by order of the Circle of Masters.*

*Payment suspended. The tribute accumulates. Let it accumulate. Power hoarded is power held.*

Tam copied each one. And with each one, something in him that had stood straight for eleven years leaned a little further sideways, the way a tree leans when the ground beneath it is quietly washing away.

He had grown up on the guild's stories the way other orphans grew up on saints' lives. The Guild of Impossible Things: the last honest broker between the world of men and the world of Vael, holding the door, keeping the accounts, paying the tribute that kept the old magic from spilling across the seam and drowning both realms in war. Every apprentice learned it. Every apprentice *believed* it. Tam had believed it so completely that when the other boys in the city sneered at scribes—*ink-stained ghosts, paper priests*—he had worn the insult like a medal.

*Payment suspended. Let it accumulate.*

He copied it in his beautiful, unshaking hand, and thought: *we didn't keep the door. We locked it and kept the key. We didn't hold the worlds apart to save them. We held them apart to hold the ransom.*

"Master." He heard his own voice from a great distance. "The entries about the tribute. The payments to Vael. They stopped."

Corvane did not look up from the fire. "They did."

"Why?"

The silence stretched long enough that Tam counted his own heartbeats against it—seven, eight, nine—and then Corvane said, "Because the payments were bleeding us. Because the tribute was not a tithe, Tam, it was a *hemorrhage*, and the men who founded this guild decided that a guild cannot serve two worlds if it bleeds to death for one of them." He looked up at last, and his eyes were not cruel. That was the worst of it. They were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic. "You are copying history, not judging it. Judge nothing. Copy."

"Yes, Master."

But Tam's pen, when it touched the next page, hesitated for the length of one full breath, and Corvane saw it, and said nothing, and that silence was louder than any rebuke Tam had ever been given.

* * *

Near dawn, the ledger reached the present, and the entries changed character entirely. No more names. No more *paid*. Now it was all *her*.

*The Sandoval girl balances the accounts to the copper. She suspects the prophecy entry is no error. She will come to me tomorrow, and I will tell her it is scribal error, and she will believe me, because she was raised to believe, and belief is the strongest seal we have.*

*The gate will hold. The gate has held for forty years. It will hold for forty more, and by then the accumulated tribute will be sufficient, and the guild will no longer need Vael at all. We will not need the covenant. We will not need the choir. We will hold enough impossible things to be impossible ourselves.*

*Let the other shore rot. It was never ours to save.*

Tam's pen hovered. The ink at the nib trembled in the candle draft, a tiny black pendulum, and in that trembling he heard the gate—the real gate, down in the undercroft, which he had passed a thousand times and never once heard make a sound—singing.

No. Not the gate. Memory. The founding myth, recited in a child's voice, his own child's voice, eleven years old and ink-stained and luminous with belonging: *the guild holds the door, the guild pays the price, the guild keeps the worlds whole.*

*Let the other shore rot.*

He copied it. Every word. Because Corvane had ordered it, and because some cold and rising part of him understood that these pages were about to become the only testimony in the world, and testimony was sacred, and sacred things were copied no matter what they cost the copier.

When he handed the last page across, Corvane studied him for a long moment in the grey pre-dawn light that was seeping through the shutters like water through a crack.

"You hesitated," Corvane said.

"Yes, Master."

"On the entries about the girl."

"Yes, Master."

Corvane closed the ledger. The chain clinked against the desk, a small domestic sound, obscene in its smallness. "Lena Sandoval is like a daughter to me," he said. "Do you know that?"

"Yes, Master."

"She was the closest thing this guild ever produced to a person without ambition. She keeps accounts because accounts are honest. She guards a door because doors should be guarded." He smiled, and it was the smile Tam had trusted since childhood, warm and tired and true. "Which is precisely why she can never be permitted to learn what the accounts were *for*. Some doors, Tam, are guarded best by people who never ask what's behind them. Do you understand?"

Tam understood that he was being asked to say yes. He said yes. His voice did not shake. His hands did not shake. He had never in his life been so grateful for his own steadiness, and never so horrified by it.

"Go and sleep," Corvane said. "You'll copy the remainder tomorrow night. And Tam—" the Master's voice followed him to the door, mild as milk— "the pages you copied tonight. Bring me your transcription in the morning. It burns with the rest."

* * *

Tam walked the long corridor to the apprentices' cell with his transcription hidden inside his shirt, against his skin, where the paper grew warm and damp with his heartbeat.

He should burn it. That was the thought he kept handing to himself, hoping one of the versions would take. *Burn it. Obey. Copy, preserve, dispose—the Master's order is the guild's law, and the guild raised you, and a man does not rob the house that fed him.* Eleven years of loyalty sat in his chest like a stone worn smooth by handling, and every instinct he had told him to carry that stone back to the study in the morning and drop it in the fire and never think again.

But he kept seeing the eleventh page. *The ninth voice has been located. The price has already been paid.* And he kept seeing, beneath it, the thing he had not let himself read twice, the marginal note in that old cramped hand, small as a spider:

*Paid by E. Sandoval. In full. God forgive us what we collected.*

E. Sandoval.

Lena Sandoval had no family. Everyone knew it. She had come up through the guild like Tam himself, an orphan of the founding charter's charity, and the Masters had always been gentle with her in that particular way the powerful are gentle with things they have a use for.

*Inheritance finds its heirs,* Corvane had written. *She was always going to.*

Tam lay down on his narrow bed in the grey light and did not sleep. He lay with the stolen pages against his heart and listened to the guildhall wake around him—the porters, the kettles, the creak of the great door—and he understood, with the terrible clarity of the sleepless, that he had already made his choice, and had made it somewhere around the eleventh page, and that everything since then had only been his loyalty catching up to his hands.

In the morning, he would bring Corvane a transcription. It would be beautiful. It would be complete.

It would not be the only one.

* * *

He copied it again that day, in the archive's deepest stacks, where the dust stood in the candlelight like suspended snow and no one had come to fetch a ledger in years. He copied it in a hand that was not his own—a plain, blunt, untraceable hand—and he hid the copy inside the hollow spine of a water-damaged trade almanac that had not been opened since before he was born. Then he sat in the dust with his back against the shelf and thought about Lena Sandoval balancing accounts by candlelight three floors above him, believing in the honesty of the ledgers, believing in the Master who had raised them both.

*The price was paid by another.*

He thought about the gate in the undercroft, double-sealed now, humming—if you pressed your palm flat to the cold stone, and Tam had pressed his palm flat that morning, and would deny it under torture—you could feel something on the other side. Not a sound, exactly. More like the memory of a sound. A held note. A choir holding its breath.

That night, Corvane summoned him again. More pages. More burning. Tam copied with steady hands and a face like closed shutters, and when the Master dismissed him near midnight, Tam bowed, and said "Good night, Master," in a voice that had never once lied in eleven years and lied now, fluently, like a native speaker.

He went back to the stacks. He took out the almanac. And he began, by the light of a single smuggled candle, to do the one thing a guild scribe is never permitted to do with an account.

He began to read it as evidence.

The tribute entries. The suspension orders. The names. The ninth voice, located, the price collected, the girl who kept the accounts and was herself an entry in them. He read until the candle guttered, and then he sat in the dark and listened to the guildhall sleep, and somewhere far below, behind two seals and forty years of locked doors, something on the other side of the gate was still holding its note.

Three days later, the wayshrine in the undercroft began to fail.

Tam felt it before anyone told him—a tremor in the floor stones, a wrongness in the air, the particular hush that falls over a house when its foundations have shifted. The enforcers came running. The Masters convened behind closed doors. And in the smoke and shouting of the collapsing shrine, while every eye in the guildhall turned toward the light spilling through the cracking stone, Tam slipped into Corvane's study, lifted the second ledger from its chain with the copying-key no one remembered he had, and ran.
