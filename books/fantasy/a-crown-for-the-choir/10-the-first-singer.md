## 10. The First Singer

The sea had been lying to Maren Salt for forty years, and she had learned to listen anyway.

She sat on the shingle below her cottage with her nets across her knees, mending by feel because the light was going the way it always went in Errenfell—thin, silvered, reluctant. The tide was out farther than it should have been. It had been coming in wrong all week, arriving late and leaving early, like a guest who no longer wished to be in the house. She could hear it breathing against the stones, and beneath its breath there was a second sound, fainter, that she did not like at all: a dry whispering, as though the water were being counted.

Everything in Vael was being counted lately. She had felt it first in her hands, which ached on nights when something far away broke. Then in her lullabies, which used to pull the tide up the sand to meet her boots when she sang them soft, and now only made the water hesitate, confused, like an old dog that had forgotten its own name.

*The song is forgetting itself,* she thought. *Or someone is making it forget.*

Her son's name rose in her, the way it always did at dusk. Tamel. Drowned thirty years ago off the Teeth, when he was nineteen and certain the sea loved him. She had sung his body back to shore herself, walking into the shallows past all sense, singing the old pulling-song until the water gave him up to her arms. After that, the sea had never quite dared refuse her anything. That was the trouble with grief done badly—it left you with power you never wanted and no one to spend it on.

She tied off the last knot and looked up, because the gulls had gone quiet, and gulls going quiet meant either weather or strangers.

Two figures were coming down the cliff path. One moved like a courier—all long stride and economy, a scarred face turned constantly toward the horizon. The other walked like no one Maren had seen walk in this world for a very long time: carefully, the way people walk when they are not sure the ground will hold them, when every stone is a question.

A stranger from over the water. Maren's hands went still on the net.

She had known, of course. You did not keep a voice like hers buried in a fishing village for sixty years without knowing that someday the debt would come calling. The murals in Bellhollow still showed eight faces and one scratched-out smear, and somewhere under that smear was a girl who had run rather than sing, and grown old rather than remember, and taught herself to call the tide with half a voice so that no one would ever ask for the whole of it.

But knowing a thing and standing up to meet it were two different tides entirely.

She stayed where she was. She finished her knot. And when they reached the shingle, she said, "You'll want to mind the third stone there. It rolls."

The courier stopped. The other one—the stranger, a woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes too old for her face—looked at Maren the way people look at a door they've been searching a coastline for.

"Maren Salt," the courier said. "Dain Ash. This is Lena Sandoval."

"I know who I am," Maren said. "And I know why you've come, or near enough. Sit down before your legs give out, girl. The path down from the headland has killed better walkers than you." She nodded at a driftwood log worn smooth as whalebone. "I'll put the kettle on. There's no use talking about the end of the world on empty stomachs. It makes everyone dramatic."

* * *

The cottage smelled of salt, tar, dried kelp, and forty years of one person's living. Lena Sandoval sat at Maren's table with her hands wrapped around a clay mug of nettle tea and looked around the room as if memorizing it—which, Maren thought, watching her sidelong through the steam, was exactly what she was doing. Ledger-keeper's habit. Count everything, trust nothing to remain.

Dain stood by the window, because couriers never sat if they could help it.

"You knew we were coming," Lena said.

"The gulls told me." Maren lowered herself into her chair with the small honest sounds of an old body. "That's a joke, mostly. But the sea's been wrong for a month, and wrong in a way I recognize. When the covenant frays, the water forgets its manners. Comes late. Leaves early. Whispers." She sipped her tea. "I haven't heard that whisper since the payments stopped. I'd hoped never to hear it again. Hoped is a strong word. Wished, maybe. The way you wish a wound would close on its own instead of needing to be opened and cleaned."

"Then you know what we're asking," Dain said.

"I know what you're *going* to ask. You haven't asked it yet because the girl hasn't let you." Maren looked at Lena. "Go on, then. Ask it. I'm old, not fragile, and I've been rehearsing my answer for sixty years. It'll be rude of you to make me wait longer."

Lena set down her mug. Her hands were steady; her voice was not, quite. "The covenant needs voices. Real ones. The score we found names nine singers, and we've found records of seven dead, one scattered—" she glanced at Dain, "—and one unaccounted for. A fisherwoman of Errenfell whose lullabies bend tides. The guild's oldest ledgers list her as *Maren of the Ninth Verse.*"

"Ninth verse," Maren repeated. The words tasted like brine and another lifetime. "They gave me the ninth verse because I was the youngest and the least afraid. That's what they told me, anyway. What they meant was that I was the most expendable and too green to argue." She laughed, short and dry. "Sixty years, and I can still recite it. Isn't that a curse? You forget your mother's face but the verse stays put, squatting in your chest like a barnacle."

"You'll sing it?" Lena asked. "For the reforging?"

And there it was. The question, come home to roost after six decades.

Maren looked at her for a long moment—at the ink stains, the careful eyes, the way the girl held herself like a ledger balanced on a knife's edge—and felt the old weariness rise in her, and beneath the weariness, to her own surprise, something warmer and more stubborn. Hope, maybe. Or love wearing hope's coat.

"No," she said.

Lena's face fell exactly one inch. Dain shifted by the window.

"Not the way you mean it. Not *yes, I'll stand on some shrine and warble while you two work the rest out.* If we do this, we do it properly, and properly means you understand what it costs before you ask anyone to pay it. Including yourself." She leaned forward. "Especially yourself, keeper. Because I don't think you know yet. I think they sent you through that gate without telling you, and I think you've been too busy surviving to wonder why a prophecy book wrote your name a hundred years ago."

Lena went very still. "How do you—"

"The sea whispers, girl. I told you." Maren waved a hand. "Word travels strange in Vael now that the roads are thin. A wayshrine keeper south of here saw you come through and rejected twice over. Stories grow legs when magic gets desperate." She folded her hands. "So. Here is the price, spoken plain, because I am the first singer you've found and I will not let you learn it from a corpse.

"When the covenant is sung, it must be paid for. Not with coin. Not with blood—not only, anyway. With memory. Each voice gives up something it holds dear. The song takes what it needs to bind itself, and it does not ask permission, and it does not give back. My grandmother sang the first covenant, and afterward she couldn't recall the smell of bread baking. That was her payment. A whole life of mornings, gone, and she only knew it was gone because the rest of us remembered it for her."

The fire ticked. Outside, the tide whispered its counting.

"That's why the singers scattered," Maren went on, quieter. "Not fear of Malvane, not fear of the guild. Fear of the *paying*. Eight voices, each asked to hand over a piece of themselves, and no one willing to say aloud what the pieces might be. So they ran. And the world got quieter, and quieter, and now the water comes late and leaves early, and a man with a crown is marching armies toward the seam because he thinks a broken world is easier to rule than a whole one."

"And you didn't run," Lena said.

"Oh, I ran. Farther than any of them. I ran across the seam itself, sixty years ago, and hid in a world where nobody sings." Maren smiled, and it was not a happy smile, but it was a true one. "Then I came back. Do you know why? Because I drowned my son."

The stillness in the room changed quality. Even Dain stopped moving.

"He went out past the Teeth in a blow, nineteen years old and immortal the way they all are at nineteen. And I stood on this shingle and I sang the pulling-song until my throat bled, and the sea brought him back to me. Not alive. Back. And I learned something that night that none of the nine ever understood, because they were singers first and mothers never." She tapped her own chest, once. "Grief doesn't leave. It just changes rooms. And a memory given to the song isn't destroyed, whatever the old texts claim. It's *spent*. Put into the walls of the thing you built. My grandmother's lost mornings are in the covenant's foundations—every dawn the two realms woke side by side for three hundred years, she paid for part of it. That's not destruction. That's becoming load-bearing."

Lena's eyes were bright and wet. "You'd pay again. Knowing what it takes."

"I'm seventy-three years old, keeper. My memories are mostly of people who are dead and tides that have already turned." Maren reached across the table and took the girl's ink-stained hand, and felt her flinch, and did not let go. "What I have left, I would rather spend than hoard. Hoarding is how you get men like Corvane. Hoarding is how you get men like Malvane. Someone has to show the world a different arithmetic." She squeezed. "Yes. I'll sing. But hear me, both of you—I'm the first, not the last, and every singer you recruit after me gets the truth from *me*, not from you. The price named aloud, before they agree. No one pays blind. Not for me. Promise it."

"We promise," Dain said.

Lena said nothing for a moment. Then, in a voice that had ledger-steel under the wetness: "We promise. And I'll write it into the record myself. Every payment, named and witnessed. No more hidden ledgers."

Maren barked a laugh that startled the gulls back into voice. "Oh, I like you. Sixty years I waited for the debt to come calling and it sends me a bookkeeper with a spine." She released the girl's hand and pushed herself up. "Right. Kettle's cold and I've a pulling-song to dig out of the cellar where I buried it. We start tomorrow at low tide, on the shrine below the point—if there's anything left of it to stand on. And you—" she pointed a finger at Dain, "—will chop wood. Couriers eat too much to be useless."

* * *

Night came down over Errenfell the way it always had, slow and salt-heavy, and Maren lay awake in her narrow bed listening to the sea.

It was closer now. Coming in on time, almost. As if the water had smelled the covenant on her skin again and remembered its manners—or as if something on the far side of the seam had noticed a ninth voice waking, and begun, very softly, to count *her*.

She thought about Tamel. She thought about what she might lose. The song would take what it needed, and it needed weight, and the heaviest thing in her was a boy of nineteen laughing on a boat deck with the wind in his hair. She had carried that laugh for thirty years through every dark morning, and tomorrow, or soon after, she might hand it over to hold up the sky.

Would she know it was gone? Would she wake some future dawn feeling for it and find only smooth wall, load-bearing, necessary?

She found she could bear that. What she could not bear was the thought of the girl downstairs—in the spare room, asleep at last, after hours of Maren hearing her turn and turn—standing in the seam one day and paying with everything, because Maren had seen it in her already, the same terrible readiness, the same arithmetic of *spend, don't hoard*. Someone would have to teach that one to keep a little back. Someone would have to survive the singing to tell the tale.

*One thing at a time,* she told herself, as she had told herself at Tamel's graveside, at her husband's, at every tide-mark of a long hard life. *One knot at a time. The net holds or it doesn't.*

Outside, the sea breathed in. Held. Breathed out.

And then, just before sleep took her, Maren heard it—faint, from the direction of the drowned shrine below the point, threading through the water's whisper like a needle through cloth:

Someone else was singing the ninth verse.

Singing it *wrong*.
