## 11. Malvane's Offer

They had been three days out of Maren's cottage when the road began to sing.

Not with birdsong—Lena had learned the difference now, the way Vael's roads hummed beneath the feet of those who listened, a low harmonic that rose from the seam of the world like breath through a reed. Dain said it was the old covenant still holding, threadbare as it was, singing to itself in the dark the way an old man hums to prove he's alive. But this morning the road's note changed. It thinned. Went sharp.

Lena stopped mid-stride, her ledger-satchel heavy against her hip, and held up a hand.

Dain read her stillness the way he read weather. His hand went to the courier's blade at his back—not drawing it, just resting his knuckles against the hilt, a habit she'd noticed he fell into whenever the world grew too quiet. Ahead of them, the road bent around a stand of white-barked trees whose leaves turned silver in the twilight that never quite lifted over this part of Vael, and where the bend straightened again there was a figure waiting.

One figure. Alone. Unarmed, or appearing so, standing with hands folded at the small of his back like a man admiring the view.

"That's far enough," Dain said quietly. "We go around."

"There is no around," Lena said. She could feel it—the way the road's song bent toward the figure, the way the trees leaned. "The road wants us to meet him."

"The road doesn't want anything. Roads are roads." But Dain's voice lacked conviction, and they both knew it. In Vael, everything wanted something. Rivers ran uphill because they longed for their sources. Stones remembered being mountains. And roads—roads remembered every foot that had ever walked them, and apparently, sometimes, they made introductions.

The figure resolved as they approached: a tall man in traveling grays, unremarkable except for his eyes, which were the pale gold of honey held up to lamplight, and the circlet of black iron at his brow, plain as a nail, worked with no sigil but a single line of script that Lena couldn't read and yet somehow understood anyway: *I speak for the one who waits.*

"Ledger-keeper," the man said. His voice was pleasant, modulated, the voice of someone who had practiced warmth until it fit him like a coat. "And the courier Ash. My lord Malvane bids you good morning, though I'm told your mornings come at strange hours, on the other side of things."

"You know my name," Lena said.

"I know all your names. That is rather the point of me." He inclined his head. "Herald Vess. I have the honor—and I use the word honestly, Keeper, honor is scarce these days—to carry my lord's words to those worth carrying them to. You are the first travelers in eleven years I've been sent to greet rather than to warn."

"Warn?" Dain asked.

Vess smiled without warmth. "Most people who see the herald coming know what follows. Armies. Requisitions. The border-stones moved six feet east, and a village learning that its shrine now belongs to someone else. You two get something rarer." He spread his hands. "An offer."

Lena's hand found the strap of her satchel. Inside it were the notes she'd taken in Maren's kitchen: the names of scattered singers, the fragments of the covenant score Maren had sung from memory, half-remembered, half-hummed, like a hymn whose words had been worn away by decades of tides. If this herald knew what she carried—

"We're not interested," Dain said flatly.

"You haven't heard it."

"We don't need to. We saw what your lord does to villages that don't kneel. We watched his riders burn the granaries at Sorrel Ford because the headman wouldn't hand over his daughter's voice for the tithe."

"Sorrel Ford," Vess repeated, and something flickered across his pleasant face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Yes. A regrettable necessity. Though I'd point out, courier, that the headman kept his daughter, his village, and his life, which is more than the old covenant ever did for anyone."

That landed strangely. Lena frowned. "What do you mean?"

"Ah." Vess turned to her fully, and she felt the weight of those honey-gold eyes settle on her like a hand on the shoulder. "You've been here what—a week? Ten days? Long enough to learn that the rivers run uphill and the tides can be sung, but not long enough to ask the question underneath all of it." He stepped closer; Dain's blade cleared its sheath an inch, and Vess paused, courteous, unbothered. "May I tell you a story, Keeper? It will take less time than you fear and cost you more than you expect. Stories usually do."

Lena glanced at Dain. He gave a small, tight shake of his head—but she was already remembering Maren's face when she'd spoken of the payments, the way the old woman's hands had trembled over her lullabies, the grief in her voice when she'd said, *the tribute stopped coming, and the world started bleeding.* Everything Lena had learned so far had come filtered through Dain, through Maren, through the dead singers' murals in Bellhollow. All of it one story. One side of a ledger.

She was, before anything else, a keeper of ledgers. And a ledger with only one column was not a ledger. It was a pamphlet.

"Tell me," she said.

* * *

Vess told it walking, because in Vael stories apparently preferred motion, and the three of them moved slowly along the road while the white trees gave way to low hills stitched with terraced fields gone wild. He spoke in the easy cadence of a man who had told this story many times and refined it down to its load-bearing beams.

"A thousand years ago—or near enough, the dates are disputed and I refuse to pretend otherwise—your world and mine were one world. One shore, one sky, one song. Then came the Sundering, and whether it was war or hubris or simply the universe discovering it had a fault line, the realms split, and the seam between them began to bleed. Magic leaked into your world in trickles—enough for your ghosts and your miracles, your prophets and your madmen. And our magic bled *out*, Keeper. Do you understand what that means, for a world made of song to be leaking its song into the dark?"

"It means dying," Lena said quietly.

"It means dying," Vess agreed. "Slowly. Beautifully. The way a singer dies, note by note. So the wise ones of both shores forged the covenant. Nine voices, standing at the seam, binding the two worlds into harmony—not united, never that, but *attuned*. And the price of attunement was tribute. Your guild would send across the impossible things. A storm bottled in glass. A year that lasted a day. A child's laughter preserved past childhood. Small impossibilities, paid regularly, feeding the seam the raw stuff of wonder it needed to keep from tearing."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, Vael's magic was *rationed*." Vess's voice didn't rise, but the road's hum did, a faint dissonance threading under his words. "Do you know what a tithe is, Keeper? Of course you do—you kept them. Well. For a thousand years, every miracle in my world was metered out by your guildmasters. Which villages got rain. Which children were born with the sight. Which shrines stayed open and which were allowed to go dark. Not decided here, by the people who lived with the consequences—decided *there*, by men in gray robes balancing columns in a ledger, weighing our lives against their wonder like grain against gold."

"That's not—" Dain started.

"Isn't it?" Vess turned on him, and for the first time the courtesy cracked, showing something older and angrier beneath. "You were born after the payments stopped, courier. You grew up in the famine years and you think the famine was the crime. Ask your grandmother what the covenant years were like. Ask her about the Quiet Decades, when whole provinces were put under *silence* because their singing offended some guildmaster's sense of order. Ask her about the tithe-takers. Ask her why the ninth singer fled to your world, if the covenant was such a blessed thing."

Lena's breath caught. "You know about the ninth singer."

"My lord knew her better than anyone alive." Something moved behind Vess's eyes—grief, or its well-polished imitation. "But that is not my story to tell. Only this: the covenant bound the realms, yes. It also bound *us*. And when the payments stopped—when your masters decided, quite reasonably, that a thousand years of tribute was enough—the seam began to tear, and my lord rose to do what the nine voices once did. To hold the seam. By himself. By any means."

"With armies," Dain said. "With burned granaries. With border-stones moved by force."

"With *order*," Vess said. "Yes. Ugly, imperfect, human order—which is more than the covenant ever offered us, because the covenant never thought of us as human at all. We were the instrument, Keeper. Your guild played us for a millennium and called it harmony." He let that sit in the air between them, then folded his hands again, the mask of courtesy sliding back into place. "So. The offer."

He produced, from inside his coat, a scroll sealed with black wax, and held it out—not to Dain, but to Lena.

"My lord offers you a place at his right hand. Bring him the covenant score you carry—yes, we know you carry it; the road sang of little else for three days—and the names of the singers you seek. Stand with him at the seam when he reforges the binding, and the free cities of your world will be spared. Every one. No riders, no requisitions, no war. He will even open the gate both ways, freely, permanently—something your guild never once permitted in a thousand years of stewardship." Vess's smile was gentle, almost kind. "You would be the tenth voice, Keeper. The one the prophecy names. The keeper who chooses. He only asks that you choose *him*."

* * *

The silence afterward was very long.

Lena looked at the scroll and did not take it. Her fingers hovered above the black wax seal, and she was aware, distantly, of Dain beside her, rigid, his breathing carefully controlled, and of the road humming its thin sharp note, and of the twilight pressing close like a listener leaning in.

Because here was the thing—the thing she would not say aloud, not here, not in front of the herald with his honey-gold eyes:

Some of it was true.

She had spent ten days in Vael, and she had seen the uphill rivers and the singing tides and the shrines gone dark, and she had grieved for this wounded world. But she had also seen, in Bellhollow's murals, the faces of the nine singers rendered in gold leaf while the small figures below them—the villagers, the tide-folk, the ordinary people of Vael—were painted as background, as texture, as *setting*. She had read the guild's founding charter in translation and noted, with the part of her mind that never stopped keeping accounts, how much of it concerned the *management* of Vael's wonders and how little concerned the wishes of Vael's people. She had watched Maren sing the tide flat calm to save their boat from a squall and then heard the old woman mutter, half-laughing, half-bitter, that under the covenant she'd needed a guild permit to do it.

A thousand years of tribute. A thousand years of another world's wonders weighed and measured and rationed by men in gray robes. Men like Corvane. Men like her.

*We were the instrument,* Vess had said. *Your guild played us for a millennium and called it harmony.*

And the offer itself—she turned it over in her mind like a coin she suspected was clipped. Spare the free cities. Open the gate. Give Vael back its own magic, unrationed. Wasn't that, in its bones, what she wanted? Wasn't that the shape of the thing she'd been groping toward since the night a century-old book had written her name in it?

The difference was Malvane. The difference was Sorrel Ford's granaries and silenced provinces and a crown forged from hoarded power. The difference was that Malvane's covenant would be a covenant of one—one man's will imposed on two worlds, mercy dispensed at the point of a spear, freedom granted the way a jailer grants a larger cell.

But oh, the trap of it—the elegant, awful trap of it—was that she couldn't refute him without refuting the truth tangled in his lie. Every argument she raised against Malvane was also an argument against the guild that raised her. Every crime she named was a crime her masters had committed first, for longer, with better handwriting.

"Lena." Dain's voice, low. "Don't."

"I'm not taking it."

"I know. But you're *listening*, and he can hear you listening." Dain stepped forward, putting his body slightly between her and the herald, and his voice hardened into something she hadn't heard from him before—the courier's voice, the one that had crossed borders during the worst years. "Here's my answer, Vess. Tell your lord the free cities don't negotiate with men who burn granaries. Tell him the covenant wasn't ours to break and isn't his to forge. And tell him—" his jaw tightened— "tell him the eighth singer sends her regards. He'll understand."

Something flickered across Vess's face at that—something real, this time, unpolished, quickly buried. He bowed, shallow and precise.

"As you wish. The offer stands until the seam closes. My lord is patient; it is nearly his only virtue." He turned to Lena last, and his voice dropped, gentled, became—for one unguarded moment—simply tired. "Keeper. I'll say one thing off the record, herald to ledger-keeper, because I think you're the first person in eleven years worth saying it to. My lord believes what he says. That should frighten you more than any lie. A man who lies knows the truth is out there somewhere, and can be bargained with. A man who believes has already decided the price everyone else pays is worth it." He tapped the scroll against her satchel, lightly, almost affectionately. "Ask yourself, before you gather your choir: if the covenant was slavery dressed as harmony, what will your reforged version be? Will you ask Vael's permission this time? Or will you simply sing *at* us, the way your masters always did?"

Then he walked away, down the road, unhurried, and the white trees closed behind him like water closing over a stone.

* * *

They walked the rest of the day without speaking.

Lena carried the unanswered questions like stones in her satchel, heavier than any ledger. That evening they made camp in the lee of a fallen waystone, and Dain built a small fire, and the flames burned a color she'd learned meant the wood remembered being alive—warm amber shot through with threads of green.

"He got in your head," Dain said at last. Not accusatory. Just true.

"Yes."

"You know he's a liar."

"I know he's a believer. He told me so himself." Lena pulled her knees up, watching the fire. "That's worse, Dain. Liars I can check against the ledger. Believers just... keep writing."

Dain fed a stick to the flames. "The covenant wasn't perfect. I won't pretend it was. My grandmother used to say the tithe-years were hard, and the silence-provinces were a stain nobody's washed out yet. But there's a difference between a flawed house and a fire, Lena. Malvane's offering to burn the house down and call the ashes freedom."

"I know." She did know. And yet. "But he asked me a question I can't answer."

"What question?"

Lena stared into the green-threaded fire, and heard again the herald's tired voice: *Will you ask Vael's permission this time?*

"When we reforge the covenant," she said slowly, "whose voices do we sing it with? Ours alone—nine singers, maybe ten, all chosen, all from the other shore, deciding the fate of a world most of us have seen for ten days?" She shook her head. "Or do we find a way to sing it *with* Vael? With Maren's people. With the villages. With whoever wants a say in the harmony that binds them." She looked up at Dain, and her eyes were dry but her voice wasn't quite steady. "Because if we can't answer that—if we just rebuild what Corvane's masters built, with better intentions—then Malvane hasn't lost. He's just been made unnecessary. Someone else will do it righter, harder, crueler, in another hundred years, and they'll be half right too."

Dain was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "You sound like the ninth singer. The murals—the way she's painted looking *out* of the frame, at the people, not at the seam. Maren said she always asked that question. Whose song is it?"

"Maybe that's why her face was scratched out," Lena said softly. "Not because she failed. Because she asked."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark beyond the campfire's reach, the road hummed its thin note, and beneath it—very faint, very far, in a direction Lena couldn't name—came another sound. Drums. Many drums, steady as a heartbeat, marching in time.

Dain's head came up. "Border-stones," he said. "That's the direction of the border-stones." He was already reaching for his pack, already moving, the courier's urgency flooding back into his limbs. "If Malvane's vanguard reaches the seam markers before we find the next singer—"

Lena stood, slinging the satchel across her chest, the covenant score pressed against her heart like a second heartbeat. Above them the eternal twilight was beginning, at last, to deepen toward a night that Vael rarely permitted itself, and the first stars pricked through like holes punched in cloth.

"Maren," she said. "We go back for Maren. Tonight."

And far off, patient as tide, the drums kept time.
