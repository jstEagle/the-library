## 12. Bellhollow Burns

Dain Ash had spent eleven years learning to read the sky over Vael, and the sky that morning was wrong.

It wasn't the color—twilight held Vael in its permanent amber dusk, and he'd stopped expecting blue decades ago. It was the birds. The rooks that nested in Bellhollow's archive-towers, ten thousand of them, rose all at once from the spires like a black cloth shaken loose, wheeling south in a single silent river. Rooks did not fly silent. Rooks did not abandon their nests. Rooks fled fire before there was any smoke to smell.

He was on the eastern stair of the Archive of Voices when he understood, and by then the first horn was already sounding from the watchtower on the causeway—a long, cracked note, the sound of a man trying to shout a warning through a wall of water.

"Fire," Dain said, to no one, to himself, to the years he'd carried like stones sewn into his coat. "They're coming with fire."

He took the stairs three at a time, past shelves of prophecy-books older than the free cities, past archivists blinking up from their copying-desks with the mild confusion of people who had never once been burned out of anything. Bellhollow had stood for nine hundred years. Its founders had built it on a shelf of living stone above the Sorrowmere, ringed it with canals, warded it with songs so old nobody remembered the words' meanings anymore. No army had ever touched it. No army had ever needed to. What use was burning books to men who could not read them?

But Malvane's riders were not men who could not read them. That was the thing Dain had tried to tell the archivists for two days, pacing their cool marble halls while they smiled at him the way one smiles at a beggar who claims to be a king: *the Usurper burns what he cannot own.* He had seen it at Karst-Under-Hill, where the mining-songs were kept. He had seen it at the shrine-fords. Malvane did not destroy knowledge out of ignorance. He destroyed it because every song Dain and Lena gathered was a note he could not control, and a Usurper's crown is made entirely of control.

And now they were here, and it was his fault, and he knew exactly how.

* * *

He found her in the Hall of Nine, standing beneath the mural, and for one absurd heartbeat he forgot the horns entirely.

Lena Sandoval had been in Vael six days. Six days since the gate peeled open like a flower and swallowed her; six days since she'd walked out of a twilight meadow demanding explanations with her arms crossed and her ledger-keeper's voice flat as a millpond. In six days she had not wept, though he knew she dreamed of home—he'd heard her talking in her sleep, small words in another language, a name repeated like a prayer. She had not complained. She had learned the geography of a dying world the way she seemed to learn everything: quickly, silently, completely, writing it down somewhere behind her eyes in columns only she could read.

Now she stood under the mural of the Choir of Nine with her hand raised toward the ninth figure—the scratched-out face, the raw gouges in the plaster—and she was saying something quiet. Not to him. To the face. An apology, maybe. Or a question.

"Lena." His voice came out wrong, scraped. "We have to go. Now."

She turned. She saw his face, and whatever she read there made her go still in that particular way of hers—not frozen, but *focused*, a lamp turned up to full flame. "How long?"

"Horn's already sounded. Riders move fast on the causeway, but the canals will slow them at the outer ring. We have minutes. Maybe less."

"The books." She was already moving, skirts hiked, taking the stairs beside him two at a time. "The original covenant score—you said it's kept here?"

"Deep vault. Restricted access. The archivists call it the First Folio."

"Then we're going to get it."

"It's warded. It takes an hour to petition for—"

"Dain." She didn't break stride. "If we die in this building, I would rather die holding it than knowing where it sits."

There was no answer to that, so he gave none, and they ran together down through the archive's bones, past reading-cloisters and song-vaults and the great echoing Stacks of Names where every singer ever recorded had a folio, and all around them the beautiful indifferent machinery of Bellhollow continued its morning: bells marking the hour, archivists carrying tea, a child somewhere practicing scales, badly, wonderfully. None of them knew yet. Or worse—they knew, and believed their walls.

* * *

The vault-keeper was an ancient woman named Ithra with clouded eyes and hands steady as roots, and she listened to Dain's pounding with the expression of someone being interrupted during a nap she had earned.

"You'll want the First Folio," she said, before he'd finished. "Yes. I've had it out since the horn."

Dain stared. "You—"

"I am ninety-one years old, courier. I have watched this city believe itself untouchable for nine hundred years, and I have read enough prophecy to know how belief like that ends." She turned, keys singing on her ring, and led them down a spiral stair into cold stone. "The warding takes an hour if you follow the rite. It takes thirty seconds if you know which stone is lying." Her hand found a block in the wall that looked identical to every other block, pressed it, and the false stone swung inward on oiled hinges.

Inside, on a lectern of black glass, lay a book the size of a paving slab, bound in something that was not leather and hummed faintly, like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing.

Lena reached for it and stopped, fingers hovering. "It's warm."

"It's awake," Ithra said. "Covenant-work always is. Take it. And take these—" she pressed a satchel into Lena's arms, already packed, already heavy. "Two others. The Ledger of Tides and the Annals of the Gate. If the city falls, let it fall empty-handed."

"How did you know to pack?" Dain asked.

Ithra smiled with most of her teeth. "Because you've been asking about the ninth singer for two days, boy, and asking loudly. Loud questions carry. Someone carried yours south." She shooed them toward the stair. "Canal-gate, east quarter. There's a barge. Go."

"And you?"

"I have lived my whole life among these books." She settled onto the stair like a woman taking her seat at last. "I will spend my last hour among them. Now run."

* * *

They ran.

The east quarter was already screaming. Through high windows Dain saw the first smoke rising off the outer ring—not the greasy black of burning wood but a strange luminous white, the signature of Malvane's fire-oil, which clung to stone and sang while it burned. *Sang* was the word the survivors used. The fires of the Usurper made a sound like a choir humming one endless flat note, and everything they touched joined in until whole streets shrieked in harmony.

"They followed us," Lena said. She had stopped running. They were in the canal-gate passage now, the barge visible ahead, and she had stopped as if her legs had been cut, and her face—

Her face was doing arithmetic. He recognized it. He'd seen it in the meadow when she'd realized the covenant math didn't balance, seen it at the ridge when the armies marched. She was adding numbers until they made a sum she couldn't put down.

"The herald," she said. "After we refused Malvane's offer. We rode north. We talked about Bellhollow on the road—we *had* to, there was nowhere else to go, and I thought—I assumed the wards—"

"Lena."

"I led them here." Her voice was very level, which was how he was learning to tell when she was closest to breaking. "I'm the reason. I walked into the archive and asked loud questions and led the Usurper's riders to the last library in the world, and now it's—"

"*Lena.*" He caught her shoulders. Below them the barge-master was shouting; above them the white fire was eating the outer ring, and the flat choral note of the burning was louder now, sliding under the doors like floodwater. "Look at me. You want to assign blame? Fine. Here is the true ledger. I served Malvane. I wore his colors. I taught his scouts the roads and I led his hunters to the eighth singer, and she died in a cellar in Karst with her throat opened, and I ran, and I have been running for eleven years telling myself the next good deed would balance the account." His grip tightened. "Nothing balances it. You do not get to take this weight from me today. It is mine. I have carried it longer and I carry it better. So pick up your share and *move*, keeper, because if we die on this dock the books burn too, and then we are both guilty of nothing worth grieving."

For a moment she searched his face with those ledger-keeper's eyes, auditing him, weighing his confession against some inner column. Then she nodded once, sharp, and shouldered the satchel, and they went down to the barge together.

* * *

The canal was a corridor of chaos. Barges jammed bow to stern, archivists throwing folios into the water rather than let them burn, a woman standing waist-deep in the shallows passing armfuls of books to strangers on the far bank like a ferry of paper and grief. The white light of the fire-oil reflected off the water and turned everyone's faces the color of candle-flame, and over it all hung that terrible humming, closer now, the flat note of the burning streets joining into something almost like music, almost like mockery.

Dain poled while Lena steered, the First Folio wrapped in oilcloth between them like a sleeping animal. Behind them the Archive of Voices caught.

He heard it before he saw it—the moment the fire reached the great towers and found nine hundred years of dry vellum waiting. The hum swelled. And then, impossibly, the burning archive began to *sing*: every warded shelf, every song-bound book releasing its contents into the flames at once, so that the smoke itself seemed to carry voices—fragments of lullabies, battle-hymns, naming-songs, the whole buried memory of Vael going up in a column of fire and sound that must have been audible across half the world.

Lena stood in the stern and watched it, and did not look away, and tears ran down her face without changing her expression at all, and Dain thought: *there. That is what a keeper looks like.*

"The mural," she said quietly. "The Hall of Nine. Is it—"

"Gone." He hadn't turned around either. He didn't need to. "The faces are in our heads now. That's the only place left."

"Then we keep them there." She knelt beside the Folio and rested her hand on the oilcloth, and through the wrapping he could hear it, faintly, that unfinished bell-note, patient as a heartbeat. "Three books. Out of a hundred thousand. We save three books and lose a world's memory, and somehow that has to be enough."

"It's not enough," Dain said. "It's a start. Those aren't the same thing, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something."

Ahead, the canal bent south toward open water and the drowned shrine of Errenfell beyond it. Behind, Bellhollow burned like a second sunset, and the rooks wheeled overhead, silent, going wherever rooks go when the world's libraries die.

Lena wiped her face with the back of her hand, briskly, the way one closes a ledger at day's end.

"Errenfell," she said. "Maren's shrine. If Malvane burns the archives, he'll come for the singers next. We reach her first."

Dain set his pole against the current and pushed, and the barge slid out onto dark water, three books aboard, two ghosts of guilt rowing toward whatever came next.

He did not see, in the smoke-shadowed doorway of the canal-gate, the rider in grey who watched them go, and marked their heading south, and turned his horse.

But the fire saw. And the fire told its master.
