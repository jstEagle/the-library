## 13. Tam Crosses Over

The wayshrine in the coal cellar had not been opened in Tam's lifetime.

He knew this because he had spent three years dusting it. It stood at the back of the guildhall's deepest vault, behind the racks of preserved eels and the crates of moon-salt, a squat arch of black stone furred with lichen that grew, impossibly, in the dark. The apprentices called it the Dead Door and dared each other to spit through it. Nothing ever came through. Nothing ever went. Master Corvane had told him once, in one of his rare gentle moods, that the wayshrines were like old bells—cast for a purpose no one remembered, rung by no one living, kept because keeping them was cheaper than explaining them.

Tam had believed him. That was the whole of his life, really: he had believed Master Corvane the way other boys believed in their fathers.

Now he stood before the Dead Door with a ledger chained to nothing—free, unchained, its iron cuff still locked around his wrist where he'd sheared the chain with a cold-chisel—and a fire starting somewhere above him, and he did the one thing in three years of apprenticeship he had never done.

He disobeyed everything at once.

* * *

It had begun, as all catastrophes in the Guild of Impossible Things began, with an errand.

"Copy these," Corvane had said that morning, setting a stack of loose pages on Tam's desk. "Then burn them. Both tasks tonight, before the second bell. No copies leave this room, Tam. I mean that as I have never meant anything."

The pages were old. That was the first thing wrong with them. The guild's ledgers were immaculate—Corvane ran the scriptorium like a surgeon runs a theater—but these were foxed and water-stained, written in at least four hands across what looked like decades. And they were not accounts. Tam had copied enough accounts to know them the way he knew his own handwriting: columns of tribute paid, impossible things rendered unto Vael—a song sung backward over water, a bell cast from a drowned man's grief, a memory surrendered willingly into a lead casket. Entries like:

*Ninth year of the Quiet Accord. Paid: the last true name of the river Erren, given by a keeper's daughter, freely. Balance: maintained.*

And then, further down, in a newer hand, a different kind of entry entirely:

*Paid no more. Let them bleed. What we withheld, we keep. What we keep, we rule.*

Tam had sat with that line for a long time while the candle guttered. He was sixteen and had been raised in the guild since his mother sold him at seven—not sold, Corvane always corrected, *entrusted*—and he knew every story the guild told about itself. The guild were wardens. The guild were balance-keepers. The guild paid its debts so that two worlds could sleep soundly.

The pages said the guild had stopped paying. Not failed to pay. *Stopped.* Deliberately. Decades ago. And the entries after that were not tribute records at all but harvest records—the hoarded power of a century of unpaid covenant, siphoned into vaults, weighed, catalogued, *kept*. There was a word that recurred in the margins, underlined in Corvane's own precise hand: *the ninth voice.* And beside it, once, in ink so fresh it still shone: *the price is already paid. She does not know what she is.*

Tam had copied every word. His hand shook so badly on the last page that the letters leaned like drunks. Then he sat with the candle and the burning-dish and understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what kind of apprentice he was about to become.

He fed the pages to the flame as ordered. All but four, which he had palmed from the middle of the stack, folded small against his palm, sweating there while the rest curled and went to ash.

Then he took the second ledger off Master Corvane's wrist.

That part he would never be able to explain cleanly, even to himself. The chain was short; the lock was guildwork; Corvane slept with the key on a cord around his neck and woke, Tam was fairly sure, if a mouse sneezed in the walls. But the master had drunk deeper than usual at supper—celebrating something, though no one said what—and when Tam brought up his evening tea, the cord was on the bedside table, coiled like a sleeping snake, and Corvane's breathing had the wet, unguarded rhythm of a man dreaming about ledgers.

Tam told himself he only meant to look. He told himself a great many things while the lock clicked open, while the ledger came free of its cuff, while the cuff stayed on his wrist because there was no time and the shear was already hot. The ledger itself was worse than the loose pages—it was the master book, the source, going back past the Accord's founding, and in its final third the entries changed voice entirely. Not clerks anymore. Masters. A council of them, deciding, in language Tam had heard used about eel-rations and roof-slates, that the payments would cease. That Vael's weakening was an *opportunity*. That the tribute-power, properly hoarded, would make the guild not the covenant's servant but its *heir*.

One entry, dated forty years back, made him sit down on the cold floor:

*The ninth singer fled across. Good. Her blood walks in our halls now, unclaimed. When the gate calls her, we will know the covenant's last strength has come home to be collected.*

Tam read that line six times. Lena Sandoval. Lena, who had taught him to swim in the cistern by pretending she couldn't herself. Lena, who slipped him honey-bread on fast days and covered for his mistakes with a smile and a lie to Corvane's face. They hadn't raised her. They'd *farmed* her.

Above him, faintly, a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the third bell, the one that meant *seal the gates*, and under it he heard boots, many boots, and Corvane's voice rising through the floors like smoke: *"Find the boy."*

* * *

The Dead Door was humming.

Tam felt it before he saw it—a vibration in his teeth, in the fluid of his eyes, a note too low to hear and too present to ignore. The lichen on the black stone had begun to glow, a faint green-gold pulse, and the air inside the arch moved. Not wind. *Movement*, the way a throat moves before speech.

He had four pages sewn into his coat lining, a stolen ledger against his chest, a hot iron cuff biting his wrist, and a choice shaped like an archway.

Behind him, the vault door rattled. Keys. Many keys.

"Boy." Corvane's voice, through the door, stripped of every gentle mood Tam had ever basked in. "Open this door and we will speak of this like family."

*Like family.* Tam looked at the cuff on his wrist. Family chained its children to its secrets. Family burned the truth and called the ash bookkeeping. Family had taken a girl who thought she was loved and turned her into a crop to be harvested.

"I'm sorry, Master," he said, and found he meant it, which was somehow the worst part. "You should have burned me with the rest."

He stepped through the arch.

Crossing was not like walking through a door. Crossing was like being *sung*. The hum climbed through him, took the shape of his bones, and for one suspended moment Tam hung between worlds feeling utterly, completely known—every theft, every fear, every small kindness, weighed and found, if not good, then at least *willing*. The light went gold. The lichen sang. Somewhere very far away, a door burst open behind him, and Corvane shouted his name—his real name, the one no one used—and then the world folded like a page being turned.

* * *

He fell out of a wayshrine into twilight, onto grass that smelled of rain and something older than rain, and the shrine came apart behind him.

Not gently. The black stones of the arch split with sounds like ice cracking on a pond, and the glow that had carried him guttered, flared, and died, leaving a ring of dead rock in a meadow where the river, he noticed distantly, was running uphill. The air here tasted of copper and flowers. Two moons hung low and fat above the hills, one white, one the color of a bruise.

Tam lay in the grass and laughed until it turned into something else, and then he lay still and let the shaking have him, because he had crossed between worlds and the door was dead behind him and he was sixteen years old and the only person he knew on this side of the sky was a woman who had vanished through a sealed gate nine days ago and might be dead, or worse, might never have wanted finding.

"Up," he told himself, in the tone Corvane used for reluctant apprentices. "Ledgers don't carry themselves."

He got up. He checked the ledger first—of course he checked the ledger first; he was guild-raised, and terror did not survive bookkeeping—and then the four sewn pages, and then his own limbs, in ascending order of importance. The cuff was still on his wrist. He decided to take it as a message. Whatever he'd stolen, he'd stolen all of it, down to the iron.

He walked uphill along the uphill river, following the moons, and it was full dark and he was beginning to understand just how large a world could be when he saw the firelight.

Two figures at a low camp among standing stones: a man built like a courier, scarred, watchful, half-rising with a knife already loose in his hand; and beside him, wrapped in a traveler's cloak, hunched over a small fire—

Tam's legs simply went. He didn't decide it. Three years of honey-bread and cistern-swimming and being *entrusted* overrode every protocol the guild had ever drilled into him, and he was running before he finished standing, crashing through the wet grass, shouting her name in a voice that cracked in the middle like a boy's, because it was a boy's, because he was one.

"Lena!"

She turned. Even by bad firelight he could see how much she had changed in nine days—harder, leaner, a sword-hilt worn familiar at her hip—but her face, when it found his, was still her face, and it did the thing her face always did, the thing that had made the guild's cold halls bearable: it opened.

"Tam?" She was up, crossing the ground between them, catching him by the shoulders like she was checking he was real. "Tam, how—*how*—"

"The Dead Door." He was laughing again, breathless, stupid with relief. "The one in the coal cellar. Nobody ever dusted it because nobody thought it mattered, but I dusted it, I dusted it for three years, and it—" He stopped. The words backed up behind the next ones, pressing. "Lena. I have to show you something. I stole it. I stole it out of his own bedchamber, and I think—I think he let me get as far as the vault, maybe, or maybe he didn't, I don't know, but you have to see it, you have to see what they've been—"

"What they've been *what*?"

Tam pulled the second ledger from inside his coat. It was heavier than a book had any right to be, as if the weight of forty years of lies had soaked into the binding. He put it in her hands and watched her face by firelight as she read.

He watched the color leave it in stages. Confusion first. Then recognition—she was guild-raised too; she could read a ledger's grammar faster than anyone alive. Then something worse than either, a stillness like deep water, like the moment before ice gives way.

"*Stopped,*" she said finally. Her voice came out flat and strange. "They didn't fail. They didn't forget. They *decided*." Her thumb moved over the harvest entries, the vault inventories, the neat columns of hoarded power. "All those impossible trades. All those debts I balanced to the copper. They weren't paying a covenant. They were building a treasury."

"There's more." Tam's mouth was dry. He almost didn't say it. He said it anyway, because she deserved it before someone crueler gave it to her. "There's an entry. Forty years old. About the ninth singer who fled across the gate. It says her blood walks in your halls. It says—" He made himself finish. "*When the gate calls her, we will know the covenant's last strength has come home to be collected.*"

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, the uphill river murmured toward its source. Lena Sandoval stood very still, holding a book that proved the only family she had ever known had raised her the way farmers raise lambs, and Tam watched her choose—watched her actually *choose*, jaw tightening, eyes going bright and hard—not to break.

"They knew," she said quietly. "Before I ever found my name in that prophecy book. They've known my whole life what I was for." She closed the ledger with a snap that sounded like a verdict. "Corvane tucked me in, Tam. When I was small. He taught me my letters. And the entire time I was a *line item*."

"He'll come for it," Tam said. "For you. For the ledger. He rang the seal-bells while I was running. He knows I crossed."

"Let him come." It was not bravado. Tam had grown up reading faces in a hall full of masters; he knew the difference. This was arithmetic. Behind her, the scarred courier—Dain, she'd called him—exchanged a look with her, some whole conversation passing without a word, and Tam understood suddenly that he had interrupted something larger than a campfire, that the girl who used to sneak him honey-bread was now a piece on a board he couldn't yet see the edges of.

"You're hurt," Lena said abruptly, turning to him, and her hands were gentle as she pushed back his sleeve and found the raw ring of flesh where the cuff sat. Her thumb hovered over it without touching. "He chained this to himself. You cut yourself free of *him*."

"Somebody had to," Tam said. "You were busy."

And she laughed—a real laugh, ragged at the edges, startled out of her—and pulled him into a hug that smelled of woodsmoke and a world that wasn't hers, and Tam held on and let himself, for exactly ten seconds, be sixteen and frightened and found.

Then she stepped back, and the hardness returned, but it was a different hardness now, aimed outward, and she tapped the ledger against her palm like a woman choosing a weapon.

"Dain," she said. "We need to talk about what a guildmaster's private accounts are worth to the people of Bellhollow. Because if the masters stopped the payments to hoard the tribute—" her eyes went distant, working it through, ledger-fast, "—then Malvane isn't the disease. He's the *symptom*. And somewhere in these pages there has to be a record of what they actually took. Where it's kept. How much of it there is."

"There is," Tam said. "I memorized the vault index on the way down the stairs. All of it. In case the book didn't make it."

Lena stared at him. Dain, behind her, made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled in its cradle.

"You memorized a forty-year conspiracy index," she said, "while fleeing through a burning guildhall."

"It's what I'm *for*," Tam said, and hated how much of the old guild obedience was still in the joke, and watched her hear it too.

The bruise-colored moon climbed. The fire burned low. And far away, back in a world of candles and cold halls, Tam knew that somewhere Master Corvane was standing in front of a dead wayshrine in a shattered coal cellar, holding an empty cuff, doing sums of his own—and that whatever the master calculated next, it would end with ships, or soldiers, or worse, coming through what remained of the doors between worlds.

Because Tam had taken the ledger.

But Corvane still had the charter.
