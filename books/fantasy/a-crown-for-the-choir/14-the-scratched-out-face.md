## 14. The Scratched-Out Face

The archive-city of Bellhollow kept its dead the way other cities kept their kings: in glass and gold, behind velvet ropes, with candles lit at intervals so precise that the flames themselves seemed to be on a schedule of mourning.

Lena had never seen anything like it. In her world—in the guildhall's cold stone corridors—history was filed. It lived in ledgers with cracked spines and in drawers labeled by year, by trade, by degree of impossibility. You could hold a century in your hands if you knew which drawer to open. But here, history was *displayed*, hung on walls like laundry left out to dry in public, and the Bellhollowers walked beneath it daily without looking up, the way she had once walked beneath the guild's motto without reading it: *All Things Have Their Price.*

She looked up now.

The mural stretched across the vaulted ceiling of the Hall of Voices, forty feet above the marble floor, painted in pigments that still glowed faintly after a hundred years—luminous earths ground from Vael's own stone, Dain had said, mixed with river-water that ran uphill. Nine figures stood in an arc across the ceiling, each rendered larger than life, each holding or wearing or standing beside the instrument of their voice. A woman with a harp strung from moonwire. A man whose throat was a hollow reed. Twins who shared a single drum between them. And at the far right end of the arc, where the painter's light fell last—

A figure with no face.

Not damaged. Not faded. *Scratched out.* Deliberately, deeply, with something sharp enough to bite through plaster and into the stone beneath. The gouges radiated outward like cracks in ice, and someone had tried to paint over them—a thin wash of sky-blue that only made the wound more visible, the way snow makes a grave look colder.

Lena stood beneath it for a long time, her neck aching, her ledger satchel heavy against her hip.

"She was beautiful once," Dain said quietly beside her. He had his arms crossed, his scarred jaw tight, watching Lena watch the wall. "My grandmother used to bring me here when I was small. She'd point up and say, *that one sang the door shut.* I asked her why the face was gone and she cuffed me and said never to ask."

"And you've asked ever since."

"Every day of my life." He glanced sideways at her. "You haven't said much since we got back from Errenfell. Since Maren—" He stopped himself. They had agreed not to say the whole sentence yet. Saying it made it true in a way that hurt to carry.

Lena didn't answer right away. She unslung her satchel instead, knelt on the cold marble, and began laying things out with the careful geometry of her trade: the guild's founding charter, wrapped in oilcloth; the second ledger, Tam's stolen prize, its pages soft as moth wings; her own day-ledger, the one she'd been balancing by candlelight the night the prophecy found her name; and finally—the book she had carried through fire without letting herself think about why—the original covenant score, its vellum cover scorched brown at one corner, rescued from Bellhollow's burning library three days before.

Three days. Maren had died two nights ago, and grief sat in Lena's chest like a stone swallowed wrong. But grief, she was learning, could be worked with, the way you worked with a broken bone: splinted, bound, given a job to do while it healed or didn't. Her job right now was this floor, these documents, this faceless woman overhead.

"Cross-reference," she said. "That's all this is. A reconciliation audit. Every ledger balances somewhere, Dain. Even lies. Especially lies—you just have to find where the missing entry went."

Dain lowered himself to sit across from her, long legs folded, courier's patience settling over him like a coat. "And you think the missing entry is her." He nodded at the scratched-out face.

"I think," Lena said, "that nobody scratches out a face by accident."

* * *

She began, as she always began, with dates.

The founding charter came first. She had read it a thousand times as an apprentice—had copied it out twice by hand as penance for tardiness—but she had never read it *like this*, with Vael's air in her lungs and a burned score under her fingers. The charter was written in the guild's oldest hand, formal and dense, and it opened with the covenant itself: nine voices, one seam, a bargain struck between realms so that neither would devour the other. Impossible trades flowing one way, magic flowing the other, balance maintained like water finding its level.

But there—halfway down the first page, in a margin so narrow most readers would skim past it—was a line she had never noticed, because she had never known to look:

*Nine sing. One keeps.*

"Read that again," she said, and turned the charter toward Dain.

He leaned in. His eyes moved over the words twice. "One keeps what?"

"That's what I want to know." Lena's finger traced the margin. "Look at the ink. Same batch as the rest of the page—same iron-gall fade, same weight of hand. This isn't a later addition. It was written *with* the covenant. Which means the guild has always known there was a tenth role. A keeper." Her mouth felt dry. "And nowhere else in the entire charter—not in the oaths, not in the trade schedules, not in the disciplinary codes I memorized as a girl—is that role mentioned again."

"They cut it out."

"They cut it out," she agreed, "and then they built a guild on top of the hole."

The second ledger told the rest—or told enough. Tam had nearly died bringing it across a collapsing wayshrine, and the pages bore the cost: water-stained, smoke-edged, several entries reduced to ghost-ink. But the tribute schedule survived intact, and Lena laid it beside the guild's own payment records, the ones she had spent ten years balancing, and did what she did best.

She reconciled them.

Entry by entry, year by year, the two books diverged. The guild's records showed payments made. The second ledger showed payments *received*—and then, beginning sixty years ago, payments received in name only. Sums logged as sent that had never crossed the gate. Impossible things traded away on paper while the real tribute accumulated in guild vaults, hoarded, compounded, growing fat in the dark. Corvane's masters hadn't neglected the covenant. They had *farmed* it.

"I knew they'd stopped," Dain said. His voice was flat, controlled, the control costing him visibly. "I told you that. I didn't know they were skimming. Sixty years of skimmed tribute—that's not negligence, that's—"

"Treason against two worlds." Lena closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "Yes."

But that wasn't the thing that made her hands shake. That wasn't the thing she kept coming back to.

It was the names.

Because the second ledger, unlike the guild's sanitized records, recorded *who* had signed off on each diverted payment—who had authorized the theft, decade after decade—and the earliest signature, the one at the top of the divergence, belonged to a name written in a strong, slanting hand:

*S. Veyra, First Voice, Keeper pro tem.*

Veyra.

Lena stared at the name until the letters swam. It was a common enough pattern of syllables. Names repeated. Coincidence was the mortar of every false conclusion she'd ever caught apprentices making, and she had thrashed more than one of them for building theories on it.

But her grandmother's name was Sera Veyra-Sandoval. And her grandmother had come from *somewhere*, though she never spoke of where, and she had taught Lena her letters using a strange alphabet alongside the common one—an alphabet Lena had assumed was just an old woman's whim, decorative, meaningless—and that alphabet, she realized now, staring at the marginal annotations in the second ledger, was *Vaeli*. Her grandmother had annotated her own theft-authorizations in the language of the world she'd helped bind.

"Lena." Dain's voice came from very far away. "You've gone white as the marble. What is it?"

"My grandmother," Lena said, and heard her own voice as if it belonged to someone else, "kept the guild's first accounts."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon while the light in the Hall of Voices shifted from amber to violet, the luminous pigments waking as twilight came, so that the nine singers seemed to breathe overhead. Lena forgot to eat. Dain brought bread and cheese from a market stall and set it beside her without comment, and she ate mechanically, one hand still turning pages, because her body needed fuel even while her mind was busy dismantling everything she had believed about her own life.

Here is what the documents said, when she let them speak in order:

Sera Veyra had been the ninth singer. The last voice added to the choir, the youngest, the one who sang the sealing verse when the covenant was forged. The murals confirmed it—Dain found the original dedication plaque pried off the wall and hidden behind a loose panel (he knew Bellhollow's secrets the way Lena knew the guild's coal-chutes), and the plaque named all nine, and the ninth name was hers.

Sera Veyra had also, per the second ledger, been appointed *Keeper pro tem*—temporary keeper—which meant the tenth role had existed, had been assigned, had been hers. *Nine sing. One keeps.* The guild's founders had split the covenant's guardianship in two and then, within a generation, erased half of it from every record but the deepest ones.

And then, forty-one years ago, the entries stopped bearing her signature. The final entry in her hand was not a payment record at all. It was a single line, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss the page beneath:

*They mean to keep it all. I will not sign. The verse goes with me.*

"The verse," Lena whispered. "The final verse. The one Malvane's counter-song broke against. The one we don't have." She looked up at the ceiling, at the gouged face wreathed in blue wash and failing light. "She took it with her when she fled. Across the gate. Into my world."

"And hid it," Dain said slowly. He had gone very still, the stillness of a man watching a trap he half-understood begin to close around someone he— 

He cared about her. She had known it for weeks without naming it, and she set the knowledge aside the way she set aside everything that wasn't the current problem, because the current problem was enormous and personal and breathing down her neck.

"Hid it where?" he finished.

Lena looked down at the founding charter. At the document every guild apprentice copied by hand. The document that hung framed in the Master's hall. The document that had been *in her keeping*, in her office, twenty feet from her desk, for six years.

"Not in my world," she said. "She never went further than the guildhall. Why would she? She'd spent her life in archives; she knew how archives work. Anything hidden is eventually found unless—" Her breath caught. "Unless it isn't hidden at all. Unless it's *displayed*. Unless it's put somewhere so obvious, so central, so sacred that no one thinks to search it, because searching it would be sacrilege." Her fingers trembled as she unrolled the charter fully, weighting its corners with the bread knife, the salt cellar, a candlestick Dain fetched without being asked. "You don't hide a verse inside a songbook. You hide it inside the *law*. Inside the thing everyone reads and no one reads *closely*, because closeness breeds blindness. I should know. I read this document a thousand times and saw nothing."

The charter was long—forty pages, densely ruled—and the guild's copyists had transcribed it faithfully for a century. But faithful transcription preserves everything, including mistakes. Including oddities. Including, if a cunning woman had engineered them, *flaws*.

Lena hunted flaws.

She found the first one within the hour: a passage in the oath section where the line-length broke pattern—one word too many, a redundancy that no competent scribe would leave (*the seal is sealed, the bound is bound*). The second was in the trade schedules: a date written in the old style rather than the new, inconsistent with every date around it. The third was a marginal flourish, a small inked vine, whose leaves numbered thirteen where every other vine in the document bore twelve.

"Thirteen," Dain breathed. "There are thirteen verses in the covenant score. We counted them in the burned book. Twelve we have. The thirteenth is the one the usurper shattered the world without."

Lena's heart was pounding so hard she could hear it in her ears. "Count the leaves on every vine. Mark the anomalous ones. Anomalies are coordinates—she was a keeper, she thought in indexes and cross-references, she'd have left a retrieval key." Her voice sounded strange to her, bright and brittle. "This is a ledger, Dain. She wrote the charter *as a ledger*. Every error is an entry. We just have to balance it."

They worked until the candles guttered and the night-watch of Bellhollow called the hours in their low, bell-toned voices. Vine by vine, flaw by flaw, Lena marked the anomalies and mapped them against the charter's structure, and slowly, terribly, beautifully, a pattern emerged: the redundant words, taken in sequence, spelled a phrase in plain script. The misdated numbers resolved to page and line references. The thirteenth leaf pointed to the charter's final page—the colophon, the scribe's signing place, the one part of any document that everyone reads and no one examines.

Lena turned to the colophon with hands that would not quite obey her.

There, beneath the official closing lines, in ink that matched the rest perfectly, ran a final inscription she had never seen because she had never been permitted to handle the *original*—only copies, and the copies, she understood now with a lurch, had been *corrected*. Some master, some chain of masters, had smoothed the charter's errors out of every duplicate for a hundred years, erasing the key without knowing it was a key, tidying the grandmother's message into oblivion in every version but the one locked in the Master's cabinet.

The original's colophon read:

*For the one who comes after: the verse is not lost. It sleeps in the house I built of words. Sing it standing, with both worlds in your ears, and pay what it asks. — S.V., who chose.*

Below it, smaller, almost tender:

*I am sorry for what it will cost you, little keeper. I watched them choose you before you were born.*

The Hall of Voices was silent except for the candles.

Lena read the last line four times. Five. The words refused to behave like words; they behaved like a door swinging open onto a room she had lived in all her life without knowing it existed.

"*Before you were born,*" she repeated.

"Lena." Dain's hand hovered near her shoulder, not quite touching. "Say what you're thinking. Out loud. Sometimes it helps."

"What I'm thinking," she said, "is that the prophecy book found my name because my name was already in the covenant. That the gate didn't open for a random ledger-keeper who happened to be balancing accounts at midnight. That I wasn't *pulled* here by accident, or chosen by prophecy, or any of the things I've been telling myself to stay angry about." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I'm the granddaughter of the ninth singer. I'm the *inheritance*. My grandmother fled your world, married a fisherman's son in mine, raised my mother, taught *me* her letters, taught me to keep accounts, taught me that every entry must balance—and then the guild trained me in their corrupted version of everything she gave me, and put me in front of the gate, and waited."

"Waited?"

"Someone waited." Her mind was moving fast now, cold and clear, the auditor's clarity that had always been her refuge and was suddenly the only thing holding her together. "Corvane knew the prophecy book held tonight's date. He ordered the gate double-sealed—he *knew*. And the second ledger passed from master to master for sixty years, and every master who signed it knew the ninth singer's name, and none of them scratched her face off the mural themselves—it was already gouged when Dain was a boy. So the erasure is old. Older than Corvane. The guild has known for a century that the keeper's bloodline survived in my world, and they raised me inside their walls anyway, training me for a role they'd officially deleted." She looked up at the faceless singer overhead, at the blue-washed wound of her. "They didn't erase her because they hated her. They erased her because she was evidence. Because her face proved the tenth role existed, and the tenth role was the one thing standing between them and keeping everything they stole."

The candles flickered. Somewhere above, in the vaulting, the painted twins seemed to lean on their shared drum.

"So you weren't chosen," Dain said quietly. "You were *bred* for it. Raised for it. Placed."

"No." Lena shook her head, and surprised herself by how certain the motion was. "No—that's the lie I have to refuse, Dain, and I can feel how easy it would be to believe it. To decide I'm a puppet and nothing more, that my whole life was someone else's handwriting." She gathered the documents into her arms, charter and ledgers and burned score, holding them against her chest like a child. "But my grandmother wrote *chose*. Twice. 'Who chose.' She chose to flee. She chose to hide the verse. She chose to leave a key instead of a weapon. Whatever the guild intended, whatever Corvane wants—she trusted whoever came after her to make their own choice. That's the whole design. That's why the verse needs a keeper who *chooses*. She built the escape hatch into the inheritance itself." Her eyes stung, and she let them. "I am not random. I am not free of this. But I am not owned by it either. I'm the part she left unfinished on purpose."

Dain was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "Your grandmother sounds like someone I'd have liked."

"You'd have argued with her constantly."

"Probably." He smiled, tired and crooked. "Sounds like someone you argue with constantly, too."

Lena laughed despite everything—a wet, broken sound—and pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. Grief for Maren rose in her then, and grief for the grandmother she had loved without knowing her, and grief for the girl she had been a month ago, balancing accounts by candlelight, believing that order was the same thing as safety. She let it all rise and pass through her, because Maren had taught her that too, in the drowned shrine's last light: *grief is a tide, little keeper. You don't fight it. You let it move you.*

When she lowered her hand, Dain was watching the mural.

"There's one more thing," he said. "If the verse is in the charter—if it's been in the guildhall all along, in the Master's cabinet—then Corvane doesn't need us anymore. He needs the charter. And the moment he realizes the original was never corrected—" He stopped.

"He'll come for it." Lena stood, rolling the charter tight, her movements quickening with purpose. "Or he'll already be coming. Tam said the masters have been burning pages, panicking. Panic makes men fast and stupid, and Corvane is neither, which makes him worse." She slung her satchel across her body, cinched it, checked the clasps twice—old habits, the gatekeeper's liturgy. "We have to get to the wayshrine. We have to get home before he understands what he's sitting on."

Dain rose too, and paused, looking up one last time at the arc of nine singers and the wound where the ninth had been.

"For what it's worth," he said, "whoever gouged her face out wanted the world to forget her." He met Lena's eyes. "Instead they left the one mark on this ceiling nobody could stop staring at. I stared at it my whole childhood. Maybe that's the real inheritance. Everything they tried to bury is exactly what pulled you here."

Lena looked up at her grandmother's absence—at the fierce negative space where a face should have been—and felt something settle in her chest, not peace exactly, but orientation. North. True north.

"Then let's go give the guild back its own mistake," she said. "Charter, verse, and all."

They hurried out through the Hall of Voices' great doors into the violet evening, and behind them the candles burned on in their perfect intervals, and high overhead the eight remembered singers kept their painted vigil around the empty place where the ninth had stood—

While, far away in another world, in a guildhall full of locked cabinets and burning pages, a man with a ledger chained to his wrist was already asking his enforcers a single question:

*Where is the original charter?*
