## 15. Maren's Tide-Song

The sea had taken Errenfell a hundred years ago, and it had never quite finished the taking.

Maren felt that truth in her bones as their boat slid over the drowned streets. Below them, pale and wavering in the green light, lay the bones of the town: a bell tower leaning like an old man against the current, rooftops furred with weed, a market square where fish now schooled through the doorways of houses that had once smelled of bread and tallow and rain on hot stone. The water was so clear here—clearer than any water had a right to be—that she could see a child's wooden horse still standing upright in what had been a nursery window, its paint long gone, its rocking legs frozen mid-motion.

"They say the shrine pulled the town down after itself," Dain said quietly from the oars. He did not row so much as guide; the current here moved the way he asked it to, which Maren found herself disliking more each day. Nothing natural obeyed like that. "When the payments stopped and the covenant began to fray, Errenfell's shrine went first. The sea came up the river road in a single night. No storm. The fishermen swore the water walked."

"Water doesn't walk," Maren said.

"No," Dain agreed. "It sings. And when the song goes wrong, everything that listened has to go somewhere."

Lena sat in the bow with the covenant score open across her knees, three books rescued from Bellhollow's ashes stacked beneath her elbows like ballast. She had not slept properly since the burning. Maren had watched her try—the girl's eyes closing, then snapping open again as if the dark behind her lids held something worse than the fire. There was ash still ground into the creases of her knuckles. She had stopped trying to wash it out.

"Is it true," Lena said, without looking up, "that you've sung here before?"

Maren looked at her own hands. Fisherwoman's hands, rope-scarred, nails cut blunt, the knuckles swollen with sixty years of hauling nets against the tide. Hands that had learned every lullaby her mother knew, and her mother before her, all the way back to songs no one remembered writing because they hadn't been written—they'd been *needed*.

"Once," Maren said. "Fifty years ago. When I was young enough to think my voice was mine alone." She nodded toward the place where the water deepened from green to blue-black, a circle perhaps two hundred paces across where the sea went suddenly, unnaturally still. Even the wind seemed to step around it. "There. The shrine of Errenfell. My grandmother sang at its last waking. And her grandmother before her. We are a family of tide-singers, girl. It is not a gift. It is a chore we inherited, like a leaky roof."

"And now?"

"Now I'm old enough to know better," Maren said, "and I'm going to do it anyway."

* * *

They moored at the edge of the still water, because the boat would not cross it. Maren tested it herself, trailing one hand over the side, and felt the boundary like a held breath—a line drawn in the sea where the sea agreed to stop. Beneath the keel, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something pulsed. Once. Twice. Patient. Waiting for a voice it half-remembered.

She waded in.

The water was warm, which was wrong; the sea off this coast had never been warm in her lifetime. It closed around her waist like a hand, gentle but insistent, and the bottom beneath her feet was not sand but smooth worked stone—flagstones, she realized, the market square of Errenfell, and she was walking down a street that had become a seabed. Fish scattered from her shins like startled thoughts. Ahead, rising from the deep circle, the shrine: a ring of nine pillars, eight still standing, one toppled and crusted white, around a basin of black stone that the centuries had not managed to silt. Water stood in the basin without moving, without reflecting the sky. It reflected instead something else—a darkness shot through with threads of gold, thin as spider silk, most of them frayed or snapped.

The seam between worlds. She had seen it only once before, as a girl, from her grandmother's shoulders, and it had been a woven thing then, bright and whole, humming like a harbor full of bells.

Now it hung by threads.

"Oh, you poor thing," Maren said aloud, and meant it the way she'd have said it to a gull with a broken wing, or a child left too long in the cold. Behind her she heard Lena wading in, gasping at the warmth, and Dain holding back at the boundary as if he didn't trust himself inside it—which, Maren thought, was probably wise of him.

"The score says the singer stands at the ninth pillar," Lena called, wading closer, the burned book held above the waterline. "The one that fell. You stand where the tenth voice would be if—" She stopped. Maren heard her swallow. "If there were one."

"There isn't," Maren said. "So we do what my grandmother's people did when a crew came up short. One woman pulls double. Sing on, girl. Whatever happens, however it sounds—sing on until I stop you. That's your whole task tonight. Can you manage it?"

"I can manage it."

Maren reached the fallen pillar and set her bare feet on it, on stone worn smooth by singers dead five hundred years, and looked down into the basin where the broken seam hung like a net someone had slashed open, letting all the world's magic bleed out into the dark.

She thought of her son.

She had decided, on the boat, that she would not think of him. That was the trick of it, she'd told herself—go in empty, go in clean, offer the song and nothing else. But standing there with the warm water at her ribs and the old stone under her feet, she understood that she had lied to herself, because the song did not come from nowhere. It came from everything. And everything included Tammen.

Tammen, who had drowned at fourteen off the shallows at Greywater Point, in water no deeper than his chest, in a riptide no one had seen coming, on a morning so bright and ordinary that Maren could still—fifty years later—taste the tea she'd been drinking when they carried him up the beach. She had sung over his body. That was the custom, for a tide-singer's child: you sang them back to the sea that took them, gave them to the water as a kind of argument, a proof that the bargain between the shore and the deep still held even when it broke your heart.

She had sung, and the sea had gone quiet around him, and she had known, in the marrow-deep way singers know things, that the covenant had been dying even then. That the tribute had stopped years before. That her son had drowned in a world whose seams were already unraveling, and that the water that took him had been hungry the way only unbound water is hungry.

She had hated the guild for forty years for that. Quietly. In the privacy of her own skull, while she mended nets and sold fish and sang lullabies to other women's children because hers was past needing them.

And now the ledger-keeper stood behind her in the warm water, holding a burned book, asking her to sing the very song that might have saved him, fifty years too late.

"Well," Maren said softly, to the pillar, to the sea, to the boy who wasn't there. "Better late than never. Isn't that what we always told the tourists?"

She began to sing.

* * *

It was not a lullaby. It was older than lullabies. It lived in the part of her voice she used maybe twice a decade, the register that made dogs go silent and grown men look at the horizon and pretend their eyes weren't wet. It was a working song, a binding song, the kind of song that tells the sea *stay there*, and the sky *hold that*, and the small bright thread between worlds *remember what you are*.

The basin answered.

The black water shivered. The golden threads in the depths lifted, swaying like kelp in a current, and where they touched the sound of her voice they *brightened*—frayed ends knitting, snapped strands reaching for one another across the dark with the desperate eagerness of things that had waited a hundred years to be whole. The still circle trembled. Far below, in the drowned streets, the bell tower rang—one low, sweet note, though no rope had been pulled and no hand had touched it—and every fish in Errenfell turned at once toward the sound.

Behind her, Lena's voice rose to meet hers, thin and uncertain and utterly necessary, carrying the harmony line from the score, and the seam *blazed*—

For one breath.

One breath, Maren thought afterward, trying to hold the memory of it the way you hold water in cupped hands. One breath in which the world was stitched right. She felt it everywhere at once: in the water going calm and purposeful around her thighs, in the air above the drowned town turning clean and cold and *ordered*, in the far-off sense—like hearing a neighbor's hearth through a shared wall—that on the other side of the seam, another shore had felt it too, and stirred, and hoped.

One breath in which her son's name rose in her throat with the song, unbidden, and she let it rise, and the sea around her glowed with it.

Then the breath ended.

The threads snapped back—not all at once but in a slow, terrible cascade, like a net giving way strand by strand, each one singing a small dying note as it tore loose. The light guttered. The bell tower's echo curdled and sank. The warm water went cold so fast it burned, and Maren staggered on the fallen pillar, her voice cracking, and the song collapsed into the ordinary sounds of the sea: slap of swell, cry of a distant bird, the ragged breathing of an old woman who had just given everything she had and been told it wasn't enough.

"It needs more voices," Lena said. Her voice was shaking. She stood hip-deep in the dark water with the score clutched to her chest, and her face was wet, and Maren couldn't tell anymore which wetness was the sea. "It opened—it *worked*—but it's a rope bridge with two planks. It needs—"

"Nine," Maren said. "Nine voices minimum. Ten to make it permanent. My grandmother wrote that much in the family book before the guild confiscated such books." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Ten. And I counted what we have on two fingers, girl, and I still had fingers left over."

Dain waded out to them, grim-faced, steadying Lena's elbow, and none of them spoke for a while. The seam hung below them in the basin, dimmer now than before they'd come—or perhaps that was only Maren's tired eyes. Perhaps it was exactly as dim as it had been. Perhaps the one breath had changed nothing at all.

Except that it had changed her. She could feel that already, the way you feel a tooth after the dentist's chair: a absence with a shape to it. Something missing. Something she'd handed over without being asked, because the song took its price the moment it was sung, and the price was always memory.

She cast about inside herself, quick and frightened, taking inventory. Her mother's kitchen—there. The smell of tar on her father's coat—there. Her wedding day, brief and sunlit—there. Tammen at three, chasing gulls—there. Tammen at ten, mending his first net badly—there. Tammen at fourte—

Tammen at fourteen was—

Maren stood very still in the cold water.

Tammen at fourteen was a boy on a beach. A boy being carried up a beach. A boy under a blanket, gray-lipped, seawater running from the corner of his mouth, and she was singing over him, and she could remember the singing, every note of it, could remember the sea going quiet—but the boy himself had gone soft-edged, blurred, like a face seen through fogged glass. She could remember *that he had drowned*. She could remember loving him. She could remember the shape of the grief, which had been the architecture of half her life.

But she could not remember his face that day. His eyes. Whether he had looked peaceful. Whether his hair had been plastered to his forehead. The last sight of her son, the sight she had carried for fifty years, the sight she had taken out and looked at every night the way misers count coin—

Gone. Paid. Spent on one breath of mended world.

"Oh," Maren said. Very quietly. The sea took the word and folded it away.

"Maren?" Lena's hand on her arm. "What is it? What did it take? The books don't say how you know—you just know, don't you. You feel the hole."

"You feel the hole," Maren agreed. She found she was smiling, which surprised her; grief and relief can wear the same face, and she supposed hers wore both. "Aye. I feel the hole." She looked at the girl—at the ledger-keeper, the keeper who chooses, the poor child who had no idea yet what the final verse would ask of her—and made a decision, the kind of decision you make the way you tie off a net: quickly, firmly, knowing it will hold or it won't.

"Listen to me, Lena Sandoval. Because you're going to want to stop. Everyone wants to stop, after the first time. You'll sing, and it will cost, and you'll think: *I'll gather the others first, I'll be stronger, I'll find another way.* And the seam will keep bleeding while you deliberate, and Malvane's drums will keep marching, and one morning there won't be a seam left to mend." She gripped the girl's wrist, rope-scarred hand on ash-grained hand. "I paid a memory tonight. I don't even know yet which one—I'll find the hole in the coming days, and it will be the worst one, because it always is. The song takes what holds you together, because that's what it's made of. Do you understand? It's not cruelty. It's carpentry. It builds the covenant out of the beams of our lives."

Lena's eyes were wide and dark. "Then why—"

"Because I heard it," Maren said. "For one breath, I heard the world the way it's meant to be. Whole. Held. Every soul on both shores safe inside a song instead of exposed to the weather of chance and empire." She released the girl's wrist and turned back to the basin, to the frayed golden threads swaying in the dark, and her voice gentled. "My son died in an unwoven world. I can't get the memory of his face back, and I wouldn't trade the breath I bought with it, and both those things are true, and that's the whole of the tide-singer's life, girl. Tender and terrible, in the same mouthful, forever."

The bell tower rang again, faintly, unprompted—as if the drowned town had heard her and approved.

* * *

They camped that night on the shoal above the still water, because Maren refused to leave. "You don't walk away from a patient mid-stitch," she said, and neither of them argued. Dain built a driftwood fire that burned strangely blue at its heart, and Lena sat wrapped in a blanket reading the score by its light, hunting for the names of the other singers, the places they might have scattered to, the words *mountain* and *bell* leaping out at her like sparks.

Maren sat apart, facing the sea, doing what she had done every night for fifty years: saying goodnight to her son.

Except tonight the ritual failed her. She reached for his face and found the fog where it should have been, and her breath caught, and for a moment—an old woman alone in the dark with the biggest hole in her life freshly dug—she nearly wept herself inside out.

Instead she started talking. Telling him about it, the way she'd always talked to him at his grave: the ledger-keeper and the courier, the burned library, the usurper with his armies, the one breath of mended light. *You'd have liked the girl,* she told the fog. *Stubborn. Frightened and stubborn, which is the best kind.*

And somewhere in the telling—she couldn't say when—the fog shifted. Not lifting, never lifting; the memory was spent, and no amount of love would buy it back. But beside the hole, around the hole, the rest of him stood clear and bright: Tammen at three chasing gulls. Tammen at ten with his badly mended net. Tammen laughing on her shoulders at the Errenfell festival, the year before the end, salt wind in both their hair.

He was not less than he had been. He was differently held. He lived now in the covenant's light along with everything else the song had taken, woven into the seam she was trying to mend—part of the stitch instead of the hand that made it.

"That'll do," Maren said to the sea, gruffly, wiping her face with a palm rough as barnacle. "That'll have to do."

Behind her, Lena looked up from the score with an expression Maren recognized from the faces of young women hearing hard truths for the first time: the exact moment wonder and dread shake hands.

"I found them," the girl said. "Two of the nine. There's a bell-ringer in the mountains—Orlin, the score calls him, Orlin of the Nine Bells. And there's a third name, but it's been inked over. Scratched out, like the mural in Bellhollow." She hesitated. "And Maren—the council convenes at Tidebreak in six days. The free cities. If we're going to ask for ships and singers, we ask there, or we ask nowhere."

Maren looked out at the dark water, at the drowned town sleeping beneath it, at the faint gold threads trembling in the deep.

"Six days," she said. "Then we'd best teach the free cities the difference between a song and a war chant. Because I promise you, girl—when Malvane hears what we mean to do, he'll bring his drummers."

Far out beyond the shoal, where the sea met the night, something answered: a low pulse of sound, rhythmic and vast, like a heart the size of a horizon beginning to beat.

Dain was on his feet before the second pulse, staring north. "Drums," he said. "That's not surf. That's Malvane's vanguard—and they're marching for Tidebreak."
