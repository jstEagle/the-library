## 17. Corvane Comes for Her

The wayshrine woke three days before Corvane did.

Lena felt it first as a wrongness in her teeth—a low, grinding hum, like a coin held between molars. Then the birds left the valley. All of them, at once, in a single silent lifting that made the morning sky look scoured. Dain noticed too; she saw him stop in the middle of mending a saddle strap and turn his head toward the shrine the way a deer turns toward a sound too low to hear.

"That's not Malvane," he said.

"No," Lena said. "It's home."

She had learned the shrines' language in her weeks in Vael—the way each one spoke a slightly different dialect of pressure and light, the way Malvane's war-magic tasted of iron and burnt sugar. This was different. This hum was orderly. Accounted for. It was the sound of double-entry bookkeeping rendered in stone, and she had grown up falling asleep to it through three floors of the guildhall.

Someone had opened a gate from her side. Someone had paid the toll, filed the forms, sealed the permissions in wax. Someone was coming through with the guild's blessing, and the guild did not bless anything without a ledger line to justify it.

"Tam's shrine?" Orlin asked, coming down the hill with a bucket of water slopping over his boots. The old bell-ringer had taken to carrying water everywhere, though no one had asked him to; Lena suspected it was the closest thing his hands knew to ringing.

"Tam's shrine," Dain said grimly. "Which means Tam either opened it or was opened through it."

They had perhaps a day. Maybe less. Lena spent it the way she had spent every crisis of her life: with ink. She copied the founding charter's hidden verse onto three separate scraps of hide and sewed them into the linings of her coat, her boots, and—after a moment's grim consideration—Dain's hat, because no one in the history of either realm had ever voluntarily stolen Dain's hat. She buried the original beneath the hearthstone of the fisherman's hut they'd borrowed, under a loose stone she'd have found in her sleep, because some habits were older than worlds.

And then, because there was nothing left to organize, she sat on the doorstep and watched the road and felt the thing she had been refusing to feel since the night the prophecy book had written her name.

He was coming for her. Not Malvane, with his armies and his grief. Corvane. Master Corvane, who had taught her to read a ledger the way priests taught scripture—who had found her at nine years old, orphaned and thieving apples off the guild barges, and instead of turning her out had said, *You counted the barrels before we caught you. Come inside where it's warm.* Who had promoted her past enforcers three times her age because she never once fudged a column. Who had dismissed the prophecy entry as a scribal error while wearing a chained ledger on his wrist like a shackle he'd chosen.

She loved him. That was the truth of it, and no amount of burned pages or sealed gates would dissolve it. And she was beginning to understand, with the cold clarity of the far side of a bridge already burned, that he loved her too—which was somehow worse, because love was the one currency the guild had never taught her to audit.

* * *

They came at dusk, when the light went the color of old brass and the hills threw shadows long enough to hide an army in.

There were six of them. Six enforcers in the guild's grey-and-slate, faces masked to the eyes, walking in the loose diamond formation Lena had drilled a hundred times in the training yard. And at their center, unhurried, a tall man in a traveling coat the color of wet slate, silver at the temples, a ledger chained to his left wrist and swinging with each step like a pendulum counting down something.

Master Corvane had not aged a day in the weeks since she'd seen him. He had, if anything, grown *more* himself—the way stone grows more stone. He stopped twenty paces from the fisherman's hut, and his eyes found Lena as though the intervening hills, the wrong-colored sky, the entire alien twilight of Vael were so much irrelevant furniture.

"Lena Sandoval," he said. His voice carried the way it always had, pitched to fill the guildhall's reading room without effort. "You are in violation of Article One, Section Three: unauthorized passage through a sealed gate. You are in possession of guild property. And you are, as of the moment you crossed, legally dead—your name struck from the rolls at my own instruction, which was, I'll note, the most difficult entry I have ever been required to make."

"Then unmake it," Lena said. She stood on the doorstep with her arms crossed, and she was proud of how steady her voice came out. "Strike the entry. Reopen the rolls."

"I intend to." He took one step closer. The enforcers did not move; they didn't need to. Six of them, and four of the choir here—Lena, Dain, Orlin, and Tam, who had gone very pale and very still by the woodpile. "That is rather the point of this expedition."

Dain shifted, and Lena felt him coiling the way he coiled before violence, the scarred courier's arithmetic already running—six against four, open ground, the hut at their backs. She put a hand on his arm without looking away from Corvane.

"Master," she said. "You crossed a world to reach me. So let's not waste it on Article One. Tell me why you're really here."

Something flickered behind his eyes—approval, maybe, that she'd learned to skip the preamble. "The charter," he said. "The founding charter of the Guild of Impossible Things, currently hidden under a hearthstone you believe I don't know about." He smiled, thin and dry. "You sewed copies into your coat, your boots, and Dain Ash's hat. The hat was clever. The hearthstone was not. You were raised on my ledgers, child. You think in hiding places I invented."

Lena's stomach dropped, but she kept her face still. *Never let them see the column wobble.* "You knew. Before you came."

"I knew before *you* knew. The charter left the guildhall the night you did. Everything in my hall leaves a mark, Lena. That is what a ledger *is*." He unclasped the chain from his wrist—not to remove it, never to remove it, but to let the book hang open in his palm. "Now. Before this becomes something neither of us wants, I am authorized to make you an offer. And I want you to understand that the authorization is mine. I wrote it. The council merely sighed and signed."

He closed the ledger and looked at her, and for one unguarded moment he looked tired. Old. A man who had carried a chained book for forty years and knew exactly what it weighed.

"Come home," he said. "Full mastery. Your name on the door of the counting room—*your* name, Lena, not some master's who died before you were born. The prophecy book goes into the deep vault, and the deep vault goes into the sea, and no one in either world ever reads your name beside an omen of war again. The gate is resealed. Vael becomes a story we tell apprentices to keep them honest. And you—" his voice did something almost gentle, "—you get your life back. The one you built. The one that was *good*, before a book full of dead men's ink told you it wasn't."

The words landed like coins on a counter, each one weighted, each one real. And the terrible thing—the thing she would carry like a stone in her boot for weeks—was that for three full heartbeats, she wanted it. She stood in the brass-colored dusk of a dying world and *wanted* the warm reading room, the smell of tallow and good paper, the satisfying click of a balanced column, the small clean life she had built out of order because order was the only thing that had never lied to her.

"You stopped the payments," she said.

"I managed a transition."

"You *stopped the payments*." She came down off the step now, one step, two, until the space between them was close enough that the enforcers' hands drifted toward their weapons and Corvane lifted two fingers and stilled them. "The tribute kept the seam from bleeding. The guildmasters hoarded it. Decades, Master. A century of missed payments, and Vael went to rot and Malvane went to war, and you stood in the reading room and called the prophecy a scribal error while wearing the proof on your wrist."

"The proof," Corvane said quietly, "that the covenant was a leash."

The words came out flat and certain, and Lena realized he had said them before—to the council, to himself, in the dark, for years. "You've read the charter's verse," he continued. "Or part of it. You think it's a hymn. It's a contract, and contracts have parties, and the parties were not equal. Do you know what the impossible trades *were*, before the guild dressed them in mystery? A tithe. Vael's singers bound the seam, and our world paid for the binding—paid in magic, in years, in *possibility*. Every impossible thing the guild ever sold was a coin skimmed off that tithe. The guild didn't serve the covenant, Lena. The guild *farmed* it. I simply stopped plowing a field that was bleeding us dry."

"And Malvane?"

"Is what grows in an unfarmed field." He said it without flinching. "Yes. I know what I unchained. I have read the same reports you have. I weighed a century of quiet theft against a war, and I chose the theft, because the theft was *ours* and the war was *theirs*, and I am a guildmaster, not a saint. I have never pretended otherwise." He stepped closer still, and now his voice dropped to the register he'd used on her when she was nine and weeping over her first mislaid column. "But you are not a saint either, child. You are a keeper. You keep accounts. And the account I am offering you is clean: come home, take the mastery, and help me do properly what I did badly alone—manage the seam's decay in a controlled fashion instead of watching Malvane weaponize it. Or stay here, and be a verse in a dead woman's song, and die in a war between a tyrant and a memory."

The dusk had gone almost fully dark while he spoke. Somewhere behind Lena, Orlin's bucket had stopped sloshing; she could hear the old man breathing, slow and deliberate, the way he breathed when his hands wanted bells and there were no bells.

"That's the offer," Lena said.

"That's the offer."

"And if I refuse?"

Corvane sighed, and it was the sigh of the reading room, of late nights and unbalanced columns, and she hated how well she knew it. "Then I am required to inform you that the charter is guild property, that you are harboring guild property—" and here his gaze moved, for the first time, past her shoulder, "—and that guild property is subject to retrieval."

Lena turned.

Tam was standing by the woodpile with the second ledger clutched to his chest—the stolen book, the proof, the thing he had crossed a collapsing wayshrine to bring them at a cost Lena still didn't fully know. He had gone white as paper. Two enforcers were already moving, unhurried, professional, flanking him the way they'd been flanking the whole conversation while everyone watched the master and the runaway.

"No," Lena said.

"Tamwell Vess is a guild apprentice," Corvane said, "who stole a sealed ledger, assaulted two senior members, and fled through an unstable gate in violation of every statute I wrote his training around. He is coming home. He will face the council's discipline, which, given his age and my advocacy, will be severe and survivable." A pause. "You have my word on that. My *recorded* word. Check the ledger if you like."

"Master—" Tam's voice cracked. He looked at Lena, and there was no plea in it, which was somehow worse; there was only the same stubborn, terrified resolve that had carried him through a burning gate with a chained book. "Don't trade the charter for me. Don't you dare. It's not— it's not worth—"

"It's worth exactly one apprentice," Lena said, "and I'm not paying it."

But even as she said it, her mind was running the column, because she was her master's student and she could not help it. Six enforcers. Four of them, and Orlin's hands were empty, and Dain was fast but not six-fast, and the charter was under a hearthstone that Corvane had already found once. If she fought, she lost everything—Tam, the charter, probably the verse-copies, possibly her life. If she bargained, she lost the charter and maybe Tam anyway. If she went with Corvane—

*Come home. Mastery. Erasure of the prophecy.*

The enforcers reached Tam. One took his arm; the other held out an open hand for the ledger, and Tam clutched it harder, and the enforcer—patient, terrible, trained by the same yard Lena had trained in—simply began to apply pressure, joint by joint, the way you'd close a stubborn clasp.

"Wait," Lena said.

And Corvane waited. That was the thing about him; he always waited, because he knew, the way she knew, that people in pain will fill silence with concessions.

She made herself look at him—really look, past the slate coat and the silver temples and the chained book, down to the man who had said *come inside where it's warm*—and she found, to her surprise, that she could speak without her voice shaking.

"I'll come home," she said. "On one condition. Not the charter. Not the verse. Tam goes free, unmarked, and the ledger he stole goes with him—because it's not guild property anymore, Master. It's *evidence*. You taught me that distinction yourself, the night you explained why we keep two sets of books for the council and the crown." She saw the flicker in his eyes and pressed it. "You want me? The keeper the prophecy names? Then you need me willing, and you know it, and you know I know it. Tam walks. Or I stand on this doorstep until your enforcers drag all four of us, and you explain to the council why the tenth voice of the covenant is a corpse."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far off, a nightbird that shouldn't have existed made a sound like a struck glass.

"You're bluffing," Corvane said, but softly, testing the weight of it.

"I'm *auditing*," Lena said. "You taught me the difference too."

And Corvane—Master Corvane, who had never once lost a negotiation in her hearing—looked at her for a long, long moment, and something moved behind his face that she could not read and did not like: not anger, not defeat, but the particular stillness of a man who has just watched a column balance in a way he did not plan.

"Release the apprentice," he said.

The pressure on Tam's arm eased. The enforcer's open hand closed on empty air. Tam stood there clutching the ledger, blinking, not quite believing it, and Lena felt a rush of relief so violent it was almost vertigo—

—until Corvane spoke again.

"However." He turned, coat swinging, and began to walk back toward the wayshrine's glow on the ridge, and the enforcers fell into diamond around him, and his voice came back over his shoulder with the terrible mildness of a man noting a discrepancy. "I find, on reflection, that I have no use for a charter I already know by heart, and little use for a verse my own archives can reconstruct. What I came for, Lena, was never the paper." He stopped at the edge of the shrine-light, and the hum of the gate rose around him like a held breath. "I came to see whether you'd choose. And now I know. You'll choose the song over the ledger, every time. Which means when the time comes—when the choir stands at the seam and needs a keeper's blood freely given—you'll give it. And I will not have the guild's future resting on a child's sentimentality."

"Master," Lena said, and her blood had gone cold, "what did you tell the council?"

He smiled at her then, and it was the smile from the reading room, warm and approving, and it was the worst thing she had ever seen.

"The council," he said, "voted this morning to deliver the choir's location to Malvane. Consider it a controlled transition, Lena. The same one I've been managing all along." The gate-light took him. "You have perhaps two days. Spend them on something other than bookkeeping."

The hum died. The brass dusk came back. And on the road, in the sudden silence, Tam began to shake, and Dain said quietly, "Errenfell. The third attempt is at Errenfell. He knows. *He told them where*—"

Lena was already running for the horses.
