## 18. Prisoner of the Guild

They took his boots first.

Tam hadn't understood why, at the time. He'd crossed through the collapsing wayshrine with Corvane's second ledger strapped against his chest like a second heart, had tumbled out into the coal-yard behind the guildhall half-broken and half-frozen, and before he could so much as stand, three enforcers had him by the arms and a fourth was pulling off his boots with the brisk efficiency of a man undressing a corpse.

It wasn't cruelty, he understood later. It was procedure. Boots had laces; laces could be knotted around throats or used to smuggle notes. The guild thought in procedures the way other people thought in prayers—constantly, and without much love.

Now he sat on a cot in a room with no windows, wearing stockings gone grey at the heels, and listened to the building breathe.

The guildhall breathed differently than it had when he'd lived here. He'd been an apprentice for eleven years; he knew its sounds the way he knew his own pulse. The settling groan of the east wing at dusk. The particular creak of the third stair from the top, which Master Corvane had forbidden anyone to repair because, he said, a stair that announced itself kept honest people honest. The drip in the scullery that no one ever fixed because fixing it would mean admitting the pipe was old, and admitting the pipe was old would mean admitting the whole hall was old, and the guild did not admit things. The guild recorded them.

That was the joke Tam carried around inside himself now, turning it over like a stone too smooth to put down: they had caught him because of what he carried, and they couldn't take it from him, because it wasn't paper anymore. It never really had been. For six weeks he had copied Corvane's private ledger by candle stub, page after page, the ink still warm from the master's own pen, and somewhere in the copying—the dates, the stopped payments, the word *tribute* written again and again in Corvane's cramped hand until it stopped meaning tribute and started meaning theft—all of it had passed out of the book and into him. They could burn the ledger. They had burned pages of it already; he'd watched them curl and blacken in the brazier while he pretended to dust. But the numbers lived in his head now, arranged and cross-referenced, and there was no procedure for searching a mind. Not yet.

He hoped there was never a procedure for that. He suspected, given enough time and enough fear, the guild would invent one.

The door opened on schedule. That was the thing about the guild—even its prisons ran on ledgers. Meals came twice daily, interrogation once, and everything else in between according to a rotation so rigid Tam could have set a clock by it if they'd let him have a clock.

The meal arrived first, as always. A tray slid through the slot at the base of the door: bread, broth, water in a tin cup. The bread was fresh today. He noted that too. Fresh bread meant someone upstairs wanted something from him, and soon.

* * *

The interrogations were conducted by a woman named Hesk, who had joined the enforcers the same year Tam had entered as an apprentice, and who had once—this was the strange part, the part he held onto—shared her supper rolls with him in the kitchen when he'd been nine years old and homesick and crying into the dough. She didn't share anything now. She sat across the small table from him with a lamp between them, and she asked questions in a voice stripped of everything personal, and Tam answered in a voice stripped of everything true.

"Where is Sandoval?"

"I don't know."

"You crossed the seam carrying stolen property. You knew where you were going."

"I was following a rumor."

"Rumors don't strap themselves to apprentices' chests." She leaned forward. The lamplight made hollows of her face. "The ledger, Tam. Where are the pages you copied?"

"In my head," he said. "Which you know. Which is why we keep having this conversation instead of you simply taking them."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a wince, but adjacent to one. She didn't like this either. He'd decided that in the second session. Hesk was good at her work precisely because she hated it, because every question cost her something and the cost made her careful. A man who enjoyed cruelty got sloppy. A woman ashamed of it checked her facts twice.

"The master wants to see you himself," she said. "Tonight."

Tam's stomach dropped, and he made his face do nothing at all. Eleven years of guild training had taught him at least one useful skill: the ledger face. Ink doesn't tremble.

"Tonight," he repeated.

"He's been patient. Patience is expensive." Hesk rose, gathered her papers, then paused at the door with her hand on the frame. She didn't turn around. "You were a good apprentice, Tam. I want you to understand that whatever happens in that room, the master believes—he genuinely believes—that everything he has done, he has done to protect the guild. To protect us. You might remember that. It may matter later."

Then she was gone, and Tam sat alone with the lamplight and let himself shake, finally, all at once, the way a wall lets go in an earthquake—one long shudder and then stillness again.

She believed it. That was the terrible thing. Hesk wasn't lying to comfort herself; she truly believed Corvane was a shield rather than a thief. And the worst part, the part that kept Tam awake in the dark hours, was that Tam understood the belief. He had shared it for eleven years. Corvane had plucked him from a debtors' auction at age seven, had fed him, taught him letters, taught him that a well-kept record was a form of honesty sturdier than any promise. *We do not lie,* Corvane liked to say, tapping the great open ledger in the counting room. *Lying is for people whose books don't balance.*

And all along the master's own book had been chained to his wrist, and its columns had not balanced, could not balance, because the difference between what the guild took and what the guild gave had been growing for forty years like rot in a foundation.

Tam pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw stars.

Fear, he told himself in the dark, was just another entry. Fear was a debit. It went in the left-hand column, and you acknowledged it, dated it, and moved to the next line. You did not pretend it wasn't there. Pretending was how books failed.

He dated his fear. He moved to the next line.

* * *

Corvane came at midnight, which surprised Tam until it didn't. Of course midnight. The guild did nothing meaningful in daylight; daylight was for clerks. Meaning happened by candlelight, in back rooms, in the margins where the real entries lived.

The master dismissed Hesk with a nod and sat down across the table, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Corvane looked older than he had six weeks ago. Or perhaps Tam had simply never looked properly before—never let his eyes rest on the deep lines around the mouth, the thinness of the hair, the way the man's hands lay flat on the table like two dead birds. There was a chain around his right wrist, glinting under the cuff. The empty place where the second ledger used to be.

"You stole from me," Corvane said.

"Yes, Master."

"Don't call me that." It came out fast and tired, and Tam realized with a strange lurch that the words had hurt him. "I'm not your master. I haven't earned the title in some time. Perhaps I never did." He rubbed his eyes. "Do you know why I took you from the auction floor?"

"Because you needed a copyist."

"Because you bit the auctioneer." Something that was almost a smile touched the old man's mouth. "Seven years old, half-starved, and you bit the man holding your collar. I bought you because I thought: here is someone who will not be held. I was right. I've been right about very little lately." He straightened. The softness folded itself away like a letter being sealed. "You read the second ledger. All of it?"

"All of it."

"Then you know why the payments stopped."

"I know what the entries say. Entries aren't reasons."

"No?" Corvane leaned back. "Then give me yours, since you've clearly composed one. Go on. Condemn me. Everyone does eventually—it's practically a guild tradition."

Tam's throat was dry. He drank from the tin cup anyway, slowly, buying time, and found to his surprise that the fear had settled into something usable. Cold. Clear. He set down the cup.

"The tribute wasn't charity," he said. "The impossible trades—the singing stones, the tide-glass, the memory-wax—they weren't luxuries. They were payments on a covenant that kept two realms from grinding each other to dust. The masters stopped paying. Not because they couldn't afford it. Because they wanted what the payments were buying." He met the old man's eyes. "You hoarded the power. Forty years of it, locked in the vaults, while Vael bled and Malvane grew strong on the vacuum. The ledger says the guild chose empire over oath. I don't need to condemn you, Master. Your own handwriting already has."

For a moment Corvane said nothing. The lamp hissed. Somewhere far above them, a door closed—third-floor west, by the sound of it, the archive corridor—and Tam filed the sound away without deciding to. He'd been filing sounds for days now. Guard rotations. Meal times. Which boots rang on stone and which were wrapped in cloth. The night watch changed at the second bell and again before dawn; the change took eleven minutes, and during those eleven minutes the corridor outside his door was held by one man, and that man stood near the stairs, not near the door, because the draft from the broken window made the door position unbearable. Tam knew these things the way he knew multiplication tables. He hadn't planned to learn them. They had simply accumulated, entry by entry, in the ledger of his mind, waiting for a use.

"They called it prudence," Corvane said at last. His voice had gone quiet, almost conversational, the voice he used for teaching. "A hundred years ago the covenant was forged, and the price was paid by nine singers, and everyone agreed it was noble, and everyone quietly hoped no one would ever have to pay it again. Then the payments came due, decade after decade, and each generation of masters looked at the cost and thought: surely there is another way. Surely the covenant can survive a missed payment. Just one. Just this year." He spread his hands. "Prudence, Tam. Missed payment by missed payment by missed payment. Until missing became policy and policy became doctrine and the vaults filled with power we told ourselves we were keeping safe." His jaw tightened. "And then a girl opened a prophecy book and read her own name in it, and the whole edifice began to come down."

"Lena."

"Lena." He said the name carefully, the way a man handles a blade by the flat. "Yes. Do you know what the final verse requires? The verse her grandmother hid in the founding charter—the one you're all so valiantly chasing?"

Tam kept his face still. *Charter.* So they didn't have it yet. Lena had it, or Dain did, or it was hidden still. He logged the relief and gave none of it away.

"I've heard rumors."

"Of course you have." Corvane studied him for a long moment, and then, oddly, he laughed—a short, bitter sound with no air in it. "Look at you. Sitting there with your ledger face. I taught you that. I should feel proud. Instead I feel like a man whose forgery has learned to sign itself." He leaned forward, and the lamp threw his shadow huge up the wall behind him. "Here is the truth, boy, since you value truth so highly. The final verse cannot be sung by voices alone. Nine singers, ten parts—the tenth part is not sung. It is *held*. A keeper must stand in the seam itself, between the realms, and hold the covenant open from the inside. And the price—" he tapped the table, once, hard, "—the price is blood. Freely given. Not spilled, not taken, not bargained for. Given. The keeper must choose it, wholly, knowing what it costs, or the verse fails. That is why we stopped, all those decades ago. Not cowardice. Arithmetic. Every generation of masters did the sums and concluded that no covenant was worth a life freely surrendered. And every generation was wrong, and I am the last wrong one, and I will not permit that girl to walk into the seam and die for a debt my predecessors incurred."

"She wouldn't die for your debt," Tam said. "She'd live for hers. There's a difference. It's in the left-hand column—you'd know that if your books balanced."

The silence that followed was very long. When Corvane finally spoke, his voice had gone flat and cold, the teaching voice packed away.

"You will tell me where she is."

"No."

"You will tell me where the charter is."

"No."

"I could order Hesk to stop being gentle. You've noticed she's gentle. You've counted on it."

"I've noticed," Tam said, and his heart was slamming against his ribs, and his ledger face held, and somewhere beneath both he felt the strangest sensation—not bravery exactly, but its accounting equivalent: the knowledge that he had already paid more than they could take. They could take his body apart line by line and the entries would remain. "But she'll refuse. You know she will. And you know why? Because eleven years ago you bought a boy who bites, and she's the same kind. You collect us, Master. You've never once asked yourself what we're for."

Corvane rose. For one suspended moment Tam thought—hoped, horribly—the old man might strike him. Instead Corvane straightened his cuffs over the empty chain and walked to the door.

"Double the watch on his corridor," he said to the enforcer outside. "No visitors except at scheduled meals." A pause. "And bring him a blanket. The nights are getting cold."

The door closed. Tam sat alone in the lamplight, shaking now, thoroughly and without dignity, and laughing too—a small, cracked, disbelieving sound.

*A blanket.* The man had just threatened him with torture and ordered him a blanket in the same breath. That was Corvane entire: kindness and ruin entered in the same column, dated the same day, never reconciled.

Tam blew out the lamp and lay down on the cot and pulled the blanket over his shoulders, and in the dark he did what he always did. He kept the books.

*Guard change: second bell. Eleven minutes. One guard, north end, near the stairs. Draft from broken window, west corridor—guard avoids door position. Coal-chute in the scullery, bolted from outside, bolt rusted—I oiled it myself two winters past and no one has checked since. Meal trays pass through slot at floor level; slot wide enough for a folded note.*

He lay in the dark adding line after line, debit and credit, fear on the left side and on the right something that had no proper name yet but was beginning, entry by entry, to look like a plan.

And then, near the bottom of the page, he added the last item, the one he had turned over all evening without wanting to look at directly:

*The tenth part requires blood, freely given. The keeper must choose.*

*Master Corvane does not want Lena to pay it. Therefore the master will try to reach the charter first, and close the verse forever—or find another keeper.*

*Find another keeper.*

Tam's eyes opened in the dark.

Because he knew the guild's arithmetic. He knew how its masters thought, had been raised inside their sums. And there was only one person in either realm whose blood the guild could claim was owed—who had been born into the covenant's service, bound by apprenticeship, sworn by charter, and who was currently sitting in a cell two floors above the vaults, writing plans in his head.

Not a singer. Never a singer. But a keeper's blood was perhaps not the same as a singer's voice, and the guild had forty years of practice telling itself that a technicality was the same as a truth.

"Oh," Tam said softly, to the ceiling, to the dark, to the rusted bolt on the coal-chute. "Oh, you clever, terrible old man."

He rolled onto his side and began, methodically, to memorize the exact weight of the door's hinges, the precise interval between the guard's footsteps, and the number of steps from his cell to the scullery—eleven years of ledgers suddenly converging toward a single entry, the boldest one he had ever contemplated:

*Tam, apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things, escaped this cell on the night of—*

He didn't know the date yet. But he would. The guild had taught him that much, at least: everything balances, eventually. Everything gets its date.
