## 19. The Second Singer

The mountain town of Karrow's Rest smelled of pine smoke and sour ale, and it clung to the mountainside the way a drunk clings to a fencepost—stubbornly, and with no dignity whatsoever.

Dain Ash had walked the last three days of the road alone. It was not sentiment that made him do it, whatever Lena thought when she caught him slipping away before dawn with his pack already shouldered. It was arithmetic. Bellhollow had burned because riders had followed him, patient as wolves, and he had not looked over his shoulder often enough. He would not make that mistake twice. So he had taken the high goat-paths where no rider could follow, and he had left the others to the slower valley road, and every mile he had counted the ways he could have done better and found the count always came up short.

That was the arithmetic of guilt. It never balanced.

Karrow's Rest sat in a saddle of the mountains where the air itself seemed thinner, as though the world up here had been stretched too far over the bones beneath. The town had one street, two taverns, and a bell tower that had not rung in eleven years. Dain knew this because he had made it his business to know. He had spent the better part of a season hunting rumors of singers, and rumors of singers, he had learned, came in two varieties: the kind that led to a farmwife whose lullabies made cream rise faster, and the kind that led to a man sitting alone in a tavern with a bell-rope's callus still on his hands, drinking himself deaf.

The second kind was rarer. The second kind was what they needed.

He found Orlin Vess in the second tavern, which was also the worse one—a low-ceilinged room called the Drowned Lamb, where the floorboards wept in wet weather and the lamplight had given up trying. The man at the corner table sat with his back to the wall, which was a soldier's habit, not a drunk's, and his hands were wrapped around a clay mug the way a drowning man wraps around driftwood.

He was younger than Dain had expected. That was the first wrongness. In Dain's imagining, Orlin Vess had become an old man, gray and sodden, a ruin with a history. The man in the corner was perhaps forty, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had spent their lives hauling on ropes, with forearms corded like tree roots. His hair was dark going iron at the temples. His eyes, when they lifted and found Dain standing in the doorway, were the eyes of a man who had already looked at everything the world had to show him and found it wanting.

They were also, Dain noted with a cold twist in his gut, red-rimmed and unfocused. The deafness was not a rumor.

"You're blocking the light," Orlin said. His voice was a ruin of its own—gravel dragged over gravel. He spoke loudly, the way the deaf do, each word placed with a hammer's care. "And I don't buy drinks for strangers. And I don't care what you're selling."

"I'm not selling." Dain crossed the room and sat down across from him without asking, which earned him a flat stare and nothing more. Up close, the man smelled of ale and woodsmoke and, beneath it, faintly, of bronze—the particular smell of a bell foundry, which clings to a man the way a grave-cliff clings to a shroud. "I'm here about the bells of Saint Erren's."

Orlin's mug stopped halfway to his mouth.

For a long moment the tavern noise went on around them—the clink of mugs, the drone of a fiddler who only knew four songs, the wet cough of the man tending bar—and then Orlin set the mug down with a click that Dain felt more than heard.

"Nobody knows about Saint Erren's," Orlin said. Quieter now. The hammer gone out of his voice. "I made sure of it. I made very sure of it."

"I know about a lot of things." Dain leaned forward. "I know that eleven years ago, on the feast of the Turning, the great bell of Saint Erren's rang the hour wrong. Rang it backward, some said. Rang a warning, others said. And I know that the town council called it a malfunction, and the ringer called it a malfunction, and then the ringer drank his way out of a job and into this chair."

"I called it a malfunction because it *was* a malfunction."

"It wasn't."

Orlin's jaw worked. His hands, wrapped around the mug, had gone white at the knuckles. "Who sent you? The council? After eleven years? They can have their apology in writing, same as I offered, same as they ignored—"

"No one sent me." Dain held the man's gaze and made himself stay in it, though the eyes looking back at him were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of a long fall. "I'm here because the bells of Saint Erren's weren't malfunctioning. They were remembering. There was a time—before your time, before mine—when the bells of this range were tuned to something older than any saint. And eleven years ago, on the feast of the Turning, something on the other side of the sky leaned against the seam of the world, and your bell heard it, and your hands knew what to do before your mind did. You rang a warning, Orlin Vess. You rang it true. And then you spent eleven years apologizing for it."

The silence that followed was total. Even the fiddler, by some mercy of the gods or simple drunkenness, had stumbled into a pause.

Orlin Vess looked at Dain Ash for a long, long time. Then he laughed—a single bark, bitter as gall.

"You're mad," he said. "You're one of those mad pilgrims. We get them every spring. They come up the mountain looking for the singing hermit, the holy fool, the—no. No." His face changed. Something moved behind it, something old and heavy turning over. "You said *seam*. You said the *seam* of the world. The pilgrims don't say that. The pilgrims talk about saints."

"I'm not a pilgrim."

"Then what are you?"

Dain had rehearsed this part on the goat-paths. He had composed speeches, elegant ones, persuasive ones, the kind of speeches that had once come easily to him—back when he had used words the way a cutpurse uses a knife, to open pockets and close mouths. He opened his mouth to deliver one now.

And found he couldn't.

Because Lena's voice was in his head, from three nights ago, from the fire on the valley road, when she had asked him—not accusing, which would have been easier—simply asked: *How do you get a man to give up the last thing he has?* And he had started to give her the old answer, the smooth answer, the answer of a man who had once talked an eighth singer out of hiding and into a guild cart and a one-way road, and she had looked at him with those ledger-keeper's eyes that weighed everything and forgave almost nothing, and she had said: *No. Try it honestly. See if honesty works. You've never tried it.*

So now he tried it.

"My name is Dain Ash," he said. "Eleven years ago, I was the man the Guild of Impossible Things sent to find the eighth singer. Her name was Ysolde Marr. She was a weaver in the lowlands who could put weather into cloth. The guild had decided the covenant payments were over—decided it quietly, in a room I never saw, for reasons I was never told—and my job was to find the scattered singers and mark them. Not recruit them. *Mark* them. So that when the guild's hunters came, they would know where to knock." His voice was steady. It frightened him how steady it was. "I found Ysolde. She trusted me. I sat at her table and ate her bread and told her I was a traveler collecting songs, and three weeks later the hunters came, and she ran, and they ran her down at the ford at Callowmere. She drowned. Or they drowned her. The reports disagreed, and I stopped asking after the second report."

Orlin had gone very still.

"Four months ago," Dain went on, "I led a courier's route through Bellhollow, and riders followed me in, and Bellhollow burned. The archive-city. Ten thousand years of song, and I brought the torch to its door by being too proud to check my back-trail." He laughed, short and ugly. "So you should understand something, Orlin Vess. I am not here to tell you that you're the hero of a prophecy. I'm not here to flatter you. I'm the man who broke the last choir, or helped break it, or stood by while it broke and took his pay. I'm here because I don't get to be clean. None of us do. But the seam is failing, and a man named Malvane is going to nail it shut with himself as the hinge, and the only thing that stops him is a choir, and the choir needs a ringer, and you—" he pointed, and his finger was not quite steady, "—you are the only man in three hundred miles whose hands remember the old tunings. So here I am. Asking. Honestly. With all my rot showing. That's what I am. That's what I've done. Decide what I'm worth to you."

The tavern noise crept back in around the edges of the silence, tentative, like water finding its level.

Orlin Vess looked down at his mug. When he spoke, his voice had dropped to almost nothing, and Dain had to lean in to catch it.

"Eleven years ago," Orlin said, "I rang the warning, and I knew—I *knew*, the way you know a bell's true note in your teeth—that something was coming. And the council called me drunk. And my wife called me drunk. And my own master, old Fenwick, who taught me the ropes, stood in the tower doorway and looked at me with pity, and I thought: well. Maybe I am drunk. Maybe it's finally showing." He turned the mug in his hands, around and around. "So I did the kindest thing anyone could do. I agreed with them. I drank to match the story. Eleven years, I drank to match the story, because a story where you're a drunk is easier than a story where you heard the world crack and everyone you loved called you a liar." He looked up. His eyes were wet and furious. "My wife left. My daughter writes twice a year, from the lowlands, polite as a tax notice. And you sit there and tell me the bell was right."

"The bell was right."

"Then where," Orlin said, and his voice cracked like a rope parting under load, "where was I supposed to put eleven years? Where do I put them now? You can't hand a man back a decade. You can't pour it back in the bottle."

"No," Dain said quietly. "You can't. I've looked for the trick of it for eleven years myself. There isn't one."

* * *

Lena and Tam found them at dusk, on the bench outside the Drowned Lamb, because Orlin had refused to discuss anything further indoors—*walls have ears and mine have been drinking with the council for a decade*, he said—and because, Dain suspected, the man did not entirely trust himself to be sober and sensible in the same room as a decision.

Lena came up the street with the covenant score wrapped in oilcloth against the mountain damp, and Dain watched Orlin watch her, and saw the moment the drunk's slouch fell away from the man's shoulders. It happened the way a bell's hum dies: not all at once, but in a long diminishing that leaves the air changed.

"You're the ledger-keeper," Orlin said. Not a question. "The one the old book named."

"I'm the one the old book named," Lena agreed. She sat down on the bench's far end, unhurried, and set the oilcloth bundle on her knees. "Dain told you what he is. He's better at it than he thinks. Now I'll tell you what I am, because you've earned the same honesty he got." And she did—she told him about the ledger and the date written a century before she was born, about the gate that sang her name, about the wayshrine that had refused her, about Maren's tide-song at Errenfell and the memory of a drowned son paid out like coin. She told it plainly, without ornament, the way a ledger is written, and Dain, who had heard the story before, found his throat tight anyway.

When she finished, Orlin was silent for a long time.

"What's the price?" he said at last. "For the ringing. For the singing. Whatever it is you want from me. Maren paid a memory. What do I pay?"

And here—Dain had seen her do this before, at the fisherwoman's hearth, and it had struck him then and struck him now—Lena did not soften it. She did not glance away. She did not reach for the gentle arithmetic that most people reach for, the *it might not be so bad*, the *perhaps you'll lose something small*.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the honest answer. Maren lost her son—not the fact of him, but the remembering of him; she says she knows she loved him the way you know a sum is correct without being able to show the work. The covenant takes what it needs, and it doesn't itemize in advance. It might be a memory. It might be several. If we fail, it might be everything, and the seam fails with us, and Malvane's crown decides what the world remembers." She held his eyes. "There may be blood too. The old verse hints at it. A keeper's blood, freely given. I may be the one who pays that. But none of us pays nothing, Orlin. I won't pretend otherwise. Every promise that pretended otherwise is how the guild got away with what it did."

The wind came down the street from the heights, smelling of snow that hadn't fallen yet. Somewhere above the town, in the dark, the silent bell tower stood against the last of the light like a headstone.

Orlin Vess looked at his hands. He turned them over, palms up, and Dain saw the calluses there—rope callus, yes, but beneath it, older and harder, the ridged pads of a man who had spent ten thousand hours at a braided bell-rope, feeding a bronze voice into the sky.

"Eleven years," Orlin said, "I thought the bell rang wrong. You're telling me it rang right, and the world was wrong instead." He closed his hands. "Then here's my price, ledger-keeper, and it's not a memory and it's not blood. My price is that when this is done—win or lose—you write it down. In your ledger, in your covenant book, wherever the true accounts go. You write that Orlin Vess of Karrow's Rest rang the warning on the feast of the Turning, eleven years before anyone believed him, and that the bell was right and the man was sober, and that his wife and his daughter can read it if they ever want to. I don't need them to come back. I need it to be *written*."

Lena was quiet a moment. Then she drew out the small traveling ledger she carried now the way she had once carried the guild's great book, and she opened it, and she wrote, right there on the bench in the failing light, her pen scratching steady as a heart. Dain caught the words upside down and they burned in him like a brand:

*Orlin Vess, bell-ringer. Rang true when no one listened. The record is corrected.*

"There," she said. "Witnessed and entered."

Orlin read it twice. His eyes did something complicated. Then he stood, and it was remarkable, Dain thought, how a man who had spent eleven years folded into a corner chair could unfold into something that filled the whole street—broad and deliberate, a man built for towers.

"Then I'll need a week," Orlin said. "The bells of Saint Erren's aren't rung by one rope anymore, and the old mechanism's been rusting since they locked the tower. If you want the old tunings, I want the tower. I want to pull the cloths off the bells and see what eleven years of neglect has cost." He looked at Dain, and there was something in his face that was not forgiveness and not trust but was perhaps the raw material of both. "And I want to know why a man who broke the last choir thinks he gets to stand in the tower with me."

"Because you'll want someone to check your knots," Dain said. "And because I'm the only one here who knows how it feels to ring something true and have the whole world call it a lie. I figure we can hold the rope together."

Orlin held the look a long moment. Then, abruptly, he laughed—the first real laugh, Dain guessed, in eleven years, rusty and too loud and slightly frightening to the passersby—and clapped Dain on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him.

"Come on, then, liar. You can buy the drinks. I'm told I've got a reputation to live down to."

* * *

They worked through the week, and Dain learned what it was to serve a bell.

He had imagined, before, that bell-ringing was strength. He learned it was listening—that Orlin's hands read the rope the way Lena read a ledger, that every bell in Saint Erren's tower had a name and a temperament and a grievance, that the tenor bell, *Old Merrow*, had a crack the length of a knife-scar that Orlin's fingers found in the dark and his face, in the lantern light, grieved over like a wound in a friend.

"She can still speak," Orlin murmured, palm flat against the cold bronze. "Cracked bells speak *truer*, my master used to say. They can't lie anymore. All they've got left is the truth." He looked up at Dain, and the lantern threw his shadow huge against the tower's stone throat. "That's us, isn't it. That's the whole bloody choir. Cracked bells. That's why it has to be us."

Dain didn't answer. He was looking at the tower's high window, where the first stars of evening were coming out over the mountains—and below them, far down in the valley to the south, where the road from the free cities climbed toward Karrow's Rest, he saw something that stopped his breath.

Lights. Many lights. Moving in order. Not the scattered lamps of a night caravan but a column, disciplined and long, winding up the mountain road with the patience of something that knew exactly where it was going.

He counted, the way you count when counting is the last thing left. Then he went to the tower stair and called down, quietly, so as not to wake the town, for Lena.

She came up into the lantern light with sleep still in her face, and he pointed, and he watched her go still the way water goes still before it freezes.

"That's not the council's road-watch," she said.

"No."

"That's not a caravan at all."

"No," Dain said again, and found that the arithmetic of guilt had come back around, patient as ever, to collect. "That's riders. That's a column of riders, and they're not following me this time." He turned from the window and met her eyes. "They're following the bell. Lena—they heard the tower. Tonight, when Orlin tested the tenor bell. One note, half a minute. And something down in the dark heard it and *knew*."

Below them, in the sleeping town, the great cracked bell of Saint Erren's hung silent in the dark, holding its one true note like a held breath—like a warning, rung eleven years too early, that the world was only now beginning to hear.
