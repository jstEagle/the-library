## 21. What Malvane Lost

The crown was heavy before it was ever made.

That was the thing no one understood about Malvane—no one who had not stood in the high tower of Vaskarren at dusk and watched him take it off. They saw it gleaming on his brow in the war-camps and the conquered halls, a circlet of black iron set with shards of seam-glass that caught the light like frozen lightning, and they thought: *there is a man who wants to be king.* They did not see him at night, alone, setting it down on the table beside his bed with both hands, carefully, the way a man sets down something broken that he cannot bring himself to throw away. They did not see that he slept facing away from it.

He had wanted to be many things. A king had never been one of them.

* * *

The war-room of Vaskarren had been a chapel once, back when the citadel belonged to the singers' order, and Malvane had never permitted the change to be completed. The altar still stood at the room's heart—a slab of pale seamstone worn smooth by two centuries of hands—and upon it now lay maps instead of hymnals, weighted at the corners with daggers and candlesticks and, once, with a tuning fork of silvered bone that he kept there for reasons he did not examine.

He stood over the maps while his generals argued. He had learned long ago to let them argue; men who were arguing were not watching him, and there were things on his face he preferred unwitnessed.

"The vanguard reaches the border-stones within the week," said General Hessa, a broad woman with a burn-scarred jaw and a voice like gravel poured slow. She traced the line of the seam with one blunt finger. "The free cities have put ships in the water. Fishing fleets, mostly, armed in haste. They will break."

"They held at Errenfell," said the younger one, Coris, whose family had farmed the twilight meadows before the meadows began to die. "They held longer than they should have."

"They held because their keeper is learning," Hessa said, and her eyes flicked up—briefly, involuntarily—to the man at the head of the table.

Malvane said nothing. The candle flames leaned in a draft that came from nowhere, from the seam itself perhaps, or from the memory of the room's old purpose. On the map, the border-stones were marked in ink so old it had gone brown, and beyond them, in the cartographer's careful hand, the words *HERE THE SONG HOLDS* — a phrase no living scribe would write anymore, because no one believed it anymore, least of all the man who had once written it himself, in a different ink, in a different life.

"Send the riders to Bellhollow's ashes," he said at last. "Confirm what the scouts reported. And ready the fleet."

"For the cities?" Coris asked.

"No." Malvane's finger came down on the map, west of everything, where a small building was drawn with more care than it deserved—a guildhall in a gray city across the seam, rendered in miniature as if the cartographer had loved it. "For the guildhall."

Hessa frowned. "The guild is finished. Their payments stopped before either of us drew breath. Whatever power they hoarded—"

"Is in their vaults," Malvane said. "And in their charter." He lifted his hand from the map. Where his finger had rested, the candlelight seemed dimmer, as though the paper remembered being touched by something that had once been able to make light itself. "I want the founding charter brought to me intact. The vaults opened. Every ledger, every sealed page, every scrap of tribute-song they have hoarded for a hundred years while my country starved of its own voice."

"And if the guildmasters resist?"

Malvane looked at her then, fully, and Hessa—who had faced drakes at the drowned shrine and the screaming hosts of the seam-wraiths without blinking—looked away first.

"They will not resist," he said quietly. "Men who steal by ledger do not know how to fight. Bring me the charter."

* * *

Afterward, when the generals had gone and the candles had burned down to stubs, he remained in the chapel that was no longer a chapel, and he allowed himself the thing he allowed himself only here, in this room, where the stones still remembered singing.

He took off the crown.

It came away with a faint reluctance, the seam-glass shards warm against his temples, and he set it on the altar—the altar, of all places, though he told himself it was simply the flattest surface—and he pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until the darkness behind them bloomed red.

Her name had been Sera Vaelin. No—that was the name she had taken when she crossed over, the name on the guild's rolls, the name carved into the ninth niche of the mural wall in Bellhollow before someone scratched it out. Before *he* scratched it out, three years ago, with his own thumbnail, standing in the burning archive-city while his riders carried out armloads of books to feed the fires. He had done it in rage. That was the excuse he gave himself. Rage was clean; rage could be worn like armor. But the truth was that he had done it because seeing her name hurt, and he had spent forty years learning to make hurting things stop existing.

Before Sera Vaelin she had been Master Sera, of the Ninth Chair, and he had been a boy of eleven with a voice like a cracked bell and nothing else in the world, brought to the singers' order by a mother who could no longer feed him, and she had knelt down in the snow of the courtyard—she had *knelt*, a master of the order, in front of a starving child—and said, *Well. Let us see what you have.*

What he had was grief. She taught him to sing with it.

Those were the years he did not let himself remember, and those were the years that came for him anyway, every night, in the space between taking off the crown and sleeping. The practice hall with its nine niches and its floor of resonant stone. The way the covenant felt when all nine voices rose together—not a sound, exactly, but a *seam*, a place where two worlds pressed against each other and, instead of grinding, *harmonized*. He had been too young to sing the covenant itself; the children sang the under-harmonies, the ground-bass of the great working, and even that had been enough to change him forever. To stand inside that music was to stand inside the knowledge that the world was not one thing but two, leaning together, and that the leaning was love.

Then the payments stopped coming from the other side. Then the guild went silent. Then the masters met behind closed doors, and Sera came out of that meeting white as bone, and packed a single bag.

"You're leaving." He had been seventeen. He had followed her down through the shrine-tunnels to the crossing-place, the old wayshrine beneath the roots of the world, and she had turned around with her hand already through the gate, already half elsewhere, and looked at him with an expression he had spent four decades trying to decode. Not guilt. Worse than guilt. *Pity*, maybe. Or the particular sorrow of a teacher who has seen the end of a story and cannot say it aloud.

"The covenant is dying, Aldric," she had said—his true name, the name no one used anymore, the name that lived now only in this room, in this hour, in the dark behind his hands. "The other side broke faith. There is nothing left to hold."

"Then we hold it ourselves. We've always—we can carry it, the nine of us, we don't need their tribute, we can—"

"Aldric." His name again, soft, final. "Go home. Forget the order. Forget me. It's kinder."

And she stepped through, and the gate closed, and the seam began to die, and he had stood in the dark of the wayshrine until his legs gave out, listening to the silence where the harmony had been.

Kinder. She had called it kinder.

* * *

He slept badly, as always, and woke before dawn with the taste of ash in his mouth, which was either memory or the actual ash of Bellhollow still drifting on the wind three hundred miles east. In the gray light he dressed without help—he refused help for dressing, one of the few vanities he permitted himself—and put on the crown, and went down to the morning reports.

The reports were waiting on the altar-table in the war-room, and atop them, sealed with black wax, lay a dispatch from the eastern scouts that made him stop with his hand still on the seal.

The keeper had survived Errenfell. The keeper had pulled three books from the fire at Bellhollow. And the scouts' informants in the free cities—one of the councilors, bought cheap, as councilors always were—reported that the keeper was gathering singers.

Malvane read the dispatch twice. Then he sat down slowly on the general's chair, which he never used, and read it a third time.

*A keeper who chooses.* The prophecy was older than he was, older than the guild's silence, scrawled in the margins of a score-book that had passed through a dozen hands and been banned by three governments. He had dismissed it for years as the fever-dream of frightened archivists. But the words had a shape he knew, the way you know a melody you heard in childhood even after you forget every note: *when the seam frays, a keeper shall come from the far shore, and the choir shall rise again, nine voices and a tenth, and the tenth shall be a keeper who chooses.*

Nine voices and a tenth.

His hands were not steady. He watched them, distantly, the way he watched everything about himself these days—as if from across a widening room—and he thought: *she found the mural. She knows about the ninth chair. She is looking for what I burned.*

He should have burned more. He should have burned everything, every score, every ledger, every scrap of the old working, should have salted the earth of every archive in Vael. Instead he had kept one thing, hidden even from his own generals, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a chest in his private chambers: a single page in a young woman's handwriting, forty years old, a page of exercises and marginal notes in a neat, patient script. *Again, Aldric. From the top. Your grief is not your enemy—it is your lowest string. Learn to play it.*

He had taken it from her rooms after she fled. It was the only theft of his entire career that shamed him.

"General Hessa," he said, when she appeared at the war-room door with the morning's second dispatch. His voice was level. It was always level. Levelness was the first thing Sera had taught him and the last thing he had left of her. "Change of orders. The fleet sails for the guildhall—but I want a rider sent ahead. A herald, not soldiers."

Hessa's scarred eyebrow rose. "An offer? For the guild?"

"An offer for the keeper." He stood, and the seam-glass shards caught the early light and threw small cold rainbows across the map, across the brown-inked words *HERE THE SONG HOLDS*. "She is gathering voices to reforge the covenant. Let her gather them. Let her sing herself hollow. And when she stands at the seam with nothing left—" he touched the crown, lightly, the way you touch a wound to check whether it still hurts, "—I will be there, and I will show her what her grandmother's covenant actually cost. What it cost *us*. Then she may choose, as the prophecy says. Between my door and no door at all."

"You speak as though you want her to refuse," Hessa said.

Malvane looked at the map a long moment—at the little guildhall drawn with such care, at the border-stones, at the whole thin bright seam of the world where two shores leaned together in a harmony that had abandoned him in a tunnel when he was seventeen years old.

"I want her to understand," he said, "that abandonment is also a choice."

* * *

That night he did not take off the crown.

He sat in his chambers with the oilcloth bundle unopened on his knees and the seam-glass humming faintly against his skull—a sound like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, a sound that had once been music and was now only pressure—and he thought about the girl from the ledgers. Lena Sandoval. Gatekeeper, ledger-keeper, raised by the guild that had murdered a world with a bookkeeper's pen. He had imagined her, in the months since Errenfell, as a bureaucrat with a sword, some gray functionary stumbling into a war she couldn't read. But the reports said otherwise now. The reports said she had saved books from a fire. The reports said singers were answering her call.

The reports said she was doing what Sera would have done.

He almost laughed. The sound that came out was closer to the cracked bell he had been at eleven years old.

"She would have liked you," he said aloud, to the empty room, to the wrapped page on his knees, to the woman forty years dead to him and alive somewhere on the far side of the seam or not alive at all, no one would tell him, no one dared. "That is the cruelest thing about you, keeper. You are exactly what she would have loved."

Outside, the wind off the dying meadows moaned through the towers of Vaskarren, and in the war-room below, the candles guttered in a draft from nowhere, and on the altar where hymnals used to lie, the map waited with its small careful drawing of a guildhall—and beneath the map, locked in an iron case that traveled everywhere he traveled, a single sheet of old music-paper bore, in a young woman's patient hand, the words:

*Again, Aldric. From the top.*

At dawn, the herald rode west, carrying terms of surrender written in the usurper's own hand—and, folded small inside his coat where no soldier would think to look, a lock of silver hair and a message Malvane had spent forty years refusing to write and had finally, at midnight, unable to sleep, written anyway:

*You are looking for the ninth voice. I am the last one who remembers it. Come and hear what she left behind—or burn as she burned. Either way, keeper, we will meet at the seam.*

He sealed it with black wax, and gave it to the rider, and watched until the horse was a speck and the speck was nothing—and then, alone on the tower with the crown humming its glassy pressure-song, Aldric Malvane, student of the Ninth Chair, last singer of a murdered harmony, finally wept, briefly and silently, the way a man weeps when he has trained himself out of it and can no longer afford the luxury.

By full light his face was dry, and the orders were signed, and the fleet was moving.

The charter would be his. The vaults would be opened. And the keeper who chose would learn, soon enough, exactly what it was that he had lost.
