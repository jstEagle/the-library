## 22. Rescue at the Guildhall

The coal-chutes still smelled of soot and childhood.

Lena crouched in the shadow of the guild's eastern wall, where the delivery ramp sloped down into darkness, and pressed her palm against the cold iron of the chute-gate. Ten years since she had last squeezed through this gap—ten years since she'd been a scrawny apprentice smuggling honey-buns past the night wardens, laughing with Tam about nothing, certain the world was a ledger that always balanced if you kept your columns straight.

Now she was breaking into her own home.

*Not your home,* she corrected herself, and the thought hurt worse than any blade. *A fortress that raised you on lies. A vault of stolen power wearing the face of family.*

Above her, the guildhall rose against the grey pre-dawn sky like a fist closed around something precious. Windows glowed amber on the upper floors—Corvane's study among them, she knew without looking, because Master Corvane burned midnight oil the way other men burned heretics. Somewhere inside those walls, Tam sat in a cell for the crime of carrying truth across realms. Somewhere inside those walls, the founding charter lay locked away, its hidden verse waiting to be read.

Somewhere inside those walls, the man who had taught her to hold a quill straight was waiting too.

She pulled the hood lower and went down the ramp.

* * *

The chutes were exactly as memory had drawn them: three iron throats angling from the coal-yard into the cellar levels, wide enough for a child, narrow enough that an adult had to go sideways, breathing in dust and old fire. Lena went in feet-first, arms crossed over her chest, letting gravity take her the way she had a hundred times before—and the way the dark swallowed her felt less like descent than like being unmade and remade, layer by layer, as each familiar stone scraped past.

At the bottom she landed in soft ash, knee-deep, and stood listening.

The cellar of the Guild of Impossible Things breathed at night. She had forgotten that—the way the pipes ticked as they cooled, the way the great furnace exhaled through vents shaped like open mouths, the way somewhere above, always, the ledgers rustled in their cabinets as though the paper itself were dreaming. As a girl she had found it comforting. The guild was alive; the guild would keep her.

Now every sound sounded like counting. Like accounts being tallied in the dark, debts accruing, interest compounding on a century of theft.

She climbed out of the ash-pit and moved along the wall to the maintenance stair, keeping to the shadows between the vent-light. Two enforcers passed above her on the landing, boots ringing, spears shouldered. She counted their steps out of habit—*fourteen to the corner, pause, fourteen back*—and realized with a cold start that she was counting them the way Corvane had taught her to count inventory. He was in her bones. That was the thing no one warned you about betrayal: the betrayer lived in you still, speaking in your own voice, and you could not cut him out without cutting yourself.

*Then don't cut him out,* said a harder voice, Dain's voice, the one she'd been carrying since Bellhollow burned. *Use him.*

She used him. Fourteen steps, pause, fourteen back. She slipped up the stair between rotations, ghosting along the corridor toward the holding cells below the scriptorium, where the guild kept things it did not want to look at directly.

* * *

Tam was not in a cell.

Tam was in a *room*—that was somehow worse. When Lena eased the door open with the warden's keys she'd lifted from a sleeping guard, she found her oldest friend sitting upright on a narrow bed, hands folded, hair combed, a tray of untouched food on the desk beside a carafe of water. The window was real glass. There were fresh flowers in a cup.

"They're trying to make it look like hospitality," Tam whispered, and then Lena was through the door and Tam was on her feet and they were holding each other in the middle of that terrible polite room, and Tam's whole body was shaking. "They didn't hurt me. They just—they keep asking me questions. About the ledger. About what I copied. And they bring flowers, Lena. Every morning someone brings *flowers*, like I'm a guest."

"You are a prisoner."

"I know." Tam's laugh cracked in the middle. "That's what makes it obscene. Come on—there's a guard change at the half-hour, and Corvane takes his walk at dawn. We have maybe forty minutes before this building remembers how to be a fortress instead of a hostess."

"How do you know his schedule?"

"Because I've been memorizing everything," Tam said, and there was something new in her friend's face—something forged rather than grown. "Every rotation, every lock, every time he checks the vault seals. They think they're interrogating me. I'm doing arithmetic." Tam held up her wrists, where faint red rings showed the ghost of chains. "I counted my way out of these. I can count us both out of here."

For one absurd moment Lena wanted to laugh—to stand in this room with its hostage flowers and laugh until the walls came down. Instead she gripped Tam's shoulder once, hard, the way the guild taught you to seal a promise, and said, "The charter first. Then the wayshrine corridor. Then we run."

"The charter's in the deep vault. Under the scriptorium. Third seal is blood-locked—yours or mine will work, we're still on the rolls." Tam paused. "Lena. There's something else. Something I have to tell you before we go down there, because if we die tonight you should know it, and if we live you'll need it."

"Tell me."

"Corvane has been burning pages. From the second ledger. Every night, after the halls empty. But he burns them *slowly*, and he reads them aloud while he does—like a funeral rite, like he owes the words that much. And two nights ago, before they moved me down here, I heard him say—" Tam's voice dropped to almost nothing. "He said, *'the ninth voice was never lost. It was spent.'* And then he looked at the charter cabinet for a long time. Lena—he knows what's in it. Maybe he's always known."

Lena stood very still. Outside the room's small window, the sky was going from black to bruise.

"Then we'd better get there before his conscience finishes dying," she said.

* * *

The deep vault lay beneath the scriptorium, behind three doors that had guarded the guild's secrets since before Lena was born. The first opened to Tam's stolen key. The second required a spoken phrase—*what the guild trades, the guild keeps*—and hearing her own voice say the words made Lena's stomach turn, because she finally understood them truly now. What the guild traded was Vael. What the guild kept was everything else.

The third door wanted blood.

Tam offered her hand first, but Lena shook her head. "Mine," she said. "If anyone's name is going to open this door, let it be the name in the prophecy book." She drew her belt-knife—a courier's blade, Dain's gift, honest steel from the other shore—and opened her palm along the old callus line where a ledger-keeper's pen rests.

The door drank. The locks turned like a slow exhale. And the founding charter lay before them in its case of lead and glass, exactly where it had rested for a century, patient as a buried seed.

Lena lifted it out with both hands. It was lighter than she expected. Lighter than a century of lies should have been.

Under the glass case, scratched into the shelf beneath where the charter had lain, someone had carved a single word long ago: *forgive*. The letters were old, half-worn, and Lena stared at them for three heartbeats before understanding crawled up her spine.

Grandmother's handwriting. The ninth singer had knelt in this vault, hidden her verse inside the charter, and left a plea to whoever found it.

"She knew someone would come," Lena whispered.

"Then let's not disappoint her," said a voice from the doorway.

Master Corvane stood in the frame of the third door, alone, unarmored, his grey robe belted simply, his hands empty at his sides. Behind him, far down the corridor, enforcers waited—but he had come ahead of them, or dismissed them, or perhaps simply outrun his own soldiers the way grief outruns everything.

In his hand he carried a sheaf of half-burned pages. The second ledger's remains.

"Lena," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded the way it had sounded a thousand times across a thousand lessons: warm, measured, certain. "Put it down."

* * *

"No," Lena said.

It came out steadier than she felt. She shifted so the charter was behind her hip, and put her free hand on the knife at her belt, and watched the man who had raised her watch her right back.

"You've crossed over with enforcers," she said. "You've seized my friend. You've burned evidence for months. And now you stand there with your hands empty like a schoolmaster catching apprentices out of bed. Say whatever you've come to say, Master Corvane. The dawn's coming, and I'm leaving with the charter either way."

"Are you?" He stepped into the vault—not toward her, but toward the shelf, toward the carved word, and his eyes touched *forgive* and flinched away from it. "Do you know what that charter is, child? Not the verse inside it. The object itself. It is the only remaining proof of the covenant's original terms—which means it is the only thing standing between the free cities and a war they cannot win. Malvane wants it. His crown is incomplete without it. Every day it sits in your pack, you carry a lit fuse through a powder store."

"So give it to the choir. Let us finish the reforging, and there'll be nothing left to steal."

"And if you fail?" For the first time his voice roughened. "If you fail, the verse dies with you, the covenant dies unfinished, and Malvane sings alone over the ashes of two worlds. I have read the mathematics of this, Lena. I wrote half of them myself. Your way ends in ruin nine times in ten."

"And yours ended in ruin ten times in ten," she said. "You stopped the payments. You hoarded the tribute. You built a vault full of power while Vael starved and bled, and when the prophecy finally named a keeper, you tried to erase her instead of answer her. Don't talk to me about mathematics. Talk to me about Maren. Talk to me about Bellhollow."

"Bellhollow was Malvane."

"Bellhollow was *possible* because of you." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that, and let it crack anyway. "You made the vacuum he grew in. You made me. You made Tam. Everything loyal in this building, you bent toward the hoard."

Corvane was quiet for a long moment. In the silence, Lena became aware of how close they stood—three paces, maybe four, in a stone room built to hold impossible things. Close enough for a thrown knife. Close enough for him to lunge. Both of them knew it. Neither of them moved.

"I trained you to balance accounts," he said at last, softly. "So balance this one. Kill me, take the charter, run. My enforcers will hunt you to the seam and beyond, but perhaps you'll make it, and perhaps the song will hold, and perhaps the world forgives us both. Or put the charter down, come home, and I will bury the prophecy book so deep no one ever reads your name in it again. You'll be master of this guild within the decade. You'll have your life back. Every life you were promised."

"That's not a balance. That's a bribe."

"It's the only currency I have left." And then—she would remember this all her life, the way the lamplight caught it—his shoulders came down. Not surrender. Just weight, finally set down. "I was the ninth singer's student too, Lena. Did Tam's ledger tell you that? No—no, of course not, that page burned years ago. I loved her the way you love a teacher. And when she fled, when she abandoned us to the consequences of our own covenant, I decided the lesson she'd really taught was that oaths break, and the only sin is being the one who breaks last. So I broke first. I broke for fifty years. And every year it cost more to stop than to continue, until stopping was unthinkable, and here we are." He spread his empty hands. "Here I am. An old man guarding a vault from a girl I taught to write."

Lena's hand was shaking on the knife.

She could feel the whole shape of it hanging in the air between them: the throw, the arc, the easy kill. He was unarmed. He was old. He was *right there*. And somewhere above them dawn was coming, and somewhere beyond the walls Malvane's armies were marching, and every rational column in her body said *do it, end it, remove the variable*—

Dain would have done it. She understood that now without bitterness. Dain carried dead singers on his back and did the arithmetic of survival with blood on his hands, and it had nearly destroyed him, and forgiveness had been a decision he renewed daily because the alternative was drowning.

She was not Dain. She was the keeper of the ledger. And a keeper's first law, older than the guild, older than the covenant, was written on the first page of every book she'd ever balanced:

*Record what happened. All of it. Even the parts that shame you.*

Killing him would be editing the record.

"No," she said again, and lowered the knife.

Corvane blinked. For one naked moment he looked genuinely lost—an old man whose final argument had been his own death, and who had just been refused it.

"You can't," he said. "You can't leave me alive. I will hunt you. I am *obligated* to hunt you. The moment you walk out that door I become your enemy again, fully, and you know it, and you're sparing me anyway? That's not mercy, girl. That's a debt you're taking on with no security. Mercy is a liability. Mercy is the one trade this guild never learned to price—"

"I know," Lena said. "That's why it's worth something."

She backed toward the door, charter hugged to her chest, Tam already moving past her into the corridor. Corvane did not follow. He stood among the vault's shadows with his burned pages and his carved word he couldn't look at, and as she reached the threshold he spoke once more, quiet enough that she almost missed it beneath the turning of the world:

"The tenth voice must be given, not taken. Remember that, when you read the verse. Whatever it asks—it must be *given*."

And then, louder, in the full cold voice of the Master of the Guild of Impossible Things: "Seal the vault! Enforcers—east stair, *now!*"

Lena ran.

* * *

They went out the way she'd come in, down through the breathing dark, into the coal-chutes with their smell of soot and childhood. Tam went first this time, sliding into the iron throat with a gasp and a rattle of falling ash, and Lena followed, the charter strapped tight against her heart, and behind them she heard boots thundering on stairs and Corvane's voice cracking orders through the cellar like a whip.

Halfway up the chute, wedged in the dark with her lungs burning, Lena heard something else—faint, from far above, filtering down through the coal-yard grate.

Singing.

Not the guild's hymns. Not Malvane's counter-song. A single voice, thin and clear, coming from beyond the eastern wall, singing a fisherwoman's tide-lullaby in a key that made the chute-iron hum.

*Maren's song.* But Maren was dead. Which meant—

"Dain," Lena breathed, and shoved upward toward the light.

They burst out of the chute-gate into the grey first light of morning, ash-covered and gasping—and there, at the top of the delivery ramp, stood Dain Ash with Orlin beside him and a dozen freed wayshrine-singers at his back, holding the yard against six enforcers with nothing but bells, blades, and a borrowed song. The guild's soldiers wavered, uncertain, their orders colliding with their superstitions.

Dain turned, saw her, and his whole face changed.

"You're late," he called.

"You're early," Lena called back, and ran to him with the charter and the dawn and the whole unwritten future pressing against her ribs.

Behind them, high in the guildhall, a window lit gold—and in it, briefly, the silhouette of Master Corvane, watching them go, making no move to stop them, one hand raised to the glass as if in blessing or farewell.

Or warning.

By nightfall, Tam would decode the first line of the grandmother's verse, and understand why Corvane had let them leave.
