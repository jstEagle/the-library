## 23. The Grandmother's Verse

Tam had copied forbidden pages before. He had done it by candle stub in the guildhall's under-closets, breath held, ink-stained fingers trembling on Corvane's stolen ledger. But he had never deciphered anything like this.

The founding charter lay open across three flat stones near the fire, weighted at its corners with river pebbles and a knife and Dain's boot. It was older than any document Tam had ever touched—older than the guildhall itself, if Lena was right—and it did not behave like paper. Its surface had the grain of something once alive, a pale hide shot through with faint silver threads that caught the firelight and moved, very slowly, the way light moves through water. When Tam bent close, he could smell salt and beeswax and, beneath both, something like rain about to fall.

"It's breathing," he said, for the third time that hour.

"It's listening," Maren corrected from her seat on the far side of the flames. She had her mending in her lap but hadn't touched it since sundown. "There's a difference. Mind which one you say around old things."

The camp sat in the lee of a fallen watchtower, half a day south of Bellhollow's ashes, where the road to Errenfell ran along a river that ran the wrong way. Orlin had built the fire too large, as he built everything—too loud, too much, as though volume could hold off the dark—and now he sat beside it turning a bell-clapper over in his ruined hands, not ringing it, just holding it, the way a man holds a thing he is trying to forgive.

Lena knelt at the charter's edge and did not look up. She had not looked up in some time. Her hair had come loose from its braid and hung across her face, and her lips moved without sound, reading and rereading.

Dain stood watch on the broken wall above them, though there was nothing yet to watch for. Tam had learned that about him: Dain kept watch even when watch was useless, because standing still made the guilt louder. Since Bellhollow burned, he slept maybe two hours a night, and never lying down.

"Tell me again what you found," Tam said quietly, to Lena, because someone had to say something or the silence would start naming their dead ahead of schedule.

"The guildhall copy of the charter—the one they let apprentices dust—it has nine verses." She spoke without raising her head, her voice roughened by smoke and sleeplessness. "Nine singers, nine verses, nine bindings. Every history agrees. Every mural in Bellhollow showed nine." Her finger hovered above the hide. "This one has ten."

The word dropped into the firelight like a stone into a well. Tam heard it fall all the way down.

* * *

He had been copying Corvane's second ledger for eleven days now, ever since the wayshrine spat him out of the guildhall's coal cellar and into this world that smelled of green thunder. Eleven days of Master Corvane's private hand, cramped and precise, recording what the guild had actually done instead of what it said it had done. Tam knew the shape of betrayal now the way he knew the shape of his own handwriting. He could find it in the dark.

But the ledger had gaps. Pages Corvane had burned before Tam ever stole it—pages whose ash Tam had sifted from the hearth grate with a pastry brush and tweezers, piecing fragments together by lamplight while the others slept. The fragments mentioned *the ninth voice*. They mentioned *a price already paid*. They mentioned a woman's name written once and then scratched through so hard the pen had torn the page:

*E. Sandoval.*

Lena's grandmother. The ninth singer. The one whose face had been scraped from the Bellhollow murals with such violence that the plaster bore gouges deep enough to hold candle-shadow.

And now, spread on stones by a dying fire, the charter she had fled with—a hundred years old and still warm somehow, still faintly pulsing against Tam's fingertips like a sleeping bird—was showing them why the guild had needed her gone.

"There," Lena breathed. "Do you see it?"

Tam leaned in. Between the ninth verse and the charter's seal—between the last singer's binding and the wax that had once held the whole covenant shut—ran a line of text no guild copy possessed. It was not written so much as *woven*, each character formed of those silver threads that surfaced and sank like fish under ice. Tam had spent four years in the guild's scriptorium learning six alphabets and two cipher systems, and none of them prepared him for writing that seemed to be deciding, letter by letter, whether to exist.

"It only comes up when the fire's low," Orlin said. He had noticed it first, an hour ago, grunting like a man who'd found a splinter. "Bright flame drives it down. Bank the coals."

Maren banked the coals. The fire sank to a red murmuration, and the silver thread rose, and the hidden verse came up through the hide like a bruise rising through skin.

Tam read it aloud, because that was his role here—he was the one who read, the one who copied, the one who carried other people's words safely through fire. His voice cracked twice and he steadied it and went on.

*"Nine voices bound the seam; nine were never enough.*
*Nine sang the covenant closed, and closed is not the same as whole.*
*A door held shut will strain until it breaks,*
*but a door held open needs a keeper in the frame.*
*The tenth voice is not sung. It is stood.*
*A keeper who chooses, standing between realms,*
*giving the gate her own ground to stand on,*
*paying passage with every memory of belonging,*
*until the two worlds learn to carry their own weight.*
*This verse was hidden so that no keeper would be bred for it,*
*taken for it, raised to it, or sold.*
*It must be given. Freely. Or not at all."*

The last line faded even as he spoke it, sinking back beneath the hide's pale grain, and the charter shivered once—like a horse shaking off flies—and went still.

No one spoke. The wrong-running river whispered behind them. Somewhere up the valley a nightbird called three notes and got no answer.

Tam realized his hands were shaking. He put them flat on his knees.

"That's why she hid it," he said finally, because someone had to make words happen or the silence would curdle. "Your grandmother. She didn't just flee with the charter. She *hid the verse inside it*, where the guild would never think to look, because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because if the guild had known, they wouldn't have searched for a willing keeper. They'd have *made* one. Raised one. Bred one." He looked up, and hated himself for where his eyes went, and went there anyway. "They'd have done exactly what they did with you, Lena. Except on purpose."

Lena still hadn't looked up. In the banked firelight her face was all shadow and ember-edge, unreadable as the charter itself.

"They did do it on purpose," she said.

The words came out level. That was the worst part, Tam thought. Not weeping, not raging—level. A ledger-keeper's voice, balancing accounts.

"My parents died when I was four. Fire. That's the story I was told." She turned the charter's corner over, gently, the way you turn a sleeping child. "Corvane told me I was brought to the guild as a charity case. An orphan with quick hands and a head for numbers." A pause. "A century ago, a woman named E. Sandoval wrote a verse into this hide saying the tenth voice must be *given freely*. And then a girl with her blood ends up in the guild's care before she can walk. Raised behind a sealed gate. Taught ledgers. Taught seals. Taught to inspect the gate every single night, alone." She laughed—one short, airless note with nothing in it. "They weren't training a gatekeeper. They were ripening one."

* * *

Tam walked away from the fire after that. He told himself it was to check the picket lines, and partly it was, but mostly his skin felt too small and walking was the only thing that fit.

Hope arrived dressed as burden. That was the phrase his mother used to use—his real mother, back in the free cities, before the guild took him as an apprentice at eleven, before Corvane became the closest thing to a father he had. *Hope arrives dressed as burden, love. Let it in anyway. Check its pockets, but let it in.* He had thought it a homely saying, the kind of thing women stitched on cushions. Now he understood it was a warning label.

Because here was the hope: the covenant could be reforged. Whole. Permanently. Not patched with nine voices straining against a seam that was never meant to hold shut, but completed—with a tenth pillar, a living hinge, a door held *open* instead of nailed closed. Malvane's war, the bleeding magic, the dying meadows, the border-stones cracking—all of it had a cure. It had always had a cure. It was written in silver thread on a grandmother's fleeing hide, and it was beautiful.

And here was the burden, wearing hope's clothes, smiling hope's smile: the cure was a person. The cure was memories. *Every memory of belonging.* The cure was someone standing in a doorway between worlds forever, giving up her childhood, her guild, her name-day candles, her mother's lullaby if she remembered it, her friends' faces—

Giving up Dain's face.

Tam stopped at the picket line and gripped the rope and stared out at the dark meadow where the grass glowed faintly, the way everything in Vael glowed faintly, and he did the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since the moment he finished reading the verse aloud.

Lena would say yes.

That was the burden dressed as hope. Not that the price existed—prices always existed; Tam had grown up in a guild that priced impossible things—but that *she* would pay it. Gladly. She had already paid pieces of herself on this road: her certainty about the guild, her loyalty to Corvane, her belief that she'd been pulled through the gate rather than chosen. Each loss had made her lighter and more determined, and Tam had watched it happen and called it growth.

But this. This wasn't a piece. This was the whole ledger, balanced to zero.

Unless.

His mind, trained in Corvane's scriptorium, went hunting for alternatives the way a hound goes hunting for scent. Could another keeper stand in the frame? The verse said *a keeper who chooses*—it didn't say Lena Sandoval by name. But it also didn't say anyone else, and prophecies that left doors open usually did it to trap the hopeful. Could the tenth role be shared? Rotated? The verse said *standing*, singular, continuous. Could the payment be refused partway—could a keeper give some memories and keep others? The verse said *every memory of belonging*, and Tam had copied enough of Corvane's ledgers to know that contracts written in silver thread on living hide did not contain loopholes. Loopholes were a human invention. Covenants were older than humans.

Could they simply not tell her?

The thought arrived slyly, wearing reason's robes, and Tam caught it by the collar before it got comfortable. Because that was Corvane's move. That was *exactly* Corvane's move—decide for the instrument, spare the instrument the knowledge of its purpose, call it mercy while you sharpen it. Tam had copied pages describing that exact logic applied to tribute, to singers, to a whole world's magic hoarded in vaults. If he let that thought live, he was just Corvane with cleaner hands.

No. You told her. You told her everything, in plain ink, and then you let her choose—which was, he realized, the entire point of the verse. *Hidden so that no keeper would be taken for it. It must be given, freely, or not at all.* E. Sandoval hadn't hidden the truth to prevent the sacrifice. She'd hidden it to protect the *choosing*. A forced tenth voice would fail—or worse, succeed crooked, a covenant of coercion binding two realms in resentment. The freedom wasn't decoration. It was structural.

Which meant Tam's job—the job he'd crossed a collapsing wayshrine carrying a stolen ledger to do—was to make sure she chose with her eyes open.

Even if what her open eyes saw was the end of her.

* * *

When he came back to the fire, the others had gathered close around the charter, and the conversation had the careful quiet of people circling a wound.

"—doesn't have to be tonight," Maren was saying. "Nothing has to be decided by firelight. Bad bargains are struck by firelight. Sleep on it. Sleep on it for a year, if the world allows."

"I'm not deciding anything," Lena said. "I'm reading."

"You've read it nine times."

"Then I'll read it ten."

Dain crouched at the charter's edge, and Tam saw him looking—not at the silver thread, at Lena. At the set of her shoulders. Tam had watched Dain watch her for days now, and had catalogued the progression the way he catalogued everything: first curiosity, then respect, then something that had stopped pretending to be either. Dain Ash, who blamed himself for Bellhollow, who confessed to hunting the eighth singer, who forgave himself nothing—Dain was falling in love with a woman who might soon forget his face, and he knew it, and he was staying anyway. Tam understood, suddenly and completely, the phrase *hope arriving dressed as burden*. It fit more than prophecy.

Orlin broke the quiet. He did that; silence made him itch. "The verse says *given, not taken*." He turned the bell-clapper over in his hands. "So the usurper can't do it. Corvane can't do it. All their power, all their crowns and vaults—they can't finish this, because they can only take." He looked up, and for once his eyes were clear, not drink-clouded. "That's not a burden, girl. That's the whole war in one line. They've got everything except the one thing that can't be seized."

"And the one thing that can't be seized," Maren said softly, "can be lost. Or spent. Don't dress it up as armor, bell-man. It's a door, and doors swing both ways."

Tam sat down at the circle's edge and pulled his satchel into his lap—the guild satchel, scarred leather, coal dust still in its seams from the chute he'd fallen through. From it he took the second ledger, Corvane's true account, and the ash-fragments wrapped in oilcloth, and laid them beside the charter. Two documents, side by side: the crime and the cure.

"If we're doing this," he said, "we do it properly. Full entry. Every clause read aloud, every price named, witnessed and recorded." He uncapped his ink bottle. "That's how honest ledgers work. Nobody signs what they haven't read."

Lena finally looked up.

Her eyes in the low firelight were the color of the coals, and steady, and Tam had seen that steadiness before—in Maren before the drowned shrine, in Orlin before the bells, in every person on this road who had already decided to pay and was only waiting for the asking. His chest hurt.

"You're going to write it down," she said. Not a question.

"Every word. Including the parts you'll want skipped." He dipped his pen. "Especially those."

She almost smiled. It was a small, tired, real thing, and it lasted perhaps two seconds before her gaze dropped back to the charter—to the tenth verse, risen silver in the dying light, waiting the way it had waited a hundred years for someone to read it and refuse to look away.

"Then write this first," Lena said. "For the record. Before anyone asks me anything." She drew a breath, and Tam's pen hovered, and the whole camp went still enough to hear the wrong-running river. "Write that I was pulled through that gate. Write that I was raised to this without my knowledge or consent. Write that whatever I choose next, the choosing is mine—and that the people who tried to breed a keeper owe her a debt no ledger can balance." Her voice didn't shake. "Write that E. Sandoval was right to hide the verse. And write that her granddaughter is going to need everyone in this circle to remember what she forgets."

The pen touched hide-adjacent parchment, and Tam wrote.

Around them the fire settled to embers, and the charter's silver threads dimmed like a tide going out, and high on the broken wall Dain stood his watch with his back very straight, looking north—not toward the guild, not toward the free cities, but toward the seam, toward the place where the world was thin and a door waited for its keeper.

And far off, past the glowing meadow's edge, past the border-stones, a sound reached the camp that none of them could name at first—too rhythmic for wind, too vast for drums, too deliberate for weather. Orlin's head came up like a hound's. Maren's hands went still over her mending.

"It's singing," Tam whispered, before he could stop himself. "Something's singing on the seam."

Lena rose slowly to her feet, the charter's last light fading against her palms, and listened the way a ledger-keeper listens to a number that doesn't balance.

"That's not a song," she said. "That's a counter-song. Someone's practicing."
