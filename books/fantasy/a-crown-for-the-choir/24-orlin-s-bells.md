## 24. Orlin's Bells

Orlin had not touched a bell in eleven years, and his hands remembered it the way a scar remembers rain.

He stood in the bell-loft of the mountain shrine of Vess, high above the tree line where the air came thin and clean as struck silver, and he let his palms hover over the rope without closing on it. Below him, through the open arches of the campanile, the world fell away in terraces—pine, scree, cloud, and beyond all of that, far to the south and east, the faint luminous seam where the sky bent wrong. Where the realms had been stitched together once, and were coming apart.

"You can still stop," Lena said from the doorway. She had a way of standing that made no demands, which was, Orlin had learned, the most demanding thing about her.

"No," he said. "I can't." He wrapped his fingers around the rope. The hemp was cold and familiar, worn smooth by dead ringers' hands, and it fit his grip like an apology finally spoken aloud. "I've been stopping for eleven years. Look what it got everyone."

Maren stood at the center of the shrine floor below, small and straight-backed among the pillars, her gray hair bound up with fisherman's twine because she refused to waste a hairpin on ceremony. She had spent the morning walking the circle of standing stones, touching each one, murmuring to them the way she'd murmur to a net with a tear in it. Now she looked up at the loft, and her voice carried up through the stone the way only a singer's voice could—unbroken by distance, arriving warm against his ear as if she stood beside him.

"Ring true, bell-man," she called. "Don't ring pretty. Pretty won't hold a seam."

"I don't know how to do anything but pretty," Orlin said. "That was always my sin."

"Then today you'll learn ugly," Maren said, and smiled, and there was something in the smile that made his chest ache—the courage of a woman who had already paid one impossible price and knew another was coming, and had decided to pay it anyway with her chin up.

Dain checked the perimeter one last time, moving along the outer wall with the restless economy of a man who had been hunted too long to ever fully stand still. Tam sat cross-legged at the base of the great bell itself, the founding charter and the three rescued prophecy books spread around them in a careful fan, following the score with a fingertip, lips moving. Sef's people had wanted to come—half the harbor fleet had volunteered—but there hadn't been time to move so many, and the mountain paths would not bear an army quietly. So it was five of them. Five, and nine bells, and whatever was left of a disgraced ringer's faith.

The bells of Vess hung in a ring around the campanile's crown: nine of them, cast before the covenant, before the guild, before anyone now living had been born. Their bronze was dark with age, and each bore a single word around its lip in the old script—*listen*, said the first; *remember*, said the second; *stay*, said the third. Orlin had read them all by torchlight the night before, running his fingers over letters deeper than his thumb, and had wept without quite knowing why, the way a man weeps at a grave he doesn't remember digging.

He knew why this morning. This morning there was no room left for not-knowing.

"On my mark," Maren said, and closed her eyes, and began to breathe in the long slow measure singers used, drawing the mountain air down into herself like a woman drawing water from a deep well.

Orlin looked at his hands. Big hands, knuckle-swollen, a ringer's hands, a drunkard's hands—he had used them for both trades and they showed the wear of both. Eleven years ago he had rung the bells of Callowmere to warn the eighth singer that the guild's hunters were coming, and he had rung them wrong—too late, too few—and she had died in a snowfield with her song half-finished in her throat, and Orlin had decided that day that his hands were weapons and should touch nothing holy again.

Lena had changed that arithmetic. Not with forgiveness—she wasn't built for easy absolution, and he'd respected her for it—but with honesty. *You might lose more memories,* she'd told him, when he'd asked what the singing cost. *Maybe blood. I won't lie to you about it.* No one had ever offered him the truth unvarnished before. Everyone else had offered him redemption, cheap and shiny, and he'd known it was counterfeit because nothing real is ever that smooth. But Lena had handed him the bill and let him read it, and somehow that had made all the difference. A man could sign his name to a true account.

He set his weight against the rope.

"Now," Maren said, and sang.

Her voice went out over the stones like dawn going out over water—not loud, not forced, but *everywhere*, filling the arches, the terraces, the thin cold sky. It was a lullaby, or the bones of one; Orlin recognized the shape of it from the nights Maren sang to the campfire, bending the flames low and gentle. But here, in this place, with the standing stones drinking it in, the lullaby opened into something vaster. He heard tides in it. He heard the pull of moons. He heard, impossibly, the sound of a shore he had never walked, calling to a sea he had never sailed.

He pulled the rope.

The first bell spoke.

It did not clang—it *woke*. The sound rolled out of the bronze the way light rolls out of a struck flint, a round golden note that seemed less made than released, as though the bell had been holding its breath for a century and Maren's song had finally given it permission. The note met her voice in the air above the shrine and *locked*, two streams joining into a river, and Orlin felt the seam of the world shiver like a sheet snapped flat.

"*Remember,*" said the second bell, and he rang it.

"*Stay,*" said the third.

By the fourth he had stopped thinking. His body remembered everything his mind had tried to drown—the timing, the pull, the way a ringer speaks to a bell not with muscle but with attention, leaning his whole self into the swing, catching the rope on the turn so the bronze sang full-voiced instead of clipped. The fifth bell. The sixth. Each note rose and joined the growing chord, and the chord grew teeth, grew wings, grew roots—it became a structure in the air, a bridge of sound laid across the broken place in the world, and Orlin, ringing, ringing, ringing, felt tears sliding hot down his face and did not care who saw.

Because he could *feel* it working.

Below and beyond, the seam answered. He saw it through the arches—a band of wrong-colored light along the southern horizon, the wound in the sky that Dain had shown him from the ridge weeks ago, bleeding magic into a world that had forgotten how to hold it. As the chord built, the wound's ragged edges began to draw together. Not healed—knitted, the way a deep cut knits when the flesh remembers its own intention. The shimmer steadied. The colors settled from frantic to merely strange. Somewhere far away, Orlin knew, rivers were remembering which way was downhill. Somewhere, hedges that had grown thorns overnight were softening. Somewhere, a child who had woken speaking a language no one taught her was falling asleep again, ordinary, safe.

"It's holding," Tam shouted from below, voice cracking with joy, charter clutched to his chest. "Orlin, it's *holding*—"

Half the world, Maren had promised. Half the world could be sewn today, and the rest would follow when they found the strength. Orlin hauled on the seventh rope, and the seventh bell—*forgive*, said the lettering, and he laughed out loud at the audacity of bronze—threw its note into the chord, and the whole mountain rang with them, every stone a sounding board, every pine a listener.

For one breath—one full, perfect breath, the length of a swung bell's hang at the top of its arc—Orlin Brightwater, disgraced and forgiven-by-no-one, stood inside a mended world.

Then the counter-song came.

It arrived the way a cold front arrives: no warning but a pressure change, a flattening in the ear, a taste of iron. The chord faltered—not broke, not yet, but *bent*, as though a giant hand had pressed down upon the bridge of sound they had built. Maren's voice strained upward against it. Orlin grabbed the eighth rope and rang, and the note fought free for a moment, and then the counter-song found its footing and came on in earnest.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was gorgeous—layered voices braided into a melody of such aching, wounded grandeur that Orlin's hands nearly stilled on the ropes just to listen. It sounded like grief given a crown. It sounded like a man singing alone in an empty hall to an audience of ghosts. And underneath its beauty ran a will, vast and patient and utterly certain, pressing down on their chord the way night presses down on a candle.

"Malvane," Dain snarled, appearing in the loft doorway, sword drawn as though steel could help. "He's found us. How in the names—"

"He didn't find us," Maren said. She had not stopped singing. Sweat beaded on her brow and her small frame trembled with effort, but her voice held, thread by stubborn thread. "He doesn't need to find us. He wears the tribute-power now. He can feel the covenant anywhere it moves, the way a scarred man feels weather. Keep ringing, bell-man. Don't you dare stop."

Orlin rang. The eighth bell—*endure*—spoke into the grinding dark, and the chord rallied, and for a handful of heartbeats the two songs hung balanced over the mountain, the lullaby and the lament, the mending and the breaking, and the seam below flickered between them like a lamp in wind.

But Malvane's song had an army behind it. They heard it now, faint under the music: drums, thousands of them, beating in the valleys to the south, keeping time for the counter-song the way oars keep time for a warship. And they had something else. Orlin felt it before he understood it—a wrongness in the chord's foundation, a sourness creeping into the ninth bell's silence, waiting.

"The crown," Tam whispered. He had gone white. He was staring at the charter, at the score, at some line of the old notation that had just become legible in the worst possible way. "The hoarded power. He forged it into a crown and the crown is a *voice*, it's a singer's voice, it's—" His eyes came up, huge. "It's her voice. The eighth singer's. He kept it. All these years he *kept it*, and now he sings with the dead."

The counter-song swelled, and Orlin heard it then, threaded through the lament's grandeur: a woman's voice, clear and young and terribly alive, singing harmonies that no living throat had written. The eighth singer, whose death his own late bells had failed to prevent, conscripted at last into the army of the man who had loved her teacher and hated the world for losing her.

The chord buckled.

Maren cried out—not in defeat, in *effort*, pouring everything into the lullaby, and for a moment, one last impossible moment, the chord stood. The seam across half the world blazed steady, a line of mended light from the western sea to the mountains' root, and Orlin rang the ninth bell with both hands and his whole heart, and the ninth bell—its word worn illegible, its meaning lost—sang anyway, sang louder than all its sisters, sang like a question that refuses to be unanswered—

And the counter-song came down like a landslide.

The chord shattered. Not faded—*shattered*, note by note, each one plucked out of the air and crushed, and Orlin felt every break physically, in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the old fracture-line behind his sternum that eleven years of drink had never quite numbed. The bells swung wild in the aftermath, clanging discordantly, mocking him. The seam below tore back open along its southern length, wider than before, angrier, and the light that bled from it was the color of bruises.

Maren caught herself on a standing stone, gasping, her song spent. She looked up at Orlin through the loft's floor-grating, and her face was gray with exhaustion, but her eyes—her eyes were furious and alive and unbowed.

"Half," she rasped. "We got half. Remember that, all of you—we *got half the world*. He can't take that back without tearing it, and tearing it costs him too. That's not nothing. That's a beachhead."

Orlin let go of the ropes. His palms were shredded, slick with blood, and he found he didn't remember the moment they'd torn, and he thought: *good. Let the pain be part of the ledger. Let it be entered honestly.*

Below, Tam was gathering the books with shaking hands. Dain stood at the wall, staring south, where—far off, at the edge of seeing—a darkness was moving through the passes. Not an army. Something smaller. Faster. A hunting party, wearing the shape of riders, sent to finish what the counter-song had started.

"They know where we are now," Dain said quietly. "The song cuts both ways. We showed him our position as surely as he showed us his power." He turned, and his scarred face wore the expression Orlin had come to dread and rely on in equal measure: the look of a man calculating how fast they could run. "We need to be gone from this mountain by dark."

"And go where?" Tam asked.

Maren pushed herself upright. She looked south toward the drowned shrine of Errenfell, and then east, toward the lowlands, and Orlin watched her tally distances and dangers the way she'd once tallied nets and tides.

"Errenfell," she said. "The third attempt has to be at Errenfell. The drowned shrine sits on the seam's oldest point—if we're going to finish this, it's there, and it's soon, before he brings his whole weight down on the half we've mended." She flexed her hands, winced, and smiled that terrible brave smile again. "And we'll need every voice we have left. Including yours, bell-man. Especially yours. You rang nine bells against a crowned god today, and the world is half-mended because of it. Whatever you think you've been paying for these eleven years—consider the debt entered and part-paid."

Orlin looked at his bleeding hands. He thought of the eighth singer dying in the snow while his bells hung silent. He thought of the ninth bell's worn-away word, and decided that its meaning didn't matter, because today it had meant *again*.

"Then we'd better move," he said. "Before he learns to sing with two dead voices."

Far to the south, almost lost beneath the wind, the counter-song began again—patient, wounded, crowned—and this time, Olin could swear he heard a third voice learning the melody.

* * *

They came down off the mountain by the pilgrims' stair, a thousand steps cut into the cliff-face, slick with meltwater and shadow. Orlin carried the smallest of the nine bells on his back, roped like a smuggler's cargo, because Maren had ordered it and no one argued with Maren anymore. *Bronze remembers,* she'd said, buckling the straps herself. *What's sung into bronze stays sung. If we lose the shrines, we carry the shrines.*

Halfway down, in a niche where the stair widened and the mountain fell away sheer below, they stopped to breathe, and that was where Tam, checking the charter against the fading light, went very still.

"Lena," he said. "Come look at this."

Orlin watched from two steps above as Lena crouched beside the boy in the blue-gray dusk. He watched her read the line Tam's finger indicated. He watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"What is it?" Dain asked.

Lena didn't answer at first. When she did, her voice was very quiet, and very steady, and that steadiness frightened Orlin more than any scream could have.

"The verse we couldn't read. The one the fire damaged." She looked up, and her eyes found each of them in turn—Dain, Tam, Maren, him. "It was never nine. The covenant was always meant for ten." She swallowed. "And the tenth isn't a singer at all. The tenth is 'a keeper who chooses.'"

The wind moved through the pass below them, carrying up from the distant south the faint, patient thread of Malvane's song—and beneath it, unmistakable now, that third voice, still learning.

No one spoke. There was nothing yet to say. But Orlin saw Lena's hand close slowly around the strap of the little bell on his back, as if to promise the bronze—and whoever the keeper was, whoever the choosing belonged to—that it would not be carried into the dark alone.
