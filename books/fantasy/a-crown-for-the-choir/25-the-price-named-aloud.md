## 25. The Price Named Aloud

The fire they built that night was small, and that was a choice.

Lena had learned enough of Vael by now to know that light drew things—moths with too many wings, and worse, the pale watchers Malvane sent skimming along the ridgelines like thoughts that had escaped someone's head. So they dug the firepit deep, ringed it with river-stones from the Errenfell shallows, and let the flames show only through a lattice of woven branches Dain bent over the top. The light came through in stripes, gold on their faces, gold on their hands, as if they were all sitting behind the bars of some gentle prison.

Below them, the drowned shrine of Errenfell glimmered in the twilight water. Where the sea had taken the old city, it had left the tops of its towers standing like candles burned down to the wick, and the water around them glowed faintly with the residue of Maren's tide-song—still there, three days after she had sung, still refusing to die entirely. A covenant flickering alive for one breath. One breath out of everything they needed.

Lena sat with her knees drawn up, her ledger—her old guild ledger, the one she'd carried through the gate without thinking, because a keeper did not put down her book—open on her lap. She wasn't reading it. She was holding it the way you hold a railing.

Tam was sketching again, charcoal on the back of a supply manifest, drawing the tower-tops below with quick, sure strokes. Orlin lay on his back with his hands laced over his chest, watching the striped firelight on the branch-lattice as if it were a ceiling he remembered from somewhere better. Dain was sharpening his courier's knife against a whetstone, the sound a slow heartbeat under everything else.

They had not spoken much since the shrine. Failure does that. It fills a camp the way floodwater fills a cellar—silently, from the bottom up, until everyone is standing in it.

It was Maren who broke the silence, though Maren had been quiet all evening in a way that frightened Lena more than any wailing would have. The old fisherwoman sat closest to the fire, wrapped in her oilskin coat even though the night was mild, her gnarled hands open on her knees, palms up, as if she were waiting for something to be placed in them.

"You're all doing sums," Maren said. Her voice was roughened by salt and years and, now, by one memory less than it had been a week ago. "I can hear it. Everyone's adding up what this is going to cost, and nobody wants to say the number out loud."

Orlin grunted. "Saying it doesn't make it smaller."

"No," Maren agreed. "But carrying it alone makes it heavier." She turned her head, and the striped light moved across the deep lines of her face. "I paid at the shrine. I'll say mine first, then. Fair's fair."

Nobody moved. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"I sang my son," Maren said simply. "The tide took him when he was nineteen—fishing the way our family had fished for two hundred years, nothing reckless, nothing wrong. Just the sea being the sea. For thirty-one years I have carried him. His laugh. The weight of him as a baby. The way he said *Mama* when he was half asleep and didn't know he was saying it." Her palms stayed open. "I gave the covenant the memory of his voice. That's what it took—the sound of him. I can remember that he laughed. I can't remember how."

Lena felt something crack quietly in her chest. Tam set down the charcoal.

"And here's the thing nobody tells you about paying," Maren went on, in the same level voice, the voice of a woman who had gutted ten thousand fish and buried one son and refused to be broken by either. "You don't miss the memory itself. You miss missing it. Grief needs something to hold. I keep reaching for him and my hands close on water." She looked around the fire, at each of them in turn. "So. That's mine. Who's next?"

* * *

Orlin sat up slowly, joints cracking like green wood in a hot stove. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a bell-ringer's forearms and a drunkard's tremor that only showed when his hands were empty—which was why, Lena suspected, he kept them full: a cup, a rope, a mallet, anything.

"Mine's not tragic," he said. "I want that on the record. Mine's just stupid."

"Stupid can hurt worst," Dain said. "It doesn't come with any dignity."

Orlin barked a laugh that was mostly real. "Right. All right." He stared into the fire. "I rang the bells at Highmoor for twenty-two years. My father before me, his mother before him. The Ashfall bells—they weren't music, exactly. They were *keeping*. Every hour on the hour, the pattern told the mountain the world was still ordered, still counted, still held. And I—" He turned his cup in his hands. "I got proud. Bored. Started improvising. Little flourishes. Then whole variations. Then one dawn I thought, *who's actually listening? Who would know?* And I rang whatever I pleased."

"The shrines went quiet," Dain said softly.

"The shrines went quiet." Orlin nodded. "Took months before anyone connected it. By then the seam had thinned over the whole Ashfall range. Things came through. Small things, at first. Then not small." He drank, though Lena had noticed his cup held only water these days; the habit of drinking remained when the drink was gone. "I lost my post, my name, my family's name. I've spent nine years telling myself I was punished enough." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "But I wasn't punished. I was *indebted*. There's a difference, and the ledger-keeper will know what I mean."

Lena did know. She nodded once.

"So what I might lose," Orlin said, "when we sing the whole thing—I've thought about it every night since the mountain shrines. I think it'll take the bells. Not the memory *of* the bells. The knowing of them. The part of me that understands what a bell is *for*. I'll stand in a bell tower someday, if I live, and hear the greatest music ever rung, and it'll be noise to me. Pretty noise. Nothing more." He swallowed. "And I deserve that. That's the ugly truth of it. I'd pay worse if worse were asked. But I wanted it said aloud, so it's not sitting in me growing."

"Thank you," Maren said, and meant it, and Orlin looked away as if gratitude were a brightness he couldn't bear.

* * *

Tam surprised them all by speaking next—or perhaps surprised no one, because Tam had always been the kind of person who could not leave an account unbalanced.

"I don't have losses yet," Tam said. "I'm the newest. I have *fears*, which are different. Fears are debts not yet incurred." Tam's fingers found the edge of Corvane's second ledger, chained no longer but still carried everywhere, wrapped in cloth like a wound. "I fear losing Master Corvane. Not the man he is now. The man I thought he was. He taught me my letters. He taught me that a ledger is a moral document—that every entry is a promise made visible. When I found out what he'd done, what they'd all done—" Tam's jaw tightened. "I keep two versions of him in my head. The teacher and the traitor. And I think the covenant won't take the traitor. It'll take the teacher. It'll take the memory of being taught, of being *chosen* by him, of standing in the scriptorium at dawn while he corrected my hand and said, *again, but gentler.*" Tam's voice caught. "I'll remember only that I was betrayed. I won't remember why it ever mattered so much."

"That might be mercy," Dain said.

"It might be theft," Tam said. "I haven't decided."

* * *

Dain went last of the three, and he went the way he did everything—without ceremony, but with a precision that made ceremony unnecessary.

"You all know most of mine," he said. "I served Malvane. I led the guild's hunters to the eighth singer. Her name was Ilsette. She was folding laundry when they came." He did not flinch saying it; Lena had learned that the flinch came later, in the pauses, in the spaces between sentences where other men put nothing. "What I might lose—if the covenant takes payment the way it took Maren's—I think it'll take her face. Or the sound of the door when I opened it for the hunters. Something specific. The covenant doesn't take generalities. It takes the keystone memory, the one the rest of the arch stands on." He looked at the fire. "Part of me hopes it takes it. Part of me knows that if it does, I'll stop being the man who remembers, and start being a man who was merely told. And men who were merely told forgive themselves far too easily."

He glanced at Lena then, briefly, and away. Their forgiveness was still new—weeks old, renewed daily, a plant watered each morning and checked each morning for rot. He did not ask her to confirm it tonight. He never asked. That was part of why she could keep giving it.

* * *

The silence that followed was long and striped with firelight, and Lena understood, sitting in it, that they were all waiting for her. Not demanding. Waiting. The way you wait for a tide.

She closed her ledger. Opened it again. Closed it.

"My turn," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Except mine isn't a list. It's a question I haven't answered, and I think—I think you all deserve to hear it asked out loud, because you're betting your memories on me and I haven't been honest about the odds."

Orlin sat forward. Tam's charcoal stopped.

"When I stood in the seam at the mountain shrines—when I felt what the tenth role actually is—I understood something I haven't said. The verse says *a keeper who chooses, standing between.* I told myself that meant standing at the gate. Holding the door. A doorkeeper's job. I know doorkeeping. I've done it my whole life." She smiled, and it hurt. "But that's not what the verse means. I felt it. Standing between realms isn't a place. It's a *price*. The keeper becomes the hinge. And hinges don't get to be doors. They don't get to swing open into either room. They stay fixed in the middle, forever, holding both sides apart and together—and everything they were on either side burns off them like frost in sunlight."

The fire popped. Below them, the drowned towers glimmered.

"Maren paid one memory and it was her son's voice," Lena said. "The tenth role pays—" She stopped. Started again. "Everything. Every memory of belonging. My childhood in the guildhall. The coal-chutes I climbed. Corvane teaching me my letters, the same as Tam. The smell of the ledger-room. My grandmother's hands, if I ever truly had them. Dain—" Her voice did break there, once, and she let it, because they had earned honesty and honesty sometimes breaks. "Every time I've chosen to stay. Gone. And I won't even know it's gone, because the knowing goes with it."

Nobody spoke. Maren's open palms had curled slightly closed, as if in sympathy, as if catching something falling.

"So that's my sum," Lena finished. "Not what I've lost. What I *will* lose, if we succeed. And the worst part—the part I need you to hear—is that I don't know if I'm brave enough until the moment comes. I can promise you my voice. I can't promise you I won't hesitate at the last note. I can't promise the woman who walks out of that seam will be anyone you'd cross a world for. She might be a stranger who holds the door." She looked around the fire, at each of them, the way Maren had. "That's the price named aloud. Now you know what you're singing for. If anyone wants to walk to Errenfell town tonight and take ship home, I'll write you a clean discharge in my own hand, and no ledger anywhere will call it desertion."

She waited.

* * *

It was Orlin who answered first, because Orlin was the kind of man who filled silences the way he'd once filled cups.

"You daft girl," he said, gently. "You think any of us came this far on the promise of a happy ending?" He jerked a thumb at Maren. "She sang her own son into the water. This one—" Tam. "—betrayed the only family he had to bring us a book. And Dain's been waiting years for a prophecy to give him a penance worth doing. Nobody here is shopping for safety." He raised his water-cup. "We're shopping for *worth it*. And you just made it worth it. A stranger who holds the door is still a stranger who holds the door. I'll sing for her."

"Seconded," Tam said quietly. "And I'll copy that into the record, if you like. In my best hand."

Maren reached over and took Lena's hand, her grip strong and fisherman-rough and warm. "Child, I gave the covenant the sound of my boy laughing, and do you know what I learned at the bottom of that trade?" Her eyes were bright. "Love isn't stored in the memory. Memory's just where we keep the receipts. The love itself is in the *choosing*, and choosing happens fresh every morning or it doesn't happen at all. You'll forget us. Fine. We won't forget you. That's what a choir *is*—one voice can't hold the whole song, so the others carry your line while you breathe." She squeezed. "You breathe. We'll carry it."

Lena could not speak for a moment. She looked at Dain last, because looking at Dain was hardest and therefore owed.

He met her eyes across the striped firelight. He didn't say anything grand. He just said, "Daily," which was their word now, their private shorthand for forgiveness renewed, and somehow it held everything else: *I'll tell you the story again tomorrow. And the day after. As many times as it takes. You will not be a stranger forever if I have anything to say about it, and I am a very stubborn man.*

"Then we sing," Lena said.

"Then we sing," they echoed—not in unison, not quite, but close, close enough that for one moment the four voices around the little barred fire made a chord, and the water below the drowned towers brightened as if it had heard.

* * *

Later—much later, when Orlin snored like a landslide and Tam slept sitting upright against a stone with the ledgers hugged to his chest like a child with a doll, and Maren breathed slow and even in her oilskins—Lena sat awake beside Dain on the ridge above the camp, both of them watching the dark water and the candle-stub towers of Errenfell.

"We move on the shrine at first light," she said. "Third attempt. Full choir, full verse, everything Tam decoded."

"Aye."

"Dain. If it works—"

"Don't," he said. "Not tonight. Tonight you're still you, and I'm still memorizing you."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she leaned her shoulder against his, and he let her, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the seam of the world hummed beneath them like a string someone had plucked and not yet silenced.

It was Dain who saw it first. He went rigid under her shoulder.

"Lena." His voice had dropped to the flat, fast register he used for danger. "Look at the ridge road. North side."

She followed his gaze. Far off along the skyline, where the old coast road bent toward Errenfell town, there was a light. Then another. Then a dozen, moving in disciplined order, hooded and low, the marching lights of soldiers who knew exactly where they were going.

They were not coming from the direction of Malvane's border camps.

They were coming from the free cities' side.

"They knew," Lena whispered, cold spreading through her chest like ink through water. "Dain—nobody knew where we were camping. Nobody except—"

"The council," Dain said grimly, already reaching for his knife, already waking Orlin with a boot to the sole. "Someone sold us."
