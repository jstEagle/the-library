## 26. Betrayal at Errenfell

Dain smelled the sea before he saw it—the drowned shrine of Errenfell announced itself a full mile inland, salt threading through pine and peat like a rumor that would not die. He had walked this coast as a younger man, in another life, in another service, and the smell had meant then what it meant now: endings.

He shifted Maren's pack higher on his shoulder and did not look at her. Looking at her too long made him want to say things he had no right to say—apologies with thirty years of rot on them—and Maren had made it plain she'd box his ears if he wasted her last good hours on contrition.

"You walk like a man carrying a coffin," she said instead, not turning. Her bare feet found the tidal path without trouble; she knew it the way she knew the inside of her own mouth. "It's a shrine, boy. Not a grave."

"Not yet," Dain said.

She laughed at that, low and rough as surf over shingle, and the sound did something to the air around them—a gull three trees over banked hard left for no reason Dain could see. Even her laughter bent the world a little. He wondered how much of that was the tide-song in her and how much was just Maren, and he suspected the honest answer was that there had never been any difference.

Behind them, Lena and Tam came down the path with Orlin bringing up the rear, the bell-ringer's hands wrapped in wool against the morning cold, his knuckles still split from the mountain shrines. The whole company moved quietly. They had been moving quietly for six days, since the skirmish at the border-stones, since Sef's fleet had held by the width of a hull's planking, since Lena had stood on the deck of the *Kestrel's Mercy* and said the sentence none of them could stop hearing: *Every battle weakens the seam. We are losing the world faster by fighting for it.*

So: Errenfell. The third attempt. The drowned shrine where the covenant's first verse had been sung when both realms were young enough to believe in each other.

The path opened onto the flats.

Dain stopped, and the others stopped behind him, because there was no help for stopping—the sight took the legs out from under you whether you were ready or not. At low tide the sea withdrew for nearly half a mile, leaving a plain of wet sand and black stone, and at its heart stood the shrine: nine arches of white coral-stone rising from the seabed like the ribs of something vast and patient, their tops furred with weed, their bases ringed with pools that glowed faintly even in daylight. The water between the arches ran uphill. It always had. It was one of the places where Vael's magic bled thinnest into Lena's world, thin as light through a cracked door, and standing before it Dain felt the seam itself—not saw it, felt it, a pressure behind the eyes like the moment before a storm breaks.

"It's beautiful," Tam breathed. The apprentice had crossed over barely two weeks ago and still looked at everything in Vael like a man reading a letter from a dead friend.

"It's dying," Maren said, but gently, the way you correct a child. "Been dying sixty years. That's why we're here, girl. To remind it what it was."

They made camp above the tideline, in the lee of a fallen arch that had come ashore centuries ago and been adopted by the pines. Dain built the fire small and screened, out of habit older than his shame, and while the others ate he walked the perimeter the way he always did, counting the things that didn't belong.

He found the first one at dusk.

A boot print in the soft ground near the old coastal road—not a fisher's boot, not a soldier's issue boot either, but the narrow-toed, high-heeled cut of a council courier. Fresh. Hours old at most. And beside it, half-buried, a scrap of wax the color of dried blood: the seal of the Free Cities Council, broken and discarded.

Dain crouched over it for a long time, saying nothing, feeling the cold move up through his knees into his chest.

Councilors did not walk the Errenfell road. Councilors sent couriers, and couriers carried messages, and messages went to people who paid for them. He thought of Corvane's enforcers, of the guild's long reach, of the vote in the council hall that had fractured the alliance after news of the border-stones—*negotiate*, they'd said, *parley*, they'd said, and Sef had defied them all and given Lena the fleet anyway, and Dain had thought, foolishly, that defiance was the end of it.

But betrayal rarely announces itself. It sends a courier in good boots.

He told no one that night. He told himself it was because he wasn't sure, and that was true, but underneath the truth was an older, uglier truth: he had spent years as Malvane's man, years more as the guild's hunter, and he knew exactly how a trap smelled, and he was afraid that if he named it aloud it would become real, and if it became real he would have to decide what to do about it, and every decision he had made in the last thirty years had cost someone everything.

So he doubled the watch silently, moving Orlin's sleeping roll closer to the fire, setting his knife where his hand would find it, and lay awake listening to the tide come in across the flats, and told himself the sea-sound was only the sea-sound.

* * *

The second day belonged to preparation.

Maren walked the flats alone at dawn, and they all watched from the tideline as she moved between the nine arches, touching each one, singing under her breath—snatches of the lullabies that bent tides, fragments of the first verse she claimed to remember from her grandmother's grandmother. Where she passed, the glow in the pools brightened. Where she lingered, the uphill water ran a little straighter, a little prouder, like an old soldier hearing his regiment called.

"She's spending herself," Tam said quietly. The apprentice had taken to standing next to Dain on watch; Dain suspected the boy found his silences easier than Lena's questions. "I can see it. Each pass costs her."

"Everything costs," Dain said. "That's the whole lesson of this war. The guild forgot it. Malvane never learned it."

Lena came down the beach toward them with the covenant score wrapped in oilcloth against the spray, Bellhollow's ashes still somehow in the creases of her sleeves no matter how many times she washed them. She looked tired in a way that sleep didn't touch. She had looked that way since the border-stones, since she'd watched free-cities sailors die holding a line of rocks that marked nothing anyone could eat or build on, and understood that the rocks were the only thing worth dying for.

"The tide turns at moonrise," she said. "Maren says the shrine answers best on the turn. Are the approaches clear?"

Dain weighed it. One heartbeat. Two.

"Clear," he said.

Lena nodded and turned back toward the fire, and Dain watched her go and felt the word sit in his stomach like a swallowed stone. Clear. He had walked the eastern road himself that morning and found a second courier mark, fresher, and a thread of pipe-smoke on the wind from the ridge above the harbor road where no fisher smoked that particular bitter blend. He had known. He had *known*, the way you know weather coming off the water.

And he had said clear, because the alternative was telling Lena that the third attempt might be a slaughter, and she would have called it off, and the seam would keep bleeding, and Malvane's armies would keep marching, and someday soon there would be no world left on either side of it to save. He told himself that was why. He told himself it was arithmetic.

He knew better. He knew that some part of him—the part that had led hunters to the eighth singer, the part that had watched her dragged away in chains while he memorized her face so he could report it accurately—some part of him believed he deserved whatever came. That the ledger of his sins wanted balancing, and if the price was paid here, at a drowned shrine, in blood that wasn't his, then at least the account would move.

The tide came in. The tide went out. Dain stood on the cold sand and hated himself with the clean, efficient hatred of a man who has finally run out of excuses, and began, silently, to plan.

* * *

Moonrise came up red.

Not blood-red—that was the sort of thing poets said—but copper, the color of a lamp behind smoke, and the light lay across the flats and turned the nine arches to burning ribs. The tide began its turn with a sound like a great indrawn breath, and Maren waded out between the arches barefoot, her gray hair loose, her voice already gathering.

Lena stood at the shrine's edge with the score open. Tam held the lantern. Orlin had brought down one of the small handbells from his ruined tower, a bronze thing no bigger than a fist, and held it ready though they all knew one bell was a whisper where a carillon was needed. And Dain stood where he always stood—at the perimeter, back to the song, watching the dark.

The song began.

He heard it through his spine before his ears: a low note like the sea deciding something. Then Maren's voice rose over it, old and cracked and absolutely certain, and the water between the arches began to run uphill in earnest, silver threads climbing stone, and the pools lit one by one—first, second, third—like windows kindling in a house waking from a long illness.

Dain counted the lights. First arch. Second. Third. Fourth—

And on the fourth arch's kindling, he saw the fires.

Three of them, maybe four, on the ridge above the harbor road. Torches, moving in formation. No fisher moved in formation. His heart went once, very hard, against his ribs, and then went quiet, the way it had learned to in bad years, and he was running before the thought finished forming, boots throwing sand, screaming it ahead of him:

"RIDERS! RIDERS ON THE RIDGE—Lena! BREAK IT OFF, BREAK IT OFF NOW!"

The song faltered. On the fifth arch the light guttered. Maren's voice wavered—and then, terribly, did not stop. Dain heard her hold the note even as she turned, heard the old woman make the choice in real time, in the space of one held breath: the covenant flickering alive around her like a candle cupped against wind, worth finishing, worth defending, worth *dying* mid-verse rather than let it gutter twice.

"Maren!" Lena was shouting. "Maren, they're coming, we have to—"

"They'll be on us before we're off the flats." Maren's voice came steady across the water, and Dain marveled at it even as terror tore at him. Sixty years a fisherwoman. Storms, wrecks, a son gone under. She had made her peace with the sea long ago and simply extended the terms. "You take the score and the children and you *go*, girl. I'll hold the verse. A held verse is a wall, if the singer means it."

"That's not—you can't—"

"I've sung men out of drowning and I'll sing riders off this sand, or I'll learn why not." And then, softer, almost tender, aimed past Lena at all of them: "Go. This is my payment. Let me choose it."

The riders came down off the ridge.

Dain saw them clearly in the copper light: guild enforcers, twelve of them, chain-mail dull under oiled cloaks, crossbows levelled, moving in the wedge formation the guild used for taking singers alive—or killing them cleanly if taking proved inconvenient. Behind them, on the ridge, a lantern signal blinked twice. Confirmation. Somewhere a councilor was already spending his bribe.

The first bolt came out of the dark and buried itself in the sand a hand's width from Tam's foot.

Then the night broke open.

* * *

Later—much later, in the years afterward, on nights when sleep would not come—Dain would try to order the battle in his memory and fail, because battles do not order themselves; they only repeat. What repeated was this:

Orlin ringing the little handbell in fast, desperate figures, and the sound doing *something*—bolts veering wide, a horse screaming and pitching sideways as if the air had turned to honey around it. Not enough. One bell against twelve crossbows was a whisper against a shout, but Orlin rang anyway, tears freezing on his face, ringing like a man arguing with God.

Tam dragging Lena bodily backward, the score clutched to his chest, both of them stumbling through the shallows while bolts hissed past and the lantern fell and rolled, throwing huge shadows across the wet sand.

And Maren, at the center of the arches, singing.

She had raised the verse into something Dain had no name for. The tide came in around her ankles, her knees, her waist, and it came in *wrong*—came in fast and tall and shaped, a wall of black water standing between the enforcers and the shrine, trembling, held, the way a note is held. The horses refused it. The first rank of enforcers broke and scattered along the tideline, and for one blazing moment Dain believed—foolishly, humanly, the way he had believed at the border-stones—that it would be enough.

Then the crossbows found their range.

He never saw which bolt. There were too many, a stuttering rain of them, and the wall of water shivered as the singer beneath it shivered, and Maren's voice—which had bent tides for sixty years, which had pulled drowning men from the sea's own teeth, which had been the first warm thing Lena Sandoval had found in a strange world—Maren's voice climbed one last interval, higher than seemed possible, higher than the night deserved—

—and stopped.

The water fell all at once, a hundred tons of sea collapsing back into the sea, and the sound of it covered everything: the riders' shouts, Orlin's bell, Tam screaming, the whole world's grief compressed into a single thunderclap of ordinary ocean.

When the spray cleared, the old woman was kneeling in the shallows between the fourth and fifth arches, one hand still raised in the shape of the final note, and she was not breathing, and the pools' light was going out around her one window at a time, like a house settling into sleep.

* * *

They got her body off the flats because Maren would have haunted them otherwise. Dain carried her himself and let no one argue; Tam and Lena walked on either side with their hands under the old woman's arms, and Orlin walked behind ringing the handbell softly, one slow figure over and over, a fisher's tolling for one of their own going out to the deep burial.

The enforcers did not pursue. Dain kept waiting for it—for the wedge to reform, for the orders that would finish the job—but the riders drew back to the ridge and held there, torches burning, watching them carry the dead woman up the beach. Watching. Counting. Reporting. Somewhere on the other side of the world, a master of the Guild of Impossible Things was learning that the first singer was spent, and adjusting his accounts.

At the top of the tideline, in the lee of the fallen arch, they laid her down with dignity, and Lena knelt and closed the old woman's eyes with two fingers, very gently, the way you close a book you intend to open again.

"She held the verse," Lena said. Her voice was wrecked, scraped raw. "Did you hear it? Right up until—" She couldn't finish.

"I heard it," Dain said.

"She paid a memory for the first attempt. Her son. She paid her *son*, and tonight she paid—" Lena's hands curled into fists against her thighs. "Corvane sent them. Corvane bought a councilor and sent twelve men to murder a grandmother on a beach, and the council *let* them, and we sang anyway, and it wasn't enough, it's never—"

"Lena." Tam touched her shoulder. "Breathe."

"No." She stood, and Dain watched something happen in her face that frightened him more than the crossbows had: the grief didn't leave, it *condensed*. It went from weather to iron. "No. We don't breathe. We don't rest. We find the next singer tomorrow, and the next, and we finish the verse she died holding, or every drop of her blood on that sand is just—waste. And I will not let it be waste."

Orlin lowered the bell. The old man's face was wet and hollow. "There's a boatman at Grayharbor," he said hoarsely. "Used to sing the anchor-hymns before I—before. If he lives, he sings."

"Then at dawn we go to Grayharbor," Lena said.

* * *

Dain stood the last watch alone, because he asked for it and no one had the strength to refuse him.

The fire had burned to coals. Maren lay wrapped in sailcloth under the fallen arch, and the sea worked at the shore below in the dark, patient, enormous, indifferent, and Dain sat with his back against cold stone and finally let himself feel the full weight of what he had done.

Because here was the truth he had carried up from the flats along with her body: he had known. He had seen the courier marks, smelled the pipe-smoke, read the trap written in good boots on soft ground—and he had said *clear*, and his silence had put Maren between the crossbows and the children. Grief as fuel, Lena had called it once, speaking of the war. But his grief was older than fuel. It was ballast. It was the thing that sank him, again and again, in exactly the same water.

He could tell her. Tomorrow, on the road to Grayharbor, he could kneel in the dirt and confess it: the prints, the wax, the smoke, the word *clear* that had come out of his mouth like a coin from a counterfeiter's purse. She would forgive him—she had forgiven worse; she had forgiven *him*, specifically, for the eighth singer, and forgiveness from Lena Sandoval was not a gift but a discipline, renewed daily, costing her something every time.

And that was precisely why he couldn't. Because her forgiveness was a discipline, and disciplines can be exhausted, and he had watched her spend herself for six days the way Maren had spent herself on the flats, and he would not be one more withdrawal against an account already bleeding.

Instead he did the only penance available to a man who cannot undo: he planned. Grayharbor, the boatman, the anchor-hymns. The routes Corvane's enforcers would watch and the ones they wouldn't. The way he would walk point from now on, always, so that the next betrayal—if there was a next betrayal—would reach him first, and kill him first, and take no one else.

The coals settled. The sea breathed. Under the sailcloth, Maren kept whatever silence the drowned keep.

Near dawn, Lena stirred in her blankets and looked at him across the dead fire, and for a moment—in the gray half-light, with her eyes swollen and her jaw set—he thought she was going to ask him something. Something with weight in it. Her gaze rested on him a long time, unreadable, the keeper of ledgers measuring a column of numbers that would not balance.

"Dain," she said at last, quietly. "Who told you the approaches were clear?"

The question hung in the cold air between them, and the sea filled the silence behind it, and Dain Ash—who had lied to generals and usurpers and masters of impossible things, who had lied so long and so well that lying was simply the grain of him—looked at the woman who had given him a second chance he had not earned, opened his mouth, and discovered he had no idea which truth was survivable.

Far down the beach, on the ridge where the enforcers had stood, a single lantern blinked twice in the pre-dawn dark.

Signal received. Signal answered.
