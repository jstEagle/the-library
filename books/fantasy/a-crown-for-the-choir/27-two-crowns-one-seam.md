## 27. Two Crowns, One Seam

The guildhall of the Impossible Things had always smelled, to Malvane, of candlewax and cowardice.

He stood in its great receiving chamber now, boots on marble that a hundred years of masters had polished smooth with their careful, dishonest feet, and he let himself feel the old contempt rise like smoke. He had walked these halls as a student once—no, not these halls; their mirror in Vael, the archive at Bellhollow before it burned—but the architecture was the same, because the guild built itself twice, once on each shore of the seam, and lied identically in both languages.

Corvane knelt where the enforcers had put him. Not bound—the man was past seventy and had surrendered his ledger without a fight—but kneeling all the same, hands flat on his thighs, spine straight, the posture of a clerk awaiting an audit. Even now, even here, he looked like a man who believed the numbers would save him.

"You came quickly," Corvane said. His voice was steady. That was the thing about guildmasters; they could keep their voices level while the world drowned. "I expected you to take another week."

"I have been coming for thirty years," Malvane said. "You simply failed to notice until tonight."

Around them, the guild's hoard lay open to the air for the first time in living memory. The vault beneath the receiving chamber had taken four hours to breach—not because the locks were strong but because the tribute-power inside resisted being seen, the way deep water resists light. Now it filled the room like weather: crates of sealed song-glass humming faintly in their straw, jars of preserved echo, ribbons of sound coiled on spools and still faintly warm, and at the center, on a plinth of black stone, the great massed weight of a century's withheld payments—a slow golden murmur, almost subsonic, that Malvane felt in his back teeth and behind his eyes.

It was beautiful. He would give the guild that much. They had stolen beautifully.

"Do you know what this is?" he asked Corvane, gesturing at the plinth.

"Tribute," Corvane said. "Payments owed under the covenant."

"Say it plainer. You're among thieves now; use the honest word." Malvane crossed to the plinth and laid his palm against the stone. The murmur rose to meet him, eager, starved, like a dog that has waited by a door for decades. "It is magic. Raw, unspent, unclaimed. Every impossible trade your guild ever brokered—a memory sold, a shadow pawned, a name traded across the seam—it passed through your ledgers, and instead of crossing over to Vael where it belonged, you skimmed it. You skimmed it for a hundred years." He turned. "My world is dying of thirst, Master Corvane, and you have been drinking from the well and calling the drought an act of prophecy."

"The payments stopped because the covenant was failing," Corvane said. "The seam was narrowing. The trades were becoming unstable. We made a decision—"

"We. There it is. The most expensive word in any language." Malvane smiled, and saw the old man flinch at it, and was glad. "How many sat in the room when you decided? How many names do I need?"

Corvane said nothing. His eyes went, briefly, to the empty space beside him—to the place where Tam should have been kneeling, if the boy had been caught, if the ledger had been recovered—and Malvane read the glance as easily as print. The apprentice had run. The second ledger was loose somewhere in the world, in the hands of a girl who sang, or near them.

It did not matter anymore. Let them carry their little book of truths from city to city like beggars carrying a bowl. Truth was slow. Power was fast. And he was about to become very fast indeed.

* * *

They worked through the night in the vault's antechamber, which the guild had once used for the appraisal of impossible goods and which Malvane now used for the appraisal of a kingdom.

His artificers—three of them, gaunt men and women from the ash-country who had learned their craft binding grief into iron—unpacked the tribute in stages, each stage tested, each test logged. Malvane insisted on logging. He had been a student of the ninth singer; he had learned from her that power without record was just noise, and noise was the one thing he refused to be.

The first crate held song-glass: forty panes, each one holding a single note that some desperate seller in the free cities had parted with decades ago. When the artificers cracked the seals, the notes escaped in a thin bright chorus and hung in the antechamber like trapped birds. One of the artificers wept without seeming to know she was doing it. Malvane watched the notes circle, watched them strain toward the ceiling, toward the seam he could not see but could feel pressing down on the world like a bruise, and he thought: *this is what they kept from us. This is what my mother's village starved without.*

The second stage was worse. The echoes—preserved voices, fragments of lives, the residue of memories sold across the seam—did not want to be handled. They whispered. They called out names. One of them, a woman's voice, young and frightened, repeated a phrase over and over in a language Malvane recognized as the low dialect of the Errenfell marshes: *I didn't mean to sell her, I didn't mean to sell her.* An echo of a father who had traded something he could not get back.

"Whose is this?" Malvane asked.

The artificer consulted the guild's own inventory tags, still knotted to the jar. "Sold to the guild forty-one years ago. Origin: Vael side. It was never delivered back across the seam."

Of course it wasn't. It had gone into the vault, into the hoard, into the slow golden murmur on the plinth. Somewhere in the marshes of his homeland there was a family missing a daughter—or a daughter missing a father's love, or whatever precise shape of absence that echo represented—and the guild had filed it under *acquisitions* and locked the door.

"Keep it," Malvane said quietly. "Keep all of them. Every voice goes into the crown."

*The crown.*

He had not spoken the word aloud yet, not to anyone, but it had been forming in him for years the way a pearl forms around grit. He had seen it first in a dream after Bellhollow fell—no, not a dream, a memory wearing a dream's clothes: his teacher, the ninth singer, standing before the mural of the nine with her hand pressed to the scratched-out face, telling him, *the covenant was never meant to be sung by nine alone, child. It was meant to be sung by everyone. That is why it fails. That is why it will always fail.* And then she had fled, and left him alone with the failure, and he had spent thirty years learning what she would not teach him: how to make a covenant that needed no one else. A covenant of one. A song so complete it required no choir.

A crown that sang.

* * *

Toward dawn, Corvane was brought up from the cell they'd given him—he'd asked for a desk instead, and been refused—and made to stand before the nearly finished work.

It did not look like much yet. That was the nature of the craft: the crown was mostly absence, a circlet of black iron open at the front, its band set with sockets where the song-glass would sit, its inner surface engraved with the covenant score copied from the original manuscript they had seized along with the guildhall. The artificers had poured the tribute-power into the iron the way smiths pour bronze, and the metal drank it and grew heavy in a way that had nothing to do with weight. Two strong men had carried it up from the forge and set it down, and both of them had stepped away from it afterward as if from a sleeping animal.

"It will hold?" Malvane asked.

The eldest artificer, a woman named Sera whose arms were scarred from a lifetime of binding things that did not wish to be bound, hesitated. He had noticed, over the years, that she only hesitated when the answer was yes but the truth was larger than the question.

"It will hold," she said. "But hear me, lord. This is not a choir. A choir argues with itself—that is what keeps it alive. Nine voices pulling against each other, correcting, forgiving. What you've built is one voice wearing nine masks. It will sing the covenant perfectly, because it cannot sing anything else. But perfect is not the same as whole." She wiped her hands on her apron. "And the price. You know the price. The singers paid in memories. The keeper pays in belonging. What does a crown pay?"

Malvane looked at the circlet on its stand, at the faint golden light moving beneath the iron like blood beneath skin.

"A crown pays in everything it rules," he said. "That is the bargain. That has always been the bargain."

Sera bowed and withdrew, and Malvane was alone with Corvane and the crown and the gray light beginning at the high windows.

"She's right, you know," Corvane said.

"I know she's right. I have known for thirty years. That has never once stopped me."

The old master moved closer, slowly, the way men move near horses they don't trust. Up close, in the gray light, Malvane could see how much the night had cost him—the tremor in his hands, the wet brightness in his eyes that he was too disciplined to let fall. Good. Let him see it. Let him carry the sight of it back to whatever cell they gave him tonight.

"When I was young," Corvane said, "I served under Master Ellery, who helped write the policy of suspension. The stopping of the payments. I asked him once—only once, I was young—*why*. Why break an oath that held two worlds together. Do you know what he said?"

"Tell me."

"He said: *because a covenant shared is a covenant halved, and a crown shared is no crown at all.*" Corvane's mouth twisted. "I thought he was being wise. I wrote it down in my first ledger. I have spent fifty years understanding that he was not being wise. He was describing his own appetite and dressing it as law." The old man looked up, and his voice finally broke its level line, cracked down the middle like river ice in spring. "You are about to put on his sentence, boy. I can see it. You think you are avenging Vael. You are not. You are becoming the last and greatest of us—the final master of the Guild of Impossible Things, the one who took the whole hoard for himself. We built this. Every year we skimmed, every payment we withheld, every lie we entered in fair hand—we were building your crown for you, and we did not know it, and that is the worst thing I know."

The silence after that was long. Outside, the garrison drums changed watch. Somewhere below, a prisoner—Tam's replacement, one of the ledger-clerks—was singing under his breath to keep his courage up, badly, a harbor song, and the sound drifted up the stairwell and touched the crown and made it hum in answer, one clear note, hungry.

Malvane lifted the circlet from its stand.

It weighed nothing. That was the first surprise. All that stolen century, all that hoarded power, and in his hands it was light as a wreath of rushes—as if the weight had gone somewhere else, into the seam perhaps, into the world's wound, adding itself to the pressure there.

The second surprise was that he hesitated.

He had not planned to hesitate. He had rehearsed this moment in the dark of a thousand camps: the lifting, the setting, the closing of his hands on a century of theft and the opening of his mouth to sing. But now, with the iron cool against his palms and the guildmaster watching him with those ruined, honest eyes, he found himself standing in the doorway of his teacher's empty study again, thirty years younger, reading the note she had left pinned to the wall above her abandoned scores:

*A covenant of one is a cage with the singer inside it.*

She had known. Of course she had known. She had fled not from the war, not from him, but from this—from the exact shape of the thing he was holding, which she had foreseen the way prophets foresee, hating every line of it.

*I am not her,* Malvane told the morning. *I am not running anywhere.*

He set the crown on his head.

* * *

The world did not end. That was the first thing he noticed, and it disappointed him more than he expected.

What happened instead was quieter and far stranger. The covenant score engraved inside the band woke against his skin, and the tribute-power rose through the iron and into him, and Malvane opened his mouth and sang the first verse of the covenant alone.

He had a good voice. He had always had a good voice; his teacher had said so, once, in the years when she still praised things. But a good voice was nothing against what the crown gave him. The moment the first note left his throat, the nine masks woke—all the voices in all the glass, all the echoes in all the jars, the father from the marshes and the hundred other sellers and the long dead chorus of the original nine, sampled, stored, enslaved—and they sang *with* him. Through him. Around him, filling the antechamber, filling the guildhall, pouring out the windows into the gray morning air of the free cities' capital, where sentries on the walls dropped their spears and clutched their ears and then, horribly, began to smile.

Because it was beautiful. That was the trap of it. The covenant of one was beautiful the way a glacier is beautiful, the way a drawn blade is beautiful. It harmonized with itself perfectly because it permitted no disagreement. Every voice in it bent toward his. Every note arrived exactly where he placed it. He could feel the seam of the world from here—a vast taut line running through the morning, through the border-stones, through the drowned shrine at Errenfell and the mountain shrines and the broken archive of Bellhollow—and he could press on it, gently, the way a man presses a thumb to a harp string, and feel the whole instrument of creation lean toward his hand.

*This is what she would not give me,* he thought, singing, exultant, sick. *This is what the choir was for. Not to share the burden. To share the reach.*

He sang the second verse, and the rivers of Vael, three days' ride beyond the seam, reversed their courses and ran clean for the first time in a decade. He sang the third, and the blight retreating from the ash-country fields curled back like a burned hand withdrawing from water. He sang the fourth—

—and the fourth verse required an answer. That was the structure of the covenant; he had copied the score himself; he knew it better than anyone alive. Each verse was a call, and the covenant only lived in the response. Nine verses, nine calls, and for a hundred years the responses had come from nine throats scattered across two worlds, imperfect, arguing, alive.

The crown supplied the answers. Of course it did. The masks answered him in perfect time, in perfect pitch, in perfect obedience, and the covenant closed each verse like a lock clicking shut, and the seam steadied beneath his song like a fever breaking.

And it was dead.

He heard it in the fifth verse. He heard it the way a man hears the difference between a forest and a recording of a forest. The answering voices were flawless and they were empty—they were echoes singing their own captivity, glass singing its imprisonment, and there was no one on the other end of the song, no weary fisherwoman bending tides, no disgraced bell-ringer, no apprentice holding harmony, no keeper choosing. Just him, multiplied. Just his own voice, returned to him from every direction, agreeing with everything.

A covenant of one is a cage with the singer inside it.

He stopped singing.

The silence that followed was enormous. In it, he could hear Sera breathing in the corner, and the prisoners below, and the city waking outside the walls, and beneath all of it the seam—steadied, stabilized, held, exactly as he had promised his armies it would be held. He had done it. The war was functionally over. The free cities would kneel within the month; their council was already fracturing; grief did that to alliances, and he had arranged for plenty of grief.

He had everything.

He stood in the gray light with a century of stolen power singing silently against his skull, and he waited to feel what he had imagined he would feel: the vindication, the arrival, the moment when thirty years of ash and marching and burying friends would resolve at last into meaning.

He felt the iron, cool on his brow. He felt the masks, waiting. He felt the seam, taut as a wire, holding because he held it—and would hold only as long as he sang, only as long as he stood here, only as long as he never slept, never faltered, never died. The covenant of one required one. Forever. There was no verse for putting it down. His teacher had designed none, because she had understood that the only safe covenant was one that could survive its singer, and he had built the opposite, deliberately, out of spite, and now he wore it.

Somewhere out there, the girl with the ledger was gathering the real thing. Scraps of a true choir. Imperfect voices that argued and forgave. He had sent heralds to offer her a place at his right hand and riders to burn the places she slept, and both gestures had been the same gesture, and he knew that now, standing crowned in the ruins of his enemy's house: everything he had done since Bellhollow was a letter to a woman who had abandoned him, written in fire, and she had never once written back.

"Lord?" Sera, softly. "The armies await your order. The council at Merrenport meets at noon. If you mean to send terms—"

Terms. Yes. He would send terms, and the terms would be generous, because generosity cost him nothing now; he could compel compliance with a verse. He opened his mouth to dictate them, and what came out, before he could stop it, before the crown caught it and smoothed it into command, was a single raw syllable of the ninth singer's name.

The masks echoed it back to him in nine-part harmony, sweet and hollow as bells underwater.

Malvane straightened. He let the crown settle its weight—which was nothing, which was everything—more firmly on his brow, and he gave the orders in a voice like leveled stone: terms to Merrenport, envoys to the fractured council, a doubled watch on the border-stones, and hunters—quiet ones, patient ones—sent after a ledger-keeper, a courier, a bell-ringer, and a boy.

Let the choir come. He would show them what their beloved covenant sounded like when it was sung properly.

But that night, alone in the guildmaster's tower with the city lights below and the seam a faint gold thread on the horizon, he took off the crown and set it on the sill, and he did not sleep, and he did not sing, and in the morning the first reports came in from the west: the free cities' council had voted to negotiate—except one delegate, a harbor girl, who had walked out in the middle of the session, and no one could find her, and someone said she had gone down to the fleet.
