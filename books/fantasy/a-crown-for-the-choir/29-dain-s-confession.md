## 29. Dain's Confession

The camp at Errenfell's edge smelled of wet ash and salt.

They had made it only a few miles from the drowned shrine before exhaustion took them—Maren's death still sitting in their throats like a stone none of them could swallow. Orlin had built the fire without being asked, his hands steady in the way of a man who had learned that work was the only prayer he trusted. Tam sat wrapped in a blanket too large for her, Corvane's second ledger open on her knees though she hadn't turned a page in an hour. Sef had taken the first watch along the road, and the distant sound of her boots on gravel was the only clock any of them needed.

Lena sat apart, as she had every night since the shrine, turning Maren's tide-bone pendant over in her fingers. The cord was still damp. It would be damp forever, she suspected; some things refused to dry because drying would mean they were finished happening.

She heard Dain's footsteps before she saw him—the uneven rhythm of them, left foot landing a half-beat heavier than the right, a cadence she knew now the way she knew the guildhall bells. He came around the fire's edge carrying two cups of something steaming and bitter-smelling, and he set one beside her without a word before lowering himself onto the fallen log across from her.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The fire cracked and settled. Somewhere down the valley, water moved through channels it no longer remembered how to fill.

"You haven't slept," he said finally.

"Neither have you."

"No." He looked into his cup. "I don't expect to tonight either. Not after what I have to tell you."

Lena's hand went still around the pendant. She had spent enough weeks on this side of the seam to know the weight of a sentence like that—to feel it land in the chest before the meaning even arrived. "Then tell me."

He didn't answer at once. Instead he pulled back the sleeve of his left arm, the one she had never seen bared, the one he kept wrapped even in summer heat, even when they'd waded the cold shallows below Bellhollow and everyone else had stripped to the skin. The bandage came away in slow turns. Beneath it, inked from wrist to elbow in the style she had seen on Malvane's soldiers—on the herald, on the riders who burned Bellhollow—was a brand. A crown split by a seam. The mark of the usurper's service.

"I served him," Dain said. "Before he was the usurper. Before there was anything to usurp. I carried his messages. I scouted for him. I believed in him." His voice did not shake, and somehow that was worse than if it had. "And then he asked me to do something, and I did it, and I have been running from it ever since."

The fire popped. Lena became aware, distantly, of Tam watching from beneath her blanket—not pretending anymore, just watching, the way you watch a bridge you may soon need to cross.

"What did he ask?" Lena asked. Her voice came out flat. Careful. She had learned to be careful with questions on this side of the world; answers here had teeth.

Dain met her eyes. That, at least, she would grant him—he didn't look away, didn't soften it, didn't arrange his face into anything but the truth.

"The eighth singer," he said. "Her name was Ilset Vane. She kept a waystation in the Thornwood, tending one of the old shrines. She was the last of the nine still singing openly. Malvane wanted her found before she could reach the others. So he sent me—because I was good at finding people, and because I was the last person anyone would suspect of serving him." He paused. "I led the guild's hunters to her door."

Lena felt the words arrive one at a time, each one landing like a stone dropped into deep water.

"The *guild's* hunters," she repeated.

"That's the part I couldn't say sooner." He set down his cup. His hands, she noticed, were not quite steady. "It wasn't Malvane's men who took her, Lena. It was ours. Yours. Guild enforcers, wearing guild grey, carrying guild writs. Malvane didn't send soldiers to silence the eighth singer. He sent word to your masters—and your masters sent their own. Because by then the payments had stopped, and a singer still singing was evidence. Proof of what the covenant had been. Proof of what they'd stolen." He drew a breath. "I told myself I was only pointing the way. I told myself the guild would only question her. They dragged her out of the shrine at dawn, and I stood in the treeline and watched, and I did nothing. She looked at me. Right at me. And she *smiled*, Lena. Like she pitied me. Like she already knew everything I've just spent twenty years trying not to know about myself."

The pendant bit into Lena's palm. She realized she was gripping it hard enough to leave its shape in her skin.

"You said you waited years for me," she said quietly. "At the meadow. You said you'd been waiting for the ledger-keeper to come through."

"I had penance to pay," Dain said. "And the ledger-keeper was where the penance led. Every road I walked away from that shrine circled back to the guild. Back to the gate. Back to whoever would come through it next." He laughed, short and joyless. "I told myself that when you arrived, I'd confess everything on the first night. Then the first week. Then—I don't know. There was never a moment that wasn't needed for something else. There was always another shrine, another singer, another mile. And every mile I didn't tell you, the telling got heavier, until it weighed more than the secret ever had."

"Why now?"

"Because Maren is dead." The words came out of him like something torn rather than spoken. "Because she died holding a line so the rest of us could run, and she died believing in all of us, and I sat by that fire tonight and realized that if we fall—if Malvane wins—it won't be because the prophecy failed or the song was too hard. It'll be because I built this choir on a foundation of rot and let you all stand on it. You can't fight a war on a lie, Lena. I've tried. I was *in* that war. I know exactly how it ends."

Tam had risen at some point without Lena noticing. She stood at the fire's edge, the ledger hugged against her chest, her face pale in the orange light. "Ilset Vane," she said. "That name is in the second ledger. Page forty-one. 'The eighth voice: contained.'" Her voice cracked on the last word. "Corvane wrote *contained*. Like she was a spill. Like she was a fire."

"She was a woman," Dain said. "She sang her son to sleep the night before they took her. I heard it from the trees. I've been trying to remember the melody ever since, and I can't, and I think that might be the cruelest thing about all of it—that even my guilt can't hold onto the one part of her worth keeping."

No one spoke. Down the valley, Sef's boots had gone quiet on the gravel, which meant she was listening too, which meant by morning the whole camp would carry this, and Lena understood that Dain knew it, and had chosen it anyway. Confession in front of witnesses. Not because he wanted absolution—but because he wanted *witnesses*. Because a sin confessed alone stays a secret, and a sin confessed aloud becomes a debt the whole company shares the burden of collecting.

Lena stood up.

Dain rose with her, and she saw him brace—not for a blow, exactly, but for whatever justice she decided he deserved. She thought of the meadow, the first day, his scarred hands pulling her out of the uphill river. She thought of Bellhollow burning and Dain screaming into the smoke, digging through embers bare-handed for books that weren't people but mattered like they were. She thought of Orlin saying, once, drunk and honest in a mountain tavern: *that man carries more graves than a plague pit, and he dug every one himself.*

"You lied to me," she said.

"Yes."

"Not just by omission. You let me trust you. You let me build—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through anyway. "Everything I have here is built on you. You were the first face I saw on this side of the world. When the gate rejected me, you're the one who talked me off the edge of going mad. And the whole time, you were—"

"A traitor," he said. "Yes. To him first, and then to you. I've been a traitor so long I barely remember what the other thing feels like."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended. "Don't make it easy. Don't hand me a neat little story where you were always secretly good. You served him. You served the guild. You served yourself, same as Corvane, same as every man who ever chained a ledger to his wrist and called it duty." She was shaking now, and let herself shake. "Maren is dead. Orlin lost half his hearing to those riders. Tam crossed a collapsing wayshrine alone because the family that raised me chose empire over oath. And you knew. You knew what the guild was, you knew what Malvane was, and you let me walk into both blind."

"I did."

"And I am *furious*." She heard her own voice break clean in half on the word, fury and grief coming out together the way they always did, indistinguishable, twins. "I'm furious and I'm tired and tomorrow we march toward the seam's heart with three singers and a fleet and no ninth verse and no plan that doesn't end with someone paying a price I can't yet name. And you pick *tonight* to tell me this."

"There's no good night," Dain said softly. "There's only the night you stop choosing the lie."

* * *

She walked.

Not away—she told herself it wasn't away, it was *along*, along the black ribbon of the old Errenfell road with the dead city's towers silhouetted against a sky full of stars that didn't belong to her world. But her feet chose the direction opposite the campfire, and she let them, because some part of her understood that if she stayed seated across from him one more minute she would say something that couldn't be unsaid, and she had buried enough people this month without burying a friend too.

The road was cracked and furred with moss. In the ruins on either side, doors stood open on rooms where families had left meals half-eaten a century ago. This was what a broken covenant looked like when you zoomed close enough: not armies, not crowns. Just doors no one closed. Just cups left on tables.

She heard him follow her. Of course he did. He kept a respectful distance—ten paces, maybe twelve—and when she finally stopped at a crumbling wayshrine whose basin held nothing but rainwater and leaves, he stopped too, and waited.

"You followed the hunters to Ilset's shrine," Lena said, without turning. "Did you follow them after? Did you find out what happened to her?"

A long silence. "She's alive."

Lena turned. "What?"

"They didn't kill her. Containment, Tam said—Corvane's word. They took her across, sealed her somewhere in the guild's deep vaults. I found out two years later, from a drunk enforcer in a border town who'd been on the raid and liked to talk when he drank. He said she was still singing when they locked her up. Said it was the worst thing he'd ever heard, a woman singing lullabies to a stone cell." Dain's jaw worked. "If she lives—if the vaults still hold her—then she's the eighth voice, Lena. Not scattered. *Contained.* Reachable."

Hope arrived in Lena's chest like a knife sliding between ribs. She hated it. She hated that he had known this, held this, and let her grieve over a mural of scratched-out faces while the answer sat behind his silence.

"You could have led with that," she whispered.

"If I'd led with that, you'd have forgiven me by breakfast, and the forgiveness would have been for the wrong reason." He stepped closer, into the starlight, and she saw that his eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and utterly unflinching. "You deserve to be angry at me for what I did, not grateful to me for what I know. Those are different debts. I won't let them cancel out."

"You don't get to manage my feelings, Dain Ash."

"No," he agreed. "I don't. I just get to stand here while you have them."

And that—gods help her—that was the thing that undid her a little. Not the confession, not the brand, not even the news of Ilset Vane alive in a vault. It was the standing there. The utter refusal to defend himself, to narrate his own redemption, to make his guilt her project. Corvane would have spun. Malvane would have made it a tragedy with himself at the center. Dain just stood in the ruins of his own making and let her look.

"I don't forgive you," Lena said. "Not tonight. Maybe not tomorrow. I want you to understand that this isn't a scene in a story where the hero says the words and the music swells and everything is mended. What you broke doesn't mend. Ilset Vane spent years in a cell because of you. Maren died on a road you helped make dangerous. I will carry the knowledge of what you did for the rest of this war, and probably the rest of my life, and some mornings I will look at you and feel sick, and I need you to accept that."

"I accept it."

"But." She held up a hand before relief could move into his face. "*But*—I choose to keep walking beside you. Today. Tomorrow I'll choose again. And the day after that. Not because you've earned it—you haven't, not yet, maybe not ever—but because I need every sword and every voice and every pair of guilty, stubborn hands I can get, and because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because belonging isn't innocence. I used to think it was. I grew up in a guild that told me family meant never asking where the money came from. I know better now. Belonging is a thing you rebuild every single day, out of truth, or it isn't real. So this is me rebuilding. Badly. Angrily. One day at a time. Take it or leave it."

Dain's breath went out of him slowly, like a man setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it had weight.

"One day at a time," he repeated. "I can live in that. I've lived in worse."

"I know," Lena said. "That's rather the problem."

But something in her chest had loosened—a knot not untied, exactly, but acknowledged, named, given a place to sit. She turned back toward the road, toward the campfire's far-off glow, and after a moment he fell into step beside her, ten paces closing to five, five closing to two, the distance of two people who had decided, provisionally and without guarantees, to keep being a *we*.

* * *

They were nearly back to the fire when Sef materialized out of the dark, falling into step on Lena's other side with the particular stride of someone who had been waiting for exactly this moment and intended to pretend she hadn't.

"So," Sef said. "The courier's a former crown-man. Good to know. Should I reassign his watch?"

"He keeps his watch," Lena said.

"Lena—"

"*He keeps his watch,* Sef." She stopped and faced the young delegate squarely. In the firelight behind her, she could see Orlin and Tam both on their feet now, the whole camp assembled into a silent tribunal none of them had planned. Fine. Let it happen in the open. Everything else in her life had. "Here's what I've learned since I fell through that gate. Every institution I trusted was lying to me. The guild. The council. Half the prophecies. If I start demanding spotless hands, I'll end up marching to the seam alone, and alone is exactly what Malvane wants. Dain lied. Dain confessed. Dain will spend the rest of his life paying for it, starting with the fact that none of us will ever quite look at him the same way again—including me. That's not mercy. That's arithmetic. We are three singers short of a covenant and I don't get to throw away voices because they come with scars."

Sef studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she smiled—tired and crooked. "You sound like an admiral."

"I sound like a ledger-keeper. Debts stay on the page. They don't get erased. They get *paid*." She glanced at Dain. "Yours is going to take a lifetime. I hope you live long enough."

"It's the only thing I'm sure I want," he said.

Orlin snorted from the fireside. "Sentimental fool. Sit down, both of you, the stew's burning." But his voice was rough in the way it got when he was feeling things he'd rather drink than name, and he ladled out four bowls instead of three, and pressed the fourth into Dain's hands with a force that was almost an accusation and almost an absolution and was, Lena suspected, both.

They ate. The fire burned down. Tam fell asleep mid-sentence over the ledger, her cheek on Corvane's treasonous handwriting, and Orlin draped a tarp over her without waking her, and Sef took the middle watch humming a harbor shanty under her breath—four notes that Lena, without thinking, harmonized, and then froze, because the harmony had come from somewhere deeper than decision.

From the choir, maybe. Already growing in her.

Later—much later, when the camp slept and only the coals breathed—Dain appeared at the edge of her bedroll and crouched, keeping his voice low.

"There's something else," he said. "About Ilset Vane. About where they kept her."

Lena sat up. "Tell me."

"The enforcer who talked—the drunk one—he said the vault she was in wasn't in the guildhall proper. It was under the *gate*. Under the sealed gate you came through. He said the singers weren't just prisoners down there." Dain's face was ash-grey in the dying light. "He said they were *anchors*. That the guild sealed the eighth voice into the foundations of the door itself, and that's why your gate opened for you, Lena. That's why it sang your name. The door has been trying to finish the covenant from the inside for twenty years—and the only person it could call was the granddaughter of the woman who wrote the tenth verse."

The cold that moved through Lena then had nothing to do with the night.

The gate. Her gate. The door she had oiled and inspected and loved since childhood, humming under her palms every evening like a sleeping animal—and all the while, sealed inside its bones, a woman singing lullabies to the dark.

"When do we march?" Lena asked.

Dain looked at her, and for the first time all night, something that was almost hope crossed his ruined face.

"At dawn," he said. "The fleet sails with the tide."
