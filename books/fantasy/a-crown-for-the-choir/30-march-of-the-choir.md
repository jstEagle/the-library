## 30. March of the Choir

Tam had counted the bells of the guildhall for eleven years. He knew their voices the way he knew his own heartbeat—the dawn bell with its cracked note that no one ever fixed, the noon bell that rang flat in summer, the midnight bell that Master Corvane had once told him was tuned to a pitch only the dead could hear. Counting bells had been a comfort. A rhythm. Proof that the world turned on schedule and that someone, somewhere, was keeping the accounts.

There were no bells here. Only the wind coming off the salt marshes, and beneath it—so faint he might have imagined it—a hum that lived in the ground itself. The seam's heart was three days' march away, and it was already singing.

Tam walked at the back of the column and tried not to think about how much his feet hurt.

* * *

They had set out from Errenfell at first light, forty-one souls and one cart. The cart carried Orlin's bells—seven of them now, salvaged from the mountain shrines, wrapped in wool and rope and Maren's old fishing nets, because Maren had insisted before she died that bells traveled better when they remembered the sea. No one had argued with her ghost about it. They simply did as she'd said.

Lena walked at the front. She always walked at the front now, though Tam could see what it cost her—the way her shoulders sat too straight, the way she checked over her left shoulder every hundred paces, as if expecting the coal-chutes of the guildhall to open behind her and pour out enforcers. She hadn't slept more than two hours at a stretch since Errenfell. None of them had, really. But grief kept its own hours, and Lena's grief wore armor.

Dain walked beside her, close enough to catch her if she stumbled, far enough to pretend he wasn't watching. Tam had watched them for days now, the way he watched everything—quietly, from the edges, counting. Dain never touched her. He simply arranged himself so that touching would be possible, should she ever need it. Tam thought there was something in that arrangement more honest than most vows he'd heard spoken.

Orlin rode the cart, because his knees were shot and his hands were the only hands allowed to touch the bells. He'd been sober nine days. His skin had gone gray as ash and twice as fragile-looking, and every mile he sweated through another shirt, but his eyes—his eyes had come back from wherever they'd been living these twenty years. When he looked at the bells, he looked at them the way Tam used to look at Corvane: like they were the last true thing in a false world.

And Sef—

Sef marched at the column's head beside Lena, young and loud and impossible, arguing strategy with a woman who had crossed between worlds while Sef was still memorizing harbor charts. Behind them, strung along the coast road like beads on a wet string, came the fleet-borne chorus: thirty-three sailors, fishers, and one retired tide-pilot who claimed to have once sung a storm into standing still. They weren't trained singers. Half of them couldn't hold a tune in a bucket, as Orlin had said the first night, spitting into the fire. But they could hold each other. And Maren had taught them, in the weeks before Errenfell, the first thread of the covenant score—the anchor line, the part that didn't require beauty, only endurance.

Tam walked behind all of them and carried the second ledger chained to nothing now. He'd broken the chain himself, in the guildhall dark, with a coal-chute poker. Some nights he still felt its weight on his wrist, phantom-heavy, like a scar that hadn't decided to form yet.

"Tam." Dain had dropped back without Tam noticing—which was itself alarming, because noticing things was Tam's whole purpose. "You're limping."

"I'm walking."

"You're limping *while* walking."

"Then I'm doing both efficiently." Tam adjusted the ledger under his arm. "Save your concern for the bells. If we lose one in a marsh, I don't think Orlin survives it."

Dain studied him a moment longer. He had that courier's habit of looking at people as if calculating distance—not to them, but around them, mapping escape routes that included or excluded them. Tam had learned not to take it personally. Mostly.

"The scouts say Malvane's vanguard is a day behind us," Dain said quietly. "Maybe less. They're not hurrying."

"Why aren't they hurrying?"

"Because he doesn't think we can do it." Dain's jaw tightened. "And because he wants us at the seam. All of us. Every voice." He paused. "He wants the choir gathered in one place, Tam. Think about what that means."

Tam thought about it. He thought about it all through the gray afternoon, as the marsh gave way to stunted pines and the pines gave way to bare black rock, and the hum in the ground grew from a whisper to a murmur to something you could feel in your teeth. Malvane wanted them gathered. Which meant either the prophecy was wrong about what ten voices could do—or Malvane believed he could unmake ten voices faster than they could sing.

Both possibilities made Tam's stomach fold itself into neat ledger-columns of dread.

* * *

They camped that night in the shadow of a wayshrine—one of the old ones, pre-covenant, its stone worn smooth by centuries of weather that had stopped mattering when the realms were stitched together. The shrine's arch still stood, though the door within it was only darkness now, dead since the payments stopped. Tam ran his fingers along the carvings while the others made camp. Interlaced figures. Two rivers braiding into one. At the apex, a small worn shape that might have been a person standing with arms outstretched, holding the arch up from inside.

*A keeper who chooses,* the grandmother's verse had said. Tam copied the words again in his head, the way he'd memorized guard rotations in a cell, the way he'd memorized everything he'd ever needed to survive. He was good at holding things. It was the one talent the guild had given him that turned out to be worth anything.

"You're the one who found the verse," said a voice behind him.

Sef stood there with two bowls of the evening's barley-and-saltwater mush, which tasted like punishment but was hot, and handed one over. Young delegate of the free cities, pledgee of the harbor fleet, owner of a voice that could carry across a council chamber and, apparently, across a battlefield. Tam took the bowl and nodded.

"Lena found it. I just—" He shrugged. "I held onto it. That's mostly what I do."

"That's not nothing." Sef sat down on the shrine's base, close enough to the dead doorway that Tam felt an irrational urge to pull him back. "My grandmother used to say the strongest thing in any house is the shelf. Nobody sings about shelves. But everything precious sits on one." He blew on his mush. "You're the shelf."

"I'm not sure that's a compliment."

"It's the highest one I know." Sef ate in silence a moment. Then, quieter: "The council voted today. Well—yesterday, by the time word reached us. They're sending envoys to Malvane. Terms of negotiation."

Tam went cold. "After Maren? After Bellhollow?"

"After Maren," Sef agreed, "and after the skirmish at the stones, where we lost nineteen ships and gained four hundred yards of marsh. Fear does arithmetic differently than grief does." He scraped his bowl. "I defied the vote. Formally. There'll be consequences when this is over—if there is an over. My seat's probably gone already." He smiled, and it was a young man's smile, bright and slightly terrified. "Worth it."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're the one who keeps the accounts." Sef stood, brushing rock dust from his coat. "When this is done, someone will have to write down who stood and who knelt. I want to be sure it gets written down right." He clapped Tam's shoulder—too hard, the way young men do—and walked back toward the fires.

Tam sat alone with the dead shrine and finished his mush, and thought about shelves, and did not sleep.

* * *

On the second day, Orlin called a halt at midday, which no one had authorized and everyone obeyed.

"Bells need tuning," he said, when Lena came back down the column with that look that meant *explain quickly.* "Seven days in salt air, wrapped in fish netting. You want me ringing them at the seam's heart sounding like a drowned funeral? Or do you want them sounding like the covenant?" He was already unwrapping the smallest bell, the one he called the Questioner, his big ruined hands moving with a tenderness Tam had only ever seen him give to bells and, once, briefly, to the memory of Maren.

"How long?" Lena asked.

"An hour. Maybe two." Orlin glanced up, and his bloodshot eyes were steady. "And I'll need quiet. Real quiet. So whatever's following us—" he nodded back down the coast road, south, toward the dust cloud the scouts had reported at dawn— "they'll hear us working. Your call, keeper."

Tam watched Lena weigh it. He was learning to read her face the way he'd once read Corvane's—the flicker at the corner of the mouth, the almost-invisible settling of the shoulders before a decision. But where Corvane's decisions always bent toward preservation, toward the hoard, toward the guild's long slow theft dressed up as duty, Lena's decisions bent toward the cost. She chose the thing that would hurt, if it was also the thing that was true.

"Tune them," she said. "We'll form a rearguard. Dain?"

"Already gone." Dain was buckling his sword. "Take six riders with me. Sef, your tide-pilot—what's her name—Vess. She can read ground-sound better than any scout I've had."

They rode south and did not come back for three hours, and those were the longest three hours of Tam's life, sitting in the enormous silence of Orlin's tuning—because it *was* silence, somehow, even though the man struck the bells over and over, soft as a heartbeat. Each strike settled something. Tam could feel it in his chest, in the fillings of his teeth, in the ledger against his ribs: the bells finding their agreement with each other, seven voices learning to be one chord. It was the first time since the guildhall that Tam had felt anything resembling safety, and he distrusted it completely, and he leaned into it anyway, greedy as a starving man at a stranger's table.

When Dain returned, his face told the story before his mouth did.

"They're not a day behind," he said, swinging down from the horse. "They're half a day. And they're not marching in column anymore. They're spreading out. Pincers." He drew in the dirt with his boot heel: two curved lines closing on a point. "He means to ring us. Not fight us—ring us. Push us onto the seam's heart and hold us there."

"Why?" Tam asked, before he could stop himself. "If he wants the choir destroyed—"

"He doesn't want it destroyed." Dain's voice went strange. "I served him, remember. I know how he thinks. He wants it *witnessed*. He wants us assembled at the seam, and then he wants to sing his crown-song over us and show us—all of us, and the free cities after us—that one voice is enough. That the covenant was never necessary. That everything we're marching toward is a lie we chose because we couldn't bear being alone." He looked at his own hand, flexing it. "It's not entirely a mad plan. That's what frightens me."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Orlin, tightening the last rope on the bell-cart, said: "One voice is enough for a command. Ten voices are enough for a promise. Man's confusing the two." He spat. "Always has been the trouble with this world."

* * *

They marched through the night. There was no discussion about it; the decision made itself, the way true decisions do. Sef sent runners back along the coast to light the beacon-fires the fleet had established—old signal points, fisher-magic, a language of flame that Vess claimed could carry three words per fire. *Come north. Come singing. Come now.*

By moonrise, the answer began arriving.

Tam saw the first boats from the ridge above the last river crossing—small craft, lanterns hooded, sliding up the black water. Then more. Fishermen from the outer banks. Kelp-cutters. A whaling crew out of Greyharbor with voices like landslide. They came ashore in ones and twos and tens, and Sef met them at the waterline and pressed each of their hands and spoke each of their names, because that was the kind of leader he was, and by the grey hour before dawn the chorus had grown from thirty-three to over two hundred.

Two hundred voices. Most of them untrained. All of them afraid. Every single one of them there anyway.

Tam stood apart, as usual, holding the ledger, and watched the fires, and felt something crack open in his chest that he didn't have a name for. He had spent his life in an institution that called itself a family and functioned as a vault. He had watched love administered like rations, loyalty enforced like debt. This—these strangers warming their hands at shared fires, teaching each other the anchor line of the covenant score by firelight, laughing badly at Orlin's terrible jokes—this was what the word had meant all along, and he had simply never seen it practiced.

"You're crying," said Lena, appearing beside him the way she did, silent as a page turning.

"I'm cold."

"You're crying *and* cold."

"Yes," Tam admitted. "Both things can be true. I keep the accounts; I would know."

Lena laughed—a short, cracked sound, the first he'd heard from her since Errenfell—and then it caught in her throat and became something else, and she turned away quickly, but not before Tam saw her hand go to her own chest, pressing flat, as if holding a door shut from inside.

"What is it?" he asked. "What do you feel? At the seam—I mean. You've been closest to it. What does it feel like?"

She was quiet a long time. The fires crackled. Somewhere in the dark, two hundred people breathed.

"Like being remembered," she said finally. "By something enormous. The way you'd remember a song you loved as a child—you don't recall the words, but your body remembers the shape of them." Her hand was still pressed to her chest. "And it's waiting for me, Tam. It's been waiting a hundred years. And I don't know yet what it wants to remember *about* me." She looked at him then, and her eyes in the firelight were the eyes of someone reading a ledger entry they already know ends in loss. "The tenth role. *A keeper who chooses.* I've been trying not to read the fine print. But there isn't any. That's the fine print. There's just the choosing."

"Then choose slowly," Tam said. "As slowly as you can. We'll walk as slow as you need."

"No." She shook her head, gentle and absolute. "You won't. Because Malvane's pincers close tomorrow, and the seam won't wait, and none of you will let me trade the world for my hesitation." She put her hand briefly on his arm—the first time she'd touched him since the guildhall, since the coal-chutes, since he'd come tumbling through a dying wayshrine with a stolen ledger and changed both their lives. "But thank you for offering. Hold onto that. Whatever happens tomorrow—hold onto the offering."

Then she walked back toward the fires, toward Dain and Orlin and Sef, toward the two hundred voices, and Tam stood alone with the ledger and the dead shrine and the humming ground, and held onto it.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of old bronze, and with it came the drums.

They'd heard drums before—at the border-stones, at Errenfell—but this was different. This was not a war-drum keeping march-time. This was low, and vast, and patient, rolling up from the south in waves that Tam felt in the soles of his boots before he heard them with his ears. Malvane's counter-choir. The crown-song's escort. Hundreds of drummers, maybe thousands, playing the usurper's grief in perfect unison, and underneath it, fainter, wrong, beautiful in the way a poisoned well is beautiful: the crown itself, singing.

Orlin stood at the bell-cart and listened with his head cocked like an old bird, and Tam watched his face go through contempt, then recognition, then something worse—pity.

"He's good," Orlin said quietly. "The boy's actually good. That counter-line under the drums—that's real craft. That's a trained voice." He shook his head. "Somebody taught him. Somebody taught him everything, and then left." He didn't say who. He didn't have to. Everyone on the ridge knew whose student Malvane had been, and whose leaving had curdled into all of this.

"Can we beat it?" Sef asked. He'd come up the ridge at a run, salt-stained, breathless. "Two hundred voices, seven bells, one tide-song's legacy. Can we?"

Orlin was silent a moment. Below them, the seam's heart showed itself for the first time: a valley where the land simply stopped agreeing with itself. Grass grew silver on one slope and green on the other. A river split midstream, half running uphill. And at the center, visible even from miles off, a pillar of light too thin to be a wall and too steady to be lightning—the seam itself, the stitch-line of the world, humming its century-long note of maintenance, thin as a wire now, fraying at the edges.

"We beat it," Orlin said, "if every voice arrives at the same instant. Not near. Not roughly. The covenant is a chord, girl-and-boy—it lives or dies on simultaneity. Bells, chorus, tide-line, all landing on the same breath." He looked at Tam. "Which means somebody has to count us in. Somebody who can hear two hundred voices and seven bells and a thousand drums and find the one true downbeat inside all that noise, and give it to us clean."

The ridge went quiet. The drums rolled. The seam hummed.

Every face turned to Tam.

"I count ledgers," he said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "Numbers. Debts. I've never—"

"You held harmony in the shrine at Errenfell," Lena said. "Maren heard it before any of us did. She said you were the metronome. She said—" Lena stopped, and Tam saw her decide whether to spend Maren's last words here, and decided yes, because Maren had never once in her life wasted anything, least of all truth. "She said, *when the time comes, put the boy where the counting lives. Everything else is decoration.*"

Tam's hands were shaking. He noticed them shaking the way he noticed everything—precisely, uselessly, from a great distance—and then he noticed something else, which was that beneath the fear, in the place where the bells had taught him to listen, he could already hear it. The drums. The crown-song. The seam's fraying note. Two hundred voices breathing in the valley below. All of it noise, all of it chaos—and somewhere inside it, if he listened the way he'd listened to eleven years of guildhall bells, a downbeat. Waiting to be found. Waiting to be given.

"Then put me where the counting lives," Tam said.

They came down off the ridge as the sun cleared the hills, and the armies of Malvane came up from the south like a tide of iron, and the seam's heart waited between them, humming, patient, enormous, remembering.

Tam walked in the middle of the choir, the ledger against his ribs, counting.
