## 31. The Battle of the Seam

Dain had run messages through wars before—through the salt-fog skirmishes off the Teeth, through the burning of the river towns in the year the guild pretended not to notice—but he had never heard anything like this.

The seam did not look like a battlefield. It looked like a scar in the morning light: a line of pale, humming air stretching from horizon to horizon where the two worlds pressed against each other like hands flattened against opposite sides of a window. The border-stones stood along it at intervals, each one tall as a watchtower, each one singing faintly to its neighbor down the line—a sound Dain had grown up hearing and never once thought to question, the way you never question the sound of your own blood.

Now the stones were screaming.

He crouched behind the third stone from the eastern end, Sef's fleet-borne chorus arrayed around him on the shingle below, four hundred voices in salt-stiffened coats, and beyond them the sea, and beyond the sea nothing but the grey approach of Malvane's host. They came out of the dawn mist in ranks that did not march so much as *pour*, ten thousand strong, and above them floated the drums.

That was the thing no report had prepared him for. Malvane's army did not carry drums; they were carried by them. Great bronze vessels the size of fishing boats, slung between teams of oxen or borne aloft on litters by twenty men apiece, and when the drummers struck them the sound was not a sound at all but a pressure, a fist of air that Dain felt in his back teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the wet of his eyes. Each beat landed like a verdict. Between beats, the whole world held its breath.

"They're tuning," Tam said beside him. She had her ledger open across her knees, the covenant score copied out in her small, relentless hand, and she was following the drums' rhythm against the written bars with one finger. "They're not attacking yet. They're finding the seam's frequency. Listen—" She held up a hand as another drumbeat rolled over them. "Every strike is a little closer to the note the stones sing. When they match it—"

"The stones stop being ours," Dain finished.

"When the stones stop being anyone's." Tam's jaw was set the way it had been set since the guildhall, since the interrogation rooms, since everything she had walked out of with her spine intact and her eyes older. "A seam nobody holds is a door standing open."

Below them, Maren's place at the head of the chorus stood empty. They had wrapped her cloak around one of the border-stones because no one could bear to leave it folded in a pack, and the wind moved it gently, patiently, like a woman adjusting her own shawl. Dain made himself look at it. He had decided, somewhere in the weeks after Errenfell, that he would make himself look at every cost this war presented him, because looking away was the skill he'd been best at once, and look where that had gotten everyone.

"Signal from the fleet," called one of Sef's runners, scrambling up the shingle. "Harbor ships are in position along the water-line. Sef says: tell the choir the tide is theirs."

Dain lifted the horn at his belt and blew three notes—the old courier's signal, the one that meant *the road is open*. Four hundred heads turned toward him. Lena stood at the center of them, and even now, even here, what struck him was how still she was. Not calm—her hands were shaking, he could see it from thirty feet—but still, the way a keel is still in rough water because everything beneath it is working.

She raised her voice, and it carried without effort, the way it always did now, the way it had begun to even before the seam chose her.

"We hold the song until the song holds the world!" she called. "Whatever comes over that field—whatever they bring—we do not stop singing. Not when we're afraid. *Especially* not when we're afraid. Fear is just a note you haven't learned yet."

A ragged laugh went through the chorus, and then Orlin's voice cut through it, gravel-deep and grinning: "I've been afraid my whole life, girl. I'm very well learned."

The bells answered him.

They had hauled nine of the covenant bells up from the mountain shrines on sledges and barges, and now they hung from timber frames along the crest of the ridge, each one taller than a man, dark bronze veined with the same pale light as the seam itself. Orlin stood among them with his mallets, an old man in a fisherman's oilskin with his grey hair tied back, and when he struck the first bell the sound went through Dain like sunrise going through fog.

It was not loud, exactly. It was *true*. Every other sound on the field seemed suddenly to be measured against it and found wanting. The chorus caught the note—four hundred voices rising together—and passed it down the line of border-stones, and the stones took it up, and for one impossible moment the whole seam rang like a struck glass, and Dain felt the two worlds lean into each other not as enemies but as singers finding harmony.

Then Malvane's drums answered.

* * *

The battle, if you could call it that, was fought entirely in sound, and Dain spent most of it understanding almost none of what he heard.

The drums came on in waves, each wave tuned a fraction closer to the seam's frequency, and each time they crested, the chorus bent the note away—Maren's tide-song, taught to them all in the weeks of marching, rising and falling like water refusing to be dammed. Where the two sounds met, the air itself warped. Dain saw grass flatten in rings. Saw a border-stone's glow gutter like a candle in wind, then flare back as the chorus swung its weight behind it. Men on both lines covered their ears and wept without knowing why. The sea itself pulled strangely, in time with the music, waves arriving early or late, as though the tide were trying to choose a side.

Sef's harbor ships worked the shallows, their decks crowded with singers, throwing the chorus's harmonies out over the water so that the seam was defended from land and sea both—and Malvane's counter-choir came down to meet them.

Dain saw them first as a smudge on the ridge to the north: perhaps two hundred figures in grey, arranged in concentric rings, and at their center a platform, and on the platform a shape that hurt to look at directly. It took him a long moment to understand that the shape was a man wearing a crown, and that the crown was singing.

Malvane did not shout orders. He did not need to. The crown poured his will outward as pure tone, and the counter-choir caught it and multiplied it, and what came off that northern ridge was a sound like grief stretched until it became geometry. It wasn't ugly. That was the worst of it. It was beautiful the way a landslide is beautiful, inevitable and total, and Dain felt it pluck at something inside his chest—an old loneliness, a memory of every door ever closed against him—and he understood, with cold clarity, that this was how Malvane fought. He didn't break armies. He reminded them they were alone, and let them do the rest.

"Hold the line!" Sef's voice, hoarse, from the water. "Hold the *line*!"

On the left flank, a knot of chorus-singers wavered, their harmony fraying as the crown's song found the cracks in them. Dain watched a young woman—one of Sef's deckhands, freckled, nineteen maybe—stop singing and simply stand, tears running down her face, her mouth open on nothing. The grey ring nearest her shifted, sensing the gap the way sharks sense blood.

And then Orlin's bells came down like judgment.

The old man had read the field the way he read weather. He abandoned the measured tolling of the covenant pattern and began to strike wild—syncopated, furious, off-beat on purpose—and the dissonance slammed into the crown's perfect chord and *shattered* it. Dain actually saw the counter-choir stagger, two hundred grey figures flinching as one, and the freckled deckhand gasped like a surfacing swimmer and dragged the note back up, and the left flank closed, and the chorus surged.

"That's it!" Tam shouted, on her feet, ledger forgotten. "Orlin! The seventh bell—hold the seventh bell against the crown's root note—it can't resolve while you—"

"I know how a broken heart works, girl!" Orlin bellowed, and laughed, and struck again.

For a quarter of an hour—for a lifetime—they held. Dain ran the line with messages because that was what he was for, because his legs were the fastest thing left in either world, and so he saw all of it in pieces, the way you see a fire through windows: the harbor ships leaning into the swell with four hundred voices braced against the drums; the border-stones glowing brighter than they had in a century, their ancient song swelling under the choir's support; Lena at the center of everything, her voice threading through every section, binding bell to chorus to stone, and the seam itself responding—*responding*, the pale line of it brightening, widening, breathing—

And then Malvane stopped fighting the bells, and started listening to them.

Dain understood it only afterward, piecing it together with Tam, the way you understand a trap after your foot is already in it. The crown was not just a weapon. It was an instrument, and its wearer had been the ninth singer's student, had spent forty years learning every voice that had ever forged the covenant—including, surely, the recordings, the transcriptions, the guild's stolen archives. And Orlin, magnificent, reckless Orlin, was playing the covenant bells according to the covenant patterns, and Malvane knew those patterns the way a scar knows the wound that made it.

The counter-choir split. Half held the chorus. The other half turned—not toward the flank, not toward the ships, but toward the ridge where the bells hung.

"No," Dain said, out loud, alone, on the run, to no one who could hear him.

* * *

He covered the distance to the bell-ridge faster than he had ever covered anything, and it was not fast enough.

The grey singers came up the slope in their perfect rings, walking through arrow-fire as though arrows were rain, and Orlin saw them coming and did not stop playing. That was the thing about Orlin that Dain would carry for the rest of his life, whatever length of it remained: the old man saw death walking up his hill in neat concentric circles, and he grinned at it, and he played *harder*.

Because he understood what Dain understood in that same terrible instant: the bells were the only thing keeping the crown's song from folding the chorus in half. If Orlin stopped—even for the time it took to run—the seventh bell fell silent, the crown's root note resolved, and everything they had bled for since Errenfell came apart in a single chord.

So he kept playing. He played while the grey ring closed. He played the covenant pattern and then broke it, improvised, invented, hammering out variations no score had ever held, buying seconds with each wild stroke. One bell-frame splintered under some focused pressure of the crown's song and he stepped to the next without missing a beat. A grey singer raised a hand and the sixth bell cracked from rim to crown with a sound like a dying animal, and Orlin's face went white with fury and he gave the fifth bell double strokes to cover its mourning.

Dain reached the ridge. Twenty yards. Ten. He had a knife and a courier's staff and no plan whatsoever, and none of it mattered, because the crown turned its attention.

He felt it like a searchlight. All that vast grief-laden power, sweeping across the hillside, finding the one man whose arms were raised, whose whole body was the weapon, whose voice—when he finally used it, roaring the low drone that anchored the bells—was the truest thing on the field.

The counter-choir parted around Orlin like water around a stone. They did not touch him. They didn't have to. The crown sang one note, precise as a surgeon, aimed at the fourth bell—the anchor bell, the one Orlin's drone was holding—and the fourth bell *shattered*.

The recoil took Orlin off his feet. Dain caught him, or tried to; what he caught was mostly momentum, an old body falling out of a song it had given everything to. They went down together on the wet grass, and around them the bells tolled on unstruck, ringing out their last momentum in long, fading, imperfect chords, and through Orlin's oilskin Dain could feel the tremor of a heart working far too hard, and slowing.

"Get up," Dain said. "Get up, you stubborn old wreck, the fifth bell needs—"

Orlin laughed. It cost him; Dain felt the laugh stutter in his chest like a failing engine. Blood at the corner of his mouth. His hands, those great scarred mallet-hands, were still curled as if around mallets that weren't there.

"Fifth bell," he rasped. "Someone take the fifth bell. It hates being ignored."

"Tam's sending runners—"

"Not runners." His grip closed on Dain's wrist with shocking strength. His eyes, clouded and bright at once, fixed on Dain's face with the terrible clarity of the dying. "You. I heard you hum. In the mountain town, before you woke me. You thought I was too drunk to notice." A wet grin. "You've got the low register. Nobody else does, now. Nobody else *left* does."

"I'm not a singer, Orlin."

"You're a man who keeps showing up." The grip tightened. "That's ninety percent of singing, boy. The other ten percent is knowing when to stop." His gaze drifted past Dain's shoulder, up, toward the seam glowing on the horizon, and something in his face gentled into wonder. "Listen. Can you hear it? Even now. Even with me going. It's still—" His breath caught. Held. "Still holding. Tell the girl... tell her the thread holds. Tell her I finally..."

The sentence never found its ending. But his hands, Dain noticed—his hands relaxed, at the last, the way a ringer's hands relax when the final peal has been rung clean and true, when there is nothing left in the tower that needs him.

The bells fell silent one by one, each in its turn, like a household going to sleep.

* * *

Afterward, Dain could never say how long the battle lasted beyond that moment. Time came apart the way fabric comes apart—thread by thread, and each thread a different length.

He knew that he picked Orlin up and carried him down the ridge because no one else was going to, and that his arms knew the weight of dead friends too well by now. He knew that the chorus faltered when the bells died—heard the harmony fray along its whole length, heard the drums surge forward into the gap, hungry—and that then Lena's voice rose alone over four hundred failing throats, raw and enormous, and dragged them back from the edge one note at a time. He knew that Tam appeared at his elbow at some point, white-faced, and took the fifth bell's part herself, humming it through chattering teeth while she ran, because someone had to and she had decided it would be her.

He knew that Sef's ships closed ranks around the shallows and that the harbor fleet's massed voices threw a wall of harmony against the drums that neither side broke, and that the sun crossed half the sky while the two songs ground against each other like millstones with the world caught between them.

And he knew, with a certainty that settled into his bones colder than exhaustion, that they were losing. Slowly. By inches. The crown did not tire. The chorus did. Every hour, another border-stone dimmed. Every hour, another handful of singers went silent—not dead, most of them, just *emptied*, hollowed out by the crown's lonely song until they had nothing left to give. The covenant held. But it held the way a rope bridge holds, strand by straining strand, and Malvane had nothing but time, and grief, and ten thousand voices willing to carry his sorrow for him.

Toward dusk, the drums changed.

Dain heard it from the shingle, kneeling beside Orlin's body with the old man's mallets laid across his chest, and he knew before Tam spoke that it was bad.

"They've stopped advancing," she said quietly. "Look."

The grey host had halted, a mile out, rank upon rank upon rank in the failing light. The counter-choir had withdrawn to its ridge. And the drums—those vast bronze hearts—had settled into a new rhythm. Slow. Patient. Almost gentle. A lullaby's rhythm.

"They're not trying to break us anymore," Tam said. Her voice was steady, but her knuckles, around her ledger, were bone-white. "They're trying to *outlast* us. They'll play all night. They'll play for a week. The crown doesn't sleep, Dain. We do."

Out on the water, Sef's signal lamps were flashing: *hold position, hold position, hold position.* Along the seam, the border-stones glimmered fitfully, singing their old song thinner and thinner, and between them the pale line of the world's wound pulsed like something barely alive.

Dain looked down at Orlin's face, at the peace in it, at the mallets. Then up the slope, to where Lena stood at the crest of the ridge with the wind pulling at her, staring north at the enemy who did not sleep, and even at this distance he could see her lips moving—not singing, not commanding. Counting. Working through something. Arriving somewhere.

She turned. Found him across the whole darkening field, as if the seam's light made a straight road between them. And she raised one hand—not a wave, not a salute. A summons.

"Dain," Tam breathed. "What's she—"

Lena pointed, once, toward the heart of the seam, where the light burned brightest and oldest and where, according to every text they had saved from Bellhollow's flames, the covenant had first been sung.

Where the final verse was waiting for a keeper who chooses.

"She's found it," Dain said. His voice came out strange, scraped hollow. "She's found the tenth role. And she's going to tell us what it costs."
