## 32. Corvane's Choice

They took the guildhall in the hour before dawn, when even the candles seemed to sleep.

Corvane had known it was coming for three days. He had read it in the way the city held its breath, in the absence of gulls from the rooftops, in the silence of his own informants—a silence so complete it could only mean they had been bought, or buried. Malvane's riders came up the coal road with their lamps hooded and their drums wrapped in felt, and Corvane stood at the great window of his study and watched them come, and he did not order the bells rung.

He told himself this was strategy. That a guildhall full of ledgers and old men could not hold against an army; that surrender preserved what resistance would burn. These things were even true. But underneath the truth, quiet as water finding a crack, lay another reason, one he would not look at directly: some part of him had been waiting years to be caught.

* * *

They bound his wrists with silk, which he almost laughed at—the courtesy of it, the theater. Malvane's officers were meticulous about such things. They walked him down the grand stair he had climbed as an apprentice, past the portraits of masters dead two centuries, through the entrance hall where the founding charter had hung behind glass until Lena Sandoval stole it back like a thief in her own house. The glass frame stood empty now. Someone had left it hanging anyway, out of habit or cruelty.

In the counting room—the beating heart of the guild for four hundred years, where every impossible trade had been weighed and priced—they sat him in the master's chair and left him alone for a long while. Long enough to notice the dust. He had always believed the counting room was cleaned daily. It occurred to him, sitting there with his bound hands folded like a penitent's, that no one had cleaned anything in this room since the payments stopped. Since *he* had ordered them stopped. Dust on the scales. Dust on the brass weights that had measured out tides and memories and years. The empire he had built to hoard power had not even kept its floors swept.

When Malvane entered, Corvane did not rise. It was the last small rebellion available to him, and he spent it.

"You are older than your reputation," Malvane said. He moved like a man who had learned posture from statues. At his brow, the crown of forged tribute-light burned without warmth, and its glow made shadows fall wrong—toward him instead of away, as if the light itself were afraid. "You are also smaller than your ledgers."

"Everyone is," Corvane said. "That's rather the point of ledgers."

Malvane studied him the way Corvane himself once studied a disputed entry: patiently, thoroughly, with the assumption that everything would eventually confess. Then he gestured, and two soldiers brought in the thing they had carried up from the vaults.

It was the tribute. All of it—or nearly all. Decades of withheld payment, decades of the guild's skimming from the covenant's account: bottled tide-songs in glass the color of bruises, memory-wine decanted into crystal, jars of captured dawn sealed with wax stamped by masters long dead, coils of raw seam-light wound on spools like thread. Corvane had catalogued every piece personally. He had signed for each acquisition in his small, exact hand. Seeing it all gathered in one place, stacked and gleaming under Malvane's wrong-shadowed crown, he understood for the first time how much there was—and how little of it had ever belonged to the guild.

"Do you know what this is?" Malvane asked.

"Tribute," Corvane said. "The covenant's share. Withheld."

"Withheld." Malvane turned a jar of dawn in his hands, tilting it so the trapped light sloshed against the glass. "My teacher used that word too. She called it *withholding*. She said it gently, as if it were a virtue. As if the world's accounts could simply be frozen and everyone would be safe." His voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about it—it never rose. "She taught me that the covenant was a leash. You taught me it was a purse. Between you, I have learned what it truly is."

"And what is that?"

"A throat." Malvane set the jar down. "And I am going to show you how it sounds when someone else holds it."

He reached up—not to his crown, but to the tribute beside him—and pressed his palm flat against the spool of seam-light. And he sang.

Corvane had heard singers all his life. He had priced their voices, debited their songs, entered their gifts into columns with the same detachment he brought to grain futures. He had thought he understood what a voice was worth. Then Malvane sang through the stolen tribute, and the counting room became a place Corvane had never been.

The sound did not travel through air. It traveled through *debt*. Every entry Corvane had ever made lit up in his mind at once—every tide bought, every memory taxed, every year skimmed from Vael's side of the seam—and the song moved through them like fire through a dry field, and where it passed, it *called things home*. The bottled tides strained toward their river. The memory-wine wept condensation, trying to return to the heads it had been taken from. The seam-light on its spools pulled so hard toward the horizon that the spools creaked, and the soldiers holding them staggered.

"That is what you stored," Malvane said, when the last note died and the room settled, trembling. "Not power. *Longing*. Forty years of everything Vael sent across the seam, and every drop of it still remembers where it belongs." He leaned close, and the crown's inverted shadow fell across Corvane's face. "I can turn it any way I choose. Against your walls. Against your city. Against the free cities' fleets. Or—" and here his voice softened into something worse than threat, something almost kind, "—against you, Master Corvane, who signed for all of it."

"I signed for the guild."

"The guild is a word." Malvane straightened. "You are a name. Names go in books. I have read yours in several." He turned toward the door, then paused. "You may keep the chair. Consider it a courtesy between men who both believe the ledger matters more than the people in it."

* * *

They gave him a cell in the guild's own deep archive, among the sealed stacks where the oldest prophecy-books slept under wool and wax. It was, Corvane reflected on the first night, a fitting prison: he had spent his life among records of things given up, and now he was one.

He expected despair. He had budgeted for it, the way he budgeted for everything—a reserve of grief, allocated in advance, to be drawn upon as needed. What came instead was stranger: clarity. Without the ledger chained to his wrist, without the weights and columns and the daily arithmetic of withholding, his mind felt enormous and empty, like a hall after the market has gone. And in that emptiness, memories he had suppressed for decades came walking in one by one, and sat down, and waited to be acknowledged.

Lena Sandoval at six years old, falling down the coal-chute and coming up black-faced and laughing, and how he had almost smiled before catching himself. Her first ledger-entry, written in a hand so careful it trembled, and how he had told her the numbers were wrong when they were right, because praise was a currency he did not spend. The night her grandmother fled across the gate, and how Corvane had stood in the counting room and watched the seals close and felt not triumph but *relief*—because the ninth voice was gone, and with it the only person who might have forced the question he refused to ask.

*What are we keeping it all for?*

He had never answered that question. He had built an entire institution to avoid answering it. And now the answer arrived uninvited, in the dark, in a child's laughing face covered in coal-dust: *nothing. We were keeping it for nothing. We were keeping it because stopping hurt less than continuing.*

Tam had asked him that once. Years ago, ink-stained, earnest Tam, who copied pages he was forbidden to copy and asked questions he was forbidden to ask. Corvane had punished him for it. He had told himself the boy needed discipline. The truth was simpler and uglier: Tam had asked the question aloud, and Corvane had not been able to bear hearing it in anyone's voice but his own.

He sat in the dark among the sleeping prophecies and did the accounting he had avoided for forty years. It did not take long. The assets column was short. The liabilities ran off the page and through the wall and across the sea.

* * *

On the third day, they came for the vaults.

Malvane wanted them opened properly—not broken, but opened, with the guild's own rites and keys, because the tribute inside responded to theft with decay and to consent with yield. Corvane knew this. He had written the rites himself. And so Malvane came down to the archive cell personally, crown blazing, and stood outside the bars with the patience of a man who already owned the outcome.

"The keys, Master Corvane."

"They are in my head," Corvane said. "Four hundred years of guild-craft, compressed into seven phrases. Kill me and they die with me. Torture me and I will garble them, and you will open a vault full of ash."

"I know." Malvane's smile was thin as a blade's edge. "That is why I have not threatened you. I have simply waited. Men like you always open the door eventually. You only need to be shown the true size of what you're guarding." He stepped closer to the bars. "So let me show you. My heralds tell me the choir has reached Errenfell. Your former keeper, your runaway apprentice, a drunk bell-ringer, a fisherwoman's ghosts. They mean to sing the covenant whole. And when they do, I will meet them at the seam with forty years of your hoard on my brow, and I will sing *alone*, Corvane. A covenant of one. Every voice in both realms bound to mine, every tide and trade and year flowing where I will. You built the treasury. I am merely spending it."

"You cannot sing a covenant alone," Corvane said. "The ninth singer proved—"

"My teacher proved nothing except that running is possible." For the first time, something moved behind Malvane's stillness—something with wet eyes and a young man's face, quickly mastered. "I loved her. Did you know that? She was my whole sky, and she looked at me one morning as if I were a debt she'd finally cleared, and she went through the gate and never came back. So yes—I have learned what abandonment teaches. I will never be abandoned again. I will make abandonment *impossible*, for everyone, forever. That is my covenant. That is what you helped me build." He turned away. "Three days. Then I take the keys from whatever is left of you."

He left. And in the dark, Corvane began, at last, to choose.

* * *

He worked through the night, and the night after, and what he built was not a plan exactly—it was a confession with a mechanism attached.

The keys were real; that much he had told Malvane truthfully. Seven phrases, each one keyed to a vault-door, each phrase requiring the speaker to name aloud what the vault contained and *why it had been taken*. The guild's founders had built it that way, in more honest centuries: the doors did not respond to authority, only to acknowledgment. A thief could not open them because a thief could not confess. Corvane had found this quaint, once. He had spent thirty years quietly working around it, opening the doors by proxy, by ritual half-truths, by training apprentices to say the words without meaning them. The doors had opened. He had told himself that proved the rite was superstition.

But the deepest vault—the First Vault, where the original tribute-songs slept, the ones that predated even the ninth singer—had never yielded to his half-truths. Forty years he had tried. Its door had stayed shut through every circumvention he devised, patient as stone, waiting for words spoken whole.

Now, in the dark, Corvane spoke them whole.

He began with the smallest vault and worked upward, and it was the strangest labor of his life: he stood at the bars of a cell and confessed, item by item, in a voice that shook and then steadied. *"The tide of Callowmere, taken in the year of the gray frost, because the free cities' fleets feared the shoals and paid us to tame them, and we kept the change."* The phrase unlocked in his mouth like a joint setting itself. *"The memory of Errenfell's drowned, harvested from grieving mothers because grief was abundant that year and grief stores well."* Another phrase seated home. By the seventh, he was weeping, and he noticed with distant surprise that the tears did not shame him; they were simply the last line item, entered at last.

The phrases were not for Malvane. That was the point. They were for whoever came next—for Tam, who had copied stolen pages by candlelight and asked the forbidden question and been punished for the crime of being right. If Tam ever stood before these doors, the words would be waiting, because Corvane wrote them out—slowly, in the dark, from memory, in the small exact hand that had signed away a world—on the only surface available. He tore the wool lining from the prophecy-shelves and wrote on its reverse, and hid the sheets inside the hollow spine of the oldest prophecy-book on the shelf: the one titled *A Crown for the Choir*, whose final page had been blank for a century and whose author, the archivists said, had died owing a debt.

Then there remained only the matter of delivery.

* * *

On the fifth day, they moved him. Not to execution—he had expected execution—but upstairs, to the counting room, where the tribute was stored and where Malvane intended, Corvane realized, to make the taking of the keys a ceremony. Power performed before witnesses lasts longer. He had taught Malvane that, or men like him had, and the student had surpassed the curriculum.

The counting room blazed with wrong light. Officers lined the walls. And near the back, among the clerks and captives the riders had swept up from the guild's lower ranks, stood a familiar figure with ink-stained fingers and a bruise along one jaw.

Tam.

Alive. Here. Corvane's heart did something complicated and old-fashioned that he had no column to record it in. The boy had come *back*, into the lion's mouth, whether for the charter or the ledgers or the people, and been taken. Of course he had. Tam had never once done the smart thing when the right thing stood nearby wearing a worse costume.

Malvane ascended the dais and gestured, and a soldier brought the key-scrolls—Corvane's personal keys, seized from his study—to the table before him. "Begin," Malvane said. "Name the vaults. Name them well, and your people walk. Name them poorly, and I begin with the boy."

So it was to be coercion after all. Corvane had known it would come to this; Malvane's patience had always been a siege engine, not a virtue. And here was the trap laid bare: the doors required confession freely given, but Malvane held hostages, and hostages made every word a transaction, and transactions were precisely what the doors rejected. If Corvane spoke under threat, the vaults would stay shut, and Malvane would learn it soon enough, and begin breaking people to find another way.

Unless the words were not for the doors.

Corvane looked at Tam across the crowded room. Tam was watching him with an expression he recognized because he had worn it for forty years in mirrors: hope fighting duty, duty losing slowly. And Corvane understood, with the terrible ease of a man finally balancing a lifelong account, what he actually possessed. Not the keys. Keys were phrases; phrases could be tortured or garbled or hoarded. What he possessed was the *truth*—the whole confession, spoken whole, witnessed—and the truth was the one asset that grew larger the more it was given away.

"Master Malvane," he said, and his voice carried in the hushed room the way a bell carries after the ringing stops. "Before I name a single vault, you should know what your crown is standing on."

Malvane's eyes narrowed. "Speak."

"This tribute you've stacked like coin." Corvane rose, silk-bound wrists and all. "It is not inert. It is owed. Every jar, every spool, every bottle in this room is a debt with a destination, and debts of this kind do not sit quietly forever. You have built your throne on a warehouse of longing, and longing, sir, is the most patient army in either realm." He turned, slowly, addressing the room—the officers, the clerks, the captives, and above all the ink-stained boy. "For forty years this guild withheld the covenant's payments and called it prudence. I signed every entry. I know the price of every item in this room, because I set it. And I will now tell you the price of the withholding itself."

"What game is this?" Malvane demanded.

"No game. An audit." And Corvane smiled, and it was the smile his apprentices had feared, except that now it was aimed inward. "Vault One: the tide of Callowmere, taken in the year of the gray frost, because the fleets feared the shoals and paid us to tame them, *and we kept the change.*"

The nearest vault-door—three rooms away, through stone—answered. It was not a sound so much as a settling, a deep structural sigh, and every person in the counting room felt it in their teeth. Malvane's officers exchanged glances. Malvane's hand rose toward his crown.

"Vault Two," Corvane continued, louder. "The memory of Errenfell's drowned, harvested from grieving mothers because grief was abundant that year *and grief stores well.*" The second sigh, deeper. Somewhere below, iron rolled back from iron.

"Stop him," Malvane said.

Soldiers moved. It did not matter. Corvane had spent his whole life learning how to speak over interruption—it is a skill masters acquire—and besides, the words were doing what words spoken whole had always done: they were leaving him and becoming architecture. "Vault Three! The dawn-jars of the eastern coast, bought from a famine-year at famine prices—" The third door. Shouting now, officers running for the stairs, and Malvane roaring something about silencing him—

But silencing was the one thing that could not work anymore, and Corvane saw that Malvane knew it too late. The confessions were in the air. They had witnesses. And a confession with witnesses is no longer property; it is *weather*. Vault Four. Vault Five. Each phrase a door. Each door a debt released toward its home, and the building beginning to hum with returning things, and the crown on Malvane's brow flickering like a lamp in wind as its hoarded fuel began, drop by drop, to remember elsewhere.

"Vault Six!" Corvane's voice was going ragged. A soldier struck him; he tasted blood and kept the count. "The years skimmed from Vael's children, entered as interest—" Sixth door. The floor shivered. Tam was crying, he could see that even from the dais, crying and mouthing the words along with him, because of course the boy had memorized them from the copied pages, of course he had—

"Vault Seven." And here Corvane stopped. Here he looked at Malvane—at the flickering crown, at the young man's face surfacing through the tyrant's stillness, at the abandoned student who had become the thing his teacher fled—and he spoke the last phrase softly, almost tenderly, the way one speaks to the wounded:

"The First Vault: the original songs, older than the covenant, that we never owned and never returned. Taken because stopping hurt less than continuing. Kept because I could not answer a child's question: *what are we keeping it all for?*"

Deep beneath them, the last door opened. And the First Vault's contents did not wait politely. The original songs rose through the guildhall like floodwater finding the sea, and every bottled thing in the counting room rose with them, and Malvane screamed—not orders this time, just screamed—as forty years of stolen longing tore free of his hoard and streamed through walls and windows toward the seam, a river of light running home in the grey morning.

In the chaos, in the noise, in the moment when every eye was on the burning crown and the fleeing light, Corvane did the last small things left in his power. He caught Tam's gaze across the room and held it, and mouthed, with the clarity of a man writing a final entry: *The wool. Inside the spine. A Crown for the Choir. The tenth voice must be given, not taken—tell her—given, never taken.*

Tam's lips formed the words back: *Given. Never taken.*

Corvane nodded once. The account balanced. Whatever came now was merely the closing of the book.

They dragged him from the dais as the guildhall's windows burst outward in a rain of returning light. He did not resist. He was humming—softly, tunelessly, a man who had never sung in his life picking up, at the end, the melody everyone else had known all along. And as the soldiers pulled him through the doors, he heard Malvane shouting for the archives to be searched, for the prophecy-books, for the wool, for the spine—and he smiled through the blood, because he knew exactly how many hours that search would buy, and exactly who would reach the book first.

The last thing Corvane saw of the counting room was Tam, sprinting for the archive stairs with a sheet of torn wool already in his fist.

Good, Corvane thought. Good boy. Now run.

* * *

They put him in a wagon with the other prisoners as the column prepared to march east, toward the seam, toward the choir, toward the reckoning he had spent four decades making inevitable and one night making survivable. His ribs were cracked. His mouth was full of blood. The crown-light on the horizon guttered where the returned tribute had thinned it, and Malvane's officers rode tight circles around their king, and no one watched an old man bleeding quietly in the back of a cart.

Except one. A young rider—barely more than a conscript—reined in beside the wagon, frowned at Corvane's face, and after a long moment passed him a water skin. Corvane drank. Their eyes met. And in the boy's expression he saw something he had never once priced correctly in forty years of ledgers: unpaid-for kindness, entered in no book, expecting no return.

"Thank you," Corvane said. It was, he reflected, the first fully honest transaction of his life, and it cost nothing, and it was worth everything, and it had taken him seventy years to learn the exchange rate.

The wagon lurched forward. Behind them, the guildhall stood with its vaults gaping open and its secrets already running east in a boy's fist. Ahead, the seam waited, and the choir, and a keeper who had to be told before it was too late: *given, not taken.*

Corvane closed his eyes and began, silently, to rehearse the confession he would offer if he ever stood before her—the whole of it, every line item, spoken whole.

He only hoped the door of her forgiveness was built like the vault doors: patient, and listening, and waiting for words that meant what they said.

And far to the east, in a camp above the drowned shrine of Errenfell, a fire burned low, and around it a keeper bent over a stolen charter, unaware that the last verse had just changed—that the price had just been named—and that somewhere west, in the back of a prisoner's wagon, an old man was finally singing.
