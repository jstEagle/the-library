## 36. Aftermath of Song

The silence after the singing was the loudest thing Tam had ever heard.

It came down over the seam like a held breath released—not empty, but full, the way a cathedral is full after the last note of a requiem has gone out through the windows and into the dark. The armies stood in it. Ten thousand soldiers on the Vael side of the stones, eight thousand on the side of the free cities, all of them listening to a sound that was no longer there, all of them unwilling to be the first to move, as though movement might shatter whatever fragile thing had just been born between the worlds.

Tam stood among them with Corvane's second ledger under one arm and ash from Bellhollow still ground into the creases of his knuckles, and he wept without any dignity whatsoever.

He was not alone in that. Down the line of the free cities' ranks, hard-faced marines who had rowed through fire at the border-stones were wiping their eyes with salt-stiff sleeves. On the far side, Vaelish spearmen—creatures of twilight and long memory, some of them not entirely shaped as men—had lowered their points into the earth and were standing with their heads bowed like mourners at a graveside. The seam itself glowed where Lena had stood, or where Lena had *been*, a thread of gold light running along the ground between the two hosts, humming faintly, patiently, like a plucked string that would not quite stop sounding.

She was still there. That was the thing Tam could not stop looking at. She stood at the very heart of the seam, where the two worlds pressed against each other like cupped palms, and she was luminous now in a way that made his chest ache to witness—the covenant's light moving beneath her skin like slow water under ice. Her eyes were open. Her face was calm. And when Dain Ash walked toward her across the trampled grass, calling her name in a voice that cracked on the second syllable, she looked at him with the gentle, courteous blankness of someone greeting a stranger who has mistaken her for a friend.

"She doesn't know me," Dain said, when he came back. He said it flatly, the way men report casualties. His hands were shaking. "She knows *of* me. She said—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "She said, 'You must be one of the singers. The covenant remembers you.' The covenant remembers. She doesn't."

Tam put his hand on the older man's shoulder and found nothing to say that wasn't a lie, so he said nothing, which was its own kind of honesty, and the three of them—or the two of them and the bright figure at the seam's heart who was no longer quite any of them—stayed there while the sun went down over two worlds at once.

* * *

The armies stood down formally on the third day.

It should have been harder than it was. Tam had spent enough nights around council fires in the free cities to know how wars were supposed to end: slowly, bitterly, through months of negotiation conducted by men who measured honor in territory ceded. But the singing had changed something in the soldiers themselves. They had heard the seam reforged. They had felt it in their teeth, in the marrow behind their sternums—a sound like the world clicking back into a joint that had been dislocated for a century—and no commander's speech could compete with that memory. When the heralds went out under white banners, the men on both sides looked almost relieved, the way exhausted children look when told at last that they may sleep.

The terms were written in the open, on trestle tables set up astride the border-stones, so that delegates from both realms could sit together in the same thin gold light. Tam attended every session. He told himself it was duty. It was also avoidance, though he was honest enough by then to admit it: the guildhall waited for him at home, and the guildhall was a wound he did not yet know how to clean.

The terms themselves were simple, because the covenant had made them simple. Where once the guild had traded impossible things in secret—one memory here, a shadow there, a first love weighed against a favor—now the exchange would happen in daylight, at the gate, recorded in ledgers any citizen might read. Vael would send its wonders: tide-songs, healing light, seeds that grew in salt. The free cities would send what Vael had starved for since the payments stopped: grain, iron honestly forged, books, music, hands willing to build. Nothing would be taken. Everything would be given, and the giving would be written down.

"An honest ledger," Sef said, on the fourth evening, watching the first supply barges come up the river with their holds full of wheat. The young delegate had the hollow-eyed look of someone who had slept perhaps four hours since the battle and intended to keep it that way until the treaties were signed. "Do you know how many times I sat in that council chamber and listened to old men explain that secrecy was safety? That the guild's ways were the city's ways, and question them at your peril?" She turned a wheat grain over in her fingers. "They weren't lying, exactly. They just couldn't imagine anything else. That's worse, I think."

"It's what institutions do," Tam said. "They forget why they were built and start defending the shape of themselves instead." He thought of Corvane, and of the message that had come to him in the last hours—*the tenth voice must be given, not taken*—scrawled in a hand gone suddenly old, sent by a man who had opened the guild vaults with his own seal and then walked out to meet Malvane's soldiers with his wrists bare. "I was raised inside one. I loved it the way you love a parent. It took me twenty years to understand that loving something doesn't mean believing everything it tells you about itself."

Sef glanced at him sideways. "And now they want you to rebuild it."

"They want *someone*. The council voted this morning." He had tried to refuse. He had stood in front of the assembled delegates with his heart hammering and said that he was an apprentice, a thief of ledgers, a boy who had crossed through a collapsing wayshrine on a gamble—and the council had listened politely and elected him anyway, forty-one votes to nine, because apparently the qualifications for leading the Guild of Impossible Things now consisted of having stolen the truth and carried it through fire. "Master Corvane is dead. Maren is dead. Orlin is dead. Half the masters fled when the vaults opened and the hoarded tribute-power was found—rooms of it, Sef. Rooms of stolen memories and bottled years, sitting there for decades while they told us the covenant had simply *lapsed*." His voice caught. He mastered it. "Someone has to write down what actually happened. Someone has to make sure the next ledger-keeper can read it without flinching."

Sef was quiet for a moment. Then she flicked the wheat grain into the river, where the current took it toward Vael, toward whatever strange country lay on the other shore. "Then build it so it can be questioned," she said. "That's all. Build it so a child can walk in off the street and ask why, and get an answer instead of a locked door."

Tam wrote those words down that night, in the first pages of a new book, before he wrote anything else.

* * *

He returned to the guildhall on a grey morning two weeks later, and it was worse than he remembered and better than he feared.

Worse, because the evidence of the flight was everywhere—master's doors hanging open, robes abandoned mid-task, the great reading room scattered with notes half-finished, as if the entire institution had been a dinner party from which the guests had risen all at once upon hearing bad news. Better, because the apprentices had stayed. Not all of them; but more than half, and they had done what apprentices do, which is keep things running badly but running nonetheless. The kitchens were warm. The lamps were trimmed. Someone had swept the coal-chutes—the same chutes through which a certain keeper had once crawled to free a prisoner—and left the sweepings in neat piles, because even in the collapse of everything, order was a comfort.

They gathered in the great hall on his first evening, the remaining apprentices and journeymen, maybe sixty souls in a room built for four hundred, and Tam stood where Corvane had stood and understood viscerally, for the first time, how heavy a master's chain was before anyone ever fastened it around your wrist.

"I'm not going to pretend I know how to do this," he told them. His voice echoed embarrassingly in the vaulted space. "I'm nineteen years old. Three months ago my highest responsibility was grinding ink. So the first honest entry in the new ledger is this one: your new master does not know what he is doing."

A ripple went through the room—shock, then, unbelievably, laughter. A journeywoman named Pell, grey-haired and formidable, called out from the back: "Neither did Corvane, near the end. At least you've admitted it early."

"Then let me admit the rest of it too." Tam unslung the satchel he had carried across two worlds and set it on the master's podium, and began, one by one, to bring out the ledgers. Corvane's second ledger, chained once to a living wrist, now unlocked forever. The three books saved from the burning of Bellhollow, their covers smoke-scared, their prophecy-pages water-stained where he had shielded them from the rain with his own body. The founding charter, with the grandmother's hidden verse pressed flat between its leaves like a flower kept a century. And last, the newest book—blank except for a single page.

"The guild taught us that our trade was impossibility," he said. "That we dealt in wonders, and that wonder required mystery, and mystery required that most of what we did stay hidden—even from ourselves. Especially from ourselves. That was the rot. Not the magic. The hiding." He laid his hand flat on the open ledger. "From tonight, every trade this guild makes will be written here, in full, and this book will stand open in the entrance hall where anyone may read it. Every price paid. Every memory taken or given. Every name. If we cannot bear to have our work read aloud, then we should not be doing the work."

Silence. Then Pell rose, crossed the hall, and wrote the first entry herself, in her careful old clerk's hand: *Day one of the reformed Guild of Impossible Things. Nothing hidden. Nothing taken without consent. All debts named.*

One by one, the others came forward and added their names beneath it—not signatures of oath, Tam noticed, but simply names, the way singers' names are listed in a score. When it was done, sixty-one names stood in the book, and Tam looked at them and felt something in his chest that was not quite hope and not quite grief but lived in the country between, where all the true things seemed to live these days.

* * *

The work, after that, was endless and strangely sweet.

The hoarded tribute-power had to be returned—that was the first great labor, and it nearly broke them. For seventy years the guildmasters had skimmed the covenant's payments, diverting memories and years and voices into the vaults below the hall, and each item had to be traced to its origin and carried home. Some were easy: a jarred memory of a summer orchard, returned to a farmer in the hill country who wept over it for an hour and then asked, bewildered, why he had ever sold it. Some were impossible: the owners dead, the memories orphaned, and for these Tam established the Garden, a walled court behind the guildhall where the unclaimed recollections were planted in silver basins of Vaelish soil, where they bloomed as small soft lights that apprentices tended like flowers. People came to walk there. Sometimes a visitor would stop before a particular light and go still, and say, hesitantly, "I think—I think that might be my mother's laugh," and the apprentices would fetch a basin and a spade, and the memory would go home to be remembered by someone at last.

The wayshrines reopened, one by one, their seals rewritten in the covenant's plain gold script: *Given freely. Returned freely. Recorded honestly.* Trade caravans began to cross within the month—grain going east into Vael's twilight country, tide-song recordings and healing light coming west. Children from both shores met at the border-stones and invented games that needed no common language, which Tam privately considered the fastest diplomacy in history.

And through all of it, the reports came north from the seam, and Tam read every one.

Dain Ash had taken up residence in the waystation beside the gate—the same gate through which Lena had been pulled a lifetime ago, though it felt like several lifetimes now. He had appointed himself her historian. The reports said he visited daily, telling her their story from the beginning, every time, patient as water wearing stone: *Your name is Lena Sandoval. You kept the ledger. You found your name in a prophecy book. You were pulled through the gate, and you chose to stay.* And every day she listened courteously, the reports said, and thanked him kindly, and remembered none of it by morning.

Sef came to the guildhall once, in the deep of autumn, and found Tam in the Garden after dark, trimming the lights.

"You should go see her," she said, without preamble. She had never learned to approach a subject indirectly; it was one of the things he liked best about her. "The keeper. Dain writes that visitors are allowed. That she receives everyone. That she—" Sef paused, choosing words with unusual care. "That she is kind to strangers. Even when the strangers are crying."

"I know." Tam snipped a dead stem away from a basin of light. Inside it, someone's forgotten birthday glowed softly. "I've drafted the letter eleven times."

"What's stopping you?"

He set down the shears. In the silver light of the memories, his hands looked older than nineteen. "Because I knew her," he said. "Before. Not well—she was the keeper, I was the ink-boy—but she slipped me honey-bread during inventory week, and she covered for me the night I broke the sealing wax, and when I ran past her with the stolen ledger she didn't raise the alarm. She *let me go*. She chose me, in her small way, before any of this started." His throat tightened. "And now I'd walk in and she'd greet me the way she greets everyone. Warmly. Politely. As no one." He looked up. "Dain can bear that. He's decided to bear it, daily, forever. I haven't decided yet. I'm afraid my deciding would break."

Sef sat down beside him on the cold garden bench and said nothing for a long time, which was its own gift. Then: "In the council," she said finally, "the worst days were the ones when I stayed silent to protect a friendship. The friendships survived anyway, mostly. The silence never did me any good at all." She stood, and squeezed his shoulder once, hard, the way sailors do. "Go in winter. Go when the snow makes everything quiet. And take the ledger with you—the finished one. Let her read what she did. Dain tells her the story, but stories fade by morning. Ink stays."

* * *

Winter came early that year, and gentle, as if the mended seam had steadied the weather along with everything else.

On the first morning of real snow, Tam packed the completed covenant book into oilcloth, told Pell he would be gone a fortnight, and traveled north with a merchant caravan whose drivers sang Vaelish work-songs in accents that were half free-cities drawl, which would have been unthinkable a year ago and now was merely Tuesday. The waystation at the gate was small and warm and smelled of woodsmoke and bread. Dain Ash answered the door himself, greyer than Tam remembered, and looked at him for a long moment with eyes that had learned to measure every visitor against a grief they could not show.

"You're the apprentice," Dain said. "The one who carried the ledger through the fire."

"Tam."

"She talks about you." Dain stepped aside to let him in. "Not by name—she doesn't have the name. But the covenant shows her things, sometimes, in the quiet. Last week she told me there was a boy who ran past her desk with a book under his coat, and that she had watched him go and said nothing, and that the saying-nothing had mattered. She asked me if that was true." Dain's mouth twisted, somewhere between a smile and a wound. "Every day I tell her the whole story, and every day it's new to her. But the covenant keeps its own accounts. Bits surface. Like flotsam after a wreck." He paused at the inner door. "Are you ready? It doesn't get easier. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the whole religion now."

"No," Tam said. "But go on."

The keeper sat by the window, where the light came through the open gate behind her—actual open daylight from another world, falling into this one without permission or toll, because the door stood open now by choice and always would. She was wrapped in a grey cloak, and her hands were folded around a cup of tea, and when she turned at their entrance her face was exactly as Tam remembered it and completely, irrevocably different: the same steady features, the same level gaze, lit from underneath by the covenant's patient gold.

"Welcome," Lena Sandoval said, in the voice of a gracious stranger. "You've come a long way in the snow. Sit, please—there's bread." Then her gaze fell on the oilcloth bundle under his arm, and something moved across her face like a cloud crossing still water—there and gone, but unmistakably *something*. "What is that?"

Tam knelt and unwrapped it with fingers that would not quite obey him. The completed covenant book lay in his hands, its cover stamped with ten interlocking circles, nine for the singers and one for the keeper who chose. He opened it to the first page and turned it to face her.

"This is yours," he said. "I mean—all of it is ours. But this part especially. Read it whenever you like. Keep it as long as you like. It doesn't fade by morning. It's ink."

She took the book the way one takes a sleeping child. She read. The fire ticked. Outside, snow came down through the light of the open gate, falling into two worlds at once, and neither realm objected.

After a long time, the keeper looked up, and her eyes were wet, and she frowned at her own tears as though they belonged to someone else.

"I don't remember doing any of this," she said quietly. "But my hands are shaking. Isn't that strange?" She touched the page where her own farewell was written—*goodbye to each of them by name*—and looked up at Tam with the first crack of something beyond courtesy breaking through the gold. "Tell me again. All of it. Slowly. And then—" her voice caught, and steadied, and caught again, "—and then come back tomorrow, and tell me again."

Outside, faint on the wind, someone was singing.
