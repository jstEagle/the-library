## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There were three ways to balance a book at the Gilded Vane, and Ada Delacroix had mastered all of them.

The first was arithmetic, which was honest and therefore rarely used. The second was approximation, which Master Corvane called *flexibility* and which Ada called Tuesday. The third was the one he liked best: finding the story the numbers wanted to tell. Every ledger had a shape, and if you held the page at the right angle — literally, sometimes, tilting it toward the lamp — you could see where a figure had been coerced, where a sum had been bullied into agreeing with its betters. Numbers, like animals, flinched when they were handled roughly.

He had learned that last part from his grandmother, though he would have denied it under oath. He denied most things under oath. It was practically a hobby.

The counting room of the Gilded Vane occupied the third floor of a building that had been a lighthouse before the harbor silted over, and it still smelled faintly of salt and old light. Nine desks, nine clerks, and at the far end, behind a door of blackened glass, the office where Corvane kept the ledgers no one was supposed to know existed. Ada's own desk sat nearest the window, which was either a courtesy or a test. He had never decided which. From there he could see the harbor cranes standing in the fog like patient herons, and beyond them, far to the north, the smudge of the Graywall Mountains where the border wards ran — invisible, ancient, and, everyone agreed, utterly sound.

Everyone agreed this the way people agree the floor will hold. It is only true until it isn't.

Ada dipped his pen and wrote: *Received, one (1) vial, tears of a basilisk, condition: dubious, provenance: worse.* The Gilded Vane traded in impossible things. That was the whole of the business model. If a thing could be bought anywhere else, the Vane didn't want it. Basilisk tears. A key that unlocked yesterday. The last honest man in Vell, sold by weight. Ada recorded them all with the same flat competence he brought to grain shipments, and if his hand sometimes slowed over the stranger entries — if he sometimes felt, absurdly, that the ink was watching him back — he had long ago taught himself to call that imagination and move on.

Imagination was a safe place to put things you didn't want to look at directly. He had been using it as a warehouse for years.

*Sixty-one crowns,* he wrote. *Sold to a buyer whose name is a rumor.*

The lamp guttered. Outside, the fog had thickened until the harbor was a rumor of its own. The other clerks had gone home hours ago; Ada stayed late because staying late was a kind of armor. A man alone with his books could not be asked what he was. A man alone with his books was, in the Vane's grammar, a man who had chosen the right side of the glass door.

He was halfway through the stormhawk's invoice when he heard the whisper.

He did not hear it with his ears. That was the lie he had built, brick by brick, over thirty years: that the hearing was ordinary, that the voice in the aviary below was probably a keeper muttering, that the shiver along his forearms was the draft from the window. The Gilded Vane kept a menagerie in the old lamp room — stock, mostly, impossible creatures awaiting buyers — and among the stock was a stormhawk in a cage of silver-wrapped ash, because stormhawks could not be caged in anything that had never been alive.

The whisper came again, thin as wire: *thin. thin. thinning.*

Ada set down his pen.

He told himself he was going downstairs to check an invoice. He told himself this the way a man tells himself the floor will hold.

* * *

The stairwell to the lamp room was unlit, and Ada climbed it the way he did everything secret: quietly, apologetically, as if the darkness might be offended by him. His boots knew the steps. That was the trouble with secrets — they got into the body. His grandmother had taught him to walk silently before she taught him letters, and she had taught him letters in code, and she had taught him, above all, the first law of their family, the law that had kept them alive since the Ember War: *Never let them hear you hear.*

Beast-speakers. The word itself was contraband. During the war, the old alliance had used speakers to command the ward-beasts, the great bound guardians who held the border lines, and after the war — after the Ember was buried and the alliance dissolved into mutual suspicion — the new councils had decided that anyone who could speak to the things that held the world together was too dangerous to be left unwatched. So the gift had been outlawed, and its holders had been burned or scattered or, in the case of Ada's line, taught to be very, very quiet.

His grandmother had gone further than quiet. She had taught him to disbelieve himself. When the neighbor's dog told him where her lost puppies were, his grandmother would say, *You guessed.* When the cart-horse complained about its harness, she would say, *You imagined it.* She had built the lie so carefully, so lovingly, that by the time he was grown it had the load-bearing strength of a wall, and Ada lived inside it the way he lived inside his own skin — without noticing, because noticing would mean knocking on it.

Except at moments like this. Moments when the wall hummed.

He opened the lamp room door and the cold hit him first, then the smell — feathers and ozone and the particular metallic sadness of caged things — and then the whisper, no longer a whisper but a voice with a shape to it, a voice like wind through a broken window.

*Thinning. The line is thinning. I can taste it through the stone. Little clerk. Little clerk who hears. The line is thinning.*

The stormhawk stood on its perch in the silver cage, and it was looking directly at him. Not the way birds look at people, sideways and dismissive, but the way a witness looks at a courtroom. Its feathers were the grey of a sky deciding, and its eyes were the color of lightning's memory. Around its left leg, where the guild's inventory band should have been, there was instead an old scar, long healed, in the shape of a knot.

Ada knew that knot. He had seen it in a drawing in his grandmother's book, the book she had burned the winter she died, the book he had memorized anyway because burning a thing does not unteach a boy who reads.

*Ward-bird,* he thought, and the thought arrived with the weight of a confession. *It's a ward-bird. It was bound to the line.*

"Thinning where?" he said — and then stopped, appalled, because he had answered it. He had spoken to it. Out loud. In a guild full of knives.

The stormhawk tilted its head, and something almost like pity moved through its voice. *North. The Graywall. The old knot. The line was woven by speakers and it is unraveling because there are no speakers left to mend it, and the ones who could mend it are hiding in counting houses pretending to be arithmetic.* A rasp that might have been laughter. *You smell like her, little clerk. The one who wove. The one who ran.*

"I don't know what you're talking about."

*No,* the stormhawk agreed, gently, horribly. *You don't. That is the whole of your tragedy. You have spent your life not-knowing so hard that you've forgotten you were ever taught.* It stepped closer to the bars, and the silver cage hummed. *Listen to me. I was taken from the line three seasons ago. I felt it let go of my leg like a hand going slack. The line is not merely thinning. It is being *unpicked*. Thread by thread. Sold. Someone is selling the border of the world, and the buyers are not buying to keep it.*

The fog pressed against the lamp room glass. Somewhere below, in the counting room, Ada's unfinished ledger lay open, and he thought suddenly — absurdly, with a clerk's instinct — of the entries. The ward-iron shipments. The *fragments, provenance: Graywall salvage, condition: viable* that Corvane had begun recording two years ago, always in small lots, always to the same unnamed buyer. He had entered them a hundred times. He had entered them the way he entered everything: flatly, competently, without looking at the shape of the whole.

Numbers, like animals, flinched when they were handled roughly. And a whole ledger, held at the right angle, could tell you a story.

*You feel it too,* the stormhawk said. It was not a question. *The dread under the floor of the day. You have felt it for two years and you have called it the weather. Call it what it is. It is the line going slack, and you are one of perhaps nine people alive who could feel it, and you have been using the feeling to balance books.*

"I balance books," Ada said. His voice sounded far away, a stranger's voice, someone standing in a doorway he couldn't remember walking to. "That's all I do. That's all I've ever—"

The hawk screamed.

Not at him — past him, through him, out the lamp room glass at the north, at the smudge of mountains invisible in the fog, and the scream had layers in it, the way a chord has notes, and one of the layers was a word, and the word was:

*TONIGHT.*

Then silence, total, the kind of silence that has a floor dropped out of it. The stormhawk gripped its perch, feathers flat, breathing hard, a soldier after a report.

Ada stood with his hand on the cage and realized two things with terrible clarity.

The first was that he had just committed, in the eyes of the councils, a capital act. He had spoken with a beast. He had *heard* one. The scar his grandmother had pressed into him — *never let them hear you hear* — was torn open, and what bled through it was not fear exactly but something worse: relief. Thirty years of pretending not to be himself, and one bird had seen through it in a minute, and some starved part of him was *grateful*.

The second thing was the ledger. The fragments. *Condition: viable.* Two years of viable fragments going north to a buyer whose name was a rumor, in the same season the line began to thin.

He was still standing there, doing the clerk's arithmetic of horror — *if each lot is a thread, and there were four lots last quarter, and the line has* — when the first lamp in the harbor went out.

Not the fog dimming it. Out. Snuffed, all at once, like a candle pinched by a wet finger. Then the next lamp. Then the next, a line of small deaths marching along the quay, and beneath his feet, through the floor, through the stone, through the old lighthouse bones of the building, Ada felt a hum change pitch — the deep, constant, unnoticed hum that the whole city stood on, the hum of wards so old no one remembered they were wards — falter.

Stutter.

And stop.

The fog did not lift. But the stars, when he looked up through the lamp room glass, were simply gone — not clouded, not veiled. *Gone*, as if the sky had been a ledger too, and someone had ruled a line through it.

*Thinning,* the stormhawk whispered, into the ringing dark. *No, little clerk. Not thinning anymore.*

Ada's hand found the cage bars. The silver was warm. Everything else was going cold.

"Tonight," he said aloud, to no one, to the bird, to the grandmother who had taught him to be small. "You said tonight."

*And it is tonight,* said the stormhawk. *And you, who hear everything, are the only one in this city who was warned. Ask yourself who else knew. Ask yourself who profits when a border dies. You keep the books, little clerk. Go read them.*

Below, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of the black glass door opening, and Master Corvane's voice, calm as arithmetic, saying to someone in the dark: "Show him the fragments. He'll want to inspect the merchandise before the roads fill up."

The roads. Fill up. As if Corvane already knew what was coming down them.

Ada stood a moment longer with his hand on the warm silver, in the cold dark, in the wreckage of the lie he had lived inside for thirty years. Then he did the only thing his body knew how to do with a secret.

He picked up his lamp, and he went down the stairs without making a sound.
