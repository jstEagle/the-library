## 2. A Night Without Stars

The trouble with impossible things, Ada Delacroix had learned in nine years of keeping the Gilded Vane's ledgers, was that they arrived without receipts.

He sat in the counting room on the third floor of the Vane's headquarters, a candle guttering beside him, and tried to make the night's column balance. It would not balance. Somewhere between the entry for forty vials of dragonmilk — diluted, if the assay clerk had any spine, which he didn't — and the sale of a singing stone to a merchant from Callowmere, three hundred crowns had simply evaporated. Not stolen. Evaporated. The Gilded Vane did not suffer theft; theft implied someone had gotten past Master Corvane's locks, and Corvane's locks were the kind that took offense.

So: evaporation. Which meant someone upstairs was skimming, and skimming meant Ada would spend tomorrow being polite to people he despised, and tonight he would spend finding out who.

He dipped his pen. Outside the window, Vespergate lay spread below like a spilled jewelry box — ten thousand lamps, the river running silver through its middle, and above it all the stars, thick as salt thrown across black velvet.

The stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Went out*, all at once, from horizon to horizon, as though a hand had passed over the sky and closed it.

Ada's pen kept moving for a full three seconds before his mind caught up with his hand. Then he looked up. The ink line ran off the edge of the ledger page and pooled against the leather binding, a small black wound.

"Quill," he said aloud, because saying things aloud made them less like dreams. His voice sounded very small in the counting room.

The stormhawk in its cage by the window screamed.

It had been delivered that afternoon — another impossible thing awaiting appraisal, a raptor the size of a heron with feathers like hammered pewter and eyes the color of lightning through smoke. It had not spoken to him. It never spoke first; nothing ever spoke first, because speaking first was how you got caught, and Ada had spent thirty-one years perfecting the art of not being spoken to. He kept his gift folded up inside himself like a letter he'd decided never to send. Beast-speakers were burned in some provinces and taxed in others, and in Vespergate they were simply disappeared, quietly, the way the Vane disappeared bad debts. So when animals addressed him — and they did, constantly, a low murmur of wants and warnings he'd spent decades learning to ignore — he pretended deafness.

But the stormhawk screamed now, a sound like tearing metal, and beat itself against the bars hard enough to crack a wing-feather, and beneath the scream Ada heard — felt — words:

*Thin. Thin. Thin. Falling.*

He stood so fast his chair went over backward. "What's falling? What's—"

The floor shuddered.

It was not an earthquake. Earthquakes had a character, a rolling patience. This was a single deep concussion, as if something vast underground had turned over in its sleep, and it came up through the soles of Ada's boots and into his teeth. The candle flame bent sideways. Inkwell walked itself an inch across the desk. In the cage, the stormhawk pressed itself flat and went silent in a way that was worse than screaming.

Then the bells began.

Every temple bell in Vespergate, ringing at once, at midnight, on a night with no stars. Ada had lived through two riots and one plague year and he had never heard this. This was the old alarm, the one from storybooks, the one that hadn't been rung since his grandmother's grandmother's time: the wards are down.

*The border wards are down.*

He knew it the way you know a tooth is cracked — not because anyone told you but because suddenly everything about your mouth is wrong. The wards. The great woven shields that had stood along the northern marches since the Ember War, that no one thought about, that were less walls than weather, more constant than the king. Down. All of them, or enough of them that the difference wouldn't matter by morning.

And Ada Delacroix, keeper of ledgers, stood in a room full of other people's money and thought, with a shame that arrived hot and immediate: *the guild knew.*

Because there it was, in front of him, had been in front of him for weeks. The odd purchases. The shipments routed through shell-houses in Lowmarsh. The entries coded in the old mercantile cipher that only three people could read, and he was one of them, and he had chosen — deliberately, comfortably, with the same care he used choosing wine — not to read them closely. Three hundred crowns evaporating tonight was nothing. There were pages in the back room, pages in Corvane's private strongroom, where the numbers stopped behaving like numbers at all.

The second concussion came, deeper than the first, and somewhere below in the street a woman began to scream, and then many women, and dogs, and the particular collective roar of a city understanding all at once that it was afraid.

Ada picked up his chair. Sat down. Picked up his pen.

It was the most human thing he could think of to do. The world's rules were breaking — the sky had been switched off, the ancient protections had failed, something under the mountains had moved — and his hands wanted, desperately, to do the only work they knew. He drew a line under the unbalanced column. Wrote, in his small precise hand: *Discrepancy carried forward.* As if the discrepancy were the problem. As if anything would be carried forward at all, if the stories about what slept beneath the Wardline were true.

The stormhawk watched him with its lightning eyes.

"You knew," Ada said quietly. "That's why they brought you today. Someone sold you here knowing."

The hawk said nothing. But it turned its head, slow and deliberate, toward the door — toward the stairs that led up to Corvane's offices — and Ada understood that too.

* * *

By the time he climbed to the fourth floor, the guild was already making money off the end of the world.

He heard it before he saw it, coming up the service stair with his clerk's satchel clutched to his chest like a breastplate: Corvane's voice, smooth as poured tallow, and beneath it the murmur of commerce. Ada should have gone back down. Every instinct honed by nine years of survival said go back down, count your columns, know nothing. Instead he stopped on the landing, in the dark beside a shelf of retired ledgers, and looked through the gap where the door stood ajar.

Master Corvane's office was lit by lamps shuttered to slits. Corvane himself sat behind his desk looking, as always, like a man carved by someone competent but uninspired — broad, grey-templed, respectable, his rings catching the light. Across from him, half in shadow, sat a figure in ash-grey robes with the hood drawn forward, so that where a face should have been there was only a suggestion of one, the way fog suggests a hillside.

Between them on the desk lay a wooden case, open, lined in velvet. Inside, nested like eggs, were six fragments of pale lattice-work, each the size of a playing card, each faintly luminous.

Ada's breath stopped.

He knew what those were. Everyone knew what those were, the way everyone knew what a saint's fingerbone was supposed to look like. Ward-shards. Pieces of the great working, broken off — *harvested*, the euphemism would be — and impossibly, criminally, for sale.

"—of course the timing is regrettable," Corvane was saying, and his voice had the mild irritation of a man discussing a delayed shipment of silk. "But I'd argue the opposite, my friend. Scarcity clarifies value. Last month these were curiosities. Tonight—" he gestured upward, at the starless ceiling, with the air of a man gesturing at a modest rise in the market, "—tonight they are the only currency that matters. Six shards. Each one sufficient to anchor a local warding, a compound, a keep, say. Perhaps a small town, if the town is willing to live cold."

The hooded figure spoke. Its voice was dry, like paper turning, like wind through a burnt field. "The mountain does not want towns warded. The mountain wants the road *open*."

"And the mountain," Corvane said pleasantly, "will have what it has paid for. The northern line is down, isn't it? All the way from Greywatch to the Salt Pass. My people assure me the harvest was... thorough." He closed the case lid with a soft click, tenderly, the way other men touched their children. "You'll forgive me if I don't pretend to understand what wakes down there. I'm a merchant. I sell certainty in uncertain shapes. What you do with it is theology."

"The payment," said the ash-grey figure, "is already moving. As agreed. And there will be more orders. Many more. When the second line falls."

Corvane smiled. It was, Ada thought, the worst smile he had ever seen, precisely because it was so ordinary — the smile of a man closing a good quarter. "Then we should discuss volume discounts."

Ada stepped back from the door. His heel found the edge of the stair and for one horrible moment the whole landing tilted — or he tilted, or the world did, he couldn't tell anymore — vertigo rising through him like floodwater. Because this was the thing his mind kept snagging on, kept refusing: the sky was empty, the wards were fallen, refugees were already screaming in the streets below, and upstairs, by lamplight, the price of salvation was being negotiated with a discount schedule. The world had ended and the books still balanced. That was the horror. Not that the rules had broken, but that they hadn't — not for the Vane. For the Vane, tonight was simply a seller's market.

He got himself down the service stairs without falling. He wasn't sure how.

* * *

Vespergate had come apart in the orderly way cities do, which is to say completely.

Ada went out into it because staying in the counting room had become unbearable, and because — he told himself — he needed to verify the street-level figures. Even now, even with the sky blind, the clerk in him reached for numbers the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood.

Gull Street was a river of torches and terror. Refugees from the north gate were pouring in — actual refugees, hours ahead of any plausible travel, which meant the wards hadn't just failed tonight, they'd failed *before* tonight, and the news had traveled faster than any rider. Farmers still in their field clothes. A drover leading horses whose eyes rolled white. A woman carrying a child who was not crying, which was somehow worse than crying, the child just staring up at the starless dark with an expression Ada recognized because he'd seen it in his own mirror: the face of someone revising every assumption they'd ever made.

"They came out of the ground," the woman was saying, to no one, to everyone. "North of Harrow Ford. Out of the *ground*. The ward-stones just — went dark, all in a row, like snuffed candles, and then—"

"Here." Ada pressed his own coin purse into her free hand. She stared at him. He had no idea why he'd done it. Nine years of hoarding salary against a future he'd never quite believed in, given away in four seconds to a stranger. "There's a temple on Candlemaker's Row taking people in. Tell them Ada sent you. They won't know who that is. Tell them anyway."

He moved on. The Gilded Vane's agents were already in the crowd — he marked their grey-and-gold sashes, the little brass pins shaped like a weathervane — and they weren't handing out coins. They were buying. He watched a junior factor corner a terrified carter whose wagon was loaded with salt-meat and offer him a tenth of its worth, and watch the carter take it, because a tenth of something was more than all of nothing, and the factor wrote it in his book with a steady hand while the sky stayed black above them both.

*Commerce continues,* Ada thought, and the thought had teeth. *The world breaks and we raise our prices. We were doing it before tonight, too. We've been doing it for years. I balanced those entries. I initialed them.*

A dog pressed against his leg in the crush — a big mastiff-type, terrified, and it pushed its fear at him without meaning to, a wordless red wash of *run run run the ground is wrong the ground is wrong* — and Ada had to grip a lamppost until it passed. Control. Fold it away. Seal the letter. No one must see his face change when the animals spoke.

Above the rooftops, where stars should have been, there was nothing. Not clouds — he could see the moon's absence as a kind of negative glow, a hole in the shape of where light ought to pool. The constellations that sailors steered by, that the almanac in his satchel promised would rise at such and such hours until the end of time: gone. The end of time, apparently, had a date on it after all.

He was halfway back to the Vane when the third concussion hit, closer now, and every lamp in Gull Street guttered at once, and in the half-dark he heard — felt, through whatever buried sense he spent his life denying — something enormous and slow and *hungry* turn its attention toward the world of men. It lasted a heartbeat. It felt like years.

Around him, thousands of people felt it too, without any gift at all. The whole street flinched like a single animal.

When the lamps steadied, Ada was on his knees on the cobbles and couldn't remember getting there.

* * *

He made it back to the counting room near dawn, though dawn brought no light, only a greyer species of dark. The stormhawk was awake, watching the door. On his desk, where he had no memory of leaving it, lay a summons card in Corvane's hand:

*Delacroix — my office, ninth bell. Bring the quarterly ledgers. All of them.*

*All of them.* Meaning the coded ones. Meaning the pages in the back room. Meaning: we both know what you might have read, and tomorrow morning we will find out together whether you read it.

Ada sat down at his desk. The unbalanced column waited for him, patient as a grave. He looked at it for a long time, and then he did something he had never done in nine years of service.

He opened the ledger to the current page and wrote, in the margin, in cipher, where no auditor would look and no honest man would think to:

*Night of the falling stars: six ward-shards sold to a buyer in ash-grey. Buyer speaks of "the mountain." Second line expected to fall. Corvane expects volume.*

He blotted it. Closed the book. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, everything was floodwater.

In the cage, the stormhawk dipped its head — once, formally, the way one soldier acknowledges another — and said, in a voice like wind over high crags:

*Now you have begun.*

Outside, the bells rang on, and the first refugees' fires kindled in the temple squares like earthbound stars, because the real ones had abandoned them, and Ada Delacroix sat alone with the accounts of the end of the world, waiting for ninth bell.
