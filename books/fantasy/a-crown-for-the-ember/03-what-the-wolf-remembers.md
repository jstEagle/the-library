## 3. What the Wolf Remembers

The gates of Vane's Crossing had not opened this wide in forty years, and Ada Delacroix stood in the flood that came through them.

Refugees poured down the causeway like water finding a crack in a dam — farmers with their seed-sacks tied to their backs, a cooper carrying his tools and nothing else, children asleep on shoulders, an old man leading a goat that kept looking back the way they'd come. The border wards had fallen two nights ago, and the news had traveled faster than any rider: *the stars went out, the wards are down, run.*

Ada had come to the gate for the guild. That was the story he told himself as he moved through the crush with a tally-slate under his arm. The Gilded Vane was feeding people at its own expense — Master Corvane had ordered it, which meant there was a ledger entry somewhere explaining why charity suddenly made sense, and Ada would find it eventually, because he found everything in the ledgers eventually. For now his job was to count. Count the mouths. Count the blankets. Count the cost.

He counted because counting was safe. Numbers didn't whisper warnings from cages. Numbers didn't go out like stars.

"Flour to the east tables," he told the guild steward, and she nodded, and he wrote *40 sacks* on his slate, and the world held its shape for one more line.

It was the third day of the flood, and the city had stopped pretending it wasn't afraid. Merchants had boarded their windows against panic rather than weather. The temple bells rang wrong — too fast, too many — and nobody corrected them. And everywhere, threaded through the crowd like rot through fruit, the same question passed mouth to mouth:

*What broke the wards?*

Nobody said *who*. Saying who meant saying what the guild sold, and nobody knew that yet except Ada, and the hooded buyer, and whatever lived beneath the mountains.

He pushed through the crowd toward the east tables, slate tucked under his arm, and that was when the wolf looked at him.

* * *

It stood near the well at the edge of the square, grey as old ash, and it did not belong there.

That was Ada's first thought, and his second, and his third, because everything else about the animal argued with itself. It was too thin to be healthy and too calm to be starving. Its coat was scarred in long pale seams, as if something had tried to peel it once and failed. One ear was torn to lace. It stood in the middle of three hundred terrified people and none of them saw it — that was the part that made Ada's skin crawl. A wolf in a refugee square should have caused a riot. Dogs should have been screaming. Instead the crowd flowed around it like it was furniture, like it was weather, like their eyes slid off it the way eyes slide off a debt you don't want to count.

Only Ada saw it.

And only Ada heard it say his name.

Not aloud. Not in words. He'd have preferred words. What came instead was a pressure behind his eyes, a door opening in a room he'd bricked shut decades ago, and through the door poured —

— snow falling upward off a burning ridge —

— a woman's hands, cracked and ink-stained, signing *be still* to a hawk the size of a horse —

— horns, deep and wrong, sounding across a valley where the mountains themselves were glowing at the seams like coals breathed on —

— and a face. A face Ada knew better than his own heartbeat, because he'd spent thirty years trying to forget it and failing every single time.

His grandmother.

Maren Delacroix, standing in a doorway with her shawl pinned wrong, saying, *You'll hide it, little fox. You'll hide it so deep even you won't know where you put it.* Except she wasn't saying it now, wasn't speaking at all; she was dead twenty years, and here she was inside a memory that wasn't his, wearing a younger face than the one he'd buried, laughing at something out of frame while a stormhawk wheeled overhead and the world was still whole and the wards still burned bright along the northern passes like a wall of dawn.

Ada stopped walking.

The tally-slate slipped from under his arm. Someone caught it, or someone didn't; he never learned which. The crowd's noise went distant and woolly, the way sound goes underwater, and all he could hear — all he could *feel* — was the wolf, standing thirty feet away, looking at him with amber eyes gone milk-pale at the rims with age, and pouring his grandmother into him like water into a cup.

*Stop,* he thought at it. *Stop, I don't —*

*I know what you are,* the memory said, in Maren's voice, in no voice, in the voice of the thing itself, which was older than either. *I remember what your blood is. I carried your grandmother on my back out of the Ember War when she could no longer walk. I have been waiting for her heir since before you were born.*

"I'm not—" Ada said aloud, and a woman with a baby gave him a strange look and steered wide around him, the way people do around madmen and priests.

*You are,* the wolf said, gentle, terrible. *Beast-speaker. Ward-kin. Last of the line that helped bind the Ember the first time. You've hidden it so long you flinch when a caged bird looks at you. But hiding does not unmake, Ada Delacroix. The wards are down. The Ember stirs. And I am dying.*

The last sentence landed differently than the others. Quieter. Ada looked at the wolf properly then — really looked — and saw what he'd refused to see: the way its ribs showed through the grey coat, the tremor in its forelegs, the milk-blindness creeping in from the edges of its eyes. It wasn't thin from hunger. It was thin the way a wick is thin when the candle is almost done.

"What are you?" he whispered.

*A guardian.* Something moved through the memory-flood that might have been pride and might have been grief. *Bound to the ward-line when it was raised. There were nine of us. I am what remains when the wards fall — the lock remembering the door. Your grandmother bound me to her line so that someone would always be able to find me. She hoped it would never matter.*

*It matters now.*

Ada's hands were shaking. He shoved them into his coat pockets where no one could see, and made himself breathe the way he breathed over a difficult audit: slow, even, four counts in, four counts out. Around him the refugees kept flowing, the stew steam kept rising, the bells kept ringing wrong. The world was ending politely, one small catastrophe at a time, and here was a dying wolf telling him he was heir to a war.

"My grandmother hid her gift," he said. "She made me promise. She said if anyone ever learned what I was, they'd use me until I broke."

*She was right,* the wolf said. *They will. Someone already has begun.*

Cold walked up Ada's spine. *What do you mean? Who?*

The wolf turned its head, slow, deliberate, toward the Gilded Vane's banner hanging above the east tables — the gilded vane itself, the weathervane-cock wrought in gold leaf, turning lazily in a wind that had no direction anymore. Then back to Ada.

*Ask your master what happened to the ward-spirits of the eastern line,* it said. *Ask him why the fragments your guild sells still scream at night when they think no one is listening. Ask him what feeds the thing beneath the mountain, and what price it paid, and who has been paying it for thirty years in installments.*

"You're wrong." The words came out flat and hard, and Ada hated how much they sounded like hope. "Corvane is ruthless but he's not — the guild doesn't deal in *that*, we deal in rare things, impossible things, but not—"

*You balance the books,* the wolf said, and there was no cruelty in it, which was worse. *Have you ever once asked where the impossible things come from?*

Ada opened his mouth. Closed it. Because there was an answer forming, an answer made of every odd entry he'd ever smoothed over, every shipment logged as "stone, assorted" that weighed too little, every payment received in coin minted by kingdoms that hadn't existed for a century. Thirty years of anomalies, and he'd filed them all under *guild business* and slept soundly, because sleeping soundly was the one luxury the ledgers allowed him.

*Your grandmother refused the crown,* the wolf said, softer now. *When they offered it to her at the end of the war. Refusing saved her life and cost the world its safety — the binding was weaker without a wearer, and everyone knew it, and everyone agreed to pretend otherwise. She chose you instead. A quiet life. A hidden gift. She died believing she'd bought you peace.*

*The peace is spent, Ada. The bill has come due. And I need you to carry what I know north, to the holdfast of the unforgiven, before my heart gives out and takes the last of the ward-line's memory with it.*

"No." Ada heard his own voice crack on it. "No, I'm a clerk. I keep accounts. I don't march north, I don't talk to wolves, I don't—" His breath hitched. "I spent thirty years making sure nobody ever looked at me. You looked. Do you understand what you've done? If the guild learns what I am—"

*They will learn regardless,* the wolf said. *The Ember wakes hungry, and hunger draws hunters, and your guild serves the hunger. The only question left is whether you are holding the ledgers when it happens — or running from them.*

For a moment neither of them spoke. The square roared softly around them. Somewhere a child was crying, and somewhere else someone laughed, because grief and relief share a table in refugee camps and neither pays the other's bill.

And underneath the terror — underneath the cold certainty that his life as he'd built it had just ended in a public square next to a well — something in Ada's chest turned toward the wolf like a plant toward light. Thirty years of hiding. Thirty years of flinching from caged birds and biting his tongue when horses complained about their shoes. Thirty years alone inside a secret worn smooth as river stone.

This creature knew. This creature had known his grandmother, had carried her, remembered her laugh. Being seen hurt like a wound cleaning. Being seen felt like the first warm thing in decades.

That was the trap of it, and Ada knew traps — he audited them for a living. Salvation and sentence, offered in the same breath, by the same scarred mouth.

"How long do you have?" he asked quietly.

*Days,* the wolf said. *Perhaps less. The wards fell; I begin to unravel with them. Come to the tannery lane after dark, when the guild's eyes are counting coin instead of clerks. Bring nothing. Tell no one.*

"And if I don't come?"

The wolf looked at him for a long moment, and what it sent him then was not words at all. It was the ridge again, the upward snow, the horns — and then Maren Delacroix, older, grayer, standing at a window with her hand pressed flat against the glass, watching the northern horizon where the wards used to burn, and weeping without any sound at all.

Then it turned and limped away into the crowd, and the crowd's eyes slid off it, and it was gone as though the square had never held anything but people.

Ada stood a long time. Eventually his legs remembered how to work, and he retrieved his tally-slate from where it lay — scuffed, one corner cracked — and looked down at the last number he'd written before the world broke open.

*40 sacks flour.*

Such a small, clean number. Such a small, clean life, balanced to the penny, and every line of it suddenly written in another hand.

He walked back toward the Gilded Vane through the refugee flood, and above him the golden weathervane turned and turned in a wind that came from nowhere, pointing at nothing, pointing at everything, and Ada Delacroix — beast-speaker, ward-kin, heir to a crown his grandmother had refused — began, for the first time in thirty years, to plan how to lie to his master.

The tannery lane. After dark.

He had until nightfall to decide which of his lives he intended to keep.
