## 4. Entries in Another Hand

The archive of the Gilded Vane lived in the cellar beneath the counting house, and like everything the guild owned, it was kept behind a lock that cost more than most people's houses.

Ada had the key because he had always had the key. That was the arrangement: Corvane handled the buyers and the blade-work; Ada handled the truth. Numbers didn't lie, and neither did he, and between them they kept the Vane's books clean enough to survive any audit the city magistrates cared to run — which was to say, clean on the pages they were allowed to see.

He came down after midnight with a lamp, a pot of cold tea, and the stormhawk's warning still lodged under his ribs like a fishbone.

*The wards are thinning.*

Three days now, and he hadn't told anyone. He'd told himself it was nothing — birds exaggerated, hawks especially, dramatic creatures who treated every cold snap as an apocalypse. He'd told himself the tremor he'd felt through the cage bars was just the bird's fear bleeding into him, the way animal emotion always bled into him when he let his guard slip. He'd told himself so many things that the telling had worn grooves, and now the lies ran smooth and deep as aqueducts.

But the ledgers wouldn't lie. The ledgers never did.

That was why he'd come down. Not for the current year's book — he knew that one by heart, could recite the quarter's margins in his sleep. He wanted the old ones. The deep ones. Because if the wards had been thinning for three days, then someone, somewhere, might have written down *why*, and if the guild traded in impossible things, then thinning wards were exactly the kind of impossible thing that left a paper trail.

The cellar smelled of dust and tallow and old ink gone brown. Shelves climbed into the dark, organized by decade, each ledger spine stamped with the Vane's sigil — a coin with a feather through it, gilded, smug. Ada pulled the oldest volume within reach of the lamplight first. Forty years back. Then, on impulse, the one before it. Then the one before *that*.

He set them in a stack on the reading table and opened the eldest, and the first thing he noticed was the hand.

It wasn't his hand. It wasn't Corvane's. It belonged to some clerk dead before Ada was born — a narrow, upright script, disciplined but human, the letters leaning slightly forward like a listener. A name was inked inside the front cover in that same hand:

*Tobias Merren, Keeper of Entries.*

Ada had never heard the name. In nine years of keeping the Vane's books, no one had ever mentioned a predecessor. He'd assumed, vaguely, that Corvane had built the ledgers himself, or inherited them from some faceless line of clerks. Clerks were interchangeable. That was rather the point of clerks.

He turned pages. Grain contracts. Salt futures. The standard commerce of a guild that laundered its strangeness through ordinary trade until the strangeness looked respectable. He skimmed a decade in twenty minutes, and found nothing, and felt something in his chest unknot slightly —

— and then, near the back of the volume, the entries changed.

Not the hand. The hand stayed Merren's, steady as ever. But the *content* shifted, the way a conversation shifts when a third person enters the room. The transactions stopped being grain and salt and started being things Ada recognized from the other side of the guild's smile.

*Sold: one ward-stone fragment, provenance border-shrine at Cael Ford. Buyer: private. Price: not entered.*

*Sold: two fragments, provenance the old watchline. Buyer: private. Note: buyer requested proof of function. Proof provided.*

*Sold: fragment, provenance [entry torn out]. Buyer: private. Note: buyer paid triple for tears in the stone. Asked whether the stone suffered. Told yes. Paid more.*

Ada read that last note twice. *Asked whether the stone suffered.*

Ward-stones weren't stones. Everyone knew that, in the way everyone knows things they've agreed not to think about. They were bound things — spirits, small gods, whatever you wanted to call the intelligence that had once agreed to hold a line against the Ember War's fire and never stop holding it. The war had ended sixty years ago. The wards had stood since. And somewhere along the way, the Gilded Vane had begun selling pieces of them off like a whale fallen on a beach, rendered for oil.

His own hands were steady. He noticed that with a strange detachment, the way you notice weather. Later, he supposed, they would shake. For now there was only the reading, and the reading was a kind of falling.

He worked backward through the stack. Merren's entries thinned as the decades turned — fewer sales, longer gaps, and then, in the last volume of Merren's tenure, a gap of eleven years followed by a single final entry in a shakier version of the same upright hand:

*I have asked Corvane to stop. I have asked him what the fragments are for. He smiled at me the way he smiles at buyers. I am writing this where only a keeper would look, because I begin to suspect the keepers do not retire.*

Below it, pressed hard enough to nearly tear the page, a single line:

*They are not selling the wards. They are unmaking them. And the buyer is feeding them to something under the mountains. The Ember is not dead. The Ember is being FED.*

The lamp flame leaned. Or Ada leaned. He couldn't tell which.

He sat very still in the cellar dark and listened to the building above him — the settling creak of timber, the muffled tread of the night guard making his rounds, the ordinary sounds of an ordinary guild on an ordinary night. Somewhere up there, Master Corvane was asleep, or pretending to be, wearing the patient, reasonable face he wore the way other men wore rings.

*The Ember is not dead.*

Everyone knew the Ember was dead. It was history, the kind with monuments. The Ember War had ended when the alliance of the northern holds and the river cities had bound the great fire beneath the mountains and strung the wards across the borders like a net of sleeping will. There were festivals about it. Children dressed as ward-wardens with paper crowns. The whole peace of the world sat on that story, and the story said: *it is finished, it is buried, go back to sleep.*

And here was a dead man's handwriting saying the guild had spent forty years quietly digging.

Ada thought of the stormhawk, pressing its breast against the bars, whispering *thinning, thinning* like a prayer. Thought of the stars, how he'd counted them last week from his window and felt, without knowing why, that the count was wrong. Thought of refugees — no, not yet, there were no refugees yet, but there would be, because wards that thinned eventually tore, and everything the wards held back was patient.

He thought, with a cold new clarity, about the prices. The Vane had raised rates twice this season. He'd assumed greed. Greed was the guild's native weather. But greed had a logic, and the logic fit: if you know the walls are coming down, you charge the drowning double.

"Keeper of Entries," Ada murmured to the empty cellar. His voice sounded wrong down here, too flat, too close. "What happened to you, Tobias?"

No answer. Dead men kept their entries and gave up their endings.

He did the thing he was made for. He kept reading.

* * *

By the third hour he had a system. He worked backward through the decades, cross-referencing Merren's fragment sales against the shipping manifests in the adjacent volumes, building in his head the shape of a trade route that had no name and no destination — goods leaving the city under innocuous labels (quarry stone, ballast, "architectural salvage") and vanishing at a relay town called Greyhollow, past which the manifests simply stopped pretending.

Forty years. Dozens of fragments. Each one a piece of a spirit that had sworn, once, to hold a line forever. Sold to a buyer who paid extra to hear that the stone suffered.

And the wards were thinning. Of course they were thinning. You could flay a god in installments for four decades and the god would not thank you for your patience.

Ada copied nothing. That was instinct, older than thought — a clerk's deepest reflex, *never leave evidence of the evidence*. But his memory was a ledger too, and he filled it, entry by entry, dates and weights and prices and the torn-out provenances, the route to Greyhollow, the phrase *being FED* with its furious capital letters. He built the whole architecture of it behind his eyes, and when it was done he understood two things with perfect certainty.

First: the Gilded Vane was going to end the world, slowly, profitably, and with excellent margins.

Second: he was the only person alive who had read all of it, and he had read all of it in a locked cellar belonging to the people doing it.

The second understanding arrived in his body before his mind, as a change in the quality of the silence. Cellars have a silence the way instruments have a tone, and this one had shifted key. He told himself it was the lamp burning lower. He told himself the tea had gone cold and that was the chill on his neck.

Then he heard the lock.

Not the outer door — the inner one, the iron gate at the stairhead, the one that cost more than most houses. It had been locked when he came down. He had locked it himself, out of habit, out of nine years of habit, and now someone was unlocking it from the stairs above, unhurried, taking their time, letting the mechanism speak for them.

Ada closed the ledger. Closed the stack. Squared the spines. His hands, which had been steady all night, chose this moment to remember how to tremble, and he pressed them flat against the table until they obeyed.

Footsteps on the stair. Measured. Unhurried. He knew the cadence the way he knew his own pulse.

"Down here, Delacroix?" Corvane's voice, warm as a banked fire. "I saw your lamp under the door. I confess I wondered."

Ada rose. Turned. Arranged his face into the mild, tired expression of a man caught doing nothing worse than working late. Nine years of practice. He'd built that face the way he'd built the aqueduct-grooves of his lies, smooth and deep, and it held.

"Quarter-end reconciliation," he said. "I wanted the historical baselines. The salt contracts don't make sense without the famine-year comparables."

"Mm." Corvane came down the last steps into the lamplight.

Master Corvane was not a large man, and this was somehow the worst thing about him. He was neat and grey and precise, with the soft hands of someone who had never lifted anything heavier than a pen or a price, and his eyes did the thing they always did: they went around the room once, gently, thoroughly, like a hand patting down your pockets.

They paused on the stack of ledgers. On the eldest volume, whose spine Ada realized, too late, he had pulled half an inch out of true. On the reading table, where a cup of cold tea sat beside an open ledger-stand — and where, he saw with a lurch, a strip of paper lay that he did not remember cutting. A bookmark. He used bookmarks. He had used one tonight, on Merren's final volume, and in his haste he had squared the spines but not cleared the table.

Corvane looked at the bookmark. Looked at the oldest ledger. Smiled the smile he smiled at buyers.

"The famine years," Corvane said. "Of course. Diligence is the soul of good bookkeeping." He drifted closer, fingertips trailing along the shelf edge, and picked up the topmost volume — not Merren's last, mercifully, but the middle one, the decades-old one, the one full of fragments sold by weight. He turned a page or two without appearing to read them. "You know, when I engaged you, I told the board you were the finest auditor in the city. I understated it. You find patterns no one else sees. It's your gift."

The word *gift* landed strangely, and Ada watched Corvane watch him land it, and understood that the watching was the point.

"It's my trade," Ada said.

"Is it?" Corvane set the volume down, aligned it with the others — a courtesy, or a message: *I know exactly which books you touched.* "Tell me, Delacroix. When you reconcile a difficult account — when the numbers fight you — what do you do?"

"I sit with them until they stop fighting."

"And how do you make them stop?"

"I listen."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes, quick and satisfied, a fish turning under ice. "You listen," he repeated softly. "Yes. I believe you do." He clasped his soft hands behind his back and regarded Ada with something that in a better man might have been affection. "You've been down here three hours. Whatever you found — or think you found — I want you to do what you always do. Sit with it. Listen to it. And bring me your conclusions when they're ready." He turned toward the stair, then paused, half-turned, lamplight catching the grey at his temple. "One caution, offered as kindness. Some accounts, once opened, cannot be closed again. Some entries, once read, make the reader a party to them. A wise keeper asks himself, before he turns a dangerous page: *do I want to know, or do I need to know?* Only one of those answers keeps a man employed."

He went up the stairs. The iron gate clanged shut behind him. The lock turned — loud, deliberate, a period at the end of a sentence.

Ada stood alone in the cellar with the lamp guttering and his heart going like a trapped bird, and he took inventory of himself the way he'd take inventory of stock. Fear: considerable. Options: fewer than he'd thought an hour ago. Certainty: total.

Corvane knew. Maybe not everything — maybe not that Ada had reached Merren's last page, the one with the capital letters. But he knew his clerk had been reading the forbidden decades, and instead of anger he had offered *kindness*, which was worse, because kindness meant Corvane didn't need anger. Anger was for threats. Ada had just been reclassified as something else. An asset, perhaps. Or a problem with a scheduled solution.

*A wise keeper asks himself: do I want to know, or do I need to know?*

Ada looked at the bookmark lying on the table — Merren's page, Merren's warning, *the Ember is being FED* — and heard his own voice answer the question with a clerk's flat precision.

Need.

He folded the page into his sleeve, blew out the lamp, and stood in the dark listening to the building breathe above him, memorizing the sound of his own life ending and another thing beginning.

Upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, he could swear he heard a caged hawk stirring.
