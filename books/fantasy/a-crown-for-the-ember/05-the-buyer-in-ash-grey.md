## 5. The Buyer in Ash-Grey

The buyer arrived on the third night without stars, and Sera Voss met him at the Salt Gate with a lantern she wasn't allowed to light.

That was the first wrong thing. The Gilded Vane did its business in the open where it could — guild law, old law, the kind that kept knives sheathed and ledgers honest — and a lantern at a gate was as much a signature as any seal. But Corvane's instructions had come by courier, written in his own cramped hand: *No flame. No names. The grey man walks beside you, not behind you. You will count nothing aloud.*

So Sera stood in the dark with six hired blades at her back and listened to the city breathe around her, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just the cold.

She had been a ward-smith once. Eleven years at the Foundry Road workshops, drawing sigil-thread through silver wire until her fingertips were permanently stained grey, until she could feel a ward-line hum through her palm like a plucked string. She had been good at it. She had loved it, in the way you love anything that asks everything of you and gives back order.

Then the Ember War ended, and the wards came down by decree, and there was no more work for a woman who could hear metal sing. The Gilded Vane had offered her a different kind of work. Guarding things. Moving things. Not asking what they were.

She had told herself for nine years that this was fine. That a skill was a skill, and coin was coin, and the guild paid better than grief did.

The buyer came up the Salt Road on foot, which was the second wrong thing. No one walked into Vaneport at night anymore, not since the wards fell and the roads filled with teeth. He was tall and narrow as a closed door, wrapped in ash-grey wool that seemed to drink the little light the clouded sky gave back, and he moved without sound. Sera had escorted smugglers, defectors, one archduke in disguise. All of them made noise. This man made none, and her skin crawled before he was within twenty paces.

"Madam Voss." His voice was soft and dry, like paper turning. "You have the cargo."

"I have the escort," she said. "Cargo stays sealed until we're clear of the walls."

A pause. She couldn't see his face under the hood — or rather, she could see *something* there, a pale suggestion of features, but her eyes kept sliding off it, the way they slide off a page you don't want to read.

"Corvane said you were thorough," the buyer said.

"Corvane says a lot of things."

Another pause, longer. Then something that might have been amusement. "Good. Thorough women keep their cargo intact. Walk."

* * *

They went out through the tannery district, along routes Sera had mapped herself years ago — the ones that avoided the refugee camps, because the camps had eyes and ears and desperate people who would sell a glimpse of anything for bread. Even so, they passed the edge of Tanner's Field, where three thousand souls huddled under tarps and overturned boats, and Sera heard the sounds she'd been hearing for two days now and still hadn't gotten used to: coughing, crying, a child singing a song with no ending because whoever taught it to her had forgotten the middle.

One of her blades, a young man called Ferren, shifted nervously. "Voss. There's — something following along the fence line. Been with us since the gate."

"I know," Sera said.

It was a dog. A big yellow camp dog, ribs like a washboard, trotting parallel to their column behind the fence posts. That wasn't strange. Dogs followed food. What was strange was that it wasn't looking at them.

It was looking at the crates.

Six crates, iron-banded, riding on a mule cart in the middle of their column. Each one the size of a coffin lid. Sera had signed for them two nights ago out of the Vane's deep vault, and the manifest said *architectural salvage, 6 units, do not open, do not stack, keep from flame.* She had guarded salvage before. Salvage didn't make dogs follow it through a refugee camp at night.

And salvage didn't hum.

She had felt it first at the vault, when her bare hand brushed crate three during loading — a vibration too low to hear, rising through the iron banding into her bones, and beneath the vibration something else, something rhythmic and faint and horribly familiar. It took her half a day to place it, and when she did, she wished she hadn't.

It was the rhythm of breathing. Slow, labored, the breathing of something very old and very tired.

Ward-stone breathed. Every smith knew that. Living stone drew in the world's memory and exhaled protection; that was the whole art of the craft, coaxing stone to keep breathing after the masons cut it. But cut stone breathed evenly, contentedly, the way sleeping men do. This was not even. This was ragged. Grieving.

*Architectural salvage.* She had signed the manifest with a steady hand and told herself the tremor in her fingers was the vault's cold.

Now, walking beside the grey man through the dark city, she let her hand trail near the nearest crate as the cart jolted, and felt it again: that broken rhythm, faster now, agitated. And underneath it — she strained, hating herself for straining — something almost like a voice. Not words. Ward-stone didn't speak in words. But grief had a shape, and the shape was saying *home, home, home* in the wordless way that a torn-out thing says the only word it has left.

These weren't fragments of dead wards being sold as curiosities. These were pieces of living ward-line, ripped out whole, still alive, still reaching for the borders they used to hold.

*Home,* the stone said. *Home. Home.*

Sera took her hand off the rail and looked straight ahead and counted her steps, because counting was what she did instead of feeling.

* * *

They cleared the walls at the Weepers' Post, where the old toll station leaned over the marsh road like a drunk against a wall. Beyond it the land opened up — black fields, dead hedgerows, and far off, the mountains, invisible tonight because there were no stars to draw their edges.

The buyer stopped the column with a lifted hand.

"Here is far enough," he said. "Your men will unload the crates to the marker stones. You, madam Voss, will walk with me to the road's end."

"That's not the arrangement."

"The arrangement changed an hour ago, when your Master Corvane accepted my final payment." Paper-dry voice, no malice in it at all, which somehow made it worse. "He sold me the escort entire. You are included in the price. I would not make it difficult."

Ferren's hand went to his knife. Sera put her arm across his chest without looking at him. Six blades against whatever walked in ash-grey wool and made no sound — she liked those odds about as much as she liked the humming coming from the crates, which was to say not at all.

"Unload them," she said quietly. "Marker stones. Gentle. If anyone drops one, I'll break his hands myself."

Her men worked in silence, and here was the thing Sera would carry to her grave, the thing that started the crack in her that never fully closed again: when they set the crates down among the standing stones of the old toll-road shrine, the humming changed.

It rose. Not louder — *brighter*, the way a voice lifts when it sees a familiar face. The ward-fragments, laid out in the open air for the first time in who knew how long, in a ring of stones that had once — she saw it now, saw the worn channels in the markers, the socket-holes — *had once held a small wayside ward herself*, decades ago, before the decrees. The fragments recognized the shape of what they'd been part of. They reached for it. Through her boots, through the frozen mud, Sera felt the whole ring shiver with something that was not quite hope and not quite prayer.

*Home,* they sang, all six together. *Home. Home.*

"Stop that," she whispered, and didn't know if she meant the stones or herself.

The buyer stood over the crates with his hood tilted, and Sera realized he was listening too. Listening the way she listened. Which meant—

"You're a smith," she said. It came out before she could stop it. "Or you were."

The pale suggestion of a face turned toward her. For a moment the hood seemed to shift, and she saw eyes — grey, patient, ancient, with a light in them like coals seen through ash — and then her gaze slid away again, refusing.

"I am a collector," he said. "Of things the world no longer wants. Your guild has been most generous. Fragments of the border line, sold a few at a time, decade after decade. Do you know how long it takes to assemble a key?" He touched the nearest crate, gently, almost tenderly, and the grieving hum inside it went silent — cowed, or comforted, Sera couldn't tell which, and couldn't decide which was worse. "Longer than your life, madam Voss. Longer than your master's. Patience is the one currency that never devalues."

"A key to what?"

The coal-light regarded her. "You guard the cargo. You needn't understand it. That is the arrangement your guild prefers — and I suspect the arrangement you prefer as well. Ignorance is such a comfortable wage."

Sera's jaw tightened. Nine years of comfortable wages sat in her chest like swallowed lead. "What happens to them? The fragments. Where they're going."

"They go home," the buyer said softly. "To the mountain. To the Ember that sleeps beneath it, which has been dreaming of its wards for a hundred years. When the last piece arrives, the dream ends." He turned back toward the north, where the starless sky pressed down on the black land like a lid. "You may tell yourself they are only stone. Most smiths do. It makes the cutting easier."

Then he was walking, and the crates rose — *rose*, all six, drifting a hand's width above the mud, trailing their grief behind them like torn veils — and followed him up the marsh road without wheels, without mules, without sound.

At the edge of the torchlight he paused. "Your master will send you north soon, I think. There are loose pages in his ledgers, and loose pages must be gathered. When you come, look for me where the mountain meets the sky." The hood tilted. "Bring your questions, madam Voss. I collect those too."

Then the dark took him, and the humming faded with him, mile by mile, like a hymn sinking under water — until Sera couldn't hear it anymore and discovered that was worse than hearing it, because now the silence was hers to fill.

* * *

She stood at the Weepers' Post long after her men had given up pretending to wait for orders and gone to the toll-house fire. Ferren came out eventually, chewing the last of someone's hard bread, and stopped beside her at the rail.

"The dog's still here," he said.

She looked. The yellow camp dog had followed them the whole way out and now sat in the mud at the base of the shrine stones, staring north up the empty road, ears pricked, hackles lifted, making a sound low in its chest that wasn't quite a growl and wasn't quite a whine.

"It heard them too," Sera said.

"Dogs can't hear ward-stone."

"No," Sera agreed. "They can't."

She thought about the manifest in her coat pocket. *Architectural salvage, 6 units.* She thought about the vault, and how many other manifests she had signed in nine years with the same neat hand — how many units, how many crates, how many pieces of the world's defenses walking out of Vaneport a few at a time while she counted her steps instead of her sins. Decade after decade, the buyer had said. A key assembled across her whole career, and she had been one of the hands that built it.

She thought about the child in Tanner's Field singing a song with no middle, and the wards that should have been keeping the roads safe for her to learn the rest of it.

"Voss?" Ferren said. "You want me to write up the delivery report?"

The report. Her report. The document that would travel back to Corvane's desk tomorrow morning, clean and professional, confirming the transfer, closing the entry, making it all official and finished and *nothing*.

Sera looked north, where the mountains would be if the sky remembered how to outline them. Somewhere up there, six grieving pieces of the world were drifting toward a sleeper who dreamed of burning.

"No," she said. "I'll write it myself. Tonight."

She wrote it in the toll-house by tallow light, and her hand was steady right up until it wasn't. She described the route, the timing, the transfer point — everything Corvane required, everything true. Then she sat for a long time with the pen hovering, and finally added one line at the bottom, small, in the margin, where reports aren't supposed to have opinions:

*Cargo vocalized continuously throughout transport. Recommend future shipments be assessed by a qualified listener prior to sale.*

Qualified listener. As if there were such a thing anymore. As if the guild hadn't spent a century making sure there wasn't.

She blotted it, folded it, sealed it with her sigil — the ward-smith's mark, hammer and arc, that she'd sworn on at nineteen and hadn't looked at honestly since — and set it aside for the morning courier.

Then she pulled her ledger of personal accounts toward her, the little coded book where enforcers logged expenses, and stared at the blank page for a long time.

Because there was a question sitting in her now, and it had teeth. Not *what* was in the crates — she knew that now, God help her, she knew. The question was *how many*. How many crates, over how many years, out of how many vaults. And who else besides the ash-grey man had bought, and what else besides border-stone had been sold, and why the stars had gone out on the same night her guild raised its prices.

Questions she was paid not to ask. Well. The pay had stopped meaning anything somewhere between the shrine stones and the silence.

Sera dipped her pen, and in her private cipher, in a code only she could read, she began a new ledger — an audit of her own, the first honest bookkeeping she'd done in nine years. *Item,* she wrote. *One buyer, ash-grey, walks without sound. Item: six fragments, living, grieving. Item: the manifest lies.*

She got as far as *Item: I signed for all of it* before the pen shook too badly to continue.

Outside, the yellow dog was still staring north, and far away — so far she might have imagined it, and would spend many nights wondering if she had — Sera thought she heard the wards scream.

Not the fragments. The *wards*. All of them, along the whole broken border, all at once, a sound like a thousand struck bells dying in cold air.

And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, rising from the direction of the Gilded Vane itself, from the vaults beneath the counting-house where she'd signed her first false manifest nine years ago:

Humming.

More crates. More grief. Being loaded for a delivery she hadn't been told about — to a destination she suddenly, desperately needed to know.

Sera snuffed the tallow, tucked both ledgers inside her coat, and went to find out what else her guild was selling in the dark.
