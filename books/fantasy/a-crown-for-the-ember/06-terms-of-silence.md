## 6. Terms of Silence

The summons came at the hour when honest clerks were asleep and dishonest ones were counting.

A boy in guild livery — too young to know what he carried, old enough to be paid not to ask — brought a card of heavy cream stock to my desk on the third floor of the Gilded Vane. No wax seal. No signature. Just three words in Master Corvane's narrow hand:

*My office. Dawn.*

I read it twice, set it down, and found my hands had gone cold around the pen I was still holding. A drop of ink fell onto the ledger's margin, blooming like a wound.

Three days since the stars went out. Three days since the wards along the border had collapsed like lungs giving out, and the refugees had come pouring through the Ashgate with their goats and their grief, and the Gilded Vane had raised its prices by a fifth before the first of them reached the fountain square. Three days since I'd heard Corvane's voice through the door of the map room, selling *fragments* to a hooded buyer whose ash-grey cloak still haunted the edges of my sleep.

Three days since I'd started lying to myself about what I'd heard.

*You misheard,* I told myself, as I had told myself every morning since. *Old buildings carry voices strangely. He said something else.*

But I was a ledger-keeper. My whole life was the discipline of reading numbers for what they actually said rather than what you wished they said. And the numbers in those overheard minutes added up to treason.

Now he wanted me at dawn.

I finished the night's entries with hands that only shook a little. Somewhere below, in the counting-house cellar where the guild kept its stranger inventory, something caged began to sing — thin and high, a sound like wind through a keyhole. The stormhawk. They'd moved it down there after the night without stars, and every evening since, at this same hour, it sang the same three notes.

I had spent twenty years teaching myself not to listen to what animals said. It was the first rule of my survival: *the gift is not real if you never use it.* But lately the walls between listening and not-listening had grown thin as the border wards, and when the stormhawk sang, I understood it whether I wanted to or not.

*Thin,* it sang. *Thinner. Coming.*

It had been singing that for three days. I did not think it meant the wards alone.

* * *

Corvane's office occupied the tower's top floor, which meant it looked down on everything the guild owned and out at everything it intended to. I had been up there four times in eleven years — once for my hiring, once for a promotion, twice for errors that weren't mine but that someone senior had needed a junior face for. Each time, the office had been exactly the same: dark wood, dark glass, a fire kept low so the light stayed amber and flattering, and Master Corvane behind his desk like a spider who had learned to dress well.

This time there was a second chair. Placed facing the desk. Placed, I noticed, so that whoever sat in it would sit with the dawn light full in their eyes while Corvane sat shaded.

He gestured me into it. "Ada. Sit. Tea?"

"No, thank you."

"Good. It's terrible. The merchants send it as tribute and I drink it out of spite." He smiled. Corvane's smiles were famous for their warmth, and famous among those who knew him for meaning nothing at all. "Eleven years, Delacroix. Do you know how rare that is? Eleven years, and your ledgers have never once needed correcting."

"I try to be careful."

"You're more than careful. You're *precise*. There's a difference." He laced his fingers over his stomach. "Careful people avoid mistakes. Precise people understand them. Understand where they come from. Understand what they're worth." He tilted his head. "Tell me — when you balance a ledger, what are you actually doing?"

"Recording what happened."

"That's what a clerk does. What does a *keeper* do?"

The question had teeth hidden somewhere in it. I chose honesty, because I judged he'd smell anything else. "A keeper decides what the record means. What gets entered, and what gets... footnoted."

"*Footnoted.*" He savored the word. "Yes. Exactly that. You see why I keep you."

Outside the window, the city was waking under a sky the color of dirty wool. Three days of no stars, and the sunrises had begun to look wrong — smeared, uncertain, as if the sky itself no longer trusted what it was supposed to do. Refugees still camped in the fountain square. The guild's guards charged them a copper apiece for access to the well.

"Let me tell you a story," Corvane said. "A long time ago — before your time, before mine — there was a war. Everyone remembers the Ember War the way children remember a fever: badly, and mostly the parts that scared them. The wards went up. The Ember went to sleep. The heroes got statues." He shrugged. "Nobody asks who paid for the statues. Nobody asks who maintained the wards afterward, or how, or at what cost. Maintenance is never heroic, Delacroix. It's just expensive. And expense," he spread his hands, "is our business."

"The wards fell," I said. "Whatever maintenance was being done—"

"Was being *defunded*. By councils. By kings. By every government from here to the Salt Coast that decided peace was permanent and permanence was free." His voice didn't rise; that was the frightening thing about Corvane, nothing ever rose, everything arrived already at temperature. "So yes. Someone stepped in where the crowns stepped out. Someone kept pieces of the old ward-line alive when everyone else let them die. And now the world outside our windows is learning the price of that decision, and the world will need us again, and we will be *ready*."

"You sold fragments," I said. "To a buyer in ash-grey. I heard you."

There it was. The room went very quiet, the way a forest goes quiet before something moves.

Corvane studied me for a long moment, and I watched him decide something, the way a man decides whether to kill a snake or pick it up.

"You heard," he repeated. "Through a door."

"The map room carries sound."

"It does. I've always disliked that about it." He rose, walked to the window, stood with his back to me looking out at the smudged dawn. "Most men in your position would have run to the magistrates. Or run out of the city. Or convinced themselves they'd imagined it. Which are you, Ada?"

"A clerk," I said. "I don't imagine things. That's the whole point of me."

"And yet you're still here. Sitting in my chair. Drinking my terrible tea you refused." He turned. "Which tells me you understand something most men take thirty years to learn: that exposure runs both directions. If you can hear through doors, others can too. If you know what I am, I can find out what *you* are."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the morning.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" He came back around the desk, unhurried, and sat on its edge rather than behind it — a deliberate informality, a man making himself approachable the way a trap makes itself look like ground. "Twenty-two years in this guild, Ada. Hired at nineteen with references from a village that no longer exists. No family. No close friends. No lovers anyone's ever seen. A man who flinches — very slightly, very briefly, once a season, always the same way — whenever an animal is beaten within earshot. A man who talks to the counting-house cat when he thinks the cellar's empty." He smiled again, gently. "I own the strangest inventory in the world. Do you truly believe I don't notice the strange things in my own house?"

My heart was going hard enough that I could hear it. I made my face do what it had learned to do across twenty years of hiding: nothing.

"I talk to cats," I said. "Lots of people talk to cats."

"Mm. And lots of people have grandmothers from the ward-lines." He opened a drawer — slowly, letting me watch — and took out a single sheet of paper, old, soft at the folds. He didn't hand it to me. Just held it. "Records burn, Delacroix. Wars eat archives. But some things survive in parish books and militia rolls, and some things survive because old women gossip, and piecing those together is a hobby of mine. Your grandmother's name appears in a border-village census from the last year of the Ember War. Occupation column: blank. But the priest's marginal note says — and I quote — '*speaks with beasts, report sent, no reply*.'" He tucked the paper away again. "Report sent, no reply. Because in wartime, a gift like that is a weapon, and weapons get conscripted, and conscripts die. So she hid it. And then her grandson grew up and hid it better. Twenty-two years of better. That's not cowardice, Ada. That's craftsmanship. I respect craftsmanship."

Every instinct I had was screaming at me to deny it, and every one of those instincts also knew denial was useless. He hadn't asked me a question yet. That was the worst part. All of this — the story, the paper, the gentle voice — was scaffolding around a question he hadn't asked.

"So," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "What do you want?"

"What I've wanted for eleven years. What I hired you for, though you didn't know it." He folded his hands. "The buyer you overheard represents interests north of the mountains. Interests with resources. And those interests have recently acquired certain... assets. Ward-spirits. Captured, bound, alive — barely. Spirits are the mortar of the old ward-line, Ada. Every stone of it was laid on a living will. To rebuild the wards — properly, durably, profitably — we need to know what those spirits know. Where the anchor-stones sleep. What the lines remember. What broke them." He paused. "And spirits cannot be interrogated by men. Torture means nothing to something that isn't flesh. Bribery means nothing to something that doesn't want. But they can be *spoken with*. By the right person."

The tea tray sat untouched between us. The dawn had strengthened enough to show the dust hanging in the light, turning slowly, indifferent.

"You want me to use the gift," I said. "The gift I've spent my whole life burying."

"I want you to stop wasting it." For the first time, something real moved in his voice — not kindness, but appetite. "Do you know what you are, Delacroix? You're a master key that has spent two decades insisting it's a spoon. Out there—" he nodded at the broken sky "—out there is a lock the size of the world. Partnership, Ada. Real partnership. A share of the northern contracts. Protection — total protection, from the buyers, from the factions, from whatever's coming down off those mountains. And in exchange, you sit with a few bound spirits, ask a few questions, and tell me what they say. That's all. Conversations. You've had conversations with animals your whole life. This is just — animals that matter."

"And if I refuse?"

He sighed, genuinely regretful, an actor honoring the role. "Then I'm afraid the gift becomes a liability instead of an asset. There are parties — church parties, crown parties — who pay extremely well for proof that beast-speakers survived the purges. Proof with a name attached. Proof with a face." He spread his hands. "I wouldn't enjoy it. But business is business, and sentiment is a cost, and I don't carry costs I can avoid."

There it was. Not a choice at all. A door and a wall, dressed up as two doors.

I thought of my grandmother, whose face I could barely remember — a smear of grey hair and a voice I'd lost decades ago, saying *never let them hear you, little one, never let them hear.* She'd hidden so well she'd hidden herself right out of my memory. And here was a man offering to unbury me, polish me up, and put me to work as a tool against the last living pieces of the thing she'd helped build.

"I'd need time," I said.

"Time."

"To consider. Properly. I'm precise, Master Corvane — you said so yourself. I don't enter figures until I've verified them. I'd like to verify this."

Something flickered behind his eyes — irritation, maybe, or amusement, or both wearing each other's clothes. "How long?"

"A day. Two."

"One day." He stood, and the audience was clearly over. "Dawn tomorrow. And Ada —" I stopped at the door. He was smiling again, warm as banked coals. "The stormhawk in the cellar. It sings every evening, doesn't it. Same three notes. I've had three different handlers swear the bird is mute. Birds aren't mute, of course. They're just particular about their audiences." He tapped the desk once, softly. "One day."

* * *

I did not go back to my desk. I walked the long way down through the counting-house, past the strong-room, past the cage-racks where the guild stored its impossible inventory, and stopped at the stormhawk's cage because my feet decided before I did.

The bird sat hunched in the dark, feathers dull, one wing held wrong — they'd caught it badly, or broken it worse. Its eyes found me in the gloom with the terrible directness birds have.

*They will make you open doors,* it said — not in words, never in words, in the pressure of meaning against the inside of my skull, like weather pushing at a window. *Openers are never forgiven. Openers are used until the hinges rust.*

"I know," I whispered.

*You do not know. Knowing is what happens after.* It shifted, wincing. *The thin place is thinner. The mountain turns in its sleep. Ask the dead pages, keeper. The dead pages remember what the living ones were paid to forget.*

"I don't know what that means."

*You will.* And it turned its head away, folding itself smaller, a creature conserving what little it had left.

I stood there a long moment in the dark, listening to the building breathe around me — the tick of the great clocks, the shift of coin-sacks, the far-off murmur of the city waking to another starless day. Then I went back upstairs, sat at my desk, and pulled the oldest ledgers from the archive shelf: the pre-war volumes, the ones in my predecessor's hand, the ones I'd catalogued a hundred times and never once *read*, because reading them wasn't my job and curiosity wasn't a survival trait.

I worked until the lamps burned low. And near midnight, in Volume Nine, between an entry for forty barrels of preserved eel and an entry for a shipment of grave-glass, I found it — a page whose ink was darker than the rest, pressed harder, written fast:

*Sold: 3 fragments, ward-line eastern anchor. Buyer unnamed per instruction. Note to self: the fragments hum when the mountain dreams. God forgive the arithmetic.*

God forgive the arithmetic.

I sat very still. Below me, faint through three floors of stone, the stormhawk sang its three notes. *Thin. Thinner. Coming.*

Tomorrow at dawn I was supposed to give Corvane my answer. Tomorrow at dawn I was supposed to become either his key or his evidence.

I looked at the page a long time. Then I did the thing that a careful man does not do and a precise man, perhaps, does: I cut the stitching, lifted the page free, folded it small, and slid it inside my shirt, against the skin, over the heart.

The ledger lay open behind me, a gap in the record like a missing tooth.

Some entries, I thought, you footnote.

And some you steal.
