## 7. The Interrogation of Small Gods

They brought the ward-spirit up from the vaults at dawn, in a cage that had been built for something larger.

Ada watched from the doorway of the interrogation room as four guild porters set it down on a table scarred by decades of harder work. The cage was iron, but not ordinary iron — he could see the old ward-marks hammered into the bars, inverted, turned from keeping-out into keeping-in. Someone at the Gilded Vane had known exactly how to do this. Someone had done it many times.

The spirit inside was small. That was the first thing that broke him, though he didn't know it yet. He had expected — he didn't know what. Fire. Grandeur. Something like the stormhawk's wildness scaled up to the size of the border itself. Instead the thing in the cage looked like a woman made of lamplight and frost, no bigger than a child, curled with her knees drawn up, her light guttering in slow arrhythmic pulses, like breathing through pain.

"You'll want to stand well back at first," Corvane said. He was dressed for the occasion, which was to say he was dressed exactly as he always dressed, immaculate grey, the guild's pin at his collar polished to a coin's shine. He treated everything the same way, Ada was learning. Weddings, funerals, the end of the world. "They can be disorienting. Like listening to a bell from inside the bell."

"I know what spirits are like," Ada said.

"Do you." Corvane smiled without warmth. "Of course you do. That's rather the point."

* * *

They had given him three days to decide, and he had used all of them, and in the end he had not decided at all. That was the truth of it, the truth he carried into the room like a stone in his shoe: he had not chosen. He had simply failed to refuse long enough that refusal stopped being available.

Corvane had laid it out with a bookkeeper's clarity that Ada still hated himself for admiring. The gift was illegal. The gift was known — to Corvane, to whoever Corvane answered to, to the hooded buyer in ash-grey whose name Ada still didn't have. Exposure meant the magistrate's court, and the magistrate's court for a beast-speaker meant the quiet room in the deep cells where they taught you to forget, and forgetting, Ada had come to understand, was not the mercy it sounded like. It was just dying with extra steps.

Or: partnership. A salary tripled. Protection. And his particular talent applied to a problem no one else in the guild could solve, because ward-spirits did not speak to ledgers, to threats, to gold. They spoke to the living world, and the only living tongue that answered them was his.

"You'll be helping us restore the wards," Corvane had said. "That's the work, Ada. Whatever you think you read in the old ledgers."

He had said it looking directly at Ada, and Ada had held his face still, and that stillness — that careful, practiced, coward's stillness — was the second thing that broke him.

The stolen ledger page was sewn into the lining of his coat. He had slept with it against his ribs for two nights like a second heart. *The Ember beneath the mountains wakes by feeding. The fragments sold north were cut living. The buyer's name is not written here, and I am glad of it.* His predecessor's hand, cramped and hurried, and beneath it, in different ink, added years later in a hand Ada recognized with a lurch as his own predecessor's predecessor: *If you are reading this, you balance our books. Balance them honestly, somewhere, somehow, for both of us.*

He had not told Corvane he'd found it. He had not told anyone. He was carrying it north in his mind's eye already, some nights, planning a route he had no right to plan, and every morning he woke up and put on his coat and walked down to the counting room and did the work, because the work was what kept him alive.

And now the work had changed shape.

* * *

"Approach it slowly," Corvane said. "Speak to it. Tell it what we want to know. The mountain, the Ember, the condition of the deep wards. It was cut from the border-line at the Vellmark pass — it will know things."

Ada crossed the room. Up close, the spirit's light was worse, because up close he could see that the pulses weren't arrhythmic at all. They were regular. Slow and regular and deliberate, the way a person counts their breath to stay calm. She was frightened. She was frightened, and she was managing it, and she had been managing it for however long she had been in the cage, and no one had noticed because no one had thought to look.

He stopped an arm's length from the bars.

"Can you understand me?" he asked.

The light flickered. Not an answer. A flinch.

"It doesn't need to understand words," Corvane said behind him, impatient. "You're the translator, not it. Touch the bars if you have to. Open yourself. That's the whole of your job, Ada. Be a door."

*Be a door.* Ada closed his eyes.

He had spent twenty-nine years learning how not to do this. How to walk past the stable yard without listening to the horses' low gray complaints about weather and oats. How to sit through dinners while the cook's old dog told the whole kitchen, in long looping images, about the boy who used to live there and didn't anymore. How to keep the door shut so firmly that some days he could almost believe there was nothing behind it — that the whispers were imagination, the knowing was coincidence, the scar of his gift had healed over into ordinary skin.

His grandmother had taught him the shutting. *Never let them hear you, little one. The world that made this law made it because it fears what we hear. And it will not hesitate.* He had been seven. He had obeyed her for twenty-two years, including the years after there was no her to obey, and now a man in polished grey was asking him to open the door on command, and the obscene part — the part that would keep him up tonight and tomorrow night — was how easily it came.

The door opened.

* * *

The spirit's mind was cold and vast and very, very old, and it was also, immediately and unmistakably, *hers* — a person, a self, with a history folded up inside her like a map. Ada gasped. Corvane must have thought it was the contact itself, and he supposed it was.

She had been a ward-stone once. Before that she had been — he fumbled for it, the way you fumble for a word in a language you almost know — she had been weather. A specific weather: the long clear cold of the Vellmark pass in autumn, the kind of morning that made travelers stop and look north and feel, for no reason they could name, that they were being watched over. The old warders had caught that feeling, that watching, and bound it into stone, and the stone had stood in the border-line for six hundred years, holding the line, holding the *watching*, and three months ago men with guild chisels had come and cut her out of it.

He felt her memory of it. The saw-note. The moment the line went slack around her like a severed tendon. Six hundred years of neighbors on either side — other ward-stones, other spirits, a whole choir — and then silence, and then the cage.

*They are cutting the line apart,* she said, or the memory said, or she said now, in the present, her voice like frost forming on a window. *Stone by stone. Selling us. Do you know what they are selling us to?*

"I don't," Ada said aloud, and heard his own voice crack.

*You do,* she said. *You read the ledgers.*

His eyes flew open. Corvane was watching from the doorway, and Ada made himself look bored, made himself say, "It's showing me the pass. The cutting. Nothing useful yet." The lie came out smooth and he despised how smooth it was, how natural, how the guild had been teaching him its native language all along without his noticing.

He touched the bars again and went back under.

* * *

*Ask your question,* the spirit said. *The grey man wants the mountain. Ask it, and I will answer honestly, because lying is the only thing left I own and I will not spend it on you.*

"The Ember," Ada said. "Beneath the mountains. What's its condition?"

And she showed him.

He had expected fire — the word *Ember* promised fire, promised heat, promised something at least as dramatic as the ledgers' dry horror. What she showed him instead was a vast darkness under the roots of the mountains, a dark so old it had its own weather, and in the middle of it, a warmth. Not a blaze. An *ember*, exactly as named: a coal in a dead fire, banked, breathing, the red of it pulsing in the same slow rhythm he had seen in her own light.

It was asleep. That was the horror of it — it was asleep the way a starving man sleeps, fitfully, and it was *dreaming of food*.

The fragments. The ward-stones the guild had sold north, cut living, carted over the passes in their inverted cages. She showed him where they had gone — a hall carved in the deep rock, a ring of cages arranged around the banked coal like stones around a hearth. They were not fuel. They were not components. They were *bait*. A sleeping thing that fed on the living memory of the world, and the guild had spent three months laying a table for it, course by course, and calling it commerce.

*It is not awake,* she said. *It turns in its sleep. It has begun to dream of waking. The men who bought us believe they can control what it does when it opens its eyes. They are wrong. It has eaten warders before. It has eaten *kings*. It will eat their ambition first and be hungry again before the ash settles.*

"And the wards," Ada whispered. His hands were shaking on the bars. "The border. Can it be rebuilt? If the fragments were returned —"

*Returned,* she said, and something moved through her light that in a person would have been a laugh. *Returned, and mended, and an apology made. Yes. The line remembers its shape. That is what a ward is, little door — a memory the world agreed to keep. But the mending wants a keeper. Someone to hold the memory while the stones forget theirs. The last one who could have done it refused.*

She showed him a face.

A woman, weathered, grey-streaked, standing at the Vellmark pass with her hands open and empty, and Ada knew her — knew her the way you know a face from a locket, from a story told so often it wore grooves. His grandmother. Younger than he had ever known her, standing before the ward-line sixty years ago, being asked — by the wolf, by the *wolf*, by something grey and enormous and patient — to do exactly what Ada was being asked to do now, in a room above the counting house of the Gilded Vane.

She had refused. He felt her refusal like a stone dropped into him: *I have a daughter. I have a life. Find another keeper.* And the wolf had bowed its head, and the line had held anyway, barely, for one more generation, on borrowed strength.

*She chose the small love,* the spirit said, without judgment. *It is a defensible choice. Most keepers have made it. That is why the wards are thin.*

"Ada." Corvane's voice, from very far away. "Ada. What is it saying?"

* * *

He surfaced slowly, the way you surface from deep water, because he needed the time — needed the darkness between the spirit's mind and the lamplit room to decide, one final time, who he was going to be.

He thought about the quiet room in the deep cells. He thought about the ledger page against his ribs. He thought about a woman at a pass, sixty years ago, choosing her daughter over the world, and how he was her daughter's son, and how the world had come collecting on her choice with interest.

He thought about the spirit in the cage, who owned one thing and would not spend it on him.

*It is afraid,* Ada said. "It's fragmentary. Confused. It remembers the pass, the cutting, the other stones. It doesn't know where it is." He heard his voice do it — the clerk's voice, the flat inventory voice, the voice that made facts sound smaller than they were. "It says the mountain is dormant. Sleeping. The buyer's people are — it showed me a hearth. It thinks they're trying to bank it, keep it sleeping. It doesn't think they can wake it. It says the deep wards are degraded but intact."

Every word was true. That was the beauty of it, the craftsmanship of it — he had learned lying from ledgers, and a good ledger never lies, it only *arranges*. Dormant. Sleeping. Degraded but intact. He simply did not mention the dreaming of food. He did not mention the ring of cages laid like a table. He did not mention the keeper, or the wolf, or his grandmother's open hands.

Corvane studied him for a long moment. Ada held the look the way he held a difficult account: nothing in the face, everything in the arithmetic.

"Dormant," Corvane said at last. "That's — good. That's what the buyer was told. It's good to have it confirmed by the source." He smiled, and for once the smile reached his eyes, and Ada understood with a cold, clean shock that Corvane had wanted this answer. Had perhaps *needed* it. The grey man upstairs had been sold a story about a banked fire, and Corvane's whole position rested on the story being true, and here was the guild's new beast-speaker confirming it.

"You've done well," Corvane said. "Better than well. There will be more of them, of course. The buyer wants the whole line assessed. You'll be rich, Ada. And safe. Both things at once — I know how rarely they come together."

"Yes," Ada said. "Thank you, Master Corvane."

Corvane left. Ada heard his steps go down the corridor, unhurried, a man whose books balanced.

* * *

Ada stood alone with the spirit for a long time. The porters had gone. The lamp burned. Outside the high window, the city went about its morning, and somewhere over the border the wards hung thin as rotten thread, and under the mountains something dreamed of breakfast.

"I'm sorry," he said to the cage. It was inadequate. He said it anyway. "I lied about you. To him. I told him you were confused."

*You told him I was small,* the spirit said. *It was well done. I have watched men lie for six hundred years, little door. That was a craftsman's lie.*

"It's the first one I've ever told on purpose."

*No,* she said. *You have been telling one your whole life. To the horses. To the dog. To the mirror. This is only the first one you told for a reason.*

Her light pulsed — that slow, counted breathing — and then, deliberately, she did something she had not done before: she pressed herself against the bars, all her cold lamplight gathering close, and showed him one thing more. Not the mountain. Not the pass.

The wolf. Grey, scarred, standing in falling snow at the edge of a refugee crowd, looking at Ada across a hundred strangers with eyes like old coins.

*He is looking for you,* she said. *He has been looking for a long time. He does not forget, and he does not forgive, and he is the last of the line's keepers, and he is dying of the line's wound. When he finds you — and he will find you — you will have to decide which of your grandmother's choices was the coward's.*

"I already know," Ada said.

*Then stop balancing,* she said. *Start spending.*

Ada took his hand off the bars. He walked to the door. He turned back once — he couldn't help it — and the spirit had curled up again, knees drawn in, light guttering, counting her breaths in the dark of an iron room above a counting house, a small god in a small cage waiting to be carried north and fed to something enormous.

He closed the door behind him.

In the corridor, he put his hand flat against his coat, over the stolen page, over his own quick heart. *Degraded but intact.* He would write it in the day-book himself, in his own neat clerk's hand, and the entry would be a lie built entirely of true words, and it would be the first entry in a ledger no one at the Gilded Vane would ever audit.

He had three days, he guessed. Maybe fewer. Corvane would want more spirits questioned, and sooner or later he would bring one up from the vaults that knew the buyer's name, or the route the fragments took, or the shape of Corvane's own signature in the margins — and then Ada's careful arrangement of true words would not be enough.

North, then. Before the vaults emptied. Before the wolf found him, or after — he hadn't decided that yet — but north, with the page against his ribs and a lie in the day-book and a small god's blessing, if that's what it was, still cold on his hands.

He went down to the counting room, and he opened the day-book, and he began to write.
