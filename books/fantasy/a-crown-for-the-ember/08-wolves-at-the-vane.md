## 8. Wolves at the Vane

The summons came at dawn, folded into a grey envelope sealed with black wax, and Sera Voss read it three times before her hands stopped shaking.

*One of our clerks has taken what is not his. Find him. Find what he took. Bring both to me unmarked.*

No signature. No name. Just Corvane's seal, pressed deep enough to crack the wax, and beneath it the single word that made her stomach drop: *unmarked.*

She had escorted buyers through the Vane's back corridors for six years. She had stood watch over crates that hummed, cages that wept, ledgers she was never permitted to read. She had learned to keep her face like a locked door. But *unmarked* was a word with teeth, and everyone in the Gilded Vane knew whose flesh those teeth were for.

She dressed in her working leathers — ash-grey, like everything the guild touched — and buckled the ward-smith's tools at her hip out of habit before remembering she hadn't been permitted to smith anything in two years. The sigil stayed. It was the last honest thing she owned.

Outside her window, the city was still coughing up refugees. They lined the canals in the grey morning light, huddled under awnings from the border districts, and the bread-sellers had already doubled their prices twice since the stars went out. Commerce, Corvane liked to say, is the only prayer that never fails.

Sera had believed that once. She was beginning to suspect belief was just the first thing a person sold.

* * *

The theft itself was almost elegant.

She started where any hunter would: the counting room, the night ledger, the record of who had entered the archive after the eleventh bell. The lock on the archive door showed no forcing. The wards on the shelves — guild work, her own colleagues' hands — showed no breach. Whoever took the page had walked in through the front of the world, which meant either the thief was very good or very trusted, and in Sera's experience the two qualities rarely traveled together.

Then there was the clerk himself.

Ada Delacroix. Second-tier ledger-keeper, eleven years' service, no debts, no vices anyone could prove, no friends close enough to blackmail. His file was so clean it read like a confession. She pulled his duty records and found a man who balanced impossible accounts without error for a decade — a man who noticed things. A man who, if he'd found something in those old ledgers, would have understood exactly what he was looking at.

And there was this: three days ago, Delacroix had requested access to the pre-war archives. The request had been approved. By whom, the record didn't say. That blank space bothered her more than anything else. Requests didn't get approved by nobody. Somebody had wanted him reading those pages.

Or somebody had wanted a reason to hunt him.

She sat back in the records room and looked at the file for a long time. Then she did the thing hunters aren't supposed to do. She asked herself a question instead of an answer:

*What could a man like that steal that would make Corvane use the word 'unmarked'?*

* * *

She found the answer in the copy-room, because the Vane was too greedy to trust its memory to originals alone.

Every ledger page of consequence got copied by a junior scribe before filing, and the copies went into a secondary archive that even Corvane's enforcers mostly ignored, because the whole point of copies was that nobody cared about them. Sera had helped organize that archive years ago, back when she still thought the work was honorable — cataloguing the guild's sins in neat alphabetical rows without knowing they were sins.

The stolen page was Entry 4471, dated forty-one years past. She found its twin between a bill of sale for "caged chorus-spirits, lot of twelve" and a receipt for "mountain salt, ceremonial grade, 300 measures."

She should have put it down. She knew that even as she picked it up. There are doors you open that don't close again, and every instinct she'd honed in six years of enforcement told her this was one of them.

She read it anyway.

*Sold to buyer unnamed (ash-grey livery): ward-fragment, first-grade, extracted live from the border-line at Cael's Ford. Fragment retains original binding-function. Buyer instructed in methods of reattachment. Payment deferred until proof of function demonstrated.*

She read it again. And again, slower, letting each word arrive separately like stones dropped into still water.

Ward-fragments weren't dead things. Every ward-smith knew that much, even the ones who'd never been allowed near the real work. A fragment cut from a living ward-line kept humming. Kept *wanting*. You could sell a piece of stone or a piece of iron, but a piece of a ward was a piece of a promise somebody had made to the border villages — the promise that nothing from beyond the line would come through in the night.

The guild hadn't just been selling scrap. They'd been selling the border's own bones to someone who wanted them *reattached* — somewhere else, in some other configuration, doing some other work than keeping the dark out.

Forty-one years of entries. She pulled the neighboring files with hands that had gone cold and steady, the way hands do when the body understands danger before the mind consents to. Sale after sale. Extraction after extraction. And running alongside them, in a different ink, a second column of payments — not from the buyer to the guild, but from the guild to a string of names she recognized from the border reports. Wardens. Gate-keepers. The men and women paid to look away while the line was picked apart joint by joint.

The stars hadn't gone out on their own.

The wards hadn't thinned. They'd been *harvested*. Sold off piece by piece across four decades, and now the buyer had enough of them, and enough instruction in their use, to do — what? Rebuild the old line facing inward? String them around something that needed keeping *in*?

She thought of the fragments she'd guarded. The humming. The way the newer ones had begun to sound almost like grieving, in the weeks before the stars went out.

They'd known. The fragments had known what was coming, and she had stood outside their cages with a blade on her hip and called it security work.

* * *

She sat in the copy-room until the light through the high windows shifted from grey to gold, and she did not move, and she did not burn the page, though her hand hovered over the brazier twice.

Here was the arithmetic, laid out plain. Ada Delacroix had found this truth and run with it. Corvane wanted him and the page brought back *unmarked*, which meant the guild's crime stayed buried and the only witness to it stopped breathing. If Sera delivered both, she kept her position, her wage, her place in the only structure that had ever given her work meaning. If she didn't, she became what Delacroix was: hunted, homeless, a name on a grey envelope.

And if she reported what she'd found honestly — wrote it up, filed it, told the truth through proper channels — the report would go to Corvane. Who would read it. And smile.

There was no channel that wasn't a throat.

She thought about the first fragment she'd ever guarded, early in her enforcement years. It had hummed a pattern she'd later learned was an old shepherd's charm from the Cael's Ford line — a melody ward-smiths wove into the border so the sheep wouldn't panic at thunder. Someone had cut it out of the living line like a tooth from a jaw, and it had gone on singing its little song in the dark of its cage for years, waiting for a flock that no longer existed.

She had eaten well on wages paid for guarding that cage.

Conscience, she was learning, doesn't arrive like a revelation. It arrives like a debt collector. It doesn't care when you became complicit; it only presents the total and waits.

* * *

Near dusk she went to see the one person in the Vane she still half-trusted, an old archivist named Maren who had catalogued the pre-war holdings since before Sera was born.

Maren listened without interrupting, which was her way, and when Sera finished she was quiet for a long moment, folding her spectacles closed.

"You're asking me whether the guild armed the people who broke the wards," Maren said.

"I'm asking you to tell me I'm wrong."

"Child, I filed those sales." Maren's voice was dry as old paper. "I filed them forty years ago, and I asked my master then what a ward-fragment was *for*, sold loose, and he told me it was guild business and gave me a raise. I have spent four decades arranging evidence in alphabetical order and calling it a career." She set her spectacles on the desk. "You're not wrong. You're just late. So am I. The question is what being late costs."

"The thief. Delacroix. Do you know him?"

"Balances the impossible accounts." Something flickered in the old woman's face — not quite a smile. "He once caught a transcription error in a ledger three generations dead because the sums felt wrong to him. Not misread. *Felt* wrong. I've often wondered what kind of mind does that." She looked at Sera steadily. "I've also wondered why Corvane approved his archive request personally and then sent dogs after him within the week. A man doesn't hunt what he doesn't fear. Ask yourself what a ledger-clerk has that frightens Master Corvane."

"He has the page."

"Mm. Or the page is the excuse." Maren turned back to her cataloguing, dismissal and blessing in the same gesture. "Whatever you decide to write in your report, Voss, remember: ink dries. Choose what it says while your hand still shakes. Once it stops shaking, you'll write whatever keeps you safe, and call that the truth."

* * *

She wrote the report that night, by candlelight, in her small precise enforcer's hand.

*Subject: Ada Delacroix, ledger-clerk, second tier. Theft confirmed: one page, Entry 4471, pre-war archive. Subject fled the Vane district shortly after the eleventh bell, direction unknown. Recommend full pursuit. Recommend the word 'unmarked' be honored.*

She wrote it the way Corvane wanted it written. She wrote it well — every detail accurate, every recommendation sound, the prose of a loyal instrument. It was a good report. It would have ended a good man, or at least a man who balanced sums that *felt* wrong, and it would have buried forty-one years of sold promises under the weight of one dead clerk.

She held it over the candle and watched the corner catch.

It burned quickly. Reports always do; they're made of dry words and dry paper, and fire has never needed much encouragement to eat the truth. She held it until the flame kissed her fingers, then dropped the last corner into the dish and watched it curl into ash that smelled, faintly, like nothing at all.

Then she took out a fresh sheet and wrote the report she would file in the morning.

*Subject: Ada Delacroix. Theft suspected but unconfirmed. Archive inventory shows no missing pages. Subject remains at his duties. Recommend continued observation.*

A lie, neatly done. It bought him days, maybe a week, before someone cross-checked the copies against the originals and noticed the gap she couldn't fully paper over. A week was not salvation. But she had stood guard over caged songs for six years, and this was the first time she had ever opened one, even a crack.

She didn't feel noble. That was the thing no one told you about choosing a side. There was no music, no sudden clarity, no sense of having become the hero of anything. There was just a woman sitting alone in a rented room, smelling of smoke, having traded her safety for a stranger's head start, and not even knowing why except that the alternative had made her sick.

Choosing, it turned out, happened before you understood what you'd chosen. The understanding came later, or it didn't.

She banked the candle, strapped the ward-smith's sigil tighter at her hip, and lay down in the dark.

Tomorrow she would file the lie. Tomorrow she would begin, quietly, officially, to hunt a man she had already decided not to catch — which meant staying one step behind him on purpose, and hoping he was fast enough to make her slowness look like diligence.

And tomorrow, too, she would need to find Ada Delacroix before anyone else did. Because a lie buys time, not safety, and somewhere in this ash-grey city a man was running blind with the guild's oldest sin folded in his coat — and Sera Voss had just become the only person in the world who knew both where he was going and what was chasing him.

She closed her eyes. Somewhere north, past the broken border, snow was already falling.

*Hurry up, clerk,* she thought, to a man she'd never met. *You have no idea how short your lead just became.*
