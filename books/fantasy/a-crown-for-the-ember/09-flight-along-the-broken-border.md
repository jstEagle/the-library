## 9. Flight Along the Broken Border

The knock came at the fourth bell of the morning, and Ada knew before he opened the door that it was over.

Three knocks. Even spacing. The rhythm of a man who wanted to be heard but not remembered — which meant Pellam Ostrey, junior clerk, second desk by the rain-stained window, the man whose arithmetic Ada had corrected so many times that the corrections had become a kind of cruelty. Pellam stood in the corridor of the clerks' lodging house holding his hat in both hands, and behind him, at the far end of the hall, stood two men in ash-grey coats who were not pretending to be anything other than what they were.

"Master Delacroix." Pellam's voice was steady. That was the worst part. Ada had expected stammering, apology, the trembling of a small man doing a small betrayal. Instead Pellam looked at him with something almost like pity. "Master Corvane asks that you come to the Vane. He asks that you come now, and that you bring nothing."

*Bring nothing.* Which meant Corvane knew about the page. Which meant Pellam had gone through the audit copies, or the night clerk had talked, or — no. Simpler. Pellam had simply watched Ada's face for three days and understood that a man carrying a secret walks differently.

"I'll need my coat," Ada said.

"Of course." Pellam stepped aside with terrible courtesy.

The ash-grey men did not step aside. The nearer one smiled with the bottom half of his face only. "We'll wait while you dress, Master Delacroix. We're told the morning is cold."

Ada dressed. He had imagined this moment — in the three days since he'd slid the ledger page from the binding of *Sales, Consolidated, Years 41–58*, he had imagined it a dozen times — and in every imagining he'd been calm, methodical, a clerk to the last. In the imagining, he palmed the page from its hiding place inside the lining of his coat, checked his satchel, and walked out the front door into a story where he was the clever one.

In the actual morning, his hands shook so badly he buttoned his shirt wrong twice, and when he lifted the coat from its hook the page whispered against the lining like a moth against glass.

The stormhawk was the problem. The stormhawk was always going to be the problem.

He'd freed it from the guild's counting-house cage two nights ago — a small treason, done in the dark, mostly because the bird had looked at him with one amber eye and said, in the pressure-and-silence way that beasts spoke to him, *the mountain is waking and you balance books.* He had meant to send it away. He had meant for it to fly south, or east, anywhere that wasn't him. Instead it had taken up residence on the lodging house roof, and when Ada stepped out into the grey morning between his two escorts, it dropped from the eaves with a sound like a torn sail and landed on his shoulder as if it had been invited.

The nearer ash-grey man's hand went to his belt.

"It's a trained bird," Ada said. "Guild property. I'm returning it."

"Guild property stays at the Vane."

"It stays with me or it stays nowhere. Corvane can take it up with the auditors." A lie so smooth it frightened him — there were no auditors left who mattered, and they both knew it. But the man hesitated, because ash-grey men were hired to handle clerks, not complications, and a bird that might be worth forty crowns was a complication.

They walked. The city of Vael Corrin was wrong in the way it had been wrong for nine days, since the stars went out — the same streets, the same smoke, the same fishwives arguing over the same silver — but underneath it all, a hum of wrongness, like a floor that had shifted a half-inch out of true and no one would admit it. Refugees still camped along the canal in the shadow of the customs house. A child was selling scorched feathers from the starless sky, claiming they were lucky. The guild had raised the toll on the north gate twice.

And the wards. The border wards that had stood for two hundred years were down, and Ada was carrying the proof that his guild had sold them off piece by piece, and the man walking three steps behind him was being paid to make sure that proof never reached anyone.

The Vane's counting-house rose ahead, all black timber and gilded gutters, and Ada understood that if he walked through that door he would not walk out again. Not as himself. Corvane would be reasonable. Corvane was always reasonable. There would be tea, and terms, and the gentle explanation that a man with Ada's particular gift — a gift outlawed since the Ember War, a gift Ada had hidden so long it had grown over like scar tissue — could be ruined with a word, or employed with a nod, and wouldn't he prefer the nod?

He had already refused once. He'd stolen the page the same night.

At the corner of Gilt Row, where the counting-house shadow reached across the cobbles like spilled ink, Ada did the only clever thing he would manage all day: he dropped his satchel.

It was heavy with a decade of ledgers and it hit the stones with a crack, papers bursting loose, and in the two seconds it took both ash-grey men to look down, Ada turned and ran.

* * *

He ran the way he had run as a boy through the orchards behind his mother's house, which is to say badly, all elbows and panic, but he ran through the fish market and he ran through the tannery lane where the smell covered everything and he ran until the counting-house was behind him and his lungs were full of broken glass.

The stormhawk rode his shoulder the whole way, wings half-spread for balance, and it said nothing, which from a stormhawk was encouragement.

Behind him — not close, not yet, but present, the way weather is present — came the sound of pursuit. Not shouting. The guild did not shout. Just footsteps, multiplying, and once, cutting across the market noise, a single low whistle: three notes, descending. The signal for *close the net.*

Ada cut south, toward the river, because south was wrong. Every hunter would expect a fugitive to run for the north gate, toward the broken border, toward the story Ada himself would have written if he'd had time to write one. South bought him an hour. Maybe two. He had a page in his coat and no plan beyond the page, and the page was written in a dead man's hand and named a fire under a mountain, and it was the only true thing he owned.

He was crossing the tanner's yard, boots slick with lime, when the wall spoke to him.

Not the wall. The rats in it. A dozen of them, pressed flat into the crevices of the stone, and their fear came off them in a wave he felt against his skin like cold water: *winged things, winged things over the river, the sky is full of hooked shadows.* Ada stopped. Looked up.

Three shapes circled above the tannery smoke. Too large for hawks. Too patient for gulls. Ward-hawks — guild-bred, spell-marked, birds that could hold a man's scent for two days and scream his location to anyone wearing a guild ring.

The stormhawk hissed on his shoulder, low and furious, and Ada felt the meaning of it settle into him like a stone into a pond: *they are not hunting a bird. They are hunting you, and they will find you by sundown if you stay in the city.*

"Then I leave the city," Ada whispered.

*South is a cage,* the hawk said. *South is where they are strongest.*

"North is where they expect me."

*Yes,* said the stormhawk, and there was something in its tone that Ada would spend the rest of his life trying to name — something almost like hope. *So go north anyway.*

* * *

He went north the way water goes downhill — by every crack and seam the city offered. Through the drain-cut beneath the chandler's wharf where the water came to his thighs and the cold closed around his chest like a hand. Up through the grate in the old customs garden, overgrown since the war, where the statues of the alliance generals stood with their faces worn to blanks. Along the canal wall in the blue hour before dusk, moving when bargemen moved, freezing when they didn't.

Twice the ward-hawks passed overhead, and twice the stormhawk rose off his shoulder and screamed at them — a raw, jagged sound — and the ward-hawks, guild-trained and guild-obedient, hesitated at the challenge of an unmarked bird and circled away. Ada crouched in the reeds with his heart slamming and understood that the hawk was spending itself for him. Ward-hawks remembered grudges. There would be a reckoning for this bird, eventually.

The north gate was impossible. He'd known it would be. The guild had men on it, ash-grey coats among the customs officers, checking faces. So Ada did not go to the north gate. He went to the aqueduct.

The old Vael aqueduct ran out of the city high on its arches, dry for sixty years, carrying nothing but pigeons and the memory of water. There was a maintenance stair behind the fullers' yard that no one guarded because no one remembered it — no one except clerks, and Ada had spent nine years reading the guild's property rolls the way other men read scripture. He climbed in the dark, the page a small hard weight against his ribs, and walked the aqueduct's spine out over the wall, out over the refugee camps, out over the last lamp-light of Vael Corrin, with the wind pulling at his coat and the whole broken city laid out below him like a ledger he could no longer balance.

He stopped once, at the highest arch, and looked back.

He had lived in that city for eleven years. He had a chair there. He had a mug with a chip in it that fit his thumb. He had a routine so finely worn that it had become a kind of armor — up at the third bell, ink, figures, the soft arithmetic of impossible things, home at dusk. He had been *safe* there, in the way a bird in a very fine cage is safe, and he stood on the aqueduct in the cold and grieved for it — genuinely grieved, with a hot pressure behind his eyes — because he knew, with a clerk's certainty, that he was never going to sit in that chair again. Whatever happened next, whether he reached the north or died in a ditch with a stolen page against his heart, the man who balanced the Gilded Vane's books was dead as of this morning, and Pellam Ostrey had killed him with three even knocks, and Ada found that he couldn't even hate him for it. Pellam had done what small men do. Ada had simply never believed he was one of them.

"Come on," he told the stormhawk, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he pretended it was the wind.

* * *

The border was visible from three miles out — a scar across the night.

Where the ward-line had run, a pale absence stretched from the eastern hills to the western marshes, a band where the stars should have been and weren't, where the dark had a texture to it, like the skin over a healing wound. The wards hadn't just fallen. They had been *unmade*, sold off in fragments to buyers whose names lived in a dead clerk's ledger, and the land along the scar had gone quiet in a way Ada felt in his teeth.

He came down off the aqueduct's end in open country, and that was when the wolf found him.

He didn't see it approach. One moment the moorland was empty, all gorse and frost and the starless dark pressing down, and the next there was a grey shape at the edge of his vision, large, still, watching him with the patience of something that had been watching for a long time.

Ada's whole body went cold. Then the wolf stepped closer, and the memories came — not words, never words, but memories, poured into him the way water pours into a dry channel — and he was standing in a burning orchard two hundred years ago, and a woman with Ada's own jaw was shouting at a council of frightened lords, and the wolf was younger, and the wards were singing, and the woman's hand was buried in the wolf's ruff as she said, *not this price, I will not pay this price, find another way* — and then the memory folded shut and Ada was on his knees in the frost, breathing hard, the stormhawk's wing across his shoulders like an arm.

The wolf — the scarred grey wolf from the refugee crush, the one that had shown him his grandmother's face — stood over him and waited.

"You," Ada said. His voice came out ruined. "You knew her. You knew *me* — before. You said I was heir to something." He got his feet under him. "I don't want it. Whatever it is. I'm a clerk. I balance books. I *hide.*"

The wolf looked at him, and the look contained, without cruelty, the entire weight of two centuries, and Ada heard the meaning of it the way you hear a door close in another room:

*The hunters are behind you. The Ember is ahead of you. There is no direction left that is hiding.*

Ada laughed — one cracked, disbelieving note — because the wolf was right, and because some traitorous part of him, the part that had stolen the page instead of burning it, the part that had freed the hawk instead of selling it, had known this was coming and had been walking toward it the whole time.

"How far to the holdfast?" he asked. "The northern alliance. What's left of it."

The wolf turned its head north, toward the scar in the sky, toward the mountains where something old and hungry was turning over in its sleep. And Ada felt the answer arrive not as a number of miles but as a shape of days — cold, and hunger, and hunters, and a pass that would be snowed shut by the time they reached it.

Behind him, far back along the dark road, a whistle sounded. Three notes, descending. Then, a moment later, answered from ahead — from the east, from the flank. They had guessed wrong about the gate but right about the direction, and the net was closing at a walk, patient as interest.

The wolf began to run. The stormhawk lifted off his shoulder and became a shadow above him. And Ada Delacroix, who had spent eleven years being careful, put his hand flat on the ledger page through his coat, took one breath of the freezing starless air, and ran — not away from the life he had lost, though he would grieve it for a long time yet, but toward the only thing left that was true.

The moor took him. The dark took him. And far to the north, beneath the mountains, the Ember turned in its sleep, and dreamed of warmth.
