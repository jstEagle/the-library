## 10. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The snow found us two days out from the border, and by the third day it had stopped pretending to be weather.

It fell in sheets so thick the world shrank to a grey bowl, and the stormhawk rode hunched under my cloak with her feathers pressed flat, muttering in that voice like wind through a keyhole: *cold, cold, cold, wrong.* The wolf said nothing at all. He hadn't since the ford, and I had learned that his silences were worse than any of his warnings.

So when we crested a ridge and saw smoke rising from a cluster of rooftops below — real smoke, hearth-smoke, dozens of chimneys' worth — I nearly wept.

"That's not on any map I've audited," I said. My voice came out cracked. Three days of flight will do that to a man. "A village this size should be paying tithes to someone."

*It pays no one,* the wolf said. His mind-voice was thinner than it had been, like a coin worn smooth. *It pays only itself.*

"What does that mean?"

He didn't answer. He was doing that more often now — starting an answer and then folding it away somewhere inside himself, the way a miser folds away a coin he can't bear to spend. I told myself it was exhaustion. The wolf was old; the flight had been hard on all of us. I told myself a lot of things those days. It's a clerk's habit. You find the entry that lets the books balance, even when you know, deep down, that the numbers are lying.

We went down into the snow together.

* * *

The village had no name, or rather, it had too many. The signpost at its edge had been carved and recarved so many times the wood was a palimpsest of grooves — *Ashen Hollow, Millbrook, Vane's Rest, Coldharbour* — each name half-effaced by the next, until the post looked less like a marker than a wound.

"They can't decide what they're called," I murmured.

"They aren't deciding," the wolf said. "They're remembering. Badly. Each one remembers a different name, because each one is remembering a different life." He paused at the signpost, and his scarred muzzle worked as if tasting the air. "Ada. Before we go in. There are things here you should be prepared for."

"More prepared than I was for the wards falling? For Corvane's hunters? For—" I gestured vaguely at everything behind us, which was everything.

"No," he said simply. "Nothing prepares you for this place. But I'd rather you flinch beside me than alone."

The first house we came to had its door open. Not broken — open, deliberately, hospitably, with a wreath of dried rowan berries hung above the lintel. A man stood in the doorway, sixty maybe, with a woodcutter's shoulders and a face gone soft around the eyes, and he smiled at us the way you smile at family.

"Travelers! Come in, come in, the kettle's just on." He stepped back to admit us before we'd said a word. "You'll be wanting soup. Everyone wants soup, coming off the pass. Sit, sit."

I looked at the wolf. The wolf looked at me. We went in, because the alternative was the snow, and because there was something in the man's warmth that made refusal feel like cruelty.

The house was clean and poor and full of small wrongnesses. A shelf of clay figurines, each one lovingly made and none of them matching any style I knew. A wall hung with portraits — charcoal sketches, dozens of faces — and beneath them, small cards in careful handwriting: *My mother, I think. She had hands like mine.* *A friend? He is laughing at something I said once.* *Unknown. Beautiful. Keep.*

"You sketch them all?" I asked.

"We sketch everyone," the man said, ladling soup into bowls chipped and mended with gold — actual gold, seams of it running through the clay like veins. "That's Tamra's doing. She says if we don't write people down, we lose them twice." He handed me a bowl and beamed. "I'm... hold on. It's here somewhere."

He patted his chest, then his pockets, and finally produced from his shirt a small tin locket, worn shiny with handling. Inside was a slip of paper gone soft as cloth.

"Barn," he read, with enormous satisfaction. "No — Barnaby. Barnaby Fell. Good. I'm Barnaby Fell." He said it the way a man confirms a password. Then, cheerfully, as if reporting the weather: "I've lost forty-one years. But who's counting?"

The stormhawk shifted under my cloak and made a sound I'd never heard from her before — low, mournful, almost human.

* * *

They called it the Tithing, and they spoke of it the way farmers speak of a drought — flatly, without drama, because drama requires a memory of before.

The last winter of the Ember War had come down off the mountains early and stayed nine years. Crops failed. Herds died. And into that famine, the story goes, came traders — not guild men, not exactly, though the guild's mark was on some of their scales — offering grain and coal and salt in exchange for something the villagers had in surplus and didn't know was valuable.

Memories.

Not all of them. Never the load-bearing ones, the traders said. Keep your name, keep your children's faces, keep how to sow and reap and mend a roof. Give us the rest. The taste of your wedding feast. Your grandfather's stories. Every summer you swam in the millpond. The color of the sky the day the war ended. Who needs all that, when there's bread?

And the villagers, watching their children's ribs show through winter skin, had paid.

"It seemed fair," said a woman named Odile, who told me this on my second evening there, while she mended a net by the fire. She was perhaps fifty, with a strong jaw and the particular stillness of someone listening to a room for sounds only she can't hear. "You have to understand. Hunger makes economists of us all. Everything has a price. We just hadn't known memory was on the market."

"And now?" I asked. "Do you regret it?"

She considered the question with genuine care, turning it over like a stone.

"I don't know," she said. "That's rather the point, isn't it? I can't miss what I can't remember missing. Sometimes I wake up crying and don't know why, and I think — well. There's a grief with no address. It wanders the house at night looking for its door." She bit her thread cleanly. "Tamra keeps the ledgers. She writes down what people tell her about themselves, before the telling goes. She says a village is just a memory that agreed to be shared. So we share."

"A ledger," I repeated. My mouth had gone dry. "Of memories."

"Of people. Same thing, out here." She glanced up at me, and her eyes were sharp and kind and utterly without self-pity. "You're the traveler with the dog. Or the wolf. Maren swears it spoke to her. We don't mind, you know. Strange things are welcome here. We've all been edited; it makes us tolerant of drafts."

* * *

I met the daughter on the third day, though neither of them knew it.

Her name was Liss, and she was maybe nineteen, quick and brown-eyed, with her mother's exact jaw — I saw it at once, the same strong line, the same way of holding her chin when she listened. She ran the village's one communal kitchen, and she fed me stew and bread and asked nothing, which after three days of hunters on my trail felt like a sacrament.

Odile came in at dusk, as she did every dusk, and sat across from Liss, and they ate together in comfortable silence. Two women sharing a table. Nothing strange in it.

Except.

"Your mother used to sing while she cooked," Liss said suddenly, apropos of nothing. "Did you know that?"

"I did not know that," Odile said, interested. "What did she sing?"

"I don't know. I remember that she sang. The song itself is..." Liss frowned, chasing something. "Gone. One of the first things traded, I think. Bread was short that year." She laughed a little, helplessly. "Funny. I kept the fact and lost the thing. Like keeping the receipt and losing the purchase."

Odile reached over and squeezed her hand. It was a warm gesture, easy, practiced — the gesture of a woman comforting a friend.

And Liss received it the way you receive kindness from a stranger: gratefully, and slightly from a distance.

I set down my spoon.

"You're her daughter," I said. It came out louder than I meant. The kitchen went quiet. "Liss. You're Odile's daughter. You have her face."

The silence changed texture. Around me, villagers exchanged glances — not shocked, not scandalized. Resigned. This had happened before. This happened often.

"Oh," Odile said softly. And then, with dawning wonder, like a woman reading a letter long delayed: "Oh, look at her. Look at her jaw. That's my mother's jaw, going back." She stood, crossed the room, and took Liss's face in both her hands, studying it with the naked delight of discovery. "Hello. Hello. I'm told I'm your mother."

"You're told every few months," Liss said gently. "Sometimes by me. You always take it well."

"How do you stand it?" The question tore out of me before I could dress it in manners. "How do either of you—"

Liss looked at me, and her expression wasn't bitterness. That was the worst part. It was patience, worn smooth as the locket Barnaby carried.

"The first year, I raged," she said. "I wrote it all down for her — everything, our whole lives, every birthday, every fever, the night my father died. I read it to her every morning like scripture. And she'd weep, and hold me, and mean it completely. And by supper it would be gone again, because the feeling doesn't stick if the memory isn't there to hang it on. You can't grieve on credit." She turned back to her mother, and her voice gentled. "So we stopped grieving forward and started loving sideways. She doesn't remember being my mother. But she likes me. She chooses my table every night. Out of everything she could be, with everything she's lost, she keeps choosing to sit with me. I've decided that counts."

Odile was still holding her daughter's face, memorizing it — I understood now that she was memorizing it, actively, the way you'd copy out figures you expected to be audited on later.

"She has lovely eyes," Odile told me seriously. "I hope I had something to do with that."

"You did," I managed. "Everything. You had everything to do with that."

"Well," she said, pleased. "Good."

* * *

I walked out into the snow afterward because the walls had become unbearable.

The wolf found me at the village edge, where the nameless signpost leaned into the wind. I was staring at the carved-over names and trying to make my hands stop shaking.

"That's the price," I said. "That's what it costs. Not death. Death is clean. That—" I couldn't finish it. Liss choosing, nightly, to love a woman who would forget her by morning. Odile's delight, discovering her own child like a stranger's portrait. *There's a grief with no address.*

"Yes," the wolf said.

"My grandmother refused," I said. "Whatever it was she refused — you've shown me her face, you've shown me the mountain, you keep saying heir, and I still don't know heir to *what* — but she refused, and I've spent my whole life thinking that made her brave." I turned to him. "What did refusing cost her? What does it cost *me*? Because I'm starting to think the crown you keep not-naming isn't made of gold. It's made of exactly this. Exactly her. A woman at a table, meeting her daughter forever for the first time."

The wolf was quiet a long moment. Snow gathered on his back and he didn't shake it off.

"When the wards were raised," he said finally, "they needed feeding. Not blood. Not souls — whatever Corvane's masters have promised you, the old wards were never built on that. They were built on *memory*. Living memory, freely given. The guardians poured in their pasts — their names, their childhoods, their dead — and the wards held those memories the way a lamp holds oil, and burned them slowly, over centuries, to keep the dark out." His breath steamed. "Your grandmother was offered the crown. Warden of the Ember Line. She looked at the price — looked at women like Odile, guardians who'd given everything and woke each dawn strangers to their own children — and she refused. She chose to keep herself. She married, had children, grew old whole. And the ward-line thinned, and thinned, and the ones who *had* paid watched their sacrifice leak away into neglect, and the mountain..."

He stopped.

"The mountain got hungry," I finished for him. My voice sounded far away. "Corvane's buyer. The fragments. The Ember stirring. It all traces back to a woman who wanted to keep her memories. To keep being someone's mother at breakfast *and* supper."

"Do not judge her," the wolf said, sharp for the first time. "Everyone judges her, eventually. The histories call her a coward. But Ada — I showed you her face. Show me the person who wouldn't choose as she chose, standing where she stood. The crown asks everything. And it gives nothing back but the world."

"The world," I said. "Which includes daughters at kitchen tables."

"Yes."

I looked back at the village, lamplit against the blue dark. Somewhere in there, Odile was sitting with a girl whose face she'd have to discover again tomorrow, and choosing her anyway. Choosing her nightly, without the anchor of remembering why. If that wasn't love, I didn't know what the word was for anymore. And if the crown demanded that I become Odile — hollowed, relearning, a stranger fed my own life secondhand by whoever survived me — then my grandmother hadn't been a coward. She'd been a woman who understood arithmetic better than the bards ever would.

But someone had to pay eventually. Debts like that don't vanish. They compound.

"The ledger page," I said quietly. "The fragments. Corvane's buyers. All of it — it's the same debt, isn't it? They're selling the wards' memories off piece by piece, and the Ember wakes a little more with every sale, and eventually someone stands where my grandmother stood and pays principal." I wrapped my arms around myself. "I'm carrying a page north. That's all. One page. And I keep having this feeling it's actually a bill."

The wolf pressed his shoulder against my leg — the closest he came to comfort.

"Rest tonight," he said. "Tomorrow we climb. The holdfast is eight days north, if the passes hold."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we learn what else the winter takes."

Behind us, a door opened, spilling gold light across the snow. Liss stood in it, a lantern in her hand.

"You'll sleep in the kitchen," she called. "Everyone does, their last night. Mother's asking for you — she's sketched your face. She wants to know if she got the eyes right." A pause, and then, wry and gentle: "Fair warning, traveler. In this village, getting remembered is a debt too. Whatever she writes about you tonight, someone will read aloud at your funeral."

I laughed, and it broke something loose in my chest, and I followed her in out of the cold.

In the morning, Tamra's ledger would carry a new entry in a stranger's hand: *The traveler with the wolf. Frightened of the wrong things. Kind to Odile. Going north to pay something.* 

And pinned beneath it, in charcoal so fine it looked breathed onto the paper, my face — the eyes done perfectly, the fear in them done even better than I could have managed myself.
