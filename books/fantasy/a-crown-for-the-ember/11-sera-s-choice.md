## 11. Sera's Choice

The road out of the village was a scar of mud and hoofprints, and Sera Voss had been walking it since before dawn with a blade she hadn't decided how to use.

Three days behind him. That was the gap the stormhawk had bought — the bird had gone up through the chimney smoke at the Gilded Vane like a thrown knife, and by the time the hunters had stopped cursing and saddled their horses, Ada Delacroix was already a rumor on the north road. But rumors slow down. Horses don't. She'd cut ahead of the hunting party on the second night, taken the old drover's track through the hills alone, and now she stood in the grey light where the track bent toward the river ford, waiting for a clerk with stolen paper to come stumbling into her reach.

Her orders were simple. *Recover the page. Recover the thief. If recovery is not possible, make it possible.*

She had written those orders herself, two winters ago, in the neat hand the guild paid for. Back then they'd been about smugglers and informants and a spice-merchant who'd skimmed from the wrong ledger. Back then they'd been words.

Now they were Ada.

She heard him before she saw him — or rather she heard what he carried. The stormhawk made a sound no hawk should make, low and conversational, a mutter of feathers, and then a man's voice answering it softly, half-laughing, exhausted. Talking to a bird on an open road like it was nothing. Like it wasn't the crime that had burned people at the stake forty years ago and still got you a shallow grave now.

Sera stepped out from behind the waystone with her blade drawn and said, "Don't."

He didn't run. She'd expected running. Instead he stopped, one hand rising slowly, and looked at her the way a man looks at weather he'd been dreading all day finally arriving. Up close he was worse than the guild's description — thinner, mud to the knee, eyes ringed dark as ash around a wick. The satchel strap crossed his chest, and beneath his coat she could see the shape of the folded pages pressing against him like a second heartbeat.

"Master Voss." His voice cracked on the name. "I wondered which of them they'd send."

"They sent three. I came first." She kept the blade level. Steady hands were the whole craft; her father had taught her that before the guild ever did. "Give me the page, Ada. Maybe I lie in my report. Maybe I don't. It's more chance than my colleagues will offer you."

"The page isn't mine to give." He said it quietly, without drama, and that was somehow worse than if he'd clutched it and begged. "You've read what's in the ledgers. You escorted him. You watched the fragments go into the ash-grey wagons."

"I guarded cargo," she said. "That's the job."

"And do you know what the cargo was?"

She knew. God help her, she'd known since the third night out of the city, when the crates had started humming against her palms through the wood — a note below hearing, patient and grieving, like a choir holding one long breath. Ward-stone wasn't supposed to hum. Ward-stone was dead rock shaped into purpose. This was something else. This was something alive, packed in straw and sold by the crate, and she had checked its manifests and counted its pieces and slept beside it without asking a single question because asking questions was how ward-smiths became unemployed ward-smiths, and unemployed ward-smiths became examples.

"Yes," she said.

Something moved through his face — not triumph. Grief, maybe, or relief. "Then you know why I can't give it back."

* * *

They stood in the rain-light while the river talked to itself below the ford, and Sera found that her arm was getting tired in a way that had nothing to do with the weight of the blade.

She thought about the buyer. The ash-grey man who never gave a name, whose gloves never came off, whose voice sounded like sand poured over sand. She had walked him out of the city herself, four times now, and each time the crates had hummed louder, and each time Master Corvane's smile had gotten warmer and his ledgers had gotten cleaner, and somewhere between the fourth escort and this muddy road the professional detachment she'd worn like armor had developed a crack, and through the crack she could hear the humming, and the humming sounded like *please.*

"You could kill me," Ada said. "I want to be honest about the arithmetic. One blade, one tired clerk, thirty paces to the water. You'd have the page back in an hour and a commendation by month's end."

"I know the arithmetic."

"But you're still talking to me."

She hated him a little for pointing it out. "I'm deciding," she said.

"No," he said, gentle as a hand on a wound. "You decided before you left the city. Nobody rides ahead of a hunting party alone unless they've already chosen. You just haven't told yourself yet."

The blade dipped. She raised it again, furious at the tremor. "You don't know anything about me."

"I know your sigil." His eyes went to her collar, where the small stamped disc hung on its chain — the ward-smith's mark, a circle broken by a keyhole. Her grandfather's trade. Her shame and her inheritance both. "My grandmother wore one like it. Before the war took her. They used to light the border stones with those, didn't they? Circle and keyhole. *We hold the door.*"

*We hold the door.* She hadn't said the oath aloud since she was nine years old, standing on her grandfather's workbench while he laughed. The guild had bought the sigils off the smiths' families one by one, cheap, during the lean years — not for the metal but so no unaffiliated hands would carry the mark. She'd sold hers the way everyone sold theirs: gradually, then all at once.

"It's just metal," she said.

"It's never been just metal," said the stormhawk, from its perch on his shoulder, in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

Sera's blade came up sharp and her whole body followed it, and Ada flinched forward with both hands out — *no, wait, wait* — and the bird regarded her with one molten gold eye and repeated, patiently, "*We hold the door.*"

"How—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "How does it know that?"

"He knows lots of things," Ada said. "He's older than the war. Sera — please. Sit down. Or don't sit, but put the sword down, and let me show you what I'm carrying. Not the page. Something else. Something I need someone with your hands to understand."

Every instinct the guild had ground into her said: *the target is stalling, the target is manipulating, the target is a clerk with a soft voice and a criminal gift.* Every instinct, and beneath every instinct, fainter than all of them but getting louder by the day, the humming of the crates, the grief in the straw, the sound of a living thing being sold by the pound.

She sheathed the blade. It felt like falling.

* * *

What he showed her was a fragment — a real one, small as a knucklebone, wrapped in wool inside a tin. When he peeled the wool back, her hands knew it before her mind caught up. She'd grown up around ward-stone. This was not ward-stone.

It was warm. It pulsed, very slightly, the way a sleeping bird's breast pulses. And when she took it — when her bare fingers touched it, the fingers that had been trained on dead stone for twenty years — it turned toward her warmth like a flower toward sun, and it *sang*, one clear thread of sound that only she seemed able to hear, and the song was a memory of standing in a line across a mountain pass with ten thousand others, holding back a fire that ate the sky, and it was proud, and it was tired, and it was so terribly lonely.

Sera sat down in the mud without meaning to.

"That's what we've been shipping," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "That's what the wagons carry. They're not fragments. They're *survivors.*"

"Pieces of the old wards that tore loose when the lines fell," Ada said. "Alive. Bound, like everything the old alliance built. And the ash-grey buyer is feeding them to something under the mountains that eats bound things. Corvane knows. The ledgers prove he's known for decades. Page after page, in my predecessor's hand — sales, escorts, prices. Your name is in there, Sera. Four times."

"Then burn it," she said instantly, and heard her own cowardice in the words, and hated it, and kept going anyway because honesty was a door you couldn't half-open. "Burn the ledgers, save yourself—"

"If I burn them, nothing stops. The Ember wakes, the buyers find another guild, another Corvane. The evidence has to reach the northern holdfast. The Unforgiven. The descendants of the alliance — they still hold the old ward-crown knowledge, whatever's left of it." He paused. "There's a problem with the plan."

"You'll never get there," she said. "Three hunters, a week behind you and closing, and every gate on the north road has the guild's coin in it. No. There are worse problems than that." She looked at the little singing thing in her palm and closed her fingers around it, careful, the way you close your hand around a sparrow. "The holdfast doesn't take strangers. The last time anyone from the south came asking, they nailed the asking to the gate. You need a key. A word. A *mark.*"

His face did something complicated. "You know how to get in."

"I know how to get in," Sera said. "Because I'm going to tell them I captured you."

* * *

She made him build a fire — small, screened, the kind of fire you build when you don't want to be found, though by now not being found was a luxury neither of them could afford — and over it she laid out what she knew, drawing in the dirt with a stick because some maps shouldn't exist on paper.

"The hunters will follow my report, and my report will say I cornered you at the ford, that you ran east toward the coast, that I wounded you and lost you in the reed-beds. That buys two days while they drag the marshes." She drew the river, the hills beyond it. "But reports get questioned. So I stay visible. I lead them myself, east, loudly, and I keep leading them until either they believe me or they decide I'm the second thief."

"Sera—"

"Don't." She said it hard enough that he actually stopped. "Don't do the thing where you thank me. I've seen men thank other men on scaffolds."

She worked the chain over her head. The ward-smith's sigil came off easier than it had gone on twenty years ago — no catch, no knot, as if it had only been resting there. She held it out to him on her open palm, and the little fragment in the tin sang once, recognizing it, and Ada stared at the mark like a man staring at water in a desert.

"I can't take that."

"You can't get through the gate without it. Show it to the woman on the wall — red scarf, always, even in summer, don't ask me why, nobody remembers. Say: *the door holds if someone holds the door.* Those exact words. She'll hate you on principle, and then she'll let you in, because that phrase hasn't been spoken inside those walls since the war and it means exactly one thing: someone's grandmother was a smith." She pressed the sigil into his hand and closed his fingers over it, the same way she'd closed them over the fragment. "It was my grandfather's. The guild let me keep it because they thought taking the oath away mattered more than taking the metal. They were wrong about which one I'd miss."

"Why?" His voice was rough. "Why any of this? You had a clean report. You had a life."

She almost lied. She opened her mouth and the lie was right there, easy, professional: *it's strategy, it's arithmetic, one courier is worth more than one enforcer.* But he'd spent years keeping a secret that was eating him alive, and he'd see through it, and she found — to her genuine surprise — that she didn't want to be seen through and found hollow.

"Because I counted the crates," she said. "Two hundred and eleven fragments out of the city in fourteen months, and I signed for every one, and I never asked where they sleep at night. Because my grandfather lit the pass at Greystone and died holding it, and I've been selling his life's work by the pound and calling it a job." She stood, brushing the mud from her knees, and picked up her blade, and the motion was steady now, steadier than it had been in days. "That's not conscience, Ada. Conscience would have stopped it sooner. Call it what it is — a debt. I'm paying mine north. You're carrying it."

* * *

At the fork above the ford they split, and the split cost more than she'd budgeted.

He hesitated — of course he hesitated, he was a clerk, hesitation was his whole training — and she shoved the satchel strap higher on his shoulder and said, "North. Don't look back, don't stop for strays, and if the wolf finds you again, listen to it. Whatever it asks. Especially if it asks for something that frightens you."

"You know about the wolf."

"Everyone in the hunting party knows about the wolf. It's why they doubled the bounty." She managed half a smile. "Go."

He went — three steps, five — and turned anyway, because clerks never learn, and called back across the water: "The door holds if someone holds the door!"

"*Go,*" she shouted, laughing despite everything, and he went, the stormhawk lifting off his shoulder in a flare of storm-grey wings, and then the reeds swallowed him and the north road held nothing but rain and distance, and Sera Voss stood alone at the fork with a drawn blade and an empty palm where twenty years of her life had hung on a chain.

Then she turned east, toward the marshes, toward the three riders who would arrive within the hour, and began composing the report she would deliver bleeding and convincing: *cornered him at the ford, he ran for the coast, wounded, lost him in the reeds.*

Behind her, far off, a hawk cried once — high, wild, ordinary, giving away nothing.

Ahead, through the rain, she could already see the dust of horses coming, and she rolled her shoulders, and set her face into the shape they paid for, and walked to meet them.

She did not pray. Ward-smiths don't pray; they hold doors.

But as the riders hove into view and her hand found her hilt, she thought — clearly, calmly, the way the little fragment had sung — *hold, then. Hold as long as you can. Hold past sense.*

The first hunter reined in ten feet away and looked at the empty road behind her, and asked where the thief had gone.

Sera smiled her guild smile, and pointed east, and started lying like a woman building a wall one stone at a time between the world and the fire.
