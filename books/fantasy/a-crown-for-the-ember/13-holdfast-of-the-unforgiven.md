## 13. Holdfast of the Unforgiven

The holdfast announced itself two days before we saw it, in the way the land changed its mind about us.

The pines thickened. The road, which had been a rumor for a hundred miles, became a thing with shoulders — cut stone, drained ditches, waymarkers carved with sigils I didn't recognize until I did: the interlocked rings of the old alliance, worn shallow by weather but still legible if you knelt. I knelt. The wolf stood beside me while I traced them, and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say that the stone hadn't already said. *Someone built this to last. Someone built this against the Ember, and then everyone who remembered why went home.*

The stormhawk rode the wind above us in wide, patient circles. She had been quiet since the pass — not silent, never silent, her mind a low pressure against my own like weather coming — but quiet, the way a witness is quiet before a trial.

The wolf was not quiet. The wolf was dying, and he made no more effort to hide it than a guttering lamp does. Each morning the grey of his coat had gone a little more ash, a little more the color of the buyer in Corvane's counting room, and each morning it took him longer to stand. He walked ahead of me now not as a guide but as a point of pride, and I had learned not to offer my arm, because the one time I had, he had looked at me with such ancient, offended dignity that I'd laughed and then, stupidly, nearly wept.

"Two more days," I said, on the morning the waymarkers began to appear every mile instead of every ten. "You can last two more days."

*I lasted the last war,* he said, in that voice that wasn't voice, that came up through the soles of my boots and the bones of my jaw. *I can last your committee.*

It was the closest thing to a joke I'd ever heard him make. I carried it with me the rest of the day like a coal in my pocket.

* * *

We saw the holdfast at dusk on the second day, and it was not what the histories in the Gilded Vane's archive had promised. The archive had a drawing — I had balanced the ledger of the archive itself, once, and read what I wasn't supposed to, the way I read everything I wasn't supposed to — and the drawing showed towers, banners, a gate of black iron. What stood on the shoulder of the mountain was a fortress that had decided, sometime in the last century, to stop pretending it was anything but a fist. Squat. Windowless on the approach side. The gate was timber banded with iron, and the iron was rusted in long weeping streaks, like the wall had been crying for years and no one had the heart to mention it.

Above the gate, the alliance sigil — the interlocked rings — had been defaced. Not destroyed. *Defaced.* Someone had chiseled a slash through it, clean and deliberate, and left the rest.

"They don't like what they are," I said.

*They don't like what their grandparents were,* the wolf corrected. *It's different. Grandchildren always think they'd have chosen better.*

There were sentries on the wall, and they saw us long before we saw them — I knew because the stormhawk banked and screamed a warning down at me, sharp as a struck flint. *Arrows. Four. Not drawn. Yet.*

"Put your hands where they can see them," I told the wolf, absurdly.

*I am a wolf. My hands are where they have always been.*

The challenge came from the wall in a woman's voice, flat and bored in the way that only truly dangerous people are bored. "Name yourself."

"Ada Delacroix," I called. "Keeper of ledgers, late of the Gilded Vane, out of Corvane's own counting room. I've come with testimony the council will want."

A pause. Then, from someone else on the wall, a laugh — short, disbelieving. "A clerk. They sent us a clerk."

"They sent no one," I said. "That's rather the point."

The gate did not open that night. It did not open the next morning either. They took my ledger page — I let them, after making them watch me memorize the numbers, which I had long since done — and they took my knife, and they did not take the wolf, because the wolf declined to be taken, and there is a limit to what eight men with spears will insist upon. They put me in a room with a barred window and a straw pallet and a bowl of something grey and hot, and they told me the council would hear me when the council heard me.

The stormhawk sat on the roof outside my window all night, and through the bars, in the dark, she spoke to me in images instead of words, because words cost her something — I had begun to understand that, that speech was for her a kind of spending. She showed me the holdfast from above. She showed me the smoke of its forges. And she showed me, three times, a thing I didn't understand until morning: a courtyard, a pyre, and a ring of stones with names on them so old the names had gone smooth.

In the morning I asked a guard — a young man with a burn-scar on his neck and the eyes of someone who had signed on for the meals — what the ring of stones was for.

He looked at me like I'd asked what the sky was for. "The Oathbreakers," he said. "Everyone knows that." And then, because I must have looked as ignorant as I felt: "The alliance lords. After the war. When they wouldn't give back what they took from each other." He shrugged. "They burned each other's oaths and put the stones up so nobody'd forget. Then everybody forgot anyway."

*There it is,* the wolf said, from where he lay in the yard below my window, pretending to sleep, fooling no one. *The whole history of your alliance in a guard's shrug. They ended the war, and then they spent a hundred years prosecuting it.*

* * *

They heard me on the fourth day, in a hall built for a larger council than the one that now used it.

I had imagined — I don't know what I'd imagined. Corvane's counting room had rotted my expectations. I think I had imagined men like Corvane: smooth, expensive, interested. What I got was a horseshoe of benches behind a table of black oak, and on the benches perhaps forty people, and not one of them dressed better than a farmer, and every one of them looking at me the way you look at weather that has come at a bad time.

There were factions. I saw them before anyone spoke, because I had spent nine years reading a room's ledgers before I read its faces. On the left: the old families, or what remained of them — three women and a man with a heron sigil worked in faded thread on their collars, sitting slightly apart, slightly straighter. In the middle: the practical sort, the ones with ink on their fingers and mud on their boots, who ran the holdfast the way you run anything, by getting up earlier than everyone else. And on the right, apart, a cluster of maybe eight — younger, mostly, hard-mouthed — who did not look at me at all, but at the left-hand benches, with an expression I recognized from the Gilded Vane's back rooms, where the clerks who'd been passed over watched the ones who hadn't.

A woman in the center rose. Grey-haired, square as a door, hands like a smith's. "I am Maren Ostrev, First Speaker of this council. You are the clerk. You have four minutes."

"I have testimony that took nine years to gather—"

"You have four minutes. The last person who stood there with urgent testimony was a wool merchant who claimed the river god had spoken to him. He had three minutes. He used them poorly."

Somewhere on the right-hand bench, a snort.

I took out the stolen ledger page — they had returned it, which told me they'd read it and found it unconvincing, or unreadable, or uninteresting — and I laid it flat on the black oak, and I began to speak the numbers. I gave them the dates. I gave them the weights, because ward-fragments were sold by weight, the way you'd sell ore, and I said that word — *ore* — and watched the old families flinch. I gave them the buyer's description: ash-grey cloak, gloves, a voice like a door closing. I gave them the route the fragments had taken, north by northwest, which ended, if you held a map in your head, at the mountain where the Ember slept.

I finished with time to spare, because I had rehearsed, because I had spent four days in a cell rehearsing.

Silence. The bad kind. The kind that has already decided.

It was one of the heron-collared women who spoke first, and her voice was dry as old paper. "Ledgers." She said the word the way you'd say a disease. "The Vane's books. Brought to us by a Vane clerk. Do you know what the Gilded Vane sold my grandmother, clerk? Ward-anchors. Promised her they'd hold the pass at Sorrel Ford. She paid in land." She smiled without any warmth in it at all. "The pass did not hold."

"The fragments were already dead by then," I said. "Or the guild had already sold the living ones north. Either way, madam, that's my testimony. The guild has been stripping the border wards for forty years and selling the pieces to the Ember's own—"

"To the Ember's own *what?*" This from the right-hand benches, a young man with a jaw like a clenched fist, on his feet now. "Say it. Say the name. You clerk-types love your euphemisms. Say what your guild armed."

I said it. "The Ember. The thing beneath the mountain. The one your grandmothers' alliance bled for thirty years to put down."

"And where," he said, taking his time, walking his words down the length of the hall like a man laying out weapons, "where was the alliance, clerk, when the wards started thinning? Where were the herons and the ostrevs when the border villages burned in my grandfather's time? Selling them grain. At interest." He turned to the center bench. "Now a Vane man walks out of the snow with a piece of paper and a wolf, and we're meant to march south and die for the honor of people who let the wards rot because mending them was *expensive.*"

"Sit down, Torv," said Maren Ostrev.

"I'm standing until someone tells me why I should sit."

And that was the hall for the next hour. I stood at the black oak table while forty descendants of the alliance that ended the Ember War conducted the Ember War, right there, in front of me, with the same grievances, the same names, the same burned farms and unpaid debts, passed down like heirlooms, kept polished, kept ready. The heron woman accused the right-hand benches' grandparents of oath-breaking at Sorrel Ford. Torv accused the herons of profiteering off the same battle. Someone's great-uncle had hanged someone else's great-uncle. Someone's family had held the wall and someone's family had held the purse and neither had ever forgiven the other, and the ring of Oathbreaker stones in the courtyard sat outside the window like a headstone for the whole argument, which had outlived every person who could have ended it.

I tried, twice, to speak. The second time, Maren Ostrev raised one smith's hand and I stopped, because I understood, suddenly and completely, what the stormhawk had been showing me from the roof. Truth doesn't convince anyone. Truth is just another banner, and every faction in that hall had a pile of true things of their own, stacked and yellowed and forty years deep.

I looked at the wolf. He had come into the hall — no one had dared stop him — and he lay at the foot of the table, grey as ash now, grey as the buyer, his breathing a slow bellows sound. He met my eyes. And what he communicated was not encouragement. It was arithmetic. *You have one thing they don't have, clerk. Spend it.*

*It will cost her,* I said. *Speaking through me. It nearly killed her over the pass.*

*Everything costs,* the wolf said. *That is the entire lesson of this hall. The only question the council is actually debating is who pays. So show them a witness who has already paid.*

I raised my hand. Maren Ostrev, out of what I think was exhaustion rather than courtesy, nodded.

"You won't believe me," I said. "That's fair. I'm a clerk. I balance books for people who sell the world by the ounce. So I won't testify. I'll call a witness." I looked up, through the smoke-hazed clerestory windows. "She's been on your roof for four days. She's a stormhawk, and she was alive when the border wards fell, and she carried the last warning out of the pass at Sorrel Ford the night it happened, and she has been trying to give that warning ever since, to anyone who could hear her." I let the silence sit for exactly one breath. "She can't speak. Not with a throat. But there are people born who can lend her theirs. My grandmother was one. I am one. It's the crime I've hidden for nine years, and I am committing it in front of this council right now, and I would like permission to spend the next minute being a criminal."

The hall went very still. Even Torv.

Maren Ostrev looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked at the wolf, and something moved behind her face — recognition, or its ghost. "My grandfather," she said quietly, "swore there were still beast-speakers in the south. The council called him a drunk." She turned her hand over. Open palm. *Proceed.*

The stormhawk came down through the smoke-hole like a thrown knife.

She landed on the black oak table between my hands and the ledger page, and the whole hall leaned back as one animal, and she looked at me, and she asked — in the way she asked, a pressure, a permission — *Are you certain.*

"No," I said. "Do it anyway."

She stepped onto my wrist. Her talons closed, gentle, terrible. And she reached into me the way she had reached into me on the pass — but this time I didn't fight it, didn't brace, didn't try to keep any of myself for myself. I opened the door of my voice all the way to the floor and told her, *speak.*

What came out of my mouth was not my voice.

It was wind. It was the pass at night. It was the sound the wards made as they died — and I hadn't known I carried that sound, I hadn't known she'd left it in me, a scream stretched over four seconds that felt like four years, the sound of a wall of living light coming apart into ash. And over it, layered, precise as a ledger entry, her testimony: the number of riders in ash-grey. The direction they rode. The cage-wagons, and the humming inside them, the grieving inside them. The stars going out one by one, like lamps being pinched by a careful hand. And last — because she made me say it, she put the words in my mouth and they were her words and they were true — *I saw the mountain open its eye. I have flown forty years and I have never seen anything open its eye.*

I came back to myself on my knees beside the table, blood in my mouth, the stormhawk breathing hard against my wrist, her talons still in my arm, holding me up as much as holding on.

The hall was silent. A different silence. The silence of forty people who had all, in the same instant, remembered the same thing their grandparents had told them and never quite believed.

It was Torv, of all of them, who broke it. He was pale. He sat down slowly, like an old man. "That sound," he said. "My grandmother heard it. At Sorrel Ford. She went deaf in one ear from it." He looked at me, and there was no hatred left in his face, which was somehow worse. "It's true. Gods help us, it's true."

And then the doors of the hall banged open, and a rider came in still wearing the snow, and everything after that happened very fast.

The rider was one of ours — one of the holdfast's, I mean, a scout, and she didn't bother with the bow or the formalities. She stood in the doorway with frost in her eyebrows and said, to the whole council, in a voice that carried:

"Beacon's lit on the south ridge. The old war-beacon, the one nobody's tended since my great-grandmother's day. It's burning green." She swallowed. "And there's an army on the march behind it, and they're driving something ahead of them in cages, and whatever's in the cages is *screaming*, and the snow where it passes comes down black."

The wolf raised his head. It seemed to cost him everything he had left.

*They're here,* he said. *Clerk — Ada — they are here, and you have one day to turn this hall of grudges into an army.*

Maren Ostrev was already on her feet. Around the horseshoe of benches, forty faces turned — not to me, not to her, but to each other, and the old hatreds were still there, I could see them, burning in every heron collar and every clenched jaw like banked coals.

The only question, the same question the hall had been asking for a hundred years, hung in the smoke:

*Who pays.*
