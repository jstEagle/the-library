## 14. Corvane's Peace

They kept her in a room with a window.

Sera understood the message in that immediately. Not a cell — a cell said *you matter*. A room with a window said *we don't need to hold you, because you aren't going anywhere, and if you did, it would change nothing*. The bars on the window were decorative. The lock on the door was the kind she could have picked at fourteen. The message was the point.

Three days now. Or four. The long winter made days hard to count; the light came grey and left grey, and the snow fell so steadily that morning and evening were the same color. She had a cot, a basin, a chamber pot, and a guard — a young woman with a crossbow and the flat, apologetic stare of someone who'd been told the prisoner was dangerous but couldn't quite see it. Sera had spent two days mapping the holdfast from her window: the wall heights, the guard rotations at the gate, the tower where they'd taken Ada after the stormhawk spoke in his voice, the courtyard where the wolves — there had been wolves, she was nearly sure, though the snow kept erasing the evidence — had come and gone.

She was a ward-smith. She had spent eleven years learning how things were held together and nine more learning, for the Gilded Vane, how they came apart. She could not stop reading rooms as structures. She could not stop seeing where the load-bearing walls were.

The load-bearing wall of this place was the council. And the council was about to be tested.

She heard it first as a change in the guard. Her young crossbowwoman was replaced at dawn — or what passed for dawn — by two men who stood differently, weight forward, hands free. Veterans. Then the bells rang the assembly pattern, three-and-two, three-and-two, the old war cadence that every child in the north learned before they learned their letters. And then the corridor filled with boots, and a voice she knew better than her own heartbeat said, pleasantly, through her door:

"Mistress Voss. Master Corvane requests the pleasure of your company. He thought you'd want to hear this in person."

* * *

They brought her to the great hall of the holdfast, and the first thing Sera noticed was that they had not bound her hands.

The second thing she noticed was Corvane, standing at the foot of the council dais like a man admiring architecture. He'd dressed for it — ash-grey wool, no guild colors, no sigil, the costume of a neutral party, an honest broker. His hands were folded. His face wore the expression she had watched him use on frightened suppliers for a decade: patient, reasonable, faintly regretful, as if the world's troubles pained him personally and he had simply come to help.

The council sat above him in a crescent of old chairs — eleven seats, seven filled. Sera knew some of the faces from the guild's files. Lord Aldric Vane-Askel, who was not, whatever the name suggested, kin to the Gilded Vane, but whose grandfather had helped build the original border wards and whose pride was therefore a structural element. Magistra Ondrey of the Saltmarsh, who had signed the guild's trade licenses and would be doing arithmetic behind her eyes. The brothers Kell, who held the eastern passes and had been quietly selling the guild timber and silence for twenty years. And in the center, the Holdfast's Voice, a thin old woman named Ilsa Renn with a voice like a bell struck in an empty room, who had said almost nothing in the files because she almost never said anything at all.

Ada stood to one side, between two guards, and Sera's chest did something complicated when she saw him. He looked smaller than she remembered. Not beaten — *held*, the way you'd hold something you hadn't decided whether to keep. His eyes found hers across the hall, and there was a question in them she couldn't answer yet.

Corvane turned, saw her, and smiled as if she were an expected guest.

"Mistress Voss. Thank you for joining us." To the council: "You'll forgive the theater. I wanted her here because she is the last honest witness to what the Gilded Vane has actually been doing — and because I believe in transparency. It's the foundation of any durable arrangement."

"Arrangement," said Lord Aldric. His voice was gravel and old anger. "You come to the holdfast of the Unforgiven and open with *arrangement*."

"I come with a proposal that will keep your people alive through a winter that will otherwise kill half of them by thaw." Corvane let that sit. Sera watched it land, watched the arithmetic begin behind Magistra Ondrey's eyes, and understood with a cold, professional clarity that she was watching a master at work. Not a villain. Villains were easy. This was worse: a man with a genuinely good offer, if you didn't look underneath it.

"Your wards are dead," Corvane continued. "The border line failed on the night the stars went out — you all felt it, you all watched your outriders' signal-fires go unanswered, your wardsmiths' stones go cold." His gaze flicked to Sera, brief and almost kind. "The Gilded Vane holds fragments of the old ward-lattice. Genuine fragments. Living material, harvested carefully over many years — and I will not insult this council by pretending that harvesting was gentle. Mistress Voss can speak to that better than I."

Every face turned to her. Ada's too. And Sera felt the trap close around her with the soft, oiled precision of everything Corvane built. He was offering her the chance to confess — truthfully, damningly — in service of his lie.

She could have lied. She could have said the fragments were dead material, carved stone, inert. But she had carried those cages. She had felt them hum against her palms at night, had heard — she'd told herself she hadn't heard, but she had — a sound like grieving from inside the lattice-work. She had been paid not to ask what they were, and she had taken the pay, and she was done taking it.

"They're alive," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "The fragments. They're pieces of the old ward-spirits. The guardians that held the border for three hundred years. The guild caged them, cut them, and sold them." She looked at Corvane. "And they grieve. If any of you have ever wondered what a cut-open god sounds like, I can describe it. It sounds like a child humming to itself in the dark so it won't be afraid."

Silence. Somewhere above, snow slid off the roof with a long hiss.

"Thank you, Mistress Voss," Corvane said, and there it was — not a flicker of anger, not a muscle out of place. "You see? Honest. Now. My proposal."

He laid it out the way he laid out everything: in clean numbered parts, each one reasonable, each one small.

First: the Gilded Vane would return the surviving fragments to the holdfast, freely given. Partial wards, restored within a season — not the full border line, but a shield over the holdfast itself, the villages of the valley, the pass. Enough.

Second: the guild would open its granaries and credit-ledgers to the north. Grain, iron, medicine, at cost. He named figures. Sera watched Magistra Ondrey's hands go still on the arm of her chair, because the figures were *good*. They were better than good. They were the numbers of a man who had studied the holdfast's stores and knew to the barrel how thin the winter was going to be.

Third — and Corvane's voice softened here, took on the gentle cadence of a man delivering hard news to family — the restoration of the fragments required fuel. Ward-spirits were not machines. They fed, as all living things fed. The old lattice had drawn its strength from the land itself, slowly, over centuries, and there was no time for centuries now.

"The Ember beneath the mountains," Corvane said, "is a reservoir. The old texts are clear — the first ward-smiths bound it as a *storehouse*, not a weapon. It holds the heat and life of the deep world. The guild has developed a means of drawing on that reservoir in controlled measure. The fragments drink, the wards rise, the north survives." He spread his hands. "The cost is souls. Not yours," he added quickly, and Sera saw Lord Aldric's jaw tighten at the word *quickly*, because Corvane had rehearsed that too. "Criminals. Debtors. The guild's own bond-laborers, who have signed agreements — voluntary agreements — and whose names I will provide to this council for review. A few hundred lives, freely contracted, weighed against tens of thousands. I don't like it. I have not slept well since the method was proven. But I like a dead north less, and I suspect neither do you."

He stopped. He had said his piece. And now he did the thing Sera had seen him do a hundred times in the guild's negotiation rooms: he said nothing. He let the silence do the selling. He let each lord sit alone with the image Corvane had built for them — warm halls, full granaries, children alive at thaw — and with the small print at the bottom, which was only a few hundred strangers, and strangers were always the cheapest thing in any ledger.

Sera had watched lords and merchants and once a king's factor make this exact calculation. She had never watched it from inside a cage with the window left open.

* * *

They argued for two hours.

Sera stood at the wall and listened, and catalogued, because cataloguing was what her hands did when her mind was screaming.

Lord Aldric refused on principle, and his principle was real, but principles were the first thing Corvane had priced. "You would feed the Ember," Aldric said, "the thing my grandfather's generation died chaining. You would hand it mouths. You would teach it to expect them." Corvane answered gently that the Ember had no expectations, that it was a force and not a mind — and Sera's stomach turned, because she had stood in the harvesting vaults, and she knew that was a lie. The fragments had *minds*. Small ones, wounded ones, but minds. And whatever the Ember was, it had been dreaming at the edge of her awareness for a year, the way a fire dreams at the edge of a sleeping room.

The Kell brothers wavered, and their wavering was the sound of a village in the eastern passes that had already lost eleven people to the cold this winter. "You ask us to refuse bread on principle," the elder Kell said, not to Corvane but to Aldric, "with my granddaughter's name on the dead-list." And Corvane, without moving, without a word, let the room feel that name.

Magistra Ondrey asked the questions Sera would have asked — where were the reservoir mechanisms, who controlled the flow, what stopped the drawing from growing. Corvane answered each one smoothly, and each answer was technically true and structurally hollow, and Sera realized with a lurch that Ondrey knew it too, that the old woman was asking the questions not to be answered but to be *seen* asking them, building her own record, keeping her own hands clean for whichever way the wind went.

And the Voice, Ilsa Renn, said nothing at all. She watched Corvane the way Sera had once watched a master smith test a weld — not listening to the words, but looking for the flaw, the seam, the place where the metal would tell the truth under stress.

Twice, Corvane glanced at Sera during the argument. Not to gloat. To *check*. The way a man checks a load-bearing wall.

Ada never looked at her at all. He stood with his guards and watched Corvane with an expression she was beginning to understand was not fear. It was the look of a man doing sums of a different kind — a man who had spent his life balancing ledgers and could see, written in the room's silence, exactly what the deal was going to cost and who had already been entered in the column marked *paid*.

It was the brothers Kell who broke first. Not dramatically. The elder simply said, "I'll not vote to starve the passes," and that was the crack, and Corvane did not smile, because smiling would have been amateur, but Sera saw the stillness of him change — the stillness of a man who has heard the ice groan and knows which way it will go.

Then Ilsa Renn spoke for the first time.

"Master Corvane." Her voice was exactly what the files promised: a bell in an empty room. "You have described a mechanism that draws life from the bound Ember and pours it into the ward-fragments. Describe the control."

"The flow is governed by a regulator lattice, constructed to the old specifications—"

"Whose specifications."

"The guild's ward-smiths, working from recovered texts—"

"Yours," said the Voice. "Your specifications. Your regulator. Your hands on the flow. And when the few hundred contracted souls are spent — and they will be spent, Master Corvane, I buried enough men to know how a quota behaves in winter — the wards will dim. And you will come back to this hall, in this same grey coat, with the same kind eyes, and you will explain that the reservoir requires a larger draw. And the arithmetic will be the same. A few thousand strangers against the living. And then a few tens of thousands. And every year the price of the north will be paid by whoever is cheapest to enter in the ledger." She folded her hands. "I have read your guild's ledgers, Master Corvane. A clerk of mine was once a clerk of yours. You are not offering to save the north. You are offering to *buy* it. And I have not yet heard what you intend to own at the end."

For the first time since Sera had known him — eleven years, through harvests and audits and one terrible night in the vaults — Corvane's stillness cracked.

It was small. A breath taken a half-second early. A hand that adjusted the fall of his sleeve when it did not need adjusting. But Sera had spent her career reading structures under load, and she saw the seam, and she saw that Ilsa Renn saw it too.

"You mistake the nature of the offer," Corvane said, and his voice was still pleasant, but the pleasantness now had edges. "The offer is not conditional on your approval. It is conditional on the return of certain guild property." His eyes moved — past the lords, past Sera, and settled, at last and deliberately, on Ada. "The stolen ledger page. And the clerk who stole it. Surrender them, and the fragments, the grain, and the wards arrive within the month. Refuse, and the Gilded Vane will conclude that the north has chosen the Ember over its own people — and we will negotiate with those who haven't."

"There it is," Lord Aldric said quietly. "The price named at last."

"The price was always named," Corvane said. "I simply let you hear it in your own time."

* * *

They took Sera and Ada back down the corridor together, which was a mistake, or a mercy, or a test — with Corvane it was always all three.

In the grey light of the stairwell, walking between guards, Ada spoke without turning his head. His voice was low, pitched for her alone.

"Some of them will vote yes."

"I know."

"Kell's already gone. Ondrey will follow if the granary numbers hold. That's enough for a majority if Renn abstains."

"I know." Sera watched the back of the guard's neck in front of her and kept her own voice equally flat. "He built it to pass. The whole performance — the fragments, the grain, the confession he let me give — it was all load-bearing. He wanted us to watch it stand up."

"He wanted *me* to watch it." A breath. "Sera. If they hand me over—"

"They're not going to hand you over."

"You don't know that."

She did, actually. She had watched Ilsa Renn's face when Corvane's stillness cracked, and she had watched the elder Kell look at his own hands after he spoke, the way a man looks at hands that have just signed something. But the holdfast was not one woman, and hunger was not a feeling, it was a *schedule*, and Corvane had brought the schedule with him in the form of figures no honest accountant could argue with.

"They'll vote within two days," she said instead. "Before the weather closes the pass. Which means we have two days."

"*We*," Ada repeated.

Sera looked at him then, really looked — this clerk with a stolen page and a dying gift and a wolf somewhere out in the snow that she had seen only once, at a distance, in the moment before everything went wrong. She had burned her own report for him. She had given him her sigil and stayed behind to be taken. She had told herself at every step that she was making the professional choice, the evidence-based choice, and every step had been a lie with good posture.

"I've been thinking about the regulator," she said. "The one he described to Renn. The 'old specifications.'" She let the quotation marks live in her voice. "There are no old specifications. I built the flow-governor for the harvesting vaults myself, four years ago, from Corvane's own drawings. Which means the mechanism he's offering this council doesn't exist yet. It has to be built. Here. By ward-smiths." She felt her mouth do something that was almost a smile and had too many teeth in it. "He needs a ward-smith, Ada. And the only one in this valley he trusts is currently his prisoner."

Ada stopped walking. The guards stopped with him, and one of them started to say something, and then the corridor's torchlight guttered — a draft, from somewhere below, from the deep places under the holdfast where the old wards had once anchored — and in the guttering light Sera saw Ada's face change as he listened to something she couldn't hear.

"What," she said. "What is it. What are you hearing."

"Not hearing," Ada said slowly. "Feeling. The same thing I felt the night the stars went out." His eyes came up to hers, and they were not afraid anymore; they were certain, which was worse. "The Ember moved. Just now. When Corvane named the price. It heard him."

Above them, muffled by stone and snow, the holdfast's bells began to ring — not the assembly pattern, not the war cadence. Something older, three-and-one, three-and-one, a signal Sera had only ever read about in the guild's forbidden files.

The summons to the deep vaults. The sound of the holdfast opening the one door its founders had sworn to keep shut.

The council, it seemed, had not waited two days after all.

"* * *" — no. Sera corrected herself, watching the guards' faces go pale, watching Ada turn toward the sound like a man turning toward a voice calling his name. The vote was happening now. And Corvane had one more card she hadn't seen, and he was already playing it, somewhere below, in the dark, with the door open.

"Move," she told the guards, and was surprised to find they did.
