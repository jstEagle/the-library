## 15. The Price Named Aloud

They gave me a night to prepare my testimony, which is to say they locked me in a room with a brazier and no windows and let me decide how much of myself I was willing to burn.

I didn't sleep. I sat on the edge of a cot that smelled of old wool and older smoke and rehearsed the truth the way you rehearse a lie — over and over, smoothing the edges, testing where it would crack. Somewhere below me the holdfast's council chamber waited with its long table and its thirteen chairs and its lords who had spent three days telling me that what I carried proved nothing, that a single ledger page could be forged by anyone with a grudge and a steady hand.

The wolf lay across the doorway like a fallen door itself. He had not left my side since we came down out of the pass, and he was thinner now, his grey gone the color of ash, his breathing a sound like wind through a ruined house. When I shifted on the cot, one amber eye opened.

*Say it once,* he said, in that voice that was not a voice, more memory than sound. *To me. It will be easier the second time.*

"Nothing about this will be easy," I whispered.

*No,* he agreed. *But it will be shorter.*

So I said it. All of it, there in the dark, to an audience of one dying wolf: *My name is Ada Delacroix. I am a beast-speaker. I have been one since I was nine years old, when a cart-horse told me my mother was crying in the kitchen, and I have hidden it every day since, because the Ember War ended with a law written in blood that says people like me are the reason the wards failed, and because hiding is easier than hanging.* I told him about the ledgers, the Gilded Vane, the columns of impossible things balanced neat as prayer. I told him about Corvane's offer, and the ward-spirit in its cage of cold iron, and the lie I told Corvane with a straight face while the mountain's hunger still rang in my skull.

The wolf listened without blinking. When I finished, he rested his great scarred head on his paws.

*Your grandmother refused the crown,* he said. *She was asked to do what you may be asked to do, and she chose her daughter instead. No one has ever condemned her for it. Least of all me.*

"She never knew you remembered that."

*I remember everything,* he said simply. *That is what I am for. Go to sleep, heir of Delacroix. Tomorrow you will need your voice more than your sleep.*

I did not go to sleep. But I stopped rehearsing, and that was nearly the same thing.

* * *

The council chamber of Holdfast Veyra had been carved into the mountain's shoulder before the Ember War, back when the northern alliance still believed stone could outlast fire. Thirteen seats ringed a floor of black granite inlaid with the old ward-lines, silver channels worn dull by centuries of boots. Only nine seats were filled now. The alliance everyone would rather forget had forgotten most of itself.

Lord-Commander Ede Veyra sat at the head, a woman past seventy with a face like a cliff face and her house's sigil — a crown above crossed keys — worked into her collar in tarnished thread. To her right, Lord Aldous Crane of the Rivermarks, whose estates bordered the Gilded Vane's southern trade roads and whose expression suggested I was something he'd scraped off his boot. To her left, the Lady Maren of the Ashfall Reach, youngest of them, who had watched me with sharp, hungry attention since the stormhawk spoke through my throat two days ago.

Sera stood against the far wall among the holdfast guards, arms crossed, ward-smith's sigil hidden under her coat. She had not looked at me since she gave me the sigil at the border. She looked at me now.

Corvane was not present. He had arrived at the holdfast two days behind me, escorted, unafraid, carrying letters of introduction from three southern houses and a proposal sealed in gold wax. He was lodged in the guest wing as an honored envoy of the Gilded Vane. That fact alone should have been enough to end him. It was not, because half the council wanted what he was selling, and the other half wanted proof bad enough to overlook whose hands delivered it.

Ede Veyra did not ask me to sit. She nodded once, and the chamber went quiet enough that I could hear the brazier hissing.

"You claim," she said, "that the Gilded Vane has spent thirty years dismantling the border wards and selling the pieces south. You claim their buyer intends to wake the Ember beneath the Greyhollow Mountains. And you claim" — here her voice flattened, deliberately, the way a blade flattens against a whetstone — "that you can speak with beasts, which has been a hanging crime in every province south of the pass since the armistice. Three claims, clerk. Two of them are treason to repeat. One of them is treason to be."

"I know," I said.

"Then begin."

I had planned to begin with the ledger page. I had planned a clerk's argument, orderly and damning: dates, sums, entries in another hand, the note naming the Ember. I had it arranged in my head like a column that would sum correctly if only they read it carefully.

Instead I heard myself say: "Before I show you anything, you should know what I am. All of it. Because if you take the evidence from a man you believe, you'll act. If you take it from a man you doubt, you'll hesitate. And hesitation is what Corvane is counting on."

Crane snorted. "A speech. Wonderful."

"My lord," I said, "in four days your eastern beacon-fires will either be lit or they won't. I'd rather spend the time honestly than efficiently."

That bought me silence. I took a breath, and let the secret out into a room full of strangers for the first time in twenty years.

* * *

"My name is Ada Delacroix," I said. "I was born in Bellharbor, in the Tidemark provinces, twenty-nine years ago. My mother was a net-mender. My father died in the last year of the Ember War, in the retreat from Cael Sarn, though I never learned which fire killed him — ours or theirs.

"When I was nine years old, our cart-horse told me my mother was crying."

I said it plainly, and watched the word *told* land in the chamber like a dropped glass. Lady Maren leaned forward. Crane's mouth thinned.

"Not in words. Horses don't use words. It was — knowing, with a shape to it. Grief, gray and salt-tasting, coming off my mother through the kitchen wall, and the horse standing beside me in the yard turning its head and showing me where it came from. I thought everyone could do it. I was nine. I mentioned it at supper." I made myself smile, because the smile was part of the truth too. "My mother went white as flour. She took me outside and explained, very quietly, what happened to speakers after the war. What the Reconciliation Edicts said. What the mobs had done in the years before anyone wrote laws about it. Then she taught me to hide it. She was better at teaching hiding than I ever was at learning it, but I learned. I learned so well that some days I convinced myself the gift was dead. Some days I wanted it dead. A gift you can't use is just a wound that talks."

"The Edicts exist," Crane said, "because beast-speakers were used to turn the ward-beasts during the war. Because the Ember's servants spoke through animals in a hundred villages and no one knew whom to trust—"

"Yes," I said. "I've read the histories. I've also read the ledgers, my lord, and I'll come to those. But you should understand first that everything I'm about to accuse the Gilded Vane of, I helped keep the books clean for. Seven years. I balanced accounts for a guild trading in impossible things, and I was good at it, and being good at it felt like safety, and I mistook that feeling for innocence."

Ede Veyra's eyes narrowed slightly. "Go on."

"The Gilded Vane trades in what can't be priced elsewhere. Storms bottled in glass. Memories in vials. The last song of a dead river. That's the public face, and it's charming, and I kept its ledgers beautifully. But there are entries that don't appear in the fair books. Entries in another hand — my predecessor's, a man named Ossen Vail, who held my desk for thirty years before me. Ward-fragments. Sold in lots. Sold steadily, decade after decade, always south, always to buyers recorded only by cipher. I found the pages six weeks ago, the night the stars went out." I reached into my coat and drew out the ledger page — Ossen Vail's cramped hand, the columns, and at the bottom, in ink gone brown: *the buyer asks after the Ember beneath the mountains; price negotiable in souls or sovereigns.*

I laid it on the black granite, on the worn silver line of the old ward-channel, and stepped back.

"That page is why I ran. Not the guild's crimes — I could have lived with reporting those, maybe, if survival hadn't gotten complicated. I ran because of what Corvane offered me when he caught me reading. He offered partnership. Protection. And a particular kind of work." My voice wanted to shake. I let it want, and kept talking anyway. "He wanted a speaker. The guild has been caging ward-spirits — the living fragments of the old wards, the pieces they've been prying loose and selling. Spirits don't testify in courts. They don't sign confessions. But a beast-speaker can hear them, and Corvane wanted me to interrogate them. Find out what the fragments remember. Where the weak points in the remaining wards are. How much the Ember knows. How much it wants."

Lady Maren had gone very still. "And did you?"

"One spirit. Once." The brazier hissed. I made myself meet her eyes. "It was small — a fragment from a boundary-stone, barely conscious, grieving the way a child grieves, without understanding what it lost. And underneath its grief I felt the mountain. The Ember. Half-asleep, and hungry, and *turning toward us* the way a sleeper turns toward a lit window. I told Corvane the spirit said nothing useful. That was the first lie I ever told my guild, and I have never been prouder of anything, and I have never been more ashamed, and both of those things are true, and I am tired of pretending true things cancel each other out."

Silence. Even Crane had stopped performing boredom.

"So yes," I said. "I am a criminal by your Edicts. I hid a forbidden gift for twenty years. I served the guild that armed the men who broke your border. I lied to a warlord's agent and called it virtue. I am not here to be believed because I'm clean. I'm here because I'm the only witness you have who's seen the whole ledger — the guild's, and mine — and I am offering you both of them."

I put my hand flat on the granite beside the page.

"The evidence is yours. Take it, copy it, burn it if that's what it takes to make you move — I'd rather the proof die than the warning. And I am yours too. If the law says speakers hang, then hang me after the beacons are lit. I'll kneel for it gladly, if kneeling buys this council one honest week. But don't hand me to Corvane to buy his 'restored' wards, because I've heard what feeds them now, and it isn't sovereigns."

* * *

For a moment nobody moved. Then Crane laughed — short, ugly, relieved, the laugh of a man handed exactly the weapon he'd hoped for.

"Well. There it is. A confessed felon asking a lawful council to weigh his testimony." He spread his hands to the room. "Gentlemen, ladies — the Edicts are clear. This creature admits to the precise crime the alliance swore never to tolerate again. We are asked to trust the fate of the north to a man who tells us himself he cannot be trusted. I say we remand him to custody, send for the southern magistrates, and consider the Gilded Vane's proposal on its merits — which, whatever else, does not require us to break our own laws within our own walls."

"On its merits," Maren repeated softly. "You mean the part where the wards are restored by feeding the Ember souls."

"I mean the part where the wards are *restored*, my lady, which is more than this clerk's scrap of paper offers—"

"The scrap of paper," Ede Veyra said, without raising her voice, and the room shut up, "is thirty years of transactions in a verified guild hand, corroborated independently by a ward-smith of this holdfast." Her eyes flicked to the wall, to Sera, who lifted her chin and did not flinch. "Which Lord Crane has known for two days and chosen to dismiss because the alternative requires courage. Sit down, Aldous."

Crane sat. The chamber breathed.

But Veyra wasn't finished with me. She rose, came around the table, and stood over the ledger page, studying Ossen Vail's brown ink for a long moment. When she looked up, her cliff-face expression had not changed, but something behind it had.

"You said your grandmother was asked to do what you may be asked to do."

The wolf, lying outside the doors, stirred — I felt it through the stone, faint as a heartbeat in the floor. "Yes, my lady."

"Journeyman Delacroix—" and something turned over in my chest at hearing the title, hearing myself named aloud in a place that might soon be a battlefield — "the crown of living memory is not a story we tell children. It is a mechanism. The founders built it into the heart of the old ward-line, and it has exactly one function: it takes everything a mind is and burns it as fuel. Every memory. Every name. The person who wears it does not survive as a person. The last time it was offered, the woman offered it refused, and the wards fell within a generation, and I have spent my whole life wondering if she was a coward." She looked at me steadily. "Now I find her grandson in my hall, confessing to crimes, offering his neck, and I wonder if refusal runs in families — or if courage does."

"I don't know what I'd do," I said honestly. "Ask me again if it comes to that."

Something almost like approval moved through the lines of her face. "Honesty. From a bookkeeper. The world grows strange." She turned back to the council. "We will deliberate—"

The horn cut her off.

One blast, then two — the eastern watch, high and ragged with urgency, and beneath it, rising through the mountain's bones, a sound I knew from a cage of cold iron: the ward-spirits of the holdfast's own boundary stones beginning, all along the line, to scream.

Sera was moving before the echo died. So was I. And through the granite under my feet, faint and vast and waking, I felt the mountain turn toward the light of our little window, and open one burning eye.

* * *

In the corridor, chaos already blooming, Sera caught my arm. Her face was pale and set.

"It's early," she said. "Corvane's timetable was weeks out. Which means he wasn't waiting for the council's answer at all. The offer was never meant to be accepted." Her grip tightened. "It was meant to keep you talking while his army got into position. Ada — the assault isn't coming. It's *here*."
