## 16. March of the Ember-Bound

* * *

**SERA**

They came out of the ash-dark like a procession of mourners who had forgotten what they were mourning.

Sera saw them first from the narrow window of her cell — a slit of glass too high to reach, angled at the western approach, and she had spent four days learning its every inch because there was nothing else in the room to learn. A pallet. A bucket. A door with no bar on the inside. And beyond the glass: snow, black at the edges where the ash fell into it, and now, moving through that snow, the vanguard of Corvane's army.

She counted by habit. It was the first thing they taught you in the guild — count everything, trust nothing you haven't counted twice. Two hundred spears, maybe more, marching in loose columns that didn't bother with formation because formation was for soldiers who feared their enemy. Behind them, wagons. Behind the wagons—

Her breath fogged the glass.

Behind the wagons walked the ward-spirits.

She had guarded them for three years without knowing what she was guarding. She had checked their cages, oiled their locks, logged their hum as "structural resonance" in reports no one read. She had stood watch over them through two winters and never once looked at them properly, the way you can stand beside a person every day and never see them until the day you do.

Now she saw them. Even at this distance, even through ash-thick air, she saw them: tall shapes like heat-shimmer given posture, like the memory of light wearing bodies it didn't want. Chains of grey iron ran from their wrists back to the wagons, and each wagon carried a cage, and each cage held a fragment — one of the pieces of the old border wards her guild had pried loose and sold, piece by piece, decade by decade, until the wards were lace and the lace tore.

The spirits were being marched ahead of the army. Not behind it. Ahead.

"They're using them as shields," Sera said aloud, to no one, because saying it made it worse and she wanted it to be as bad as possible. If it was going to live in her, it should arrive at full weight.

Because ward-spirits could not be harmed by ward-craft. Every holdfast defense, every sigil-warded gate, every rune-laced arrowhead — useless against the things the defenders were sworn to protect. The archers on the wall would have to choose, every single shot, between killing the enemy and killing the last living pieces of the world's oldest promise.

Corvane hadn't built an army. He'd built a moral instrument.

Sera sat down on the pallet and put her head in her hands and did the thing she had been refusing to do for four days, which was admit that she had helped build this. Not the chains, not the march, not the warlord in his ash-grey hood. But the ledgers. The routes. The escort contracts in her own neat hand. She had carried these fragments across half the north and called it logistics.

A key turned in the lock.

She was on her feet before the door opened, weight forward, hands loose — old training, older than her conscience. The door swung and revealed not a jailer but a boy, fifteen maybe, holding a ring of keys in a fist that shook so hard the metal sang.

"You're Voss," he said. "The ward-smith."

"I'm nobody. Who sent you?"

"Nobody." He swallowed. "I heard the guards talking. They said when the fighting starts, the lady in this cell gets a knife in the dark. Quiet-like. Because Master Corvane doesn't want witnesses walking around." His eyes were wet and furious. "My sister works the kitchens. She carried your meals. She says you always said thank you. She says people who say thank you—" He stopped, disgusted with himself, or with the thinness of the logic. He thrust the keys at her. "There's an armory stair behind the cistern. East side. The fragment-cages are stored down there — the ones they brought for the assault. Somebody has to—"

"Somebody has to what?" Sera took the keys. They were warm from his hand.

"I don't know," the boy said. "But you're the only one here who would."

He ran. She listened to his footsteps fade up the stair, and she thought about the word *would*, about how it had become the whole of ethics in a winter like this one — not *may*, not *must*, just the bare question of what a person would do when the counting stopped.

Then she went down toward the cistern, and the cold came up to meet her like water.

* * *

**ADA**

The horns began at the hour when even hope sleeps — that dead interval past midnight when the watch changes and the fires burn lowest.

Ada was awake anyway. He had been awake most nights since the council, since he stood in front of lords whose grandfathers had burned his grandmother's kind and told them the truth with his voice shaking. They hadn't believed him. The stormhawk had believed him enough for all of them, testifying in Ada's own stolen voice until the hall went silent — but silence is not belief, and belief is not action, and the action they had finally taken was to double the watch and argue about supply lines while the long winter ground on outside the walls.

So he was awake, in the chamber they'd given him near the armory, with the ledger page spread open under a shuttered lamp — the page in his predecessor's hand, the one naming the Ember beneath the mountains, the one that had cost him everything — when the first horn sounded, ragged and wrong, and then a second horn answered it, and then all the horns at once.

He was in the corridor before he finished lacing his boots.

The holdfast woke around him the way a body wakes to pain — not gradually, but everywhere at once. Doors banging. Children crying and being hushed. Old Lord Askelund's voice booming from the central stair: *"To the walls! Ward-bearers to the gates! Everyone else below, below, below!"* And under it all, threading through the stone itself, a sound Ada felt in his teeth before he understood it with his ears:

Screaming. Thin and vast at once. The sound of the dead wards feeling the dead wards coming.

"The spirits," he said, catching Askelund's arm as the old lord thundered past. "He's driving the ward-spirits ahead of his line. Your defenses—"

"I know what my defenses are worth against them, clerk." Askelund's face was grey as the snow. Sixty years ago he had fought in the last battles of the Ember War as a boy; Ada could see the boy still in him, surfacing now like a drowned thing. "We let them pass through the gates or we cut down our own ancestors' work. Either way we lose something that doesn't grow back." His jaw worked. "Your wolf. Where is your wolf?"

That was how they spoke of the wolf now — *your wolf* — though Ada had never owned anything in his life except debts. "Resting. She barely—"

"Get her to the gatehouse. Whatever she is, whatever she remembers — get her there." Askelund was already moving. "And Delacroix." He paused on the stair, firelight carving his face into something ancient. "You wanted us to fight. This is what fighting looks like. I hope it looks like what you imagined."

It didn't. Nothing ever did.

Ada ran the other way, down, toward the kennels and the small warm room where the wolf lay curled. The corridors strobed with running lanterns. Somewhere a smith was already hammering, absurdly, urgently, as if iron could answer this. And beneath everything, growing louder as he descended, the screaming of the bound spirits — and threaded through it, fainter, a second sound that stopped him mid-stride with his hand flat against the frozen stone.

Humming. The fragments in the wagons, humming. The same note he'd felt through the guild's storeroom floor for years and never once questioned.

They weren't screaming because they were afraid, he realized. They were screaming because they were being dragged home in chains — home to the ward-line they had been torn from — close enough now to feel the broken places where their own missing pieces should be.

The wolf lifted her head when he opened the door. Her muzzle had gone almost white since the pass. When she rose, it took her three tries, and she leaned into him afterward, ashamed of it, and he put his arms around her neck and felt the enormous engine of her heart working hard at simply continuing.

*They are bringing our bones to our grave,* she said. Not words — never words, only meaning arriving whole, like weather. *And they think the grave will open for them.*

"What do we do?"

*What your grandmother refused,* she said, and said nothing else, and he hated her a little for it, and followed her toward the gatehouse through the burning corridors of a holdfast waking to its last night.

* * *

**SERA**

The cistern chamber smelled of ice and rust, and it was full of cages.

Twelve of them, guild-make, ash-grey iron with the Vane's sigil stamped on every lock — her guild, her sigil, stamped on the instruments of this. Each cage held a fragment of the old wards, and the fragments knew she was there. She felt it the moment she crossed the threshold: the hum rising, sharpening, turning toward her the way sunflowers turn toward a lie of light. Three years of guarding them and she had never once stood in a room where they could all see her at once.

"I know," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "I know what I did. I'm going to fix it or die stupid trying. Both of those are on offer tonight."

She had grown up in a ward-smith's shop. Her mother had forged sigil-anchors for farmsteads, little honest charms against frost-wyrms and hedge-hags, and the first law of the craft had been sung into her before she could read: *a ward is a promise made of attention.* You did not forge protection. You forged the shape that caring took so it could outlast the carer. Everything else — the guild's ledgers, the escort contracts, the neat columns of her own handwriting — had been a way of forgetting the song.

The cages used inverted anchors. Of course they did. She should have seen it years ago: take a promise-shaped thing, turn it inside out, and it holds not attention but its absence. It doesn't imprison a spirit. It convinces the spirit that no one is attending, that it is alone, that struggling is pointless because no promise was ever made to it at all.

Loneliness, weaponized. The guild had never needed chains. It had needed only to make the oldest beings in the world feel forgotten.

Sera knelt at the first cage and drew her knife and began, with hands that shook and then steadied, to cut the inverted anchor out of its setting.

The fragment screamed — not in pain, in *recognition*. The hum broke apart into something like a chord, something like a name being remembered, and the cage's grey iron flushed dull red and then, along the seams, a thin defiant gold.

"Oh," Sera whispered. "Oh, you're not things at all. You're *listeners.*"

Above her, muffled by forty feet of stone, the horns of the assault reached the walls. The battle had begun without her. She cut faster.

* * *

**ADA**

From the gatehouse parapet, Ada watched the Ember-Bound come, and understood that he had never once, in all his ledgers and audits and careful balanced columns, actually comprehended what a number was.

Two hundred spears had been a number. This was not a number. This was a tide of men in ash-grey, breath steaming, shields rimed with frost, advancing at a walk because they did not need to hurry — and before them, drifting like banners of heat-haze, twenty-three ward-spirits in chains of grey iron, and the arrows of the defenders loosing above them and *stopping*, mid-flight, some of them, turning aside as if embarrassed, because you cannot kill a ward-spirit with ward-work and half the holdfast's arsenal was ward-work, blessed, sigiled, singing.

The arrows that flew true found flesh. Men fell. The line did not break. And the spirits passed over the fallen, and where they passed, Ada saw — he pressed his knuckles to his mouth — he saw the dead men's faces go calm. Forgiven, almost. Touched by the things they were marching behind and eased, briefly, of something.

"They won't reach the gates," Askelund said beside him. His voice was steady the way a rope bridge is steady. "The ballistae have plain iron. We'll make them pay at the ditch."

"And the spirits?"

Askelund said nothing, which was the whole answer.

Below, at the ditch, the first crash came — plain-iron bolts, screaming men, the ugly ordinary arithmetic of a siege. And the ward-spirits drifted on through it untouched, grieving, chained, closer, closer, until they hung above the ditch itself like a curtain of dying light, and every defender on the wall could see their faces now, if spirits had faces, could see the shape of what the guild had sold.

Ada's hands were shaking. Beside him the wolf stood at the parapet, gaunt and silent, watching her kin dragged toward the wound in the world they had died defending.

*Soon,* she said. *When they reach the gate, the chains will be grounded to the ward-stones. That is the design. That is how he means to wake it — press the fragments home into the broken line, feed them the souls of the garrison, and the Ember will rise to eat.*

"How do we stop it?"

*We don't,* she said. *Someone unbinds them. From inside the chain-line. While two hundred men are trying to kill everyone in reach.* Her yellow eyes turned to him, and there was apology in them, and farewell, and he was too slow understanding either. *You asked me once why I chose you, clerk. I chose you because you count. Now count what remains, and spend it well.*

The gate shuddered. The battle came down to the wood and iron of the doors, and the world became noise.

* * *

**SERA**

The twelfth anchor came free as the gate below her split along its braces, and Sera Voss stood in a room full of gold-seamed cages and understood that she had perhaps one minute of usefulness left in her life, and intended to use all of it.

She couldn't free them all. Twelve anchors cut, twelve fragments awake, but the cages themselves still held — guild-forged, warded against exactly the people who would try this, which was to say against her. But the wagons outside, the field-cages in the assault train, those were transport-grade. Those were hers. She had signed off on their specifications three winters ago, complaining about the cost of proper locks, and the quartermaster had cut corners, and she had let it go because the cargo was, after all, only freight.

Freight. She laughed, or sobbed, and threw the great lever that opened the cistern doors to the lower yard.

The fragments poured past her like a river remembering downhill. They did not run — they *flowed*, gold-threaded, shrieking joy and grief in the same breath, out into the ash-fall, through the shattered postern, into the churned snow of the battlefield where two hundred ash-grey soldiers were hacking at the holdfast gates.

Sera ran after them because someone had to aim them, and she was the only person alive who knew the release-sigils for the field-cages, and because — this was the true reason, the one she'd admit to no one, maybe not even herself — because she had escorted those wagons. She had walked beside those cages for three years humming her mother's song without hearing it, and if this was the debt coming due, then she would pay it standing in front of the thing she owed it to.

The battlefield received her like a mouth.

Snow, ash, torchlight, screaming. A soldier saw her — guild-grey coat, no weapon but a smith's knife — and hesitated, one heartbeat of confusion, and she was past him. The wagon-train sat fifty yards out, horses long fled, cages lashed and stacked. She climbed the lead wagon with bolts striking sparks off the rails around her, found the master-lock, and set her knife and both bleeding hands against the release-sigil she herself had designed.

"Attention," she said — to the cages, to the fragments, to the whole rotten architecture of forgetting. "I am attending. I am *attending.*"

The lock opened. Then the next. Then all of them, cascading, the corner-cut locks failing in sequence like a lie collapsing, and the field-fragments rose out of their cages into the smoke and turned, all together, toward the chained ward-spirits at the gate.

Toward their own missing pieces.

What happened then, no survivor ever managed to describe cleanly. The fragments and the spirits reached for each other across the ditch and the dead, and where they touched, the grey iron chains did not break — they *unmade*, quietly, like frost leaving a window. Twenty-three enslaved ward-spirits felt themselves become whole again, and the wholeness went through the battlefield like the first day of spring going through a long winter, and the ash-grey army — hardened men, paid men, men who had marched behind gods in chains without flinching — broke.

Not all at once. But breaking, everywhere, the way ice breaks: unpredictably, totally, all at once in the end.

Sera stood on the wagon with her hands burned and her ears ringing and watched the guild's freight undo the guild's army, and she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *this is what choosing looks like. It looks like this. Ordinary. Bleeding. Late.*

Then, through the rout, through the smoke, she saw the hooded figure in ash-grey standing untouched amid the flight, and beside it a man in fine dark wool with a ledger-case slung at his hip, and both of them were looking up — not at the freed spirits, not at the broken gates.

At the mountain.

Where, deep beneath the snow, something that had slept for six hundred years had just felt its shackles loosen, and turned over in its sleep, and begun, very slowly, to wake.

* * *

**ADA**

The wolf was dying. Ada knew it the way you know a lamp is guttering — not by any single sign but by the quality of the light she cast.

She had unbound eleven of the twenty-three spirits herself, walking into the chain-line under arrowfall no ward could stop, letting the grey iron drink from her the last reserves of a guardian's strength. She had done it without hesitation, without ceremony, the way she had done everything — and now she lay in the trampled snow inside the gate, sides heaving, frost forming on her fur though the battle's fires burned thirty feet away.

Ada knelt in the slush and held her head in his lap. Around them the holdfast shouted and wept and counted its dead, and the freed ward-spirits drifted above the walls like auroras given grief, and somewhere beyond the ridge the mountain breathed a warmth that had no business existing in the long winter.

*Listen,* the wolf said.

"To what?"

*To what is underneath.* Her eyes were fixed on the mountain. *It is not angry. That is the worst of it. It is only hungry, and it has waited so long, and it smells the wards lit again — lit wrong, lit partial, lit by sacrifice instead of covenant. It thinks the door is opening.* Her breathing hitched. *It is not entirely wrong, little counter of things. Someone must go to the heart of the old line. Someone must finish what your grandmother refused.*

Ada looked down at her, and the cold went through him deeper than the snow ever could.

"What did she refuse?"

The wolf smiled. Wolves cannot smile, and she did it anyway.

*Come,* she said. *While there is still time to say goodbye to yourself properly.*

Far off, beneath the mountain, something exhaled, and the stars — the few that had crept back — went out again, all at once, like candles before a draft.
