## 17. What the Mountain Wakes

The mountain did not erupt. That was the terrible thing about it. Mountains, in the stories, erupted — fire fountaining, stone screaming skyward. This one simply *opened*, the way a mouth opens before a scream, and what came out was not fire but attention.

Ada felt it from three ridges away.

He was riding the wolf — actually riding her now, because she could no longer run, because the wards had been dark for too long and she was their last bound guardian and they were killing her by degrees — when the ground beneath them shuddered once, softly, like a sleeper turning over. Then the horizon to the south turned the color of a forge seen through a keyhole.

"There," the wolf said. Her voice had grown thin over the weeks of flight, a thread of a voice, a voice worn down like a coin passed through too many hands. "Do you feel it?"

He did. It felt like standing in a doorway when someone behind him says his name — that full-body turn of the soul. The Ember had woken, and it was *looking* for something, and Ada understood with sick certainty what it was looking for. Him. The gift in his blood. The crown his grandmother had refused.

"Ride," he said, and hated himself for it, because every stride hurt her. He could feel her pain through his knees, a grinding wrongness in her hips, her breath coming in a rasp that had nothing to do with exertion. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

"Do not apologize to me for running toward the thing I was made to guard," the wolf said. "Apologize if you fail."

* * *

The sky stayed black. Not night-black — Ada had known night all his life, night with its honest darkness and its scattered lamps of stars. This was a black with weight to it, a black like a lid. The stars had gone out the night the wards fell, and they had not come back, and now he understood why in his bones: the stars were not lights. They were reflections. The old wards had caught the sky's own fire and thrown it back down in pinpricks, a mirror held up to heaven, and with the wards dead the heavens had nothing to shine with.

The world without its wards was a room with the mirrors covered. Everything still moved in it. Nothing in it could see itself.

And the wards — the dead ones — were screaming.

He had heard them faintly for days, the way you hear a ringing in your ears after a concert. Now, as they crested the last ridge before the old ward-line, the sound came through whole, and Ada pressed his hands flat against the wolf's shoulders and wept without deciding to.

They screamed in no language. They screamed in *structure* — a vast chord of broken geometry, of promises torn mid-word. Each ward had been a spirit once; he knew that now, had learned it from a captive one in a cage in Corvane's cellar, a small god the size of a fist that had begged him in a voice like wind through a keyhole. The guild had spent decades buying them, fragmenting them, selling the pieces north to a warlord who fed them to the Ember to sweeten its sleep. And now the Ember was awake, and the ward-spirits along the border — the ones too old, too rooted, too stubborn to be caged — were dying in their standing stones, and their dying was not silent.

"They can be relit," Ada said. It was not a question. It had stopped being a question somewhere on the road, somewhere in the holdfast council chamber where he had stood before lords who hated each other more than they hated the dark, and confessed everything, and been believed only when a stormhawk spoke through his throat. "The crown. The ward-line. That's how she did it. That's how they all did it, before."

"Yes," said the wolf.

"And my grandmother refused."

The wolf did not answer for a long time. Below them, the pass opened up — a corridor of standing stones marching down the valley like the spine of some buried god, each one dark, each one screaming, snow falling between them black with ash.

"She refused," the wolf said at last, "because the crown does not take what you offer. It takes what you *are*. Your grandmother stood where you will stand and she looked into it, and she saw what it would cost, and she loved her daughter too much to pay it. She walked away. The wards held for another generation on what was left of the old fire, and everyone praised her wisdom, and the Ember slept, and the guild began, very quietly, to sell the difference."

"And now there's no difference left to sell."

"Now there is only you."

* * *

They came down into the ward-line as the mountain's attention swept over them.

It was not heat, though the air was warm now, impossibly warm, snow melting as it fell. It was *regard*. Ada felt it pass across the valley like a hand across a table, feeling for objects, and where it touched the standing stones the screaming changed pitch — rose, almost hopefully — and then collapsed again into that broken chord. The Ember could feel the wards. It could feel that they were empty. It was looking for what had filled them.

"It knows I'm here," Ada whispered.

"It has always known," the wolf said. "The gift in your blood is the same fire that made the wards. When you speak to beasts, you are speaking to the small surviving pieces of what the wards once were. That is why it was outlawed, child. Not because it is dangerous. Because it is *valuable*, and the people who won the last war did not want to share."

The wolf stopped at the first standing stone.

Up close, Ada could see what the wards had been. The stone was granite, older than any kingdom, but threaded through it ran veins of something else — something that had once glowed, he realized, the way the stormhawk's feathers glowed when it spoke. The veins were dark now. But as he laid his palm against the stone, something moved under his hand. Faint. Flickering. A pulse.

"It's alive," he breathed. "It's still — there's still something in there."

"A fragment. A splinter of the last guardian who fed this stone, two hundred years ago. It is not enough to wake the ward. But it is enough to *remember* how." The wolf lowered herself to the snow, carefully, her hind legs folding with a stiffness that made Ada's chest ache. "Climb down. I cannot carry you to the heart. I am sorry. I had hoped to be the one to bring you all the way."

Ada slid off her back and stood in the black snow and did not know what to do with his hands.

"Tell me," he said. "All of it. Before we go any further. Tell me what the crown does. Not the version the holdfast lords tell, not the version in the ledgers. The truth."

The wolf looked at him. Her eyes were going pale at the edges, he noticed — frost-colored creeping in from the rims, like ice forming on a pond. He had been pretending not to see it for three days. He could not pretend anymore.

"The truth," the wolf said, "is that the wards are made of memory. Not metaphor — memory. Actual. The first guardians discovered that a soul's past can be spun into structure, the way a spider spins silk from its own body. Every ward-stone on this border holds the childhood of someone who loved this land enough to give it away. When the Ember wakes, it eats the future — it burns what *will be* until nothing is left but ash and appetite. The only thing it cannot eat is what has already been given. A gift already spent. A memory already poured out. It passes over the wards the way water passes over glass."

"And the crown—"

"The crown is the loom. It takes the wearer's past and spins it into the ward-line, stone by stone, all down the border. It is not painless. It is not quick. And it does not stop when you change your mind." The wolf's voice was gentle, and that gentleness was the worst thing Ada had heard in a winter full of terrible things. "Your grandmother stood at the heart-stone with her hands on the crown, and she saw — the crown *shows* you, first, everything you will lose — and she saw her daughter's face, and her daughter's voice, and every morning of her daughter's life that she would not remember living through, and she took her hands away."

"And the wards began to die."

"And the wards began to die. Slowly. So slowly that a whole generation could pretend it was natural. So slowly that a guild could form, and grow, and find buyers for the pieces." The wolf rose, shakily, and leaned her grey shoulder against the standing stone. "I do not tell you this to turn you back. I tell you because you deserve the truth that she was not given. The crown shows you the price *before* you pay. She was not a coward, Ada Delacroix. She was a mother. Both things can be true."

Ada stood in the black snow and thought about his mother's voice.

He thought about it the way a drowning man thinks about air. Her voice, humming in the kitchen of the flat above the countinghouse. The way she said his name — *Ada*, two syllables, the second one rising, always a small question, always a small delight, as if she were checking that he was still there and pleased each time to find it was true. He had not heard it in fifteen years. She had been dead eleven. And he realized, standing there, that the voice lived in him now only as memory — and that the crown would take even that. That somewhere in the ward-line there would be a stone holding his mother humming, and he would walk past it for the rest of his life and feel nothing, the way the woman in the village that forgot its name had looked at her daughter's face and seen a stranger.

"That's the price," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Not death. *Her.*"

"Not death," the wolf agreed. "Death would be simpler. Death takes everything at once and evenly. The crown takes selectively. It takes what you love most first, because that is the strongest thread. You will keep the ledgers. You will keep the roads you walked and the prices of things. You will keep your hands' knowledge and your gift, quieter now. But the warm things —" the wolf's frost-rimmed eyes closed briefly — "the warm things go into the stones. And they will glow there. And every traveler on this border for the next two hundred years will be warmed by your mother's voice without ever knowing it. That is not nothing, Ada. That is the whole point. Memory given is not destroyed. It is *spent* — spent into the world, where it does its work."

Above them, the mountain's attention swept the valley again, closer this time, and Ada felt it snag — felt it *notice* — like a fisherman feeling a line go taut.

"It's found me," he said.

"It has found the gift in you. It cannot reach you yet. The dead wards still scream, and their screaming is a fence even the Ember respects, for now. But the holdfast will fall within days. Corvane's forces are driving the caged spirits ahead of them like cattle, and when the cages break —" the wolf shook her head — "there will be nothing between the Ember and the north but you."

"Then we should not waste time."

* * *

They walked the ward-line together, the dying wolf and the clerk, down the corridor of screaming stones.

Ada had expected the walk to the heart to be a march. It was more like a funeral procession, or a waking. At each stone, the wolf stopped, and pressed her muzzle to the granite, and spoke a word in no language Ada knew — the binding-tongue, she said, the language the wards had been made in — and the stone's fragment stirred, and showed him something.

He saw a shepherd's boy on this very pass, four hundred years gone, laughing as his dog chased crows, and understood that the boy's laughter was baked into the stone's foundations. He saw a woman setting wards with her own hands, singing under her breath, a song Ada somehow knew without ever having heard it. He saw, at the seventh stone, a face that stopped his heart: an older version of his own face, sharp-cheeked, grey-streaked, furious and afraid.

"Grandmother," he whispered.

"She came this far," the wolf said softly. "She walked the line. She touched each stone. She was measuring the cost. And at the heart-stone she put her hands on the crown, and the crown showed her your mother at six years old, learning to whistle, getting it wrong, laughing at herself — and Isandro—" the wolf caught herself, the old name slipping out, the guardian's name for the line he came from — "*Ada*. She took her hands away. I have carried the memory of that choice for two hundred years. I do not condemn it. I want you to know that. I have watched her granddaughter's granddaughter balance the books of the men who are killing me, and keep a secret until it scarred him, and flee north through a dead winter to give that secret away, and I do not condemn her, and I do not condemn you, whatever you choose at the heart."

"What did you call me?"

The wolf was quiet for three strides. "Isandro was the name of the first guardian of this line. It has been the name of the line's heirs ever since, waiting. Your grandmother carried it. She meant to give it to your mother, and your mother refused it, because she had watched what the waiting did." The wolf looked up at him, and her frost-rimmed eyes were clear and ancient and unbearably kind. "The crown will ask you for your name, when you pour your past into it. It takes the name last. It is the keystone. And when it is gone, you will need another one, and it will have to come from someone who remembers you. That is not a warning. That is an instruction. *Do not climb the heart alone.*"

"I have no one," Ada said. "Sera stayed behind. The hawk—"

"The hawk lives. And the woman who burned her own report to save you does not strike me as someone who stays dead for long. But that is not what I mean." The wolf stopped at the foot of the last rise, where the valley narrowed and the standing stones drew together like mourners, and beyond them, half-buried in ash-black snow, Ada could see it: the heart-stone, vast as a chapel, and on its crown of granite a circlet of woven light, dim, waiting, patient as a debt.

"I mean," the wolf said, "that a name kept by no one is a name lost. Whatever happens on that stone, make sure someone is watching who will carry it back down the mountain. Promise me."

"I promise," Ada said, and found that he meant it, and found also that he did not know how he would keep it, and found also that it did not matter, because the mountain chose that moment to wake all the way.

* * *

There was no sound at first. That was the horror of it — the largest thing that had happened in the history of the world arrived in silence, a pressure, a *turning*, like a vast eye opening beneath the earth. The snow stopped falling. Every dead ward on the line screamed at once, one chord, one voice, and then — Ada felt it rather than heard it — the Ember *spoke*.

Not in words. In appetite. It said: *I remember when this valley was mine. I remember when the sky was mine. Give me back what was taken.*

And every caged ward-spirit in Corvane's army, miles south, screamed in answer, and the screaming was *yes*.

The wolf staggered. Ada caught her, or tried to — she weighed more than he did, but she was folding now, her legs going, her frost-rimmed eyes rolling — and he knelt with her in the black snow and held her great grey head in his lap and felt, through the bond he had never asked for and could not sever, the wards draining out of her like blood from a wound.

"It's pulling at the line," she whispered. "Through me. The last binding. It wants me to let go. It wants me to *open*."

"Fight it."

"I have fought it for two hundred years, little clerk." She huffed, almost a laugh. "I am so tired. Do you know what it is to be the last of your kind and the keeper of a promise everyone has forgotten? I have been dying since the night the stars went out. The Ember is only hurrying the arithmetic." She lifted her head, with effort, and set her muzzle against his chest, over his heart. "Listen to me. The heart-stone. Climb. Put your hands on the crown. Let it show you the price — let it show you *all* of it, do not look away, she made the mistake of flinching and the flinch is what she remembered most — and then choose. I will hold the line here as long as I have anything left to hold it with. I am the last bound guardian, and I am telling you, heir of Isandro, heir of the woman who walked away: *the wards can be relit.* It is not too late. It has never been too late. That is the secret the guild was built to bury."

Ada's eyes were burning. "Come with me. To the stone. Please."

"I will come as far as I can," the wolf said. "And where I stop, I will stop *guarding*, and that is not a small thing. Go. The mountain is waking faster than we planned. Go now, and when you stand at the heart, when the crown asks you for your name —" her voice frayed, thinned, became for a moment the voice of something much older and much larger than a wolf — "give it gladly. A name given is a name *kept*, little clerk. Nothing given is ever truly lost. That is the first law, and the last, and I have kept it two hundred years, and I am — I am so glad —"

She did not finish. Her head sank into the snow. But her ribs still moved, slow and stubborn, and her eyes still tracked him as he rose, and as Ada Delacroix turned and climbed the last rise toward the heart-stone, toward the crown of woven light, he heard her one more time, faint as a bell across a valley:

"*Do what she could not.*"

The mountain opened wider. The sky stayed black. And the crown, patient as a debt two hundred years overdue, began — very softly, very sweetly — to hum his mother's song.
