## 18. A Crown for the Ember

The wolf could not carry me the last mile.

I knew it before he stopped. His gait had been failing since midnight — a hitch in the left hind leg that came and went like a bad memory, then stayed. By the time we reached the foot of the old ward-line, where the mountains folded together around a valley of black snow and screaming stone, he was moving on three legs and sheer will, and his breath came in ragged white plumes that smelled of ash and something older: pine, I thought, and river water, and my grandmother's kitchen, though none of those things had any business being in a wolf's breath.

He stopped at the base of a ridge of fused glass — sand melted by the first lighting of the wards, two hundred years ago — and looked back at me.

*Here,* he said. Not in words. In the feeling of arriving somewhere you have been trying to reach your whole life.

I slid off his back and my legs nearly went out from under me. Three days of flight, two of them in the saddle of a dying guardian, one of them carrying him when he could no longer run. My hands were cracked from cold. My ribs ached where Sera's hunters had clipped me at the ford. Somewhere behind us, Corvane's forces were breaking against the holdfast like a slow black tide, and the sky above was still empty of stars, and the dead wards were still screaming.

That was the worst part. Not the silence of the stars — I'd almost gotten used to that, the way you get used to a toothache. It was the screaming. When the wards fell, they hadn't died cleanly. They'd broken the way living things break, and what remained of them along the border — stumps of light, fragments humming grief in cages, whole leagues of ward-line gone dark and raw — screamed without pause, without voice, without hope. The stormhawk had flown ahead of us an hour ago, and even she, who had crossed the Ember War's aftermath once before, kept shaking her head as if to dislodge the sound.

*It is worse here,* the wolf said. *This is where they broke first.*

The valley opened below us like a wound. At its center, half-buried in ash-snow, stood the Heartstone — or what was left of one. A pillar of pale stone the height of three men, cracked down the middle, its surface crawling with dead script. Around it, arranged in a ring wider than a village, were the anchor-stones of the old ward-line, each one dark, each one leaking a thin smoke that wasn't smoke. Memory, maybe. The land forgetting itself in real time.

And on the Heartstone's crown — because of course it had a crown; everything in the old stories had a crown, and I had spent thirty years believing the old stories were ledgers' embellishments — sat a circlet of woven light. Dim now. Flickering. Made of what looked like threads of every color the night sky used to hold, braided into something small enough to lift with two hands.

*The Crown of Living Memory,* the wolf said. *My grandmother carried it. And hers.*

*You're a wolf,* I said. *Wolves don't have grandmothers who carry crowns.*

*Everything bound has a line,* he said. *You are bound too. You simply never asked what to.*

I wanted to laugh at that, or argue, but the wind came down the valley then and it carried voices — not the wards' screaming this time but something underneath it, rhythmic and vast and patient, rising from beneath the mountain like a heartbeat heard through a floor. The Ember. Waking. Corvane's buyers had spent decades buying the wards apart, fragment by fragment, ledger entry by ledger entry, and all of it had been foreplay for this: the thing beneath the stone turning over in its sleep, opening one eye, finding the world above it unguarded.

*How long?* I asked.

*A day,* the wolf said. *Perhaps less. The mountain is already warm.*

I looked at the crown. Small. Almost delicate. The kind of thing you'd find in a display case at the Gilded Vane with a price tag and a provenance letter, and some rich fool would buy it to wear to a masque.

*Tell me plainly,* I said. *What does it cost.*

The wolf lay down then, folding his ruined legs under him, and looked up at me with eyes the grey of old ice, and I understood that he had been waiting three days to be asked exactly that question, and dreading it.

*Memory,* he said. *All of it. Everything you are is fuel. The crown burns a life to light the line. That was always the price. Your grandmother refused it.* A pause, and in the pause I felt her face again — the woman from the refugee crush, the one whose memory the wolf had shown me weeks ago, laughing in a kitchen I didn't remember ever standing in. *She chose to live, and let the wards fall slowly instead, and spend two hundred years watching the world forget how to protect itself. She was not wrong. But she was not right either.*

*And you?* I said. *What do you pay?*

His tail moved once against the ash. *Guardians are bound to the line. If it lights, we go with it.*

*I didn't ask you to—*

*No,* he said. *You asked me to guide you north. Everything after that, I chose.* He rested his great scarred head on his paws. *Ada Delacroix. Clerk of impossible things. Do you know why I found you in that crowd?*

*Because I'm heir to something,* I said. *You said so. Heir to the ward-speakers, the beast-speakers, whatever we were.*

*Because you balanced the books,* he said, *and hated yourself for it. Because you read the ledgers and did not look away. Anyone can refuse a crown they've never touched. I needed someone who understood what things cost, and would pay anyway, and would hate every minute of paying, and do it regardless. That is rarer than magic. Magic is everywhere. That is nowhere.*

The wind screamed. Under it, the mountain breathed.

I walked down into the valley alone.

* * *

The Heartstone was warm. That frightened me more than anything else had — more than the assassins, more than the starless sky, more than the interrogation cell where I'd pressed my gift against a captive ward-spirit and felt the Ember stirring in its dreams. Warmth meant proximity. Warmth meant the thing below was close enough to heat stone through two hundred feet of mountain.

The dead script on the pillar's surface shifted as I approached. I am a clerk; I read scripts the way other people breathe. This one wasn't language, exactly. It was accounting. Columns of names, running up the stone in spirals, each name struck through in silver fire — every life the crown had ever burned, recorded in the only format eternity respects. I found entries in hands I somehow recognized. A name near the top made my chest seize: **MARA DELACROIX**, and beside it, not struck through but written in a different ink, a single word: *refused.*

My grandmother. Who had stood where I was standing, with the same blood in her veins and the same outlawed gift in her skull, and turned away.

*She lived forty years after,* the wolf said quietly, from the ridge. *She taught your mother to bake bread. She held you once, when you were an infant, and told your mother you had the eyes.* Another pause. *Do not mistake refusal for cowardice. Or acceptance for courage. They are only choices. You get one.*

I reached up and took the crown off the stone.

It weighed nothing. That was the trick of it, I suppose — the way the Gilded Vane's cruelest contracts always weighed nothing on the day of signing. The woven light settled into my palms, cool as riverwater, and hummed against my skin in a register I felt in my teeth, and the moment it touched me it *knew* me. I felt it reading me the way I read ledgers: page by page, column by column, patient and thorough and utterly without mercy.

It found the secret first. Of course it did. Thirty-one years of hiding the gift, of flinching from stable-boys' horses and market geese, of pressing my hands flat under tavern tables when dogs spoke to me, of keeping the scar of it so deep I'd half-convinced myself it wasn't there. The crown peeled that concealment away like wet paper, and for one vertiginous moment I stood in the valley with every wall down, hearing everything — the worm-thoughts in the frozen soil, the argument of crows three miles east, the low continuous moan of the dying wards, the mountain's slow hungry pulse — and it was too much, it was far too much, I dropped to one knee and vomited bile into the ash—

—and the wolf was suddenly beside me, leaning his weight against my shoulder, solid and warm and smelling of pine and river and bread.

*Breathe,* he said. *One thing at a time. That is how it must be given. One thing at a time, or it takes all of you at once and lights nothing.*

*How do you know?*

*Because I watched your grandmother try,* he said, *and fail, and choose.*

I knelt there in the ash with the crown in my hands and the wolf against my side, and above us the starless sky pressed down like a lid, and below us the mountain counted its heartbeat, and I thought about what I was about to lose.

Not death. Death would have been simpler. Sera had explained it to me at the pass, holding out her ward-smith's sigil with a hand that shook: *The crown doesn't kill you. It spends you. You'll walk away. You just won't know what you're walking away with.* The village that forgot its name rose in my mind — the woman who had looked at her own daughter with polite blankness and offered her soup. That was the price named aloud. Not my life. My *self*. Every birthday. Every scar. My mother's voice singing in the kitchen — I had lost her at nine years old and the crown would take even the losing. My own name, which I had carried through guild audits and assassin flights and a war's beginning like a coin clenched in a fist.

And underneath all of it, the gift itself. The beasts. The last piece of my grandmother that lived in the world.

*Ask me if I'm sure,* I said to the wolf.

*No,* he said. *Certainty is a merchant's word. Ask me instead whether the world is worth it.*

I looked at the valley. At the dark anchor-stones. At the south, where the holdfast's fires were tiny points against the black, where Sera was fighting or captured or dead, where lords who despised each other were holding a line they barely believed in because a clerk with stolen paper had convinced them to try. At the child I'd never met, laughing somewhere in a future I was about to stop remembering, at a talking bird.

*Yes,* I said.

*Then begin,* said the wolf. *And I will end beside you. That is the whole of the arrangement.*

I lifted the crown and put it on.

* * *

It did not hurt. Everyone assumes it hurts. It doesn't. It *unmakes*, and unmaking feels like relief, which is the obscene part.

The first thing I gave it was small, deliberately small: the smell of the Gilded Vane's counting room, ink and lamp-oil and Corvane's clove-scented pomade. The crown drank it and the nearest anchor-stone flickered — a stutter of blue-white light, there and gone, like a struck match. Far to the north, along the broken border, I felt a ward-stump stop screaming for one full second.

One second. Out of two hundred years of dark.

So I gave it more. The taste of the bread my grandmother baked — a memory I didn't know I had until the crown pulled it forward, her flour-dusted hands, the burn scar on her wrist. An anchor-stone lit and held for ten seconds. The screaming along the border faltered, rippled, changed pitch — grief becoming attention.

*It works,* I gasped. *Wolf, it's working—*

*Faster than she managed,* he agreed. His voice was thinner now. I looked down and saw why: his fur was going translucent at the edges, silvered, as if the crown had already begun drawing on him too, spending the guardian's long hoarded centuries. *Keep giving. Do not stop to watch. Watching is how you start bargaining with yourself.*

So I stopped watching and started pouring.

My childhood. All of it, in order, because order mattered — the crown took it chronologically, greedy and gentle at once. The apartment over the chandlery. My mother teaching me letters with a stick in spilled flour. The first time a horse spoke to me, age six, complaining about its bit, and the terror and the wonder braided so tight I couldn't tell them apart for twenty-five years. My mother's voice — God, her voice, low and slightly off-key, singing a song about a lighthouse keeper whose name I realized, even as the memory drained away, I would never retrieve again. I gave it. The stone at the valley's eastern arc blazed alight and stayed lit, and a mile away another caught like flame passing along a fuse.

The wolf was fading faster now. Half of him was light. He leaned harder against me, or tried to; there was less of him to lean.

*Your turn,* I said. *Stop. Stop giving, you don't have to—*

*I have two hundred years,* he said. *You have thirty-one. Fair is fair.* And he laughed — a real laugh, rough and glad, the first time I'd ever heard him make that sound — and gave the crown his memories too. I felt them leave him in a rush: forests that no longer existed, the Ember War's last battle seen from a hillside, my grandmother young and furious and magnificent, the taste of snowmelt, the names of eleven other guardians, all dead, all carried in him alone until this moment. He poured two centuries into the stones and they drank it and *blazed*, and the ring of light closed link by link around the valley, and the border-wards beyond caught fire like a signal chain running north to south across the whole wounded country.

The mountain screamed. The Ember, waking fully now, slammed its heat against the relit line — and the line held. Held the way a ledger holds: not with strength, but with *record*. Every memory burning in those stones was a fact the darkness couldn't erase, an entry eternity had to respect.

I was on my knees. The crown was molten-bright above my brow and I could feel what was left of me the way you feel coins left in a purse after paying a debt. I inventoried, because I was a clerk to the end.

A mother's voice: gone. Her face: gone. I knew I had loved her; the knowing was all that remained, love with the details burned out of it, a column with the figures struck and only the sum surviving.

My childhood: gone. The Gilded Vane: mostly gone — I kept, absurdly, the exact weight of a gold ounce and the smell of ink, the two least human facts of that life.

The flight north: fraying. Sera's face at the pass — I gripped that one, held it the way you hold a door shut against wind, and felt the crown pry at my fingers, patient, inevitable.

My name. It was asking for my name last. Of course it was. Names go last; even the oldest contracts know that.

*Give it,* the wolf whispered. He was nearly all light now, a wolf-shape of silver fire with two grey eyes floating in it, and his voice sounded like my grandmother's kitchen sounded, which I no longer remembered, which broke something in me that had no memories left to break. *Give it freely, Ada. What is given freely cannot be stolen later.*

"I am Ada Delacroix," I said aloud, to the valley, to the stones, to the starless sky, to the child in the future. "Clerk of the Gilded Vane. Beast-speaker. Grandson of Mara, who refused, and of no one else I can name. I balance the books."

I took off the crown — because that was the final term, I understood it suddenly, completely, the way you understand the last line of a contract — and set it on the Heartstone, and stepped back, and let the last of me pour out of my open hands into the light.

"Close the accounts," I said.

The world went white.

* * *

Snow was falling when I could see again. Ordinary snow, or what passed for it now — grey with ash, settling softly on the anchor-stones, which burned in their ring like a circle of small calm suns. The screaming had stopped. In its place, faint and vast and everywhere, the wards were *humming*: the sound of a held note, of a ledger balanced, of something enormous sleeping soundly behind a locked door.

Far overhead, through a tear in the cloud, one star came out. Then another. Then a handful, tentative, like witnesses approaching a fire.

The wolf was gone. Where he had lain, a patch of snow lay undisturbed, and in the center of it, a single grey fang, and pressed into the ash beside it, unmistakable, a pawprint filled with light that had not yet finished fading.

I sat down in the snow because my legs informed me they were done negotiating. I felt — hollow. Scoured. Peaceful the way an emptied room is peaceful. I knew things: how to read, how to survive cold, the weight of gold, the smell of ink. I knew I had done something important tonight. I did not know what, or why, or who had done it.

There was a man sitting in the snow in a burnt coat, and he was looking at a wolf's fang in his palm, and he could not remember his own name.

Footsteps crunched behind me. I turned, hand closing around the fang — the one thing in the world that felt like mine, though I couldn't have said why — and saw a figure picking her way down the valley slope with a sword at her hip and ash in her hair and an expression I couldn't read at all, though some part of me, some struck-through column deep in the wreck of the ledgers, insisted I had seen it before and trusted it.

She stopped ten feet away. She was staring at me like a woman looking at a house that had burned down and finding one wall still standing.

"Ada," she said.

The name landed in me like a key turning in a lock that had rusted shut. Nothing opened. But I felt, distinctly, the shape of the mechanism — the sense that somewhere inside me there was a door, and this woman knew where it was, and someone had to.

"That's—" My voice came out cracked and strange. "Is that mine?"

She crossed the snow and knelt in front of me, and took both my hands in hers, and her grip was steady now, steadier than it had been at the pass, wherever and whenever that was.

"It's yours," Sera Voss said. "I kept it for you."
