## 21. The Name They Keep for Me

The holdfast wakes me the way it always does now: with bells.

Not alarm bells — those I still flinch at, some animal part of me braced for hunters in the stairwells. These are the morning bells, three soft strokes from the tower by the east wall, and after them the sound that has become more familiar to me than my own reflection: the ordinary noise of people deciding to live another day. A door banging open below. Someone cursing at a frozen pump. The smell of barley porridge and woodsmoke climbing through the floorboards like weather.

I lie in the narrow bed and do what I do every morning.

I try to remember my mother's face.

It's a ritual, not a hope. The crown took it — took her voice first, then her face, then the whole warm country of her, and left me only the fact of her, the way you know a room existed behind a locked door because the hinges are still there. I reach for her the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. *She had hands,* I tell myself. *She held me. Her name began with an A too.* That last one is Sera's contribution. She told me it in the second week, when I woke crying without knowing why, and she sat on the edge of my bed with her ward-smith's calluses folded in her lap and said, "Your name is Ada Delacroix, and your mother's name was Ameline Delacroix, and she sang off-key and didn't care."

"How do you know?" I asked.

"I read your file," she said, "when the guild sent me to kill you." And then, because Sera is Sera: "I memorized it instead."

That is how I have learned myself. Borrowed light. Secondhand sun.

* * *

The journal is the strangest of my teachers, stranger even than the stormhawk, though I would never say so where Perrin could hear me. Perrin holds grudges.

The journal lives in the bottom of my sea chest, wrapped in oilcloth, and for the first month I could not bear to touch it. It had crossed half the broken border with me. It had been in my pack when I climbed the pass with a dying wolf beneath me and the sky screaming. Whatever was inside it, I had written it, and I did not trust the man who wrote it. He had kept secrets from himself for thirty years. He had balanced ledgers while the world was sold. Who knew what else he'd done.

But one night in deep winter — the real deep winter, before the wards came back, when the black-ash snow fell so thick it hushed the bells — I unwrapped it. I told myself it was research. I told myself a lot of things that winter.

The handwriting is mine. That was the first shock: seeing my own hand, the small cramped letters, the way I cross my sevens. It looked like a stranger wearing my clothes. The entries were mundane at first — supply lists, route notes, the price of oats in villages that no longer exist. Then, halfway through, the handwriting loosens, as if the man holding the pen had started running:

*The wolf is not what I thought. She remembers Grandmother. She showed me her face. I need to write it down before I doubt it — dark grey muzzle, scar through the left ear, and she laughed at me, or something like laughing, and for one moment I smelled bread baking and woodsmoke and I was seven years old again on a porch I don't remember ever standing on.*

*Her name was Wren. My grandmother's name was Wren Delacroix, and she was a beast-speaker, and she refused the crown once, and everyone paid for it.*

*I am going north. If anyone reads this: the guild sold the border. Corvane sold the border. Check the ledgers. Check entry 4471.*

*If I die out here, tell someone — anyone — that I was afraid the whole time, and I went anyway. I want that recorded somewhere. Even if I'm the only one who reads it.*

I sat with that page for a long time, in a room full of strangers' snoring, and felt something loosen in my chest that had been knotted since the stars went out. Because that was the thing about the crown, the thing nobody warns you: it doesn't just take your past. It takes your evidence. Every kindness I'd ever done, every cowardice, every small brave stupid choice — all of it sealed away behind the same locked door as my mother's voice. I couldn't prove to myself that I had ever been good. Or brave. Or anything at all.

But the journal proved it. My own hand proved it. *He was afraid the whole time, and he went anyway.*

Some nights that sentence is the only thing holding me up. I have taken to reading it aloud, quietly, the way other men say prayers. The man who wrote it feels like someone I might have liked. I keep waiting to grow into him.

* * *

Sera comes to my room most evenings, officially to check the ward-lattice in the window frame — the holdfast's masons wired the new wards into the old stone, and they hum at a pitch that makes her teeth ache, she claims, and requires constant supervision, which requires tea, which requires sitting down, which requires telling me about my day.

This is the arrangement, and we both pretend it isn't one, and neither of us has ever once suggested changing it.

Tonight she arrives with snow in her hair and a look on her face I've learned to read: the look she gets when someone at the council said something stupid and she swallowed it for diplomatic reasons, which is to say, she's saving it for me.

"Lord Aldous proposed a census of gift-bearers," she says, dropping onto my one chair and unlacing her boots with the violence of a woman who has been polite all day. "Registered. Badged. Tracked."

"What did you say?"

"Nothing. Master Ferren said it for me. Loudly. With a table involved."

"Ferren threw a table?"

"Ferren *stood up* and the table moved. Close enough." She accepts the tea I pour her and cradles it, staring into the steam. "They're scared, Ada. The Ember sleeps but it doesn't sleep forever. Everyone can feel it down in their back teeth. And scared people want lists."

"And you?"

"I think lists are how the Gilded Vane started." She says it flatly, without drama, the way she says everything that matters. "A ledger of impossible things. First you write the names down to protect them. Fifty years later you're selling the pages." She glances up. "You'd know better than me. You wrote in that book longer than anyone."

"I don't remember writing in it at all."

"No." She sets down the cup. "Which is why I'm going to tell you about yourself, since apparently that's my job now. Listen. Today you got angry."

"Did I."

"At the well. When that boy was teasing the goat. You didn't shout. You just went very still and looked at him, and he stopped, and later you bought the goat an apple and pretended you hadn't." She leans forward. "Ada. You were *furious*. I've seen your fury twice now, and both times it was for an animal, and both times you tried to pretend afterward it hadn't happened."

"That's not—" But it is. The anger rises in me hot and wordless when creatures are hurt, older than language, and I have been smoothing it over every time because I don't know yet whether it belongs to the man I was or the man I'm supposed to be. "Maybe I'm just a man who likes goats."

"You're a man whose grandmother was burned out of three villages for talking to beasts, and who hid his own gift under ledgers for thirty years, and who spent his entire self to save a world that would have jailed him. Of course your fury lives close to the skin. It's earned." She takes the tea back. "Stop apologizing for it. That's an order. I'm technically still an enforcer; the rank transfers."

"Sera."

"Hm?"

"Why do you do this? Every night. All of it." I gesture vaguely at the room, the tea, the whole architecture of borrowed memory she has built around me. "You owe me nothing. You gave me your sigil. You stayed behind for hunters—"

"I stayed behind because it was tactically sound," she says, in the tone of a woman revising history in real time, "and I owe you plenty. You want the list? You carried my guilt out of that city for me. Before you, I was a woman guarding cages of grieving things and calling it a salary. You made me burn my own report, Ada. Nobody does that alone. So yes, I owe you." She drinks. "Also the tea here is terrible and I refuse to drink it by myself."

* * *

Perrin teaches me different lessons than Sera does.

The stormhawk nests these days on the south parapet, having concluded sometime around midwinter that the holdfast is his territory and its inhabitants his staff. He is enormous, storm-grey, with eyes like chips of lightning, and he speaks to me in the new quiet way — not the desperate whispers of the old days, when the world's edges were fraying and every wild thing screamed along them, but something slower. Calmer. The wards hum steady now, and the beasts speak the way they might have spoken before the Ember War, before everything: unhurried, faintly amused, entirely unimpressed by human institutions.

*The tall one fed me rat today,* Perrin reports, riding the thermals above the training yard while I split wood below. *She pretended it was a treat. It was a rat. I ate it anyway because I am gracious.*

"She's trying. Say thank you."

*I said thank you in hawk. Your kind just doesn't listen.* A long spiral upward. *You split the wood crooked again.*

"The wood is fine."

*Your grandmother split it straighter.*

I stop, axe raised. "You remember her."

*Two hundred years is nothing to a stormhawk, little clerk.* He lands on the woodpile, folds his wings, and fixes me with that lightning stare. *I remember her splitting wood in this exact yard, badly, while the wolves laughed at her. I remember her arguing with the mountain. I remember the night she stood where you stood and was asked to wear the crown, and said no, and walked away, and lived forty more years with the weight of it.* He tilts his head. *You said yes in one night, with no preparation, and died to your own name for it. She watched you do what she wouldn't. From wherever her kind goes, she watched.*

"Perrin. What was I like? Before."

The question hangs there. Wind moves through the yard. Somewhere below us the morning bells' echo has long faded and the holdfast goes about its business, and this bird who has seen two centuries considers whether to answer.

*Afraid,* he says at last. *All the time. You flinched at doors. You counted coins to calm yourself — did you know that? Little sums, under your breath. One and one and one.* His feathers ruffle, something almost gentle in it. *And underneath the fear, a stubbornness like bedrock. You freed me from that cage with a hairpin and a lie, and your hands shook the entire time, and you did it anyway. I have known ten thousand humans. Most of them, given a cage and a hairpin, would have found a reason to walk past.* He hops closer, claws clicking on the split logs. *So when you ask what you were like: afraid, and stubborn, and kinder than you believed. The crown took the memories. It did not take the man. The man is still here. He is splitting wood crooked and feeding rats to stormhawks and letting a woman drink bad tea in his rooms every evening because he is too proud to ask why she stays.*

My throat has gone tight. "Why does she stay?"

*Because you are the only person alive who asks her questions she doesn't already know the answers to,* Perrin says. *Now fetch me the rest of the rat. Graciously.*

* * *

Spring comes late to the pass, and when it comes it comes shy — green shoots testing the ash-heavy soil, the black snow retreating up the slopes in dirty patches. The holdfast opens its gates wider. Farmers return to terraces their grandparents abandoned. And the beasts begin coming down out of the high country, cautiously, the way animals approach a place that hurt them once.

They come to me.

That's the part I still can't explain, and don't fully trust. Word travels in the ways word travels among wild things — scent and wingbeat and the low humming network of the relit wards themselves — and one morning there will be a fox sitting at the edge of the yard watching me, and the next a blind old mountain goat, and the next a raven with a broken wing who talks the entire time I splint it, mostly complaints, occasionally insults, and once, devastatingly, a thank-you.

The gift survived. Quieter, the healers say — the crown spent most of what I was, and whatever power remains runs shallow and slow, a stream where there used to be a river. But it runs. And the animals come to the man at the holdfast who listens, and the people of the holdfast watch me kneel in the mud talking softly to limping wolves and injured kestrels, and they have started — slowly, in the way of frightened people thawing — to bring me things.

A child brought me a sparrow with a bent feather last week. Just walked up, held it out in both grubby hands, and said, "They said you talk to 'em. Is it true? What do they say?"

"They say," I told him, "that you have crumbs in your pocket and they'd like them."

His laugh was the best thing I'd heard since the stars came back.

That's the strange arithmetic of it, the thing I turn over at night like a coin: I lost everything that made me *me*, and in exchange I got a life where a child trusts me on sight and a woman brings me tea and a two-hundred-year-old hawk tells me embarrassing truths about my grandmother. The man in the ledger — the real Ada Delacroix, the auditor, the keeper of secrets — had thirty years of memories and no one to share a single one of them with. The man I am now has almost nothing, and gives away what little he has daily, and receives it back multiplied.

Borrowed light, all of it. Sera's stories. Perrin's memories. A dead wolf's loyalty I can't recall earning. A journal written by a stranger with my handwriting. I am a house rebuilt from other people's furniture, and some mornings I wake and grieve the house that burned.

But houses are for living in, not mourning. That's Sera's line. She stole it from somewhere; she refuses to say where. I've decided not to press her, because pressing her means she'll go quiet, and her going quiet is the one thing in this world I still fear.

* * *

Tonight the tea is worse than usual — someone has stretched the leaves with dried nettle, and Sera makes a face of profound betrayal and drinks it anyway. Outside my window the new wards hum their steady note, and beyond them the mountains stand black against a sky salted thick with stars, more of them returning every month, constellations reassembling themselves like a sentence being remembered word by word.

"Council session tomorrow," Sera says, turning her cup. "The census question again. Aldous has allies now. And there's a delegation coming up from the south — traders, they claim." She pauses, and I hear it: the small hitch, the thing she's been carrying all day and finally sets down between us. "They arrived at dusk. Nine of them. Grey cloaks. Ash-grey."

The cup goes very still in my hands.

"Ash-grey," I repeat.

"It's probably nothing. Cloth is cloth." But her jaw is set the way it sets before she burns a report, before she draws a blade she means to use, and Perrin — who has been dozing on the sill outside the window — lifts his head, and his eyes catch the lamplight like struck flint.

*Little clerk,* the stormhawk says softly, into the silence between us. *One of them smells of cages.*

Sera looks at me. I look at Sera. Neither of us says the word *Corvane*, because saying it would make it real, and because we both already know.

"Tomorrow," she says finally, "wear your boots to the council. And Ada—" she stands, drains the terrible tea like a soldier taking medicine, and pauses at the door, "—whatever happens, remember: you're not the man in that journal anymore. He was afraid and alone. You've got a hawk, a woman with excellent tactical instincts, and an entire holdfast that knows your name."

"That's the problem," I say. "I'm the only one who doesn't."

"Then tomorrow," Sera Voss says, pulling the door shut behind her, "we'll remind you."

Outside, Perrin spreads his wings against the starlight, watching the south road, and does not sleep.
