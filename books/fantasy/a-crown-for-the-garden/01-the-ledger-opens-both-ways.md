## 1. The Ledger Opens Both Ways

The trouble with impossible things, Lena Sandoval had learned, was that they were never actually impossible. They were merely expensive, badly scheduled, and—more often than not—somebody else's idea.

Take tonight. Midnight at the Guild of Impossible Things, and Lena was the only soul awake in the counting room, hunched over the great brass-bound ledgers with a candle stub, an abacus missing its seven-bead, and a pot of tea gone cold enough to qualify as soup. Somewhere above them, the guild's resident storm-caller snored hard enough to rattle the weathervanes. Somewhere below, the alchemists' cellar burped a smell like burnt lavender and regret.

Lena balanced accounts. That was the whole of their employment, and they liked it that way. Scribes who kept their heads down did not get volunteered for expeditions into the Hollow Sea or asked to transcribe the memoirs of sentient fog. Scribes who kept their heads down got paid, got fed, and—most importantly of all—got forgotten.

Invisibility, Lena had decided years ago, was not a curse. It was a craft. They had honed it the way other people honed swords: the mousy posture, the ink-stained cuffs that made one look perpetually mid-errand, the trick of answering questions so precisely boring that the asker's eyes glazed before the second sentence. *No, Guildmistress Vane, the third-quarter tallow expenditure is unremarkable; yes, the discrepancy of four coppers was traced to a moth.* Four coppers! A moth! People fled from conversations with Lena Sandoval as though from plague.

It was perfect. It was safe.

And it was why, when the sealed ledger fell off the top shelf and landed at their feet, Lena felt the floor tilt beneath them like a ship changing its mind.

* * *

The shelf in question was the Archive of Settled Matters—the guild's polite euphemism for things nobody wanted to think about anymore. Lena had only climbed the ladder to retrieve the Tallow Ledger, third volume, which someone (the moth's lawyer, presumably) had shelved among the antiques. The sealed book had been wedged behind it, black leather gone grey with age, its clasp stamped with the guild's sigil: a key inside a flower.

The seal was wax, and the wax was old, and old wax breaks if you sneeze near it. That was what Lena told themselves later, anyway. The truth was simpler and more damning: the book fell open in their hands, and the page it fell open to was lit by their own candle, and there—in faded brown ink, in a hand a century dead—was written:

*Lena Sandoval. This night. The debt comes due.*

Lena read it three times. Their first coherent thought was that this was a prank, and a bad one, because any forger worth their salt would have spelled "Sandoval" correctly on the first attempt. Their second thought arrived cold, all the way up from the cellar of them: *a century-old book does not know my name.*

They turned the page.

The handwriting changed—a different scribe, or perhaps the same one decades older, the letters grown cramped and hurried. It was an account, and Lena knew accounts the way priests know psalms. But no account had ever made their hands shake like this one.

*Received: the founding of the Guild of Impossible Things, in perpetuity.*
*Given: one soul, held in pledge by the Court Beneath the Mountain.*
*Terms: interest payable in memory. Principal redeemable by the debtor or his heirs.*

There followed a hundred years of entries. Small ones, mostly, entered with the tidy horror of a bookkeeper who has stopped asking questions. *Paid: the color of rain, from the town of Marrow's Ford.* *Paid: the taste of bread, from the baker Aldous Wren.* *Paid: her mother's face, from a girl whose name is withheld by request.*

Every year. Every year for a hundred years, the guild had reached out into the city and taken a little of somebody—taken it quietly, taken it without receipts—and carried it down to pay the interest on a debt most members had never heard of.

Lena sat down on the ladder step. The candle guttered. The storm-caller upstairs rolled over in his sleep, and somewhere a weathervane creaked, and none of it mattered, because Lena Sandoval's name was in the oldest book in the building, dated tonight, and the last line of the final page said:

*Principal due. One soul. The debtor being dust, the debt descends.*

Descended to whom? The founder had died childless—that much was guild legend, embroidered and possibly false. But debts, Lena thought with a rising sickness, do not care about bloodlines. Debts care about *ledgers*. And whoever kept the ledgers—

Whoever kept the ledgers would be named in the book. Wouldn't they? Wasn't that how it worked?

They turned back to the first page with numb fingers. Beneath the founder's flourish and the court's cold countersignature, small as an afterthought, was a line Lena had missed:

*Recorded by: L. Sandoval, keeper of accounts.*

Their own initials. In a century-old hand. In ink that matched nothing they had ever written, and everything they were about to.

* * *

That was when the knock came.

Three knocks, spaced with the terrible patience of people paid by the hour. Then a voice through the counting-room door, pleasant as a knife wrapped in wool:

"Scribe Sandoval. Guildmistress Vane requests your presence regarding certain irregularities."

Nobody called Lena Sandoval by name. Nobody *knew* Lena Sandoval's name; that had been the entire architecture of their life. Lena stood very still, candle held high, and watched the shadows of boots under the door—two pairs, standing the way enforcers stand, weight forward, patient as gravestones.

"Of course," Lena heard themselves say, in their flattest, most forgettable voice. "One moment. I am reconciling a moth."

Silence. Then, softly, the second voice: "She says the prophecy book is missing."

The word went through Lena like a fishhook. *Prophecy.* So the guild knew about the book. Had known. Had perhaps been waiting a century for the date on its page, the way you wait for a bill you cannot pay—and now the date was tonight, and the book was in the hands of the one person the ledger said must carry it.

"The moth," Lena called back, "has eaten several pages. I will need to consult the—"

The door handle turned. It did not open—the door was locked, and Lena had locked it hours ago out of pure habit, the habit of invisible people—but the handle turned anyway, slowly, testingly, like a finger probing a bruise.

Lena looked down at the book in their arms. Black leather, grey with age, warm as a heartbeat against their chest where they had pressed it without noticing. On the counter beside the cold tea lay the Tallow Ledger, third volume, four coppers adrift, a moth unaccounted for. A whole life arranged in neat columns. A life that had lasted exactly as long as nobody looked at it.

The handle turned again. Something in the lock clicked—not breaking, *deciding*.

Lena blew out the candle.

They went out through the window over the alchemy cellar, because everyone knows scribes cannot climb, and that assumption was the finest armor Lena Sandoval had ever owned. They dropped into the reek of burnt lavender, crossed the dark courtyard past the sleeping gargoyles (one of whom winked, but gargoyles keep better secrets than guilds), and slipped out the postern gate into streets still wet from evening rain.

Behind them, faintly, the counting-room door gave way. Voices rose, then quickened, then began calling a name down every corridor of the Guild of Impossible Things:

"Sandoval. *Sandoval.*"

Not invisible anymore, then. Never again invisible. Lena ran, clutching a century-old debt to their chest, toward the only place in the city where nobody would ever look for them—the ash-fields beyond the walls, where the air was too poor to burn and even hope packed its bags by sundown.

Where, if half the rumors were true, something old slept among the cinders.

Something with fire left in it. Barely. But some.

The moon rose over the ash-fields as Lena ran, and far beneath the mountain at the world's edge, Lena could not have said how they knew, but they knew: somewhere below, a court of bartered justice opened its eyes, checked its ledger, and smiled.

The debt had come due. And it had legs.
