## 2. What the Fire Remembers

The ash-fields began where the guild's maps politely gave up. Lena had copied enough survey documents to know the cartographers' trick: a decorative border of stylized mountains, and beyond it, nothing. Now they understood why. The ash-fields were nothing, or close to it—a plain of gray dunes that sighed and shifted underfoot, soft as flour, treacherous as debt.

Lena had been walking since the bells rang three. The prophecy book rode against their chest, wrapped in their coat, and every time their heart kicked, they imagined they could feel it kick back.

*One soul, owed to the court beneath the mountain,* the ledger had said. *Interest accruing.*

And beneath that, in ink gone brown as old blood: *Lena Sandoval. Tonight.*

They had not stopped shaking since. It was not the cold, though the cold was considerable. It was the particular vertigo of discovering that the life you had built—small, quiet, invisible—had been built on someone else's foundation, and the foundation had come due.

* * *

Emberkin smelled the scribe a mile off. Ink, tallow, fear, and under it all something the dragon had not smelled in sixty years: honesty. It was a rare vintage among the two-legged. Most of them came to the ash-fields reeking of ambition, wanting a scale for a charm or a tooth for a war, and Emberkin had eaten two of those and regretted only one.

This one smelled like a person who had never once been looked at properly.

The dragon lay coiled in the hollow of a dead caldera, gray as the fields around them, so still that the ash had drifted over their flanks like snow on a hearthstone that had gone cold. That was the joke of it, Emberkin thought, in the slow, bitter way dragons think jokes. The last dragon, disguised as a hill. Hiding in the one place no one wanted. It was, they supposed, a strategy they and the little scribe had in common.

The scribe crested the caldera's rim at dusk, saw the vast gray shape, and made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling.

"State your business," Emberkin rumbled, without lifting their head. "And be quick. I am dying, and I find I resent interruptions more than usual."

* * *

Lena's mouth went dry. Every story they had ever copied about dragons involved fire, gold, and a swift and educational death. This dragon offered none of the first two, and seemed too tired for the third.

"I—" Lena's voice cracked. They started over. "I need your fire. The gates of the court beneath the mountain are sealed, and the old texts say only dragonfire can unseal them."

"And the old texts," said the dragon, one eye opening at last—a slit of ember-orange in the gray face, banked but not dead—"say a great many things. Most of them flattering. All of them wrong." The eye fixed on Lena. "You are the one from the book."

Lena's stomach dropped through the ash. "You've read it?"

"I *am* in it. Page forty. *'And the last dragon shall gutter like a candle in a drafty hall.'* Your guild's founder had a gift for prophecy and a tin ear for poetry." Emberkin's nostrils flared, releasing a thread of smoke that smelled of old coals. "So. You want my fire. What will you give me for it?"

"I don't have anything."

"Everyone has something. That is the first law of the court beneath the mountain, and the second law of everywhere else." The dragon's head rose, at last, unfolding like a ship's sail. "I will take the truth. All of it. What the book says, what you know, what you suspect. Lies taste bad to me, and I am in no mood to be polite about it."

Lena sat down in the ash, because their legs had decided the matter without consulting them. And they told it—the ledger, the name, the date, the debt of one soul, the enforcers with their questions like drawn knives.

When they finished, the dragon was silent for a long moment. Then Emberkin laughed. It was a terrible sound, like a landslide learning to sing.

"Pawned," the dragon said. "Not sold."

"What?"

"Read it again, scribe. The precise wording. You're a scribe; words are your trade. What does the ledger actually say?"

Lena pulled the book from their coat and opened it with numb fingers, reading aloud: "*A soul delivered into the keeping of the court beneath the mountain, against a sum sufficient to the founding and perpetuity of the Guild of Impossible Things, redeemable by the bearer or their heirs—*"

"Or their heirs," Emberkin repeated. "A pawnshop receipt, dressed up in prophecy's Sunday clothes. Your founder didn't sell their soul. They *hocked* it. And the guild—" the dragon's voice dropped into something almost gentle, "—the guild has been paying the interest ever since. You didn't wonder why the Guild of Impossible Things has such trouble remembering its own history? Why your oldest members forget their mothers' names, their first loves, the faces of their children? Interest on a soul-debt is paid in memory, scribe. Your guild has been feeding the court its own past for a hundred years, one stolen recollection at a time, and calling it tradition."

Lena thought of Master Corvane, who could recite any contract verbatim but could not remember his own wedding day. Of old Sera Ives, who kept a portrait of a child in her desk and wept when asked whose it was. Of the guild's great vault of "archived memories," which no one was permitted to open, and which everyone had somehow agreed not to wonder about.

They were crying before they knew it. "They're not paying a debt. They're paying *rent*. Forever."

"Now you understand why the enforcers want the book back. A pawned soul can be redeemed. A sold one cannot. The difference between those two words is the difference between their power and their ruin." Emberkin lowered their great head until the ember eye was level with Lena's face. Warmth came off the dragon in waves, faint but real, like an oven long gone cold that still remembered bread. "So. Here is my price, and I will name it plainly: take me with you. My fire is dying, but it is not dead, and it will burn exactly once more. I would rather it burned open a door than guttered out warming my own scales."

"Why?" Lena asked. "Why help me?"

"Because you are invisible, little scribe, and I am the last of my kind, and we are both of us things the world agreed not to look at." The dragon's wing extended, an invitation and a bridge. "Belonging is a strange coin. It is only worth anything once you give it away. Climb."

* * *

They flew by night, because Emberkin's fire was brightest in the dark, and because the enforcers would not think to look up. Lena rode in the hollow behind the dragon's neck, the prophecy book lashed to their chest, and watched the ash-fields fall away beneath them like a gray tide going out.

Emberkin flew slower than any dragon in the old stories. Each wingbeat cost something visible—a dimming along the spine, a graying at the wingtips, as if the ash-fields were climbing up onto the dragon and claiming them inch by inch.

"Talk to me," Emberkin said, when the silence grew heavy. "It keeps the fire fed. Fire runs on more than fat and fury. It runs on *wanting*."

So Lena talked. About the guild, and the small safe life of an invisible scribe. About how they had chosen the shadows, and how the shadows had chosen them back, and how neither arrangement had ever quite felt like a choice.

"I used to think being unseen was the same as being safe," Lena said.

"And now?"

"Now I think safety was just loneliness with good accounting."

The dragon made a low sound that might have been laughter. "When we are done," Emberkin said, "and if we are done, and when my fire has gone where fires go—there is a garden gate on the mountain's southern face. The old stories say a crown grows there. I would like to see it. I would like, at the end, to have been *toward* something instead of merely *away*."

"I'll take you," Lena said. "I promise."

"Careful, scribe. Promises are the court's favorite currency." The ember eye rolled back to fix on them, and in it Lena saw their own small reflection, held and lit, seen fully for perhaps the first time in their life. "But I accept."

Below them, the mountain rose out of the dark—and at its base, torches. A line of them, moving with the terrible coordinated patience of guild enforcers, converging on the only gate.

"They followed the ash-trail," Emberkin rumbled. "Clever, for people who keep forgetting things."

Lena gripped the dragon's neck. "Can you outrun them?"

"No," said Emberkin, and folded their wings, and began to fall toward the firelight. "But I can land *on* them."
