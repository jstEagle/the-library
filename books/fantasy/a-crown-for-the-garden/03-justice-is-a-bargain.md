## 3. Justice Is a Bargain

The mountain did not have doors so much as it had moods.

Lena learned this on the second hour of the descent, when Emberkin's fire — held cupped in the dragon's throat like an old woman holding her last coin — licked against a wall of black stone and the stone *considered* it. Considered it at length. Considered it with the leisurely insolence of a customs clerk who has already decided to ruin your afternoon.

"It's thinking," Lena said.

"Everything down here thinks," Emberkin rumbled. The vibration of it traveled up through Lena's boots and settled somewhere behind their ribs, where it intended to stay. "The stone thinks. The dark thinks. I once met a puddle with strong opinions about taxation."

"What did it think?"

"That everything should be taxed except puddles."

The fire flared — a stubborn, guttering flare, the flame of a candle that has made peace with its own ending — and the stone sighed, all its grains realigning like a crowd parting for someone important. Or someone *owed to*. A passage opened, breathing out air that smelled of ink and iron and something older underneath, something like the inside of a locked drawer.

Lena hugged the prophecy book tighter against their chest. They had carried it through the ash-fields, over the glass flats, down switchback roads that no map admitted to. Their hands had memorized its weight. Their thumbs knew the exact spot where the cover was scorched, where a century ago some other scribe had pressed too hard while writing a name that would not be read until tonight.

*One soul, owed to the court beneath the mountain.*

And beneath that, in smaller script, almost apologetic: *Interest to accrue in memories, collected quarterly.*

"You're doing the thing," Emberkin said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you go quiet and small and convince yourself you're not here. I can smell it. It smells like unsent letters."

"I don't—" Lena started, and then stopped, because it was true, and because lying to a dragon whose fire could taste truth seemed unwise even by their standards. "I spent eleven years being invisible, Emberkin. On purpose. It's a skill. I'm very good at it. And now every step I take toward this court is a step toward being *seen*, and I don't know how to do that without dropping something."

"Dropping what?"

"I don't know yet." Lena looked down into the dark ahead. "That's the terrifying part."

Emberkin made a sound that might have been laughter if laughter were made of gravel and regret. "Good," the dragon said. "Fear means you're carrying something worth dropping."

* * *

They descended through the dragon's own light, which was a strange way to travel — walking always at the edge of a warmth that was visibly spending itself. Emberkin's scales had gone from ember-orange to the dull rose of a hearth at dawn. Every gate they melted open cost the dragon something Lena didn't want to name aloud, and Emberkin, who noticed everything, noticed them not naming it and was gracious enough not to make them.

The passages changed as they went deeper. First stone, then stone carved with ledgers — actual ledgers, columns of figures chiseled into the walls, debts recorded in rock because paper burns and rock merely *waits*. Then the carvings became judgments. Lena read them as they passed, their scribe's eye unable to help itself:

*Owed: one first kiss, never given. Paid in full, 40 years' service to the ferryman.*

*Owed: an apology, 300 years outstanding. Compounded. The debtor's descendants weep salt; the court accepts salt.*

*Owed: one soul. Pawned, not sold. Redeemable by equal price freely given.*

There it was again. *Pawned, not sold.* The founder hadn't thrown their soul into the dark — they'd set it on a shelf, like a coat checked at a door, and walked out wearing the guild's survival instead. And the guild, rather than redeeming it, had let the interest run. Decades of stolen memories, skimmed from members who signed their contracts without reading the clause about *quarterly adjustments*. Lena thought of old Master Corvin, who could no longer remember his mother's face. Of Junie, who'd forgotten why she loved bells. Of themselves, choosing invisibility so thoroughly that perhaps the choice hadn't been entirely theirs.

"We're close," Emberkin said quietly. The fire in their throat had shrunk to something the size of a fist. "I can smell escrow. It smells like a bank vault full of sleeping people."

"That's horrifying."

"Yes. Most true things are."

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was not what Lena expected, which was a throne room, or a pit, or anything with edges. Instead it was a garden gone to seed — vast, cavernous, lit by veins of dim gold in the ceiling, filled with rows of glass vessels, each one holding a small slow light. Souls in escrow. Some burned steady. Some flickered. One, near the center, on a pedestal marked with the Guild of Impossible Things' seal — a quill biting its own tail — glowed the particular warm amber of a soul that had been patient for a hundred years and had not enjoyed it.

Before the pedestal stood three figures in grey, and Lena's stomach dropped, because they knew those coats. Guild enforcers. Vess and the twins, who had once walked Lena home through a rainstorm and bought them chestnuts, and who now stood between them and the amber light with expressions of genuine, pained apology.

"Lena." Vess spread her hands. She sounded tired. She sounded, horribly, kind. "You've made this so much harder than it needed to be. Put down the book. Walk away. We'll tell the Guildmasters you died in the ash-fields. You'll be free. No debt, no ledger, no quarterly collections. You can be invisible again. Isn't that what you wanted?"

And there it was. The offer, gift-wrapped, exactly shaped to fit the hollow place in Lena's chest. Eleven years of careful nothing, offered back like a kindness.

Behind them, Emberkin's fire gave a thin, crackling cough.

"You'd let the debt stand," Lena said. "The founder stays on the shelf. The memories keep getting skimmed. Junie forgets her bells. Corvin forgets his mother."

"The guild survives," Vess said. "That's the whole point of it. That's what she built it *for*."

"She built it to survive," Lena agreed. "But she paid for it with a pawn ticket, and nobody's ever gone back for the coat." They stepped forward, past the point where sensible people stopped stepping forward, and their voice came out steadier than they felt, steadier than they had ever felt in their careful invisible life. "Vess. What's the oldest law of this court? It's written on your own walls. I read it on the way down."

Vess's jaw tightened. "You don't know what you're—"

"*Any soul may be ransomed,*" Lena said, loud enough that the gold veins in the ceiling seemed to lean in, "*by an equal price, freely given.*"

The garden went still. Even the flickering souls in their glass seemed to hold their breath.

"And I'm giving one," Lena said. "Freely. Right now. Before witnesses." They turned to the pedestal, to the amber light that had waited a century, and then — because it deserved to be said to someone — turned back to Vess and the twins. "You want to know what I treasured? What I spent eleven years building? Not safety. Not comfort. *Unseenness.* Nobody looking at me. Nobody expecting anything. Nobody able to hurt me, because nobody knew where I kept myself." Their voice cracked, and they let it. "That's my price. My invisibility. Take it off the scale and put hers back."

The pedestal's amber light pulsed — once, twice — like a heart remembering its job.

A voice came from everywhere, from the stone and the soil and the glass, dry as a ledger closing: *"An odd price. But the law does not weigh quality. Only equality. Is it freely given?"*

"It's freely given," Lena said, and meant it, and the meaning of it went through them like a key turning.

*"Then the scales accept. The pawn is redeemed. The debt of the Guild of Impossible Things is—"*

The voice stopped.

The gold veins in the ceiling flickered. Somewhere far above, faint but unmistakable, came a sound like a great door being knocked upon from the outside — a sound Lena had heard only once before, in the prophecy book's final pages, described in handwriting that shook:

*When the last fire dies, the gates open both ways.*

Beside them, Emberkin sank slowly onto their belly, and the last of the fire left the dragon's throat — not spent, Lena realized, but *given*, poured downward through the mountain's roots toward gates that had been waiting a hundred years for a key.

"Oh," Lena whispered, watching the light go out of their friend. "Oh no. Emberkin—"

The dragon's eyes found theirs, ancient and amused and utterly unafraid.

"Don't you dare grieve yet, little scribe," Emberkin said. "You haven't seen what my fire remembers how to grow."
