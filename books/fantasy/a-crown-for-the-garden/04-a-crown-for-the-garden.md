## 4. A Crown for the Garden

The scales of the court beneath the mountain were not made of brass or bone, as Lena had imagined in the long dark of the descent. They were made of names.

Lena felt it the moment the ransom was spoken aloud: their own name lifting out of them like a thread drawn from a hem. *Lena Sandoval, scribe, second desk by the window, invisible.* The court took hold of that thread and weighed it against another — *Vessaline Corrigan, founder, first desk by no window at all, sold a century past and pawned ever since* — and somewhere in the deep quiet between the two pans, something ancient and patient considered the exchange.

"Invisibility," the magistrate said. Its voice was the sound of ledgers closing. "Freely given?"

"Freely given," Lena said. Their voice cracked on the word, but they said it again anyway. "Freely given."

Beside them, Emberkin lay with her head flat against the cold stone, her fire down to a coal no bigger than a candleflame. She had carried Lena through gates that had not opened in a hundred years; she had burned away the seals that three generations of guild-magic could not touch. Now she watched the scales with one amber eye half-lidded, the way old creatures watch things they have waited a very long time to see.

"You understand," the magistrate said, "what you are trading. The court will not know you. The world will not know you. You will walk through crowds like weather."

"I've been practicing," Lena said, "for twenty-six years."

Something that might have been amusement moved through the dark. The scales tipped.

And Vessaline Corrigan came home.

There was no fanfare. That was the strange part. Lena had expected light, trumpets, some great rending of stone. Instead there was simply a warmth that spread through the chamber like sun through a window, and a presence standing where nothing had stood — a woman in a scribe's coat gone soft with age, ink-stained to the elbow, looking around the court beneath the mountain with the expression of someone who has finally been let out of a very long meeting.

"Well," said the founder of the Guild of Impossible Things. "That took longer than my notes suggested it would."

Emberkin made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and smoke curled from her nostrils in a shape that might once have been a word.

"The debt is paid," the magistrate intoned. "The pawn redeemed. The ledger closes." A pause, vast and deliberate. "All ledgers close."

Lena did not understand what that meant until later. At the time, they were too busy watching the last of Emberkin's fire gutter — too busy kneeling beside her, pressing their forehead to the great ridged brow, saying all the foolish things people say when something they love is ending.

"Don't," Emberkin murmured. Her voice was embers now, barely a glow. "Don't waste your last moments in here being sad at me. I have been dying for two hundred years, little scribe. Tonight I got to be *useful*. Do you know how rare that is? For either of us?"

"You could have had more time. If we'd found another way—"

"There is always another way." Her eye found Lena's, amber going grey at the rim. "And it is always worse. Hush now. I want to see the garden."

"The garden's above us. You'll never—"

But Emberkin was already gathering herself, and Lena understood: not the garden above the mountain. The gate. The great sealed doors of the court, carved with vines that had never bloomed, stone flowers that had waited a century for a reason.

The dragon drew one breath — the last breath, the whole of what remained of her fire gathered into it like a held note — and breathed it across the gate.

The stone bloomed.

It happened the way frost happens, only in reverse: color racing through grey carving, petals unfurling from rock as if rock had only ever been a flower pretending to sleep. Ivy went green and living up the doorframe. Roses the size of dinner plates opened along the arch. And at the crown of the gate, where the founders' sigil had been, a single branch burst into blossom so bright it lit the whole chamber gold.

Emberkin watched it happen with both eyes open. Then she laid her head down on the warm stone, in the light of the flowers she had made, and closed them.

"There," she said. Or perhaps she only thought it. Either way, Lena heard it, and kept it, and never told anyone the exact wording, because some things belong to the person who was there.

* * *

Coming up out of the mountain was like being born slowly, in installments.

The garden gate opened onto morning — actual morning, though Lena had lost track of how many days the descent had eaten. The guild's ash-fields stretched below, grey and ordinary, and beyond them the rooftops of the city, and beyond those the sea. Lena stood in the blossoming doorway with the prophecy book under one arm and the founder at their side, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

Then Vessaline said, "I owe you an apology. And possibly several decades of back wages."

"You owe me nothing. I traded for you."

"I know what I'm owed. I invented most of this guild's accounting." She squinted at the ash-fields. "Is that my guild hall? It's *crooked*."

"It leans," Lena admitted. "It's been leaning since roughly the year after you left."

"Mm. I'll fix it." She started down the slope, then paused. "Come along, Sandoval. You're the only person alive who knows what I look like, and I find I'd rather not be invisible today. I've heard it's terrible."

Lena laughed — a real laugh, startled out of them — and followed.

They reached the guild hall by midmorning, and that was where the strangeness began, because the enforcers were already there. All twelve of them, kneeling in the courtyard in a rough crescent, their badges of office set on the ground before them like offerings. Their leader, a broad woman named Hesk whose face Lena knew mostly from the wrong end of a lantern beam, looked up as they entered — looked *at* Lena, fully, without the sliding-away that had met every glance Lena had ever earned — and her expression was not anger.

It was relief.

"They came to us at dawn," Vessaline murmured. "Didn't you."

"The ledgers burned," Hesk said hoarsely. "All of them. Every debt in the guild, every bond, every interest-note — gone to ash in their cabinets, no flame, no smoke. Just... finished. And we felt it. What we were. What we'd been carrying for other people's profit." She swallowed. "We didn't know who to kneel to. And then the mountain bloomed, and we thought — maybe her. Maybe whoever brought her home."

"You may stand up," Vessaline said. "All of you. Kneeling is for courts, and I have just spent a century learning exactly how much I hate courts." She surveyed the courtyard, the crooked hall, the stunned faces turning toward her — toward *both* of them — with something dangerously close to tears in her eyes. "Right. First order of business: breakfast. Second: we are going to rewrite everything. Third—" she glanced sideways at Lena, and her voice gentled, "—third, somebody tell me this young scribe's name, because I intend to put it in the founding charter, and I refuse to get it wrong."

"Lena Sandoval," said a dozen voices at once, and Lena, who had spent a lifetime being the person nobody quite looked at, discovered that being seen by a crowd feels less like standing in a spotlight and more like standing in sunlight. Warmth everywhere at once. Nowhere to hide, and — for the first time — nowhere they wanted to be.

* * *

They planted the book in autumn.

Not burned it, though the guild voted on burning, twice. But Lena argued for planting — stood up at the long table, in front of forty faces that all knew their name now, and said that a book which had predicted one impossible thing deserved the chance to predict others, and that seeds were simply prophecies that hadn't sprouted yet. Vessaline had backed them immediately, on the grounds that she'd founded a Guild of Impossible Things and it would be embarrassing to flinch at one more.

So they dug a hole behind the new greenhouse, in soil Emberkin's ashes had been folded into over the summer, and laid the prophecy book in it cover-down, like tucking in a child.

"What do you think will grow?" asked Pip, the youngest apprentice, who had appointed herself Lena's shadow within a week of the mountain and showed no signs of resigning the post.

"Dunno," Lena said. "That's rather the point."

They watered it anyway, every week, all winter, though nothing showed. It was Pip who spotted the first shoot in early spring — a pale green curl pushing up through the ash-dark soil, uncurling leaf by leaf into a shape none of the guild's botanists recognized and all of its scribes immediately began trying to catalog.

Lena crouched beside it in the cold morning, hands dirty, name known, debt paid, dragon mourned and honored in a stained-glass window over the reading room (the artist had given her far too dignified an expression; Emberkin would have laughed herself sick).

The seedling put out a third leaf. On it, faint as watermark, was a line of writing no one else could see:

*Chapter five begins with a knock at the door.*

Lena smiled, stood, and went inside to wait for it.
