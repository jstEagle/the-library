## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bees did not care that Edmund Brightwater had once pulled lightning out of a clear summer sky.

They cared about the clover, the warmth of the morning stone, the slow gold work of the season. They climbed over her gloved fingers with the blunt indifference of small gods, and she loved them for it. Ten years in the Hollow had taught her that the only worship worth keeping was the kind that asked nothing back.

"Easy," she murmured to the hive, easing the frame loose. "Easy, now. I'm not taking anything you'll miss."

The valley lay below her in its usual silence—the kind of silence that had weight, that pressed down like snow. The Hollow was a cup of green land folded between two gray shoulders of mountain, and nothing in it rang true. Bells sounded dull here. Rivers made less noise than they should have. And magic—magic could not follow her past the ridgeline. She had tested it the first year, desperately, secretly, standing in the high passes with her hands open to the sky, and the sky had simply declined her. It was like praying into a locked room. She had come down the mountain weeping with relief and grief in equal measure, and she had never gone back up to try again.

That was the bargain of the Hollow: it forgot you, and in exchange, it let you be forgotten.

She set the frame back and worked in the rhythm she had worn smooth as river stone over a decade. Smoke, lift, look, replace. The village of Marrow's Ford called her the bee-widow, though no one remembered whose widow she was supposed to be. She had let them. She had given them a story in pieces—a husband dead of fever, a house up the slope, a woman who kept to herself and sold good honey and never, ever sang. She had even taken a name that wasn't hers: Eda. Eda of the hives. It fit her like a borrowed coat, and after ten years she had stopped noticing the sleeves were too long.

The morning was fine. The morning was always fine. That was the Hollow's other gift: no storms came here. Weather arrived already tamed, wrung out over the mountains, and fell on the valley as gentle rain or soft snow, like a letter someone else had opened first. Edmund had spent the first two years flinching at every cloud, and the last eight learning to sleep through thunder that happened somewhere far away, in the real world, where weather still had teeth.

She was closing the third hive when she heard the bells.

Not the chapel bell—that rang dull and short, as everything did here. This was a sound underneath the sound, a resonance in the stone of the valley itself, and Edmund's hands went still inside the gloves because her body remembered it before her mind did. That was the feel of a prayer being answered. She had heard it a thousand times in Vessany, in the storm-temples, in her mother's voice on the high days: the low thrum of the world leaning down to listen.

It had not happened in ten years. It had not happened anywhere, as far as she knew. The gods had gone silent the same season Vessany was erased—struck dumb all across the world, as if the same knife that cut her country out of the maps had cut the tongue out of heaven.

The thrum faded. Edmund stood in the bee-yard with her heart slamming against her ribs and told herself she had imagined it. She was good at that. She had practiced.

She was coiling the smoker's rope when the dog from the lower farm began to bark—not the lazy bark of a stranger on the road, but the high, urgent voice of something wrong—and then she saw him, down where the bee-yard path met the cart track: a man on foot, walking wrong, the walk of someone holding themselves upright by will alone. He came up the track in a lurching, stubborn line, and even at a distance she could see the state of him. Mud to the thighs. A coat scorched along one side. Blood on his face, dried in dark rivers from the hairline, as if he had bled from the scalp and kept walking.

He was not from the Hollow. No one came to the Hollow. The valley sat three days from any real road, down a track the maps marked with a cartographer's shrug, and the people here liked it that way. Edmund had chosen it for exactly that reason.

She should have gone inside. She knew it even as her feet carried her down the path instead. Ten years of discipline, ten years of being small and dull and harmless, and it all came apart at the sight of a bleeding stranger, because underneath the bee-widow there was still a Brightwater, and Brightwaters did not watch people die on their doorstep if they could help it.

He collapsed at the gate.

She got an arm under him—lighter than he looked, fever-light—and dragged him into the shade of the eaves. Up close the scorch on his coat was worse, a great burned crescent across his back, and she thought, with a cold drop in her stomach, *fire from above, that's what that is, that's what a strike from a clear sky does*—and then she made herself stop thinking it.

"Water," he said. His lips were cracked gray. "Please. I've come a long way."

She brought water. He drank like a man who had forgotten how, spilling half down his chin, and she knelt beside him in the dust and did the arithmetic she didn't want to do: the burns, the blood, the fever-brightness of his eyes. Three days' walk from the road. He had come here on purpose.

"Who sent you?" she asked. Her voice came out flat. Bee-widow flat. "Who told you where I am?"

His eyes found her then, and they were clear for a moment, terribly clear, the way dying men's eyes are. "No one sent me," he said. "I didn't know. I swear it. I've been walking since the lowlands, following—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Following a voice."

"Whose voice?"

"A god's." He said it simply, without shame, the way you'd say *the sun*. "Vessan. The old one. The storm-father." He laughed, and it turned into a cough. "They'll tell you he's dead. They've been telling us that for ten years. But three nights ago, on the road, I prayed—just out of habit, just the way my grandmother taught me, the old words—and something *answered*."

The ground did not move. The air did not change. But Edmund felt the sentence land in her chest like a struck bell, like the thrum in the stone that morning, and she knew—she knew with the certainty of her own name—that he was not lying.

Vessan. Her god. Her mother's god. The god whose name was the first half of her country's own, the storm-father who had walked the thunderheads over Vessany since before the kings of the lowlands had learned to write. The god who had gone silent the night the mages burned, the night the borders blurred like wet ink, the night Edmund had run with nothing but her mother's hymnbook and a scar on her palm and a name she could never say aloud again.

"Answered how?" she heard herself ask.

"Thunder." He smiled, and there was blood on his teeth. "Out of a clear sky, over the lowlands. One roll, long, like—" his hand fluttered, searching, "—like a door opening somewhere. And then a voice, or not a voice. A *knowing*. It said—" He closed his eyes. Recited, and his accent cracked her heart open, because it was Vessanyan, the lowland drawl over the old vowels, the accent of the diaspora, the accent of everyone who had ever escaped: "*The door is fraying. Come home.*"

Edmund did not move. Inside her, something that had been buried for ten years under smoke and honey and careful dullness turned over in its grave.

"That's not possible," she said. "There's no home to come to."

"Then where did the thunder come from?" he asked gently, as if she were the one wandering lost. "I've been walking toward it ever since. It keeps—pulling. Like a fish on a line. And the voice keeps saying the same thing. *Come home, come home.* I thought—" the fever took him again, his eyes going glassy, "—I thought if I followed it far enough it would lead me to Vessany. But Vessany isn't anywhere. I've looked. There's just a valley where it used to be, and the valley is full of winter, and the winter is wrong, it's *hungry*, and the witch-finders burned the last village that sang about it, and I ran, and I prayed again, and the god said *the Hollow, the Hollow, she will know the door—*"

He stopped. His eyes came back to her, and this time they stayed, and she saw him see her—the scar on her palm where a storm-oath had been sworn, the way she stood, the thing under her skin that even the Hollow's silence couldn't quite smother.

"You," he whispered. "You're one of hers. One of Vessany's."

"I'm a beekeeper," Edmund said.

But the words came out wrong. They came out in Vessanyan, the old tongue rising off her tongue like water from a cracked well, and the traveler wept when he heard it, openly, like a child, and reached for her hand with his burned one.

"Then you know," he breathed. "You know the hymn. Sing it. Please. I've walked three hundred miles to hear it sung by someone who—"

"Stop." She pulled her hand back. Her heart was a fist in her chest. "Stop it. You're fevered. You don't know what you're asking."

"I know exactly what I'm asking." His grip, though weak, did not let go of her sleeve. "The god said *she will know the door*. Not *a* door. *The* door. You know what that means. You must know what that means."

She knew. She had known since the thunder that morning, since the thrum in the stone. The Door. The border-spell. The thing the crown's mages had built out of stolen worship and the blood of her people, the thing that had folded Vessany out of the world like a letter into an envelope—and if it was fraying, if the gods were leaking through the seams, then everything she had buried was coming up out of the ground like a flood, and the Hollow would not hold, and the bee-widow would not hold, and ten years of careful silence was already, right now, in this moment, at an end.

"Rest," she said hoarsely. "Rest, and then you'll go back the way you came."

He smiled at her with blood on his teeth, and his eyes were already somewhere else, already walking the far road. "You sing it first," he said. "Then we'll see who goes where."

* * *

He died at dusk.

It was quick, in the end, the fever taking him between one breath and the next, and Edmund sat beside him in the lamplight and closed his eyes and did the things you do for the dead. She washed his face. She folded his scorched coat over him. She could not bury him until morning—the ground in the Hollow was stubborn, like everything else here—so she covered him with her good quilt, the one with the pattern her mother had stitched, the one with the storm-spirals she had told the village were just flowers.

Then she sat in her kitchen with her hands around a cup of cold tea, and the house was very quiet, and the quiet had a shape she recognized. It was the shape of Vessany's last night. It was the shape of every silence she had ever hidden inside.

*The door is fraying. Come home.*

She had spent ten years learning to be no one. She had unlearned her own name, her own tongue, her own sky. And all it had taken to undo it was one dying stranger with a god's words in his mouth, and now she sat in the ruins of her careful life and found that underneath the bee-widow, underneath Eda of the hives, Edmund Brightwater was still there—whole, and grieving, and *listening*.

Because that was the terrible thing. The thing she could not say to the dead man, could not say to anyone. When he had spoken the god's words, some traitorous part of her had not been afraid.

It had been *homesick*.

Outside, far over the mountains, in the real world where weather still had teeth, thunder rolled—long and low and patient, like something knocking at a door.

And in the stone beneath her house, in the silence of the Hollow itself, Edmund felt the valley listen back.
