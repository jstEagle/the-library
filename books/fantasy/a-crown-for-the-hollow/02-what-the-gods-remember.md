## 2. What the Gods Remember

The traveler died at dawn, as Edmund had known he would from the moment she'd laid her hands against his chest and felt the hollowness there, the way a winter field feels hollow under its crust of snow. She had done what could be done—cleaned the wound in his side with boiled water and honey, bound it with linen, kept a fire going through the long hours while he shivered and muttered. But the wound had gone bad before he ever stumbled into the Hollow, and whatever god had answered his prayer had not seen fit to answer her plea for his life.

He died quietly, which was more than most did. Near the end his muttering resolved into words again, that same Vessanyan phrase she had heard when he first fell through her door: *Svetlana veth ira domne.* The god remembers the door. His eyes had found hers then, clear for the first time in days, and he had smiled—a small, astonished smile, the smile of a man hearing his mother's voice after twenty years.

"You speak it," he whispered. "You dream in it."

"I don't," Edmund said.

But they both knew she was lying, and then he was gone, and the lie didn't matter anymore.

She buried him on the slope behind her cottage, where the bees would find the wild thyme come summer, and she marked the grave with a flat stone because she could not bring herself to leave him nameless. She still didn't know his name. He had carried no papers; witch-finders burned papers along with their owners. On the stone she carved nothing but a single line in Vessanyan, the old script she hadn't written in ten years, her hand remembering the strokes the way a hand remembers a ladder climbed daily in childhood: *He prayed, and someone listened.*

Then she went inside, scrubbed the blood from her floorboards, and told herself that was the end of it. A dying man. A strange prayer. A coincidence dressed up in thunder.

The valley did not agree.

* * *

That night, for the first time in ten years, Edmund dreamed in Vessanyan.

Not the language itself—not exactly—but fragments of it, drifting up through the dark like bubbles through deep water. A word for *threshold*. A word for *the space between two breaths*. A hymn-verse she half-remembered from her grandmother's kitchen, sung low over rising bread:

*Domne svet ira, domne keth—*
*the door is not a wall, the door is a mouth—*

She woke with the taste of the words still in her ears, syllables she had not spoken since she was nineteen years old and running south with smoke behind her, and she lay in the grey light listening to her own heart and feeling something move beneath it that she had spent a decade convincing herself was dead.

It happened again the second night. And the third. Each time the fragments came a little clearer, a little closer to the surface, as though something were standing just outside the walls of her sleep, pressing its face to the glass. Not threatening. Patient. The way a dog waits at a gate it knows will open eventually.

On the fourth night she dreamed of the sea.

There was no sea anywhere near Vessany—it was a country of high meadows and black pine forests, of rivers the color of cold iron—but in the dream there was a sea, and it was made of voices. Thousands upon thousands of them, layered and braided, singing in Vessanyan, and she understood without being told that this was what a god sounded like when it was whole: not one voice but a congregation, a nation's worth of prayer woven into a single vast attention.

And the sea was draining away. She could hear it going, voice by voice, the song thinning at its edges, and beneath the thinning came another sound—the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep, beginning to wake, and a voice that was not a voice saying, in the space between her heartbeat and the next:

*Svetlana veth ira domne.*

The god remembers the door.

Edmund woke sitting upright, her hands burning—actually burning, a faint heat in her palms like sun-struck stone—and outside her window the bees were stirring in the hive though it was hours before dawn, humming a note she felt in her teeth.

"All right," she said aloud, to the empty room, to whatever was listening. "All right. I'll go."

* * *

Ilya lived at the far edge of the Hollow, a day's walk if you took the shepherd paths, in a lean-to cottage against the cliff face with a chapel ruin leaning over it like a drunk uncle. The chapel had been Catholic once, or something close enough—the valley's founders had been fleeing their own gods, a century back, and had built small and plain—but it had been roofless for longer than anyone living could remember, and Ilya used the nave to dry herbs and the altar slab to sharpen knives.

Edmund had traded with him for years. Honey for tallow, beeswax for salt. They spoke perhaps forty words to each other in an average season, and both of them preferred it that way. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with a beard he never trimmed so much as abandoned, and eyes that had the particular flatness of a man who has looked at something once and decided never to look at it again.

He was splitting wood when she came up the path, and he saw her face before she said anything, and set down the axe.

"No," he said.

"I haven't asked you anything."

"You walked a full day to ask nothing." He wiped his palms on his apron. "Come inside before you say it out loud. Walls here are deaf, but habits die hard."

Inside, among the hanging bundles of yarrow and St. John's wort, she told him. The traveler. The prayer that landed like thunder. The dreams, the fragments, the sea of voices draining away. She told it flatly, in the tone of a woman reporting a broken fence, and her hands would not stay still in her lap, and she hated them for it.

Ilya listened without moving. When she finished he stood, went to the hearth, and fed the fire a knot of pine until the room filled with resin-smoke, and only then did he speak.

"Say the phrase. The one he kept saying."

"*Svetlana veth ira domne.*"

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small contraction around the eyes, and turned back to her, and she saw that the flatness in his gaze had cracked and underneath it was something older and much worse.

"That's not a folk-prayer," he said. "That's liturgy. First verse of the Opening of the Door, from the Vessanyan rite. It hasn't been spoken aloud in ten years because everyone who knew it is dead or scattered, and because speaking it was made a capital crime by decree of the crown." He sat down heavily on his stool. "Where did you learn it?"

"My grandmother. Before."

"Before," he repeated softly. "Yes."

"Ilya." She leaned forward. "Gods don't answer prayers anymore. That's the whole architecture of the world now. The crown burned the mages, the priests, the shrines—ten years ago, everywhere, all at once—and after that the gods went silent. Everyone accepted it. Everyone *had* to accept it. So explain to me why a dying smuggler's prayer got answered in a valley where magic can't follow."

"Because silence isn't death," Ilya said. "A god that's silenced is a god that's bound. And bindings—" he looked into the fire, and his voice dropped as though the flames might repeat him, "—bindings can be unbound. From either end."

"What does that mean?"

"It means somebody is working at the knot." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Edmund. You're asking me how I know these things, and I'm going to tell you, and when I tell you, you're going to wish you'd stayed home with your bees. The binding that silenced the old gods wasn't a weapon. Weapons are loud. It was a spell of forgetting, woven into every shrine, every hymnbook, every grave-marker across three kingdoms. It didn't kill Vessany. It erased it—from maps, from records, from memory. People who'd traded in Vessanyan ports their whole lives couldn't tell you the country's name. Its gods weren't slain; they were locked outside the world's attention, and a god nobody attends starves toward sleep."

Edmund's mouth had gone dry. "You talk about it like you were there."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, in the ruined nave, wind moved through the empty window-frames with a sound almost like breath.

"I was there," Ilya said. "Not at the casting—I was nobody, a parish priest with doubts and a drinking problem. But my order wrote the liturgy the binding was threaded through. We were told it was mercy. End the war, end the purges, end the burning, by making there be nothing left to burn. One generation of forgetting, and grief would have nothing to hold on to." He laughed, short and joyless. "I believed it. God forgive me, I believed it right up until the night I watched a village forget its own dead. Mothers who couldn't remember their children's faces. That's when I defrocked myself, before they could do it properly. That's why I live at the edge of a valley where nothing holy can reach me. I didn't flee the crown, Edmund. I fled the consequences of my own pen."

The silence stretched between them, long enough that the fire settled twice.

"And now?" Edmund said finally. "Now the gods are waking. Someone's unbinding them. Who?"

"I don't know. But I know what it means." He met her eyes, and she saw he was afraid in a way that had nothing to do with himself. "A binding that size doesn't fray on its own. Either the spell is failing—which means everything it held back comes loose at once—or someone is deliberately pulling threads, and they've started with the gods because the gods are the loudest part. Either way, the Hollow won't stay hollow much longer. This valley is quiet because the binding's shadow falls over it too. If the binding goes—"

"—magic comes back," Edmund finished. Her hands were burning again. She looked down at them, at the faint light in her palms she'd been pretending not to see for four days. "Everything comes back. Including everything that hunts people like me."

"Yes."

She stood. Walked to the doorway. Outside, the evening lay blue and cold over the valley, the bees quiet, the pines black against a sky where the first stars were coming out—and for the first time in ten years she looked at that sky and did not see safety. She saw a lid.

"The traveler," she said, without turning around. "Before he died, he said I dreamed in Vessanyan. He was right. Something's been talking to me in my sleep, Ilya. Teaching me. Verse by verse, like a child learning a hymn." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Why me? There are a thousand exiles scattered across the lowlands. Why press your face against my window?"

Behind her, Ilya was quiet for so long she almost turned.

"Because a door opens from the side of the one who remembers it," he said at last. "And you, Edmund Brightwater, are the last person alive who remembers Vessany from the inside."

The words landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard the echo of them going down and down, past ten careful years, past the buried grief, past the girl with smoke behind her, all the way to the bottom where something very old and very patient had been waiting.

She should have run, then. She knew that later, looking back. She should have walked home, sealed her doors, stopped her ears, and let the world do whatever the world was going to do.

Instead she heard herself say: "Then we'd better find out who's pulling the threads. Before the witch-finders do."

Behind her, Ilya rose, and began, grimly, to pack.
