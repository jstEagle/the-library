## 3. Witch-Finders at the Ford

The river told Ilya before the riders did.

He had lived at the valley's edge long enough to know its moods—the way the water went glass-still when frost crept down from the high passes, the way it muttered over its stones when rain gathered in the west. On the morning the witch-finders came, it did neither. It simply stopped singing. The current slid past his knees as he knelt to fill his bucket, and for a long moment there was nothing in the sound of it at all, no voice, no argument between water and stone. Just movement without meaning.

Ilya had heard that silence once before. He had helped write it.

He stood slowly, water dripping from his hands, and looked downstream toward the ford where the lowland road crossed into the Hollow. Three riders were coming through the shallows, their horses stepping high and delicate over the stones as though the water offended them. They wore the grey-and-gold of the crown's Inquisitorial Office, and even at this distance Ilya could see the instruments they carried: iron censers swinging from saddle horns, thin chains that held no incense but something heavier, something that drank the light.

*They've come for her,* he thought, and the thought arrived with a strange, exhausted calm, like a debt collector who has finally found the right door. *Ten years. They've come for the Brightwater girl.*

He set down his bucket and walked up the bank to meet them, because running would have been a confession, and because some old, buried part of him—the part that had once stood in the great scriptorium at Karavel and written binding-law in ink mixed with consecrated ash—still believed that a priest's place was between danger and the innocent. Even a defrocked priest. Especially one.

* * *

The leader was younger than Ilya expected. That was the first cruelty of it. He had imagined witch-finders as old men, scarred and half-mad, men who had stared too long into the fires they tended. But the one who dismounted at the ford was perhaps thirty, clean-shaven, with careful eyes and a voice like a man reading a legal document aloud. His companions said nothing at all. One of them never blinked enough.

"Father," the young man said, inclining his head. "Or were you stripped of it?"

"Stripped," Ilya said. "You have me at an advantage."

"Verren Corhane." He did not offer a hand. "Inquisitor Second Rank. We're asking after travelers. A woman, mid-thirties, foreign accent she's trained out of herself or nearly. Dark hair going early grey. She would keep to herself." He produced a folded paper, but did not open it; he only held it, the way a man holds a knife he hasn't yet decided to use. "She is called Brightwater."

The name landed in Ilya's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He had known it was coming—had known since the traveler died in Edmund's care with a god's whisper still on his lips—but knowing did nothing to soften the weight.

"There are many hollows and valleys between here and the coast," Ilya said. "People pass through. I don't keep a census."

"We know you don't, Father. That's rather the point of you." Corhane smiled faintly, without warmth. "A defrocked priest living at the edge of a magic-dead valley, tending no flock, keeping no records. The Office finds your kind useful. Men who have seen both sides of the veil and chosen neither." He turned, surveying the valley mouth behind Ilya—the terraced fields, the smoke of morning fires, the beehives on the far slope like small golden shrines. "Tell me what you know of answered prayers."

It was such an unexpected question that Ilya's composure cracked visibly; he felt it go, felt his face do something honest before he could stop it.

"Ah," Corhane said softly. "So you've heard."

"Heard what?"

"Three weeks ago, in Dossel Ford—" he nodded back the way he'd come, toward the lowlands, "—a dying drover prayed to Vessuun-of-the-Crossings, god of a country that no longer exists. And the god answered. Healed the man's fever outright. Left him weeping in the road, speaking a language no one had heard in ten years." Corhane's careful eyes fixed on Ilya. "Since then? A well in Marrowick that gives sweet water when blessed. A child in Tarnholt who spoke prophecy in her sleep. Small things. Local things. The gods of a dead nation, waking up one village at a time."

Ilya said nothing. Behind him, somewhere up the valley, he could imagine Edmund at her hives, hands bare in the cool air, believing herself safe inside the Hollow's silence. Believing the silence would hold.

"You misunderstand the nature of our work, Father," Corhane continued, folding the paper away at last. "You think we hunt mages. Traitors. Survivors of the Vessanyan purges. Once, perhaps. But the crown learned something in the war, something the priests of that era understood and the present ones have forgotten." He leaned slightly closer. "Magic doesn't die when you kill the magicians. It goes *somewhere*. And lately, it has been coming *back*."

"The Hollow is warded," Ilya said. "Nothing wakes here. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows wrong." For the first time, something moved behind Corhane's careful face—not anger, but a kind of tired certainty that was worse. "The wards hold against power, Father. Against storm-callers and hedge-witches and the old blood. But prayer isn't power. Prayer is *address*. You can seal a door against everything that might walk through it, and still hear knocking." He straightened. "Every village where a prayer has been answered, we burn. That is the standing order. Not because the villagers did anything—they didn't, most of them, they just wanted rain or healing or a good death. But because an answered prayer is a crack, and cracks spread."

"And the people in those villages?"

"What people?" Corhane asked, and it was not cruel. That was the horror of it. It was administrative. "Dossel Ford is ash. Marrowick will be by week's end. Tarnholt, likely, after. We are not murderers, Father. We are gardeners. Pruning."

* * *

They made camp that evening at the ford, and Ilya—because they commanded it, and because refusing would draw their eyes up the valley—brought them bread and salt fish and sat with them while they ate. The blinkless one watched him the whole time. Corhane talked, easily, the way lonely men talk when someone finally sits with them.

"I read the old files," he said, tearing bread. "The binding of Vessany. Do you know how it was done? Not with armies. Armies tried first and broke. It was done with worship. The crown took every shrine, every hymnal, every holy well in nine provinces and redirected them—bound the gods' names into a single great working, fed the Door with ten years of stolen devotion, and used it to fold the whole country out of the world. Elegant. Horrible. The priests who wrote it called it mercy." He glanced up. "Some of those priests are still alive, I'm told. Living quietly. Doing penance in odd corners."

Ilya kept his hands very still around his cup. "Is that so."

"One of them wrote the central clause. The keystone verse. A prodigy—youngest canonist ever seated at Karavel." Corhane chewed, swallowed, watched him. "Ilya Vaschenko. Ring any bells, Father?"

The fire popped. Downstream, the river made its soundless motion.

"No," Ilya said.

Corhane studied him for a long moment, then smiled again, that document-dry smile. "No," he agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. Well. If the Brightwater woman passes this way—and she will, eventually, they always come back to the places that hide them best—you'll send word to the garrison at Marrowick. Or we'll come back, and we'll ask more carefully. And Father?" He rose, brushing crumbs from his grey coat. "If any more prayers get answered anywhere near here—if so much as a headache lifts at the wrong moment—we'll know the crack runs through this valley too. And we'll prune accordingly."

* * *

They left at dawn. Ilya stood in the shallows until the riders were gone, until the river remembered how to sing, and then he climbed the slope toward Edmund's hives with his heart hammering out a confession his mouth hadn't made yet.

But halfway up, he stopped.

Because he finally understood it—not the danger to Edmund, that was simple arithmetic, but the other thing, the thing Corhane had said so casually among all his horrors: *every village where a prayer has been answered, we burn.*

Dossel Ford. Ash. Marrowick, burning by week's end. Villages of farmers and ferrymen and children, pruned like weeds, and why? Because a god had answered a dying man. Because the binding *he had written*—his beautiful, elegant keystone, the mercy that ended the war—was fraying, and the fraying let small mercies leak through, and the crown answered each leaked mercy with fire.

His silence had not been penance. It had been permission. Every year he had sat at the valley's edge saying nothing, writing nothing, warning no one, the burnings had gone on unopposed, unwitnessed, unremembered—because he was the last man alive who knew exactly how the Door worked, and he had chosen quiet over confession. He had told himself exile was atonement. It was cowardice wearing atonement's robe.

And now the gods themselves were clawing at the seams of his spell, dragging whole villages into the flame to do it.

Ilya stood on the hillside with the bees droning gold around him and felt the full weight of what he owed, and to whom, and how late it already was.

Then he saw Edmund coming down the path toward him, moving fast, her face pale beneath the sun-hat, and he knew from the way she walked that she had dreamed again—and that whatever the god had whispered to her in the night, it was worse than witch-finders.

"We need to talk," she said. "Now. About the Door."
