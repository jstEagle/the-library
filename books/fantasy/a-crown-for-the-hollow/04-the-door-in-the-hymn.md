## 4. The Door in the Hymn

The whispers began on the third night after the traveler's burial, and they came in Vessanyan.

Edmund woke with the syllables already in her mouth, half-spoken into her pillow like a lover's name she'd sworn never to say again. *Ashen-vel. Ashen-vel, kiri domai.* A line from a harvest hymn. She lay still in the dark of her cottage and listened to her own heartbeat, waiting for the words to fade the way dreams were supposed to.

They did not fade. They settled into her chest like bees settling into a hive at dusk, a low warm hum she could feel behind her sternum.

*You are dreaming of a dead language,* she told herself. *That is grief. Grief does this.* Ten years in the Hollow had taught her every shape her grief could take—the sudden smell of woodsmoke that meant nothing, the way she still set two cups out some mornings before remembering there was no one to pour for. But this was different. This was not memory. Memory did not have a direction.

Because the whisper was pulling her somewhere. Faintly, patiently, the way water pulls a leaf toward a drain, something north of her cottage wanted her to come.

She got up and lit no lamp. In the Hollow, Edmund Brightwater had learned to move through darkness without it; light invited questions, questions invited neighbors, neighbors invited the slow accumulation of a life she could not afford to be seen living too carefully. She dressed, pulled her hair back under a plain wool cap, and stood a long moment with her hand on the door latch, feeling the hum in her chest turn gently, insistently, like a finger tapping glass.

"I buried you," she whispered to it. "I put you in the ground by the beehives with a stone at his head. Whatever you are, you buried yourself with him."

The hum did not answer in words. It answered in weather: outside, though the sky had been clear at dusk, a single cloud now crossed the moon, ragged-edged, moving against the wind. Moving north. Moving the way she was being asked to go.

Edmund had spent ten years teaching herself not to notice things like clouds that moved against the wind. Tonight she noticed, and hated herself for noticing, and opened the door anyway.

* * *

The valley at night was a country of its own. The Hollow kept its own hours, its own silences; even the streams seemed to run more quietly here than anywhere else in the world, as if the water itself had agreed to hush. Edmund walked the shepherd paths above the terraced fields, past the charcoal-burners' mounds banked and sleeping, past the shrine-stone where farmers left bread for a saint whose name they'd forgotten, keeping only the kindness of the custom. Her boots found the way without her. That frightened her most of all—that her feet knew where they were going before her mind consented to it.

The god—she had begun, reluctantly, to call it that—did not speak so much as *remember* at her. Fragments drifted up through the hum like silt rising in stirred water: a kitchen in Vessany with blue tiles gone cold; a bell tower whose bells had been recast into cannon; her mother's voice, not saying anything in particular, just saying it in Vessanyan, the vowels round and low as river stones. Edmund walked and wept without deciding to, tears freezing in the wind before they reached her jaw, and she let them, because there was no one to see, because the valley was empty, because for ten years she had been the only audience of her own mourning and tonight the mourning had grown loud enough to walk ahead of her like a lantern.

Toward dawn, the path left the trees and climbed a shoulder of grey rock, and there it stood.

She knew it at once, the way you know your own face in a dark window. A shrine. Small, roofless, built of the same pale stone as the hills, so old that the mountain seemed to be growing back through it rather than around it. Lichen furred the walls. Snow sat in the hollows of the carvings—and the carvings, when Edmund's eyes finally made sense of them, made her knees go soft.

Bees. The walls were carved with bees. And wheat sheaves, and storm-clouds with faces, and a woman with her arms raised holding a crown of lightning—not the crown of any king Edmund knew, but the old crown, the Vessanyan one, the one the priests had said was only ever weather given a throne.

No one had worshipped here in a hundred years. No one in the Hollow had ever worshipped here at all; the Hollow had no gods, that was the whole point of it, that was why she had chosen it. And yet the stones were humming. She felt it through her boot-soles before she touched them, a vibration like a struck bell that would not finish dying away, and when she laid her bare palm flat against the wall—

*Ashen-vel, kiri domai.*

The hymn rose up through the stone and into her bones.

It was not loud. It was barely audible, a thread of sound woven into the mountain's own silence, but Edmund knew it the way you know a lullaby sung to you before you could speak. It was the Harvesting of the Storm, the first hymn every Vessanyan child learned, the one her mother had sung while kneading bread, the one the storm-callers sang standing in open fields with their arms raised, calling the lightning down to bless the wheat. The last thing Edmund had heard, ten years ago, from the far side of a closing border, was ten thousand voices singing this hymn as her country went out like a lamp.

Her mouth was dry. Her hands, scarred since the night she'd broken her vow of stillness, ached inside their wrappings. Every sane instinct she had cultivated over a decade said: *walk away, walk down the hill, go home, check the bees, be nobody.*

Edmund Brightwater took a breath of the cold thin air, stepped into the ruined shrine, and sang.

* * *

Her voice cracked on the first line. Ten years of disuse had left it rusty; she had spoken only in the valley's flat accent, had sung nothing but wordless humming while working the hives, and the Vessanyan came out of her crooked and half-remembered, like a dance learned from watching through a window. But the stone remembered better than she did. Where her voice faltered, the walls carried her, the hum swelling beneath her poor notes and bearing them up, correcting them, forgiving them, until by the third verse she was not singing alone but singing *with*—with lichen and snow and mountain, with the carved woman and her crown of lightning, with whatever patient fragment of divinity had spent three nights leading her here by the hand.

And the world opened.

Not the sky. Not the shrine. Something else—a seam, high above the altar where the roof should have been, where there was only starlight and cold air. Edmund saw it the way you see movement at the edge of vision, and then the way you see it plainly, all at once, undeniable: a line of pale gold hanging in the darkness, thin as a hair, tall as the horizon. It ran from the mountaintop into the sky and out of sight, and along its length hung—she had no other word for it—*stitches*. Great luminous sutures, thousands upon thousands of them, each one knotted with a name. She could not read the names, but she knew what they were. She knew the way you know your own hands in the dark.

They were prayers. Stolen ones. Every prayer whispered to a Vessanyan god for ten years, unanswered, unclaimed, gathered up and knotted into this seam to feed it, to keep it shut, to keep a whole nation sewn out of the world.

And the seam was fraying.

Even as she watched, a stitch near the top popped—one moment taut gold, the next a drifting spark that guttered and died—and through the gap came a sound that made Edmund's heart stop and then start again wrong: wind. Real wind, Vessanyan wind, smelling of rain on hot wheatfields, of the sea at Solenne's harbor, of woodsmoke and beeswax and her mother's kitchen. A whole country's air, breathing through a pinhole in the sky.

*This is what woke the gods,* she thought, and the thought arrived with the weight of certainty, the way true things do. *Someone is picking the stitches loose. Someone is letting them answer.* The dying traveler's prayer hadn't been a miracle. It had been a leak. The gods weren't returning—they were *escaping*, seeping back through a Door their worship had been welded shut, and every prayer that got through pried the seam wider, and every stitch that broke let more of them through, and the process was feeding itself now, growing, accelerating—

The hymn faltered in her throat.

Because she saw the rest of it then, the part that curdled the awe in her chest into something colder. The Door was not merely fraying. It was *straining*. The stitches were not being picked; they were being *pulled*, from both sides at once—from outside by the waking gods desperate to return, and from inside by something else, something vast and patient and cold that pressed against the seam the way ice presses against a lake in late winter. She could not see it. She felt it, the way animals feel an earthquake coming, a pressure without shape or name, and the gold thread of the Door trembled between the two forces like a wire about to sing.

*It isn't opening,* Edmund realized. *It's tearing.*

And when it tore—if it tore—it would not release Vessany gently back into the world. It would rip. Everything on either side of the seam would be caught in the rip: the valley, the lowlands, the villages where children might already be humming fragments of a hymn they didn't know, everything within reach of a wound in reality the size of a nation.

The vision closed like a hand snapping shut. Edmund came back to herself kneeling on the frozen altar-stone, her palms bleeding where her nails had cut through the old scars, her voice hoarse, the stars wheeling overhead indifferent and ordinary. The seam was gone—or hidden again, folded back into the sky's silence. Only the hum remained, fainter now, almost apologetic.

She stayed until the sun rose, sitting in the ruins with her back against the carved woman, watching the light creep down the eastern peaks toward the valley. Below her, smoke rose from the chimneys of the Hollow. Ordinary smoke. Ordinary lives. Children who would wake soon and run out to check on lambs due in March.

One of those children had sung, three nights ago, at the ford. She'd heard it herself and thought nothing of it—a skipping rhyme, nonsense syllables, except that they weren't nonsense, they were Vessanyan, worn smooth by generations of mouths that didn't know what they were carrying. The hymn was spreading. Through rhymes and lullabies and the memories of people who had loved a country enough to carry pieces of it out through the Door before it closed. The gods were riding those fragments home.

And the witch-finders, Ilya had warned her, followed exactly such fires.

Edmund unwound the cloth from her scarred hands and looked at them—at the pale ridged skin that ached whenever the air remembered how to carry power. They had been a warning, she understood now. The first toll taken on a road she had already started walking whether she willed it or not.

She rose, brushed the frost from her skirts, and turned down the hill toward the valley—toward Ilya, who owed her truths he had not yet told, and toward a conversation she dreaded more than any witch-finder.

Behind her, faint as breath, the shrine began to sing again without her.

And far to the north, where no map showed anything but empty steppe, the first stitch snapped with a sound like a struck bell, and something on the other side of the Door lifted its head, and listened.
