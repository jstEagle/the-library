## 5. Ash on the Wind

The bees knew first.

Edmund had learned to read them the way she had once read cloud-shapes over the Vessanyan coast—every tremor in the hive a syllable, every sudden stillness a sentence left unfinished. On the morning the witch-finders came to Millbrook, the hives went quiet as held breath, ten thousand small bodies pressing low against their combs, and Edmund stood in the apiary with smoke curling from her tin censer and understood that something had crossed the ford.

Not soldiers. Soldiers she could have borne. Soldiers were merely men, and men could be lied to, fed, sent away with a sack of turnips and a story. But the bees had gone silent the way the valley itself went silent when magic came near—recoiling from a wrongness in the air, the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth.

She left the censer burning and walked up the sheep-track to the ridge above Millbrook, and there she saw them: four riders in ash-grey cloaks, and behind them a wagon, and on the wagon an iron brazier that burned with no color at all.

The flame was the color of forgetting.

Edmund had seen that fire once before, ten years ago, from the deck of a smuggler's boat, watching the coastline of her country dissolve into a haze that was not fog and not sea-spray. She had told herself since that she had imagined it—that grief had painted the memory. But here it was again, small and portable and terrible, carried in a brazier like a relic, and the sight of it reached into her chest and closed its hand around everything she had spent a decade burying.

*They are not hunting you,* Ilya had said. *They are following the gods.* He had said it as though it were a mercy. He had been wrong, or he had been lying, or both, because the wagon was turning up the lane toward Millbrook, and Millbrook was where the Weaver children lived—the Weaver children who had learned the hymn from their grandmother, who had sung it skipping rope, who had sung it at the well, who had sung it so sweetly that the old god in the ruined shrine had turned his ear toward them the way a sunflower turns toward light.

Edmund's hands began to shake. She pressed them flat against her thighs and made herself count her breathing the way she had taught herself on the boat, in the years after, in the long grey decade of being no one: *in for four, hold for four, out for four.* A storm-caller's discipline. A coward's liturgy. She had a vow. She had made it with her scarred palms flat on the stones of the Hollow's oldest shrine, the day she arrived: *I will be still. I will be small. I will let the dead stay dead if it means the living stay alive.*

Below her, the witch-finders dismounted at the well.

* * *

She could not hear what they said. The wind was wrong that day, carrying sound up the valley and away, and so Edmund watched the scene below her the way one watches a play performed behind glass: the reeve of Millbrook coming out with his hat in his hands, the grey-cloaked leader producing a scroll, the villagers gathering at their doors with the particular stillness of people who have heard the rumors and are hoping, desperately, to be mistaken.

Then the leader gestured, and two of his men went into the Weaver house, and came out with the children.

There were four of them. The youngest couldn't have been more than six, a girl with her grandmother's dark curls, and she was not crying, which was somehow worse. She was looking at the brazier with the solemn, puzzled expression of a child confronting something she has been told is holy and cannot make herself believe it.

Edmund heard the leader's voice then, carried up on a sudden shift of wind, thin and clear as a knife drawn along a whetstone:

"—taught them a song. We only want the song. Give us the song, and the rest of you may keep your roofs."

The grandmother came forward. Old Marta Weaver, who sold Edmund honey-cakes at the autumn fair, who had once, without knowing what she said, told Edmund that her hands were like her mother's hands, and Edmund had gone home and wept into a pillow for an hour because no one had spoken of her mother to her in nine years. Marta stood in front of the children with her arms spread, and Edmund watched her mouth move—*they're only children, they don't know what they sang, it was a skipping song, it was nothing*—and watched the leader shake his head with the gentle patience of a man correcting a slow pupil.

"It is never nothing," he said. "A prayer is a door. A hymn is a wide door. And behind that door, madam, is a thing that ate a kingdom."

*No,* Edmund thought, with a clarity that felt like the moment before lightning. *You ate a kingdom. You and your crown and your stolen worship, and now you burn the children who remember.*

The brazier flared. The colorless fire leaned toward the Weaver house like a hound scenting meat.

Later, Edmund would try to remember making the decision, and she would fail. There was no decision. There was only the gap between one heartbeat and the next, the way a door stands open between one room and another, and through that gap she stepped out of ten years of stillness as simply and as completely as a diver steps off a ledge.

She raised her hands.

*Come,* she said, in the old tongue, in the storm-tongue, the language she had not spoken aloud since Vessany died. *Come to me. I am your daughter. Come.*

And the sky—

The sky said no.

* * *

The Hollow was not merely a place where magic was quiet. Edmund had always known this, had felt it in the way her power sat inside her like a stone at the bottom of a well, but she had never understood the truth of it until now: the valley was not empty of magic. It was *hungry* for it. The binding that had erased Vessany had not simply silenced the old gods here—it had built a thirst, a great dry throat of a place, and every scrap of power that entered it was swallowed, consumed, *used*.

So when Edmund called the storm, the storm came.

It came wrong.

It came the way water comes through a cracked dam—not in the shape she had given it but in the shape of the crack, all her discipline and her decades of craft torn apart and reformed by the valley's hunger into something that did not answer her but merely *passed through her*, the way lightning passes through a tree, using her as a road between the sky and the ground.

The first thunderclap knocked her off her feet. She had called storms in a hundred skies; she knew their voices like her mother's voice, and this was not a storm's voice, this was the binding itself speaking through borrowed weather, and what it said was: *no.*

The second strike took her hands.

She would remember it in fragments afterward: the white. The smell of her own skin, sweet and obscene, like pork left too near a fire. The astonishing fact of her own scream, which she did not recognize, high and ragged and nothing like the composed silence she had worn for ten years. And beneath it all, below the pain, the terrible clarity of the magic-dead air *drinking* her—pulling the storm through her body because she had opened herself as a channel and the Hollow would not let her close it until it had taken everything she offered and more.

She rolled onto her back in the wet grass. The sky above her was boiling, grey-black, shot through with lightning that struck nothing, that struck only itself, a storm eating its own tail. Rain came down in fat, cold drops. And through the rain, from the valley below, she heard screaming—not the children's screaming, she realized with a lurch, but the witch-finders', and the neighing of terrified horses, and the crash of the wagon.

She dragged herself to the ridge's edge and looked down.

The colorless fire was out. Doused—she would learn later that the brazier's flame could not survive open sky, that it needed the valley's hunger to burn, and Edmund's stolen storm had given it more sky than it could bear. The witch-finders were scrambling onto their horses, the leader shouting, the children running for their grandmother, and the whole of Millbrook stood in the downpour watching the sky with their mouths open, because none of them had ever seen a storm in the Hollow, not in living memory, not in the valley's long deaf silence.

The riders fled north through the rain. Edmund watched them go and felt nothing that resembled victory.

* * *

She did not go down into Millbrook. She could not have borne their gratitude, and she could not have borne their fear, and she suspected—correctly, as it turned out—that she would receive both in equal measure. She crawled instead, and then walked, and then half-fell down the sheep-track to her cottage, and it was there, in the doorway, that she finally looked at her hands.

The scars ran from her palms to her forearms in branching white lines, like the veins of a leaf, like the rivers of a country on a map. Lichtenberg's flowers, the old storm-mages had called them, the marks lightning left on those it loved too hard. But these were not that. These were the valley's signature, the binding's brand, the mark of a power that had passed through her and left her scoured. Her fingers would not fully close. When she tried, the skin pulled taut and hot, and beneath the pain she felt something else, something quieter and more frightening: a thinness, as though the storm had taken not only her flesh but a portion of whatever she had been.

She sat down on her doorstep in the rain and held her ruined hands in her lap and laughed, once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it.

*Still,* she had vowed. *Small. Harmless.*

She was none of those things now. The valley had made certain of that. Every farmer in Millbrook had seen a storm where no storm could be. Every child would tell the story until the story reached the wrong ears. And the witch-finders—she had seen the leader's face as he fled, had seen him look back up at the ridge, had seen the certainty kindle in his eyes like the colorless fire itself.

He had not known where the storm came from. Now he knew there was a *where*.

Edmund looked at her hands. In the branching white lines, if she squinted, if she let her grief do the seeing, she could almost read them: the river Vessa, the coast, the shape of a country that no longer existed, written on her skin in the only ink left to it.

The rain eased toward evening. When it stopped, she rose, wrapped her hands in linen, and took down her traveling cloak from the peg where it had hung untouched for ten years.

There was only one person in the valley who would know what the binding had done to her, because there was only one person in the valley who had helped write it.

She was going to find Ilya, and this time, he was going to tell her everything.
