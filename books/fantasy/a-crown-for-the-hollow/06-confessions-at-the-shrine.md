## 6. Confessions at the Shrine

Ilya had always believed that confession was a kindness. That was the first lie the priesthood ever taught him, and the last one he still carried like a stone in his shoe.

He knelt at the ruined shrine because his knees were old and the floor was cold, and because he had spent three days watching Edmund Brightwater's bandaged hands and saying nothing, and the silence had grown teeth. She sat across from him on a fallen pillar, wrapped in her cloak against the shrine's strange warmth—the hymn-walls hummed even now, faint as a dying man's pulse—and she was looking at him with an expression he had seen once before, ten years ago, on the faces of the Vessanyan mages as they were marched to the border.

Not hatred. Worse than hatred. Recognition.

"You knew," she said. "When I told you about the traveler. When I sang for you in my kitchen like a fool, when I asked you what the gods wanted—you already knew what I was."

"I suspected."

"Don't." Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Don't you dare soften it. You knew. Say it plainly, priest. It's the one honest thing left in this valley."

Ilya bowed his head. The shrine's hum seemed to lean closer, listening, the way it always did when truth was spoken inside it. He had written treatises on the sanctity of confession. He had absolved murderers, adulterers, men who burned granaries in famine years so the price of bread would rise. None of them had ever looked at him the way Edmund looked at him now, and none of them had deserved it more.

"Yes," he said. "I knew."

* * *

The words came out of him the way water comes from a cracked dam—slowly at first, then all at once, drowning everything downstream.

"I was not always a defrocked drunkard tending goats at the edge of nowhere," he said. "I was a canon of the Cathedral of the Sundered Crown. Do you know what that means, Edmund? It means I had access. To the archives, to the crown's theologians, to the war councils that met behind the altar when the fighting went badly enough."

"The war ended," she said flatly. "Everyone knows how. Vessany lost."

"Vessany didn't lose." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Vessany *stopped*. There is a difference, and the difference has a name, and I helped write it. We called it the Concordance of Quiet. Forty pages of theology and nine lines of true spellwork, hidden in the liturgy like a knife in a loaf of bread. Nine lines, Edmund. I drafted six of them myself. I was proud of them. I thought they were the most merciful sentences ever composed in any language."

She had gone very still. Even wounded, even furious, she had the stillness of someone who had learned that movement attracted lightning. He wondered if she knew that about herself.

"The war had been going for thirty years," he went on, because stopping now would kill him. "Thirty years of storm-callers burning fleets, of crown legions drowning in their own blood, of villages on both sides emptied into the ground. Your gods answered every prayer your priests made. Our god answered none. Do you understand what that does to a people? To watch your enemies' crops flourish under a smiling sky while yours rot? We weren't losing the war. We were losing *God*."

"So you stole theirs."

"We *bound* theirs." The correction came out of him with the reflexive precision of a scholar, and he despised himself for it. "The Concordance didn't kill the Vessanyan gods. Killing a god is impossible—it would have been simpler if it weren't. We bound them into the land itself, sealed them beneath the border, and then we erased the border. Not the country—the *border*. The line between Vessany and the world. A door with no frame, a wall with no face. Your nation didn't fall, Edmund. It was folded away, like a letter no one wants to read, and the binding feeds on worship to stay shut. Every prayer that would have gone to your gods goes instead to keeping the Door closed. Ten years of silence. Ten years of stolen voices."

"And you." Her voice was barely above a whisper now, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "You wrote it. And then you came *here*. To the Hollow. Why? Why would you—"

"Penance." He said it simply, because there was no other word and every other word would have been a decoration on a grave. "The Concordance required witnesses. Signatories who would live inside the silence to make certain it held. I volunteered. I told myself it was penance then, too. I told myself a great many things." He looked up at her, and let her see all of it, the whole rotten architecture of his justifications. "And then you walked into my valley, Edmund Brightwater, last daughter of the last house of storm-callers, and you bought honey from me at market, and you smiled at me, and I thought: God has a sense of humor after all. He has put me beside the people I erased, and He expects me to watch them be ordinary until they die."

* * *

For a long time there was only the hum of the walls, and the wind outside moving through the dead grass of the valley, and Edmund's breathing—controlled, deliberate, the breathing of a woman holding something enormous back.

Then she stood, and the motion was wrong, too fast, and she crossed the space between them and struck him across the face with her bandaged hand.

The pain was bright and clean. He took it without moving. He had earned worse; he had administered worse.

"Ten years," she said. Her voice shook. "Ten years I have been *nobody*. I buried my mother's name. I buried my language. I let them call me a beekeeper's widow and I thanked them for it. I watched the traveler die praying to Ashvael and I felt hope for the first time in a decade, and do you know why I felt hope, Ilya? Because I thought the gods had escaped on their own. Because I thought somewhere, somehow, Vessany had fought free." She was crying now, furiously, wiping her face with the backs of her scarred hands as though tears were another enemy to be driven off. "But it wasn't escape. It was *fraying*. Something is tearing the Door open by accident, and the gods are leaking through the cracks like smoke through a broken roof, and the witch-finders are burning children for singing, and it's all—" her voice broke entirely— "it's all *your nine lines*. Everything. My whole life since the border closed has been your handwriting."

"Yes," Ilya said. "It has."

He did not defend himself. That was the discipline he had left, the last scrap of the priesthood: to receive judgment without flinching. He knelt on the cold floor of the shrine he had helped desecrate before it was ever built, and he waited.

Edmund turned away from him. Walked to the humming wall. Put her palm against it—the bandaged one, and he saw her wince—and stood there as if listening to something very far away.

"My mother sang this hymn," she said quietly. "Before bed. I thought it was a lullaby. It's not, is it. It's older than the war. It's part of the Door."

"It's the hinge," Ilya said. "The Concordance used your own sacred songs as its framework. It was the only way to make the binding self-sustaining—a spell the victims themselves would keep alive by loving it. Every mother who sang her child to sleep was turning the key." He swallowed. "That was my contribution. The others wanted force. I argued for beauty. I told them it would be kinder."

"Kinder," Edmund repeated. The word fell between them like a dropped blade.

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned to face him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and terrible and clear. "Because here is what I keep circling, priest, and I want you to answer me honestly, since honesty is apparently the only currency you have left. If you could kneel before the Concordance again—forty pages, nine lines, thirty years of war ending in a single night—would you sign it again?"

Ilya opened his mouth. Closed it. The old answers rose in him like sediment: *the war, the dead, the mercy of it, the thousands saved.* He had rehearsed this defense for a decade. He could deliver it in his sleep. He had delivered it in his sleep, many nights, waking with the taste of justification on his tongue.

"No," he said.

It surprised him. It surprised her; he saw it land in her face like a physical blow.

"The war would have ended anyway," he said slowly, feeling his way through the sentence as through a dark room. "Wars end. They rot from the inside, they exhaust themselves, they collapse. What we did wasn't peace. It was amputation performed without consent, and we called the wound mercy because we couldn't bear to look at it. No. I would not sign it again. I would burn the archive and take the hanging." He laughed again, softer. "Ten years too late. Forgive me. Or don't. I've no right to ask either way."

* * *

Edmund looked at him for a long moment. Then she sat down on the pillar again—not near him, but not far—and pulled her cloak around herself, and was silent for the length of three breaths, four, five.

"When the traveler died," she said at last, "you stayed with me while I dug the grave. You didn't pray over the body. I noticed. I thought you'd simply lost your faith like everyone else in this valley."

"I did lose it. Before the Concordance. During. After." He flexed his hands. "A priest who helps bind the gods cannot afterward pretend to speak for them."

"But you're helping me anyway."

"I'm helping *Vessany*," he said. "Or trying to. It's the only prayer I have left, and I no longer believe anyone is listening, which makes it the most honest prayer I've ever said."

Something moved through the shrine then—not the hum, not quite, but a deepening of it, a chord shifting beneath the floor, and both of them felt it. Edmund's head came up sharply.

"It heard that," she whispered.

"They hear everything here now," Ilya said grimly. "The cracks are widening. Which brings us to the matter I should have raised days ago, before you learned what I was, and which I raise now knowing you may never trust me again." He drew a breath. "The fraying isn't random, Edmund. Bindings don't fray. They are *unbound*. Someone is working at the Door from the other side—from inside the Hollow, or from beyond it—and the witch-finders aren't hunting you. They're following the same thread you are. And yesterday, at the ford, I saw their maps." His voice dropped. "They've marked this valley. They've marked the shrine. And they've marked a third place, Edmund, in the high passes where no village stands—somewhere cold enough that the ink froze on the page."

Edmund stared at him. Outside, the light through the broken roof had begun to fail, and the hymn in the walls swelled, soft and urgent, like a mother calling a child home before dark.

"The winter," she said. "Ilya—the dreams. The gods have been begging me about a winter coming."

"I know," said Ilya. "I think whatever waits in those passes is where it will begin."
