## 7. The Long Winter Falls

The first snow came in the last week of harvest, which was the first sign that the world had gone wrong.

Edmund stood in her apiary with smoke curling from the beekeeper's veil she no longer needed to wear—her hands had healed into something smooth and strange, scarred silver where the valley's dead air had turned her storm against her—and watched flakes drift down through a sky that should have been heavy with apples. The bees clustered on their combs, confused, refusing to settle. One landed on her wrist and simply stayed there, trembling, as if it had decided that if the world was ending it would rather end on a warm hand.

"It's too early," she told it. The bee did not disagree.

Behind her, down the slope, the village of Marrow's Ford was pulling in its harvest under a sky the color of old pewter. She could hear the carts, the shouted arguments about whether to cut the grain wet or lose it altogether. Ten years ago she would have known what to do about weather like this. Ten years ago she could have pushed the storm off the valley's shoulders with two words and a handful of lightning held like a knife.

Now she could only stand in her ruined garden and feel the cold arrive like a guest who intended to stay.

It wasn't natural cold. She had grown up in Vessany, where winter came off the mountains like a wolf and everyone learned its habits. This was different. This cold came from *inside* the ground, rising through the soles of her boots, and it carried something with it—a taste at the back of the throat, faintly of iron, faintly of hymns. When she breathed it in, she dreamed in a language she had spent ten years refusing to speak.

*The Door is leaking,* said the god in her dreams that night. *And we are too small to stopper it.*

* * *

She dreamed of Vessany the way you dream of drowning: not the place itself but the fact of it, the borders of it, a country-shaped absence lying over the maps like a burn scar. In the dream she stood on a hillside she knew—the terraced vineyards above Serevan, the capital that no longer appeared on any chart—and the vines were white. Not with frost. With a whiteness that moved, that crept, that ate the green out of the world row by row while she watched.

Her grandmother had tended those vines. Her grandmother had sung to them in Vessanyan, songs about rain and patience, and Edmund had memorized every one before she was seven because that was what storm-callers' children did: they learned the words that made the sky listen.

In the dream, the whiteness reached the top terrace and the vines began to sing back—a thin, cracked chorus, hundreds of voices, all of them begging.

She woke with her own voice raw and Ilya shaking her shoulder, his face gray in the dark of her kitchen.

"You were singing," he said. "In your sleep. The whole house heard it."

"What did I sing?"

He didn't answer for a long moment. He was an old man now, or older than he'd been in spring; the year had taken years from him the way a river takes banks. "The lament for the drowned fields," he said finally. "We sang it at the binding. To make the land sleep gently." His hands were wrapped around a cup of tea that had long gone cold. "Edmund, it isn't supposed to be sung by the land. It's supposed to be sung *to* it."

"Then tell me what's happening."

"The Door is fraying faster than we thought. Faster than I thought it could." He looked at his hands, the penitent's hands, the hands that had helped write the spell that erased a nation. "A binding is a promise, and promises need feeding. The crown fed it worship—stolen prayer, tithed faith, ten years of it. But the gods are awake now, and they're pulling at the seams, and every seam they pull lets in—" He stopped. Started again. "You've seen the white. Don't pretend you haven't. Half the valley's seen it in their dreams."

"I've seen more than dreams." She pulled on her coat. "Come with me."

* * *

They walked north along the river road, past Marrow's Ford, past the shrine where Edmund had first glimpsed the Door, up into the hills where the mapmakers' ink simply gave up. Three days out, they found the first empty village.

Not destroyed. Empty. Doors standing open. A cradle with its blanket thrown back. A pot of porridge still on the hearth, frozen solid, untouched. Snow lay in the streets though the clouds overhead were thin and high and dry, snow that had fallen from nowhere, out of a clear sky, as if the cold no longer needed weather to carry it.

Ilya went door to door anyway, calling, though neither of them believed anyone would answer. In the shrine at the village's heart—a squat stone thing, older than either nation, dedicated to no god in particular—he found the reason written in charcoal on the wall, in a child's unsteady hand:

*THE WHITE WALKS AT NIGHT AND IT SINGS GRANDMOTHER'S SONGS.*

Below it, another hand, adult, desperate: *GONE SOUTH TO KELLIN'S CROSS. FOLLOW IF YOU LIVE.*

"They're running," Edmund said. "From their own dreams."

"From the leak." Ilya stood in the doorway of the shrine, looking out at the white fields, and for once he sounded less like a priest than like a man reading his own death warrant. "Vessany didn't die, Edmund. That was always the lie we told ourselves. We sealed it—its people, its gods, its dead—all of it, folded behind the Door, alive and dreaming and *waiting*. And now the Door is coming apart, and everything on the other side is pressing against the tear, and most of it is grief, and some of it is hungry, and none of it knows it's been dead for ten years."

"And the white?"

"The cold is just the shape it makes getting through." He turned to her. "There's worse coming through than cold."

That night, camped in the empty shrine with a fire that burned blue at its edges, the gods came to her again. All of them this time—not the one fragmentary whisper she'd been hearing since the traveler died, but a crowd, a congregation, the whole silenced pantheon of Vessany crowding against the inside of her skull like refugees at a gate.

*Little storm,* they said, and their voices layered until she couldn't tell one from another, the mother-god and the field-lord and the small nameless god of thresholds, all of them speaking at once, all of them pleading. *You felt us when we woke. You sang our hymn. You are of the Door and the Door is of you—we sealed it with the bloodline of its makers, and you are the last of the bloodline left outside.*

"I know what I am," Edmund said aloud, and Ilya, watching her talk to the dark, bowed his head.

*Then you know what we ask.* The voices gentled, which was somehow worse than the pleading. *Open it. Sing it wide. Let us out before the tearing widens on its own—for a torn door does not close, little storm, it only opens wider, and what comes through next will not be us. It will be the country itself. Its hunger. Its ending. Everything we swallowed when we agreed to sleep.*

"And if I open it?"

*Then we come home to a world that forgot us, and we take our chances, and so do you. And the cold stops. Because the Door will be a door again, and doors can be shut behind us.*

"And if I don't?"

Silence. Then, very quietly, in a voice she recognized—her grandmother's cadence, or something wearing it—*Then the world ends quietly, and no one will even be angry. They'll just forget, the way they forgot us. You know how forgetting works. You've lived in it for ten years.*

She woke with frost on her eyelashes, inside the shrine, beside a fire that had died, and Ilya sat across the ashes watching her with an expression she couldn't read.

"They asked you," he said. Not a question.

"They asked me."

"And?"

"And nothing." She stood, stamped feeling back into her feet, refused to look at him. "I'm a beekeeper. I keep bees. I don't open doors between worlds."

"Edmund—"

"*I keep bees.*" Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "Ten years, Ilya. Ten years I made myself small enough to fit in this valley. I buried my name, my language, my—everything. And now the gods want me to unmake the only peace I've ever had, and half of me wants to tell them yes, and the other half wants to let the white take the whole rotten world because at least then it would be *quiet*, at least then nobody would ever again look at a woman and see a weapon, and I don't know which half is me anymore."

The silence after that was very long. Outside, snow fell upward for a moment—an impossibility, a hiccup in the world's grammar—and settled again.

"That second half," Ilya said at last, gently, "isn't temptation. It's exhaustion. I know the difference. I lived on the wrong side of it for twenty years." He rose, stiffly, and put what remained of their food into his pack. "But here's what an old coward knows that a young exile doesn't: quiet bought with everyone else's silence isn't quiet. It's just a bigger version of the Hollow. And you of all people know what living in the Hollow costs."

* * *

They came down out of the hills toward Kellin's Cross and found the road full of the displaced—carts and handcarts and families walking with what they could carry, a whole river of the valley flowing south away from the spreading white. A woman with a baby told them the cold had reached the ford in the night. A man with frostbitten fingers said he'd heard singing under the ice of the river, dozens of voices, patient as tide.

And at the crossroads itself, where the refugee stream bent south, Edmund saw the thing that ended her hesitation.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, standing apart from her family's cart, facing north. Facing the white line visible now even at this distance—a horizon-wide shimmer, like heat-haze inverted, like the world holding its breath. The girl was humming. Edmund knew the tune. Everyone from Vessany knew the tune. It was a lullaby, and the child could not possibly have learned it, and yet it poured out of her as naturally as breath, and as Edmund watched, the white line on the horizon seemed to lean toward the sound, the way a plant leans toward light.

The girl's mother snatched her up and carried her, crying, into the southern crowd.

"She's a vessel," Ilya said quietly. "The Door is reaching through the bloodlines. Looking for keys. There aren't many of us left, Edmund. There's you, and there are children who hum songs they never learned, and there is not much time."

Edmund looked north a long while. The white line pulsed, slow, like something breathing in its sleep. Like a country dreaming it still existed.

When she finally spoke, her voice was steady, and that steadiness frightened her more than the cold had.

"The witch-finders took you once for covering for me. They'll come again when they learn what I'm going to do. So we do this properly." She turned south, toward the lowlands, toward the shrine-fortress she had sworn never to approach. "If I'm the key, then someone has to hold the lock open while I turn it—and the crown keeps its locks well guarded. We're going to need a storm, Ilya. A real one. And for that, I need to leave the Hollow."

Above them, the first thunder in ten years rolled across the valley—not hers, not the valley's, but leaking through from somewhere far to the north, patient and vast, like a god clearing its throat.

The bees, she thought absurdly, would have to fend for themselves.
