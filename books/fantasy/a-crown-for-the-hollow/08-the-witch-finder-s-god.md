## 8. The Witch-Finder's God

The witch-finders came to the Hollow on a morning so still the bees sounded like distant bells.

Ilya heard them before he saw them—the wrongness of hooves on the valley road, where no one rode at speed because there was nowhere to be going. He was splitting kindling outside his hermit's hut when the sound reached him, and he set down the axe with the deliberate calm of a man who had spent eleven years practicing how not to flinch.

They came around the bend of the ford road in grey: six riders in the livery of the Crown's Office of Purity, ash-grey cloaks, iron-grey masks with no mouths cut into them, only eye-slits like the seams of closed coffins. At their head rode a man without a mask—a broad, comfortable face, silvering hair combed back, the mild eyes of a parish priest or a well-fed magistrate. Ilya knew that face. He had known it across a council table, twenty years and one lifetime ago, when they had both been young men arguing theology by candlelight.

Magister Corven Hale. First Voice of the Office. The man who signed the burn orders.

"Hale," Ilya said, when the riders drew rein in his dooryard. His voice came out flat as slate. "They let you out of the capital. I'd hoped the paperwork had buried you."

"Ilya Vosk." Hale smiled, and it was the same smile he'd worn over those candles—warm, patient, utterly without warmth underneath. "Still keeping bees for a god who won't answer? Or have you found yourself a new congregation?"

"You know there are no gods here." Ilya did not move from between the riders and the narrow track that wound up toward Edmund's apiary on the hillside. It would not stop six armed men. But it was something to stand behind, and standing behind things was most of what priesthood had ever been. "That is the whole point of this valley."

"Is it?" Hale dismounted, unhurried, and looked up at the sky as if checking the weather against some private ledger. "Strange, then. Strange that prayers are being answered again. Strange that hymns are crawling out of ruins. Strange that a dead country has begun to *hum*." He turned the mild eyes back on Ilya. "You were at the Binding, Vosk. You know better than anyone what a hum like that means."

Ilya said nothing. Behind Hale, the masked riders sat their horses like furniture, like things waiting to be used.

* * *

They took him to the ford, which surprised him until it didn't. Of course they'd want the ford—the low crossing where the valley's silence ran thinnest, where a traveler had died last autumn whispering to a god and been heard. The witch-finders had made camp there: three wagons, a brazier burning something that gave off no smoke, cages stacked like loaves. In two of the cages, people. A miller's boy from the lowlands who'd sung the Vessanyan hymn at his sister's wedding. An old woman who left offerings at a ruined shrine. They were alive. Ilya noted it the way you note a debt.

Hale had them bring out a table, a chair, a decanter of wine—as though this were an ecclesiastical visitation and not a hunting camp—and poured two cups with the ease of long habit.

"Sit," he said. "Please. I'm not here for you, old friend. I'm here because of the Brightwater girl."

"There is no Brightwater girl."

"No," Hale agreed pleasantly. "There isn't. There hasn't been for ten years, because we wrote her out along with everything else she loved. But someone in this valley called a storm three weeks ago. Small storm. Localized. Enough to drive off my men from a village that will nonetheless be remembered as having harbored heresy." He sipped his wine. "Magic doesn't work here. Everyone knows magic doesn't work here. And yet."

Ilya sat, because sitting meant listening, and listening meant time. "If you know she's here, why haven't you taken her?"

"Because I don't want her taken. I want her *employed*." Hale set down his cup and leaned forward, and for a moment the parish-priest warmth fell away like a mask slipping its strap, and beneath it was something older and hungrier and very tired. "Do you know what the Binding actually feeds on, Ilya? You helped write the liturgy; surely they told you the mechanics."

"They told us it would end the war."

"It did end the war. Do you know *how*?" Hale's voice dropped, intimate, confiding, the tone of a man sharing a sin. "A border-spell needs fuel. To erase a nation—not conquer it, *erase* it, unwrite it from every map, every memory, every mouth—you must feed the spell continuously. Worship is the only currency strong enough. So we bound the old gods. All of them. The Vessanyan pantheon first—they were the richest—but then all the others, every god in every province whose power might be turned against the crown. We didn't kill them. We *harvested* them. Every prayer whispered in the last ten years, every candle lit, every desperate bargain struck in the dark—all of it flows into the Door. That is why the gods went silent. That is why your faith died, Ilya. It wasn't abandonment. It was taxation."

The brazier crackled its smokeless fire. Somewhere in the cages, the old woman was murmuring—praying, even now, even here, to a god who could not hear her. Ilya thought of ten years of empty chapels. Ten years of his own voice echoing off stone, addressing a silence he'd believed was divine indifference. He had grieved that silence like a death. He had built his exile on top of it like a cairn.

"You're lying," he said quietly. "To make me despair. Or to make me complicit twice."

"I wish I were lying." Hale reached into his coat and produced a ledger—thick, leather-bound, official—and opened it on the table. Columns of figures. Names of provinces. Names of gods, written in the old shorthand of the seminaries: *the Ninefold Mother. The Lantern-Bearer. Vessany's Storm-Crown.* Beside each name, numbers. Flow rates. "This is the tithe schedule. Crown property. I am showing you a treason, Vosk, because I need you to understand what happens if the Door fails." He turned a page. "Every prayer ever stolen is stored in the Door's fabric. If it opens—if it *fully* opens—that worship comes home. The gods come back fed and furious, with ten years of harvest to draw on. And the men who bound them—" he touched his own chest, lightly, almost fondly, "—are the first thing they'll look for."

* * *

Edmund came down from the hillside at midday, and Ilya saw her coming along the track and could not decide whether to be glad or afraid.

She walked like she always did now—shoulders down, hands loose, a woman doing nothing more remarkable than fetching honey—but Ilya had watched her too long not to see the coiled thing under the calm, the storm-caller holding herself in the shape of a beekeeper. Her hands were still scarred where the valley's dead air had turned her own lightning back on her. She kept them half-curled, as if guarding the wounds.

Hale rose when she entered the camp. Bowed, actually bowed, the way one bows to royalty.

"Lady Edmund Brightwater," he said. "Storm-Crown elect of Vessany. Last heir of a drowned throne. Forgive the theater—I find titles open doors that plain speech can't."

"I'm a beekeeper," Edmund said.

"You were. Before you sang a hymn in a ruin and called weather out of a sky that shouldn't permit it." Hale gestured to the chair opposite him. She didn't take it. She stood beside Ilya instead, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and Ilya understood she was positioning herself between him and the masked riders without appearing to.

"What do you want," she said. Not a question. A wall.

"A pardon," Hale said simply. "Signed by the crown. Full restoration of name, title, lands. The purge reversed posthumously for every mage who died in it. Your country's memory returned to the histories—Vessany taught in schools, mourned properly, honored. Everything you've spent ten years wanting." He paused, letting it land. "In exchange, you rebind the Door. Permanently. Strengthen the Binding, restore the tithe, put the gods back to sleep. Forever."

The silence stretched. Ilya watched her face—the flicker there, quick as a fish turning, of exactly how much that offer hurt. Because it *was* everything she wanted. He knew it. He'd known it since the shrine, since she'd told him about seeing the Door fraying and wept without seeming to, angry tears, the grief of a woman being offered her homeland's corpse as a bargaining chip.

"And the gods?" Edmund asked.

"Safely contained."

"The winter leaking through the fraying edges?"

"Patched. Managed."

"And the villages you burn whenever a child sings a hymn?"

Hale's smile did not waver. "Order requires sacrifice, lady. You of all people understand sacrifice."

"Yes," Edmund said softly. "I do. That's rather the problem."

Ilya felt it then, rising in him like water finding a crack—the rage he had been managing for twenty years, since the night he'd signed his name to the Binding liturgy believing he was saving the world. He had told himself a thousand stories about that signature. *It ended the war. It saved more than it burned. Someone else would have done it worse.* And now here was the ledger, the arithmetic laid bare: every prayer in the world, taxed. Every god, milked dry to keep a murdered country murdered. Every village flame, bookkeeping.

And Hale offering it all back—offering *her* everything back—for one more signature.

"No," Ilya said.

Both of them turned. Hale's eyebrows lifted, genuinely curious. "No?"

"She won't rebind it. And if she were fool enough to consider it, I'd remind her what she'd be signing." Ilya stood, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "You're asking her to choose the cage because the cage comes with her name engraved on it. You're asking her to become the jailer of everything holy so the crown can keep pretending the war ended cleanly. I helped write that binding once, Hale. I know exactly what it costs, because I've been paying it for twenty years in a valley full of silence. She should not pay it. No one should pay it. It was never justice—it was theft dressed as peace, and I will not watch it signed twice."

"Beautifully put." Hale sighed, and there was something almost tender in it. "But you mistake the structure of the offer, old friend. The pardon was never hers to refuse."

He snapped his fingers.

The masked riders moved—not toward Edmund. Toward Ilya. He had half a heartbeat to understand his error, that he had stood *up*, that he had placed himself forward and Edmund back, that twenty years of careful invisibility had ended the moment he opened his mouth—and then iron hands closed on his arms, and a hood came down smelling of ash and lamp-oil, and the world went dark and woolen.

Through the cloth he heard Edmund's voice, and it was not the beekeeper's voice at all. It was low and rolling, thunder gathering past the horizon of itself.

"Let him go."

"Or what?" Hale, conversational, unhurried. "Call a storm, lady. Please. In the valley where magic dies. Show my men what you are, and give me cause to take you both today. Or walk away, and let the crown deal gently with a defrocked priest who has, after all, committed no crime worth mentioning." A pause, silk over steel. "We ride for the shrine-fortress at dusk. He'll keep until then. Whether he keeps at all beyond it depends entirely on your next ten days."

The hands hauled him, stumbling, boots scraping on ford-stones. He heard Edmund shout his name—one word, cracked down the middle—and heard Hale murmur something courteous and unfollowable. Then a wagon bed under him, chains, the creak of wheels, and the long gray roll of the valley carrying him away.

In the dark behind the hood, Ilya did not pray. Prayer was tax, now; he knew where his prayers would go, into what ledger, feeding what door. Instead he did the only thing left that was his own: he began, silently, to recite the Vessanyan hymn. Every verse. Even the forbidden ones. Especially the forbidden ones.

Because he finally understood—twenty years late, chained in a wagon, rage burned down to a single white coal of purpose—that the hymn was not worship.

It was a key. And keys did not care whose mouth they were in.

The wheels rolled north, toward the shrine-fortress, and somewhere above the hood the sky began, very faintly, to darken.
