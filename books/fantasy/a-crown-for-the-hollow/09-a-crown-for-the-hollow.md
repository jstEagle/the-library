## 9. A Crown for the Hollow

The winter had a shape now.

Edmund had learned to see it in the three days since the witch-finders took Ilya—a vast, slow spiral turning above the place where Vessany used to be, like water finding a drain. It was visible only when she closed her eyes, but she could not stop closing her eyes. The cold came down through it in threads, fine as spider silk, and where the threads touched the world, rivers forgot how to move and birds fell mid-flight with their songs still in their throats.

She walked toward it through snow that had never been forecast, carrying nothing but her scarred hands and the hymn.

Her palms were a map of what the Hollow's dead air had done to her—silvered skin, nerves burned down to a dull hum—but they did not hurt anymore. That frightened her more than pain would have. Pain meant the body still believed it had something left to lose.

*You are the key,* the gods kept saying, all of them at once now, crowded into her skull like refugees at a door. *You are the last one who remembers the whole of it.*

*I'm not,* she told them. *Ilya remembers. Ilya wrote it.*

*He remembers the binding,* said the god whose name was Vessanyan for *the space between breaths*. *Only you remember what was bound.*

The shrine-fortress rose out of the white ahead of her, black stone furred with frost, its towers leaning like old men listening. It had been a holy place once—she could feel that under everything else, a patience in the stones—and the crown had built its witch-finders into it the way rot builds into a tooth. Torches burned along the walls without warming anything. The flames bent away from the fortress, as if even fire wanted no part of what happened inside.

Edmund stood at the edge of torchlight and let herself be afraid. She had spent ten years refusing fear, and it had done nothing but ferment. Now she drank it down like cold water and found, at the bottom of it, something steadier: Ilya's voice saying *I helped write it because I believed an ending was kinder than a war*, and her own voice answering, too late and too quietly, *then help me unwrite it.*

She began to sing.

* * *

The hymn was not long. That was the terrible joke of it—the Door that had swallowed a nation hung on eleven lines of melody any child could learn, which was why the crown had spent a decade burning every throat that knew them. Edmund sang the first line and felt it leave her body like heat leaving a stone. She sang the second and the snow around her stopped falling, hanging in the air, listening.

By the fourth line, the fortress had noticed her.

Doors opened along the wall. Figures came out into the blue dark—witch-finders in their grey lacquered coats, their faces hidden behind masks of hammered tin shaped like serene, eyeless faces. They carried the long iron staves that drank prayer out of the air. Edmund kept singing. Her voice cracked on the high note of the fifth line and she let it crack; the hymn had never required beauty, only truth, and the truth was that she sang badly and desperately and could not stop.

A stave swung toward her and the storm answered before she did.

It came up out of the ground itself—not summoned so much as *released*, ten years of held breath leaving the valley in a single exhalation. Wind took the torches. Snow rose instead of fell. Two witch-finders went down as if the sky had put its hand flat against them, and Edmund felt the power move through her scarred hands like water through a broken dam, and this time it did not turn on her. The valley was letting go of her. Or she was letting go of the valley. She could no longer tell the difference, and the not-telling felt like freedom.

*Sixth line,* whispered the gods.

She climbed the outer stair singing it, boots skidding on ice, wind shouldering her upward as though the storm had decided she was cargo worth delivering. At the top, in the great courtyard where the shrine's altar had been replaced by a block of black iron, the witch-finder waited.

He had removed his mask. She had expected a monster and found instead a tired man of perhaps fifty, grey at the temples, with the mild face of a schoolmaster. His borrowed god stood behind him—or rather, stood *through* him, wearing him like a coat, and Edmund recognized it with a lurch: it was one of hers. One of Vessany's. A lesser god, a hearth-god, something that had once blessed bread ovens and first steps, now stretched over a stranger's skeleton and starved down to a hook of appetite.

"You're the Brightwater girl," he said. His voice was two voices, braided. "The crown offers you pardon. Rebind the Door. Feed it your grief, as your elders fed it theirs, and live out your life as whatever small woman you've been pretending to be."

"I tried being small," Edmund said. "It didn't take."

She sang the seventh line.

The hearth-god flinched. She saw it flinch—saw the whole architecture of the man shudder—as if the hymn were a name it had been trying to forget. And she understood, then, with a clarity that felt like falling: the crown had not made a new god to hunt the old ones. They had *starved one into service*. Every village burned, every hymn silenced, every stolen prayer poured into this hollowed thing until it knew nothing but hunger and obedience.

"You poor creature," she said, and meant both of them, the man and the god.

"Bind it," the man gasped, and his hand rose with the iron stave, and behind his eyes his god screamed—

*Eighth line.*

* * *

Ilya was in the crypt beneath the altar.

She found him after—after the ninth line, after the tenth, after the moment she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe—chained to the wall among the bones of the shrine's older dead, frost on his beard, lips moving. Not praying. Counting. He had been counting the hours since they took him, the way he counted everything, penance arithmetic.

"Edmund." His voice was a ruin. "You shouldn't have—I told you not to—"

"You told me a great many things," she said, breaking the chains with hands that should not have had the strength, that had the strength anyway because the storm was in them now and the storm does not negotiate. "Most of them wrong."

"The Door—"

"Is open."

She said it simply, because there was no other way to say it, and watched his face do the work of ten years in a single breath.

* * *

Here is what happened, as well as it can be told:

The eleventh line of the hymn is not a note. It is a silence, held, and into that silence Edmund had poured everything—the bee-smoke mornings, the false name worn smooth as river stone, the night her mother pushed her through the last gap in the world with ash in her hair, the traveler dying in the road praying to a god who finally answered. She had thought opening the Door meant restoring Vessany, raising its borders from the earth like a drowned city surfacing. She had braced herself to give the world back a nation.

But the gods had never asked for borders. Nations were the crown's currency, drawn lines and taxed loyalties. What the gods asked for was memory.

So the Door opened inward instead of outward, and Vessany did not return to the map. It returned to the *world*.

Its dead came through first—not as ghosts, not as bodies, but as remembrance settling over the land like weather. Suddenly every stream in the lowlands remembered the mill that had stood on it. Suddenly the mountains wore their Vessanyan names again, syllables rolling off them like snowmelt. The language came back into the mouths of strangers who had never spoken it, rising like groundwater, and people in villages a hundred miles apart woke weeping, holding words they didn't know they'd lost.

And the gods came through—all of them, the great ones and the small, the hearth-gods and the storm-fathers and the nameless ones who had lived in the pause between two verses of a hymn. They poured past Edmund like light pouring through a broken window, and the winter spiral above the Hollow caught them and *shredded*, the endless cold unraveling thread by thread as something warmer moved through the hole it left.

The hearth-god in the witch-finder's body heard the others coming and simply—left. Edmund saw it go: the starving thing pulling itself out of the man like a splinter drawn from flesh, standing a moment in the courtyard in its true shape, small and soot-colored and heartbreakingly ordinary, before it turned and followed its family home through the Door. The witch-finder collapsed to his knees in the snow, empty, weeping, fifty years of borrowed purpose gone, and Edmund stepped around him without hatred. There was no room in her for hatred. She was full to the brim with everything else.

The binding shattered. She felt it go the way one feels a bone set wrong finally break right—a soundless snap, a release running through the roots of the world. The black iron altar split down the middle. In the crypt below, the chains' frost bloomed into flowers of clear ice, and then into nothing at all.

And the storm, freed of its errand, turned on her.

* * *

It took her the way the sea takes a swimmer who has swum too far from shore—not maliciously, merely completely. All the power she had ever called, all the power her country had ever been, funneled through the open Door and through her, because she was still the key, and keys do not get to choose when the lock turns back. She went down under it. She felt her scarred hands fill with lightning and her heartbeat scatter like startled birds. Somewhere very far away Ilya was shouting her name, dragging himself up the crypt stairs on ruined legs, and somewhere closer than that the gods were singing the hymn back to her, all eleven lines, in voices like her mother's, like home.

*Drown, then,* said the space-between-breaths, gently. *Or breathe us.*

She breathed them.

* * *

Later—hours later, or years—Edmund lay on her back in the courtyard snow, which was no longer falling, because the sky had remembered it was only sky. Ilya knelt beside her with her head in his lap, and his hands, when they touched her face, were shaking.

"You died," he said. "For a moment. I felt it."

"No," Edmund said. Her voice came out smoke-soft and new. "I just stopped being invisible."

Above them, where the winter spiral had turned, the stars were coming out over a horizon that finally, unmistakably, ended somewhere—and beyond that ending, faint as breath on glass, the lights of a country that existed again in every memory but its own borders, waiting to be given a name.

"They'll come," Ilya murmured. "Pilgrims. Scholars. The crown's soldiers, eventually. Everyone will want to see the place where the Door was."

"Then we'd better decide," Edmund said, watching the stars, feeling the storm settle into her chest like a bird returning to a roost it had always known, "what we're going to tell them it was."

His laugh broke in the middle, the way things break when they've been frozen too long and finally thaw. Down in the valley, far below, a single bell began to ring—one of the gods, walking humbly among the living, announcing spring to a world that had almost forgotten the word.
