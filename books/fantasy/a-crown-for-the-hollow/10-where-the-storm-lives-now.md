## 10. Where the Storm Lives Now

Spring came to the Hollow the way forgiveness comes—early, unasked for, and impossible to trust at first.

Edmund noticed it in the bees. They had begun flying a full month before they should, pouring out of the hives on mornings that still smelled of frost, drunk on blossoms that had no business opening. The plum trees along the lane had gone white overnight, as if someone had shaken snow up out of the ground instead of down from the sky. And the light—there was something wrong with the light, something generous. It fell through the valley not the way it used to, flat and gray and careful, but slantwise and golden, the way it fell in Vessany in the springs before the war, when she was small enough to believe the world was held together by hymns.

She stood in her doorway with her scarred hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea and let herself look at it. That was new too. For ten years she had trained herself not to look—to keep her eyes on the ground, her hands busy, her heart folded small and buried deep where nothing could find it. Now she looked at everything. The mountains wearing their crowns of late snow. The river running loud with meltwater. The sky, which was only a sky again, ordinary and blue, with no winter leaking through it, no Door fraying at its seams, no gods screaming through her dreams.

Just a sky. She had forgotten how much a person could love an ordinary thing.

Her hands ached where the valley's dead air had turned her storm against her—the old ache, the one that came before rain now instead of after it. But the scars had changed over the winter. What had been raw welts had healed into pale lines that branched across her palms like lightning frozen mid-strike, like rivers seen from very high up. Ilya said they looked like maps. Edmund thought they looked like roots. Either way, they were hers, and when she flexed her fingers the small thunder under her skin answered—not the wild, drowning power that had nearly killed her at the shrine-fortress, but something quieter. A storm that had learned to live indoors.

* * *

The children came on the fourth morning of the week, as they always did now, clattering up the path from the village with the particular chaos of the young who have been promised a lesson and intend to collect on it.

There were nine of them this spring. Last autumn there had been four. Word traveled strangely in the Hollow—it always had—but it traveled faster now that the valley's silence was broken, faster even than the crown's slow, embarrassed attempts to pretend the whole affair had never happened. There would be no pardon issued, no decree read aloud. The crown did not apologize for burning villages or binding gods; it simply stopped sending witch-finders down roads that led to Edmund Brightwater's door. That, in the language of thrones, was apology enough.

"Magistra!" The youngest, a gap-toothed girl named Sefa who had appointed herself Edmund's herald, arrived first as always, breathless, hair full of straw. "Toma says the god came to his mother's bakery."

"Toma says many things," Edmund said, but she was already smiling.

"It's true! The little one, the hearth one—" Sefa screwed up her face, reaching for the name. "Vesna-of-the-Ovens. He sat on the flour bin all morning and everything rose twice as fast, and he *sang* while he did it, and Toma's mother cried into the dough."

"He does sing," Edmund agreed gravely. "It's mostly the same three notes. Don't tell him I said so."

The other children spilled into the yard behind her, and Edmund set them to work the way she always did: hands first, voices second. She had learned, over the long winter, that you could not teach the old songs from a book. The books were ash, most of them, burned in the purges or lost when Vessany went out of the world. What remained lived in her body—in the way her throat shaped words that had no country left to hold them—and so she taught the way her grandmother had taught her, before either of them knew there would be a last generation.

"Hold out your hands," she said. Nine pairs of hands rose, some scarred by farm work, some soft, all of them open. "Feel for the rain that isn't falling yet. Don't call it. Don't pull. Just say hello."

They closed their eyes. The wind moved through the plum blossoms, scattering white petals across the yard like a slow snowfall, and Edmund watched nine small faces go solemn and listening, and felt the grief and the joy of it arrive together, the way they always did now, inseparable as twins.

Because that was what remained of Vessany. Not borders. Not a throne, not a flag, not a single stone of its cities standing anywhere on any map. The Door had opened and given back its gods and its dead to the world's memory, and the world remembered—but remembering was a humbler thing than being. Vesna-of-the-Ovens sat on flour bins in other people's bakeries. The river-god walked the lowlands as a gray-cloaked traveler who paid for his bread with songs. The great ones, the storm-fathers and the mountain-mothers, kept mostly to the high places, and were seen rarely, and were spoken of softly, the way you speak of the beloved dead who have somehow come home.

But the names survived. Mothers sang them to children who would never see Vessany and would know it anyway, the way you know a grandparent's face from stories. And every week nine children in a hidden valley held out their hands and said hello to the rain in a dead language that was not dead anymore.

"That's enough calling for today," Edmund said, when the air began to smell of ozone and ambition. "Voices now. From the top."

And they sang. The hymn that had opened the Door, the one whose walls still hummed in the ruined shrine if you pressed your ear to the stones—the children knew the first three verses now, and fumbled the fourth, and always laughed at themselves in the fourth, and Edmund sang the fourth alone, loudly, badly on purpose sometimes just to make them braver.

Ilya appeared at the gate partway through, as he always did, drawn by the sound the way he pretended not to be. He leaned there with his arms crossed, gray-bearded, defrocked, disgraced, exiled twice over—once by his church, once by his own confession—and listened to the hymn he had helped write the binding that silenced, sung by children in a valley that magic had abandoned, and if his eyes were wet, no one was rude enough to notice.

He had never asked her to forgive him again after that night at the shrine. Once had been enough. Instead he had simply stayed—carried water, mended fences, sat up with her through the nights when the returning storm in her blood ran too hot and she shook with it, learning her the way he had once learned the liturgy, line by line, without flinching at the hard passages.

Love beginning where duty failed them both. She had not planned it. She suspected neither had he. Some things you do not build so much as stop preventing.

* * *

In the afternoon, when the children had gone, they walked up past the hives to the ridge, because the bees were swarming—a small, restless knot of gold hanging from the plum branch like a second blossom—and someone had to talk them down into the new hive Ilya had built over the winter, clumsy and beautiful, joints pegged without a single nail.

"You could go back, you know," Ilya said, while Edmund worked the swarm with bare, scarred hands, humming the settling-song low in her throat. "The exile's ended. Formally, informally, however you want it. The lowlands are open. Half the world wants to see the woman who opened the Door. There are scholars in the capital who'd give their libraries to ask you a hundred questions."

"I know."

"The coast, even. You loved the coast, you told me. The storms come in off the sea there like they're coming home." He paused. "You could call a real storm again. Not these polite valley drizzles. Something with teeth."

Edmund eased the swarm off the branch, a living handful of summer weighing almost nothing, and set them gently at the hive's mouth. For a moment she just stood there, feeling the small thunder of them against her palms, thousands of wings making a weather of their own.

"I spent ten years," she said finally, "learning how to be invisible. How to be nobody, from nowhere, tending bees. And then I spent one winter learning that I'd rather be somebody from somewhere that doesn't exist anymore than safe and erased." She turned to him. The light came slantwise through the blossoms and caught the map-lines on her hands. "But here's the thing Ilya—I'm not going back to Vessany. Nobody is. Vessany isn't a place you return to. It's a thing we're carrying now. All of us. In the names, in the hymns, in whatever this is—" she gestured at the valley, the bees, the man, the whole improbable peace of it, "—that we're building here."

"And the Hollow?" he asked quietly. "Magic can live here now. The valley's silence broke when the Door did. You could feel it anywhere."

"I could." She smiled, and it was the real smile, the rare one, the one that took years off her. "But I spent ten years learning every stone of this valley pretending I didn't love it. Turns out I did the whole time. You can't unlearn a home just because you lied about it. Besides—" she nodded toward the path below, where small figures were already scrambling back up through the orchard, drawn by the smell of the honey-bread Ilya had left cooling on the sill, "—my students are coming, and the fourth verse won't teach itself."

Ilya laughed then, that rough, surprised laugh he still seemed startled to own, and put his arm around her shoulders, and together they walked down through the falling blossom-snow toward the house, the smoke, the waiting bread, the ordinary miraculous day.

Behind them the swarm settled, humming, into its new home.

Above them the sky was only a sky.

And far to the north, where Vessany used to be, the land lay green and empty and remembering—and on the wind that came down from those hills, faint but unmistakable, Edmund heard singing. Not the gods. Not the dead.

Something new. Something that sounded almost like children, learning a fourth verse.
