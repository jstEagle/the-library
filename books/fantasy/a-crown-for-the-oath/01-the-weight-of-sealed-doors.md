## 1. The Weight of Sealed Doors

The wards were humming wrong again.

Wren Galloway felt it before they heard it—a faint dissonance in the soles of their boots, the way a musician feels a string gone sour before the note reaches the ear. They stood in the antechamber of the Hollowgate, lantern raised, and listened. The gate itself was a slab of black stone older than any kingdom, set into the hillside like a door into the root of the world, and around its frame ran the ward-lines, silvered channels cut into rock that had been old when the first kings of Ellsmere were still learning to write their names. Those lines should have sung one continuous note, low and steady as a sleeping heartbeat.

Instead they stuttered. A hitch, every seventh pulse, like a breath caught and released too quickly.

Wren pressed two fingers to the stone beside the gate's left hinge and closed their eyes. Through the skin came the familiar cold—not the cold of winter but the cold of *elsewhere*, the particular chill that seeped from the Otherworld through the seal, thin as water through cloth. It had been thinner lately. That was the trouble. For ten years Wren had kept this gate, and for ten years the cold had come through at exactly the same strength, patient and constant as tide. Now it varied. Some nights it barely whispered. Tonight it pressed against their fingers like a cat leaning on a closed door, wanting out—or perhaps wanting in; with the Otherworld it had never been entirely clear which side was which.

"Talking to it again?" said Bram from the doorway behind them.

Wren didn't startle. Ten years of sharing a roof with Keeper Bram Hollis had worn away most of Wren's flinches. "It's hitching. Seventh pulse."

Bram came forward into the lantern light. He was seventy if he was a day, built like something the mountain had decided to grow rather than carve—broad, weathered, slow to move and impossible to shift once planted. His beard had gone the color of ash and his hands, when he lifted the lantern to inspect the ward-line himself, were spotted and scarred in patterns Wren had never asked about. Guild keepers learned early not to ask about other keepers' scars.

"Hmm," said Bram, which was what he always said, and which meant nothing and everything.

"It's been three weeks now. It started small. I thought it was the frost getting into the outer channel." Wren traced the silver line with a fingertip, careful not to break the skin of light that lay over it. "But I re-packed the mortar at the solstice and it didn't change anything. And tonight—" they hesitated, then said it plainly, because lying to Bram was like lying to a wall, "tonight it felt like it was *leaning*."

Bram lowered the lantern. In its glow his face did something complicated and brief, there and gone, like a fish turning under dark water. "Gates lean," he said. "Hills lean. Everything leans a little. You'll wear yourself hollow chasing a hum."

"You've been saying that for three weeks."

"And you've been counting pulses for three weeks, and we're both still here, and supper's going cold." He turned for the stair, unhurried, certain that the world would wait for him because it always had. "Come on. The ledger won't balance itself, and if you leave those figures overnight they breed."

Wren lingered a moment longer, hand flat against the black stone. Through the palm, the cold leaned. Then they followed him up.

* * *

The Vellum Guild kept its accounts in a ledger that no honest accountant would survive reading.

It sat on its iron stand in the scriptorium above the gate-chamber: a book the size of a paving slab, bound in grey leather gone soft as doe-skin with handling, its pages thick as felt and crowded edge to edge with entries in a hundred different hands across four centuries. Most ledgers recorded what was bought and sold. This one recorded what had been *moved*—and moved between worlds, which made all the difference.

*One dream of flight, sold to a miller's daughter in Lowmarsh, payment: seven years of her father's shadow.* *One grief, returned to a widow who had sold hers in youth and found she could not mourn her husband properly without it.* *One door-knock heard from the far side of a locked room, delivered to a condemned man the night before his pardon, price unknown, entry marked PAID IN KIND.*

Wren worked down the columns by lamplight, quill scratching, matching entries against entries. This was the night's true labor, the one no one outside the Guild ever saw: not guarding the gate, though that mattered, but keeping the books of impossible things balanced. Every gift that crossed from the Otherworld into the human realm had to be answered by something crossing back—an echo, a debt, a weight on the other pan of the scale. If the ledger drifted, the covenant drifted. And if the covenant drifted—

Well. Wren had been taught the consequences in their first year, when they were nine years old and the Guild had taken them in off the streets of Ellsmere with nothing but lice and a talent for noticing things. Keeper Aldous had drawn them a picture: two bowls on a balance-beam, one filled with water, one empty. "The world you know is the full bowl," he'd said. "The world behind the gate is the empty one. The covenant is the beam. You are the hand under it. Forget that, and both bowls hit the floor."

Wren had never forgotten. They checked the beam nightly, and for ten years it had held level within a hair's breadth.

Tonight it did not.

They found it at the bottom of the current page, in the running tally where the year's crossings were summed. The column of gifts given out by the Otherworld ran long—longer than usual, longer than any year Wren could remember, though each individual entry looked innocent enough. A charm against fever here. A whisper of luck there. Small things, the kind of trades the Guild brokered a dozen times a season. But summed together, the column outweighed its partner by a margin that made Wren's stomach tighten. The return column—the payments flowing back across the gate—was short. Short by a quantity that had a shape, though Wren couldn't have said what shape. It was like looking at an empty chair and knowing someone had just risen from it.

They went back through the last month. The imbalance began twenty-six days ago. Twenty-six days ago, the wards had begun to hitch on the seventh pulse.

"Bram," Wren called, not looking up. "When did you last audit the return column?"

A pause from the hearth, where Bram was doing something to a pot that involved more violence than cooking usually required. "Audit it yourself. You're the keeper of record."

"I am. And it's short."

Silence. The kind of silence that has furniture in it. Wren looked up. Bram stood with his back to them, stirring the pot, and his shoulders had gone very still in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"How short?" he said, to the stew.

"Enough to matter. Enough that I should have seen it weeks ago, and the only reason I didn't is that the entries are all small and honest-looking." Wren laid the quill down. "Someone's been drawing on the other side. Many someones, or one someone many times. And nobody's paying."

Bram stirred the stew. Stirred it again. Then he turned around, and his face was carefully, deliberately mild, which was how Wren knew something was badly wrong, because Bram's face was naturally about as mild as a landslide.

"Ledgers drift," he said. "Cold snaps make folk buy charms. Fever-year does it too. It'll right itself."

"That's the third time you've told me it'll right itself. You don't believe it either." Wren rose. The chair scraped. "You've known about the humming since before I did. Don't think I haven't noticed you walking the outer circuit at odd hours, or that you've stopped letting me handle the deep-vault keys. Something's coming through the gate, or trying to, or being *pulled*—"

"Wren."

"—and you're treating me like an apprentice who can't be trusted with—"

"*Wren.*" Bram set down the spoon. When he spoke again his voice was low and even, and underneath the evenness ran something Wren had heard only twice in ten years, both times in stories about the bad years: fear. "Sit down. Eat your supper. Tomorrow I'll tell you what needs telling, and not before, because tonight I need to think, and I think better when you're fed and quiet."

Wren stood there with their heart hammering and their argument dying in their throat. Ten years ago, a frightened child had learned that when Bram Hollis used that voice, the world had already changed shape somewhere out of sight, and arguing with him about it only wasted time that thinking needed.

They sat. They ate. The stew was mutton and barley and too much salt, and it was, as it had been a thousand nights, the best meal in the world, because it was supper at home.

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and Bram had gone up to his cell with his lamp, Wren took the lantern down to the gate-chamber one more time. Not to check anything. Just to be near it, the way you sit up with a sick friend.

The black stone waited in the dark, patient as it had been for four thousand years. Wren sat on the keeper's bench opposite it, wrapped in a blanket, and let the silence settle.

"Whatever you are," they murmured to the gate, "you've been good to us. To me. Ten years of listening to me talk at you like a fool." The cold breathed against their face, faint and uneven. "So I'm asking. Whatever's wrong—and *something's* wrong, don't pretend otherwise—hold on a little longer. We'll sort it. Bram knows something he isn't saying, and tomorrow he'll say it, and whatever it is, we'll meet it. That's the whole job, isn't it? You hold. I hold. Nobody lets go."

The hitch came, seventh pulse, right on schedule, like a cough in the next room.

Wren stayed until the candle burned low, watching the silver ward-lines breathe their broken rhythm, and told themselves the dread sitting in their chest was only the cold coming off the stone. It was not. It was the particular dread of someone who has spent ten years learning every sound a house makes, and who has just heard it clear its throat.

Upstairs, behind his closed door, Bram sat awake with a page of vellum on his knee—one page, loose, hidden these twenty years inside the binding of a ledger—and did not sleep at all.

And far to the south, in the city of Ellsmere, criers were being woken in the small hours and handed proclamations bearing the king's own seal, to be read aloud at every crossroads by dawn:

*By order of Aldric, King of Ellsmere and the Marchlands: let every bearer of the gift present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who conceal the gift are traitors to the crown. Traitors burn.*

The ink was not yet dry.
