## 2. A Proclamation in Ash

The bells woke Ellsmere before the sun did.

Wren Galloway had lived beneath the sound of those bells for ten years—matins, markets, funerals—and knew them the way a sailor knows weather. But this was none of those. This was the alarm peal, the one rung only when riders came with royal seal and royal fire, and it dragged Wren out of a thin sleep in the guildhouse loft with their heart already hammering against their ribs like something trying to escape.

Below, Bram was already moving. Wren heard him on the stairs, slow and deliberate as ever, the tap of his walking stick marking each step like a metronome that refused to be hurried. By the time Wren pulled on their coat and came down, the old Keeper stood in the doorway of the common room, dressed properly for winter, his white beard combed, his face arranged into something almost like calm.

"You heard," Wren said. Not a question.

"I heard." Bram did not look at them. He looked out through the frost-furred window toward the market square, where lanterns were blooming one by one in the dark like feverish stars. "Put your cloak on, child. If we're to hear our death sentence read aloud, we'll do it standing up, among our neighbors, like honest folk."

*Our death sentence.* The words settled in Wren's chest like swallowed stones.

* * *

They gathered in the square with everyone else, because there was nowhere else to go and because not going would have been its own confession. Ellsmere was a town of perhaps four hundred souls, huddled at the edge of the king's maps where the roads went ragged and the hills remembered older names. Wren had counted every roof from the Hollowgate's ridge a hundred times. They knew which chimneys smoked first in the morning—the baker's, then the tanner's, then old Marta's by the well, who burned wet wood because she was too proud to ask for dry. They knew which doors stuck in the rain, which dogs bit, which marriages were kind and which were quiet wars.

And now they watched all of it—all of them—press together in the freezing dark, breath rising in a single gray cloud over the square, while three riders in the king's black-and-scarlet took positions before the moot-stone.

The crier dismounted first. He was a broad man with a voice built for harbors and battlefields, and he unrolled his proclamation with the reverence of a priest unveiling a relic. Beside him, two witch-hunters sat their horses without moving, and it was the hunters everyone looked at, not the crier. Their armor was enameled matte-black, unornamented except for the badge at the breast: a closed eye stitched in scarlet thread. They wore no plumes. They wore nothing bright. Brightness, Wren supposed, was for men who wanted to be seen. These were men who wanted to be *remembered* being seen.

The crier struck the moot-stone thrice with an iron rod, and the sound cracked across the square like ice giving way.

"By order of Aldric, third of that name, King of Vellmark and the Marchlands, Protector of the True Faith—" the voice rolled out over the rooftops, scattering rooks from the belfry "—be it known to all subjects: the Gift is a corruption of the natural order, a door by which devils enter the world of men."

Wren felt Bram shift beside them. Just slightly. Just enough that the old man's shoulder pressed against theirs, warm through wool.

"Every bearer of the Gift shall present themselves to a Crown registrar within forty days of this proclamation, to be named, numbered, and bound to lawful service of the throne. Those who come freely shall be fed, housed, and granted the king's protection." A pause, engineered, practiced. "Those who conceal the Gift in themselves or shelter it in others—shall burn."

The word fell into the square and lay there.

*Burn.* Not executed. Not imprisoned. Burned, spoken plainly, in the voice men used for grain prices and road tolls, as though it were simply another matter of civic administration.

Wren's hands, buried in their pockets, had gone cold past feeling. They thought absurdly of the ledger upstairs, of ink and vellum, of the careful columns where impossible things were recorded in ordinary handwriting. *Entry the ninth: a storm bottled in green glass, traded for a widow's grief. Entry the tenth:* —and now this. A whole kingdom entered into the ledger's opposite page, names written down not to be kept but to be hunted.

"Let no man plead ignorance," the crier continued. "The Gift may hide in your neighbor. It may hide in your wife. It hides most cunningly in children, who know not what they carry. It is therefore the duty of every loyal subject to *observe*, to *report*, and to *testify*. Rewards are established for true report. Penalties await false silence." He let that hang, then rolled the parchment closed with a snap. "Gods keep the king."

For a moment, nobody moved. Four hundred people stood in the ash-gray light and breathed, and did not look at one another, and that was the worst thing Wren had ever seen—not the hunters on their black horses, but four hundred pairs of eyes suddenly learning how *not* to meet.

Then someone coughed, and the square broke apart like a kicked anthill, everyone flowing back toward their houses and stalls and duties with terrible, studied normalcy.

* * *

Wren stayed. So did a dozen others, frozen in place, watching the riders. One of the hunters finally turned his horse—a heavy destrier, breath steaming from its nostrils—and walked it slowly along the square's edge. Not hurrying. Looking. At the well, at the shrine alcove, at the faces of those who remained. His gaze passed over Wren the way a hand passes over cloth, checking for lumps beneath.

Wren kept their face blank. Ten years of practice made it easy; that was the horror of it, how easy it had become. They had spent a decade learning to be furniture, to be wallpaper, to be a person no eye snagged on. The gift in them—the small, humming, door-shaped thing coiled behind their sternum—lay flat and still, the way a hare lies still under a hawk's shadow.

But the hunter paused at Marta's door by the well.

Old Marta herself stood in it, wrapped in three shawls, her chin up in the way of the very old and very stubborn. "Can I help you, sir?" she said, loud enough for the square to hear, and there was something in her voice Wren had never heard there before. Fear, yes—but underneath it, a strange brittle defiance.

"Name," said the hunter.

"Marta Coss. Same as it's been sixty years."

"Anyone else live in the house?"

"My cat."

Someone near the bakehouse laughed—a short, terrified bark of laughter that cut off instantly. The hunter turned his head toward the sound, slow as a door swinging, and the laughter's owner became intensely fascinated with his own boots.

"No cat is registered in this town's census," the hunter said.

"It's a *cat*."

"Everything that lives under a loyal subject's roof is registered. Everything unregistered is suspect." He produced a slate from his saddlebag and wrote something on it. "Marta Coss. One unregistered animal. To be verified."

He rode on. Marta stayed in her doorway until he'd gone, and then Wren saw her shoulders come down, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and she went inside and shut her sticking door, and the door caught twice before it closed, because it always caught, because Wren had been meaning to plane it for her for three years—

*Three years of meaning to, and now maybe I never will.*

That thought frightened Wren more than the proclamation had. Fear arrived first as a shout, but it stayed as arithmetic. Forty days. Names, numbered, bound—or burning. And between here and the burning, a town full of neighbors suddenly instructed to watch each other, paid to report each other, punished for silence. Wren had grown up hearing stories of the last hunt, eighty years gone, when the crown had tried this before and failed—but the stories all agreed on one detail, the one that mattered: it hadn't been the hunters who found the gifted. It had been the *neighbors*. The brothers. The wives. The friend you'd trusted since childhood, testifying with tears in his eyes and reward coin in his purse.

The Guild's shield had always been anonymity. Small trades, quiet favors, no names in any crown ledger, a brotherhood so careful it barely admitted it existed to itself. Wren had believed in that shield the way children believe in walls.

Standing in the emptying square, watching the hunters' backs shrink down the south road, Wren understood—with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down the spine—that anonymity was not a wall. It was a clock. It had always been a clock, ticking, and today someone had finally told them what hour it was.

Forty days.

* * *

They walked home through streets performing normalcy. The baker had opened after all; the smell of bread drifted out and nobody bought any, because nobody wanted to stand in line beside anyone else longer than necessary. Two women who had gossiped together at that same corner every morning of Wren's memory stood a full arm's length apart and spoke about turnips.

At the corner by the chandler's, Wren stopped.

Tomas Reeve was there—young Tomas, nineteen, who mended nets and had once spent an entire summer helping Wren repair the guildhouse roof for nothing but suppers and stories. He was talking to Dannel Fitch, the farrier's son, and they were speaking low, and when Wren came within earshot both of them stopped, and the silence had a shape to it, a weight, and Wren saw Tomas glance at Dannel and Dannel glance away and *something pass between them*, some small agreement or suspicion, some seed.

"Wren," Tomas said, too brightly. "Cold morning."

"Freezing," Wren agreed, and smiled, and walked on, and did not let their stride change, and counted their own heartbeat the whole way down the lane.

*Tomas. Who I have known since he was in leading-strings. Whose father I healed of the lung-rot with a tea from the Otherworld's border-meadows, quietly, free, because Guild law says mercy is traded forward and never priced.*

Would Tomas remember that? Would it make him loyal—or would it make Wren *evidence*? That was the trap inside the proclamation, Wren realized. Kindness done in secret could now be recast as crime discovered. Every favor the Guild had ever done was a hook waiting for a fisherman. The choice each neighbor faced was no longer *do I believe in witches* but *what will it cost me if I say nothing, and what will it pay me if I speak*—and Wren had watched enough of humanity, in ledgers and in life, to know how most of those sums would be worked.

Fear was already doing its work, and it was only morning.

* * *

The guildhouse felt different when Wren shut the door behind them. Smaller. As though the walls had drawn in overnight, listening.

Bram was in the kitchen, and he had not lit the stove. He sat at the long table with both hands flat on its surface, staring at nothing, and when Wren came in he didn't startle, didn't move at all—which was itself alarming, because Bram startled at everything, had the nervous heart of a man who'd survived too many decades by noticing.

"They'll come within the season," Bram said, without preamble. "Not for us first. First they'll take the towns along the border roads. Test the machinery on people nobody will miss." He tapped one finger against the table, once. "Then the registries will cross-reference. Trade records. Grain bought in quantities too large for one household. Candles sold in midsummer. Travelers who arrive at night." Another tap. "Anonymity is arithmetic too, child. And our sums have always left a remainder."

"The wards," Wren said. "The humming. That's why you wouldn't tell me."

Bram was quiet a long moment. The light through the kitchen window was strengthening, gray turning to white, and it caught the old man's face and showed Wren things they had trained themselves not to see for years: the depth of the creases, the tremor in the hands, the exhaustion of a man carrying a secret the way a mule carries stone, uphill, forever.

"The wards hum because the Hollowgate hums," Bram said at last. "It has been humming wrong for weeks, and I have been trying to decide whether telling you would protect you or destroy you." He pushed back his chair. It scraped, loud as the iron rod on the moot-stone. "I've decided this morning decides for me. Come upstairs. Bring the ledger."

"Bram—"

"*Bring the ledger,* Wren." And there was something in his voice Wren had never heard in ten years of shared suppers and shared silences: urgency wearing the mask of calm, and beneath the mask, fear.

Wren fetched the ledger.

It was the Guild's great book, the one that looked from outside like an inventory of a candle merchant's warehouse—because that was its genius, its camouflage, the joke only Keepers got to appreciate. *Vellum & Sons, Purveyors of Fine Wax, Est. Under Favorable Signs.* Three hundred years of impossible trades recorded in the language of commerce. Wren carried it up the narrow stairs to Bram's study, where the shutters were closed and the air smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of the gate itself—that cold mineral smell, like rain on old stone, that clung to everything Wren owned whether they warded it or not.

Bram had cleared the desk. In its center lay a knife—not a working blade but an old one, ceremonial, its handle wound in wire gone green with age—and a shallow dish of salt, and a length of candle he had not yet lit.

"What is this?" Wren set the ledger down. "What are you doing?"

"What I should have done the day I named you keeper." Bram opened the ledger to its middle, to the spine, and Wren saw for the first time in ten years that the binding there was different—newer thread, darker, worked in a pattern they didn't recognize. "There are pages in every book, child, and then there are pages that hold other pages. Sit down. We haven't much time, and I am an old man who talks slowly, and what I have to say will take longer than we have."

Outside, far off, a dog began to bark, and did not stop.

Bram drew the knife across his thumb and let three falls of blood strike the spine's stitching, and the stitching drank the blood the way dry ground drinks rain, and the ledger opened—to somewhere it had never opened before. Wren leaned close despite every instinct screaming caution, and saw a page that was not vellum at all but something paler, something that seemed to hold light the way snow holds footprints, and on it, in a script older than the kingdom, older than the alphabet it resembled, words that Wren's gift recognized before their mind did.

The words hummed. The same note the wards had been humming for weeks. The same note, Wren understood now with sickening clarity, as the Hollowgate itself.

"This," Bram said softly, "is why they're really hunting the gift. Not fear. Hunger. And this page—" his bloodied thumb hovered above it without touching "—is the reason you were born, Wren Galloway, or near enough."

The dog outside stopped barking. In its place: hoofbeats, many, coming up the hill road toward the guildhouse. Lantern-light flickered through the shutter seams, though the sun was fully risen.

Bram looked up, and for the first time since Wren had known him, the old Keeper looked afraid.

"Too soon," he whispered. "They weren't due till spring. *Who told them?*"
