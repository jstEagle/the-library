## 3. The Ledger of Names

The snow came down in fat, unhurried flakes that evening, the kind that muffled the world and made the Hollowgate hill feel like the last quiet place in creation. Wren Galloway climbed the switchback path with a basket on their arm and a knot of unease riding between their shoulder blades, the way it had ridden there for six weeks now, ever since the wards had begun to hum.

Not sing. Hum. There was a difference, and every keeper learned it in their first year. A ward that sang was a ward at peace, a low sweet note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and you could walk beneath it your whole life and never think twice. A ward that hummed was a ward thinking. Working. Bracing itself against something on the far side of its own skin, the way a man braces against a door he suspects someone is about to push.

Wren had asked Bram about it eleven times. Eleven times Bram had given them one of his answers: *old stone settling*, or *the moon's doing*, or, once, memorably, *mind your supper before it minds you*. Bram was seventy-one years old and had kept the gate for forty years before passing the night-watch to Wren, and he had a genius for answers that were technically true and entirely useless. But tonight, when Wren had mentioned the humming again over breakfast, Bram had set down his spoon, looked at them for a long moment with those pale river-grey eyes, and said only: "Come up after dark. Bring nothing but yourself."

So Wren brought supper anyway, because Bram was seventy-one and stubborn as a gatepost, and because if the world was about to end it seemed poor manners to face it hungry. Bread still warm from the guildhouse ovens, hard cheese, a jar of pickled beets that Bram pretended not to love and always finished. The basket swung against their hip as they climbed, and above them the ruined arch of the Hollowgate rose against the darkening sky like the ribcage of something enormous that had lain down to die a thousand years ago and never quite finished.

It was beautiful, in the way that wounds are beautiful. Twice the height of three men, black stone shot through with veins of silver that caught whatever light was offered and gave back more than it received. The doorway itself was empty—no door, no gate, just a frame of standing emptiness through which you could see the hillside beyond, the pines, the snow. Most folk from Ellsmere who came up this far—and few did—saw exactly that. An old ruin. A curiosity. They'd picnic in its shadow in summer and never feel the way the air changed inside the frame, the way sound went slightly wrong, the way your own heartbeat seemed suddenly audible, as though the emptiness were listening.

Wren felt all of it, every time, the way you feel a hand laid gently on the back of your neck. Ten years of nights, and the touch had never once become familiar. They suspected it wasn't supposed to.

Bram was waiting beneath the arch, wrapped in his good cloak, the one with the fox-fur collar he claimed had come from a king's hunt and which everyone knew had come from a peddler's cart. He'd built a small fire against the gate's eastern pillar, sheltered from the wind, and he stood warming his hands with his back to Wren, which was unlike him; Bram liked to watch people arrive, liked to read their walks the way other men read letters.

"You brought food," he said, without turning.

"I brought food."

"I said bring nothing but yourself."

"You're seventy-one. You'll want the beets."

A pause. Then, grudgingly: "I'll want the beets." He turned, and Wren saw his face properly for the first time, and the easy banter died somewhere in their chest. Bram looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with age. He looked like a man who had been carrying something heavy for a very long time and had finally decided, tonight, to set it down—or to show someone else where it sat, so that when his arms gave out, someone would know how to lift it.

They ate by the fire. Bram ate slowly, deliberately, and told two stories about his early years as keeper—one involving a goat, one involving a visiting Guild assessor and an unfortunate enchantment upon his trousers—and Wren laughed in the right places and waited, because they understood now that the stories were not stalling. They were a kind of blessing. A last ordinary hour, being saved up like coins.

When the plates were empty and the fire had burned down to a red heart, Bram brushed crumbs from his knees and said, "Show me your hands."

Wren held them out, palms up. Old habit. Every month of their ten years, some variation of this: *show me your hands*, and Bram would turn them over, trace the lines, check the faint silvery scarring across both palms where, at seventeen, half-terrified and wholly ignorant, Wren had pressed their bleeding hands against the gate-stone and the gate had *answered*.

But tonight Bram didn't take their wrists. Tonight he reached into his coat and drew out a knife—a small one, plain-hilted, the kind used for trimming wicks—and held it out across his open palm.

"Again," he said. "One drop. Left palm. I need to show you something, and it won't show for me anymore. My blood's gone quiet. Yours hasn't."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere down the hill, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"Bram," Wren said carefully, "what is this?"

"The thing I should have shown you nine years ago," Bram said. "And didn't, because I'm a coward in the specific way that careful men are cowards—I keep telling myself there'll be a better night, a safer night, and the nights don't get better, Wren. They only get fewer." He nodded at the knife. "Please."

Wren took the knife. The steel was cold and honest, and it bit the heel of their left palm easily, a thin bright line, and they held the fist out over the fire and let one drop fall—not into the flames, but onto the flat grey stone at the arch's base, where the foundation-stone lay flush with the earth, worn smooth by centuries.

The drop did not soak in. It sat on the stone for a heartbeat, gleaming, and then it *moved*—crawled in a fine bright thread along channels so shallow they might have been scratches, hairline lines radiating out from the foundation-stone in patterns too regular for nature, and the silver veins in the great black pillars flickered, once, like a sleeper shifting under a blanket. And then, from the stone itself, from a seam Wren had walked past ten thousand times without ever seeing, a shape rose.

It was a book.

Not conjured—*revealed*, the way a face is revealed when a veil is lifted. A ledger bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth with age, its corners guarded in dull metal, its cover stamped with a sigil Wren knew intimately: the interlocked circle and key of the Vellum Guild. It sat on the frozen ground as though it had always been there, as though the hill had simply been holding it, waiting.

"Ten years you've tended the wards," Bram said quietly. "Balanced the ledger. Walked the perimeter. And never once asked why the Guild keeps a gatekeeper at a ruin with no door."

"I asked," Wren said. Their voice came out strange. "You gave me answers."

"I gave you answers," Bram agreed. "Because the truth has a weight, and I watched my predecessor die under it, and I told myself you'd have enough weight soon enough without me adding mine." He crouched, groaning, and lifted the ledger, and held it out. "This is the Ledger of Names. Not the copy in the guildhouse vault—the *original*. Every covenant, every pact, every bargain struck between the human realm and the Otherworld since before either realm had names worth using. And this—" he opened it, and his thumb moved through pages of dense, beautiful script, past seals and sigils and columns of terms written in ink that shifted color when the light touched it wrong, "—this page. The first page. The root of all of it."

He turned to it, and Wren leaned close, and the cold went through them deeper than the snow ever could.

The page was older than the rest. The vellum had gone amber and fragile, and the writing upon it was in no script Wren knew, and yet—as their eyes moved across it—the meaning arrived directly, wordlessly, the way the gate's listening arrived. *Covenant*, the page said, in the voice of something vast and patient and tired. *Between the realm of humankind and the realm beyond the hollow places. Sealed by blood of the keeper line. Renewed each hundred years at midwinter, when the veil wears thinnest. The gate opens to the keeper's blood alone. The balance holds while the keeper holds.*

"While the keeper holds," Wren repeated. The words tasted strange, like a phrase overheard through a wall. "Holds what? Holds the gate?"

Bram closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a dying man's eyes, and looked at Wren across the fire, and everything in his face was apology.

"Holds the *balance*," he said. "Wren. Listen to me now, and don't interrupt, because I've rehearsed this for nine years and I'll lose my nerve if you give me room." He drew a breath. "The gift isn't a disease. It isn't a corruption. It's a *leak*. When the covenant was struck, the two realms were knitted together along their whole border, and power passed between them the way water passes through a shared wall—gently, evenly, both sides drinking. But walls wear. Borders fray. Three hundred years ago the covenant began to fail, and the leak became a flood in some places and a drought in others. Children born knowing what locks are thinking. Women whose words lit candles. Men who dreamed true. The Guild was founded to manage it—to find the gifted, record them, teach them, protect them, and above all to keep the ledger, because the ledger is the covenant's memory, and a covenant without memory is just a wound."

"And the gate?"

"The gate is the knot in the middle of it all. The place where the wall was first built. And it doesn't open for keys or spells or armies." Bram's pale eyes held theirs, steady and sorrowful. "It opens for keeper's blood. That's why we chose you, Wren. Not because you were clever—you are—and not because you were careful—you weren't, at seventeen—but because your grandmother's grandmother stood where you're standing, and her blood signed the covenant's renewal, and blood like that remembers. Your blood isn't just yours. It's a *key cut generations ago*, waiting for a lock. We found you when you were sixteen, working the dye-works, and we watched you for a year before Bram—" he stopped, corrected himself with a ghost of a smile, "before *I* approached you. Because taking a keeper means taking a life and reshaping it around a duty, and I wanted to be sure. And then I was sure, and I brought you here, and I taught you wards and ledgers and patience, and I let you believe the work was smaller than it is. Because if I'd told you what you were—if I'd told you that the covenant comes due at midwinter, *this* midwinter, twenty-two days from now—that the renewal must be spoken by keeper's blood at the Hollowgate or the border fails entire—"

His voice cracked, just barely, on the last word, and Wren heard what lived underneath the crack, and their whole body went cold.

"Bram. What happens if it fails?"

"The wall comes down," Bram said simply. "All of it, at once. Everything the Otherworld is pours through, and everything we are pours back, and neither side survives the mixing. It's not an invasion, Wren. It's a *drowning*. And the crown—" he laughed, short and bitter, "—the crown has been feeling the fraying for years. That's what the Gift-Hunt truly is. Not fear. *Harvest*. Aldric's witch-finders aren't burning the gifted—they're taking them, and feeding them to something, trying to force the gate open on their own terms rather than kneel to a covenant. And the wards have been humming for six weeks because the gate knows what's coming. It can feel them coming. All of them. The hunters, the hunger, the hundred-year turning. Everything arrives at once, at midwinter."

The fire popped. Snow hissed softly where drifts crept too near.

Wren looked down at their palm. The thin cut had already sealed itself—keeper's blood did that, another small strangeness they'd carried for a decade without asking why—and they flexed the hand, and thought of ten years of suppers and ledgers and songs and the small warm rooms of the guildhouse below, and felt the shape of their whole life rearrange itself around this new weight, the way water rearranges itself around a dropped stone.

"You should have told me," they said. It wasn't anger. It was something quieter and worse.

"Yes," Bram said. "I know." He put the ledger in their hands. It was heavier than any book had a right to be, heavy the way trust is heavy. "I'm telling you now. That has to count for something, even if it counts for less than it should."

"It counts," Wren said, and meant it, and hated that they meant it, and loved him for it, all at once, in the tangle that was Bram.

And that was when they saw it—over Bram's shoulder, down the long white sweep of the southern slope, where the pines broke and the road from Ellsmere climbed toward the gate.

Lanterns.

Five of them. Six. More behind, strung out along the switchback like a rosary of small orange fires, bobbing with the rhythm of men walking fast and armed. Even at this distance, even through falling snow, Wren could see the light was wrong—it burned too steady, too white-hearted, the flame of lanterns fed on something other than oil. Hunter's fire. Consecrated flame, the kind that showed a gift no matter how deep it hid.

Bram had seen them too. He didn't startle. He'd known, Wren realized. He'd known they were coming—known for days, maybe weeks—and that was why tonight, why the ledger, why the confession dragged out of nine years of cowardice at the final possible hour. He'd waited until the last night there would ever be.

"How many?" Wren whispered.

"Enough," Bram said. He rose, joints cracking like green wood, and his hand went to Wren's shoulder and gripped hard. "Listen. The page—the first page—is the covenant entire. Without it, the rite can't be spoken, and without the rite, midwinter ends us all. The hunters will tear the guildhouse apart looking for it, and they'll find the decoy vault, the false ledger, the pretty trap we've kept for exactly this day. But they cannot find *this*." He pushed Wren back from the firelight, into the arch's shadow, where the gate's listening presence settled over them like a second cloak. "You take it. You take the iron key from my belt. You go down the north path, the one that doesn't exist unless you've bled on the gate-stone, and you don't stop until Ellsmere is a memory behind you."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are." Bram smiled, and it was the saddest thing Wren had ever seen, sadder than the burning that was surely already beginning below. "That's the whole of keeping, Wren. Someone always stays with the door." He stepped out of the shadow, toward the fire, toward the path, an old man in a fox-fur collar placing himself between the hill and the lanterns like a sentence placed between a name and the axe. "Run true," he said. "And when you speak the covenant at midwinter—speak it for both of us."

Below, the first hunter's voice rang out against the rocks, high and hard: *"The Guild is named! None leave the hill!"*

Wren clutched the ledger to their chest, felt the gate lean against them like a living thing, and ran.

* * *

The north path was real only as long as Wren's blood remembered it. They felt it flicker under their boots—snow that held, then didn't, then held again—and behind them, growing, the orange bloom of fire where the guildhouse lights should have been only lanterns, and were becoming something else.

At the tree line Wren stopped, once, breath tearing, and looked back.

The guildhouse windows were glowing. Not with lamplight.

With fire.

And on the wind, thin and terrible, came the sound of the wards singing—for the first time in ten years, singing, high and clear and grieving, the note a gate makes when its keeper is about to be taken from it.

Wren Galloway pressed the ledger tighter, turned north into the dark, and did not look back again.
