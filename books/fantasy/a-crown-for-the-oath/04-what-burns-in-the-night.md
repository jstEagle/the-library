## 4. What Burns in the Night

The guildhouse burned the way a liar dies—quietly at first, then all at once.

Serah Vane stood at the edge of the dooryard and watched the fire take the roof, and she felt nothing that she would have called doubt. Doubt was a luxury for priests. She was an instrument, and instruments did not doubt; they measured. The Vellum Guild had trafficked in impossible things for two hundred years, and tonight the crown had finally come to collect its arithmetic.

"Vane." Captain Hollis of the witch-hunters jogged toward her through the trampled snow, his lantern swinging, throwing her own shadow back at her in long, shuddering strokes. "The cellar doors are warded. Iron won't bite them."

"Nothing is warded against patience," Serah said. "Surround the building first. No one leaves. Not through windows, not through chimneys, not through the ground itself if they can manage it. I've read the Guild's methods, Captain. They once moved an entire granary three miles to escape a tax assessor."

Hollis blinked. "Three miles."

"The ledger says so." She did not say which ledger, or how she had come to read it, or why the margins were annotated in a hand she had spent years learning to recognize without ever admitting she knew it. "Seal the perimeter. Then we wait for the fire to drive them down into their vaults, and the vaults will open when their wards starve."

The captain saluted and turned, bellowing orders into the dark. Around the guildhouse the hunters spread out like ink dropped in water, thirty lanterns becoming a noose. Above them the winter sky was clear and enormous and indifferent, stars sharp as nailheads, and beneath it Ellsmere's outlying farms slept on, shuttered against a light that was not theirs.

Serah turned back to the burning house.

It had been a beautiful building, once. She had seen sketches of it—the low stone bones of some older fortification wearing a merchant's face, slate roof, leaded windows, a door of black oak carved with interlacing patterns that scholars had politely called decorative. She had walked past it a dozen times in her years in Ellsmere, had bought bread from the baker two lanes over, had nodded to a gray-bearded man in the market who sold honey and never haggled.

She had known what he was. Of course she had known. Knowing was her whole life: knowing the miller's daughter who made dolls walk, knowing the ferryman whose boat never shipped water, knowing, knowing, knowing—and writing it all down in reports sealed with the king's wax, feeding names into the great machine of the Gift-Hunt as though she were feeding grain into a millstone.

Bram Halloway. Keeper of the Hollowgate. Third of his line to hold the office, or fourth—she had never been certain, because certainty about the Guild was like trying to hold water in a net. He sold honey. He remembered everyone's name. And he had sat across from her at the autumn fair, two months ago, sharing a bench in the cold sun while his horse cropped grass, and he had said:

*You look tired, child. When did you last eat something that wasn't eaten standing up?*

And he had pressed a jar of honey into her hands and refused her coin, and she had gone home and filed her quarterly report, and Bram Halloway's name had been on page nine.

She had written it herself. Her own pen. Her own hand, steady as always.

*You look tired, child.*

Serah flexed her fingers inside her gloves and made herself watch the fire.

* * *

They brought the prisoners out at moonset.

There were seven of them—apprentices mostly, young ones, dragged blinking from the smoke-black vaults where the wards had finally guttered out. Two scribes, older, their hands stained with the particular silver-gray ink the Guild used for its true records. And one old man, upright even in chains, his white beard singed at the edges, his eyes finding Serah across the dooryard as though the crowd of thirty hunters were merely weather between them.

He was not supposed to be here. That was the thing she could not stop noticing, the splinter working under her skin. The intelligence had said four residents. The intelligence had been hers. Seven meant the Guild had been hiding people inside its ordinary face for years—children, perhaps, or fugitives, or worse—and every report she had ever filed about the quiet honey-seller had been a portrait painted by a man who only showed her one room of his house.

"Line them up," Hollis said. "Inquisitor, do you want confession first, or—"

"Confession is a courtesy," Serah said. "We'll take inventory first. The Guild keeps a ledger of everything it trades in. Names. Debts. I want that ledger found before anything else burns."

"It's burning now, my lady."

"Then dig it out of the ash. Paper remembers longer than men think."

Two hunters went back into the smoking shell with iron hooks, raking through collapsed floors while the others held the apprentices in a row. One of them—a girl, fifteen at most, her braid half-burned away—was crying without any noise at all, tears cutting clean tracks through the soot on her face. Beside her, one of the old scribes kept trying to stand in front of her, shifting left each time a hunter shifted, a slow-motion dance of shielding that everyone present understood and no one acknowledged.

Serah walked the line slowly, boots crunching on frozen mud and scattered vellum scraps. The fire had gotten into the guildhouse's upper rooms, and pages were still drifting down out of the heat like black butterflies, settling on the snow, curling. She picked one up without thinking. A trade record: *one memory of a summer afternoon, sold by a farmer of Dunmore Vale, price: remission of a debt.* Below it, in different ink: *repaid in kind, with interest, per covenant terms.*

Per covenant terms. There it was again, that word, threading through everything the Guild touched like a vein of ore through dull rock. Covenant. She had found references to it in eleven separate documents over six years—a treaty, a contract, an oath, she could never pin down what form it took—but every document that described it directly was missing. Not destroyed. Missing. As though removed by careful hands, consistently, across two centuries of records.

Someone had been editing history, very thoroughly, very quietly, and Serah Vane had built a career on being the person who noticed exactly this kind of thing.

"Inquisitor." Hollis's voice had changed. She looked up. He was standing in front of Bram Halloway, and there was something uneasy in the set of his shoulders. "This one claims he's just a keeper. Says the gate business is old law, crown-recognized, predates the Gift-Hunt statutes by centuries. Says there are documents—"

"There are," Serah said. "I've looked for them for six years."

Bram's eyes met hers, and there was no fear in them, and that was somehow worse than hatred would have been. Hatred she could work with. Hatred was fuel. But he looked at her the way a teacher looks at a student who has answered every question correctly and learned nothing at all.

"You're the Vane girl," he said. His voice was roughened by smoke but steady underneath. "From the tanner's row. You used to bring your father's accounts to be checked, because you couldn't abide an error, even at eight years old."

"I don't recall."

"No," he agreed gently. "I expect you don't. Memory's a funny ledger, isn't it? Some entries you keep, and some entries someone else keeps for you." He lifted his chained hands, an odd, formal gesture, almost a bow. "You've come for a page, Inquisitor. I can see it in how you hold yourself—you've been sent for one specific thing, and everything else here is smoke to cover the taking of it. So ask me properly, and I might tell you where it isn't."

The cold went through Serah like a blade finding a gap in armor.

Because he was right. That was the intolerable part. Her orders—her sealed orders, above Hollis's clearance, above everyone's but the Lord Chancellor's—named a single objective: *the original covenant-page, secured intact, all other materials expendable.* All other materials. She looked at the crying girl, at the scribes, at the burning library behind them, and heard the phrase again in the Chancellor's dry voice: *all other materials expendable.*

"Where is the page?" she asked.

"Gone," Bram said, simply. "Gone where you can't follow, into a hand you can't catch. And before you put me to the question—" his gaze flicked, quick and human, to the girl with the burned braid, then back— "know that the page doesn't answer to force. It answers to blood, and only one sort. Whatever you do to me tonight, Inquisitor, you're holding an empty purse and shaking it."

"Captain," Serah said quietly, "take the prisoners to the wagons. This one stays with me."

* * *

They stood alone in the dooryard, the two of them, while the guildhouse settled into its own ashes behind them with sounds like slow sighing. Somewhere beyond the hedge, the hunters were loading the wagon, and a child's voice rose once in protest and was cut off—not by violence, Serah noted, but by a gag, which was procedure, which was lawful, which was somehow more obscene than violence would have been.

"You knew I'd come," she said.

"I hoped you wouldn't." Bram settled himself onto the mounting block by the ruined fence as though it were a church pew, chains clinking, an old man taking his ease. "Sit, if you like. My legs have earned a rest and yours have too, whatever your pride says."

"I'll stand."

"You always did." Something moved across his face that was not quite a smile. "Your father was the same. Stood through every negotiation, every service, every funeral. As though sitting would let the world get a grip on him." He studied her with those unhurried eyes. "Do you know what the gift feels like, Serah Vane?"

The night went very still around her.

"That's a strange question for a condemned man."

"It's the only question I have left, and I mean to spend it well." He tilted his head. "Not *do you have it*. I'm not accusing. I'm asking what it feels like. Which means either you know—and have known since you were small enough to hide it under your bed—or you don't, and my question is just an old man's foolishness. Either way, no one's listening but the snow."

Her heart was beating hard enough that she felt it in her throat, in her wrists, in her teeth. Thirty yards away, Hollis was arguing with a quartermaster about chains. The fire crackled and sank. No one was listening but the snow.

"I wouldn't know," she said.

"Mm." He looked at the fire. "When I was a boy, my grandmother kept bees. She'd put her bare hand into a living hive, gentle as you please, and the bees would crawl over her fingers and never sting. I asked her how, once. She said: *they know when you're afraid, and they know when you're lying about it, and most folk are doing both.*" He turned back to her. "The gift is a bit like the bees, child. It stirs when it's near its own. It stirred just now, standing in front of you, strong enough that my teeth hummed. Strong enough that I'd stake the last of my reputation—which, granted, is ash—that somewhere under all that iron and law, there's a hive of your own."

The world tilted, very slightly, and righted itself.

Serah became aware that she had stopped breathing. She made herself start again, made her face into the smooth official mask that had carried her through tribunals and interrogations and her father's funeral, and heard herself say, in the flat tone reserved for confessions of the guilty:

"You're attempting to corrupt an officer of the crown."

"No," Bram said. "I'm attempting to save one. It's less profitable, but the interest compounds better." He leaned forward as far as the chains allowed, and his voice dropped to something almost tender. "Listen to me, because I haven't much time and less reason to lie. The hunt you're running isn't what you think it is. Ask yourself why the crown wants a covenant-page *intact* instead of burned with the rest. Ask yourself why registration lists go to the Chancellor's private office and nowhere else. Ask yourself what a dying man pays any price to buy. Do that much, Serah. Just that. Before you sign another name onto a wagon list."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you'll be very good at your work," he said, "right up until the day your work is done with you. They always need the instrument until the job is finished. Then instruments get melted down." He smiled, and it was the smile of the honey-seller at the autumn fair, warm and rueful and unbearably kind. "*You look tired, child.*"

The words landed like a hand laid flat on a wound.

And the gift—

The gift stirred.

It came up through her like groundwater rising in a cellar, sudden and vast and unmistakable, a warmth behind her sternum that was not her heartbeat, a pressure behind her eyes as though the whole dark landscape had suddenly become legible—as though every shadow in the dooryard had printed itself on the inside of her skull in silver-gray ink. She felt the wards of the dead guildhouse, guttering out below the ash like embers in a banked stove. She felt the seven prisoners in the wagon, each one a small distinct flame. She felt the iron hooks in the ruins, singing faintly of everything they'd touched. She felt Bram Halloway, bright as a struck bell, already fading at the edges like a candle burned to its final inch.

And she felt him feel *her*—felt the old keeper register the flare of it, the way a man in a dark house registers a lamp lit in a window across the street—and saw his expression break open into something between grief and relief.

"Oh," he breathed. "Oh, there it is. There you are."

"Stop," Serah whispered. Her voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "Whatever you're doing—stop it."

"I'm doing nothing, child. That's the whole point. It's *you*."

She slammed down on it, whatever it was, hauling the warmth backward, downward, drowning it the way she had drowned every strange thing in herself since she was nine years old and had made a locked box open with a wish and been beaten for lying about how. The pressure receded, sulked, sank. The night became ordinary again: snow, ash, lantern-light, a chained old man watching her with terrible gentleness.

No one had seen. She checked—she could not help checking—and no one had seen. Hollis was still fifty feet away. The nearest lantern threw its light elsewhere. For six years she had hidden it; she could hide it for six more; she had built her entire self into a lockbox and the lockbox had never once failed.

Until now. Until a dying beekeeper had spoken to it directly, the way you'd speak to a child hiding in a cupboard.

"Captain!" she called, and her voice was steady, her voice was perfect, her voice was the finest instrument of statecraft in three counties. "Prepare the prisoner for transport."

"My lady." Hollis came jogging back, breath steaming. "Transport where? The warrant says the guildsmen go to the dungeons at Carrowmoor for trial."

"The warrant covers the apprentices and scribes." Serah heard the words leave her mouth and had a distant, vertiginous sense of watching someone else say them. "This one's different. He claims keeper's office under old crown law. That's a matter for the Chancery, not the courts. I'll take personal custody."

Hollis frowned. "Personal custody, my lady? Alone?"

"With two men of your choosing, Captain, whom you trust absolutely. I'll interview him en route and deliver a full deposition." A full deposition. Signed. Sealed. Filed in the great machine. *All other materials expendable.*

Bram was looking at her, and his eyes were shining, and she hated him for it.

*Ask yourself what a dying man pays any price to buy.*

*Just that much, Serah. Just that much.*

The wagon creaked under its load of prisoners. The girl with the burned braid stared at Serah through the slats with an expression that contained no hope at all, and Serah made herself meet it, made herself hold it, filing it away in whatever chamber of herself was not yet a lockbox—the chamber where she kept her father's accounts, checked twice, because she could not abide an error, even at eight years old, even now.

"Move out in ten minutes," she told Hollis. "Ellsmere road, then north. I want the guildhouse grounds searched again at dawn—if a single scrap of vellum survived, I'll have it catalogued personally."

"Yes, my lady."

He went. The lanterns began to gather themselves up, a noose untying itself strand by strand, and Serah stood alone a moment longer in the ash-fall, looking at the black skeleton of the Vellum Guild, and felt the warmth behind her sternum still there, banked, patient, humming like a hive in winter.

*I'm doing nothing, child. It's you.*

She turned to the wagon, to the old man in chains who had looked at her across thirty years of her own carefully constructed darkness and simply *known*, the way bees know, the way doors know, the way—she suspected—ledgers know, keeping true accounts beneath all the false ones.

"Ride beside me," she told him quietly, as the guards lifted him up. "And Keeper Halloway—if you have more questions to spend, you'd best spend them fast."

Bram smiled through split lips. "Oh, I have one or two," he said. "But first, Inquisitor, answer me this: when you burn a letter, where does the truth in it go?"

Ahead of them the north road ran white and empty through the frozen hills, toward Carrowmoor, toward the Chancellor's private offices, toward whatever waited at the end of a trail of missing pages—and somewhere behind them, unseen in the dark, a figure fled south with a stolen covenant and a keeper's blood, carrying the one truth the crown would burn a kingdom to possess.

Serah Vane climbed into the wagon beside her prisoner, and did not yet know that she had just begun to defect.
