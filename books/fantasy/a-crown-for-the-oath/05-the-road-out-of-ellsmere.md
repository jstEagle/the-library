## 5. The Road Out of Ellsmere

The page against Wren's chest had stopped feeling like paper. It felt like a coal, like a second heartbeat, and by the third mile out of Ellsmere they had stopped trying to convince themselves otherwise.

They ran the old drover's road north, where the hedgerows grew tall and tangled enough to swallow a person whole, and they did not look back at the glow on the horizon because looking back would mean seeing it, and seeing it would mean believing it. The guildhouse burned. Bram was dead—or worse than dead, which for a man like Bram meant dying slowly in a witch-hunter's cellar while men with kind voices asked him questions he would never answer. Wren had watched the lanterns crest the hill, had heard Bram say *go* in a voice that brooked no argument, had taken the iron key from his palm and the covenant-page from its hiding place inside the ledger's false back, and had gone.

*Go,* Bram had said, as if it were simple. As if ten years of suppers and ward-checks and grumbling arguments about the proper steeping time for feverfew could be folded up and carried away in a satchel the size of a loaf of bread.

Wren's hands shook so badly they had to stop twice to retie the satchel cord. The second time, they noticed blood on their knuckles and could not remember whose it was. That frightened them more than the fire did.

The key hung at their throat now, threaded on a bootlace, cold through the linen of their shirt. Iron, plain, unremarkable—the kind of key that opened nothing worth stealing, which was precisely why no one had ever stolen it. Wren had polished it every month for a decade without knowing why. Now they knew. The knowledge sat in them like swallowed riverstone.

*Your blood opens the Hollowgate.* Bram had said it with an apology in his voice, the same apology he'd used when he told Wren the wards were failing, the same apology he'd used the night he'd hired a grieving sixteen-year-old to sweep floors and file receipts and never once explained why the ledgers listed things like *one memory, gently used* or *a door that opens only on apologies*. *I'm sorry, child. I should have told you years ago.*

He hadn't finished telling them everything. The lanterns had seen to that.

So Wren ran, and the page burned against their sternum, and behind them Ellsmere glowed orange against the low clouds like a wound in the world.

* * *

By full dark they had put six miles between themselves and the town, and the road had degraded into a cart track, and the cart track into a suggestion, and the hedges had given way to gorse and blackthorn scrub rolling away toward hills that looked, in the starlight, like sleeping animals. Wren's legs had gone from aching to numb to a distant throbbing they observed the way one observes weather in another country. They knew they should keep moving. Hunters traveled fast and light and did not stop to sleep until their quarry did.

But the body is a traitor, and theirs chose that moment to remind them it had eaten nothing since noon.

There was a fallen elm off the track, its roots torn up in some long-ago storm, forming a wall of dark wood and hanging earth. Wren wedged themselves into the hollow beneath it, pulled the satchel into their lap, and ate half a heel of bread with the mechanical diligence of someone feeding a furnace rather than a self. The other half they wrapped and saved, because hunger was going to be a companion for a while yet and companions deserved fair treatment.

Then, because there was nothing else to do and no one to see, they took out the page.

Bram had wrapped it in oilcloth, and the oilcloth in waxed linen, and even so Wren unwrapped it with the care of a midwife. In the darkness it was only a pale rectangle. But Wren had read it three times already—once by lightning-flash during the first mile, once in the lee of a milestone, once by holding it close enough to their eyes that the words swam—and they knew what it said the way one knows a scar.

It was not a spell. That was the thing that kept catching in Wren's understanding like a nail in cloth. It was not a spell, not a rite, not a list of ingredients and incantations. It was a *treaty*. Terms, set down in two hands: one human, looping and hurried; the other flowing in lines that moved if you looked at them too long, letters that tasted of green things and deep water. The human hand had signed first. *In exchange for the gate held open in balance, the realm of men offers stewardship, honesty, and remembrance.* And beneath, in the moving script: *In exchange for passage freely given, the Otherworld offers its gift, portioned and bound, that neither realm may starve the other.*

And beneath both signatures, in a third hand—a keeper's hand, worn and careful—a line that made Wren's chest ache every time:

*Let this covenant stand while a keeper keeps it. Let the keeper be chosen by the gate itself, and let their blood be the hinge, and their word the ward, and their keeping faithful unto the last page.*

*Unto the last page.* Wren turned the parchment over. The back was blank. It had been blank for four hundred years, and something about that blankness had begun to feel less like emptiness and more like patience.

A sound broke the night—not loud, but wrong. A hoof-stone clinking against buried flint, somewhere back along the track. Then another. Then the soft, carrying jingle of harness that any drover would know and dread: bounty men rode heavy, with chains and cages, and chains make music.

Wren wrapped the page with fingers that had gone very steady, the way hands do when there is no more room for shaking. They slid it into their shirt, pressed the iron key flat against it, and listened.

Three riders, maybe four. Coming slow, sweeping the verges with lantern-light the color of sick honey. Not hunting blind—they were working the road in a line, the way you work a field. Someone had told them which way a fugitive would run. Someone had described a face.

Wren thought about running. Their legs reported, in no uncertain terms, that running was a fiction. They thought about hiding deeper under the elm roots, but the lantern-sweep would find them the way water finds a low place. They thought, briefly and with a strange calm, about the knife in their boot, and what Bram would have said about it, and what Bram would have done instead.

Bram would have talked. Bram could talk a bailiff out of his own boots. But Bram's voice was ash now, and Wren's voice had never learned the trick, and the lantern-light was crawling closer, painting the gorse in stripes of gold and black.

The lead rider reined in twenty feet away. He was a big man in a mail shirt too small for him, with a crossbow resting easy across his saddle-bow and the bored competence of a man who had done this many times and found it paid better than farming.

"Road's closed," he said, to the darkness in general. "Curfew edict, by order of the crown. Come out where I can see you."

Wren did not come out. The rider sighed, the sigh of a man delayed from his supper, and lifted two fingers. His companions fanned out—one left, one right, closing the pocket with the unhurried ease of sheepdogs who had done this to better runners than a guild clerk.

"Look," the big man said, conversational, "you can be a name on a list and a coin in my purse, or you can be a stain on the gorse. Both pay the same. One's easier to carry."

Wren's hand found the knife. Under the elm roots, in the dark, they made a decision that felt less like courage and more like arithmetic: they would take one of them, at least, before—

The lantern on the right went out.

Not guttered. Not dropped. *Extinguished*, all at once, and then the left one too, and in the sudden black there came a sound Wren had never heard outside of stories: steel leaving a scabbard with intent, a long singing note cut short, followed by a wet impact and a scream that ended in a gurgle.

"Off the road," said a woman's voice, low and level as a mason's line. "Both of you. Last chance."

What happened next, Wren would spend years failing to describe properly, because it happened too fast and mostly in the dark. There was a clash and a curse; a horse screamed and bolted; the big man roared something about *the Ferrant bitch* and fired his crossbow at a sound instead of a shape, and then there was a second impact, and a body fell heavily enough to shake the ground, and then silence, except for breathing—two sets of it, one ragged, one controlled.

Then a flint scraped, and a small flame bloomed, and Wren saw her.

She stood over the big man's body with a sword in one hand and a dead lantern in the other, and she was not what stories promised. Stories gave knights shining armor and faces like coins. This woman wore a quilted jack gone gray with road-dirt, a sword with a nicked edge and a grip worn smooth as driftwood, and a face that might have been handsome once and had since been rearranged by weather and worse. Her hair was cropped short and going iron at the temples. Across her left cheek ran an old scar, thin and white, like a signature she hadn't chosen.

On her breast, scratched out with deliberate violence—gouged, actually, deep enough to catch firelight—was the badge of her order. Beneath the gouge, faintly, you could still read the shape: a crown above crossed keys.

"You're the clerk," she said. It wasn't a question. She was looking at Wren's satchel, at the mud on their knees, at the way they held the knife like a pen they'd been handed by mistake. "From the Vellum house. The hunters in Ellsmere are offering thirty crowns for a 'guild functionary, age indeterminate, last seen fleeing north.' Age indeterminate." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile and entirely without warmth. "They couldn't even be bothered to learn your face."

Wren found their voice somewhere near their boots. "You killed them."

"I did." No apology in it. She wiped the blade on the dead man's cloak with the economy of long habit. "You're welcome."

"They might have just—" Wren stopped. Rethought. "No. They wouldn't have just arrested me."

"No," she agreed. "The edict says register or burn. It doesn't say anything about what happens between. Most of the men riding under it have decided the middle part is optional." She sheathed the sword and finally looked at Wren directly, and her eyes in the flint-light were a gray so pale they seemed lit from behind. "You're not registered. Which means either you're clean, or you're gifted, or you're something else. I don't much care which. What I care about is that those three were the rear picket of a sweep-line, and the main party is half a mile back, and they'll have heard nothing because I was quiet, but they will find these bodies by dawn. So you have a choice, clerk. You can sit under your tree and wait for them, or you can walk north with me and hate my company, which most people manage within the hour."

Wren should have run. Every instinct bred into a decade of secrecy said: trust no one, need no one, the Guild survives because it is alone. But the Guild was smoke now, and Bram was a question in a cellar, and the page against Wren's chest pulsed like a second heart, and their legs had already voted.

"Who are you?" they asked.

"Dame Isolde Ferrant." She said it flatly, the way you'd recite a sentence you'd been sentenced to. "Formerly of the Order of the Crown and Key. Currently of nowhere." She touched the gouged badge on her chest, one brief motion, like touching a bruise. "They struck my name eight months ago. Refusing a lawful order."

"What order?"

Something moved behind her eyes, quick and deep, like a fish under ice. "The Order hunts the gifted now. Children first—they're easiest to find, the gift shows early." She picked up her bedroll from where she'd dropped it and slung it across her shoulder. "I was ordered to bring in a girl of nine. I declined. Vigorously." A pause. "Two of my brothers-in-arms declined with me. One of them is dead. The other sells turnips in Marrowdale and won't speak to me."

"And the girl?"

Isolde looked at the darkness beyond the firelight, at the north, where the hills slept on. "Alive, last I heard. Which is more than I can say for my pension." She jerked her chin at the track. "Walk. Talk later. You can hate me properly once we're clear of the sweep-line."

Wren got up. Their legs protested; they ignored the protest. They gathered the satchel, checked the page and the key with a touch that was becoming ritual, and followed the disgraced knight north into the dark.

* * *

They walked until the stars wheeled past midnight and the track climbed into the hills, and Isolde set a pace that was punishment and mercy at once—fast enough to outrun pursuit, slow enough that Wren didn't die of it. She said little. When she spoke, it was practical: *drink here, eat now, step where I step, the ground lies on the left side of this path.* Once, passing a shrine stone crusted with lichen, she touched it with two fingers, quickly, the way you'd touch a hot pan, and Wren pretended not to notice.

Near dawn they sheltered in a shepherd's bothy, dry-stone walled, abandoned since lambing season. Isolde built no fire. She sat with her back to the wall, facing the door, sword across her knees, and watched Wren eat the rest of the bread with an expression that was hard to read in the gray light.

"You keep touching your chest," she said finally. "Left side. Every quarter hour, like a man checking a wound. What's there?"

"A page."

"What's on it?"

"The reason the king burned my home."

Isolde considered that. Outside, wind worried the gorse; somewhere far off, a fox screamed its thin terrible joy. "I spent eleven years enforcing the king's law," she said at last. "Eight months ago I learned what the law was for. Now I'm a bandit with good manners." Her pale eyes settled on Wren, weighing. "You're what I used to hunt. Or you're what I wish I'd been protecting all along. I genuinely can't tell, and it's making me itch."

"I'm a clerk," Wren said. "I kept ledgers. I swept floors. Ten years, and nobody ever—" The words caught. They tried again. "Last night a man died for me. He was old, and he complained about my tea, and he hid me inside his life for a decade, and I don't even know why he chose me. I don't know what I'm carrying. I only know everyone wants it, and everyone who knows about it dies, and I am so tired." Their voice cracked on the last word, and they hated it, and Isolde did not look away, which was somehow worse and better.

The knight was quiet a long moment. Then she reached into her pack, produced a strip of dried pork, and held it out.

"Eat," she said. "Grief is heavier than mail. You can't march under it hungry." And when Wren took it, she added, gruffly, staring at the door as though reporting to a superior officer: "For what it's worth, clerk—your Bram chose you for a reason. Old men who hide people for a decade don't do it out of charity. He saw something. When we get wherever we're going, you find out what, and you make it count. That's how you pay him. Not with tears. With *use*."

Wren chewed the pork, which tasted like salt and leather and, unexpectedly, like being told to stand up straight. "Where *are* we going?" they asked. "I don't even know where we're going."

"North and west. There's a flooded quarry town called Greyhollow—half drowned, half empty, full of people the crown stopped counting. If a man can hide us and move us, he's there." Isolde's jaw tightened around the next words, as if each one cost her. "Smuggler. Named Corvo Ashe."

Something cold walked down Wren's spine. "Corvo Ashe testified to the crown inquisitors. Eleven years ago. His testimony—" Wren's family. The word *family* had become a locked room in Wren's mind, and Corvo Ashe's name was the key that fit it, and they had sworn, quietly and often, that if they ever met the man they would—

"Yes," Isolde said, watching Wren's face go through whatever it went through. "That Corvo Ashe. He owes the Guild a debt—he told me so himself, drunk, two winters back, crying into his cups about a ledger he'd wronged. He wants to pay it. He hates wanting to." She shrugged. "Welcome to the company of the fallen, clerk. We're none of us clean. We're just pointed in the same direction."

Outside, the sky was turning the color of dishwater, and the wind had changed, and Isolde's head came up sharply, the way a deer's does.

"Horses," she said. "More than three. Coming up the valley road." She rose in one fluid motion, sword already clearing its sheath, and her voice dropped to that mason's-line calm. "Get behind me. And clerk—" a ghost of the almost-smile, "—whatever's in that satchel, I hope it's worth what it costs. Because I think it's about to start costing."

Through the bothy's narrow window, Wren saw them: riders cresting the far hill, six or seven, and at their head a banner snapping in the dawn wind—a white hand closed around a flame. The mark of the King's Inquisition.

And riding beside the banner, slight and straight-backed in gray, a figure Wren had seen only once before, across a burning courtyard: a woman with a lantern raised, whose face even at this distance seemed to be searching the hills for something she had lost.

Serah Vane had found the trail.
