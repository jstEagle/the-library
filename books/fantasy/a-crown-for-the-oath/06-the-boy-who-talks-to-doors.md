## 6. The Boy Who Talks to Doors

The rain had followed them for three days, and by the time the road dipped toward Marrowdeep, Isolde Ferrant had stopped believing it would ever end. It fell in gray sheets off the quarry cliffs, pooled in the ruts of the cart track, and turned the whole valley into a drowned mirror of itself—half-sunken houses staring up out of brown water, their rooftops like the backs of sleeping animals.

"Charming place," Wren said beside her, hunched under an oilcloth that did little good.

"They call it a town," Isolde said. "The water disagrees."

Marrowdeep had been a slate quarry once, before the seam ran out and the pumps were abandoned and the pit filled with cold green water deep enough to drown a church spire. What remained of the population clung to the slopes above the floodline in houses built of the same black stone as everything else, so that the town looked less like a settlement than like a wound in the hillside. Smoke rose from a few chimneys, thin and defeated.

They had come for two reasons. The first was practical: Corvo Ashe's name was on a smuggler's manifest pinned to a door in the last village, and the road through Marrowdeep was the only route south that the witch-hunters hadn't yet thought to watch. The second reason Wren hadn't spoken aloud, but Isolde had seen it in the way they kept touching the iron key at their belt, the way they'd gone quiet whenever talk turned to children on the road.

There was a rumor. There was always a rumor now. A boy in the flooded towns who could open any lock with a touch. A boy the king's men had taken.

Isolde had spent eleven years as a knight of the realm learning to weigh rumors against oaths. She had broken her first oath—the one about hunting children—in a barn outside Ellsmere when she'd put her sword between a witch-hunter and a girl of nine who could make candles gutter with her fear. She had broken her second when she'd cut Wren Galloway loose from bounty men instead of collecting the price on their head. She was running low on oaths. She intended to be careful with what remained.

And yet.

* * *

They found the cage on the second day, or rather they heard it—a sound like a struck bell coming from the old pump-house at the edge of the flood, a ringing that wasn't metal and wasn't wind. Wren stopped dead in the mud and went pale in a way Isolde had learned to read.

"That's ward-song," Wren said. "Old work. Guild work." Their voice had gone flat, careful. "Someone's put a suppression ward on something alive."

The pump-house squatted on a shelf of rock above the waterline, its great wheel long since seized with rust, its door hanging open. Inside, where the machinery had been stripped out, someone had built a cage.

It was not a jailer's cage. Isolde knew jails; she had built enough of them in her service to the crown, back when she still believed the law and justice shared a bed. This was different. This was a thing of black iron and silver wire, worked in patterns she recognized now—she'd been seeing them everywhere since Ellsmere—in the shape of the Guild's wards, inverted, perverted, every line of protection turned inward into a line of containment. It was small. It was the size of a thing meant to hold a child.

A boy sat inside it.

He couldn't have been more than eight or nine, thin as a winter branch, with hair the color of wet sand and eyes too large for his face. He wore a sackcloth shift and nothing else, and his bare feet were blue with cold. He wasn't crying. That was the worst of it. He sat with his back straight and his hands folded in his lap like a small gentleman at prayer, watching them come through the doorway with an expression of terrible, patient attention—as if he had learned that hope was a currency he could not afford to spend.

Around the cage, driven into the stone floor, stood six iron stakes hung with lanterns. The lanterns burned without oil. Witch-fire, the hunters called it. Isolde had watched men burn under it once and had told herself it was necessary.

"Hello," Wren said softly, kneeling at the cage's edge, keeping their hands visible the way you'd approach a hurt animal. "We're not with them. The ones who put you here."

The boy said nothing. But his eyes moved—to Wren's face, then down to Wren's hand where it rested near the iron key, and something flickered there. Recognition? Longing? Isolde couldn't tell.

"He can't speak," said a voice behind them.

Isolde spun, sword half-drawn, and found an old woman standing in the inner doorway—bent, white-haired, in a fishwife's shawl, holding a lamp. Her face was a map of quarried lines. She looked at Isolde's drawn blade without flinching, which told Isolde more than anything else could have: this woman had already made her peace with dying.

"Not that he was ever mute," the woman went on. "He stopped talking the day they took his mother. Talked plenty before. Now he just... listens. To things." She nodded at the cage. "To doors."

Wren's breath caught audibly.

"You're the one who feeds him," Isolde said.

"I'm the one who brings bread when the soldiers forget, which is most days." The woman's mouth twisted. "They're gone to Greywater for supplies. Left him caged because—" she gestured vaguely at the ward-lanterns, "—they're frightened of him. Fools. He's a child."

"What's your name?" Wren asked the boy gently.

Nothing. But the boy unfolded himself, crossed the small space of the cage, and pressed one palm flat against the bars—and the bars, Isolde saw it plainly, saw it and felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world—the bars *leaned toward him*. Like grass bending to wind. Like iron remembering it had once been soft.

The nearest lantern guttered.

"Gods above," Isolde breathed.

"He doesn't break locks," the old woman said quietly. "He asks them. And they open for him, because no one has ever asked them anything before. They took his mother for it. Then they came back for him, and the magistrate sold him to the hunters rather than burn him local—bad for trade, a burning is." She spat, with feeling. "They mean to take him to the capital after midwinter. I've heard the soldiers talk. The king wants gift-children *alive*."

Isolde looked at Wren. Wren was staring at the boy with an expression she couldn't parse—hunger and grief and guilt all fighting for the same ground. The key at their belt. The covenant-page hidden against their ribs. *Keeper's blood opens the gate.* Bram's last words, which Wren still wouldn't fully share.

"We can't," Isolde said—but she said it low, and she said it knowing even as the words left her mouth that they were already dead.

"The ward will flare," Wren said. "Guild-work inverted. If we break the circle wrong, it won't just hold him—it'll mark him. Burn the gift out or burn him up. We'd need to unmake it properly, and that takes time we don't have if those soldiers come back early."

"So we don't break it," Isolde heard herself say. "We *open* it."

Wren turned. "What?"

"It's a cage. Cages have locks." Isolde looked at the boy, at his palm still resting against the leaning bars. "And apparently locks adore him."

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took an hour to build and four minutes to nearly kill them all.

The problem was the lanterns. Six stakes in a ring, each flame fed not by oil but by the boy's own suppressed gift—the ward drank his power and burned it back at him, a closed circuit of cruelty. Touch the cage without dousing the flames and the fire would leap. Douse the flames without opening the cage and the soldiers would see the dark from the causeway road.

So Wren knelt in the mud and did something Isolde had never seen done: they spoke to the ward. Not words—or not words alone. A low, humming murmur, half-chant, half-apology, their fingers tracing the air along the invisible lines of the circle. The guild-tongue, Isolde realized. The language of the ledgers. Wren's voice cracked twice, and Isolde understood that they weren't just unwinding a spell—they were undoing something a brother of their order had built, maybe with their own hands, years ago, before any of them knew what the crown would do with it.

One by one, the lanterns dimmed. Not extinguished—*persuaded*, banked down to embers like coals settling overnight.

"Now," Wren whispered.

Isolde stepped forward and set her hands on the cage door—not forcing it, just holding it, feeling the mechanism beneath the iron skin. A hunter's cage-lock, triple-set, the kind designed to laugh at picks. She'd trained on these. She also knew, with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life around both locks and lies, that every mechanism had a moment of honesty.

She didn't get the chance to find it. Small fingers slipped past hers through the bars—impossibly, the bars parting like reeds to let them through—and touched the lock.

The lock opened.

Not broke. Not picked. *Opened*—with a click so gentle and complete that it sounded almost grateful, the tumblers rising like birds released, and the cage door swung wide on hinges that suddenly remembered how to move, and the boy stepped out into the lamplight of the old pump-house and stood there blinking, free, as if he didn't quite believe the floor would hold him.

Then the nearest lantern flared.

Wren lunged, smothering the flame with their cloak and their bare hands, and the smell of scorched wool and scorched skin filled the room, and Wren made no sound at all except a long breath out through their nose, the way you breathe through pain you refuse to dignify.

"Wren—"

"I'm fine. Go. All of you, *go*—"

The old woman pushed past them all, took the boy's hand, and walked him out the door into the rain without a word, moving with the brisk competence of someone who had smuggled contraband bread for months and found children only marginally harder. Isolde hauled Wren up by the arm. Outside, the rain had thickened into true dark, and the flood below lay black and silent, and somewhere across the water, on the causeway, a lantern swung. Voices. The supply wagons, coming home early.

"Back way," the old woman said. "Through the sluice tunnel. It floods at high water, so either run or swim."

They ran.

* * *

The sluice tunnel was exactly as pleasant as it sounded—a throat of black stone breathing cold water, ankle-deep then knee-deep then thigh-deep, the boy carried on Isolde's shoulders with his arms locked around her neck and his cheek pressed against her soaked hair. He weighed nothing. That fact lodged in her chest like a splinter: *he weighs nothing, they have been starving him in a cage, in my king's name, under my king's seal.*

She had sworn oaths to that seal. She had knelt in the cathedral at Carreth and sworn them with a naked sword under her palms, and she had believed—gods help her, she had *believed*—that the law was a wall between the weak and the fire, and that she was a stone in that wall.

Now she was wading through a drain with a stolen witch-child on her back, and it was the first honest work she'd done in eleven years.

Behind her, Wren stumbled and swore softly, cradling their burned hands. Ahead, the old woman—Maren, she finally admitted her name was, Maren Ostwick, widow, fishwife, and apparently accomplished criminal—led them by feel through the dark, muttering directions to herself like prayers.

The tunnel ended in a boathouse, and the boathouse ended in a marsh of reeds and drowned fences, and by full dark they had put a mile of water between themselves and Marrowdeep, holed up in a duck-hunter's blind with a brazier of scavenged coal and the boy wrapped in every dry scrap they owned.

He still hadn't spoken. But he had eaten—two bowls of barley porridge with the focused gravity of a small creature making up for lost time—and afterward he had sat between Isolde and Wren, close enough to touch, and fallen asleep sitting up, mid-blink, like a puppet with its strings cut.

Isolde caught him before he toppled and laid him down against her cloak. His hand, even in sleep, curled loosely around one of her fingers.

"Well," Maren said, from the far side of the brazier. "That's that, then. You'll take him south?"

"We'll take him wherever he's safe," Isolde said.

"There's nowhere safe. You know that better than I do, Dame Ferrant." Isolde stiffened. The old woman smiled thinly. "A knight without colors walks into a drowned town asking after smugglers' roads. You think folk don't talk? Half of Marrowdeep knows who you are. The other half is deciding whether the bounty's worth the walk to Greywater."

"Then we leave tonight."

"You'll never cross the fens in the dark. And the boy—" Maren glanced at the sleeping child, and her voice gentled despite itself. "The boy needs sleep, food, and a week of not being hunted, though he won't get the third. Take my boat at first light. Follow the drowned church spire south until you hit the old towpath. There's a man on the towpath who owes debts to people like your friend here." She nodded at Wren. "Corvo Ashe. Smuggler. Rat. But a rat who knows every hole between here and the border, and owes the Vellum Guild more than he'd like anyone to remember."

Wren's face did something complicated. "You know about the Guild?"

"Child, I'm eighty-one years old. I knew the Guild when it had a *building*." Maren rose, joints cracking like green wood, and gathered her shawl. "I'll walk back before the soldiers miss me. An old woman's grief makes poor bounty news." At the door of the blind she paused, silhouetted against the rain, and looked back at the boy. "His name's Pip. His mother called him Pip. See someone calls him that again someday."

Then she was gone into the dark, and Isolde sat with a sleeping child against her shoulder and a stranger's trust in her hands, and found she was crying, quietly, in a way she hadn't cried since the day they'd stripped her rank.

* * *

Dawn came gray and slow over the fens, and with it, movement on the towpath.

Isolde saw them first from the boathouse door: three riders picking their way along the raised causeway through the mist, and her hand went to her sword before her mind caught up—then stopped. These weren't hunters. No livery, no witch-fire lanterns. Just tired men on tired horses, leading a fourth horse loaded with crates.

Except the man walking beside the lead rider wasn't riding at all, and moved like a man who trusted no horse ever foaled, and even at two hundred paces Isolde recognized the rolling, bowlegged swagger of someone who had spent his life on ship decks and in trouble.

Beside her, Wren went very still.

"That's him," Wren said. "That's Corvo Ashe."

Pip appeared at the door on Isolde's other side—he'd woken silent as a cat, as he seemed to do everything—and looked out at the riders. Then he looked up at Isolde, and then at Wren, and for the first time since the cage, since his mother, since whatever silence had swallowed his voice whole—

He smiled.

And every lock within earshot—the boathouse door, the padlock on the coal chest, the rusted hasp of the duck-blind shutter—clicked softly open, all at once, like a round of applause.

Down on the towpath, Corvo Ashe's head snapped toward the sound, and Isolde watched the smuggler's hand drift toward the knife at his belt as he stared directly at the boathouse where three fugitives stood framed in the doorway, one of them a child the entire kingdom was hunting.

"Well," Isolde said, drawing her sword anyway, out of habit rather than hope. "Let's go introduce ourselves."
