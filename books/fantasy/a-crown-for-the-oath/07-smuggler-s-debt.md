## 7. Smuggler's Debt

The road to Saltmere smelled of brine and old rot, the way all roads to Corvo Ashe did.

Wren had not made this journey in eleven years, and they had sworn—sworn on Bram's ledger, on their mother's grave, on every quiet god of thresholds and doors—that they never would again. Oaths, it turned out, were like wards: strong only until something worse came along. Something worse had a name now. It was called the Gift-Hunt, and it was written in royal ink on every post from Ellsmere to the coast, and it burned people in the market squares with the same tidy efficiency that it burned letters of denunciation.

So here Wren walked, at dusk, down a road that stank of fish heads and tar, with a knight who limped, a child who hummed to locked things, and a page of vellum that could unmake the border between worlds folded against their heart.

"You're sulking," Isolde said behind them.

"I'm remembering."

"Same posture."

Pip laughed—a bright, startling sound, like a coin dropped in a chapel—and Wren found, to their irritation, that they didn't want him to stop laughing. Not tonight. Tonight the laughter would be in short supply.

Saltmere rose out of the marsh fog ahead: a town built on stilts and grudges, its streets half-drowned at high tide, its loyalties entirely drowned. No crown writ ran far here. The king's criers came to the edge of the boardwalks, shouted their proclamations at the water, and went home unpaid. Which meant Saltmere belonged to whoever owned the boats, and everyone knew who owned the boats.

Everyone had always known.

* * *

Corvo Ashe's counting house sat at the end of the longest pier, a squat black building with lanterns the color of drowned suns. Two guards flanked the door—big men, marsh-born, with the flat eyes of people paid to notice nothing. They looked at Wren, then at Isolde's sword, then at Pip, and one of them spat thoughtfully into the water before knocking.

The door opened from inside without being touched. Of course it did. Pip made a small delighted noise, and Wren felt the boy's gift brush past them like a cat investigating a stranger—the locks of Saltmere apparently knew a friend when they met one.

Inside: ledgers stacked to the rafters, none of them honest. A hearth burning green-tinged driftwood. And Corvo Ashe himself, seated behind a desk scarred by knife-marks and candle-wax, pouring three cups of something amber with the unhurried grace of a man who had known for two days that his visitors were coming.

"Wren Galloway," he said. His voice hadn't changed. Low, warm, ruined at the edges, like a hull scraped on sand. "Eleven years. You look terrible."

"You look rich."

"I look comfortable. There's a difference, and it cost me everything you think I have." He set down the bottle. "Sit. All of you. The girl can have my chair; I stand when I'm nervous, and I intend to be nervous shortly."

Isolde didn't sit. She stood with her back to the door, which Wren suspected was habit rather than tactics, and looked at Corvo the way one looks at weather coming in off the sea. "You know who we are?"

"I know who she is." Corvo nodded at Wren. "Keeper of Hollowgate. Last of the Vellum Guild's inner circle, unless Bram finally died, in which case—" He stopped. Read Wren's face. Something moved behind his eyes, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "Ah. So he did. I'm sorry. I mean that in the way I mean most things, which is badly and too late."

"Bram is dead," Wren said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them. "Burned. Along with the guildhouse, forty years of records, and eleven people whose only crime was keeping accounts of impossible things. The Guild's ledger of names is in the hands of Inquisitor Serah Vane. What isn't in her hands is this." They did not show him the covenant page. They let him understand that it existed, close enough to touch. "And what I need from you is a boat."

"A boat."

"A boat, a crew that doesn't ask questions, and a route north along the coast that avoids every checkpoint between here and Greywater. The Guild extended you credit once, Corvo. A great deal of credit. Bram called it an investment."

Corvo laughed. It was not a kind sound. "Bram called it a leash. Your precious Guild bought my debt the winter my ship went down—paid my creditors, fed my crew, kept me out of a debtor's hole—and then let me marinate in gratitude for a decade. Do you know what it's like, Wren? Owning nothing, not even your own ruin?" He leaned forward. The firelight caught the gray threading his beard, the old scar bisecting his left eyebrow. "I have waited eleven years for someone to walk through that door and ask me to pay. I've rehearsed this conversation. In some versions I throw you in the harbor."

"What happens in the other versions?"

"In the others I pour the good brandy." He pushed a cup across the desk. "Drink. We sail on the midnight tide."

* * *

They settled terms in the back room among the smell of canvas and pitch, and it was there, with Isolde checking the windows and Pip asleep in a coil of rope like a kitten in a nest, that the truce curdled into what it actually was.

Because Corvo said, casually, folding a chart of the northern coast, "How is your sister?"

Wren's hand stopped moving.

The room got very quiet. Outside, the tide knocked against the pilings with the patience of something that had all night.

"Don't," Wren said.

"It's a reasonable question. Family news travels. I'd hate to make conversation about the weather for a whole voyage."

"Your testimony put her in front of a magistrate." Wren heard their own voice go thin and cold, the way it did in dreams, the way it had gone in the courtroom eleven years ago while a smuggler with beautiful sad eyes explained to the crown's assessors exactly where the Galloway household kept its unregistered gifts, exactly which neighbor girl could coax fire from wet wood, exactly how often she did it, and how young she'd been when she started. "She was fourteen. She could light candles when she was scared. That was the whole of it. That was the entire crime, and you read it out like poetry because they promised you immunity, and they took her to the corrective house in Dunmark, and she wrote me letters for two years and then she stopped writing letters, and I don't know if that means she's dead or married or broken, because the corrective houses don't return correspondence, they return *receipts*."

The words came out faster as they went, gathering weight, and by the end Wren was standing, though they had no memory of rising, and Corvo had not moved at all. He sat very still with the chart in his hands, and he took the whole of it—every word, every year of it—without blinking.

"Yes," he said quietly.

"*Yes?*"

"Yes. That's what happened. I've had eleven years to dress it up, and I'm not going to insult you by doing it in front of you." He laid the chart flat. Smoothed it with both palms, twice, a man straightening something that couldn't be straightened. "They had my first mate's son in a cell. Eight years old. Gifted—they said. Whether he was or wasn't, I never learned. They told me one name for another. I gave them yours." He looked up. "I've regretted it in the specific ever since. Not generally. Specifically. Her name. Your face in that courtroom. The way your mother didn't cry until the verdict, and then cried like a door coming off its hinges."

Wren's breath caught. They hadn't known he'd seen that. They hadn't known anyone had seen that but Wren.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," Corvo said. "I'm telling you the price of the boat includes my company, and my company comes with this stain on it, and you should decide now whether you can sleep below decks with a man who did what I did. Because if you can't, I'll still give you the boat. I'll give you my best crew. But I won't lie to you across a galley table for six days and call it payment."

Isolde spoke from the window, without turning. "In the army we had a saying. The man who betrays you once knows the price of betrayal better than any man alive. Sometimes that makes him the best guard you have. Sometimes it makes him the first to run. You pays your money."

"That's a horrible saying," Wren said.

"It's a soldier's saying. Most of them are horrible."

Wren looked at Corvo Ashe for a long time. At the gray in his beard. At the knife-scarred desk and the dishonest ledgers and the green-burning driftwood, and underneath all of it, at the thing he had just done that no one in eleven years had done: he had named it. Plainly. Without ornament. Her name had been Lira. Nobody had said Lira aloud to Wren since the funeral-that-wasn't, since the empty coffin the family had buried because the corrective house returned only receipts.

"Lira liked the sea," Wren heard themselves say. It came out strange, rusty, like a lock opening after years. "Before. She used to sneak down to the estuary. I covered for her maybe fifty times. If I'd known I was practicing—" They stopped. Swallowed. "If I'd known what I was practicing for."

Corvo closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, they were wet, and he did not pretend otherwise, and somehow that—not the apology, not the brandy, not the boat—was the thing that tipped the scale.

"Midnight tide," Wren said. "We sail. But you'll tell me about her. Everything you saw. Everything they said. On the voyage, all of it, even the parts that shame you. Especially those."

"That will take six days."

"Then we'd better hope for bad weather."

Something broke in Corvo's face—not grief exactly, but the ice over grief, cracking. He nodded once, sharply, the way men nod when they've been given a sentence they deserve and expected worse.

* * *

They loaded in the dark. The ship was called the *Kindly Tide*, which Corvo admitted was a joke he'd grown tired of explaining. She was a coastal runner, shallow-keeled, fast, ugly, and crewed by nine people who materialized out of Saltmere's fog without a single question—which, Wren realized, watching them work, was itself an answer. These were people Corvo had also pulled out of fires, in his way. A debt ran through the whole crew like a keel.

Pip woke just long enough to press his palm flat against the mainmast and whisper something to it. The mast creaked. Pip grinned and went back to sleep.

"He does that to everything," Isolde murmured, standing beside Wren at the rail as Saltmere's lights sank astern.

"Doors. Locks. Masts."

"The world, probably. Give him time." She was quiet a moment. "You did well in there. With him."

"I hated every minute of it."

"Yes. That's usually how it's done." She glanced sideways at them, and in the dark her face was younger than it ever looked by daylight. "My order taught that forgiveness is a discipline, like drill. You do it before you feel it, and the feeling follows. Mostly they were wrong about everything else. They may have been right about that."

Wren watched the water slide black and silver beneath the bow. Somewhere behind them lay a burned guildhouse and a dead old man who had kept secrets the way other keepers kept gates. Somewhere ahead lay Greywater, and checkpoints, and an inquisitor whose face Wren had seen across a burning courtyard and would see again—he was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of weather. And somewhere south, in a corrective house that returned only receipts, a girl who lit candles when she was scared might still be alive, and Wren had just signed on to a voyage with the man responsible, because the man responsible was the only door open.

The family you chose, Bram used to say, is chosen harder than the one you're given. Wren was beginning to understand that he hadn't meant it as comfort. He'd meant it as arithmetic.

Below decks, faintly, Corvo's voice had begun—low, halting, telling a story to no audience yet, rehearsing, the way he'd said. Rehearsing eleven years of shame so he could hand it over honestly at the first bad weather.

Wren turned toward the bow, toward the north, toward Greywater, and did not go below.

Near dawn, the lookout sang out: smoke on the horizon, far behind and gaining, and a sail flying colors none of the crew would name aloud. Corvo came up the companionway two steps at a time, chart already in hand, and when he reached the rail his jaw was set like a man who has just remembered the exact size of a debt.

"That," he said, "is going to complicate the payment schedule."
