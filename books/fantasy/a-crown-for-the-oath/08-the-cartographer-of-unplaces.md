## 8. The Cartographer of Unplaces

Pip had never owned anything before, and now he owned a map that didn't exist.

He'd made it himself, which was the part that mattered. It lived in the back of his head, folded up small the way doors folded themselves when they weren't being doors, and every night in Corvo Ashe's hold he took it out and spread it across the floor of the cabin he shared with Dame Isolde, drawing it again with a stub of charcoal on the backs of cargo manifests Corvo said were already paid for and therefore nobody's business.

The map was of roads. Not real roads—Pip knew real roads, muddy ones, cobbled ones, the kind you could walk with your eyes shut—but the other kind. The ones that only went somewhere if you believed hard enough while walking them, and even then only sometimes, and never the same way twice.

He called them unplaces, in his head. He hadn't told anyone that word yet. He was saving it.

* * *

The hold of the *Kindly Gull* smelled of salt pork, tar, and old lies, and Pip loved it completely.

It was not a large ship. Corvo had explained this twice, defensively, as though someone had accused him of it: a coastal trader, half-legitimate, quick enough to outrun customs cutters and ugly enough that no one wanted to board her anyway. She had one mast, a hold full of smuggled brandy and honest wool stacked together like old friends who'd agreed to disagree, and below all of it, behind a door that Pip could hear humming the way his own cage-door used to hum, a hidden compartment where four fugitives were spending their days pretending to be cargo.

Pip spent his days doing something better than pretending. He spent them listening to the ship.

Ships talked. Nobody else seemed to know this, or maybe they did and just didn't say, but the *Gull* muttered all day long in her sleep—the timbers ticking as she warmed, the rigging complaining about the wind, the bilge whispering its wet secrets. And her doors! A ship was mostly doors, when you thought about it: hatches and companionways and cupboard-lids and the little round porthole-covers that were just doors lying down. Pip lay in his hammock at night and listened to all of them breathing, and for the first time since Bram's guildhouse burned, since the cage, since the man with the iron tongs who had asked him questions in a voice like a file drawn over rust—for the first time in longer than he could count, Pip felt almost safe.

Almost. Because safe wasn't a place. Safe was a road, and roads moved.

That was what he was mapping.

* * *

On the fourth morning aboard, Wren found him in the hold, cross-legged on a bale of wool, surrounded by charcoal drawings.

There were eleven of them by then. Pip had been working through the nights, because the roads came clearest in the dark, and he'd pinned the sheets along the bulkhead with tar so they wouldn't blow around when the *Gull* rolled. Each sheet showed a stretch of country between them and the mountains where the Hollowgate sat in its ruined crown of stone—but the country was wrong, or rather, righter than right. Roads forked where no roads were. Rivers bent uphill. In three places Pip had drawn little circles with a dot inside, because those were places where the world got thin, and thin places deserved marking the way wells deserved covers.

Wren stood very still among the drawings for a long time.

"You drew Greywater," Wren said at last. Their voice did something complicated on the second word. "The toll bridge."

"I didn't draw it," Pip said. "I listened to it. Doors talk, and roads are just doors lying down." He pointed at the circle-dot near the bridge. "That one's a thin place. You can feel it from the water. Like standing too close to an oven."

Wren crouched slowly, the way people crouch when their knees have opinions, and studied the sheet. Their finger hovered over the dot without touching it.

"How do you know these things?" Wren asked, and there was no suspicion in it, only wonder wearing a tired coat.

"Same way you know things," Pip said. "You're a gatekeeper. I'm a... a listener." He hesitated, then gave away the word he'd been saving, because Wren's face looked like a face that needed something given to it. "A cartographer of unplaces."

Something happened to Wren's expression then—a crack, a softening, the look of someone setting down a weight they'd forgotten they were carrying. They laughed, actually laughed, short and startled out of them, and ruffled Pip's hair with a hand that smelled of ink and woodsmoke.

"A cartographer of unplaces," Wren repeated. "Bram would have loved you. He collected impossible titles. He once tried to convince the Guild that 'Senior Auditor of Miracles' should come with a stipend."

"Did it?"

"He gave himself the stipend. That was the whole trick of Bram." Wren's laugh faded into something quieter. They looked at the maps again, all eleven sheets, the wrong-right country with its uphill rivers and its covered wells. "Pip. These forks. Are you certain? This road here—" their finger traced a line west of the quarry town, "—doesn't exist. I've walked that valley. There's nothing there but bracken and a dead shrine."

"There's a road," Pip said firmly. "It's just shy. Roads get shy when lots of people stop believing in them. But it remembers being walked. If we go there and walk it like we mean it—" he shrugged, the way Isolde did when she was explaining why she'd broken another oath, "—it'll remember harder."

Wren stared at him. Then at the maps. Then at him again, and Pip watched a whole argument happen behind their eyes and lose.

"We might actually make it," Wren said softly. Not to Pip. To the maps, maybe. Or to themselves.

"That's the plan," Pip said. "Plans are just maps that haven't happened yet."

* * *

Isolde came down that evening and stood in front of the sheets with her arms crossed and her jaw set in the shape she wore when she was trying not to feel things.

She was terrible at it. Pip had known her four days and he could read her like a broadsheet: the jaw meant moved, the crossed arms meant frightened of being moved, and the long silence meant she was losing.

"Dame Ferrant," Wren said, from the corner where they were mending a strap, "before you say anything, know that I defended these already and lost."

"These are..." Isolde stopped. Started again. "In the king's army we had surveyors. Men with instruments and chains and ten years of training. None of them ever drew a river going uphill."

"It's not going uphill," Pip said patiently. "It's going *sideways*. Uphill is just sideways you can see."

Isolde looked at him. Then at Wren. Then she sat down heavily on the wool bale next to him, which was, Pip understood, a surrender.

"My grandmother had the gift," she said abruptly, to the middle distance. "Small one. She could keep bread from staling. Just bread. We told no one, obviously, but she'd hand me a loaf on winter mornings and wink, and I thought—" Her jaw worked. "I reported two children to the provosts my first year of commissioning. Followed orders. They were registered, which meant branded, which meant..." She didn't finish. Her thumb rubbed the hilt of the sword across her knees, back and forth, like soothing an animal. "Your maps are the first thing in ten years that's felt like hers. Like something small and true and warm."

Pip considered this seriously. Then he unpinned the sheet showing the road west of the quarry town—the shy one—and pressed it into her hands.

"You can have that one," he said. "For your grandmother. When we walk it, you have to believe hardest, though. Knights are heavy with not-believing. It drags the road down."

To his enormous satisfaction, Dame Isolde Ferrant, stripped of rank, breaker of oaths, terror of bounty men, laughed until she had to wipe her eyes.

* * *

Corvo held out longest, which surprised no one, least of all Pip.

The smuggler appeared in the hold on the sixth night, ostensibly to complain about charcoal dust in his manifests, actually, Pip suspected, because he'd been listening at the companionway for two days and his pride had finally lost to his curiosity. He picked up a sheet, turned it upside down, turned it back, and grunted.

"This is the coast road past Saltmere. There's no fork there. I've run that road since before you were whelped."

"It's new," Pip said. "Or old. Both. Roads near the Otherworld's border don't keep still. They're like—like a dog that belongs to two houses."

Corvo's grunt this time had a different texture. He set the sheet down carefully, the way he'd probably never set anything down carefully in his life, and Pip saw him glance—at Wren, quickly, and away, and there was a whole history in that glance, a debt and a wound sharing a bed.

"The Guild kept records of such things," Corvo said, to the manifest in his hands. "Roads that wandered. I carried some, once. Before." Before, Pip had learned, was a word that did a lot of work around Corvo Ashe. "Never believed them. Thought it was guild-superstition. Smoke to justify fees."

"And now?" Wren asked quietly.

Corvo looked at the eleven sheets for a long moment. At the boy who'd drawn them. At the knight holding her sheet like a relic. Something in his face shifted, tectonic and slow, a coastline deciding to be a harbor.

"Now I'm thinking," he said, "that maybe the debt I owe your Guild is bigger than I priced it." He tapped the coast-road sheet with one blunt finger. "This fork. If it's real, it saves us two days off the march to the mountains, and puts us around the checkpoint at Greywater entirely."

Pip shook his head. "Greywater you can't go around. Not on any road I've heard. The bridge is a hinge. Hinges have to be gone through." He didn't know how he knew that. He just did, the way he knew doors. "Sorry."

Corvo exhaled through his nose. "Of course it does. Nothing in my life is ever easy." But he rolled up the coast-road sheet with something close to reverence and said, "Keep drawing, whelp. And keep the charcoal off my manifests."

Which, from Corvo, was practically a blessing.

* * *

The nights settled into a rhythm after that, and Pip, who had never had a rhythm belonging to him before, hoarded it greedily.

Evenings, they gathered in the hold among the maps: Wren mending and listening, adding small corrections in ink—*this ford drowns in spring; this shrine is not dead, only sleeping*—in a precise clerk's hand that made Pip's charcoal scrawls look suddenly like part of something official. Isolde drilled him in knife-work with a wooden practice blade, gently, stopping each lesson the moment his face went anywhere near the cage-memory, and taught him dice instead, at which he cheated magnificently using a gift she pretended not to notice (doors, it turned out, included dice-cups). Corvo grumbled and fed them and told stories of the coast that were forty percent true, and Pip learned to sort the true forty percent by watching his hands.

One night Isolde brought up a blanket that smelled of cedar. Another night Wren taught him letters, proper ones, and Pip discovered that writing a word made it more real, the way naming a door made it open easier. He wrote *unplaces* on the corner of his best sheet, and Wren read it aloud, and nodded like it was a title worth putting in a ledger.

And one night—Pip would keep this night folded up in his head forever, next to the map—Wren sat back from the ink corrections and looked around the hold: at the knight, at the smuggler, at the boy, at the eleven impossible sheets strung along the bulkhead like prayer flags, and said, quietly, almost afraid of it:

"Bram used to say the Guild wasn't a brotherhood. It was just strangers who decided to be a family and then lied about it in triplicate." Wren's mouth did something between a smile and a wound. "I think we're lying in triplicate now."

Nobody answered. Nobody had to. Isolde dealt the dice. Corvo poured four cups of the brandy that officially did not exist and pushed the smallest one toward Pip with a stern look that meant *watered*, and it was watered, and it was the finest thing Pip had ever tasted.

Above them the *Gull* creaked northward through the dark water, and below the water the old roads shifted and remembered, and on the bulkhead the maps waited, patient as doors.

* * *

Pip woke in the deep of the twelfth night because every door on the ship screamed at once.

Not loudly—that was the horrible part. A scream like a held breath, like a hundred hinges all flexing toward something. He was out of his hammock and down the companionway before his feet fully woke, and he found the others already in the hold, already armed, already looking at him, because they'd learned that when Pip's face went white, the smart money was on listening to him.

"They know," Pip whispered. His voice came out small. "The roads. All of them. Every road to the mountains just... shut. Like someone slammed a door on our side of it." He pressed his palm flat against the hull, against the timber, listening with everything he had. "And there's a road coming *toward* us. Fast. With lanterns on it."

Wren was already rolling up the maps, hands steady, eyes not. Isolde was buckling her sword. Corvo was on the ladder, shouting softly up to his crew to make sail.

"Pip." Wren crouched to his height, and their eyes were calm in the way that cost them something. "How long?"

Pip listened. The doors told him. The doors always told him, even when what they said was terrible.

"Till dawn," he said. "Maybe less."
