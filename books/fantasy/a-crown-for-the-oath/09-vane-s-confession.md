## 9. Vane's Confession

The cells beneath the Bastion of the Flame were not built for holding people. Serah Vane had understood that within her first week of service, though it had taken her three years to say so aloud, and even then only to herself, in the dark, in the hour when honesty was a kind of fever. The cells had been carved from the foundations of something older—she'd seen the seams in the stone, the ghost-edges of archways bricked over, the way the corridors bent at wrong angles as if following the bones of a previous building's logic. Whatever had stood here before the witch-hunters came had been torn down and its bones used to cage what replaced it.

Now one of those bones held a scribe.

They had taken him at the ferry crossing at Marlow's Bend, two days after the burning of the guildhouse in Ellsmere. He had been carrying nothing but a satchel of blank vellum and a knife he clearly did not know how to use, and he had surrendered so quickly, so gratefully, that Serah had known before he said a word that he wanted to be caught. Fugitives ran. This man had stood in the shallows with his arms raised like a man greeting rain.

His name, he had told them, was Aldous Fenwick. Journeyman scribe, third circle, Vellum Guild. He had a stutter that surfaced when he was frightened, which was always, and ink stains on his fingers gone down into the nail beds, the permanent kind that marked a man who had spent his life bent over a desk rather than standing over anyone.

Serah sat across the cell from him now with a lantern between them and a slate on her knees, because she had learned early that paper made witnesses nervous and nervous men lied. She had sent her lieutenant away. That, too, was becoming a habit she did not examine too closely.

"You've told me about the guildhouse," she said. "The layout. The vaults. The hierarchy. You've been very helpful."

Aldous flinched at *helpful*, the way a dog flinches at a raised hand offered gently. "I—I don't want to be hanged."

"No one hangs you for talking. They hang you for lying." She turned the slate over. "So let's test the second thing you told me. You said the Guild keeps ledgers of names. Bearers of the gift. Registered in secret, hidden from the crown."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He blinked. It was a small question and it should have had a small answer, but he hesitated, and Serah watched the hesitation the way she had been trained to watch everything—as fuel, as evidence, as a door.

"Because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because names have weight. In the old craft. If you write a person's true name in a ledger kept under certain conditions, you can—you can find them. Follow them. Some say you can do more than follow." His voice dropped. "That's why the Guild kept theirs hidden. Not to protect the gift-bearers from the crown. To protect them from anyone who might read the ledger."

"The crown has the ledger now," Serah said. "We took it out of your burning house. Every name."

"I know." And there it was—the thing that had been sitting in the corner of his fear since they'd fished him out of the shallows. He looked at his ink-stained hands. "I know what happens to written things when the wrong hands hold them."

* * *

She should have ended the interview there. She had what the Inquisition required: confirmation of the ledger's function, corroboration of the theft by the keeper Wren Galloway, enough to file a report and move up the road after her quarry. Serah Vane was good at her work precisely because she knew where the edges of an investigation lay and did not wander past them into the fog.

But the fog had been following her since Ellsmere.

She had watched Bram the Keeper die. Old man, iron-spined, refusing until the smoke took him to give up the location of the covenant page—and in the moment of his dying, kneeling in the ash with his hand pressed flat against the scorched floor as if listening to the building's heart, Serah had felt something move in her own chest. A warmth behind the sternum. A sense of the room's geometry suddenly legible, the wards in the walls singing their death-note, the fire itself bending slightly, almost politely, around the old man's final breath.

She had reported none of it. She had stood in the cold afterward until the warmth faded, and told herself it was grief, or smoke inhalation, or the ordinary horror of watching a man burn, which any decent person would feel.

Any decent person, she thought now, looking at Aldous Fenwick, would also wonder why the crown needed a ledger of names at all. The Gift-Hunt proclamation had been clear: register or burn. Registration was punishment. But the Inquisition's orders, passed down sealed from the Lord-Inquisitor's office, had contained a second clause, one that had nagged at her for weeks the way a loose tooth nags at the tongue: *all registered bearers to be conveyed to the Bastion, not the pyre.* Conveyed. Alive. The pyres were for the unregistered, the runaways, the ones like Wren Galloway who slipped the net.

Why burn the ones you couldn't catch and keep the ones you could?

"Master Fenwick," she said. "In your time with the Guild, did you ever handle correspondence with the crown? Before the Hunt. Under the old king, or the regency."

His head came up sharply. For the first time since the ferry, real fear entered his face—not the generic terror of a prisoner, but something specific. "No. The Guild doesn't correspond with the crown. The Guild exists so that no one has to."

"And yet someone sold you to us. Someone gave us the ferry crossing, the timing, the satchel. We don't find Guild men by accident, Aldous. The Guild has survived two hundred years by being impossible to find. So either your tradecraft failed all at once—which I don't believe—or someone inside the crown already knew exactly where you'd be." She leaned forward. "Which means the crown has had eyes inside the Vellum Guild for some time. Which means this hunt is not new. Which means the proclamation was not the beginning of anything. It was the harvest."

The word left her mouth before she understood it, and once it was spoken she could not unspeak it, because Aldous Fenwick went white as his own vellum.

"You've seen it," he whispered. "You've seen the conveyance orders."

"I've seen prisoners taken alive while others burn. I've seen the Bastion's lower levels closed to my own rank since midsummer. I've seen carts go down at night and not come back up." She set the slate aside. "Tell me what a third-circle scribe knows that he shouldn't."

* * *

What he knew, it emerged slowly, in fragments, between stutters and long silences in which he seemed to argue with himself, was everything and nothing—a journeyman's view of a machine whose whole shape only the masters saw. But journeysman's views, assembled carefully, can draw a picture too.

The Guild, he told her, had never been only record-keepers. The ledgers were tools. Names, properly inscribed, could anchor a bearer—could let them be found, yes, but also could let their gift be *drawn through* them at distance, like water through a pipe. It was old craft, border-craft, the kind of thing the first keepers had developed to maintain the Hollowgate without needing a keeper's presence at all times. A ledger was a way of many hands sharing one burden.

"A covenant," Serah said. "Shared burden."

"That's the theology of it. The masters called the great ledger 'the covenant' among themselves, though the actual covenant—the original one, the treaty between the realms—is something else. A page. A single page." He swallowed. "Bram kept it. Hidden inside the master ledger, because who would search for the most important thing the Guild owns inside the most dangerous thing?"

"And the crown seized the master ledger at Ellsmere."

"They burned the house to flush it out. They didn't want the building, Inquisitor, they wanted the book." His voice steadied as he spoke; knowledge, she observed, was a kind of courage, even terrible knowledge. "And they got it. Except the page was gone. Galloway took it. Everyone downstairs knows the story—the keeper's apprentice fled with the covenant. What nobody downstairs asks is *why the crown wants it.*"

"Power," Serah said flatly. "The old answer. Kings want power."

"Do you know what the Hollowgate *is*?" Aldous asked, and when she didn't answer, he answered for her, leaning forward as far as his chains allowed, his voice dropping to the register men use for blasphemy. "It's not a wall. It's not a lock. It's a *treaty*. Two realms agreed to flow into each other in balance—the gift crossing over thin and slow, life crossing back the same way. That's why the gift fades in our world, why bearers are born fewer every generation. The tap was turned down. On purpose. By both sides. Because when it runs fast, it doesn't just make wonders. It makes appetite."

Serah felt cold crawl up from the base of her spine. "Appetite."

"There are stories. From before the last sealing. Kingdoms that opened the gate wide and drank deep—crops that grew overnight, wounds healed by touch, storms commanded. And the people who drank became—" he made a gesture with his stained fingers, opening and closing, opening and closing, "—*hollow*. The Otherworld's power isn't free, Inquisitor. It trades. It takes shape somewhere else. Drain the gate, and the land on the other side dies, and the drain keeps pulling until it finds something living on *this* side to take instead." His hands dropped. "The Guild's whole purpose, the reason we hid, the reason the ledgers exist, was to keep the flow thin. To keep everyone safe from the temptation."

"And the crown."

Aldous looked at her with something close to pity, which from a chained man to a free one was an unbearable inversion, and said:

"The crown has been taking registered bearers to the Bastion since midsummer. You've seen the carts. Ask yourself where they go. Ask yourself why the king hasn't held court in daylight since spring. Ask yourself why the Lord-Inquisitor answers questions about the lower levels with a smile and no words." His stutter returned, worse. "They're not hunting the gift, Inquisitor. They're *farming* it. The ledger gives them the anchors. The registrations give them the supply. And the covenant page—the page Galloway carries—they need it to open the Hollowgate *wide*. All the way. Permanently. So the flow never thins again, and whoever stands at the gate drinks forever."

The lantern flame stood perfectly still, as flames do not, and Serah would remember that afterward—the stillness of it, as if the fire itself were listening.

* * *

She walked the long way back through the Bastion, which was a coward's route and she knew it, taking the upper corridors instead of passing the sealed stair to the lower levels. But the upper corridors had windows, and the windows faced south, toward the distant smudge of the capital, where the palace stood, and beyond it the hills where—according to every map she had ever drawn in service of the crown—the ruins of the Hollowgate lay folded into the winter country like a scar into skin.

Conveyed to the Bastion, not the pyre. Carts going down at night. A king who ruled from behind curtains. A Lord-Inquisitor whose zeal had always struck her, in the privacy of her own mind, as slightly too *convenient*—as if the hunt served him less than he served it.

She had believed, all these years, in the work. That was the honest truth of her, the truth beneath the discipline: Serah Vane had joined the Inquisition at nineteen because she had seen what ungoverned gift-work did to a village—had seen a hedge-witch's bargain rot an orchard and a family with it—and she had believed that law was the only fence strong enough to stand between ordinary people and power that fed on them. She had burned people. She had signed the orders herself, in her own hand, and she had slept, because the alternative was to stop sleeping forever, and the work required a whole woman.

Every pyre, she thought now, standing in the corridor's window-light with her reflection faint against the winter sky. Every registration she had processed. Every name she had entered into the rolls in her careful inquisitor's script—names that, according to a stuttering scribe in a cell below her feet, were anchors. Handles by which the crown could reach into a living person and pull.

*I built the handles,* she thought. *I sharpened them. I handed them up the stairs one by one and thanked the crown for the honor of service.*

She had spent her career telling herself the difference between the Guild and the Inquisition was the difference between hoarding power and policing it. Now the shape of the thing reversed itself in her mind with a click she felt physically, in her teeth: the Guild had hidden the names to protect the people in them. The Inquisition had collected the names to feed them to something. She had spent ten years on the wrong side of a locked door, guarding it for the wolves.

And beneath the horror, quieter and more dangerous, the other thing stirred—the warmth behind her sternum, the one she had felt in Bram's burning hall. She pressed her hand flat against her breastbone, alone in the corridor, and felt it again: a low answering hum, like a ward recognizing a signature. Like a ledger recognizing a name.

She was not merely complicit. She was inventory.

* * *

She went back down. This was the part she would examine later and not understand—why, given the choice between filing her report and sealing the scribe's fate, she descended the stairs she had avoided all evening, past the sealed door to the lower levels, hearing through the stones a sound she had told herself for months was water in pipes. It was not water. Water does not breathe.

Aldous looked up when she entered, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop pretending to be calm.

"Inquisitor?"

"Galloway has the covenant page," she said. "The keeper's blood opens the Hollowgate. Your masters' records will say whether that's true—whether the gate answers to a lineage, a person, a *name*." She crouched to his level, chains clinking, and heard her own voice come out level and cold, the inquisitor's voice, repurposed now for a different inquisition entirely. "So tell me the last thing you know, Master Fenwick, and tell it completely. Who was Wren Galloway, what does the gate want, and how much time do we have before the crown forces it open?"

He stared at her. Outside, somewhere above, the wind off the winter moors found the Bastion's high windows and moaned through them like something calling home.

"Midwinter night," Aldous whispered. "The gate opens widest at midwinter night. And that's—" he counted, lips moving, the arithmetic arriving in his face like a blow. "That's eleven days."

Eleven days. Eleven days to find a fugitive she had sworn to burn, carrying the one page that could save or damn both worlds, and decide—with no law left to hide behind—which side of the locked door she truly stood on.

Serah Vane rose, took up her lantern, and went to steal a horse.
