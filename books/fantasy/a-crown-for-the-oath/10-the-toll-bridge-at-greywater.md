## 10. The Toll Bridge at Greywater

The river came out of the north like a rumor of spring, black water sliding beneath grey ice, and where it met the road there was Greywater Bridge—three arches of old kings' masonry, a tollhouse squatting at its near end like a toad that had learned to collect coin. Wren saw the banners before they saw anything else: the sun-and-scales of the Crown's inquisition, snapping on two poles flanking the checkpoint, and beneath them, wagons. Cages, rather. Wagons with barred sides, and shapes huddled inside them.

"Turn around," Isolde said quietly. She had gone very still in the way she did when she was measuring ground for a fight she did not want. "We find another crossing."

"There is no other crossing," Corvo said. "Greywater's the only bridge over the Vell between here and the mountains that isn't a ferryman's fancy. Ferries are burned. I'd know; I paid for half of them." He spat over the side of the cart, a habit Wren had stopped finding charming somewhere around the third betrayal joke. "We go through. We go through as what we look like—a smuggler hauling salt pork, a hired sword, and two orphans bound for their aunt's kitchen in Harrowmere."

Pip made a small sound from under the sacking in the cart bed. It might have been a laugh. It might not have been.

Wren pulled the canvas down over him anyway, tucking it at the edges the way Bram used to tuck ledger pages so they wouldn't catch a draft. The boy had been quiet for two days—not frightened-quiet but *thinking*-quiet, which from Pip was worse. He kept drawing doors in the frost on the wagon boards. Little doors, with little hinges, and once, worryingly, a door drawn open onto nothing at all.

"They're checking hands," Wren said. "At the tollhouse. I've seen it done at Ellsmere before the burnings started. They make you hold your palms flat on the table while the clerk watches. Gift shows in the bloodline—it pools at the wrist like a bruise, if you know how to look."

"And do they know how to look?" Corvo asked.

"The man at the table does." Wren had seen him through the spyglass an hour back, when the road bent along the ridge and the whole checkpoint lay below them like a painting of everything they feared: the tollhouse with its chimney smoking, the line of carts waiting, the clerk in his grey coat moving from driver to driver with his little book. And behind him, apart from everyone, a woman in a long dark riding cloak who did not move at all, who stood at the rail of the bridge looking down into the water as though it were telling her something. Even at this distance Wren knew her. You did not forget the posture of someone who had watched your teacher burn. "That's her. Serah Vane."

Isolde's hand went to her sword hilt and then, deliberately, away again. "Then we are already dead," she said, "or we are about to be interesting. Both have happened to me before."

* * *

They joined the line of carts a half-mile out, which gave Wren time to count the soldiers—eighteen, maybe twenty, most of them young, most of them cold, none of them looking forward to standing in river wind until dusk—and to notice the thing about the cages that made their stomach turn over slowly, like a boat taking water.

The cages were nearly empty.

Two held people: an old woman with white hair braided tight, and a boy perhaps sixteen, wrists bound, staring at nothing with the particular blankness of someone who had been told what would happen next and had chosen to stop being present for it. The other four wagons were bare, their floors stained, straw scattered and filthy.

They were not here to transport prisoners, Wren realized. They were here *collecting*. A trap laid across the only bridge, baited with fear, and every traveler who showed a shadow of the gift went into those wagons and got carried south to whatever the crown was doing with its harvest. Wren thought of Serah's face at the guildhouse fire—the way she had looked at Bram's death not with triumph but with a kind of terrible attention, as though reading a page she hadn't wanted to be given—and thought, *she doesn't know either. She doesn't know what the wagons are for.*

Or she did, and had decided it didn't matter. There was no way to know. That was the worst of it.

"Palms on the table," Corvo murmured, coaching them one last time. "Don't flinch at the questions. If they ask where you're from, you're from Millbrook—burned last summer, no records left, clever of us. Pip—" he reached back and squeezed the boy's ankle through the sacking, "—you stay under there and you think small thoughts. Small as you can make them. Doors that barely exist. Mouseholes."

"I can't turn it off," Pip whispered. "It's always talking. Everything with a hinge talks if you listen."

"Then don't listen," Corvo said, and Wren heard in his voice the rough gentleness he pretended not to own. "Just this once. Be deaf for ten minutes and I'll buy you a whole harbor full of things with hinges."

The line moved. The wind came off the river carrying ice-smell and smoke, and somewhere ahead a soldier laughed too loudly at nothing, the laughter of men trying to stay warm by staying cruel.

Wren's palms were sweating. They wiped them on their coat, thought of Bram's iron key against their sternum where it hung on its cord, and breathed the way the old keeper had taught them: slow in, slower out, the breath a door opening onto stillness. Whatever came next, they would not run. Running put Pip in the open. Running put everyone in the open.

They were three carts from the table when the screaming started.

* * *

It came from the far cage—the boy, the sixteen-year-old, who had been blank and absent all morning and chose that moment to come violently back into his body. One of the soldiers had been rattling his cage bars with a spear butt, some idle cruelty to pass the hours, and something in the boy must simply have finished breaking. He threw himself against the bars, screaming words that didn't quite resolve into language, and the gift went off in him like a struck bell.

Wren felt it before they understood it. Every lock within thirty feet shuddered. The padlock on the near cage wept rust as though grieving. The tollhouse door strained against its latch hard enough to crack the frame, and the clerk's inkwell shattered across his ledger in a bloom of black, and the boy in the cage screamed and screamed because he could not stop it, could not close what he had opened, because no one had ever taught him that a gift could be shut like a door instead of merely endured like weather.

"Gift-bearer!" someone shouted, uselessly, as though the wagons weren't proof enough. "Manifestation! Manifestation!"

What happened after that, Wren's mind recorded in pieces, the way it recorded the burning of the guildhouse—in fragments that would come back later at bad moments.

Soldiers converging. The spear butt rising again. The old woman in the far cage pressing her face to the bars, saying *no, no, leave him, he's only frightened.* The boy collapsing inward, sobbing now, the locks all through the checkpoint rattling in sympathy like teeth chattering. And Serah Vane turning from the bridge rail, and Wren watching her face across sixty feet of frozen mud, watching the moment she recognized what the manifestation meant—not just that the boy bore the gift, but that the boy could not control it, and that uncontrolled gifts ended one way in the crown's arithmetic, and that way was written on the warrant she carried in her coat.

The order, when she gave it, was almost gentle. "Restrain him. No brands. He'll travel better calm." As though calm were a thing you could pack into a cage like straw.

And the spear went up again anyway, because soldiers hear orders the way they want to, and the boy screamed—

—and Wren moved.

Later, they would not be able to say whether they decided it or simply discovered they had already done it. What they remembered was the cold of the mud through their boot soles, and the weight of Bram's key suddenly enormous against their chest, and the sound their own voice made, pitched to carry, pitched the way keepers had pitched it across a thousand years to speak to things that were older than speech:

"*Hold.*"

Every lock on Greywater Bridge held.

Not broke—held. Seized. The cage padlocks fused. The tollhouse door welded into its frame. The shackles on the boy's wrists locked so fast to each other that they became a single bar of iron, and the spear butt came down on nothing because the cage door would no longer open in either direction. Twenty soldiers stood in sudden silence, holding weapons they could no longer use against anything with a hinge, and every eye on the bridge turned toward the mud-spattered person in the smuggler's cart who had spoken one word and stopped the world's small machinery dead.

Wren stood in the traces with their hand raised, palm out, and knew—knew with the sick certainty of a ledger balancing wrong—that there was no hiding this. The gatekeeper's blood answered the gatekeeper's voice, and the voice had been heard, and the blood was visible now, pooling dark at their wrists like a bruise the size of a confession.

"Well," said Corvo Ashe, beside them, very quietly, to no one. "There goes the salt pork business."

* * *

Serah Vane crossed the distance between the bridge rail and the cart at a walk, which was worse than running. Running would have meant urgency, and urgency could be fought. This was procedure. She stopped six feet away, hood back, and Wren saw that she was thinner than at Ellsmere, and paler, and that there were shadows under her eyes like bruises, and hated themselves for noticing, and noticed anyway.

"Open the cage," she said—not to Wren. To the soldiers. Then, when none moved, because none of their keys worked anymore: "You. Gatekeeper. Open it."

"You know what I am," Wren said.

"I know what you *did*." Her eyes flicked to the cart bed, to the sacking, and Wren saw her arrive at the conclusion the way a reader arrives at the end of a page: completely, without needing to be shown. "There's another one under the canvas. Small. Young." Her jaw tightened. "Take the canvas off."

"If I do," Wren said, "you'll put him in a wagon."

"If I don't, my lieutenant will, and he'll do it badly." Something moved behind her eyes, quick and buried. "I am offering you the version of this where nobody bleeds. I cannot offer you more than that."

"You could," Wren said. "You could let us pass."

For a moment—one heartbeat, two—the whole checkpoint seemed to lean in. The soldiers, the clerk with his ruined ledger, the old woman at her bars, the boy hiccupping himself silent. The river ran under the arches with its patient black sound. Serah Vane stood with her warrant in her pocket and twenty witnesses at her back and the truth of the crown's harvest sitting in her chest like swallowed coals, and Wren watched her weigh it, actually watch her weigh it, and understood that they were seeing a woman stand at her own threshold.

"My report will say," Serah said slowly, as though composing it aloud, "that the gatekeeper manifested, that the manifestation was contained, and that the gatekeeper escaped south across the ice when the soldiers' weapons failed. Which will be true." She glanced at the frozen river, at the black water sliding under grey ice, and something almost like humor touched her mouth and died. "The Vell ice won't bear a rider this week. Everyone knows it. So no one will ride after you onto it, because no one rides onto river ice that everyone knows won't bear them."

"That's a lie," Wren said.

"It's a *report*," Serah said. "Open the cage. Take your boy. Go south. And gatekeeper—" her voice dropped, low enough that only Wren and the wind caught it, "—whatever you're carrying, whatever Bram Nye died keeping, get it past Greywater. Get it somewhere they can never reach. Because I have seen what the king's physicians do with what they take out of the wagons, and I have started having dreams, and I am not yet certain which frightens me more." Her hand closed briefly, hard, on the cart's rail, knuckles white, as if holding herself to it. "Go. Before I remember what I'm supposed to be doing."

Wren opened the cage. It took both hands and the key's cold weight and a word said softly, a door asked rather than commanded, and the lock sighed open like something released from pain. The boy stumbled out into the mud, wrists still ringed in fused iron, and Isolde Ferrant stepped down from the cart, drew her sword, and cut the shackle-bar with two strokes—her oath-breaking made metal and public, done in front of the crown's own soldiers with her chin up and her eyes daring any man among them to comment. None did. Perhaps it was the knight's bearing. Perhaps it was the gatekeeper's word still humming in every hinge on the bridge.

Pip emerged from under the canvas on his own, small and grave, and looked up at Serah Vane with the frank appallingly clear gaze of a child deciding something permanent.

"You lied," Pip said to her. "In your head. Just now. It sounded like a door opening the wrong way. It was a good sound."

Serah stared at him. For a moment her composure cracked clean through, and underneath it was not hardness but exhaustion, and grief, and a terror so old it had grown comfortable. "Get in the cart," she said roughly.

They went. Corvo turned the team with a smuggler's economy, muttering about wasted years and ruined cover stories and, underneath it, inaudible unless you knew to listen, *thank you*, which he would deny to his grave. The wheels found the bridge stones. The arches passed over them one by one, shadow, light, shadow, light, like pages turning.

At the far end Wren looked back once.

Serah Vane stood where they had left her, in the middle of the checkpoint she commanded, surrounded by soldiers awaiting orders and prisoners awaiting judgment and a clerk awaiting instruction, and she had not moved. She was looking down at her own hands—turning them over, palm and back, palm and back—as though checking them for bruises, for shadows, for the pooling dark of a bloodline she had spent her whole life hunting in others.

Then she straightened, and turned to her men, and began dictating her report in a clear carrying voice: *the gatekeeper, contained, escaped south across the ice; pursuit impossible; the bridge secure.*

A lie. The first one. Wren heard it land behind them like a stone dropped into deep water, and did not see the ripples—but they would spread. Ripples always spread.

The road bent south. The wind came off the river at their backs now instead of their faces, pushing them onward, and Pip pressed close against Wren's side under the canvas and whispered, "She's coming with us someday. Not yet. But the door's unlocked."

Wren looked down at the boy. "What door?"

"The one she nailed shut inside herself," Pip said, and closed his eyes, and slept.
