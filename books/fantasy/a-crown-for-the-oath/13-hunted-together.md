## 13. Hunted Together

The snow had been falling for two days when Isolde first saw the rider.

She marked her the way she'd been trained to mark everything on a long patrol: by silhouette first, then gait, then the small betrayals of posture that told you whether a body was tired or hungry or afraid. This one sat her horse like a woman who had learned to ride from books and pain in equal measure—spine straight as a lance, shoulders squared against the wind, but with the faint hitch of someone whose thighs had long since stopped complaining and started simply enduring. The rider came down the far slope of the valley at an angle that was neither pursuit nor flight, which was somehow worse than either.

"Rider," Isolde said quietly, not turning from the fire she was feeding with the last of their dry wood. "South ridge. Alone."

Wren looked up from the covenant-page they were studying by firelight, and Isolde watched them do the thing they always did when danger arrived: go very still, very quiet, like a door deciding whether to open. Pip, curled against Corvo's bedroll with his head full of fever from three days of cold rain, did not stir. Thank all the patient gods for small mercies.

"One rider?" Corvo asked. He was propped against the cart wheel, gray-faced, one hand pressed to the wound beneath his coat that he still pretended was a bruise. "That's either a fool or a trap."

"It's Vane," Wren said.

Isolde turned. Wren hadn't looked at the ridge at all. They were staring into the fire, and their face had gone to that place Isolde had learned to hate—a place past fear, past anger, into something flat and cold as river ice in deep winter.

"You can't know that," Corvo said.

"I can." Wren folded the covenant-page with hands that did not shake, which Isolde thought must have cost them something enormous. "I've felt her since Greywater. Like a splinter under the skin. She's been following us the way a hound follows blood, except she isn't hunting us. She's *following* us. There's a difference, and I don't think she knows it herself yet."

Isolde rose, hand going to the sword she'd carried since before her disgrace—the same sword, though the king's mark had been hammered off the pommel, leaving only a raw silver scar where it had been. "Then I'll meet her before she reaches camp. If she comes alone, we talk. If she doesn't—"

"If she doesn't, you die on an open hillside," Wren said, "and she rides back to Ellsmere knowing exactly where we are, and Pip dies of fever in a ditch because we ran without our cart. Sit down, Dame Ferrant."

It was the first time Wren had used her title since the quarry town, and they used it now like a blade laid flat across a table—not drawn, but present. Isolde sat down. It galled her, sitting, while an inquisitor of the Gift-Hunt descended on them through the snow, but Wren had been right about the toll bridge, and right about the shepherd's dogs, and right about the ice on the Greywater ford when every instinct Isolde owned had said otherwise. Being right that often earned a person the right to be obeyed, even when obedience tasted like copper.

So they waited. That was the worst of it, Isolde thought—the waiting. She had spent fifteen years in the king's service learning that waiting was where courage went to rot, that action was mercy, that a charge beat a siege nine times in ten. But this wasn't a war. This was a family huddled around a dying fire with a sick child, watching a single figure pick its way down through the drifts, and there was no charge to make that wouldn't break something they couldn't replace.

The rider stopped thirty paces out. Deliberately, theatrically, she dismounted and walked the last stretch leading her horse by the bridle, reins loose, both hands visible. Snow gathered on the shoulders of her gray cloak. Her hood was back despite the cold, and Isolde felt her stomach tighten with old recognition.

Serah Vane. They had never met—Isolde had left court before Vane's elevation—but every knight in Aldric's service knew the name. The Inquisitor Who Never Smiled. The Witch-Finder Who Burned Her Own Mentor. Stories traveled faster than truth, and Vane's stories had arrived ahead of her everywhere she went, growing teeth in the telling.

"Keeper Galloway," Vane said, and her voice carried flat and clear across the snow. "I'm alone. I have no writ, no hunters, no warrant. I'm asking for shelter until the storm passes, and then I'm asking for passage with you."

"*Passage*," Corvo repeated, with the delicacy of a man tasting something spoiled. "With us. To the Hollowgate."

"To wherever you're going." Vane stopped at the edge of the firelight. Up close, Isolde could see what the stories never mentioned: the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the way her weight favored her left leg, the fine tremor in her right hand that she stilled by gripping the reins harder. "And I know what I am to you. All of you. I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you not to let me freeze."

"You burned the guildhouse," Wren said. They had risen without Isolde noticing, and stood now with the firelight behind them, so their face was shadowed and unreadable. "You burned it with people inside."

"I burned a building I was ordered to burn, on a night when I believed—" Vane stopped. Breathed. Started again, and Isolde recognized the effort of it, the terrible discipline of a person rebuilding a sentence brick by brick because the first version had collapsed. "I believed what the crown taught me to believe. I was wrong. I've been wrong for longer than I can stand, and I found out how wrong the night I read your scribe's testimony, and I stole the missing fragment of your rite out of the king's own strongroom, and I deserted my post in the middle of a blizzard, and I have been riding after you ever since. So yes. I burned the guildhouse. And yes, Bram Hallowell died in it. And if you put a knife in me right now, I will not argue that I deserve it."

The silence that followed was the kind Isolde had only heard a few times in her life—on battlefields, mostly, in the gap between the order to advance and the first arrow. A silence with weight and edges.

It was broken by a small, hoarse voice from the cart.

"She's telling the truth."

Everyone turned. Pip had pushed himself up on one elbow, fever-bright eyes fixed on Vane with the unnerving directness he reserved for doors and other things most people couldn't see. "Her gift is awake," he said. "It woke up when Bram died. It's been awake ever since, and it hurts her, and she didn't tell anyone, and she's been carrying it alone for weeks like a stone in her chest." He coughed, wet and rattling. "Doors talk about her. Doors *like* her. Doors don't like anybody."

"Pip," Isolde said gently, "you're burning up. Lie down."

"No." He struggled upright, and Corvo caught him, steadying the boy against his shoulder. Pip's gaze never left Vane. "You broke me out of the cage," he said to Isolde, "because you chose mercy over law. You said oaths are just promises, and promises are just words, and words can be *re-said*." His voice cracked on the last word, fever pulling it thin. "Well. Re-say yours."

Isolde looked at Serah Vane standing in the snow with her empty hands and her dead mentor's crimes stacked around her neck, and she understood—with the awful clarity that sometimes came to her in the moment before a decision—that the boy was right, and that being right was going to be the hardest thing she'd done since the day she'd refused to hunt children and lost everything for it.

She drew her sword.

Vane didn't flinch. Isolde gave her credit for that; most people flinched. Instead the inquisitor watched the blade come free of its scabbard with the weary attention of someone confirming a sum already calculated—and then Isolde reversed it, drove it point-down into the snow between them, and stepped back from it.

"That's my oath," she said. "Re-said. No king's mark, no hunt, no law but the one we make around this fire. Touch anyone here and I won't need the sword—I'll use my hands, and I'll take my time. But until then, you're under my protection, Serah Vane, and my protection has held for fifteen years against worse than you."

Vane stared at the sword standing upright in the snowdrift, its scarred pommel white with frost. Something moved through her face—too fast to name, too raw to be anything simple. "Why?" she asked. Not suspiciously. Genuinely, as if the word had no furniture in her house anymore.

"Because somebody once broke me out of a cage too," Isolde said. "It just took me twenty years to notice."

* * *

They let her inside the ring of the fire because the alternative was her corpse, and whatever else Wren Galloway was, they were not yet a killer of unarmed women in the snow. But Isolde noticed that Wren took the seat farthest from Vane, angled so the fire was between them, and that Wren's hand rested near the iron key on its cord around their neck the whole time Vane spoke—as if the key might need defending even from a story.

Vane told it plainly, which Isolde came to respect. No performance, no self-flagellation dressed up as confession. She laid out the facts like a scribe laying out documents: the scribe's interrogation, the ledgers showing gift-bearers harvested rather than judged, the king's wasting illness and the apparatus of stolen vitality that kept him on his throne, the rite fragment she'd taken from the strongroom, the desertion, the pursuit. When she reached the part about Bram—about holding the old keeper down while the guildhouse burned, about feeling her own gift wake in her chest like a second heartbeat while the smoke rose—her voice did not break, but it went somewhere below breaking, to a register Isolde recognized from men describing battles they'd survived when their friends hadn't.

"I didn't kill him," she said. "I want that understood, not because it absolves me—it doesn't—but because you should know the shape of what happened. The hunters killed him. I held him for them. I told myself it was necessary, that the mission mattered more than one old man, and I told myself that lie so smoothly that I didn't even feel it as a lie until later." She looked directly at Wren across the fire. "He spoke to me. At the end. Do you want to know what he said?"

"No," Wren said.

"He said—"

"*No.*" Wren was on their feet, and the firelight showed Isolde their face at last, and it was worse than anger; it was grief with nowhere to go, packed so tight it had become geological. "You don't get to give me his last words like a gift. You don't get to make yourself the courier of his final message so you can feel the weight pass out of your hands and into mine. You held him down. Whatever he said, it belongs to *you* now—you carry it, you keep it, you wake up with it at night the way I wake up with the sound of the roof beams coming down. That's your penance, Vane. Not absolution. *Custody.*"

The words fell into the snow like stones into deep water. Vane absorbed them without moving, and Isolde saw her jaw work once, twice, and saw her swallow whatever answer she had built. "Understood," she said finally, hoarsely. "Custody. Yes."

Corvo snorted softly from the cart. "Gods preserve me. Fifteen years I ran cargo past every checkpoint in three provinces, and I never once saw a witch-hunter take a dressing-down and say *understood*. You're sure she's not a changeling?"

"Corvo," Isolde warned.

"What? I'm making an observation. Observations are legal." He shifted, wincing, and Isolde saw him press his hand again to the wound he wouldn't show anyone, and saw the grayness around his mouth that meant the pretending was costing more than it used to. "Here's my observation, then, freely given. We need her."

"We don't," Wren said.

"We need her *exactly*," Corvo said, and his voice had shed its levity entirely, leaving the trader underneath, the man who counted lives like coin because he'd learned young that sentiment spent badly. "She knows the rite fragment. She knows the king's host—its numbers, its routes, its commander's habits. She turned her own hunters' fire aside at the bridge, which means she can probably turn it aside again, and come midwinter at the Hollowgate I would very much like to have a person among us who can do that. And she deserted, which means the crown wants her head as much as ours. She's not our enemy anymore, Keeper. She's our fellow condemned. There's no closer kinship in the world."

"There's Bram," Wren said. Quietly. Terribly.

"Yes," Corvo agreed. "There's Bram. And there's Pip, fevered half to death in a cart, and there's a gate that needs opening in eleven days, and there's a king with an army who will burn every one of us and dance on the ashes. Bram is dead, Wren. I loved the old bastard too—he bought my first honest contract when no one else would touch a smuggler with a writ against him. But the dead don't get a vote at this fire. The living do. And the living need every sword, every secret, and every pair of awake eyes we can get."

Isolde watched Wren stand there in the firelight, torn between the grave and the road, and she understood that no argument she could add would help. Some doors only the person themselves could open. She'd spent enough time around Pip to know that much.

So she said nothing, and Corvo said nothing, and Vane said nothing, and the snow came down through the dark like the world slowly erasing itself, and Pip's breathing rattled wetly in the cart, and the fire burned low.

It was Pip who ended it, because of course it was.

He had slid back down against Corvo's shoulder, half-asleep, but his voice came drifting out of the dusk of fever, small and certain and utterly without guile:

"The gate likes her too."

Wren closed their eyes.

When they opened them again, Isolde saw that the flat cold thing had gone out of their face—not replaced by forgiveness, nothing so generous, but by something wearier and more durable. The look of a person accepting a burden they'd hoped to refuse.

"Vane." Wren's voice was rough. "You sleep outside the fire ring. You eat last. You carry your own weight and Pip's water and half the wood, and you don't touch the covenant-page, and you don't speak Bram's name to me again unless I ask—which I may never ask. And when we reach the Hollowgate, if anything you've told us proves false—if there's one hunter, one writ, one hidden brand following your trail—there won't be a trial. Do you understand the terms?"

"I understand the terms," Vane said.

"Then welcome to the family nobody survives."

It was meant, Isolde thought, as bitterness. But Vane received it like a sacrament, bowing her head over the fire as if over a blessing, and Isolde found her chest aching with something she couldn't name—pity, maybe, or recognition. She knew what it was to be taken in on sufferance. She knew the taste of grudging bread.

Later, when the others slept, Isolde kept the watch and found Vane awake beside her, staring north through the snowfall toward the distant black line of hills where the Hollowgate waited under its ruin.

"How bad is it?" Isolde asked. "At the gate. What's coming."

Vane was silent long enough that Isolde thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "The king's host marches in force. Not hunters this time—an army. He knows the rite exists now, and he knows what it can do for him, and he is *dying*, Dame Ferrant, and a dying king with stolen power is the most dangerous creature in either world." She paused. "And there's something else. Something the rite fragment says about the price. I need to speak to Wren about it. Alone. Before midwinter."

Isolde fed another branch to the fire and watched the sparks climb into the dark, and felt the hook of those words sink in deep.

"Then you'd better hope they ask you soon," she said. "Because the way Wren's been reading that page at night, when they think we're sleeping—I don't think they're planning to survive midwinter. And I'm starting to be afraid they're right."
