## 15. The Long Midwinter March

The cold came for Corvo Ashe the way debt collectors came—patiently, at first, and then all at once.

He felt it in the old wound first, the one below his ribs where a Harbormaster's knife had left him with a scar shaped like a fishhook and a promise that someday the sea would collect. Ten years it had slept. Now, on the third day out from Greywater, with the wilds gone white as unmarked paper and the wind sawing through the pines like a blade across a whetstone, it woke up hungry.

He didn't tell them. Not yet.

Corvo rode at the rear of their small procession, which was where he'd always ridden, in smuggling runs and honest ones alike—the back of the line was where you watched for knives, and Corvo had spent his whole life watching for knives. Ahead of him moved the others: Dame Isolde Ferrant on her bony gray mare, armor traded for furs that made her look less like a knight and more like something the winter itself had dreamed; the boy Pip bundled on her saddlebow, chattering to every door they passed, and there were doors out here, doors only Pip could see, leaning against trees or standing alone in snowfields, and the boy greeted each one by name. And ahead of them both, wrapped in a cloak too thin for this country, walked Wren Galloway.

Wren wouldn't ride. That had become clear on the first day, when Isolde had tried to boost them onto the mare and Wren had gone stiff as a fence post and said, quietly, *I need to feel the ground.* Corvo understood. Gatekeepers probably did. You didn't trust your feet to anything you couldn't feel breathing beneath you.

What Corvo understood less well—and what gnawed at him worse than the wound—was why Wren kept looking at the Hollowgate's direction the way a man looks at a gallows he's already paid his fee to climb.

*You're planning something,* Corvo thought, watching them pick their way over a frozen streambed. *You've been planning it since the bridge, and you haven't told a soul.*

Well. He'd been a smuggler long enough to know that everyone carried cargo they didn't declare. The question was whether Wren's cargo would get them all killed, or just the one carrying it.

The wind picked up around midday, driving ice crystals sideways so that the whole world became a smear of white and gray. They made camp early in the lee of a granite shelf, Isolde setting the fire with the grim competence of someone who'd wintered in worse places, Pip gathering deadfall and narrating to it—"this one's tired, this one wants to be a spoon"—until even Isolde's mouth twitched at the corners.

Corvo sat apart, as he did, and pressed his hand flat against his side, and looked at what came away on his palm.

Not much. Yet. But enough.

"Corvo." Wren had come to crouch beside him without sound, which was a gatekeeper trick Corvo had never gotten used to—people who moved like they were afraid of waking the ground. "Your color's wrong."

"My color's always wrong. I'm a smuggler. We're born gray."

"You know what I mean."

Corvo looked at them—at the sharp face, the eyes that held more years than they should have, the mouth set in that particular line of someone carrying a weight they'd decided no one else could touch. He'd betrayed this person once. He'd stood in a courtroom ten years ago and given testimony that had cost Wren Galloway their family, their name, their place in the world, and he'd told himself then that he'd had no choice, that the Guild's ledgers were a fantasy and the crown's gold was real, and he had believed it right up until the moment he'd seen what his words had done.

He'd spent ten years paying that debt. He was beginning to suspect he'd run out of time to pay it in full.

"I mean," Wren said, sitting down beside him in the snow, close enough that their shoulders touched, "that you've been bleeding since Greywater, and you've been hiding it since Greywater, and I'm the keeper of a guild whose entire trade is noticing what people hide. So."

Corvo laughed, and it hurt, and he let the laugh cover the hurt because that was also an old trick. "You want honesty from me now? After everything?"

"I want honesty from you *especially* after everything. It's the only currency between us that isn't counterfeit."

He looked at his palm again. Then, because she was right, because lying to Wren Galloway had stopped being possible somewhere on the road out of Ellsmere, he pulled back the layers of wool and showed them the bandage, and the stain spreading through it like ink through water.

Wren went very still. Stillness, Corvo had learned, was what Wren did instead of screaming.

"How long?"

"Harbormaster's knife. Ten years ago. It healed crooked, and crooked things break." He shrugged, and immediately regretted it. "It's been breaking since the cold came. It'll keep breaking slower if I rest, faster if I don't, and either way it breaks. That's the whole ledger entry."

"Why didn't you say something?"

"Because," Corvo said, and here he found himself looking out at the darkening pines rather than at Wren's face, "you've got enough weight. Because the boy needs someone to teach him card games and the knight needs someone to argue with, and if they knew, they'd start treating me like cargo instead of crew. And because—" he paused, and the truth came out before he could dress it up, the way truths do when you're tired and cold and dying, "—because I wanted a few more days of being a person instead of a patient. Sue me."

Wren didn't laugh. Wren reached out and took his wrist—not gently, exactly, but firmly, the way you hold someone when you're afraid they'll drift off—and said, "You don't get to spend yourself in secret. Not anymore. Whatever you're carrying, we carry it together. That's the whole point of what we are now."

"And what are we now?" Corvo asked, and heard how raw his voice came out. "I sold you once, Wren. Don't think I've forgotten the exchange rate."

Wren's grip tightened. "You carried us through the marsh country. You gave up your route, your contacts, your ship's papers. You've been paying that debt since the day we met again, and I—" they stopped, and Corvo watched them wrestle with something, and lose. "I forgave you a long time ago. I'm not sure I ever told you. So. Consider it told."

The wind moaned through the shelf above them. Somewhere behind, far back along their trail, Corvo thought he heard a horn—or maybe just the wind wearing a horn's voice. He'd spent his life telling those two sounds apart, and lately the wind had gotten very good at lying.

"Eat," Wren said, pressing a heel of bread into his hand. "And tomorrow, you ride. No arguments. Isolde can walk; she's built like a cathedral."

"Aye," Corvo said. And then, because the night was dark and the wound was talking and Wren was warm beside him: "Wren. Whatever you're planning at that gate—you'll tell me before you do it. Promise me that much. I've watched people sacrifice themselves for causes my whole life. It never once ended the way they thought it would."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. The firelight moved on their face like water.

"I promise you won't be surprised," Wren said finally.

Which, Corvo thought, settling back against the stone with the bread untouched in his hand, was the answer of a person who intended to be surprising.

* * *

They crossed the frozen wilds for six more days, and Corvo taught while he could still stand upright.

It started as an accident. Pip had found him checking the snares at dawn—he'd insisted on the snares, because a man who couldn't march could still feed people, and Corvo Ashe had never in his life tolerated being useless—and the boy had squatted down beside him in the snow and asked, with the terrible directness of children, "Are you going to die?"

"Pip—" Isolde began, from across the camp.

"It's all right," Corvo said. He'd decided something in the night, something quiet and complete. "Yeah, lad. Probably. Everyone does. I'm just getting a schedule."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Does it hurt?"

"Some. Less than some things. More than others." Corvo reset the snare's trigger, showing the boy the tension of it, the way the green wood bent and held. "Here. You want to learn this? A snare's honest work. You put something into the world—a little wire, a little patience—and the world gives something back. That's the oldest trade there is."

"Is that what you do?" Pip asked. "Trade?"

"That's what I *did*. Smuggling's just trading with extra steps and worse company." He glanced up and found Wren standing at the edge of the trees, watching them, arms crossed against the cold, and something in their expression made him decide to keep talking. "You know what nobody tells you about trade, though? Real trade? It's not about what you give up. Everybody thinks it is. Give this, lose that, sacrifice, cost—all words for the same cowardly arithmetic."

"What's it about, then?" Pip asked.

"About what you're buying." Corvo tied off the snare and sat back on his heels, and the movement sent a hot wire of pain up through his side, and he talked through it because the talking was the point. "Every deal I ever made—and I've made deals that would curdle milk—I was buying one of two things. Time, or people. Time to run, time to plan, time to see another spring. Or people—the right sailor, the right fence, the right friend in the right doorway. And here's the secret, lad, the one they'll never print in any ledger: the best trades are the ones where you give away the thing you thought was most precious and find out it wasn't. You give your gold and learn you needed courage. You give your name and learn you needed love. The world's a better merchant than any of us. It never takes what actually matters unless you insist on offering it."

"That doesn't make sense," Pip said.

"No," Corvo agreed, laughing, and the laugh cost him. "It won't. Not yet. File it away. Open it in twenty years."

"He's teaching you to be a criminal," Isolde said, arriving with the firewood, her breath steaming.

"I'm teaching him to be a *merchant*. There's a difference, and it's mostly paperwork."

But that evening, when Pip had gone to sleep and Isolde stood the first watch, Corvo found Wren awake by the banked fire, turning Bram's iron key over and over in their fingers like a rosary. He lowered himself down beside them with the care of a man handling his own last full purse, and for a while neither of them spoke. The fire ticked. Far off, a wolf sang one note and thought better of the rest.

"Tell me about the worst trade you ever made," Wren said.

Corvo huffed. "That's a tournament field, that is. There's the testimony—" he watched Wren flinch and didn't stop, because stopping would be mercy and mercy would be a lie, "—but honestly? The testimony wasn't the worst. The worst was the year before. I had a cargo of fever-medicine bound for the quarry towns, and a buyer in Ellsmere offered me triple to reroute it to a wine merchant who'd lost a shipment. Triple, Wren. I had debts. I took the wine money and the medicine sat in my hold for six weeks, and when I finally ran it, eleven people in the quarry towns were dead. Children among them." He stared into the coals. "I've robbed, lied, sunk a customs boat with men aboard. None of it sits like those eleven. Because the robbery and the sinking were *against* people, and that I could understand. This was just... arithmetic. I did sums, and people died of my sums."

"And that's why you testified," Wren said quietly. "Against the Guild. Against me."

"That's why I told myself I testified. Crown's men said the Guild was a fever too, and I believed them, because believing them meant my sums had been right all along." He turned to look at Wren then, really look, firelight and shadow. "They weren't. I know that now. Took me ten years and a burned guildhouse to know it, but I know it."

Wren was silent a long moment. Then: "Bram used to say the Guild's first law wasn't secrecy. It was *proportion*. Never take more than the thing is worth. Never pay more than the thing costs. He said most evil in the world is just bad accounting."

"Bram," Corvo said, "sounds like he'd have hated me."

"Bram forgave you before he died. He told me to bring you if I could spare you." A ghost of a smile. "He said you were 'a scoundrel with an honest spine,' and that those were rarer than honest men and more useful."

Corvo laughed, then coughed, then laughed again anyway, because what else was there. "Old man had good taste in insults."

"Wren." Isolde's voice, low, from the edge of the camp. She was standing rigid, head cocked toward the south. "Firelight. Two points, maybe three miles. Moving parallel to us."

The warmth went out of the night all at once. Corvo pushed himself upright, hand pressed to his side, and followed Isolde's gaze to where, between the black trunks of the pines, a faint orange smudge crawled along the darkness like a slow spark along a fuse.

"Hunters," Isolde said. "King's remaining hunters. They've been paralleling us since yesterday. Keeping pace. Herding."

"They're not attacking," Wren murmured. "Why aren't they attacking?"

"Because," Corvo said, and his voice came out flat and cold as the ground, "they don't have to. Look at the terrain ahead—that ridge line funnels into the valley. There's one pass through the high country to the Hollowgate, and everyone born within fifty miles of these wilds knows it. They're not chasing us, Wren. They're *escorting* us. To a place of their choosing."

Isolde swore softly. Pip stirred in his blankets and did not wake.

"There's a waystation," Corvo continued, "half a day short of the pass. Old shepherd's station, stone walls, one door. If we make it there, we can hold it. If we're caught in the open—" he didn't finish. Nobody needed him to.

"Then we move tonight," Isolde said. "Cold march, moonless. We gain the waystation before they expect us to."

"We move tonight," Wren agreed.

Corvo looked at the firelight crawling in the distance, then down at his own hands, steady as a surgeon's, and made his own private arithmetic. A night march. A stone waystation. A pass beyond it, and hunters behind, and somewhere ahead a gate that wanted a keeper's blood—he'd read enough of Wren's silences by now to smell it, the same way you smelled a hidden cargo hold.

He had perhaps two days of walking in him. Maybe three, if he spent himself entirely.

Good, he thought. That was nearly exactly enough.

* * *

They marched through the frozen dark, and Corvo taught his last lesson walking.

Wren fell into step beside him an hour out, when the moonless black was total and they moved by Pip's whispered word alone—the boy walking at the front, hand trailing along the invisible seams of the world, saying *left here, the snow lies differently, trust me*—and for a long time they simply walked, boots crunching, breath smoking, four lives moving through a country that did not care whether they lived or died.

"You knew," Wren said at last. "At the fire. About the pass. How?"

"Ran this country for fifteen years. Smugglers don't use roads, we use *options*, and I've memorized every option between here and the coast." He paused, breath ragged. "There's one thing I never told you about options, though. Want to hear it?"

"Always."

"An option's only worth what you're willing to spend to keep it open." He glanced sideways at Wren, a dark shape against darker snow. "You've got a look about you, Wren Galloway. I've seen it on men about to burn their own ships. So I'm going to say this once, plain, no trade-craft, no cleverness: whatever price you think that gate is asking—you don't pay it alone. That's not sentiment. That's *tactics*. A sacrifice spent alone buys nothing. Spent together, it buys everything. You want to reforge a covenant between two worlds? Then learn the first law of my trade, the one I learned too late and eleven graves too late: nothing of value was ever purchased solo."

Wren stopped walking.

Ahead, Isolde halted too, hand going to her sword out of habit; Pip turned, sensing the change in the air the way he sensed doors. For a moment the five of them—for Corvo counted himself among them now, counted himself proudly, counted himself as a man counts coins he means to spend well—stood in the vast white silence, and the wind moved through the pines like a congregation shifting in its seats.

"The waystation," Isolde said quietly, pointing. "There. Smoke from the chimney."

A thread of woodsmoke rose against the stars, thin and gray and terribly human. Shelter. Warmth. An hour's rest before the pass.

And Corvo, watching that smoke, felt the fishhook scar pull tight beneath his ribs like a finger curling shut, and understood—with the strange clarity that comes to men near the end of a ledger—that the shepherd who lived at that waystation would have seen the hunter-fires too, and would be weighing, right now, in the privacy of his own poor soul, what a king's bounty was worth against the lives of strangers.

He'd made that kind of sum himself, once. He knew exactly how heavy the coin felt.

"Come on," Corvo said, and started walking toward the smoke. "Let's go meet our host. And Wren—" he didn't turn around, because he couldn't have said the next part to their face, "—whatever happens at that gate, remember what I told the boy. The world's a better merchant than any of us. Trust it to take only what matters."

Behind them, far to the south, the hunter-fires had stopped moving.

They were waiting for the waystation too.
