## 16. Betrayal at the Waystation

Pip knew the shepherd was going to sell them before the man did.

That was the thing about doors and the people behind them—they told him things whether they meant to or not. The shepherd, Hobb, had a face like a door with a bad latch: it wanted to hold shut, but the hinges were loose, and behind it Pip could hear the rattle of a thought turning over and over. *The bounty. The bounty. Two hundred crowns for the gatekeeper, fifty for the rest.* Pip had watched the thought rattle all through supper, through the barley stew and the flat ale, through Hobb's wife ladling seconds into Corvo's bowl and calling him *poor lamb* for the way he coughed.

So when Hobb slipped out of the waystation after midnight, boots in hand, door held so carefully on its hinges that it whispered rather than creaked, Pip was already awake. Already dressed. Already sitting up in the loft with his knees pulled to his chest, watching through the gap between the boards.

He should have woken the others. He knew that. But there was a smaller door inside Pip, one he didn't like to name, and behind it lived the fear that if he told them—if he said *the shepherd's gone for the soldiers, they never wanted us, nobody wants us*—then it would become true in a way it hadn't been yet. So long as he said nothing, they were still a family eating stew by a fire. The moment he spoke, they would be fugitives again.

He lay back down and stared at the rafters and listened to the snow, and hated himself in the quiet, patient way he was learning to hate himself, the way you learn it on the road, where there is no time to do it loudly.

* * *

The waystation sat in a fold of the Whitefell passes, a stone shepherd's house with a byre and a low wall, one road in and one road out—the out running up through a pass the locals called the Shepherd's Throat, a narrow defile where the rock leaned close enough on both sides to touch with outstretched arms. Isolde had chosen it for exactly that reason. *One road in, one road out,* she'd said, *and a throat is easy to choke.* Corvo had laughed his dry, rusted laugh and said that in his experience throats were usually choked from the inside.

Pip woke them an hour before dawn, and by then it was too late for anything but arithmetic.

He told them what he'd seen. Isolde was armored and armed before he finished the second sentence, moving with that terrible quiet efficiency she had, buckling straps without looking at them. Wren stood very still by the cold hearth, the covenant-page's oilcloth wrapping crinkling faintly in their clenched fist, and Pip watched them do the thing Wren did—go somewhere inside themselves, behind a door Pip couldn't open, and come back with a plan and no fear showing.

"Up the Throat, then," Wren said. "Before light. If Hobb's sold us, the soldiers will come from the valley road, and the pass is our only—"

"They won't come up the valley," Corvo said.

He was sitting on the edge of his bunk, and he had not reached for his boots. Pip noticed that first—the boots—and then noticed the rest: the gray in Corvo's lips, the way his hand pressed flat against his side, low on the left, over the old wound he never talked about and never stopped favoring. There was a dark stain spreading between his fingers, slow as ink through paper.

"Corvo." Wren crossed the room in two strides.

"Ah, don't make the face." Corvo waved them off with his free hand. "It's been opening and closing for a month. You've all seen me favor it. Smuggler's trick—never let them see the ledger's bleeding." He tried to grin and got most of the way there. "The wound's from the war. From *before* the war, if we're honest. It's not Hobb's fault and it's not yours. It's just arithmetic."

"What do you mean, they won't come up the valley?" Isolde had gone very still. She was the only one of them, Pip thought, who understood Corvo completely, because they were made of the same iron in different shapes.

"Because I know how I'd do it," Corvo said. "If I were the king's captain and a shepherd sold me five fugitives in a pass, I wouldn't waste men climbing in the dark. I'd seal the Throat. I'd put forty men at the top and torches on the ridgelines, and I'd let the mountain do the work." He looked at Wren, and his voice went gentle in the way that Pip had learned to dread, because Corvo was only ever gentle when the truth was heavy. "You can't go up. And you can't go back down the valley, because that's where they'll come from when the light's good. Which leaves—"

"Here," Pip said.

Everyone looked at him. Pip's gift had been quiet all night, the way it went quiet around places that were honest—Hobb's waystation had good doors, well-hung, no lies in them—but now it woke up, and he felt the house all around him the way you feel a held breath. The byre door. The root cellar hatch. The little window in the loft that had swollen in its frame thirty years ago and never been opened since. The hearth's back flue, wide enough for a child, maybe, or a very determined cat.

"Here," Pip said again, and his voice cracked, and he hated that. "There's a way. There's always a way. That's the whole—that's what I *am.*"

Wren crouched in front of him. In the dark, with the first gray hint of dawn touching the frost on the window, Wren's face was all shadows and one bright line of eye, and Pip thought, not for the first time, that Wren Galloway looked the way doors sounded when they were about to open onto something enormous.

"Not this time, little locksmith," Wren said softly.

* * *

The soldiers came at first light, and they did not come quietly.

There were perhaps thirty of them, in the king's black and ash-gray, and they came up the valley road at a walk, unhurried, the way men move when they are certain. Pip watched from the loft window with Corvo's spyglass, and beside him Isolde counted under her breath the way she did, and below them Corvo was putting on his boots after all, which was the worst thing Pip had seen in a year of bad things.

"Thirty," Isolde reported. "Crossbows. They'll form a line at the wall and demand surrender. Vane's not with them—no white cloak. This is a bounty crew, not the Inquisition. Bounty men take prisoners when the price is right, and corpses when it's not."

"Two hundred crowns," Corvo said, buckling his belt. He'd put on the good coat, the one with the deep pockets, and Pip saw him loading those pockets in a particular order—flask, flint, the little hooked knife, the coil of thin cord. Pip knew that order. It was the order of a man arranging his affairs.

"Corvo." Wren's voice was low and very controlled. "We go out the back, through the byre, down the scree on the north side. Isolde and I can hold them at the wall long enough—"

"No." Corvo said it without heat, the way he said everything, and it landed harder for that. "The scree's a mile of open rock and you'd be silhouetted against the snow by the second step. And Isolde holding thirty crossbows at a stone wall is a eulogy with extra steps." He turned, and Pip saw that he had to brace a hand on the bunk to do it, and that his knuckles were white, and that the stain on his side had spread to the waistband of his trousers, and Pip's chest went tight and small. "There's one road out of this station that thirty men can't watch, because it's not a road. It's a *story.*"

"What story?" Isolde asked.

"The one where the old smuggler walks up the valley road with his hands up and his coat full of lies, and every eye in the king's crew is on him, because Corvo Ashe is worth fifty crowns and a story to tell in the taverns, and men are men." He smiled at them, and it was the real smile, the one he saved for the hold at night when Pip was supposed to be asleep, the one that made his whole ruined face into something young. "And while they're all looking at me, the rest of you go up the Throat. Slow, quiet, hugging the east wall where the shadow lies. You'll be through the narrows before they realize their prisoner is a pocketful of fire and cord."

"No," Wren said.

"Yes," said Corvo.

"No." Wren's voice broke on it, just slightly, a hairline crack in stone. "Corvo, we go together or—"

"You go together," Corvo said. "That's the entire point of everything I've ever done that was worth doing. I spent thirty years moving goods for people who paid. This is the first cargo I've ever carried that I loved." He looked around at all of them—at Isolde, at Wren, and last and longest at Pip—and Pip felt his eyes go hot and his throat close. "You're the cargo. Get the cargo out."

Pip did the thing he had never done. He ran to Corvo and put his arms around him and held on, and Corvo's hand came down on his hair, careful, the way you touch something you're afraid of breaking, or something that's already broken you.

"Hey," Corvo said into his hair. "Little locksmith. Listen. You know what a smuggler's debt is? It's not coin. It's this. You spend your whole life owing, and then one morning you find the thing that pays it all, and it's four people in a shepherd's house eating bad stew, and you'd burn every ledger in the world to keep them." His voice was steady. His hand was shaking. "You opened every door for us. Let me open this one."

* * *

They went up the Throat while Corvo walked down the valley road.

Pip looked back eleven times. Isolde made him stop at eight, her hand on his shoulder, iron-gentle. But he counted the last three anyway, because he was Pip, and counting was how he kept the world from coming apart.

The first look: Corvo small on the white road, walking with his hands raised, his gait deliberately crooked so he'd look wounded and harmless, and the line of black-and-gray soldiers turning toward him like iron filings toward a lodestone.

The second look: Corvo among them, talking, his hands moving in the storyteller's gestures, and a ripple of something—laughter?—going through the soldiers, because Corvo Ashe could make a hangman laugh, had done it twice that Pip knew of.

The third look: Corvo turning, as if to point out the waystation, and his eyes finding the dark slit of the Throat's mouth across the valley, finding Pip in it, Pip was sure of it, across all that distance, and lifting one hand in a little wave. *Go on, then. Cargo.*

Then the Throat's walls closed in and took the valley away, and there was only rock and snow and the sound of their own breathing and Isolde's voice counting paces, and Wren ahead of them with the covenant-page against their chest, climbing for all of them, climbing the way people climb when they are carrying something heavier than paper.

It was almost noon when the sound came.

Pip felt it before he heard it—through the soles of his boots, through the stone, the way he felt every door in a house when one of them was kicked. A *whump*, muffled by distance and rock, and then a bloom of heat in his gift-sense, sudden and enormous and then guttering, like a lamp dropped down a well.

He knew. He knew the way he always knew. He sat down in the snow in the middle of the pass and the sound arrived properly then, rolling and echoing, and Isolde caught him before he fell sideways, and Wren stood very still with their face turned back down the pass they had climbed, and nobody said anything, because there was nothing to say that the mountain hadn't already said.

The fire had been in the coat. The flask, the flint, the cord. A pocketful of fire, exactly as promised. Thirty soldiers would live to tell a story about a smiling smuggler who burned like a bonfire and took four of them off the mountain with him, and the story would be wrong in every particular that mattered, and it would be the only monument Corvo Ashe would ever get, because there would be no body to bury—there was barely anything left to bury—and no ledger with his name in it that anyone would keep.

Pip cried until there was nothing in him, and then Isolde held him while the wind came down the pass, and Wren stood apart with snow gathering on their shoulders and did not move for a long, long time. When Wren finally turned around, their face was the door with the bad latch, and behind it Pip could hear something rattling that would not be quieted, and he understood that Wren had added Corvo to the weight they carried, the weight that was already Bram and the burned guildhouse and a decade of sealed doors, and that the weight was getting heavy enough to bend a person double.

* * *

They came down out of the mountains three days later, into a valley where the snow was thinner and the roads were honest mud, and none of them had said more than a hundred words since the pass. Grief had made them quiet the way deep water is quiet.

It was Pip who broke first, because Pip was the one who felt things through walls.

They were walking a rutted track between bare hedgerows when he stopped, head cocking, and held up a hand. "Someone's coming," he whispered. "Fast. One person. On foot. And—" He frowned, because the feeling was strange, familiar in a way it had no right to be. "The door they're carrying inside them is one of ours."

Isolde's sword was out in a breath. Wren put a hand on her wrist. Down the track, around the bend, a figure came running—white cloak torn to rags, dark hair loose, a satchel bouncing against her hip, and in her hand a folded paper that she held the way Wren held the covenant-page, like a thing that could save the world or end it.

Serah Vane skidded to a halt ten paces away, chest heaving, frost on her lashes, and looked at the three of them—at the knight, at the boy, at the gatekeeper—and Pip felt the truth of her before she spoke, felt it through the ruined door she'd been carrying her whole life: *deserter, thief of the king's rite, hunter of the gift who had discovered she was the gift.*

"They're coming," Serah Vane said. "The whole host. And Aldric means to open the gate himself."

She held out the stolen fragment of the rite with a hand that was not quite steady.

"And I need to speak with whoever Corvo Ashe trusted—because I know what the covenant costs, and you have to hear it before midwinter."

Pip looked at Wren. Wren had gone very still, and behind the door of Wren's face, something Pip had never heard before went silent—the rattling stopped, all at once, like a house the moment before it falls.
