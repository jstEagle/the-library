## 17. The Price on the Page

Serah Vane had read a thousand confessions in her years as an inquisitor. She had learned to read them the way a butcher reads a carcass—by weight, by the give of the flesh, by where the knife should go. Confessions were meat. You carved out what fed you and discarded the rest.

The page in her hands was not a confession. It was older than confession, older than the crown that had taught her to carve, and it did not want to be read so much as *endured*.

She sat in the shepherd's abandoned hut with the storm worrying at the shutters like a dog at a bone, and she read by the light of a tallow stub while the others slept—or pretended to—in the loft above. The complete rite. Both halves at last: the fragment she had stolen from King Aldric's own strongbox folded against the covenant-page Wren Galloway carried like a second heartbeat. Two pieces of vellum, laid edge to edge on the scarred table, completing each other the way a blade completes its hilt.

She read it once for meaning. She read it twice because she could not believe what she had understood the first time.

She read it a third time because there was nothing else in the world left to do.

*The covenant is renewed by a keeper's freely given name,* the rite said, in script that seemed to shift when she blinked, letters crawling like insects disturbed under a stone. *And the keeper who gives their name gives all that the name holds: face, memory, love, and self. They pass beyond every ledger. No hand shall write them. No tongue shall speak them. No heart shall hold them. The gate drinks the keeper whole, and the balance stands.*

Serah set the page down very carefully, as though it might go off.

"That's not—" she began aloud, and stopped, because the hut was empty except for her and the wind. "That's not a sacrifice," she said anyway, to the tallow flame, to the dark, to whatever part of herself still believed the law and the truth were the same animal. "That's annihilation. That's worse than death."

Death left a body. Death left a grave, a name on a stone, a grief with edges you could hold. This left *nothing*. A shape of absence. People would stand around a table in ten years' time feeling grateful toward someone they could not name, and they would never know why their hands shook when they passed a certain door.

She thought of Bram. Old Keeper Bram, whom she had watched burn in the guildhouse yard, whose eyes had found hers through the smoke—not with hatred, which she could have borne, but with *recognition*, as if he had seen the gift stirring in her before she'd admitted it to herself. He had mouthed something as the flames took him. She had told herself it was a curse. She had needed it to be a curse.

Now, reading the rite's final lines—*the keeper's blood opens the way; the keeper's name closes it; thus the door swings both ways, and only one may pay*—she wondered if what he had mouthed had been *her name*. Not a curse. An instruction. A passing of the burden to the one person in the king's service who might yet be worth the weight of it.

The candle guttered. Above her, the loft boards creaked—the particular creak of someone trying not to be heard, which meant Pip, or meant Isolde, or meant Wren Galloway lying awake doing arithmetic with their own life.

Serah gathered both pages, folded them into her breast pocket, and climbed the ladder.

* * *

They were all awake. Of course they were. In the two days since Corvo Ashe had died holding the pass at the waystation—died grinning, gods rot him, died making a joke about tolls—none of them had slept properly, and they had all become the kind of people who woke when another person breathed differently.

Wren sat by the small fire they permitted themselves, turning Bram's iron key over and over in their fingers. Isolde stood at the window slit with her sword across her forearms, not guarding so much as holding something familiar. Pip was curled against the knight's side, awake, watching Serah climb into the loft with the flat, unblinking look the boy gave to doors and locks and other things that kept secrets.

"You've read it," Wren said. Not a question. They had seen her face change, and Wren Galloway had always been able to read her better than she liked—which was, she supposed now, the whole trouble between them.

"I've read it." Serah came to the fire and crouched, holding her hands to the heat she didn't deserve. "Tell me what you think it says."

"The covenant renews at midnight on midwinter night. A keeper's blood opens the gate. A freely offered name seals it." Wren's voice was level. Too level. The voice of someone reciting lines rehearsed in front of a mirror until the cracks were sanded out. "That's all."

"That is *not all*." Serah pulled the pages from her pocket and held them up. "I've read your half and the king's fragment together, Galloway. I know what you're planning, because it's written down in ink older than the crown, and you've known since the shrine, haven't you? Since the gate showed you the keepers who came before. How many of them do you remember? How many names?"

Silence. The fire popped. Isolde turned slowly from the window, and her face had gone to granite.

"Say it plainly," Isolde said.

"It takes everything." Serah heard her own voice crack and let it. Let them hear the inquisitor break; she had been breaking for weeks and was done pretending otherwise. "Not just the life. The *name*. Face, memory, everyone who ever loved them. Wren walks through that gate at midnight and stops existing in any mind in either world. We will stand on that causeway tomorrow and watch our friend die more thoroughly than if we burned them—and then we will walk away not knowing who we lost." She looked at Wren directly. "You were going to let us. You were going to stand there tomorrow and say goodbye without saying goodbye, and let us carry you home as a blank page."

Pip made a small sound. Not words. The boy spoke to doors, not people, but this sound needed no translation.

Wren set the iron key down on the hearthstone with exaggerated care, the way a person does when they are afraid of what their hands will do otherwise. "It has to be a keeper," they said quietly. "My blood opens it. My blood is the hinge. There isn't a version of tomorrow where I don't pay, Serah. There's only a version where I choose how. And I choose the one where none of you have to grieve."

"That's not mercy," Serah said. "That's theft."

"Excuse me?"

"You're stealing our grief. You've decided we don't get to mourn you, don't get to bury you, don't get to hate you a little for leaving—that you'll spare us the *inconvenience* of loss by erasing the fact of it." She was on her feet now, and she didn't remember standing. Ten years of hunting people like Wren Galloway, and here was the truth she'd never spoken in any interrogation chamber: she had always envied them. People with a gift, with a purpose, with a place in a story. And this one wanted to opt out of being remembered. "Do you know what I did when I learned what the crown was really doing? What Aldric is? I committed treason. I stole from my king and deserted my post, and I did it because I finally understood that some laws are just hunger wearing a uniform. And you—you've got a law of your own, don't you? *The keeper pays alone.* Who wrote that law, Wren? Was it the gate? Or was it just you, alone in the dark for ten years, deciding you were the only one allowed to be hurt?"

Wren flinched. Actually flinched, like a struck thing.

Isolde crossed the loft in three strides and took the pages from Serah's hand—gently, which was somehow more frightening than if she'd snatched them. She read. Her lips moved. Pip crept close and read over her arm, and the boy's face did something terrible: he understood faster than the adults, children always did, and his eyes went huge and wet and fixed on Wren.

"No," Pip said. "No, no. You can't. You're the one who knows my name. Nobody else asks my name. Everybody else just calls me *the door boy*."

"Pip—" Wren started.

"*No.*" The boy's voice cracked across the loft like a whip, and somewhere below them, Serah would swear, the hut's old latch rattled in sympathy. "You said—on the cart, you said names matter, that's the whole point of the ledger, that's the whole point of *everything*. You can't write yourself out. That's not fair. That's not *fair* and I'll—I'll lock the gate. I'll lock it and swallow the key. I talk to doors, Wren. Doors tell me things. I'll ask this one never to open for you."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Isolde set the pages down on the hearth beside the iron key, and knelt—Dame Isolde Ferrant, stripped of rank, breaker of her last oath, kneeling in a shepherd's loft so she could look Wren Galloway in the eye at the same height.

"When they took my spurs," she said, "it was because I refused to ride with the hunters to a village where they'd caged a girl of nine. I told the marshal that some orders are sins, and he told me that oaths are oaths. Do you know what I learned in the year since?" Her voice was low and even and terrible. "Oaths made to unjust men are just chains with pretty links. And you, Wren, have sworn an oath to a law older and crueler than the crown—the law that says the strong pay so the rest needn't count the cost. I broke one oath. I'll break yours too."

"You can't rewrite the covenant," Wren whispered. "It's not ours. It's older than—"

"Everything is ours," Isolde said. "That's what they never understand, kings and gates and old dead keepers writing prices into vellum. Everything built on people belongs to the people it's built on. If the covenant demands a name, then the covenant is a door, and doors—" she glanced at Pip, and something almost like a smile touched her ruined face, "—doors can be renegotiated."

Serah sank back down beside the fire. The anger was still in her, hot and bright, but underneath it now she could feel the other thing, the thing anger had been protecting all along, the way armor protects the body it damns to carry. Love. Ugly, inconvenient, furious love for these people she had hunted and betrayed and followed into the frozen dark.

"Wren," she said, quieter. "Look at me. All those keepers before you—the gate showed you. Did any of them have this?" She gestured around the loft: the knight, the boy, the ashes of a smuggler's debt paid in blood, the stolen pages, the traitor inquisitor. "Did any of them walk up to that gate *accompanied*?"

Wren's jaw worked. "...No. They went alone. Every one of them. Alone and unnamed and—"

"And they assumed the price was the price because no one had ever stood next to them and said *no*." Serah leaned forward. "I spent my career believing the law was justice because I'd never seen justice anywhere else to compare it to. Then I saw four people in a burned guildhouse cellar sharing half a loaf, and I understood: justice isn't what's written. Justice is what people refuse to accept. So we refuse. Tomorrow night, we go to the Hollowgate—all of us—and we don't pay the written price. We offer a new one. Let the gate take a trade no ledger anticipated. Let it take—" she faltered, because she hadn't finished thinking it, and said it anyway, "—let it take something shared. A piece from each of us. Enough to hurt. Not enough to erase. If the covenant runs on a keeper's freely given name, then fine—but a name is held by more than one mouth. Ask it for *ours* too. Make the balance balance on all of us."

"That could kill us all," Wren said hoarsely. "Or unseal both realms. Or—"

"Or it could work," Pip said fiercely, wiping his nose on his sleeve, "because doors *like* honesty. Doors hate liars. The old price is a lie—it says one person's enough, and it isn't, it never was, that's why the wards hummed wrong for ten years." The boy looked around at all of them. "The gate's been lonely. I'm telling you. Doors get lonely."

The wind screamed against the shutters. Somewhere far off, too far to be real and too near to be ignored, a horn sounded—the king's host, coming down out of the hills, hunting the gate and everyone bound to it. Dawn was hours off. Midnight was a day away.

Wren Galloway looked at the iron key. At the pages. At the three faces arranged around the fire like a verdict.

"If we fail," they said at last, "the Otherworld bleeds out, the gift dies in the land, and everything Bram burned for burns with it."

"Then we won't fail," Isolde said, rising, buckling her sword on with the ease of long habit. "We'll simply be the first company in history to die arguing with a door. I've been excommunicated, outlawed, and hunted; I find I've lost my fear of institutions."

Serah laughed—a short, broken sound, the first laugh she'd let out since Ellsmere. Wren stared at the three of them, and Serah watched the last of the resistance go out of them like a tide withdrawing, replaced by something rawer and more frightened and more alive.

"You're all fools," Wren said. Their voice shook. "You know that. Absolute fools."

"Yes," said Pip, and burrowed against Wren's side, and did not let go.

Outside, the horn sounded again, closer. Serah rose and went to the window slit beside Isolde, and together they watched the far ridge, where torchlight was beginning to crawl like a lit fuse toward the valley of the Hollowgate.

"They'll reach the outer shrine by dusk tomorrow," Isolde murmured. "Aldric means to open the gate himself, with or without us."

"Then we'd best teach him," Serah said, "what happens to men who try to collect a debt that's already been renegotiated."

Behind them, by the fire, Wren drew the covenant-pages close and, for the first time since the shrine, began—to Pip's dictation, to all their voices—to write in the margin.
