## 18. Storm Over the Hollowgate

The snow came down like ash.

Wren felt it first as a change in the wards—a tremor in the old iron key at their throat, a taste of copper on the wind. Then the horizon lit. Not dawn; midwinter night had hours yet to run. Torchlight. Hundreds of torches, strung along the mountain road like a burning rosary, and beneath them the drums of the king's host, a heartbeat too slow to be human.

"They're here," Isolde said. She stood at the edge of the causeway with her sword already drawn, snow gathering in the hollows of her pauldrons. She did not look afraid. Wren had learned that about her: fear lived in Dame Isolde Ferrant, but it had long ago been made to kneel.

"Then we begin now." Wren pulled the covenant page from its oilcloth wrapping. In the storm-light it seemed less paper than skin, the ink moving faintly, like veins beneath flesh. "Pip—"

"I know." The boy was already at the inner door, palms flat against the stone. His gift hummed through him visibly, a shimmer in the air around his small hands, and the door—the Hollowgate itself, vast and blind and older than any kingdom—leaned toward him like a dog hearing its name. "It's listening. It's *so* big, Wren. It's been waiting so long."

"Open it when I call for you. Not before."

Pip nodded, solemn as a priest. Behind him, huddled in the lee of the shrine wall, the guild apprentices clutched one another, their faces pale ovals in the dark. Serah Vane stood apart from all of them, torchlight from below catching the silver of her inquisitor's badge—she had not yet taken it off. Wren still could not look at her without seeing Bram's blood on the stones of Ellsmere. But she was here. That had to be enough for tonight.

The drums grew louder.

* * *

Isolde counted the torches out of habit, the way she'd once counted lances before a charge. Two hundred. Perhaps two hundred fifty. Against one knight with a sword, a causeway barely wide enough for three men abreast, and a wind that wanted to throw them all into the gorge.

Good odds, by her old reckoning.

She planted herself at the causeway's narrowest point, where the mountain's shoulder pinched the road against a drop of ninety feet into black water. The snow came sideways now, stinging. Below her, the torches resolved into faces—young men mostly, conscripts wrapped in the king's grey, driven up this frozen road by officers who rode behind them with drawn crossbows. She knew that formation. She had commanded it, once, before the day she'd been ordered to burn a cage full of children and had instead broken her oath, her rank, and her life into pieces.

"Hold!" The shout came from the front rank—an officer on a barded horse, visor up, face blue with cold. "In the name of King Aldric, stand aside! The witch and her brood are under sentence!"

"The sentence is unlawful," Isolde called back, and her voice carried the way voices carry over water, flat and enormous. "I am Dame Isolde Ferrant, and I say so."

A ripple went through the ranks. Names still meant something, even now. Even stripped.

"You're a traitor," the officer said, but softer.

"I'm a knight," she said. "There's a difference. You're welcome to learn it."

The crossbows came up. Isolde settled her weight, breath steaming, and thought—not of heaven, not of honor, but of Pip's laugh, of Corvo's crooked grin the last time she'd seen him alive, of Wren's hands steady on the covenant page. Things worth being a wall for.

The first bolt glanced off her raised shield. The second found the gap at her knee and she grunted, staggered, held. They came on then, the front rank forced forward by the points behind them, and Isolde met them the way winter meets a river—not with fury but with patience, turning their own crush against them, letting the narrow stone do half her killing. A man fell screaming into the gorge. Another dropped his spear and simply sat down in the snow, weeping, done.

She took a blade across the forearm. She took a boot to the chest that cracked something inside her and made the world go white at the edges. She held.

And through the ringing chaos she heard, rising from the shrine behind her, the first note of the rite—Wren's voice, thin and clear, speaking words older than the crown—and she understood that every second she bought was being spent somewhere that mattered.

*Hold*, she told herself, spitting blood into the storm. *You are the door, and doors do not move.*

* * *

Serah Vane walked into the fire of her own hunters and turned it aside.

She had left the king's host an hour before it reached the mountain, slipping away in the whiteout while Aldric's captains argued over maps, carrying the stolen rite-fragment against her heart like a coal. Now she moved down the flank of the column unseen, hood up, gift coiled tight beneath her skin where no witch-finder's charm could taste it. Her own charm. She had forged it herself, years ago, to hide from men exactly like the man she had been.

The rear guard never saw her coming. She put three of them to sleep with a touch and a whispered word—the gift used gently, the way Bram might have used it—and took their signal lantern. With it she walked openly among the siege company massed below the causeway, past the fire-throwers crouched behind their wicker shields, and no one looked twice at the tall figure in inquisitor's grey bearing the king's own light.

"Ready on my mark," the fire-captain was shouting. "On the knight's fall—we burn the shrine and everything in it!"

Serah reached him, touched his shoulder, and let the gift pour through her like cold water. "Stand down," she said, and her voice was the voice of his own conscience, of every doubt he'd ever swallowed. "Look at what you're aiming at. Children. Look."

He looked. Whatever he saw there, he lowered the taper from the pitch-pot, and his hands were shaking, and he did not ask why.

But the officer on the causeway was made of sterner stuff, or more frightened stuff, and when Isolde's shield finally split along its grain he screamed the order anyway: "Fire the shrine! Burn the witch's work!"

The pitch-pots went up in a chain of orange blooms—and the flames rose, curled, and bent backward, streaming away uphill like banners in a gale, guttering out against the snow. Serah stood in the middle of the fire-line with both arms raised and her hood blown back, her gift blazing off her in visible ribbons, and every man on that slope could see what she was.

"Witch!" someone shrieked.

"Yes," Serah Vane said, and her voice broke across the whole hillside like a bell. "Yes. And so were the ones you burned in Ellsmere. And so is the child in your cages. And your king knows it, because your king *feeds on us*—" She flung her arm toward the distant glow of the royal pavilion, where the dying king drank his stolen years. "I am Serah Vane, First Inquisitor of the Gift-Hunt, and I am telling you what I have seen with my own eyes. The hunt was never for your protection. It was for his supper."

The line wavered. Men looked at each other. Somewhere a crossbow clattered to the stones, abandoned.

Serah turned and ran for the shrine, tears freezing on her face, knowing she had just burned her own name off every ledger in the realm—and finding, to her astonishment, that she felt lighter than she had in twenty years.

* * *

Inside the gate's threshold, Wren spoke the old words and felt the world begin to listen.

The covenant page floated above their open palms, ink swimming, and around it the air thickened until each breath was like drinking. The rite required three voices: the keeper to speak, the door to answer, and a witness to bind. Serah arrived bleeding snow and smoke just as Wren lifted their head, and there was no time to refuse her, no time for anything but the words.

"Witness," Wren said.

"I witness," Serah answered, and her voice shook only a little.

"Pip. Now."

The boy pressed both hands flat against the inner door and *pushed*—not with muscle, Wren saw, but with everything he was, every locked thing he'd ever opened, every door that had ever loved him. The Hollowgate groaned. Frost shattered from its arch in a wheel of glittering dust. And the door swung inward onto a darkness that was not empty but *attentive*, a darkness with weather in it, seasons, tides—the Otherworld leaning close after ten long years of famine, hungry and hopeful and terrible.

The covenant page began to burn without burning.

Wren read the final lines. Read the price. Felt it settle over them like a hand on the crown of the skull, gentle as Bram's had been, and did not falter, because Isolde was bleeding on the causeway and Corvo was dead in a shepherd's pass and these people had carried them this far on the promise of a lie they'd all agreed to believe.

*"A keeper's life,"* the old words said, *"for the realms' renewal. Freely given, freely taken."*

Behind them, Serah drew breath to argue. Wren spoke faster.

They gave their name.

Not their life—the gate was wiser than the page, or kinder, or simply hungrier for the thing keepers hoard most. Wren Galloway spoke their own name into the dark, syllable by syllable, and felt each one leave like a tooth drawn clean, and the darkness drank, and the covenant caught fire in earnest, gold flooding up through the gate-stones, through the mountain, through the roots of the world—

—and the last thing Wren knew, in the final instant before the erasure took them, was four voices screaming their name from four directions, trying to hold onto it, trying to give it back.

The storm stopped.

Snow hung motionless in the air, a thousand suspended stars. The gate stood sealed and whole, humming with balance, warm as a hearthstone. And in the sudden silence, a stranger stood swaying at the threshold—someone with kind eyes and no history, holding a scrap of blank vellum, looking at the four people around them with the expression of a person arriving at a house they have never seen and somehow recognizing every room.

Pip picked up a pen.

* * *

Outside, the king's host knelt in the unmoving snow as the light of the reforged covenant washed over them, and far away in his pavilion King Aldric cried out once, like a man whose chair has been pulled from beneath him, and did not cry out again.

Serah Vane walked down through the kneeling army with the truth in her mouth, ready to spend the rest of her life saying it aloud.

And at the gate, a boy named Pip held out a pen to a stranger, and said, with absolute certainty, the only word that mattered:

"You're expected."
