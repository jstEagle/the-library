## 19. A Name Given Freely

The Hollowgate did not look like a gate anymore.

It had not looked like a gate for ten years—not since Wren first stood before it as a girl of sixteen with Bram's hand heavy on her shoulder and the iron key cold in her fist. Then it had been a wound in the hillside: a doorway of black stone framed in carved arches, its threshold choked with ivy and old blood, humming like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Now, with the outer shrine cracked open and midwinter's moon pouring down through the torn clouds, it was worse than a ruin. It was a mouth.

Frost crawled across its lintel in patterns that were not frost-patterns—letters, perhaps, or the fossilized fingerprints of hands far larger than any human's. The air around the threshold bent, the way heat bends above summer roads, so that when Wren looked directly at the arch the world behind it seemed to ripple like water disturbed by a stone. And beneath everything, beneath the wind keening down from the crags, beneath the distant clangor of steel on the causeway below, Wren heard the sound she had been hearing wrong for weeks in her nightmares: the wards, no longer humming out of tune, but *screaming*—a thin, high note only gatekeeper blood could hear, rising toward some terrible crescendo.

"It knows we're here," Pip said beside her. His small voice carried strangely in the shrine's acoustics, neither echoing nor dying. "It's been waiting. Doors always know."

Wren knelt at the edge of the threshold and unwrapped the covenant page from the oilcloth where it had lived against her heart since Ellsmere burned. The vellum was warm. It should not have been warm—it had crossed frozen wilds in the dead of winter, had slept against her ribs through sleet and terror—but it lay against her palms like a living thing, like a bird with a racing heart, and the ink of the old covenant seemed to move when she wasn't looking directly at it, letters swimming like fish beneath ice.

Behind her, Isolde Ferrant stood at the broken shrine wall with her sword drawn and her breath steaming, watching the causeway. Below them, torchlight moved in serpentine lines—the king's host, hundreds strong, come up the mountain road with fire and faith and the dying King Aldric's desperate hunger. And somewhere among those torches, Serah Vane.

"Serah bought us until midnight," Isolde said. Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a woman who had spent the last three days holding a bridge of ice and stone against men she'd once called brothers. There was blood on her gauntlets that she had not bothered to wipe away. "Maybe less. They've stopped trying to take the causeway. They're burning it."

"Burning it?" Pip's head came up sharply. "You can't burn stone."

"You can burn the wood they braced it with," Isolde said. "And you can let the ice do the rest. When it goes, nothing crosses this valley but birds." She turned, and in the moonlight her face was drawn and gray with exhaustion, but her eyes were steady as they found Wren's. "So whatever you're going to do, Wren Galloway—do it now."

Wren rose. The page trembled in her hands.

She had read the complete rite three times since Serah brought them the stolen fragment, and each reading had stripped away another layer of mercy from the ancient words. The covenant between the human realm and the Otherworld was not a treaty, not truly. It was a hinge. And hinges were made to bear weight, and the weight the old powers had demanded, ten centuries ago, was a keeper's life—a life poured out at the threshold on midwinter night, freely given, so that the door could drink and be satisfied and sleep another age. Every keeper whose vision the gate had shown her stood in her memory now: the woman with river-gray eyes who smiled as she stepped into the light; the old man who sang; the child, gods forgive them all, the child who had walked forward without hesitation because no one had told her what the walking meant.

Bram had known. That was why he had never said why the wards hummed wrong. That was why he had chosen her—an orphan, unattached, someone the Guild could grieve without breaking. He had been preparing her for this since she was sixteen years old, and he had died with the kindness still on his lips rather than tell her the cost.

But Wren had decided something on the long march, in the dark, with Corvo's teachings warm in her memory and his absence an ache in her chest like a missing tooth: she would pay the price. She alone. She would not ask Isolde to add a keeper's death to the list of oaths she'd already shattered. She would not let Pip watch. She would send them down the goat path before midnight and stand alone at the threshold, and when the gate opened its mouth, she would walk into the light the way the river-eyed woman had walked, and the realms would balance, and her family would live.

That was the plan. It had been a good plan. She had held onto it through the storm and the ambush and the burning of the waystation, held it like a coal cupped in both hands.

Then Serah had read the final fragment aloud by firelight, and her voice had shaken, and Wren had understood that plans were just prayers made of pride—and the covenant, it turned out, was stranger and older than even Bram had guessed.

"The rite," Wren said now, aloud, because saying it made it real, and real things could be done. "I need the threshold. I need the key. And I need—" Her voice caught. She steadied it. "I need you two gone before I speak the words. What happens next isn't something you should see."

"No," said Isolde.

"No," said Pip, at exactly the same moment, with the identical stubbornness, and despite everything Wren almost laughed. Found family, Corvo had called them once, sideways, dying by inches on a frozen road. Family meant never letting each other die politely alone.

"Isolde—"

"I have buried enough people this year." The knight's voice cracked like a whip and then like something much softer underneath. "Corvo. My order. My name. I will not stand on this hill and listen to you die behind me while I guard your corpse from soldiers. If the price is a life, then we find another way or we all stand here and let the worlds crack together. But you don't get to spend yourself like a coin, Wren. Not tonight. Not after everything."

"The covenant doesn't bargain," Wren whispered. "The visions showed me. It takes what it takes."

"Then maybe," said Pip quietly, "you haven't read it right."

He stepped past her—small, quick, inevitable, the way he did everything—and laid his palm flat against the black stone of the arch. Wren started forward and Isolde's arm barred her, gently, immovably.

Pip closed his eyes. "Doors talk to me," he said. "Everyone knows that. Nobody asks what they say back." His brow furrowed, and his lips moved slightly, listening. The screaming note in Wren's skull shifted—warped—softened into something almost like speech. And when Pip looked up at her again, his eyes were wet and wide and certain.

"It doesn't want a life," he said. "It hasn't wanted a life for a hundred years. The keepers kept giving lives because the *page* says lives, because the people who wrote the page thought sacrifice was the same thing as love. But the gate is a door, Wren, and doors open both ways. It wants what every keeper gave it along with the dying. It wants the *giving*. Freely. Completely. All of it." He swallowed. "It wants a name."

The words fell into the shrine and rang there like a bell struck in a cathedral.

"A name," Isolde repeated.

"Names are how the Otherworld knows us. Names are the thread." Pip touched his own chest, where a boy with no family and no history had once been nothing but hunger and locked doors. "If a keeper gives their name—speaks it into the threshold, gives it away whole, every memory tied to it, every person who holds it—they pour themselves out just as fully as blood. The gate drinks. The hinge turns. The covenant reforges. But the keeper..." His voice broke, and he finished in a whisper. "The keeper walks out the other side empty. No one remembers them. No ledger holds them. Not the crown's, not ours. Not even theirs."

Silence, except the wind, except the distant roar of the burning causeway, except the thin singing of the gate.

Wren looked down at the covenant page in her hands—at the elegant, merciless script of powers who had measured devotion in loss—and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was worse than dying and better than dying and entirely, perfectly in keeping with everything the night had already taken. Corvo, who had traded in debts and payments all his crooked, generous life. Bram, who had kept secrets the way other men kept faith. The whole shape of her journey stood up suddenly and showed itself to her: every step had been teaching her this. Surrender. Not the sword-stroke kind. The other kind. The kind where you open your hand and let go.

"If I give my name," she said slowly, working it through, "the hunt ends anyway. A witch-hunter can't burn a witch no one remembers. The ledgers blank themselves. Even the king's stolen power—" She looked at Isolde. "Serah said Aldric is sustained by harvested gift. If the covenant reforges, the flow reverses. He falls. All of it works. Everything we came to do."

"And you vanish." Isolde's jaw was tight. "Into nothing. While we stand here and forget you ever—"

"Yes."

"I won't allow it."

"You don't get a vote," Wren said, and was surprised to find herself smiling, tired and fierce and free. "Isolde. Dame Isolde Ferrant, who broke her last oath to pull a child out of a cage. Who held a causeway against five hundred men with a borrowed sword. You taught me what it costs to keep a promise. Let me keep mine." She held up the page. "This is the promise. Ten years of sealed doors and hidden ledgers and lying low while neighbors burned—all of it so that someday, someone could stand at this threshold and say *enough*. This is the someday. Don't you dare make it smaller."

Below them, a section of the causeway went up in a gout of orange flame, and the sound rolled up the valley like thunder learning to speak. Shouts drifted up. Torches began to move faster, angrier. Somewhere in that host, Serah Vane was turning her hunters' fire aside, spending her hidden gift like a spendthrift, buying minutes she knew she could not replace.

Isolde stared at her for a long, long moment. Then she did something Wren had never seen her do: she sheathed her sword, crossed the shrine, and took Wren's face in her bloodied gauntlets, careful as a woman handling glass.

"I am going to forget you," she said. "I can feel it already, the way you feel a storm coming in a bad knee. And I want you to know—I will hate the hole you leave. I will hate it every day, and I will never know what I'm hating, and it will be the truest thing I own. Do you hear me? That is not permission. That is a complaint. File it however you like."

"Heard," Wren whispered. "Filed. Overruled."

Isolde laughed—a broken, wonderful sound—and pressed her forehead to Wren's for one heartbeat, two, and then stepped back and became a knight again, spine straight, facing the burning valley. "Then do it, Gatekeeper. Before I find my courage and stop you."

* * *

Wren walked to the threshold.

The key was in her pocket, Bram's iron key, worn smooth by a century of other hands. She did not need it—her blood was the older lock—but she held it anyway, because some inheritances deserved to be carried to the end.

Up close, the arch was beautiful in the way avalanches are beautiful. The black stone drank the moonlight and gave nothing back, and the carvings upon it resolved, the longer she looked, into faces: every keeper, every century, their expressions varying from terror to ecstasy to something beyond both. At the center of the arch, where a door's heart would beat, the air hung folded like a curtain of gray silk, and behind it—faint, fading, starving—she could see colors that did not exist in the human realm. Greens with no name. A blue like remembered music. The Otherworld, pressing close, hungry and hurt and waiting.

"Pip," she said. "The inner seal."

He was already moving, scrambling up the fallen shrine stones with the surefootedness of a boy raised on rooftops and locked things. He pressed both hands to the secondary ward—the one the Guild had layered over the gate a generation ago, the seal that had kept even the king's harvesters out—and spoke to it, low and coaxing, the way he spoke to doors. "It's okay," he murmured. "Nobody's going to hurt you. We're just opening. We're just letting the light back in." The ward shivered, glowed faintly gold, and unraveled like a knot slipping loose.

The gate's scream cut off.

In its place: silence so profound Wren heard her own heart, heard Isolde's breath, heard, impossibly, a sound like distant bells coming from the other side of the world. The folded gray curtain of air began, very slowly, to part.

"Wren." Isolde's voice, tight. "They've found the goat path. Ten minutes. Maybe less. Soldiers are coming up."

"Then I'll be quick."

She stepped over the threshold line—one foot, then the other, onto stone that hummed against her boot soles like a plucked string—and turned to face the two people she loved most in a world about to forget her. Pip stood at the base of the arch, tears freezing on his cheeks, making no effort to hide them. Isolde stood with her hand resting on her sword's pommel, standing guard over a ceremony instead of a bridge, which was, Wren thought, finally the right use for all that stubbornness.

"I had a speech," Wren said. "I've been writing it since Greywater. Something noble." She shook her head. "Forget the speech."

She held up the covenant page, and spoke the rite.

Her voice sounded strange in her own ears—layered, almost, as if something vast were speaking with her, using her mouth as a doorway too. The old words came easily, though she had never studied them, flowing up from wherever gatekeeper blood keeps its memories: words about balance and hinge and toll, about the debt of ages and the closing of accounts. The gate brightened with each phrase, the colors behind the veil deepening, sharpening, the starving Otherworld leaning in like a beggar at a feast. Frost blazed off the stones. The carved faces seemed to turn toward her.

And then came the moment the rite existed for. The hinge-moment. The offering.

Every instinct screamed at her to kneel, to bare her throat, to give the blood the page demanded. She felt the gate's expectation, old and patient, the weight of ten centuries of keepers who had knelt. The darkness at the center of the arch waited for it the way a noose waits.

Instead, Wren Galloway opened her arms, lifted her face to the light of the Otherworld, and spoke her own name.

"Wren."

Just the word. Just the word, but she gave it *everything*. She reached inside herself and found it—the thread that ran from her mother's voice saying it over a cradle, through Bram's gruff blessing at her choosing, through ten years of ledgers and sealed doors, through Isolde's shout across a frozen field and Pip's laugh in a smuggler's hold and Corvo's dying smile—took the whole length of it in both hands, and *poured*.

It did not hurt. That was the astonishing thing. It felt like the moment at the end of a long illness when fever breaks. It felt like setting down a load carried so long the shoulders had forgotten their shape. Memories went with it, cascading: her mother's kitchen, gone. Bram's supper table, gone. The first time the wards sang to her, gone. Ellsmere's cobbled streets, the smell of the guildhouse, the weight of the ledger, the taste of Corvo's terrible stew—gone, gone, gone, each one a bright coal dropped into the gate's open light, and the gate drank them the way the earth drinks rain, gratefully, completely, and *brightened*.

The colors behind the veil exploded outward. Light poured through the arch—not harsh, not burning, but green-gold and alive, flooding the ruined shrine, rolling down the mountainside like a tide, and Wren heard, distantly, screams below that were not screams of fear but of wonder, the king's host stopping mid-charge to stare at a hillside suddenly blooming with impossible spring in the dead of midwinter.

She was forgetting herself as the light took her. She could feel it happening, edges dissolving, and strangely there was no panic left in her for it. There was only a vast, quiet gratitude, and one final coherent thought, which she sent outward like a thrown rope toward the two figures silhouetted at the shrine's edge:

*Take care of each other. Remember to be happy. Whoever you were to me—thank you.*

The covenant flared white.

Somewhere in the blaze, a page turned itself blank. Somewhere in the blaze, a name dissolved into the balance of two worlds, becoming not nothing but everything—become the hinge, the toll, the paid debt, the reason the door would stay shut and the fields would stay green and no child would ever again be caged for the color of their gift.

The light faded.

The gate stood sealed—whole, silent, its carved faces at peace, the frost on its lintel melted into clean running water. Midwinter wind moved through the shrine carrying the scent, impossibly, of growing things.

And in the center of the threshold, kneeling on the ancient stone with no memory of how she had come to be there, a young woman with dark hair opened her eyes.

She looked at her hands. She looked at the sealed door, at the melting frost, at the stars wheeling overhead, and felt—nothing. No fear. No recognition. Only an emptiness shaped precisely like a question, and beneath the question, faint as a heartbeat, a warmth: the sense of having given something away, recently, gladly, to someone worth the giving.

Footsteps crunched on frost behind her. Two figures stood at the shrine's edge, staring—tall woman in scorched armor with tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face; small boy clutching an iron key he did not know why he was holding.

The woman opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"I—" Dame Isolde Ferrant said, and stopped, because the name she had been about to say was simply not there, a tooth-tongue searching a gap that had always, somehow, been there. Her hand trembled on her sword hilt. "Who—" She swallowed hard, and tried again, softer, lost, wondering: "Who are you?"

Below them in the valley, the king's army stood motionless in the strange green light, and on the burning causeway a lone figure in an inquisitor's coat sank slowly to her knees—and the young woman in the threshold looked at the two strangers who felt like home, and did not know why, and answered the only truth she had left.

"I don't remember," she said.
