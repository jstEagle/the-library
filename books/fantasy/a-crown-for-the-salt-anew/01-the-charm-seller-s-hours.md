## 1. The Charm Seller's Hours

The best hours for selling charms were the ones nobody else wanted.

Rafael Vasquez had learned that in her first year of trade, back when she'd been too green to know which alleys flooded and which constables could be paid in bottled laughter instead of coin. The honest hours—morning markets, midday squares—belonged to fishmongers and ribbon-sellers and men who shouted about the quality of their onions. But between midnight and the fourth bell, when the lamps burned low and the warded city of Corvel hummed its drowsy lullaby overhead, people came looking for her. People with things they wanted to set down for a while.

She kept her shop in a cellar off Tallow Lane, beneath a cobbler who complained about the smell of her inventory but never once asked what it was. The sign above the stair read VASQUEZ & DAUGHTER — CHARMS, KEEPSAKES, SMALL COMFORTS, which was a lie twice over: there was no daughter, and Rafael Vasquez was not the name she'd been born with. But lies, properly aged, became indistinguishable from truth, and this one had fifteen years of patina on it now. It gleamed like anything.

Tonight's first customer arrived at half past midnight, soaked to the knees from the canal fog and clutching a paper-wrapped parcel as though it might hatch.

"Mistress Vasquez?" A young man, maybe nineteen, with the particular desperation of someone who has rehearsed his speech on the walk over and is about to deliver it wrong anyway. "I'm told—I was told you buy things. That you can take a thing and—" He made a gesture like wringing out laundry. "Put it somewhere."

"Sit," Rafael said, and kicked a stool toward him with one boot heel. "Tea?"

"I don't have money for tea."

"The tea's free. It's everything after the tea that costs." She poured two cups from the dented pot she kept warm on a brazier of coals. Her cellar smelled of beeswax and cold iron and, faintly, of other people's summers—a scent no candlemaker could replicate, because no candlemaker had ever bottled a July afternoon whole. "Now. What are you carrying?"

He unwrapped the parcel with trembling fingers. Inside, nested in straw, sat a glass stopper the size of a plum, and inside *that* swirled something that wasn't quite light and wasn't quite smoke: a memory, freshly harvested, still warm.

"It's my first kiss," he said, going red to the ears. "From the girl at the pie stall on Cooper's Row. And I need it gone before Thursday."

Rafael raised an eyebrow. In fifteen years she had heard every reason a person might want to forget something—the griefs were terrible and the shames were strange, and sometimes both at once—but first kisses were usually the opposite trade. People came to her to *buy* those back.

"Thursday," she said. "What happens Thursday?"

"She's marrying someone else. Her family arranged it. And I—" He stared into his tea. "Every time I see her, I feel it again. All of it. Like it's happening right now, forever. I can't stand at a wedding and feel that. I'll ruin it. I'll ruin *her* day, and she deserves a good day, even if it isn't mine."

Well. That was a decent reason, as reasons went. Rafael turned the stopper in her fingers and watched the memory swirl—she could taste its edges without opening it, the way a baker knows bread by crust alone. Rosewater. Rain on canvas awnings. The specific terror-joy of being seventeen and touched on purpose for the first time. Good vintage. Someone had taught this boy well, or he'd been born with the knack; most people couldn't bottle their own memories without spilling half the flavor.

"Who showed you how to draw it out?" she asked.

"A woman in the Saltmarket. She said you'd pay fair."

"The woman in the Saltmarket says a lot of things." Rafael set the charm down between them. "Here's what I'll do. I'll give you eight silver for it—not because it's worth only eight, but because here's the trick of it: I'm not buying your kiss. I'm holding it. You come back in five years, if you want it back, and you pay me ten, and we call it even. Interest for my trouble."

His head came up. "I thought you—you *keep* them. Sell them to other people."

"Some sellers do that." Rafael let her voice go flat, the way it did when she was stating law rather than opinion. "I don't. Rule one of this establishment: I never sell a memory to anyone but the person it came from. What you felt belongs to you. Even the parts that hurt. *Especially* those."

"But then how do you make any money?"

"Bless you, boy, how do you think? Half this city wants to borrow somebody else's courage for an hour. I sell rentals. First kisses, though—" she tapped the stopper, and the light inside swirled indignantly, "—first kisses stay home. House policy."

He left with eight silver and a lighter step than he'd arrived with, pausing at the top of the stairs to say, "You're not what I expected," which was the nicest thing anyone had said to Rafael all week, and also the most alarming, because she worked very hard at being exactly what people expected: unremarkable, unmemorable, a shopkeeper shaped like a shadow.

* * *

The second customer came at the second bell, and she wasn't a customer so much as a fixture, the way rain is a fixture of spring.

"Mistress Vasquez." Old Marta filled the doorway the way weather fills a valley. She was seventy if she was a day, wide as a doorframe, with hands like tree roots and a voice that had once, rumor held, been heard across the entire harbor during a fire. "I've brought you something."

"You always bring me something, Marta, and I always tell you I can't keep taking your—"

"Lullaby." Marta set a stopper on the counter with the gentleness of a woman laying down an infant. Inside it glowed soft gold, shot through with threads of blue. "The one I sang to my girls. Both of them gone now—one to the fever, one to a husband in Vellmark who doesn't write. My voice is going, Rafa. Doctor says another winter and I won't hold a tune long enough to order soup." Her chin trembled, just once, and was mastered. "I want it put somewhere safe. Properly safe. So that when I'm gone, it isn't."

Rafael looked at the charm and felt the old ache, the one that lived behind her sternum next to everything she refused to think about. This was the part of the trade nobody warned you about: not the stealing, not the selling, but the *keeping*. Being the vault where a city stored its tenderness. Some nights she felt less like a shopkeeper and more like a lighthouse keeper, tending a thousand small flames against a dark she didn't like to name.

"Marta," she said carefully, "you know I don't deal in permanence. Charms fade. Glass cracks. If you want something guaranteed past your own—"

"I want *you* to keep it." Marta leaned across the counter, and her eyes, watery and fierce, pinned Rafael like a moth. "Fifteen years I've watched you. You pretend to be a merchant, girl, but merchants don't look at memories the way you do. Merchants see price. You see *weight*." She straightened. "Ten silver. And you'll sing it back to me every year on Founding Day, whether I ask or not, so I know it's safe. That's the bargain. Take it or I'll sit in your shop until you do, and I've a bladder of iron and nowhere to be."

It was, Rafael reflected as she counted out coins she probably shouldn't have spent, impossible to run a respectable criminal enterprise in a neighborhood where everybody loved you. She'd have to move. She'd been meaning to move for fifteen years.

She held the lullaby up to the lamp. Gold and blue, warm as fresh bread—and underneath, faintly, the sound of two small girls laughing through the melody, off-key, delighted. Rafael's throat did something complicated. She filed the charm in the cedar cabinet with the others, third shelf, under L, and did not let herself wonder which of the hundred small lights around her would outlive their owners.

"Founding Day," Marta reminded her from the stairs. "Don't think I won't remember."

"Nobody in this city ever remembers anything except at me," Rafael muttered, and Marta laughed all the way up the steps, a big rolling laugh, free of charge.

* * *

The third customer of the night was trouble, but Rafael didn't know that yet, and so she poured him tea.

He came in at the third bell wearing a soldier's coat with the insignia cut off, which meant either deserter or veteran, and in Corvel those were often the same man. He was maybe forty, gray at the temples, with the careful way of moving that said old injuries had opinions about stairs. He sat without being invited and put a stopper on the counter that made Rafael's teeth ache.

Inside the glass churned something dark red and restless. Courage. Battlefield courage, the distilled kind—not the everyday sort that gets you out of bed, but the screaming, holy madness that lets a person climb a wall while arrows fall like weather. Rafael had handled perhaps six of these in her career. They were rare, dangerous, and worth more than the rest of her inventory combined.

"Where did you get this?" she asked.

"Took it out of myself." The soldier wrapped both hands around his teacup like it was the last warm thing in the world. "After Harrow Ford. After what I did with it." His jaw worked. "I don't want it anymore. I want it *out*. I'd pour it in the canal if I thought it would drown."

Rafael studied him. Here was rule two of her little establishment, the one she'd carved into herself early and never regretted: *never steal from the desperate.* Buying was different from stealing—buying, done right, was a kindness—but there were kinds of desperate where even buying became theft. Where a man sold you the last of himself and called it fair.

"This isn't a bad memory," she said quietly. "This is a *capacity*. You give this away, you don't get brave again. Not like this. You'll be a man who flinches."

"Good," he said. "Flinching keeps you alive. Bravery's what killed everyone I served with." He pushed the stopper an inch toward her. "Name a price."

And here was the moment, Rafael would think later, when the night should have stayed ordinary. She should have named a price, or refused him, or sent him to the Saltmarket woman who dealt in the rougher trades. Instead she reached for the stopper—and stopped, because her fingertips had brushed the glass and the courage inside had *surged*, pressing against the walls of its prison like a living thing, and beneath its heat she tasted something else. Something threaded through it that didn't belong.

Fear. Not the soldier's fear. Something older, colder, patient as frost.

Her hand withdrew. "Changed my mind," she heard herself say. "Not tonight. Come back tomorrow, after noon. I want to examine the vintage properly."

The soldier's face went gray. "You think I'm lying about it. You think it's stolen goods—"

"I think it's late, and I'm tired, and I don't handle battlefield stock without sleep." She slid eight silver across the counter anyway—an insultingly low amount for what the charm was worth, but enough to send him away fed—and he took it with shaking hands and left without another word.

Rafael stood alone in her cellar and listened.

Corvel was a warded city. Everyone knew that the way everyone knows the sea is wet: the border wards had been raised a thousand years ago by founders whose names were on every schoolroom wall, great woven workings of salt and stone and starlight that kept out the wild dark beyond the mountains and, more importantly, kept out *them*—the court beneath the mountain, though polite people called it a story mothers told. The wards had a sound. Not a sound you heard, exactly. A sound you *were*, the way you're inside your own heartbeat. A low perpetual hum in the bones of the city, so constant that nobody noticed it, the same way nobody notices their own breathing until it stops.

Rafael noticed it now.

The hum had changed key.

It was very slight—half a note, maybe less, a sourness underneath the familiar drone like a cracked bell in a distant tower. Anyone else would have missed it entirely. But Rafael had spent fifteen years listening to the texture of memories, training her senses finer than any instrument, and she knew dissonance the way a mother knows a cough in the next room.

She climbed the cellar stairs two at a time, unlocked the street door, and stepped out into Tallow Lane.

The fog lay thick along the cobbles. The lamps burned their usual yellow. Somewhere a drunk was singing, badly, about a girl from the Saltmarket. Everything was exactly as it should be, and yet the hair on Rafael's arms was standing up like the fur on a cat's spine, because the hum was wrong, it was *subtly* wrong, and it was getting wronger by the minute—no, wait. Not getting wronger.

Getting *closer*.

She stood in the empty street with her breath fogging and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for fifteen years. She was a thief of intangibles in a city built on wards, and wards were made of memory—every ward-stone in Corvel was packed with the founding generations' recollections, that was the secret under the secret, and a woman with her particular talents could *read* a ward the way other people read signs. And if she pressed her attention outward now, carefully, the way you test ice—

The nearest ward-stone, set in the church wall across the lane, was dreaming. That was normal; stones dreamed. But this dream had a hole in it. A clean, round absence, like a memory with the middle scooped out, and around the edges of the absence she caught a flavor she knew intimately, professionally, *personally*:

Memory-magic. Her own craft. Or something wearing its shape.

Rafael stood very still. Above her, past the fog, past the rooftops, the sky hung low and clouded—and for one strange heartbeat she could have sworn the clouds themselves seemed dimmer than they ought to be, as though the stars behind them had drawn breath to speak and decided against it.

Then the drunk finished his song, the fog rolled on, and the hum of the wards settled—or pretended to settle—back into its ancient, comfortable drone.

Rafael locked her door, went down into her cellar full of other people's treasures, and did not sleep. She sat among her shelves of small lights until the fourth bell, listening to the city breathe, and told herself it was nothing. Old wards shifted. Old thieves got nervous. There was no reason on earth or under the mountain why the flavor of her own forbidden craft should be haunting the defenses of Corvel.

At the fourth bell, far above the fog, the first star went out.

She didn't see it. Nobody saw it. But every charm on her shelves flickered at once, all hundred small lights guttering like candles in a draft, and Rafael Vasquez sat alone in the dark of her shop and understood, with the certainty of a woman hearing the first crack in a dam, that the ordinary hours were over.
