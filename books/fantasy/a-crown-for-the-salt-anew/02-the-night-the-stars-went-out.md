## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with a quiet life, Rafael had learned, was that it never announced itself before it ended.

She was closing up shop that evening—sweeping spilled salt from behind the counter, counting the day's takings into a tin with a dented lid—when the first customer of the night came in out of the rain. Corvel in autumn meant rain the way other cities meant weather: constant, gray, and mildly insulting. The lamps along Saltglass Row hissed in it, and the shop's little bell gave its tired jangle, and for one more hour everything was ordinary.

"Closed in ten minutes," Rafael said without looking up. "Unless you're dying or buying."

"Buying," said the woman at the door, shaking out her shawl. "Though those may be the same thing, at my age."

Rafael smiled despite herself. Widow Merren came every third week for the same charm: her late husband's laugh, bottled on their wedding day by some long-dead charm-smith whose name had worn off the label. Rafael didn't make charms like that anymore. She only kept them alive, feeding them a drop of her own attention each visit so they wouldn't fade. It was the closest thing to charity she allowed herself, and she'd have bitten anyone who called it that.

"The usual?" Rafael asked, already reaching for the shelf where the small blue bottle sat among its neighbors.

"The usual." Merren settled onto the stool by the counter as if she owned it, which in a way she did; she'd been sitting there since before Rafael bought the lease. "You know, my granddaughter asked me yesterday what I keep coming back for. I told her it's medicine."

"It is medicine."

"For the heart," Merren said, "which no physician will admit exists, because they can't cut it open and bill you for it."

Rafael laughed and set the bottle down between them. Inside, the laugh glowed faintly gold, the way old joys do when they're well kept. "One drop on the tongue before bed. Don't take two. You'll wake up cheerful and frighten the neighbors."

"I've frightened them plenty with less." Merren paid in copper counted out coin by coin, each one inspected, and then paused with her hand still on her purse. "Girl. Have you noticed the sky?"

"The sky?"

"Since sundown. My knees say storm, but my eyes say—" She frowned, hunting for the word. "My eyes say nothing. That's the trouble. I looked up tonight and couldn't find a single star. Not one. And I know my stars, girl. I navigated by them forty years on the coastal runs."

Rafael glanced toward the window. Rain streaked the glass, and beyond it Corvel's lamplight made its usual orange smear against the clouds. Cloudy, then. That was all. Clouds happened.

"Cloud cover," she said.

"Mm." Merren said it the way judges say *noted*, and took her bottle, and left.

* * *

The wards broke at half past eleven.

Rafael was upstairs, in the flat above the shop, doing the thing she did every night before sleep: taking inventory of herself. It wasn't vanity. It was maintenance. Fifteen years of bottling other people's memories taught you that a person is a shelf, and shelves need checking for cracks.

On the top shelf, metaphorically speaking, sat the things she never touched. A jar wrapped in black cloth, sealed with wax from three different temples so that no single god could be blamed for it. She didn't look at it. She counted it, the way you count a scar by touch rather than sight, and moved on.

Below that, the working stock: courage, borrowed and returned; a fisherman's patience; somebody's grandmother's recipe for plum brandy, which she sold mostly to people who missed their own grandmothers. Below that, the harmless stock—the first kisses, the lullabies, the smell of bread from a bakery that had burned down nine years ago. Small mercies, bottled. Nothing anyone would miss for long. That was the rule. Rule one: never steal from the desperate. Rule two: never bottle your own past. Rule three, unwritten but iron: leave people more than you found them, even if they never know it.

She had just blown out the candle when the sound came.

It began at the edges of the city and rolled inward, and Rafael's first thought was that some enormous window had broken somewhere—a window the size of the horizon. Then the second thought arrived, cold and complete, and she knew exactly what had broken, because she had spent fifteen years living inside the thing that just shattered.

The border wards. All of them. At once.

Every charm on her shelves rang in sympathy—hundreds of tiny voices crying out like struck glass—and then went silent, which was worse. Bottled memories don't go silent unless something has eaten the air around them. Rafael stood in the dark of her bedroom with her heart going like a drum in a parade and listened to the city listen.

Then the screaming started, and there was no more listening required.

* * *

She went down to the street because staying put felt like drowning politely.

Saltglass Row had emptied onto its cobbles in various states of dress. Rain still fell, indifferent. People stood in clusters, faces tipped upward, and Rafael followed their gaze and felt her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

No stars.

Not clouded. Not hidden. *Gone.* The sky above Corvel was a smooth, unbroken black, like the inside of a closed lid. She knew clouds; clouds had texture, movement, the occasional silver lining of moonlight. This was nothing wearing the shape of sky. When she narrowed her eyes she could almost convince herself she saw a faint seam overhead, north by northeast, as if the dark were a curtain someone had drawn—and beneath the seam, if she breathed very slowly and let her craft rise in her like water, she tasted something.

Cold. Old. Patient. The flavor of a memory being held just out of reach.

Someone nearby said, "The mountain," and someone else said, "Don't say that word," and someone else said it louder, because fear is a rumor that improves with repetition.

"Inside," Rafael said, to nobody, to everybody. "Get inside. If the wards are down, curfew'll come by morning and prices'll come before that."

Nobody listened, which was normal. She went back inside herself and barred the door, and then stood in her dark shop surrounded by sleeping charms, and did the arithmetic she least wanted to do.

The border wards of Corvel were not municipal work. They were not even particularly good work, though Odile Voss had built them, and Odile Voss was the finest ward-smith the city had produced and been ruined for her trouble. No—they were old work, layered work, work laid down by founders who had bargained for the city's safety in ways the history books recorded only as footnotes. Wards like that did not fail. They were *broken*. From outside, or—

Her eyes drifted, unwillingly, to the black-wrapped jar on her mental shelf.

*From inside,* finished a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like her own.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty shop. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

The shop, being a shop, did not answer. But every charm on the shelves stayed silent, and silence, that night, sounded a great deal like agreement.

* * *

By dawn, Corvel had done what Corvel always does with catastrophe: it opened negotiations.

Rafael watched it happen from her doorstep with a cup of tea going cold in her hand. The baker tripled his prices by seven o'clock and quadrupled them by eight, citing "supply difficulties," which everyone understood to mean panic. The chandler sold out of candles by nine and was taking orders for candle futures by half past, an innovation Rafael suspected would end in either fortune or riot. Salt—plain, honest salt, the stuff of thresholds and wards and preservation—went from a copper a pound to a silver by noon, and by one o'clock there was none to be had at any price, because somewhere in the last hour the whole city had remembered, all at once, that salt keeps things out.

A priestess of the Vigilant Flame stood on the fountain steps declaring that the darkness was a test of faith. A man beside her sold protective amulets that Rafael could smell across the square—painted tin, no craft in them at all, and yet a queue formed anyway, because hope will buy anything when it's frightened enough.

And through it all, the sky stayed black. Not one star. Not the moon. Sailors at the harbor refused to put out; farmers at the gates refused to come in; both groups stood in the drizzle glaring at each other across a border that no longer technically existed, united only in their conviction that the sky had committed a personal betrayal.

Rafael sold nothing that day. She tried opening the shop and got three customers, all of whom wanted the same thing: forgetfulness. Forget the black sky. Forget the sound of the breaking. One man offered her his life savings to unmake the memory of his daughter's face, because his daughter had been outside the walls visiting her betrothed when the wards fell, and he could not bear to remember her out there under that lidless dark.

Rafael turned him away gently and hated how gently, because rule one existed precisely for him, and rule one had never cost so much to keep.

She closed early. She sat in the back room among her stock, and for the first time in fifteen years she considered her hoard—not as inventory, but as a life raft. Every charm here was a moment somebody had wanted to survive. If the city went truly bad—if the winter the priests were muttering about arrived, if the thing beneath the mountain (and everyone was saying *beneath the mountain* now, in whispers, in broad daylight) came collecting—then she had shelves full of proof that people had been happy once. Proof might be worth more than bread.

The knock came at midnight.

Three knocks, evenly spaced, unhurried. Nobody knocked on Rafael's door at midnight except trouble, and trouble usually announced itself with shouting. This knock was polite, and politeness at midnight frightened her more than shouting would have.

She opened the door with her hand already curled around the stopper of a courage-charm in her pocket.

The figure on her step wore a hooded cloak of good wool gone gray with rain. Beneath the hood: shadow, and the suggestion of a face that her eyes kept sliding off of, the way a thought slides off the name of someone you've just met. Rafael had spent fifteen years handling memories. She knew what it meant when your perception refused to settle. Either this person wore a veil-craft older than hers, or this person was not entirely a person.

"You're the charm-seller," the figure said. The voice was dry and pleasant, like paper turning pages. "Vasquez. The one who does no harm."

"That depends who's asking."

"Everyone asks, eventually." The hood tilted. "I'm told you're discreet. I'm told you can hold a memory in a bottle so gently it doesn't even bruise. I'm told—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid down on silk, "—that you're the best in Corvel, and that Corvel's best have always been better than Corvel deserves."

"Flattery costs extra."

Something that might have been a smile moved in the hood. A purse appeared from the folds of the cloak—leather, heavy, clinking with the particular dense music of gold. The figure set it on the doorstep between them, on the stone, where the threshold-salt should have been and wasn't, because Rafael had run out like everyone else.

"A commission," the figure said. "Simple work, for one of your gifts. I want you to steal something for me."

"I don't steal," Rafael said. "I trade. People give me what they can spare."

"This, you'll have to steal." The figure leaned closer, and the cold came off it like breath off ice, and beneath the cold Rafael caught that flavor again—the one from last night, the one under the seam in the sky. Old. Patient. Held just out of reach. "A soul. Lightly used. It won't be missed—it never is, until afterward, and afterward isn't our concern. Name your price. That purse is a beginning, not an offer."

Rafael's mouth was dry. "Whose soul?"

The hood regarded her for a long moment. Rain ticked off the eaves. Somewhere across the city a watch-bell rang the hour wrong, or perhaps time itself had begun ringing itself wrong, and no one had told the bells.

"Now *there's* an interesting question," the figure said softly. "Because when I asked after the best charm-smith in Corvel, do you know what I was told? I was told about a woman who came to this city fifteen years ago with nothing, out of the burning country, and bought her new life with a bargain no one has ever been able to trace." The hood tilted, and though there were no visible eyes, Rafael felt them, and they were amused. "You've dealt with us before, Vasquez. We remember our customers. We remember *everything*. It's rather our whole nature."

The purse sat on the step between them, gold-heavy, patient as the dark overhead.

"We'll speak again," the figure said, and stepped backward into the rain, and the rain accepted it the way water accepts a stone, closing over the place where it had been.

Rafael stood a long while on her threshold, in the starless dark, holding the doorframe like a woman holding a rail on a ship in weather. Then she picked up the purse—it weighed as much as a newborn, which felt significant and horrible—and carried it inside, and barred the door, and did not sleep.

In the back room, her charms sat silent on their shelves. On the highest shelf, in her mind, the black-wrapped jar seemed almost warm.

Fifteen years, she thought. Fifteen years building an invisible life, quiet as dust, harmless as salt. And it hadn't ended because she'd been careless, or caught, or brave. It had ended because the past had finally finished walking to her door.

Outside, the sky stayed black. Inside, Rafael Vasquez began, for the first time since she'd sealed it, to think seriously about opening the jar.
