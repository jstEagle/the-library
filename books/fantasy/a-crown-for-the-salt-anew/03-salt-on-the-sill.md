## 3. Salt on the Sill

The curfew bell had rung twice by the time Rafael gave up on her shop and started walking.

She hadn't bothered to close it properly. There was nothing left worth closing. She'd spent the day after the stars went out selling nothing, giving away half her stock just to watch people's faces soften — a widow who needed her husband's laugh one more time, a baker who couldn't remember why he'd ever loved his work and wanted the wanting back. The other half she'd packed into a satchel, because a charm-seller with no product was just a woman with opinions, but a charm-seller with inventory could still buy bread when bread cost its weight in silver.

The streets were wrong. That was the only word for it. Corvel at night was usually a river of lanterns, a city so proud of its wards that it lit itself up like a challenge to the dark. Now the ward-lamps burned sullen and brown, the way candles burn in a draft, and the shadows between them had teeth. People hurried with their collars up and their eyes down, and every third doorway had a line of salt poured across its threshold, thick as snowfall.

Rafael counted the salt lines as she walked. Every house. Even the ones whose owners couldn't afford salt, who'd swept their floors to find a handful's worth. Salt kept out spirits, everyone knew that, though of course it did no such thing. Salt kept out *memories* — that was its true trade, though hardly anyone remembered even that much anymore. A line of salt across a sill meant: *nothing crosses here that isn't invited.* It was the oldest ward there was, older than the great border-stones, older than the mountain. And now the whole city was doing it, without being told, the way a body flinches from a flame.

The wards had fallen two nights ago. The sky was still empty.

She turned down Tanner's Cut, past the shuttered chandlery, and stopped at a door that looked like all the others except that its salt line had been poured by someone who understood the craft — an unbroken circle, keyed with three knots of intent, refreshed that morning. Above the lintel hung a small iron bell that wasn't decorative.

Rafael stood in the cold a moment longer than she needed to, telling herself she was checking the street.

Then she knocked the agreed rhythm — two, pause, three — because Odile had insisted on a rhythm, Odile insisted on everything, and waited.

The door opened on a chain. One grey eye appeared in the gap.

"You're late," said Brannic.

"The streets are slow."

"The streets are dead. You're slow." But the chain came off, and the door opened wide enough for her, which from Brannic was practically an embrace.

Inside, the house smelled like solder and rosemary, which should not have worked together and somehow did. It had been a ward-smith's workshop once — Odile's, before the disgrace, though nobody said the word *disgrace* inside these walls; they'd all silently agreed it was a word for other people's houses. The ground floor was a chaos of copper wire, stone blanks, jars of ground silver, and a long workbench where Odile sat hunched like a gargoyle over a ward-stone the size of a fist, engraving it with a needle finer than an eyelash.

She didn't look up. "If you're here to tell me the sky is still broken, I know. If you're here to tell me the price of salt has tripled, I know. If you're here to steal something, put it back."

"I haven't touched anything."

"That's what a thief would say."

"It's also what an honest woman would say."

Odile snorted and set down her needle. Up close she looked worse than yesterday — smaller, as if the starless nights were slowly unscrewing her — but her hands were steady, and her hands were always the truest part of her. "Well? Did you sell anything today?"

"Everything I could spare."

"Did you charge?"

"Some."

"Rafael."

"It was a widow, Odile. Her husband died in the first panic. She wanted his voice. What was I going to do, haggle?"

"Yes! You're going to haggle, because if you don't charge then people learn charms are free, and if charms are free then the Guild learns you're giving away guild-craft without a license, and if the Guild learns that, they come here, and I am *not* hiding you under my floorboards again, the last time ruined the tea chest." Odile jabbed a finger at her. "Charity is a luxury of women with clean reputations. Yours hasn't been clean since the day I met you."

It was, Rafael reflected, oddly nice to be scolded. Two days ago her life had been a shop and a locked box under the floorboards and a set of rules she'd mistaken for a personality. Now it was this room, and these people, and she had no idea how it had happened or where the door was.

"I'll charge next time," she said.

"You won't." Odile picked her needle back up. "Go see if Tam's eaten. He lies about it."

---

Tam was on the roof.

Of course he was. The boy had developed a habit, since the sky went dark, of climbing out onto the tiles whenever anyone wasn't watching, sitting with his knees pulled up, staring upward at the blankness as if it might apologize. Rafael found him through the attic window, climbed out beside him, and settled onto the warm slate with a grunt she tried to disguise.

"Evening," Tam said.

"Evening yourself. Odile says you haven't eaten."

"Odile says that every day whether I've eaten or not. It's how she loves people. Through accusations about food." He grinned at her sideways. His face still had the softness of someone younger than his seventeen years, though his hands had started catching up lately, calloused from helping Odile with the heavy stones. "I ate. Bread and the last of the honey. Don't tell her the honey part, she'll want to discuss it."

"Discuss it how?"

"Loudly." He looked back up at the sky. "Do you think they're still there? The stars. Behind something."

Rafael considered lying, decided against it, and considered the truth, which was worse. "I think if they were behind something, we'd see edges. Clouds have edges. Smoke has edges. This doesn't. It's more like—" she searched for it— "like someone reached up and took them off a shelf."

"Someone took them off a shelf," Tam repeated, and laughed, short and unhappy. "That's such a specific thing to say."

"It's how a thief would think about it."

"You're not a thief."

"No?"

"No. Thieves take things people miss. You give things back." He said it simply, the way he said everything, with no idea what it did to her. "Brannic thinks you're a thief. He told me. He also told me to tell you he thinks it, which I thought was strange."

"He *told you to tell me*—"

"He said, and I'm quoting, 'Tell her I know what she is, and tell her I haven't decided if it matters.'" Tam shrugged cheerfully. "He's very fond of you. In his way. His way is mostly threats."

Rafael stared at him. Below them, through the attic window, lamplight moved across the workshop floor — Odile bent over her bench, Brannic standing in the doorway pretending he wasn't watching over her, the whole small lit box of them against the enormous dark. Fifteen years she had lived in Corvel without a single person knowing her name's true weight. Fifteen years, and now within two nights she had a boy quoting threats at her on a roof and a captain announcing his suspicions through intermediaries like a man courting.

"When did he say this?" she managed.

"This morning. While sharpening the axe."

"There's an axe now?"

"There's been an axe since the second night. He sleeps with it." Tam hugged his knees. "I like it here," he added, abruptly, in the tone of someone changing the subject away from a cliff edge. "This house. I don't remember anywhere else I've liked, so maybe it's just that I can't compare it. But I don't think so."

Something in Rafael's chest went tight and hot. She looked at the starless sky instead of at him.

"Maybe it doesn't need comparing," she said.

---

Downstairs, Brannic had laid the table for four, which he did every night without acknowledgment, and was arguing with Odile about salt.

"You can't ward a house with table salt."

"I can ward a house with *anything*, Brannic, that's the entire discipline—"

"With intent, yes. With structure. Not with whatever you scraped off the market floor because the market floor was cheaper."

"The market floor salt has *character*."

"The market floor salt has *grit*." Brannic sat down heavily, and the chair complained the way furniture always complained around him, as if it had been warned. He was a big man gone soft at the middle, grey at the temples, with a warden's posture that thirty years of retirement hadn't managed to unbend. On the wall behind him hung his old sword, and beside the sword, closer to hand than any retired man's weapon ought to be, the axe.

Rafael took her seat. Tam slid in beside her, smelling faintly of slate dust and honey. For a while there was only the sound of soup and the small domestic percussion of Odile defending her salt.

Then Brannic set down his spoon.

"Two things happened today," he said, in the voice he used for reports. Everyone's spoon slowed. "One: the Guild posted criers at noon. Official position on the starless sky is 'a temporary atmospheric disturbance,' official position on the wards is 'under repair,' and official instruction is to remain indoors after dusk and report unusual strangers to the wardens' remnant."

"And the unofficial position?" Rafael asked.

"The unofficial position is that the wardens' remnant is eleven men, half of them gout-ridden, guarding a city built on forty-one anchor stones that are all, as of two nights ago, singing flat." He let that sit. "Two: I went to the old records hall this afternoon. Before the curfew. I wanted to read up on the mountain."

The room got quieter. Even Odile stopped defending her salt.

"What do the records say?" Rafael asked.

"Very little that's useful and quite a lot that's frightening. The court beneath the mountain appears in the founding records exactly once, in a marginal note by the first Warden-Captain of Corvel." Brannic's eyes came up and rested on Rafael, steady and unreadable. "It says the court pays in wishes and collects in souls, and that no bargain struck with it has ever stayed bought. Then the rest of the page is torn out. Torn out carefully, along the binding, centuries ago, by someone who didn't want it read."

Tam had gone pale. Odile's needle-hand had curled into a fist around her spoon.

"Why would someone tear that page out?" Tam asked.

"Because knowledge is a debt," Brannic said, "and somebody wanted the debt forgotten." He was still looking at Rafael. "Which brings me to my question of the evening. The one I asked you this morning through our mutual friend here, since direct conversation apparently frightens us both." A ghost of humor crossed his face and was gone. "You deal in memories. You know what memory-magic looks like — the residue of it, the fingerprints. The border wards didn't fail two nights ago, Rafael. They were *blinded*. Something made the wards forget how to be wards. And whoever did it used a craft I'm told is rare. Rare enough that I can count its practitioners in this city on one hand, and one of those fingers is pointing at the woman sitting across my table eating my soup."

The silence stretched. Outside, the wind moved through the empty streets, and somewhere far off a dog began to howl and thought better of it.

Rafael set down her spoon. She could lie. She was good at lying; it was most of her trade, honestly, the bottling was just the delivery mechanism. She could laugh it off, offer to examine the ward-stones for a fee, redirect, deflect, do all the clever slippery things that had kept her alive and anonymous for fifteen years.

She looked at Tam, who was watching her with absolute, uncomplicated trust, the kind that made lying feel like breaking something that couldn't be repaired. At Odile, whose eyes were sharp but not yet hard — deciding, Rafael realized, deciding right now, this minute, over soup. At Brannic, who had already decided everything except whether it mattered.

"I didn't blind the wards," Rafael said. "But I think I might know who taught the person who did."

Odile went very still. "Taught?"

"Fifteen years ago," Rafael said, and heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when she approached her own locked doors, "I sold something to the court beneath the mountain. And I think tonight I need to show you what."

* * *

They gathered around the workbench lamp, the four of them, and Rafael took out her key.

Under the floorboard where the shop's profits used to sleep, in the house she was starting to suspect she'd already left forever, she kept a box. In the box were her rules, her tools, her hoard. And at the bottom of the box, wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed twice, lay a single charm she had never sold, never opened, never even looked at directly in fifteen years. It was small and ugly, badly made — the work of a frightened girl of nineteen — and it was the only thing she owned that had never once been for sale.

She carried it back through the dark streets with the satchel of inventory pressing against her hip like a passenger, and when she came into the lamplight of the workshop, all three of them were waiting exactly where she'd left them, as if they'd known she'd come back. As if coming back had been assumed.

Nobody had ever assumed that about her before.

"This is it," she said, and set the little charm on the workbench between the copper wire and the jar of ground silver. "What I bought my escape with. What I paid."

Odile leaned in, reading the craftsmanship the way she read everything. "This is early work. Clumsy. The seal's uneven—"

"It's a soul," Rafael said quietly. "Bottled. My sister's."

The lamp flickered. Tam made a small sound and put his hand over his mouth. Brannic didn't move at all, but something in his face closed like a gate.

And beneath their feet, deep beneath the city, beneath the foundations and the anchor stones and the roots of the mountain itself, something vast and patient turned a page in an iron book, and wrote, in the column of debts long overdue:

*Vasquez.*

The charm on the workbench shivered, very slightly, though there was no wind.

Odile saw it first.
