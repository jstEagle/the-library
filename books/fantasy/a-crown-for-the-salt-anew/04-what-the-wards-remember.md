## 4. What the Wards Remember

The ward-stone had been Odile's responsibility once, back when she still had a workshop and a name that opened doors instead of closing them. Eleven years ago, before the Guild of Ward-Smiths voted her out with the polite efficiency of people removing a stain from carpet. So when Brannic hauled the fallen border-stone into the cellar of his safehouse on a handcart, grunting like a man birthing a boulder, something in Odile's chest that had been sleeping for a decade sat up and asked for its tools.

"Careful," she said, though he was already setting it down with exaggerated gentleness, which was somehow worse. "It's not a keg of ale."

"It weighs like one," Brannic said. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, silver in his beard, with the permanent expression of someone who had spent thirty years being disappointed by paperwork. "And it's the only one they'd let me have. The wardens' remnant has the rest locked up tighter than the treasury."

"The rest." Odile crouched beside the stone. It was the size of a wine barrel, hexagonal, its faces carved with the old Corveline script that nobody under forty could read anymore. Dead now. She could feel it the way you feel a cold hearth—the shape of warmth that used to be there. "How many fell?"

"All of them. Every stone on the ring. Same night, same hour, same—" He gestured vaguely at the sky, which was visible through the high cellar window and was wrong in a way Odile's eyes kept refusing to process. Black. Not night-black, not cloudy-black. *Empty*-black, like a page where someone had scraped off the writing. "Same nothing."

"Stars don't just go out."

"No," Brannic agreed. "They don't."

Tam was sitting on the stairs, because Tam was always sitting somewhere nearby, watching with the open, unembarrassed attention of a child or a saint. He was maybe nineteen, thin as a bundle of kindling, with a face that seemed assembled from spare parts—too-large ears, a crooked nose, an earnest mouth. He remembered nothing before four years ago except kindness, which is what he called it: *I remember somebody was kind to me once, and I've been trying to be worth it since.* The court had taken the rest of him, though none of them had known that yet, not for certain.

"Can you fix it?" Tam asked.

Odile ran her palm along the stone's face. "Fix it? Lad, this isn't a broken cart wheel. This is—" She stopped. Her fingers had found something.

There was a groove in the carving, fine as a hair, running through the primary sigil. Not damage. A cut. Deliberate, precise, made by someone who knew exactly where to press a knife—or a stylus, or a needle dipped in something that wasn't ink—to make a ward forget itself.

"What?" Brannic said, reading her face the way old soldiers read weather.

"Someone didn't break this." Odile's voice came out flat and strange. "Someone *edited* it."

* * *

She worked through the morning while the city outside curfewed itself into a held breath. Brannic had rigged the safehouse—his old warden garrison house, decommissioned but never sold, full of furniture under sheets like patient ghosts—with salt lines and a few of his own crude charms, and Odile approved of the salt if not the charms. Salt was honest. Charms were only ever as honest as their maker.

That thought kept circling back to her as she worked, and she couldn't have told you why. Not yet.

By noon she had the stone's death mapped. She'd cleared a space on Brannic's billiards table—he'd covered it with canvas and called it a workbench, and she'd decided not to hold the indignity against him—and laid out her instruments: magnifying lenses in brass frames, a stylus of bone, a dish of ash for reading residue, and her own hands, which were the finest instruments she owned and the reason the Guild had hated her. Too good. Too quick. A woman who could read a ward's history the way priests read scripture, and who had once, publicly, at a Guild convocation, pointed out that the Master Ward-Smith's celebrated design for the harbor lights would fail within five years.

It had failed within three. They had still voted her out. Competence, Odile had learned, was forgivable; being right early was not.

The stone's history unfolded under her lenses like a long letter from a friend. She could see the smiths who had cut it, two hundred years of maintenance layered over the original work—here a repair in the reign of some duke whose name she'd forgotten, here a patch during the Salt Riots, done hastily by someone whose hand shook. Living things, wards. They accumulated.

And then, three nights ago, someone had walked up to this stone in the dark and spoken to it.

Not struck it. Not blasted it. *Spoken.* The cut in the primary sigil was a listening-cut, a technique so old most smiths thought it theoretical—you whispered a command into the ward's memory-channel and the ward obeyed, because wards were built to remember, and anything built to remember can be taught.

Taught to forget.

Odile sat back. Her tea had gone cold hours ago. Through the window the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

"Wards are memory," she said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud helped her think and Brannic had long ago stopped commenting on it. "A ward is a promise the stone remembers. To blind them all at once, you'd need to teach every stone on the ring the same lesson in the same hour. You'd need—" She frowned. "You'd need to know how they remember. You'd need memory-craft."

Which was a problem, because memory-craft wasn't ward-smithing. It was something else entirely, something the Guild pretended didn't exist, practiced in back rooms and night markets by people who bottled first kisses and lullabies and soldiers' courage and sold them to the soft-hearted and the desperate.

People like Rafael Vasquez.

The name arrived in her head with a small, unwelcome click, like a key turning.

*No,* Odile told herself. *Coincidence. The woman sells trinkets. Pretty little bottles. Half of it's probably theater.*

But her hands were already moving, lifting the ash-dish, tilting it toward the light to catch the residue she'd brushed from the cut. Residue told you what had touched a ward last, the way flour on a sleeve tells you who's been baking. Most residue was boring: rain-mineral, pigeon, the faint iron tang of the mountain's deep water.

This residue was neither. It was pale gold, almost invisible, and it moved. When she tilted the dish, it flowed toward the light like something curious. Like something *remembering* light.

Odile had seen memory-magic exactly twice in her life. Once at a fair, from a charlatan selling bottled courage that was probably onion juice and suggestion. And once, eleven years ago, in the Guild hall, when a young woman with clever hands had been brought in as a curiosity—a charm-seller caught trading near the wards, hauled before the Masters as an example—and Odile, then still a member in good standing, had watched the girl bottle a memory in front of the assembled Smiths, and had seen that pale gold light move toward her lamp flame like a moth.

The girl had been released with a warning. The Masters had found her amusing. Odile had found her terrifying, and had said so, and had been told she was jealous.

The residue in the dish flowed toward the light.

"Oh," Odile said quietly. "Oh, you absolute fool."

* * *

She didn't confront Rafael immediately, which surprised her. The old Odile—the Guild Odile—would have marched straight to the woman's shop and demanded answers in front of witnesses, because righteousness performed publicly was the only currency the Guild respected. But eleven years of exile teaches you things. It teaches you that being right is cheap and being *certain* is expensive, and that you should count the cost before you spend either.

So she waited until evening. She waited through supper, which Tam cooked—some kind of stew that tasted mostly of enthusiasm—and through Brannic telling a story about a warden horse named Duchess who had once eaten an entire report on smuggling routes, saddle-leather and all, and through the small domestic rituals of four strangers pretending to be a household because the alternative was admitting they were afraid.

Rafael washed the dishes. That was the moment Odile chose, watching the woman's hands move in the basin—clever hands, quick hands, hands she had now seen twice doing impossible things.

"You'll want to dry those thoroughly," Odile said, coming to stand beside her. "Salt water's hard on the glaze."

"We're out of salt water. We're nearly out of everything." Rafael handed her a dish without looking up. She was small, dark-haired, with the watchful brown eyes of someone who counted exits in every room. Odile had liked her immediately, which was precisely why the next part was going to hurt. "Something on your mind, ward-smith? Besides my dishwashing technique?"

"The stones," Odile said. "The border stones. I finished reading them today."

Rafael's hands did not stop moving. Odile noted that. Guilty people freeze; professionals keep working. "And?"

"And they weren't broken. They were taught to forget. Someone used memory-craft on them—listening-cuts in the primary sigils, all sixteen stones, same night, same hour. Whoever did it knows wards better than the Guild does and memories better than anyone I've ever met." Odile took the dish from her hands and set it down, deliberately, on the shelf. "Better than anyone I've met *twice*, actually. Once at a fair. Once in the Guild hall, eleven years ago, when they dragged in a charm-seller named Vasquez to show the Masters her tricks."

The kitchen went very quiet. From the parlor came the sound of Brannic losing gracefully to Tam at cards, and the boy's laugh, bright and unguarded, and Odile hated that she noticed it, hated that it mattered.

Rafael dried her hands slowly on her apron. When she turned around, her face was composed, but Odile had spent a career reading micro-fractures in stone, and faces were easier.

"You think I did it," Rafael said.

"I think the residue on the cuts matches your craft. Pale gold, light-seeking. I'd stake what's left of my reputation on it, and my reputation is the only thing I own, so understand that I don't say it lightly."

"And you thought you'd tell me over dishes. Gently. So I'd have time to think of a story."

"I thought I'd tell you privately," Odile corrected, "because if you're guilty, the others deserve to hear it from me and not find you gone one morning with the silver. And if you're innocent—" She paused. "If you're innocent, then someone with your exact craft blinded a city, and you may be the only person alive who can tell us how they learned to do it. Either way, I wanted to look at your face first. Faces are honest for about four seconds. I've had mine now."

Something flickered across Rafael's face—not guilt exactly, and not innocence either. Something older and more tired than both.

"You're right about the craft," Rafael said finally. "It's mine. Or—it's mine the way a child is yours. I made it. I've been making it since I was fifteen years old, and I never showed anyone, and I certainly never taught anyone." She pulled out a chair and sat down, heavily, like a woman setting down luggage she'd carried too far. "But I didn't touch the stones, Odile. On my life, whatever that's worth."

"That's the problem," Odile said, sitting across from her. "It's worth a great deal to me, and I don't know why. I've known you nine days. Nine days, and a bowl of stew, and one evening of cards where you cheated and let Tam win anyway." She leaned forward. "So convince me. Not with oaths. With craft. Show me something only the maker of that magic would know—something a thief who studied your work couldn't fake."

Rafael was quiet for a long moment. In the parlor, Tam laughed again at something, and Brannic groaned theatrically, and the sound of it made them both glance toward the door, and Odile saw Rafael's composure crack—not with guilt, but with something that looked horribly like fear *for them*, and that, more than any residue, was what decided her.

Because culprits didn't look like that. Culprits looked like the Masters of her old Guild: smooth, defended, already composing the record they wanted history to keep.

"There's a signature in the work," Rafael said at last, very quietly. "In mine. In whoever copied it. Memory-magic carries intention the way bread carries the baker's hands. If you can read the residue properly—if you go back to the stones and read not *what* was done but *how it felt*—you'll find the difference. My craft is gentle. I built it to be gentle, because I was stealing from people who were already hurting, and I couldn't—" She stopped. Started again. "Whoever did this wasn't gentle. Sixteen stones, taught to forget themselves in a single hour. That's violence. That's violence all the way down into the technique. Read it. You'll know."

"I will," Odile said. "Tomorrow. First light."

"But?" Rafael said, because Odile hadn't stood up, hadn't ended it, and women like Rafael noticed unfinished sentences the way sailors notice weather.

"But there's a second thing." Odile turned her teacup—a habit, a fidget, a small dishonest comfort. "The listening-cuts weren't clumsy. They weren't even competent. They were *elegant*. Better than elegant. There were flourishes in that work I've never seen, techniques I've only ever theorized about, and I'll tell you something I've never told anyone: I tried to invent one of them myself, years ago, and failed, and concluded it was impossible." She looked up. "Your craft is good, Rafael. Whoever did this is better. Which means either you've been hiding a master's depth behind a market stall of love-charms—which, frankly, would insult us both—or someone learned your foundation and built a cathedral on it."

Rafael had gone very still. Stillness, Odile was learning, was this woman's native language.

"Someone taught by me," Rafael said slowly, "or someone who *took* from me. My memories. My—" Her hand went, unconsciously, to her coat pocket, where Odile had noticed before she carried a small collection of bottles, and where her hand now pressed flat as if holding a wound closed.

"Your what?"

"Nothing." Too fast. Then, catching herself, softer: "Not nothing. Something I'm not ready to say. Ask me again tomorrow, after you've read the stones. If I'm right about what you'll find, you'll understand why I need the night."

Odile studied her. Nine days. A bowl of stew. One rigged card game. By every measure she trusted, she should have reported this to Brannic, laid it all out, let the ex-warden do what wardens did.

Instead she heard herself say: "One night. But Rafael—if I read those stones tomorrow and find gentleness anywhere in that work, any trace of your hand in the actual cutting, I will wake you myself, and it won't be gently."

"Fair," Rafael said. And then, with the ghost of the humor that had gotten under Odile's skin these nine days despite herself: "For what it's worth, I'd make a terrible saboteur. I get attached to things. It's a professional hazard."

"For a memory-thief, that's either a lie or a tragedy."

"Yes," Rafael said. "Both, usually."

* * *

Odile went back to the stones at first light, as promised, alone, with fresh lenses and a pot of tea she had no intention of drinking hot.

Reading intention in residue was slow, delicate work—the magical equivalent of reading a letter by the pressure of the pen rather than the words. She knelt beside the fallen stone in the grey cellar light and let her awareness sink into the pale gold traces, past the technique, past the elegance, down to the layer where craft became feeling.

And there it was.

The work was beautiful and it was cruel, exactly as Rafael had said—but that wasn't what stopped Odile's breath. What stopped her breath was what lay underneath the cruelty, woven into it so tightly you'd miss it unless you were looking: a familiarity. The cutter had known these wards. Known their maintenance history, known the shaky-handed patch from the Salt Riots, known which smiths had worked which faces. Had known them the way you know a house you grew up in.

And beneath *that*—faint, almost scrubbed away, but there, because nothing in memory-magic ever fully leaves—one more signature. A single thread of pale gold, gentle, careful, unmistakably Rafael's own hand. Not cutting. Not teaching the stones to forget.

*Healing.* Someone had used Rafael's craft to mend a hairline fracture in the stone's memory-channel, months ago, quietly, asking nothing, leaving nothing but the mending.

Rafael hadn't blinded the wards. Rafael had been *tending* them—for who knew how long—in secret, in the dark, like a person visiting a grave.

Which meant the cutter had come to these stones *after* her, had walked the same circuit she walked, had studied her repairs the way a forger studies a master's brushwork, and had used her gentleness as a map for finding the soft places.

Odile sat back on her heels in the cold cellar, tea forgotten, and felt betrayal bloom in her chest—though to her surprise, and she examined this feeling with a craftsman's honesty, the betrayal was not aimed at Rafael.

It was aimed at whoever had taken a woman's private kindness and turned it into a weapon's blueprint. Because she recognized that crime. The Guild had done it to her, once, in smaller letters.

She climbed the stairs slowly. Through the kitchen doorway she could see Rafael already awake, sitting at the table with that coat across her knees and her hand inside the pocket, holding something, her face the face of a woman standing at a door she had locked fifteen years ago and hearing, at last, knocking from the other side.

"Well?" Rafael asked, without looking up.

"You were right," Odile said. "It wasn't you. It was someone who learned from you—learned from your *repairs*. Rafael, you've been healing those stones for years, haven't you? Quietly. And someone followed your footsteps."

Rafael's hand came out of the pocket. In it was a small bottle, pale gold, older than the others, its stopper sealed with wax stamped by a seal Odile didn't recognize—and Rafael was looking at it the way people look at things they've promised themselves never to open.

"Then it's time," Rafael said quietly, "to tell you about the night I sold my sister's soul."
