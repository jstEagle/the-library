## 5. A Debt Fifteen Years Old

Odile did not knock. That was the first sign of trouble.

In the three weeks since the stars had gone out and the four of them had folded themselves into Brannic's cramped house above the old tannery, Odile had developed a whole etiquette of intrusion: two knocks for *I'm coming in*, one knock for *I'm thinking out loud and you may ignore me*, and no knock at all only when something had gone badly wrong or she'd had too much chicory coffee, which was often both.

She came through the door of Rafael's workroom with a ward-stone cradled in her arms like a sick cat and set it down on the table with a click that made Rafael's teeth ache.

"Tell me what you see," Odile said.

Rafael looked at the stone. It was a border-ward fragment, one of the hundreds Odile had been prying out of the city's foundations since the night the sky went dark. This one was scorched along its seams, the engraved lattice of it blackened as though something had crawled through it eating light.

"I see a dead ward," Rafael said carefully.

"You see a dead ward," Odile agreed. "Now tell me what I see."

"Odile."

"What I see," Odile continued, in the bright, terrible voice she used when she was being patient, "is a signature. Wards don't just fail, Rafael. They're unmade. And whoever unmade this one left fingerprints all over it, because they were sloppy, or hurried, or—" her voice caught on the word, "—or young. The technique is memory-craft. Bottling-work. A charm-smith's hand, used to blind a city." She finally looked up from the stone, and her eyes were wet and furious at once. "It's your hand, Rafael. I've watched you work for weeks. I know your hand."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, Tam was teaching himself to whistle, badly, and Brannic was pretending not to enjoy it.

Rafael set down the charm she'd been holding—a little bottle of someone's memory of rain, harmless, sweet—and made herself meet Odile's gaze.

"You think I pulled down the wards."

"I think either you pulled down the wards," Odile said, "or you know who did. Because nobody else alive works magic like yours. Nobody else alive works magic like mine, either, and I've been asking myself some very uncomfortable questions for three weeks." She dragged a chair around and sat in it backward, arms folded over the backrest, close enough now that Rafael could smell the solder-smoke on her sleeves. "So. One more time, and then I stop asking nicely. What did you do?"

* * *

Here is what Rafael wanted to say: *Nothing. I sell kisses and lullabies to people who can't sleep. I have rules. I've never hurt anyone.*

Here is what she actually said, after a silence long enough that the whistling downstairs stopped:

"There's a bottle under my floorboards. Under the third board, under the loose tile, behind the false wall I built into this room the day I moved in. It's older than everything else I own. And if you want the truth out of me, Odile, you'll have to help me open it, because I have spent fifteen years making sure I couldn't do it alone."

Odile blinked. "That's the most alarming sentence anyone has ever said to me, and I once worked for the Wardens' Guild."

"It gets worse."

* * *

The floorboard came up easily; Rafael had oiled its edges every year, telling herself it was practical maintenance and knowing perfectly well it was penance. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth inside a lead-lined box, lay a single glass vial no bigger than her thumb.

Inside it swirled something the color of woodsmoke and embers. Even sealed, even after fifteen years, it was warm to the touch. Memory always is, when it matters enough.

"Rules first," Rafael said, holding it up between them so the lamplight caught the swirl inside. "This is my own memory. My deepest one. I bottled it myself, the night it happened, and I built the seal wrong on purpose—I'm good enough at this craft that I could have made it easy to open, and instead I made it need two hands. Mine and someone else's. So that on the nights I wanted to forget I'd forgotten something, I couldn't get at it." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Which, yes. I understand the irony. I bottled my own forgetting of my forgetting."

"That's not irony," Odile said quietly. "That's just grief with good craftsmanship."

"Grief with good craftsmanship. Write that on my grave." Rafael looked at the vial a moment longer. "When we open it, I'll be in it. Part of me. The person who opens a memory lives it again, whole, like it's happening now—you know that, you've seen my customers come out of charms dazed and smiling. Only this one isn't going to make me smile. So I need you to hold onto me, and I need you to believe me when I say that whatever I say in there, whatever I do in there—it happened fifteen years ago. I'm not that person anymore. Or. I am. But I'm also this one."

Odile took off her smith's gloves, finger by finger, and set them aside. Then she put her bare hands on Rafael's shoulders—warm, calloused, steady—and nodded once.

"Together, then," she said. "On three?"

"No," Rafael said. "There's never a three. There's just doing it before you can talk yourself out of it."

She unstopped the vial.

* * *

Smoke.

Not metaphor—actual smoke, thick and orange, rolling over rooftops under a sky full of stars that were still there, still burning, indifferent as coins spilled on black velvet. Rafael was seventeen again and her lungs were full of ash and her arm was locked around a smaller arm, dragging a girl of twelve through a street that had become a river of fire.

*Sera. Sera, keep moving, don't look at the house—*

*I wasn't looking at the house,* said Sera, twelve years old and already too stubborn to die properly, coughing, laughing, half-hysterical. *I was looking at the goat. Rafa, the goat got out, maybe he—*

*The goat is fine. Goats are immortal. Move.*

The village of Saltmere burned behind them the way villages do—not all at once but in pieces, the mill first, then the chapel, then the row of houses where everyone Rafael had ever known had slept the night before. She never learned what started it. In fifteen years she never learned. Some part of her had decided that not knowing was kinder than any answer, and that part of her ran the show from then on.

They reached the crossroads at the edge of the fields, where the old waystone stood, and the waystone was humming.

Rafael remembered the hum. She would remember it forever, which was the problem—that was the entire shape of her curse, that she remembered everything except the one thing she most needed to remember, and here it was, arriving again, fresh as a wound: the low, sweet, resonant hum of a door standing open where no door should be.

The air beside the waystone had peeled back like skin. Through it, torchlight—but cold torchlight, blue-white, starless—and beyond it a hall of black stone, and figures in the hall wearing beautiful clothes and borrowed faces, turning to look at her with polite, patient interest.

A voice came through the opening. It was kind. That was the worst thing about it, how kind it was, how it sounded like an auntie at a market offering you a taste of honey.

*Two children of Saltmere,* the voice said. *The village burns. The road south is closed by fire for three days—you will not outrun it. But I keep doors, little thief. I have always kept doors.*

*I'm not a thief,* Rafael said, seventeen and lying already, already reaching for the craft she barely understood, the knack she'd had since childhood for holding other people's moments in cupped hands.

*No? Your pockets are full of them.* The voice sounded amused. *Your neighbor's wedding. Your mother's hands kneading bread. You carry memories like a magpie carries buttons. What a talent. What a waste, to die in a field with it.*

Sera's grip tightened on her arm. *Rafa,* she whispered. *What is it saying?*

*It's saying nothing. Keep walking. We go around—*

*You cannot go around,* the voice said gently. *But you may go through. One passage. Two children, out of the fire, safe, anywhere in the world you wish to wake tomorrow. My price is small. I collect souls the way you collect buttons—one at a time, lightly held. Give me one soul, and both of you walk free.*

And here was the moment. Here was the exact grain of sand at the center of fifteen years of anesthesia, the pearl of rot Rafael had built her whole careful life around:

Her sister's hand was in hers. Her sister was looking up at her with absolute, unbroken trust—the trust of a child who had never once been failed by the person holding her hand—and Rafael felt the bargain settle into her chest like a coin dropping into a well, and she heard herself say:

*Take mine.*

The pause that followed lasted a heartbeat. In that heartbeat, Rafael knew—she had always known, she knew it now, living it again with thirty-two years of hindsight pressing against the inside of the memory like a face against glass—that the court would not take hers. She was the one with the talent. She was the magpie with the pockets full of buttons, and the court did not collect things freely given by their owners; it collected things *traded*, and a trade required value, and the value in Rafael's grip was not her own threadbare, guilty, seventeen-year-old soul.

It was the bright, unbottled, entirely trusting soul of the girl whose hand she was holding.

*Take mine,* she said again, louder, and squeezed Sera's hand—squeezed it the way she always had, twice, their old signal, *it's all right, I've got you*—and while Sera smiled up at her, while Sera opened her mouth to argue, the court answered:

*Agreed.*

And the world tilted, and there was a sound like a page turning in an enormous book, and Sera's hand—

Sera's hand went slack in hers.

Not dead. Never dead. Just... elsewhere. The girl was still standing there, blinking, looking at Rafael with a polite faint smile, the smile of a stranger at a market stall, and when Rafael screamed her name the girl tilted her head and said, in Sera's voice with nothing of Sera behind it:

*Should I know you?*

Then the door took her. Cold torchlight swallowed her whole, and the waystone went silent, and the fire roared on, and Rafael Vasquez stood alone in a burning field holding the shape of a hand that was no longer there.

She didn't remember running south. She didn't remember the three days, or the road, or arriving in Corvel with singed hair and empty pockets. The next clear thing she remembered was buying her first set of bottles, and the thought that arrived with them, fully formed, like it had been waiting:

*If I can hold other people's memories, I can hold my own. If I can hold my own, I can put this one somewhere I never have to touch it.*

And she had. For fifteen years. She had built a life on top of the buried thing the way you build a house on a filled-in well, and told herself the water she tasted in everything was just the season.

* * *

Rafael came back to herself on the floor of her own workroom, with Odile's arms around her and her own voice hoarse from screaming a name.

"Sera," she said. "Her name was Sera. Is Sera. Is—"

"I know," Odile said. "I heard. I heard all of it." Her voice was shaking, but her arms weren't. "Rafa. Breathe. You're here. It's fifteen years later and you're here."

"She trusted me." Rafael's hands were fists against the floorboards. "She was twelve and she trusted me and I sold her. I didn't even—Odile, I didn't even hesitate. I said *take mine* knowing—I *knew*—"

"You were seventeen and the world was on fire."

"I knew." Rafael pushed herself upright. Her whole body felt scoured, raw, the way a limb feels when blood returns to it after too long asleep—painful, alive, unbearable. "That's the thing you have to understand about me, Odile. Everyone in this house thinks I'm careful. Kind, even. Tam thinks I'm kind. Brannic watches me like he's waiting for me to slip, and honestly he's the only one reading me right. I'm not kind. I'm *in debt*. Every charm I've ever sold, every rule I've ever kept—never steal from the desperate, never bottle my own past—I built all of it like a fence around that night. Like if I was just good enough, just disciplined enough, the ledger would balance." She laughed, wet and awful. "Fifteen years of anesthesia. And the whole time, the wards of this city were falling down around us, and the thing that bought my sister is waking up under the mountain, and I—"

She stopped. Went still.

"Odile," she said slowly. "The signature on the ward-stone. You said it was young work. Sloppy. Hurried."

"Yes—"

"How old did it feel?" Rafael grabbed her by the wrist. "The craft on those stones. When you read it. Was it recent? Days? Weeks?"

Odile frowned, and Rafael watched the answer arrive in her face before she spoke it.

"Older than that," Odile said. "Much older. Decades. I assumed it was you because it matched your hand, but the dating—" She paused. "Rafa. The blinding on those wards was laid down fifteen years ago. It's been sleeping in the stones this whole time. Something just woke it."

Rafael stood up. Her legs held, mostly. On the table between them, the little vial lay empty, and the last curl of smoke rose off it and vanished, and for the first time in fifteen years Rafael Vasquez could feel the whole shape of her past, sharp-edged and present, like a key she'd been sitting on without knowing it was a key.

"Fifteen years ago," she said, "the court let me walk out of Saltmere. It didn't let me go for free. Nothing in that place is free." She was already pulling on her coat, already reaching for the satchel of tools she'd sworn she'd never use for anything like this. "It seeded the wards with my own craft the night it took her. A door, left unlocked, waiting for the day it wanted back in. And three weeks ago, somebody turned the key."

Downstairs, the whistling had stopped entirely. Footsteps on the stairs—Brannic's heavy tread, and Tam's quick ones behind it—and Brannic appeared in the doorway with his old captain's face on, the one that meant bad news delivered at parade rest.

"You'll want to hear this," he said. "A buyer's been asking after you at the night markets. Hooded. Pays in old coin. And Rafael—" his jaw worked, "—it asked for you by name. Not the charm-seller's name."

He met her eyes.

"It asked for the girl from Saltmere."
