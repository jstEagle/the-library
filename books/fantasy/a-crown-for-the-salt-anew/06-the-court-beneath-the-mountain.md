## 6. The Court Beneath the Mountain

Brannic Hale had spent thirty-one years in the wardens' service learning one lesson above all others: when the records frightened you, read them again slowly, and then make tea.

He made the tea first. The kettle in Odile's workshop was dented and whistled off-key, like everything else in that house, but it boiled all the same. He measured out the leaves with a soldier's precision—two spoons for the pot, one extra because Tam would want some and Tam always wanted some—and while the water worked itself up to a temper, he laid out what he'd carried home from the wardens' remnant archive in a canvas satchel that smelled of dust and old decisions.

Three ledgers. A folio of loose reports, water-stained at the corners. And one sealed dispatch, marked with the black wax of the old Captain-General, which Brannic had never been authorized to open and had opened anyway on his last day of service, because retirement is when a man finally reads his mail.

The sky outside the workshop's high window was still wrong. Four nights now without stars, a darkness so complete it looked painted on, and the city beneath it had gone strange and quiet in its panic, like a dog that has decided not to bark at the thing in the yard.

Brannic poured the tea and began at the beginning.

* * *

The wardens' records did not begin with the court beneath the mountain. They began with a complaint.

That, Brannic thought, was very Corvel. The founding charter of the city was a memory—the founders had bottled their own vision of the place and set it into the first ward-stone, so that every wall and gate since had been anchored to a single shared act of remembering—and yet the oldest surviving document in the warden archive was a grievance filed by a salt merchant named Aldous Ferren, dated two hundred and eleven years ago, complaining that his daughter had gone down into the old mine shafts on a dare and come back up three days later "agreeable to an unnatural degree."

Agreeable. That was the word the records kept reaching for, over and over, across two centuries of reports. Agreeable. Compliant. *Contented.* Wardens' language was built for describing bandits and smugglers, and it kept fumbling against something it didn't have vocabulary for: people who came back from the mountain having lost nothing they could point to, and gained nothing they could seize, and were nonetheless unmistakably diminished.

Aldous Ferren's daughter, the report noted, smiled constantly, agreed with everything, and could no longer remember her mother's face, though her mother was alive and well and living upstairs. She remembered, instead, a hall full of chandeliers made of frozen light, where everyone was kind to her forever.

"She has been promised this," Aldous wrote, in a hand that shook. "She shows me the promise as though it were a coin she is owed. I cannot take it from her without taking everything."

The Captain-General of that era had sent four wardens into the mines. Three came back. The fourth, Warden Ilsa Voss, returned eleven days later with her lantern unlit and her eyes reflecting light that wasn't there, and sat down immediately to write the most detailed account in the entire archive before whatever she'd brought back with her finished settling into her.

Her account ran forty pages. Brannic had read it perhaps sixty times in his life, first as a cadet, later as a captain training cadets, and now, tonight, as a man with no rank at all reading by lamplight in a disgraced ward-smith's workshop while three other people slept badly in the rooms above.

He took a sip of tea and turned to the copy he'd made in his own hand, years ago, because there were things in Voss's report that the archive's official version had quietly trimmed.

*The mountain is hollow,* Voss had written. *Not mined hollow—hollowed, the way a gourd is hollowed, deliberately, for holding. The stair descends past the old workings into halls that were never cut by human tools. The walls are warm. This is the first wrongness. Everything below is warm, and everything above it is cold.*

*There is a court there. I will write what I saw and let wiser heads decide whether I saw truly.*

*The hall is lit by chandeliers of frozen starlight. I know how that sounds. I am telling you they were stars—not likenesses of stars, but actual starlight, caught and stopped mid-fall, hung in iron frames like fruit. There were hundreds. Thousands. The courtiers beneath them wore beautiful faces, and I understood only later, when one of them turned too quickly, that the faces were worn the way we wear gloves.*

*They received me courteously. They offered me wine, which I did not drink, and a chair, which I did not take, and a wish, which I nearly did.*

*This is the mechanism. Write it plainly so no one can pretend they weren't told: the court pays in wishes. Small ones, at first. A widow wishes her grief lighter; it is. A farmer wishes the blight off his fields; it is. The wish costs the wisher nothing—that is the genius of it, if you can call a trap ingenious. The payment comes due later, and it is never collected in coin.*

*It is collected in souls. Not killed—kept. A soul in that place is put to work. It burns. I stood in the great hall and felt them burning, hundreds of them, thousands, and the warmth of the walls is the warmth of the burned, and the cold above—*

Here Voss's handwriting deteriorated. Brannic knew the next lines by heart anyway.

*—the cold above is the harvest. You have wondered why our winters lengthen, why the frost comes earlier each year and stays later, why the old men say the winters of their childhood were kinder. I will tell you what the court told me, because it told me proudly, the way a merchant boasts of his ledger. The court does not endure the winter. The court makes it. Every soul it holds is fed into the long cold, and the long cold presses up through the stone and spreads across the land, and a colder world is a more desperate world, and desperate people wish harder, and wishing harder brings it more souls. It is not a court that suffers winter. It is a furnace that wears winter as a coat.*

*I escaped—I do not know how; I think it grew bored of me—but I did not escape clean. Something of me stayed below, or something of below came up with me. If I begin to agree with you too readily, if I begin to smile without cause, if I forget your face, Captain, I ask you to do what must be done.*

*The last thing the court said to me, at the top of the stair, was this: "We are patient. Winters are long, and people are short."*

Warden Ilsa Voss lived another nine years. By the accounts of her fellows, she was unfailingly agreeable. She smiled constantly. In her final year she stopped coming to muster, and when they went to her house they found it warm—warm in deep winter, warm as a hearth though no fire burned—and found her sitting contentedly by the window, watching the frost come down the glass, waiting, she said, to be called back to the hall where everyone was kind to her forever.

Brannic closed his eyes briefly. Then he opened the second ledger.

* * *

"You're doing the voice," said a small voice behind him.

Tam stood at the foot of the stairs in his nightshirt, hair sticking up in six directions, holding a blanket like a cape. He was fourteen and had the unerring instinct of his age for appearing exactly when adults believed themselves alone with difficult documents.

"I'm not doing any voice," Brannic said.

"You're doing your reading-out-loud-to-rookies voice. Low and grim, like the words might bite." Tam padded over and peered at the ledger upside down, which was his preferred method of reading anything. "Is that the mountain book? The one you said you'd tell us about 'when you're ready,' which is code for 'never'?"

"It's late."

"The sky's broken. Nobody's sleeping. Odile's up on the roof arguing with a ward-stone, and Rafael hasn't come down from her room in hours, which means she's either working or hiding, and with her those look identical." Tam climbed onto the stool across the workbench, wrapped his blanket around his shoulders, and held out his hand for tea with the entitlement of a much younger child. Brannic poured him a cup. He always poured him a cup. "So tell me about the mountain."

Brannic considered refusing. He considered it the way a man considers locking a door he knows is already open.

"Tam," he said carefully, "you told us once that you don't remember anything before the orphanage. Nothing at all. Just—" he gestured vaguely, "—kindness. That's what you said. You remember kindness."

"Best thing to remember," Tam said comfortably. "Most people remember worse."

"What if," Brannic said, and set down his cup, and made himself go slow, "what if the reason you don't remember isn't ordinary? What if there are ways of losing a memory that aren't just... time passing?"

Tam went still. It was remarkable, the way stillness changed him—he was all motion ordinarily, a boy made of elbows and questions—and in stillness he looked suddenly, terribly older, as though the missing years were pressing up under his skin looking for a way out.

"Like the mountain ways?" Tam asked.

"Like the mountain ways."

Tam looked down into his tea. For a long moment the only sound was the kettle ticking as it cooled, and somewhere overhead, faintly, Odile's voice swearing at a ward-stone in three languages, two of which Brannic suspected she'd invented specifically for the purpose.

"Okay," Tam said at last. "Tell me the rest. But do the voices."

"There's only one voice."

"Do the grim one."

Brannic sighed, and did the grim one, and told him the rest.

* * *

The second ledger covered the century after Voss. It was thinner than it should have been—whole decades missing, pages razored out with a care that suggested official hands—and what remained told a story of institutional forgetting that Brannic had once attributed to sloth and now, sitting in a starless night, read very differently.

Because here was the pattern, plain as a scar: every few decades, the wardens' attention to the mountain simply... faded. Reports thinned. Patrols near the old mine roads were quietly reassigned. A standing order from 140 years ago instructed wardens to log "anomalous contentment among mining families" and then, forty pages later, a marginal note in a different ink: *discontinue logging; see attached.* The attachment was gone. Of course it was gone.

"They forgot on purpose," Tam said, when Brannic showed him. "Or somebody made them forget."

"Wardens don't forget on purpose. It's the one thing we're for."

"Then somebody paid for the forgetting." Tam said it lightly, the way children say devastating things, and reached for the biscuit tin. "Who pays for forgetting, Brannic? Rafael pays for remembering. Who does the other job?"

And there it was, the question Brannic had been circling for four nights, ever since the wards fell with that sound like the whole sky being dropped from a height. Because he had spent his career around memory-magic at one remove—he'd arrested charm-sellers, confiscated bottles, testified at tribunals—and he knew the shape of the craft the way a man knows the shape of a knife by the wound. Memory-magic could take. It could give. It could blur, soften, redirect.

And someone had used it to blind Corvel's defenses, and someone else had used it, over two centuries, to make an entire institution look away from a hole in the ground that ate people.

"Rafael," Tam said, watching him. "You've got your suspecting face on. The one where you look like a door that won't open."

"I don't have faces."

"You have all the faces. You're a whole gallery." Tam bit his biscuit. "For what it's worth—and I know it's not worth much, because I'm the one person in this house who can't even suspect anybody properly, on account of having nothing to suspect them *with*—I don't think Rafael blinded the wards."

"Why not?"

"Because she's been miserable since it happened. Guilty-miserable, not caught-miserable. Those are different." Tam pointed the remaining half-biscuit at him. "Caught-miserable watches the door. Guilty-miserable watches the floor. She's been watching the floor for four days like it owes her money."

Brannic looked at the boy for a long moment. *Remembers nothing but kindness,* the matron had written on his intake papers, and the wardens' physicians had shrugged and pronounced it grief-shock, and everyone had moved on, because moving on was what institutions did best. But a boy who remembered kindness and nothing else, sitting in a city whose defenses had been blinded by memory-craft, asking sharp questions about who pays for forgetting—

"We'll come back to that," Brannic said, which was a coward's sentence, and they both knew it, and Tam generously pretended not to.

He turned instead to the third ledger, the thin one, the recent one. His own handwriting appeared in it, in fact, in the final entries—the last reports filed by the wardens' remnant before the collapse scattered them. And it was the third ledger that turned his blood from cool to cold.

* * *

The pattern in the third ledger was the harvest.

Every record of the mountain's activity—every spike of disappearances, every cluster of the agreeable, every winter that arrived early and overstayed—followed the same rhythm. The court fed slowly, patiently, a soul here and there across decades, the way a careful farmer rotates his fields. But roughly once a century, the rhythm broke. The disappearances clustered. The winters bit deep and lasted years. And each such period in the ledger was annotated, in various hands across two centuries, with variations on the same phrase:

*Great hunger observed.*
*The long cold advances beyond all precedent.*
*Harvest season. God keep the outer villages.*
*It is feeding for something.*

And beside the last annotation, in the Captain-General's black wax seal and his own cramped hand, a note Brannic had read only once before, on the day he opened mail he wasn't authorized to open:

*Voss's figures hold. Each century the hunger grows, because each century there are more souls above ground to wish, and more souls below to burn. The court is not sustaining itself. It is growing toward something. Find out what, before the sky goes dark. When the sky goes dark, it has begun.*

Four nights ago, the sky had gone dark.

Brannic set the ledger down with hands that were perfectly steady, because thirty-one years of service teaches a man that too, and looked out the high window at the painted-on blackness where the stars should have been.

Here was the arithmetic he could not stop performing. The court beneath the mountain held thousands of souls—it had held them for centuries, burning them gently, endlessly, to press the cold up through the stone. A long winter was not weather. It was appetite. And the starless sky meant the appetite had entered a new phase, meant the furnace had been stoked for a purpose, meant that somewhere below, in a hall lit by stolen starlight, something was preparing to spend everything it had saved.

Not one soul, then. Not Sera Vasquez, whoever she had been, wherever she was—not even primarily. One soul was a debt. This was a *season*. A harvest. Every wisher in Corvel, every grieving widow and desperate farmer and ambitious merchant who had ever wished a careless wish within reach of the mountain's cold—all of them were already written in a ledger none of them had signed.

And the worst of it, the part that sat in Brannic's chest like swallowed river-stone, was that the court had not needed to breach the walls to do it. It had needed only patience. Winters were long, and people were short. Two hundred years of small mercies, small wishes, small warm rooms in deep cold—and a city that had forgotten, on schedule, how to be afraid.

"Brannic," Tam said quietly. The boy had gone still again, blanket-cape and all, and his eyes in the lamplight were very clear. "The people in the mountain. The burned ones. Are they dead?"

"No."

"Can they be *un*burned?"

Brannic thought of Voss's account. *I stood in the great hall and felt them burning.* He thought of the warmth of the walls. He thought—and he hated himself for thinking it—of Rafael, watching the floor for four days like it owed her money, and of the bottled charms she sold in her little shop, harmless ones, she'd sworn, harmless as prayers.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But I know a woman downstairs who bottles memories for a living, and I know a smith who can read a ward like scripture, and I know that ledgers—" he tapped the iron-bound book in front of him, "—are only ever as strong as the belief that they can't be rewritten. So my answer is: nobody's tried. And that is not the same as impossible."

Tam nodded slowly, absorbing this, and then said, with the gentle tactlessness that was somehow his defining feature: "You're going to make us go down there, aren't you."

"I'm going to *ask*—"

"You're going to make your grim voice and lay out the maps and say 'I won't order anyone,' which means you're ordering everyone." Tam drained his tea and stood, blanket sliding off his shoulders. "Good. I was worried you'd try to leave us behind, and then I'd have to follow you anyway, and I'd get lost, because apparently I'm the only map of that place that exists, and I don't even have myself."

He was halfway to the stairs before Brannic's mind caught up with the sentence.

"Tam." His voice came out lower than he intended. "What did you say?"

Tam paused on the bottom step, one hand on the rail, and for a moment his face did something complicated—young and old at once, certain and utterly lost.

"I said I don't have myself," he repeated. "Isn't that what I'm for? Being the map?" He frowned, as if hearing his own words from a distance. "I don't know why I said it like that. Like I'd *been* there." He shook himself, boy-shaped again, and grinned. "Anyway. If you're making tea for the war council, make it strong. Odile swears when she's scared, and Rafael's floor-watching is getting worse, and somebody in this house has to be agreeable."

He clattered up the stairs.

Brannic sat alone with the ledgers and the cooling pot and the starless window, and performed, one final time, the arithmetic.

A court that paid in wishes and collected in souls. A hunger that grew each century. A harvest begun. And somewhere in the middle of it—a boy who remembered nothing but kindness, and spoke of the mountain's halls like a man describing his childhood home.

Brannic reached for the teapot, poured the last cup, and drank it standing, the way soldiers do when the news is bad and the morning will be worse.

Then he went upstairs to wake the others, because the records had one more thing to say, and it was this: the court did not send collectors for the willing. It only ever had to wait. Which meant that if Rafael Vasquez's sister was down there—if any of them were going down that stair—they would need to move before the mountain came up to collect what it had already been promised.

And from the room above, faintly, he heard Rafael's floorboards stop creaking. As if she'd heard him thinking.
