## 7. The Buyer with No Face

The buyer came back on the third night of the starless dark, and this time it knocked.

That was the first wrong thing. The first time—the night the wards fell—it had simply appeared in my shop like condensation on glass, hooded and patient and smelling faintly of cold iron. This time it stood in the alley doorway and rapped its knuckles against the frame, three polite little taps, as though it were a widow come about a charm for her late husband's boots.

I knew it was the same one. You don't forget a silhouette that doesn't quite fit the space it occupies. It was too still where a person would fidget, and the shadows pooled around its feet the way water pools around a stone that doesn't want to be moved.

"Closed," I said through the door. "Come back when there's a sky again."

Another knock. Softer. Almost apologetic.

I should have left it standing there. I want to be clear, in whatever account of this eventually gets written down by someone braver than me, that I *knew* better. Fifteen years in Corvel teaches you rules the way a riverbed learns the shape of water: never trade after midnight, never buy what weeps, and never—*never*—open your door to something that casts no reflection in the rain barrel.

It was raining. I checked. No reflection.

I opened the door anyway, because the alternative was standing in my own shop listening to it knock until I went quietly mad, and because—and I'll admit this only once, and only here—a small, treacherous part of me had been waiting for it since the stars went out. Hope is a stupid animal. It comes when you whistle for it even when you know the whistler means you harm.

"You," I said.

"Me," it agreed. Its voice was like paper being folded very carefully. Under the hood, where a face should have been, there was a suggestion of features the way a suggestion of a face appears in fog—or perhaps didn't; my eyes kept sliding off it, refusing to commit. Two hollows where eyes might live caught the lamplight and gave nothing back. "You are difficult to find, memory-seller. Your shop moves."

"My shop doesn't move. Your sense of direction does." I leaned in the doorframe, arms crossed, projecting a confidence I was renting at ruinous interest. "Also, I don't sell to things without faces. House rule. It's written on a little sign."

"There is no sign."

"I'm having it made."

The hood tilted. If it had had a mouth, I think it might have smiled. "You joke. Good. The court values those who can still joke. They last longer."

The word *court* went through me like a fishhook. I kept my face pleasant and my hand, very casually, near the shelf where I kept the charms I actually believed in—not the bottled kisses and lullabies, but the ugly ones: a scream I'd bought off a drowning man, a moment of pure white terror lifted from a fire. Weapons, if you knew how to throw them.

"What does the court want with a charm seller?" I asked. "We're small goods. Trinkets. A first kiss here, a grandmother's kitchen there."

"It wants the maker." The hollow eyes found mine, or performed finding them. "Your craft has a signature, Rafael Vasquez. We have seen it before. Long ago. In work much cruder than this."

The world did a slow, sick half-turn, the way a floor does when you've had bad news and bad wine in the same hour. My name. Not *memory-seller*, not *the woman on Salt Lane*. My name, spoken in a voice like folding paper, by a thing from beneath a mountain I'd spent fifteen years pretending was a story mothers told to keep children from the mines.

I laughed. It came out approximately one-eighth laugh and seven-eighths something else. "You have the wrong woman. Vasquez is a common name. Half of Corvel is Vasquez. There's a whole street of us."

"There is no such street."

"There could be. Cities grow."

"Rafael Vasquez," it said patiently, "of the village of Ash Hollow. Which burned. Which you left. Which no longer appears on any map, because maps are also memories, and we ate that one."

I stopped joking. That's how you know it's serious, when I stop joking; Odile says she can measure danger by how quiet I get, like a barometer with a flask of gin instead of mercury.

"How do you know that name?" I asked. Quietly.

"We keep books." The thing stepped forward, and I let it, which was the second mistake of the evening, though the first had been opening the door and the zeroth had been being born with a conscience shaped like a trapdoor. "Every bargain ever struck beneath the mountain is written in the Soul-Ledger. Iron pages. Ink that does not fade, because ink is only memory wearing a black coat, and we do not lose memories. We *keep* them." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down on silk. "There is an entry. Fifteen years old. Two names. One payment rendered: passage out of fire. One payment owed: a soul, lightly used, delivered in full."

My mouth said, "I don't know what you're talking about."

My hands, traitors both, had gone cold as river stones.

"The entry is signed," it said, "in the seller's own hand. Memory-magic leaves a signature, did you know? Like a fingerprint in wax. Yours has improved enormously since Ash Hollow. The early work was—" it paused, savoring, the way a critic savors—"enthusiastic."

*

Here is what I did instead of screaming or running or throwing the drowning man's scream at it, any of which would have been wiser: I made tea.

Because that's what you do, isn't it, when the abyss knocks and knows your name—you put the kettle on, because your hands need a job or they'll do something stupid, and because hospitality is armor of a kind, and because some stubborn peasant strand in me believes that no conversation over tea has ever ended in anyone's soul being collected. (This belief has since been corrected. But that night, it held.)

The buyer sat in my one good chair without appearing to sit, the way frost sits on a window. It declined tea with a small movement of the hood that managed to convey both gratitude and the information that it had not eaten or drunk anything in several centuries and had strong feelings about the matter.

"You want something," I said. "Things from under mountains don't make social calls. What is it?"

"Sraightforward. I admire that." It folded its hands—did it have hands? I genuinely could not tell you; my memory of that evening has gaps shaped exactly like whatever its hands were. "The court is engaged in a harvest. You have noticed the sky."

"Hard to miss. I keep checking whether it's just cloudy."

"It is not cloudy. It will not be cloudy again until the harvest concludes. The court feeds its winter on souls, and souls are gathered more easily from a city that cannot see the sky, because hope requires somewhere to point." The hollow eyes regarded me. "But the harvest has met an obstacle. A large one. Old. Sitting in the middle of your city like a stone in a shoe."

"The Archive of Founding," I said, before I could stop myself. Odile had been muttering about it for days—the great vault under the Warden's Hall where Corvel kept its founding charter, the city's first memory, pressed into crystal the way I press moments into glass. Every ward in Corvel drew from it, root-drawn. Kill the charter, and every ward in the city dies with it.

"You know of it," the buyer said, pleased. "Good. Then you know it cannot be taken by force. The Archive remembers everyone who enters, and it does not forgive. Wardens cannot approach it; their oaths are woven into its defenses. Smiths cannot approach it; their craft is known to it. But a thief—" the hood tilted, considering me the way one considers a lock—"a thief of memories, with a signature the Archive has never catalogued, might walk in where armies would burn."

"And why," I said slowly, "would I do the mountain's burglary for it?"

"Because we know where your sister is."

The kettle chose that exact moment to begin its low rising whine, and I have never been so grateful to an object in my life. It gave me ten seconds to arrange my face while I poured, and I used all ten, and I still don't think I entirely succeeded, because the buyer watched me with those hollows and said, gently, horribly:

"You thought she was dead. That is what you told yourself, wasn't it? It's easier. Dead is finished. Dead is a closed bottle." It accepted the tea I'd already poured it, holding the cup without drinking, steam curling up into the hood and simply stopping, as if the steam too preferred not to look. "She is not dead, Rafael Vasquez. She is kept. She serves in the winter hall. She wears beautiful memories that are not hers, and she is content, in the way a bird is content in a gilded cage that has removed its memory of flight. She does not remember you. But you could change that."

I sat down across from it. My legs decided this without consulting me.

"What's the price?" I asked. My voice came out level. Somewhere inside, the fifteen-year-old girl who ran out of a burning village was screaming so loudly I could barely hear myself, but the outside of me, the outside I'd built brick by brick over fifteen years, stayed level, and I want credit for that, whoever keeps these accounts.

"No price. An exchange. Steal the founding charter-memory from the Archive and deliver it below, and the Ledger will strike Sera's debt. Her soul returns to her. Her memories return to her. She walks out of the winter into the starless night, alive, whole, and free." The cup turned in its impossible grip. "And you, Rafael, get your sister back. Fifteen years of guilt, undone. One burglary. You've done harder jobs for less. I am told you once stole a soldier's courage for the price of a hot meal."

"That was a sale, not a theft. He wanted to forget it."

"All the more efficient." The hood leaned closer, and the cold coming off it smelled like deep places, like water that has never seen sun. "Consider what you are being offered. Not wealth. Not power. *Rescue.* How often does rescue knock on your door? Usually you have to dig it out of other people's heads and sell it back to them by the dram."

And here is the terrible thing, the thing I have turned over every night since like a stone looking for the soft underside: for one full breath—one honest, unforgivable breath—I wanted to say yes. Not because of the scheme, not because I trusted it, but because Sera was *alive*, and alive was a word I had buried with her, and you do not leave a word like that buried once someone digs it up and shows you it still breathes. For one breath I was fifteen again, running from fire, willing to trade anything, and the anything was sitting right there in my shop asking politely for a charter.

Then I breathed out. And I remembered rule two, the one carved under rule one: *never steal from the desperate.* And I realized, with a clarity that felt like falling, that I was the desperate one now, and the thing across the table was the buyer, and the whole elegant architecture of the offer—rescue dressed in reasonable clothes, guilt offered a ladder out—was built on one foundation:

They needed *me* to take it. Armies burn, wardens fail, smiths are known. But a thief with an uncatalogued signature walks in clean. Which meant the mountain, for all its iron books and frozen chandeliers, couldn't crack Corvel's oldest vault on its own.

Which meant it feared something in that vault. Or needed something. Or both.

"Why does the court want a charter?" I asked. "You have an entire winter. You have souls by the ledger-page. What can one old memory of farmers signing a treaty possibly matter?"

For the first time, the buyer hesitated. It was small—a stillness inside the stillness, a fold in the fold—but I have spent fifteen years reading people's pauses for a living, and I know a flinch when it doesn't have a face to flinch with.

"The court's reasons are the court's," it said.

"Mm." I sipped my tea. "See, that's the answer of something that's been told not to answer. Which tells me the charter matters *very* much. Which tells me that if I stole it, I wouldn't be buying my sister's freedom. I'd be trading the city's front door for one hostage in a house full of them." I set the cup down. "Sera's debt stays on the page either way, doesn't it? Strike it for the charter, and the harvest goes faster, and the next Rafael Vasquez sells her sister a few winters from now, and the Ledger gets a new entry in familiar handwriting. You're not offering rescue. You're offering me a discount on becoming you."

The hollow eyes regarded me for a long moment. Steam rose off the untouched tea and stopped.

"You misunderstand," it said at last, and there was something almost sad in the folding-paper voice, which frightened me more than the menace had. "I am not offering you a choice. I am offering you a *deadline*. The offer stands until the moon would rise, if there were a moon. Refuse, and the court takes the charter itself, by slower means, and Sera remains where she is, and the harvest continues, and your city learns what winter tastes like when it is *personal*." It rose, frost leaving the chair. "Accept, and come to the old Custom House on the salt quay when the bells would ring midnight, if there were bells. Bring the charter. Bring nothing else. Trust no one with what you intend."

"Everyone keeps telling me that lately," I said. "It's remarkable how consistently 'trust no one' precedes getting robbed."

At the door, it paused. And then it did the worst thing it did all night, worse than my name, worse than the Ledger, worse than *she does not remember you*: it reached up with one uncertain hand and lowered its hood.

There was no face. I had known there would be no face. But knowing prepared me for emptiness, a skull, a dark—and not for what was actually there, which was a face worn smooth, like a coin rubbed featureless by a thousand years of thumbs. And in the middle of the smoothness, faint as a watermark, was the ghost of an expression. Not menace. Not hunger.

*Pity.*

"She has your mother's laugh," it said. "The parts of her that are still hers. We could not wear that out, try as we did."

Then it stepped backward into the alley shadow and was not there anymore, in the thorough way that things from beneath mountains are not anywhere anymore, and the rain filled in the space where it had been, and I stood in my doorway holding a cold teacup with my heart going like a trapped bird.

*

I didn't sleep. I sat at my workbench with the lamp turned low and took out the false bottom drawer where I keep the charms that aren't inventory, and I looked at the deepest one—the little green bottle, sealed in wax, that I have not opened in fifteen years and opened once, and that was enough—and I thought about signatures.

Memory-magic leaves a mark. The buyer said so. My early work, it said, was enthusiastic.

And a thought arrived, unwelcome as a cat bringing in a bird, and it was this: the border wards hadn't failed on their own. Something had blinded them with memory-magic. Odile had been saying so for two days, turning fallen ward-stones over in her scarred hands with an expression like a woman reading her own obituary. *Someone fed them a forgetting,* she kept saying. *Wards don't die of natural causes.*

Enthusiastic early work. A signature the mountain recognized. A thief who could walk into the Archive uncatalogued.

I looked at the green bottle, and then at my own hands, and for the first time in fifteen years I asked myself a question I had carefully never asked:

What else had I done, back in the year of the fire, in the crude clumsy first months of my craft, that I had chosen to forget?

Outside, the starless dark pressed against the windows like a face at the glass. Somewhere across the city, in the Warden's Hall, the founding charter slept in its crystal, dreaming of farmers and treaties and a sky full of stars. And somewhere beneath all our feet, in a hall lit by the last frozen light, a woman with my mother's laugh was pouring wine for the thing that owned her, and did not know my name.

Midnight was coming—if there were midnights anymore.

I put the green bottle away unopened, got my coat, and went to find Odile.
