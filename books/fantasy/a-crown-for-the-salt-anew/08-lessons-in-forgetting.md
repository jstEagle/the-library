## 8. Lessons in Forgetting

Tam had three rules for remembering, and he'd invented all of them himself, which was the only kind of inventing he was any good at.

Rule one: write everything down. Rule two: when the writing doesn't help, ask someone who loves you. Rule three: when nobody's around to love you at that particular moment, make tea, because tea is a kind of loving you can do with your own two hands.

He was on his fourth pot of rule three when Rafael came down the stairs of Odile's workshop with a lamp in one hand and her whole face arranged into an apology.

"You're up," she said.

"I'm always up," Tam said. "That's not bragging. It's just true. Sleep and I are old acquaintances who nod at each other across a market square." He poured her a cup without asking. The tea was mostly ginger because ginger was cheap and mostly hope because hope was free. "Sit. You've got the look of somebody about to ask me for something difficult."

Rafael sat. She wrapped both hands around the cup like it might bolt. "Is it that obvious?"

"Rafael. I have known you four days, and in those four days you have lied to me twice, stolen from me once — my good pen, don't argue, I found it in your coat — and fed me soup when I was sad. You're the most readable person in this city." He slid his notebook across the table. It was fat as a well-fed cat, stuffed with scraps: receipts, letters, lists, little drawings of doors. "You want to know what I remember about the mountain."

Her breath went out slow. "Odile thinks the Ledger might be the key. If we can find Sera's name in it, we can find what she owes. But none of us has ever seen one. Except—"

"Except," Tam agreed, "the boy who used to live there."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere upstairs Odile dropped a hammer and swore with the fluency of a woman who'd been expelled from the Wardens' Guild for swearing exactly like that.

"I should tell you," Tam said, "that 'remember' is a strong word for what I do."

* * *

Here is what Tam knew about himself, in the order he'd learned it:

His name was Tam. Someone had given it back to him, years ago, a salt-wife in the lowtown who'd found him wandering the tideline with no shoes and no past and a smile that worked too easily. She'd called him Tam because he kept saying *tam, tam* like a drumbeat, some last syllable of himself rattling around in an empty barrel.

He had been in the mountain. He didn't remember being in the mountain. He remembered *around* it, the way you remember the shape of a room you've left — the draft under the door, the color of the light on the wall outside.

And he remembered kindness. That was the strange mercy of it. Whatever they'd taken from him in the court beneath the mountain, they'd taken it cleanly, and what grew back first, like grass through cobblestones, was kindness. The salt-wife said he'd smiled at her before he could say his own name. Brannic said it too, gruffly, the way Brannic said anything approving, which was like a man apologizing for a door he'd held open.

So when Rafael asked him to teach her how the soul-ledgers worked, Tam did what he always did with hard things. He made tea, and he opened his notebook, and he started with the parts that didn't hurt.

"The Ledger is a book," he said. "Iron covers, iron spine. Bigger than a bread box, smaller than a coffin. I know that much because—" he tapped a drawing on the page, a rectangle with hinges, done in wobbly ink, "—I drew this. Before. Or after. One of the whens."

Rafael leaned over it. Her fingers hovered above the paper without touching, the way she handled everything that mattered, like the world was made of charms that might go off. "You drew this from memory?"

"I drew it from whatever's left where memory used to be. There's a difference. Memory is a room. What I've got is more like... standing outside the room, feeling the heat through the wall." He turned the page. More drawings: a long hall, chandeliers hanging like frozen rain, a stair. Faces, blank ovals, because faces were gone but *arrangements* of faces stayed — who stood near whom, who was tall, who bowed. "I can't give you the halls themselves. But I can give you the habits of them. Courts are creatures of habit, Rafael. Even winter courts. *Especially* winter courts. Cold things hate changing."

"That's useful," Rafael said slowly. "That's actually — Tam, that's very useful."

"I know." He beamed at her. "It's the only kind of useful I am. The kind you get by losing everything else."

She flinched. He hadn't meant her to flinch. He reached across the table and patted her wrist, once, firmly, the way you'd steady a ladder. "Hey. No. None of that. I'm not a tragedy, I'm a map. Maps aren't sad. Maps are *directions*."

* * *

They worked until the candle drowned in its own wax.

Tam talked; Rafael wrote. He told her about the ledger-keepers — faceless ones, or nearly faceless, their features worn smooth like river stones, because a keeper who remembered his own name might start wondering about other people's names in the book. He told her the entries weren't written in ink but in *weight*: every debt recorded pressed its page down a little, and the older the bargain, the deeper the page sank, so that the oldest entries lived at the bottom of the book like stones at the bottom of a well.

"How do you read it, then?" Rafael asked. "If the pages sink?"

"You don't read it. You *weigh* it." He watched her face do the thing it did when she was working — eyes going still and bright, the rest of her forgotten. "A keeper carries a balance-scale. Sets it on a page, sets a soul-token in the pan, and the book tells you true or false. Debts are heavy. Paid debts weigh nothing at all."

"Paid debts weigh nothing," Rafael repeated, soft, like she was tasting it.

"That's the trick of it, isn't it? A bargain isn't undone by tearing out the page. Torn pages scream. Everyone in the hall hears it, and everyone comes running, and the running things in the dark halls are not friendly." He said it lightly, but his hand had stopped moving on the page, and he noticed that, and made himself keep moving. "No. A debt ends the way it began. Somebody pays it. Somebody puts enough weight in the other pan that the book lets go."

"What kind of weight?"

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Kindness first, always kindness first — but kindness wasn't the same as lying, and she'd know if he lied, because she was the most readable person in the city and apparently it was catching.

"Memories," he said. "Or love. They weigh the same, I think. Maybe they're the same thing, weighed differently."

Rafael put down her pen.

Upstairs, Odile's hammering had stopped. Footsteps crossed the floor overhead — Odile's uneven tread, then Brannic's heavier one falling in beside it, the two of them orbiting each other the way they did now, all suspicion burned off, just habit left. Tam liked listening to them walk. It sounded like a house.

"There's something else," he said. "And I need you to let me say all of it before you do the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you decide to protect me from the truth. It's a nice face. I hate it."

Rafael folded her hands and waited. Good. That was rule two, sort of — letting someone love you by listening.

"I don't remember my entry," Tam said. "But I remember the *sound* of the book. When a name gets written, it makes a noise. Like ice settling on a pond. And when I heard it — whenever it was, whoever I was — I remember thinking, right at the end, before the smooth came over everything—" His voice did something small and cracked, and he let it, because maps were allowed cracks, that was how you folded them. "I remember thinking, *but I never agreed.*"

The lamp flame stood very straight.

"They took a deal someone else made for me," Tam said. "Or no deal at all. Just took. And the book wrote it down anyway, because the book doesn't care whose mouth the yes comes out of. Only that somewhere, someone said it." He pushed the notebook toward her. On the last written page, in his careful round hand, he'd copied out the one phrase that had come back to him whole, word for word, in the night, sitting up with his heart hammering like a stranger at the door:

*A soul entered in good faith weighs double.*

"I don't know what it means," he said. "But I think it means the book keeps score of honesty. And I think — Rafael, I think it means if you go down there planning to trick them, the book will know, and the book will be heavy in your hands, and you'll lose. But if you go down there meaning every word—" He shrugged. "Then maybe the scales tip your way."

Rafael was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "What was your name? Your real one. Before."

And Tam smiled, because that was the easiest question anyone had ever asked him, easier than *how do you take your tea*, easier than *are you cold*.

"Don't know," he said cheerfully. "Isn't it wonderful? I get to be entirely new. Most people have to drag their old selves around behind them like a cart. I got a fresh start whether I wanted one or not, so I decided to want it." He stood, stretched until his back popped, and gathered the cups. "Now. You'll want the layout of the lower halls, and I can only draw it in order of how much I trust myself not to cry, so we'll start with the kitchens and work our way up to the bad rooms—"

The door banged open.

Brannic filled it, which was Brannic's way of entering anywhere, gray-bearded and grim as a headstone, and behind him Odile, and Odile was holding a ward-stone at arm's length like a dead rat, and her face was the color of paper.

"It's humming," she said. "The stone. It hasn't hummed since the wards fell, and now it's humming, and Rafael — it's humming *down*. Toward the mountain. Something down there just woke up and started counting."

Tam set down the teacups, very carefully, so they wouldn't ring.

Because he knew that sound too. Not the stone — the counting. Ice settling on a pond. Somewhere far below Corvel, in a hall he couldn't remember, a great iron book had opened itself, and a smooth-faced keeper had dipped a pen, and somewhere in the city above, a name was about to be entered.

He only hoped — with the whole of his small, rebuilt, secondhand heart — that it wasn't one of theirs.
