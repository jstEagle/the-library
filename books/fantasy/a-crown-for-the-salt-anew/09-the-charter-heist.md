## 9. The Charter Heist

The Archive of Founding did not look like the most important building in Corvel. That was, Odile explained as we crouched behind a rain barrel across the square, entirely the point.

"Look at it," she said, nodding at the squat granite pile with its mossy roof and its one flickering lantern over the door. "No wards on the windows. No guards but old Hesk and his bad knee. You know why?"

"Because nobody wants to steal a founding charter," I said.

"Because nobody *remembers* what's in there." Odile's voice had that flat, careful quality she got when she was about to say something that frightened her. "The charter isn't paper, Rafe. It's a memory. The founders poured the whole of Corvel's first night into it—the reason for the walls, the words that raised them, every name that gave blood to the border stones. It's the oldest, strongest memory in the city. And they keep it in a building with one lantern because the wards themselves make people walk past without looking."

I looked at the building. I made myself really look, the way I looked at a mark before lifting a charm off him—and there it was, faint as breath on glass: a softness in my attention, a little slide, like trying to recall a dream from childhood. My eyes wanted to skip from the archive to the bakery next door. Something in that ugly granite box was telling me, gently, *nothing to see here.*

"That's good work," I said reluctantly. "That's better than mine."

"It's not better than yours," Odile said. "It's just older. Craft gets smooth when it's been sitting a long time." She paused. "Which is why I know you'll be able to unpick it. Old craft is still craft. And you—" she pointed at me with a warding-chalk stub she seemed to carry everywhere now, like a cigarette she'd given up smoking, "—are the best thief of memories this city has ever produced, which is a sentence I hate saying out loud, by the way, given recent revelations."

"I said I was sorry."

"You said you were sorry about the *sister*. The general crime of being Rafael Vasquez remains an open wound." She said it dryly, but her hand found my shoulder and squeezed once, brief and hard, before letting go. That was Odile's way of forgiving people: insult first, grip second, never discuss it again.

Brannic cleared his throat from behind us, where he was failing to be inconspicuous. Brannic was built like a wardrobe and moved like one too—a large, honorable piece of furniture that had decided, against all advice, to go sneaking.

"Hesk does rounds at the half-bell," he rumbled. "Twenty minutes inside if we're lucky. Thirty if we're liars."

"We're always liars," I said.

"Then thirty minutes." He settled his shoulders under his borrowed dockworker's coat, which fit him the way a coin purse fits a cannonball. "And Rafael. If it comes to running, you run. I'm too old to run and too heavy to catch. It's the one advantage of my condition."

"Your condition is that you're enormous?"

"My condition is that I've spent forty years learning how to stand in doorways. Tonight I stand in a doorway for love." He said it without a trace of irony, which was somehow worse.

* * *

Getting in was almost disappointingly easy. Odile's chalk ate through the forget-ward on the service door like vinegar through limescale, and Hesk, bless him, was asleep at his post with a mug of something that smelled strongly of brandy and self-medication. We stepped over his boots. He snored. Somewhere above us, the starless sky pressed down on the city like a lid on a pot, and everyone in Corvel was pretending very hard that nothing was boiling.

Inside, the archive smelled of dust and cold iron and, underneath it, something sweeter—old magic, the way old wine smells of the barrel it forgot it came from. The main hall was a single vaulted room lined with shelves, and every shelf held bottles.

Not charms. Not the pretty little stoppered things I sold in my shop, each one tuned to a single moment, a single flavor. These were *kegs*. Barrels of memory, some taller than Tam, their glass gone dark with age, their contents shifting sluggishly like pond water under ice. The founding griefs of Corvel. The plague years. The fire that took the tanners' quarter. A whole city's worst nights, bottled so the living could get up in the morning and build something anyway.

"They stored the pain," I whispered. "All of it. Down here where nobody has to feel it."

"That's what archives are," Brannic said quietly. "Places to put the weight down."

We found the charter in the center of the room, on a plinth of black stone, under a bell jar the size of a coffin. And even filtered through the glass, even dulled by fifteen decades of careful forgetting, it hit me like a hand on the chest.

It was warm.

Every other bottle in the room was cold or neutral or aching. This one was *warm*—a hearth-warmth, a bread-in-the-oven warmth, the warmth of a hundred people standing together in the dark deciding to be a city. I could feel it through my gloves. I could feel it in my teeth. Inside that jar was the memory of the night Corvel's founders stood on bare rock and spoke the words that pulled the border wards up out of the ground, and they had poured it in so completely, with such love, that the memory hadn't faded. It had *ripened*.

"Oh," I said. My voice cracked on it. "Oh, that's—Odile, that's the most beautiful thing I've ever touched, and I haven't even touched it yet."

"Don't touch it yet," Odile said sharply. She was already circling the plinth, chalk out, reading the containment wards with her lips moving. "Beauty first, theft second. What did I teach you?"

"Plan the exit before the entrance."

"And?"

"And don't fall in love with the merchandise."

"See? Teachable." But she'd stopped moving. She was staring at the base of the plinth, and the color was draining out of her face in that particular way she had, like ink dropped in water.

"What," I said.

"Rafael." Her voice had gone very small. "Come look at the bottom of the jar."

* * *

There was a hole in the ward-glass.

Not broken—*eaten*. The edges were smooth, scalloped, delicate as lace eaten into cloth by moths. Someone—or something—had drunk through the containment the way I'd drink through a cheap charm on a slow Tuesday: patiently, invisibly, leaving no crack, no scar, no sign anything had happened at all. Just absence, shaped like a mouth.

And through that hole, the charter-memory itself had been fed upon.

I knelt and put my palm flat against the bell jar, and let myself read it the way I read everything—taste first, texture second, story last. The warmth was still there. But it was thinner than it should have been, the way soup is thin when someone's been ladling off the top for weeks and topping it up with water. Whole swaths of the founding night were simply *gone*: I reached for the faces of the founders and found smooth blankness where they'd stood; I reached for the words of the raising and found the shape of them, the rhythm, but the syllables had been sucked out like marrow from a bone.

Someone had been drinking Corvel's founding memory. Slowly. Carefully. In sips small enough that no warden, no archivist, no soul in the city would ever notice the difference.

"How long?" Brannic asked behind me. His voice had dropped into the register he used for casualty reports.

"Years," Odile said. "Decades, maybe. Look at the growth pattern—it's layered. Feed, rest, feed again. Like rings in a tree." She laughed, one short, horrible note. "Everyone thought the wards fell because the court beneath the mountain finally found a way through. But what if the wards didn't *fall*, Brannic? What if they were *hollowed out* first? What if somebody spent twenty years eating the strength out of them, sip by sip, until the night came when there was barely anything left to break?"

I wasn't listening anymore. Or rather, I was listening to something else.

Because I knew that feeding pattern. I knew that delicacy, that patience, that particular greed that takes only the best parts and leaves the structure standing so no one notices. I had invented half of it. Every thief in Corvel who bottled memories learned techniques that traced back, in one way or another, to me—to the girl who'd come out of a burning village with nothing but her sister's hand and a talent for taking what wasn't hers, and who'd gotten very, very good at it.

This was my craft. Older, smoother, colder—but mine. Or close enough to mine that the resemblance felt like looking in a mirror and seeing someone else wear your face slightly wrong.

"The buyer," I said.

"What?" Odile turned.

"The hooded buyer. The one who offered me a fortune to steal 'a soul, lightly used.' The one with no face under the hood." I stood up slowly, my knees cracking, my heart doing something arrhythmic and unpleasant. "When it talked, it talked about memory-craft like it *knew* it. Not like a scholar. Like a practitioner. And when I asked how it knew my work, it said—" I closed my eyes. "It said, *who do you think taught the mountain to want?*"

The silence in the archive was total. Even the dust seemed to hold still.

"So the court beneath the mountain," Brannic said, each word placed like a stone in a wall, "has been feeding on the city's founding memory for years. Through a servant. Who walks around above ground. In our streets. Offering jobs to the best memory-thief in Corvel."

"Yes," I said.

"And the starless sky, the fallen wards, the harvest of souls—that's not an invasion."

"No," I said. "It's a *harvest*. You don't burn a field you've been cultivating. You wait until it's ripe." I looked at the half-eaten charter, at the beautiful warm thing with the hollows scooped out of it, and felt vertigo open up under my feet—not fear of falling, but the sickening realization that the floor had been gone for years and we'd all been walking on air, held up only by the fact that nobody had looked down.

"The enemy isn't coming," I said. "The enemy has been here the whole time. Sitting at our tables. Buying rounds. Hiring thieves." I swallowed. "Maybe it hired me fifteen years ago too. Maybe everything since the village has been one long job, and I never once asked who was paying."

"Don't," Odile said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't do the thing where you decide you're responsible for every terrible thing that's ever happened because you're good at one terrible thing." She grabbed my wrist, hard, her chalk-dusted fingers pressing white into my skin. "Listen to me, Rafael Vasquez. Whatever taught the mountain to want—I promise you it was hungry long before it ever heard your name. Thieves don't teach hunger. Hunger finds thieves because hunger is *patient* and thieves are *available*." Her voice shook, just slightly, on the last word, and I understood that she was talking about herself too—about the disgraced ward-smith who'd once, in another life, built locks she'd later regret. "We are not the disease. We are the symptoms it likes to collect."

Brannic grunted. From Brannic, this was a eulogy, a battle speech, and a hug, all at once.

"Half-bell's passed," he said. "Hesk will wake soon. Do we take it?"

I looked at the charter-memory. Half-eaten. Hollowed. The founders' faces gone, the raising-words gutted. Everything left of the night Corvel was born fit in maybe two-thirds of a jar.

But two-thirds of a thing like that was still the strongest memory in the city. Still warm. Still *theirs*.

And the buyer had promised me Sera's location in exchange for it.

Here is the arithmetic I did in that moment, kneeling on the cold archive floor with my family around me and a stolen god's meal ticket under my hands: the buyer wanted the charter-memory. The buyer had been *drinking* the charter-memory for years. Which meant the buyer didn't need me to steal it—it needed me to steal it *openly*, legally, with witnesses, so that the theft would be recorded in the city's own ledgers as the work of Rafael Vasquez. So that when the harvest came, the city would tear itself apart looking for the traitor in its midst, and never once think to look beneath the mountain.

They weren't hiring a thief. They were hiring a scapegoat with excellent references.

"No," I said.

"No?" Odile blinked.

"We don't take it to the buyer. We don't take it at all." I stood, and something in my chest had gone very quiet and very clear, the way it used to go clear back in the village, back before, when Sera and I would split the last apple and I'd always, always give her the bigger half—before I learned there were other ways to divide things. "But we don't leave it here either, in a jar with a hole chewed in it, for that thing to come back and finish its meal. Odile. Can you move the containment wards?"

"To where?"

"To the shop. To the salt cellar under my floorboards, where I keep the charms I'd never sell." I put my hand on the bell jar one more time, feeling the diminished warmth pulse against my palm like a wounded bird's heartbeat. "If Corvel's going to survive whatever's coming, it's going to need its founding memory whole. And if I'm going to face whatever's under that mountain, I'd like to bring it something it can't eat."

Odile stared at me. Then she grinned—wide and wild and slightly unhinged, the grin of a woman who had lost her license, her reputation, and her faith in institutions, and had recently discovered this was enormously freeing.

"Steal the city's memory," she said, "in order to *save* it. From the thing that's been stealing it. While framing ourselves for a theft we're refusing to commit." She cracked her knuckles. "Rafe, this is the worst plan anyone has ever proposed to me, and I once worked for the Wardens' Guild."

"That's the spirit," said Brannic, and took up his position in the doorway, enormous and immovable, standing guard over three criminals and a dying memory while old Hesk snored ten feet away.

It took us nineteen minutes to move the jar. It should have taken five, but the charter-memory, half-eaten as it was, *did not want to go*—it clung to its plinth like a child refusing to leave a burning house it couldn't remember living in, and I ended up cradling the whole impossible weight of it against my chest, whispering to it the way you'd whisper to a spooked horse, telling it about the sky, about the stars that used to be there, about the people below who still needed the walls it had raised.

It calmed. Eventually. Memory always wants, more than anything, to be remembered by someone.

We were three steps from the service door when Hesk woke up, saw us, fumbled for his whistle—and then stopped, squinting at Brannic with the dawning, delighted expression of a man recognizing an old comrade.

"Captain Vane?" Hesk wheezed. "Saints' teeth, it *is* you! They told me you were dead!"

"Reports of my death," Brannic said gravely, "were filed by me."

And Hesk laughed, and waved us through, and asked no questions at all—which was, I understood as we slipped out into the starless dark with the city's founding memory warm against my heart, exactly the kind of small mercy the enemy counted on. Everyone in Corvel was so busy remembering kindly that no one was checking what had been forgotten.

We made it four streets toward the shop before I saw it: a figure in a hooded cloak, leaning against a lamppost that had gone dark with all the others, waiting for us with the patience of something that had waited twenty years already.

It raised one pale hand. Under the hood, where a face should have been, there was nothing at all—and then, horribly, briefly, there was *something*: a smile I recognized. My mother's smile. Worn like a glove.

"Ah," it said pleasantly. "You brought it to me after all. How efficient of you, Rafael. And here—" the smile widened, "—I'd prepared such a disappointment for you instead."

Behind it, at the end of the street, my shop was burning.
