## 10. Brannic's Choice

Brannic Halt had served the wardens of Corvel for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things that mattered.

First: paperwork is how cities remember. A city that forgets its own records forgets how to defend itself, and a city that can't defend itself becomes a story someone else tells.

Second: never trust a staircase you haven't counted.

Third — and this was the one he'd learned hardest, and latest, and least willingly — duty and honor are not the same animal, no matter how often people harness them together.

He was thinking about all three of these things at roughly four in the morning, standing in the Archive of Founding with a lantern held at arm's length, looking at a hole in the world's most boring wall.

"They came through the drainage culvert," said Warden Osk, who was young enough to be excited by crime. "Look at the mortar — they ate it. Softened it somehow. There's residue."

Brannic looked at the residue. It glittered faintly, like sugar mixed with crushed glass. He recognized the craft. Of course he recognized the craft. He had spent three weeks now eating soup across a table from the woman whose craft it was, watching her salt her potatoes with suspicious precision while Odile lectured about resonance frequencies and Tam arranged his spoon collection by how friendly the spoons looked.

Rafael Vasquez had dissolved the mortar of the Archive of Founding with memory-salts, slipped inside with a disgraced ward-smith and — presumably, because somebody had to hold the door — a retired captain of the wardens' third company.

Not him. He hadn't gone. He'd told himself that mattered. He'd sat at home in his small square house with his small square furniture, drinking tea like an honest man, and when the knock came at midnight he had answered the door, and Rafael had stood on his step with her hood up and said, "We need to know if the charter-memory is still in the east vault," and he had said, "I can't come with you," and she had said, "I know," and then he had drawn them a map anyway.

On the map he had marked the patrol rotations. On the map he had written, in his own careful hand, *east vault door sticks; lift then push*. He had done this because the charter-memory might tell them why the stars went out, and because Rafael's hands had been shaking when she asked, and because Brannic Halt was fifty-nine years old and had discovered, somewhere in the last month, that there were exactly four people alive whose shaking hands he could not bear to look at.

"Captain?" Osk was saying. "Captain Halt?"

"I'm not your captain anymore, boy. I'm a consultant."

"Yes sir. Sorry sir. What do we do, sir?"

*Boy*, Brannic thought, *you are going to be a very fine warden someday, and I am about to teach you your first terrible lesson, which is that the worst part of the job isn't the criminals. It's deciding which rules are load-bearing.*

"We secure the scene," he said aloud. "Nobody touches anything until I've walked it."

* * *

He walked it slowly, because walking it slowly gave him time to think, and he needed time, because what he found made the thinking genuinely difficult.

The break-in was clean. Too clean for amateurs, too strange for professionals. The east vault door had indeed stuck — lift, then push — and beyond it the warding on the charter-case had been unpicked rather than broken, thread by thread, with a smith's patience. Odile's work. She'd left the threads coiled neatly beside the empty case, which was either arrogance or apology, and knowing Odile it was both.

But here was the thing that stopped him mid-stride: nothing else was taken.

The east vault held eleven artifacts of founding-era significance. Brannic had catalogued them himself, twice, out of boredom during his last year of service. Eleven items, and ten remained, undisturbed, dust unbroken. Only the charter-memory was gone — and the charter-memory, according to every record Brannic had ever read, was nearly inert. It wasn't power. It wasn't a weapon. It was the city's oldest memory of itself, a founding moment bottled in lead and glass, and it was worth something only to someone who wanted to understand Corvel rather than rob it.

Which meant whoever broke in last night was trying to save the city, or thought they were.

Which meant Brannic knew them personally.

He stood in the cold vault air for a long moment, listening to the building settle around him like an old dog rearranging itself, and he thought about his oath.

He could still recite it. Every warden could. *I will hold the line between Corvel and the dark. I will keep the city's memory whole. I will report truly, even when the truth costs me.* Thirty-one years, and he had reported truly through two scandals, one mutiny, and the slow bureaucratic strangling of everything he'd once loved about the service. He had turned in his own mentor for taking bribes. He had turned in his own protégé for falsifying gate logs. His reputation was a wall built entirely of things like that, stone by stone, each one a person he had handed over because the rule said so, and the wall had kept him warm right up until the month the stars went out and the wall turned out to have no roof.

Because here was what thirty-one years had taught him about the dark outside the wards: it collected souls. It paid in wishes. And now it had put out the sky, and the wardens' remnant — forty-three aging men and women in a headquarters designed for nine hundred — had responded by holding meetings. Agendas were circulated. Subcommittees formed.

Meanwhile, a thief, a disgraced smith, a boy who remembered nothing, and a woman Brannic refused to describe as anything but complicated had actually *done something.*

And he had given them a map.

"Sir?" Osk again, from the doorway. "There's more. The night ledger."

Brannic followed him back up the counting stairs — twelve steps, he noted absently, same as always — to the duty room, where the night ledger lay open on its stand. It recorded everything: every patrol, every anomaly, every entry and exit logged by the ward-stones' tripwires. And there, dated last night, in the tidy automatic hand of the western tripwire-stone, was the problem:

*Culvert 7 breached. Archive perimeter crossed. Two signatures, unidentified. Wardens dispatched: none.*

The stone had seen them. The stone had written it down. All that remained was for Brannic to countersign the entry, file it, and let the machinery of the remnant grind into motion — inquiries, interrogations, a description circulated to every gate, and eventually, inevitably, a knock on the door of a charm shop on Salt Lane where a certain woman kept her lamp burning late.

Or not.

Osk hovered. "Countersign at the bottom, sir? Then I'll take it to Commander Iles first thing."

"Yes," Brannic said. "Give me a moment with it. Procedure."

There was no procedure that required him to be alone with a ledger. Osk nodded anyway, because Osk was young and believed senior wardens were honest, and went off down the corridor whistling something tuneless, and Brannic Halt stood alone with the book and understood that he had arrived, at last, at the place his whole life had been walking toward without telling him.

*Report truly, even when the truth costs me.*

The truth would cost him. That was the joke of it. Either way it cost him. If he signed, the cost was four names — because Osk was competent and would find Tam's footprints, would trace the memory-salts, would pull a thread that led through Salt Lane and a shared safehouse and ended with Brannic's own handwriting on a map entered into evidence. If he didn't sign — if he did what he was already, shamefully, already planning to do — then the cost was the last thing he had, the wall itself, thirty-one years of stonework pulled out from under him one piece at a time, and he would spend whatever years he had left knowing exactly what kind of man he'd chosen to become.

He thought about his mentor, whom he'd turned in. Good man, weak winter, took the bribes to feed his sister's children. Brannic had reported him truly, and the sister's children had gone hungry, and everyone had agreed Brannic had done the right thing, and Brannic had agreed too, loudly, for twenty years.

He thought about Rafael's hands, shaking on his doorstep.

He thought — this was the thought that decided it, though he'd deny it later — about Tam. About Tam two nights ago, solemnly presenting him with a spoon ("this one's called Gregory; he likes you"), and asking, in genuine puzzlement, "Why does everyone assume I'd want my memories back even if it hurt the people who took them? Wouldn't it be better to just make new ones with people who are kind?" And Brannic had said something wise and evasive, because the true answer was *yes, boy, probably yes, and the fact that it frightens me is my problem, not yours.*

Brannic picked up the pen.

Here is what a lifetime of paperwork teaches you about forgery: the amateur changes facts. The professional changes emphasis. You don't write that nothing happened — nothing happening is loud, in a ledger; it echoes. You write that something smaller happened. You adjust the size of the truth, not its existence.

He dipped the pen, and in a hand he had practiced into perfect anonymous competence over three decades of incident reports, he wrote beneath the stone's entry:

*Investigated. Culvert 7 breach assessed: structural failure of aged mortar, aggravated by frost. No perimeter crossing confirmed — stone signature consistent with rodent activity displacing residual salts (cf. incident log, 14th of Emberfall). Vault seals checked and intact. Recommend masonry assessment, Culvert 7, priority moderate.*

He read it twice. It was good work. It was, he thought grimly, some of the best work of his career, which told him something about his career.

Then he added the detail that would sell it, because a lie is only ever as strong as its most boring component: he took the key from the duty board, walked back down to the east vault, dragged his thumb hard along the charter-case's empty frame, and pressed his own thumbprint into the frost-dust on the floor beside it. When Osk or anyone else checked the seals — and they would check, because the charter-memory was listed as *present* in the registry — they would find the case sealed, the room orderly, and the senior-most investigating officer's prints exactly where a thorough inspector's prints should be. The registry would be corrected to *removed for preservation, pending review,* with Brannic's signature authorizing it. A clerical transfer. Dusty, dull, deniable.

The charter-memory would simply have moved, officially, into a filing limbo from which no alarm would ever sound.

He was signing that second document when Osk came back.

"Commander Iles wants a word, sir."

Brannic's heart performed a maneuver it had not managed since the siege of Fen Hollow. "About the breach?"

"No sir." Osk shifted, uncomfortable. "It's — odd, sir. There's a woman at the front gate asking for you by name. Won't give her surname. Says she's a consultant client. But sir, she's—" he lowered his voice, scandalized, "—she's brought *food.*"

* * *

Odile had brought a basket. This was so far outside protocol that the gate wardens apparently had no category for it, and Brannic found her standing in the entrance hall amid falling snow-ash, holding bread and pickled things and wearing the expression of a woman conducting a hostile inspection of a building she despised.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look like a picnic," he said. "What are you doing here?"

"Confirming you're still breathing. Also, Rafael is spiraling and won't say why, and Tam has eaten all the eggs, and I needed to leave the house before I said something permanent." Her eyes flicked past him, cataloguing the hall — the thin ranks of the honor guard, the water stains, the tripwire-stone conduit running up into the walls like veins. He watched her do it. Even carrying bread, the woman couldn't stop measuring defenses.

"The archive," she said quietly. "Was it found?"

"It was assessed," Brannic said. "Structural failure of aged mortar. Rodents, possibly."

Odile looked at him. Brannic looked back. Somewhere overhead the tripwire-stones hummed their low note, faithful and blind.

"Rodents," she repeated.

"Diligent ones. Very quiet. Left almost no trace." He paused. "Though I'm told they took only one thing, and only the thing they needed, and left the rest in better order than they found it."

Something in Odile's shoulders came down half an inch. It was, he suspected, the closest thing to gratitude she permitted herself in public.

"What did you find in it?" she asked. "The charter. When you read it."

And there it was — the reason she'd really come, wrapped in bread and insults. Brannic glanced at the nearest honor guard, well out of earshot, and lowered his voice.

"I haven't read it. They have. But Rafael sent word ahead, didn't she? Through you."

"She says it's worse than starlight being stolen." Odile's voice had gone flat, the way it did when she was frightened and converting fear into inventory. "She says the charter-memory isn't just the city remembering its founding. It's the founding *itself* — the original bargain. The first contract between Corvel and what lives under the mountain. And it's been altered. Someone's been editing the city's memory of its own deal, for years. Maybe centuries." She swallowed. "And she says it's half-eaten, Brannic. Something got to it before we did. Something is feeding on Corvel's past *inside the walls,* and the wards didn't even notice, because the wards are fed by the same memory they've been chewing on."

Brannic stood very still. Thirty-one years of holding lines, and the line, it turned out, had been quietly eaten from underneath while he polished it.

"All right," he said finally.

"*All right?*"

"That's the word I chose. I'm standing by it." He took the basket from her, because his hands wanted a job. "Go home. Tell Rafael the archive is clean, the record is quiet, and she has perhaps six days before the masonry assessment at Culvert 7 turns up anything interesting — less if it snows ash heavy enough to close the roads. Tell her to use them." He hesitated, then added, because loyalty apparently required him to keep paying for it in installments: "Tell her also that if she needs a door held, next time, she should ask me properly instead of sending a map. Maps get entered into evidence."

Odile studied him for a long moment, and he braced for mockery, or worse, thanks.

Instead she reached into the basket, produced a jar of pickled onions, and set it on top. "For the road," she said. "Consultant clients bring provisions. It's practically regulation."

Then she was gone, out into the ash-fall, and Brannic Halt climbed the counting stairs to the duty room — twelve steps, always twelve — where the forged ledger lay closed under his hand and the commander's door stood open down the corridor, waiting for a report he would now deliver with a straight face and a criminal heart.

Thirty-one years, and it had taken him one hour to burn it all down. The strange thing, the thing he would carry privately for the rest of his life, was how light the ashes felt.

At the end of the corridor, Commander Iles looked up from her desk and beckoned.

"Consultant Halt," she said. "Sit down. We have a problem, and I'm told you're the only one old enough to remember the mountain treaties." She slid a paper across the desk — a requisition, stamped urgent, from the western gate. "Three hours ago, a hooded figure presented papers at Culvert 7 and asked, by name, for the wardens who filed the breach report."

Brannic sat down slowly, the basket of pickled onions cold against his ribs, and prepared to lie to the last person in Corvel he'd never lied to before.

"What," said Commander Iles, "exactly did the rodents take?"
