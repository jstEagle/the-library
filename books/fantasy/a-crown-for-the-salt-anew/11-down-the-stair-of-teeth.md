## 11. Down the Stair of Teeth

The mine mouth looked like a wound that had half-healed and given up on it.

It sat in the hillside above the old quarry road, a black arch braced with timbers gone silver-grey, and someone — a hundred years ago, maybe more — had nailed a horseshoe over the entrance for luck. The horseshoe was still there. The luck had clearly moved out.

"That's it?" Brannic asked, in the tone of a man hoping to be told no.

"That's it," Tam said. He stood a little ahead of the rest of them, the way he'd been doing since we left Corvel before dawn, drawn forward by memories he didn't know he had until they started surfacing like drowned things. "There's a shaft behind the first gallery. And stairs behind that. Stairs going down a very long way."

"Stairs," said Odile. "In a mine."

"They weren't for ore." Tam's voice went carefully flat, the way it did when he talked about the mountain. He kept his hands in his pockets. I'd noticed he did that now — since the memories began coming back, he touched things less, as if he no longer trusted his hands to remember what they were for. "They were for taking people down."

Nobody said anything to that. There wasn't really anything to say. We had lanterns, rope, three days of food, one retired wardens' captain with an axe he swore he'd stopped needing, one disgraced ward-smith with a satchel full of tools that were mostly illegal, one boy who remembered nothing except kindness until last month, and me.

I had forty-one bottles of other people's lives in my coat, and I was beginning to suspect I'd need every one.

"All right," I said, because somebody had to, and it was apparently going to be me forever. "Rules. Nobody touches the walls without asking Odile. Nobody drinks the water. And if anyone hears their own name spoken from further down, they tell the group immediately instead of doing something brave."

"Do something brave" was aimed at Brannic, who had the grace to look mildly ashamed. "I've heard about you wardens."

"We're famous for it," Brannic admitted.

* * *

The first hour was ordinary mine. Ordinary is a generous word — it was dark and cold and smelled of wet stone and old iron — but at least it was honest dark, the kind that meant only that the sun couldn't be bothered. Tam led us past collapsed galleries and a rusted winch, turning left where there was nothing to turn left toward, right where the passage plainly wanted him to go straight, and every time, when we followed, the way opened.

"How do you know?" Odile kept asking, and kept not quite being able to help herself.

"I don't." Tam touched the wall once — with permission, I'll note, he was better at rules than any of us — and pulled his hand back like he'd expected it to be warm. "It knows me. Or it knew me. It's like walking through my own house with the furniture rearranged. My feet argue with my eyes, and my feet are winning."

I knew what that was like. I'd spent fifteen years making sure I didn't.

The shaft came sooner than I wanted it to: a square throat in the floor of the gallery, rimmed with iron, with a ladder bolted inside it going down into blackness. And around the rim of that throat, set into the iron like teeth in a jaw, were wards.

I should say here that I have looked at a great many wards in my life. I've picked them, sold them, bottled the memories of the smiths who made them. Wards have accents, the way people do. A city ward-smith lays her lines confident and square, trusting the grid beneath her. A country hedgewitch knots hers like braids. Odile's work — I'd seen it by then, in her workshop above the bakery — was all long patient spirals, work done by someone who expected to still be maintaining it in thirty years.

These were none of those things.

"These are wrong," Odile breathed, crouched at the rim, her lamp held close. She said it the way you'd say *the milk smells off* — professional dismay, almost personal insult. "Look at the anchoring. They're not tied to the stone at all, they're tied to—" She stopped. Her face did something complicated.

"Tied to what?" Brannic asked.

"Sound." She glanced up at me, and I saw she'd already worked out the rest and hated it. "Not sound. Screams. These aren't ward-stones. They're bottles."

I got down beside her and looked properly, and she was right, and I wished with everything in me that she wasn't. Dozens of small vessels, glass gone smoky with age, each one sealed with wax and a sigil, each one holding — I could feel it from a hand-span away, the way you feel heat off a stove — a scream. Bottled at the moment of its making, still screaming, forever, quietly, into the lattice that fed the ward above it. A scream is the loudest memory a person has. It carries farther than love. I'd known that academically for years. Every charm-seller does; it's why courage sells better than joy.

"Can you disarm it?" Brannic asked.

"Don't touch it," I said, before Odile could answer. My voice came out strange. Flat. "Don't pick it, don't cut the wax, don't do anything clever. If you crack even one bottle the whole ring discharges everything it's stored at once, and we will be a smear." I swallowed. "We go around. There's a gap on the north side — see? The ladder's offset. Someone wanted to get down without walking through their own work."

"You sound very sure," Odile said slowly, watching me.

"I am very sure." I took a breath, and then, because lying to Odile had stopped working somewhere around the time she caught me with a fallen ward-stone in my hands, I said the true thing. "Because I recognize the seal-work. That's a Vasquez closure knot. My grandmother's pattern. My version of it."

The lamplight wobbled. I don't know whose hands were shaking; everyone was holding a lamp.

"Rafael," Tam said gently, "you've never been down here."

"No," I agreed. "But I taught a lot of people to make charms, before Corvel. Before I got careful. Refugees on the roads, mostly. People who needed to hide what they carried. You teach a starving girl to bottle her grief so the soldiers can't smell fear on her, you don't ask what else she learns to bottle." I looked at the little smoky jars, and the little smoky jars, in some sense I can't explain and won't defend, looked back. "The court didn't invent this. It learned it. From us. From people like me, who found out that pain keeps better than milk."

Nobody said *it isn't your fault*, which I was grateful for. It would have been true and useless, and these were people who had learned the difference.

* * *

The stairs were exactly as bad as Tam had promised.

They descended in a spiral so tight we could see our own lantern-light from above and below at once, which does something to your sense of how many of you there are. The steps were worn hollow in their centers — worn by feet, thousands upon thousands of feet, all going the same direction, and I tried not to think hard about which direction most of those journeys had ended in. Tam walked in front with his hand on the wall, and after a while he started talking, and we let him, because the alternative was listening to the stairs.

"There were hooks," he said, conversational as anything. "Along the outer wall. For coats, I thought at first. Little brass hooks, very polite." His voice stayed pleasant. That was the worst part. "They weren't for coats. You hung your name on your hook. That's what the attendants said. *Hang your name here, you can fetch it on the way out.* Everyone did, because everyone believes they're the exception." He paused on a step. "I hung mine on hook number nine. I remember the number now. Isn't that funny? I can't remember my mother's face but I can remember the number."

"Nine," Odile repeated softly, and I understood she was writing it somewhere in herself, the way she wrote ward-lines — filed against future use.

"What was your name?" I asked, very quietly, because it seemed suddenly monstrous that none of us knew.

Tam considered this all the way down two full turns of the stair. The lanterns hissed. Somewhere far below, something that might have been wind or might have been a hall full of people breathing said otherwise.

"Tam," he said finally. "That's the joke, isn't it? They took everything and left me with the word for what I was missing. Some miner gave it to me, after. Said I looked like a boy named Tam he'd once known." He laughed, a small astonished huff. "He was right. He just had the order backwards."

Brannic put one enormous hand briefly on top of Tam's head, ruffling his hair like a man patting a dog because he cannot safely pat a boy, and said gruffly, "Hook nine doesn't get to keep you," and that was the most eloquent thing any of us managed for another hour.

* * *

Two hours down, the bottles started again, and this time I knew the maker personally.

They lined the stair-walls now, hundreds of them, set in niches like candles in a shrine, and the seals had progressed. The early ones — up near the shaft — had been crude: thick glass, sloppy wax, the closure knot drawn by a shaking hand. These were fine work. Delicate. The knots flowed. And the progression between the two ran down the wall beside me like a timeline of somebody's apprenticeship, and I knew whose, because the middle section — the part where the craft turned competent, where the knots stopped shaking and started singing — was work I would have sworn in front of a magistrate was mine.

Except I had never been here. Except the hands that made these had had fifteen years and infinite material to practice on.

"Stop looking," Odile murmured at my shoulder.

"They learned from me."

"They learned from a frightened girl on a road with soldiers behind her. That's a different person. I've met her." Odile's voice was dry as ward-dust. "She's grown up into somebody who pours her hoard into a book to save her sister, if the plan holds. Whatever the court copied, it copied the draft, not the final."

I wanted to believe her the way you want air. I kept walking, and I kept looking anyway, because it seemed like a debt I owed — to look at every bottle, every niche, and know what my cleverness had become in worse hands. Halfway down a long flight I stopped, because one seal was different. Newer. Fresh wax, white as bone, and under the sigil, scratched into the glass in a hurry:

*RAFAEL VASQUEZ — RESERVED.*

My name. In the mountain. Waiting for me, the way a bed is turned down in an inn that expects you.

"Well," said Brannic, reading it upside down with the fatal calm of an ex-warden, "on the bright side, we're expected."

* * *

Near the bottom, the stair opened its jaw.

We came out onto a ledge overlooking a space so large the lanterns failed it — a cavern where the dark had texture, where distant lights moved in patterns too regular for torches and too graceful for machinery. Below and ahead, across a floor that gleamed faintly like ice, stood columns, arches, the suggestion of halls beyond halls. Cold poured upward out of it, the specific patient cold of deep winter rather than deep earth, and it carried sounds up to us: music, very far away, and underneath the music a vast slow rhythmic sound like a loom, or a heart, or a ledger's pages turning.

And along the ledge where we stood, running the whole curve of the cavern wall, was the last row of bottles — the newest, the finest, the masterset of whatever cruel curriculum had been running in this mountain for longer than Corvel had been a city. I raised my lantern along it, meaning only to count exits, and stopped.

Every bottle in the final row held starlight.

Frozen, chipped, dimmed almost to nothing, but unmistakable — the same light I'd seen over Corvel harbor every night of fifteen years, the light that had vanished from the sky five weeks ago. Hundreds of stars, jarred and shelved like preserves, and the labels beneath them written in a hand I recognized, because it was neat and slanted and it spelled *Vasquez* in the old village spelling, with the little flourish my mother used to add to the z.

The court hadn't just learned from thieves like me.

Somewhere in its long hunger, it had learned from my family specifically — or it had gone looking for a Vasquez seal the night the sky went dark, and found one it liked, and kept it. Either way, the stars of Corvel were sitting in jars with my name on them, and the hall below was waiting, and Tam was already three steps onto the ice, walking toward hook number nine like a man coming home.

From somewhere beneath the music, clear across all that frozen distance, a voice that sounded heartbreakingly like my sister's called out, warm and welcoming and utterly empty:

"Rafael. We kept your place."
