## 12. The Winter Court

Odile had rebuilt wards for twenty years before Corvel took her license, and in all that time she had never once been cold the way she was cold now.

It wasn't the temperature. Well—it was partly the temperature. The stair of teeth had ended in a corridor of blue ice that breathed, somehow, a slow glacial exhale that crept through every seam of her coat and settled into the marrow of her hands. But the true cold was older than weather. It was the cold of a room where nothing had ever been allowed to change. She could feel it through her boots, through the stone, through the thin ward-lattice she'd woven around the four of them like a shawl: the stillness of a thing that had decided, centuries ago, what it was going to be forever.

"Tell me again," Brannic murmured behind her, "why we didn't bring more lamps."

"Because lamps announce us," Odile said, "and because you were saving your coin for ale."

"I was saving my coin for *retirement*."

"You retired into a mountain full of stolen souls, captain. Adjust your expectations."

Tam, walking between them with the unbothered gait of someone who had never learned to be afraid of anything larger than a goose, reached back and patted Brannic's arm. "I think it's pretty," he said. "The ice. It's like the inside of a bell."

And it was, Odile had to admit. That was the horror of it. The corridor opened without warning into a hall so vast that their little lamp seemed to shrink to an ember, and the ceiling—no, there was no ceiling, there was a *sky*, an inverted sea of dark glass—and hanging from that sky were chandeliers.

Not crystal. Not candlelight.

Stars.

Frozen starlight, hundreds of them, each one caged in a lattice of black iron shaped like a snowflake's skeleton, each one burning with the small desperate light of something that used to be free. They hung at every height, some near enough to touch, some so distant they might have been memories of stars rather than stars themselves. Their light fell on the hall below in long silver stripes, and everything they touched looked preserved. Looked *kept*.

"Oh," Tam said softly. "Oh, no. I know this light."

He stopped walking. Odile felt him stop more than saw it—a small hitch in the weave of her protective lattice, like a hand tightening on a rope.

"This is the light from my bad dream," Tam said. "The one I have when I'm not having good dreams." He laughed, which was worse than crying. "That's a joke. Mostly."

Rafael put her hand on his shoulder. Odile watched her do it—the thief's hand, quick and clever, gone suddenly careful, resting on the boy like he was made of the same glass as the sky.

"Stay close to me," Rafael said. "Whatever you remember next, stay close."

* * *

They moved along the wall, because walls were where Odile had always done her best work, and because the center of the hall belonged to other things.

Courtiers, for one.

They stood in clusters among pillars of ice veined with silver, and they were beautiful in the way that Odile supposed drowning men were beautiful to other drowning men: flawless, symmetrical, utterly still until they moved, and then moving with a grace that had no warmth in it anywhere. Faces like portraits. Hands folded. And the faces—

"The faces are wrong," Brannic breathed. He'd gone gray around the mouth. "Odile. The faces are wrong."

She'd seen it too. A courtier near them turned his head to follow their progress along the wall, slow as a weathervane, and his face turned *with* the head but not quite *on* it, a half-second lag, like a mask settling. Like gloves being pulled on by someone who couldn't quite feel the fingers.

"Borrowed," Odile said quietly. "Every face in this room is borrowed. Look at the seams." She nodded toward a woman whose cheek caught the starlight just wrong—there, at the jaw, a faint line like the join of a well-made thing. "Ward-smith's eyes, captain. You wear a face long enough and it starts to fit. These haven't finished fitting yet. Or—" she swallowed, "—they're wearing them over something else entirely."

"Over what?"

"Do you want the honest answer or the kind one?"

Brannic looked at her with the expression of a man who had spent thirty years asking honest questions of dishonest institutions. "We came down a stair of teeth," he said. "Honest."

"Over the holes where their own faces used to be."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. The hall said it for them: a sound like wind through a keyhole, vast and thin, rising and falling. If Odile hadn't known better she'd have called it music. Court music. Somewhere, unseen, something was playing a glass instrument, and every note landed in her chest like a small cold stone.

"That's the orchestra," Rafael whispered. "Or what's left of one. Don't hum along, whatever you do. Tam—especially you. Don't hum along."

"Why would I hum along?"

"Because it's lovely," Rafael said, "and that's how it gets you."

Tam considered this with the seriousness he brought to all instructions, which was total, and then said, "What if I whistle instead? Whistling isn't humming."

"*Tam.*"

"Fine. No whistling. This is very hard for me, you should know. I whistle when I'm nervous. It's my whole personality."

"It is not your whole personality," Odile said. "Your whole personality is kindness and an alarming knowledge of geese. The whistling is just the part people notice first."

Something loosened in the four of them at that—not warmth, exactly; the hall wouldn't permit warmth—but a knot coming untied, a reminder that they were still people who teased each other, still a *them*, even here. Odile held onto that. She had a professional suspicion that down here, holding onto being a *them* was going to matter more than any ward she could cut.

* * *

The Soul-Ledger sat on a dais at the far end of the hall, and Odile felt it before she saw it.

Every ward-smith knows the sensation: the pressure of a large working nearby, a hum in the teeth, a tightness behind the eyes. Wards sing, if you've trained yourself to hear them. Good wards sing in tune. What sat on that dais did not sing. It *ground*, like a millstone turning somewhere beneath the floor of the world, and everything in the hall turned slowly around it, courtiers and chandeliers and the frozen sky itself, all of it orbiting a book.

An iron book, chained to its stand by nine chains, each chain thick as her wrist. Its cover was worked with figures too worn to read—hands passing something between them, over and over, a relief repeated a thousand times across the binding until the metal had gone smooth and dark with handling. Its edges were furred with frost that did not melt. Frost that grew, Odile realized as she watched, very slowly, the way a wound scabs. The book was healing itself. Or sealing itself. Or digesting.

"There it is," Rafael said. Her voice had gone flat and focused, the voice Odile had learned meant the thief was fully awake behind her eyes. "There's the ledger."

"How can you tell from here?" Brannic asked.

"Because I sold something to it fifteen years ago," Rafael said, "and I can feel it reading me back."

Odile went still. Of all of them, only she knew what lived in Rafael's deepest bottle—knew because she'd forced it out of her, weeks ago, in a room above a shuttered shop, with salt on the sill and shame in the air. Sera. The fire. The bargain. Rafael had spoken the words like a woman describing a scar she'd refused to look at for fifteen years, and then had asked Odile to help her stopper the memory again, and Odile had refused, and they had fought, and then—because they were who they were—they'd made tea, and neither of them had ever mentioned it again.

But Rafael was mentioning it now, in her way. Standing in the winter court, looking at the iron book, and saying *I sold something to it* out loud, in front of Brannic and Tam, in a voice that didn't shake.

Odile found she had to look at the floor for a moment. Ward-smith's discipline. Feelings later. Workings now.

"Right," she said, when her voice was steady. "Then let's do what we came to do. Rafael—you're on names. Get close enough to the Ledger to find Sera's entry, and don't touch it. Not yet. Whatever you're planning, don't touch it yet." She pointed at the dais, then traced the sightline up. "Brannic—count guards. Real ones, not courtiers. Courtiers are decoration; decoration doesn't stop thieves. Tam—"

Tam raised his hand like a schoolboy. "Yes?"

"You know this place. From before. Your memories are coming back the closer we get—I can see it happening, you keep touching your own ears like they belong to someone else. So you're on *wrongness*. Walk with me and tell me everything that doesn't match what you remember. Every door that's new, every corridor that's sealed, every spot where your skin crawls. You're not a map, Tam. You're a map of changes. And changes mean defenses."

"And you?" Rafael asked.

"I'm going to do the thing I was actually licensed to do, before Corvel took the license away," Odile said. "I'm going to stand in a room full of hostile workings and take notes."

* * *

Taking notes, it turned out, meant standing very still for a long time in a hall where stillness was the native language, and letting the workings show themselves.

Odile had a method. She'd invented it during the border years, mapping smugglers' wards for the wardens before the wardens decided her methods were theft-adjacent and her fees were extortionate, which—in fairness—was half true. The method was patience. You picked a working apart the way you'd pick a lock: slowly, by feel, listening for the pins. You never touched. You watched what the magic *did* when it thought no one was doing anything.

So she stood against a pillar of veined ice, half in its shadow, and she watched.

First: the chandeliers. Each frozen star sat in its iron cage, and each cage was anchored—she followed the anchor-lines with her eyes until she could see them, faint as spider silk, running upward into the dark glass sky. Hundreds of lines. Thousands. The whole ceiling was a net, and every star was a knot in it, and the net gathered downward, funneling, converging on—

The Ledger.

Her stomach dropped. The starlight wasn't decoration. It was *harvest*. The court had pulled the stars out of Corvel's sky and hung them here, caged, and the cages drained them, drop by drop, feeding the light down those silk-fine lines into the iron book. The endless winter outside wasn't the court's cruelty. It was the court's *pantry*. Every night without stars was a night the book grew fatter and the frost on its edges climbed higher.

"They're not keeping the stars," she breathed. "They're eating them. Slowly. While they're still alive."

"Eating them," Brannic repeated flatly. "Stars."

"Light is just another kind of memory, captain. Ask any child who's ever tried to hold onto a summer evening. The court figured out how to bottle it." She was already writing, scratching notes into the wax tablet chained inside her coat, her fingers stiff and stupid with cold. "Which means the sky comes back when the book stops feeding. Which means we don't have to fight the winter. We have to fight the *book*."

Second: the courtiers. She watched them for the better part of an hour, and the pattern emerged the way patterns always did, slowly and then all at once. They weren't guards. They were *anchors*. Every courtier stood within sight-line of the dais, spaced with a regularity no natural crowd would ever manage, and faint threads ran from each of them—from the borrowed faces, from the smooth places at their throats where a pulse should have shown—to the book's nine chains. The courtiers were living guy-wires. The book was held taut by the souls wearing the faces.

"You can't cut the chains," Odile wrote, and underlined it twice. "Cutting a chain snaps its thread back into whoever's wearing it. Nine chains, dozens of anchors. You'd shred every soul in the hall including ours. The book has to be *unwritten*, not broken. Fed something it can't digest. Overwritten."

She didn't know yet how. But knowing *that* was worth more than steel.

Third—and this was the one that made her hands shake, so that she had to press the tablet against her ribs to steady it—third was the floor.

Beneath the dais, beneath the frost-furred iron, the stone itself was veined with channels, hair-thin, glowing faintly with a light that wasn't starlight. Colder. Bluer. She tracked the channels outward and understood, with the particular nausea of a craftsman recognizing another craftsman's fingerprints, what she was looking at.

Bottled screams. The stair of teeth had been lined with them—wards made from bottled screams, crude, early work, the clumsy handiwork of a young thief learning her trade. Rafael had recognized them on the stair and gone white as the ice. Now Odile saw where the trade led. The channels in the floor carried the same material, refined past recognition, drawn up through the stone and into the book itself. Pain, distilled. Grief, decanted. The court hadn't invented its cruelty. It had *commissioned* it, generation after generation, from people like Rafael—people who could bottle a feeling, who needed coin, who told themselves the bottles were harmless.

"We taught it this," Odile wrote. "Corvel's thieves taught the mountain to eat feelings. We built its appetite and called it craft. Remember this, O.M., when you start feeling righteous. You licensed half these techniques before they took your name off the rolls."

She underlined that three times.

* * *

Rafael was gone most of that hour.

She'd slipped along the wall with the silence that was her actual gift—not stealth, exactly, but a way of moving that made the hall forget to notice her, the way a room forgets a piece of furniture. Odile tracked her by the tiny perturbations in the lattice: a flicker here, a lean there, the protective weave stretching and reforming as its maker moved through the hostile air. Twice the lattice snagged on something Odile couldn't see, and twice Rafael doubled back and took another line, patient as water finding its level.

The third time, the snag didn't release.

Odile felt it happen—a hard pull on the weave, a note struck somewhere in the deep registers of the hall's great grinding hum, a single discordant clang like a spoon dropped in a bell. And then, from the direction of the dais, a voice.

Not the court's voice. Not the hollow courtiers, not the glass orchestra. A voice Odile knew, thin and human and furious with cold, calling across the frozen hall:

"*Don't come closer. Any of you. It knows you're here.*"

Rafael, kneeling in the star-striped shadow before the dais, one hand hovering a finger's width above the iron cover of the Soul-Ledger, not touching, never touching, reading it the only way a thief reads a thing too dangerous to open: by the light of what leaked through its seams.

And leaking through those seams, Odile saw—everyone saw, the whole hall saw, the light spilling up between the pages like dawn through a shut door—was a name.

Written in the book's own iron script, in letters of banked ember-glow, in the column of debts fifteen years old:

**SERA MARCHETTI. ONE SOUL, LIGHTLY USED. PAID IN FULL.**

Paid in full. Paid by Rafael Vasquez, fifteen years ago, with everything she had, to buy a road out of a burning village. The debt wasn't owed anymore. That was the worst of it. The bargain had been kept, scrupulously, the way such courts kept bargains—every clause honored, every coin delivered, and a girl's soul filed away like a deed of ownership, accruing interest in the dark, wearing finer and finer gloves.

Rafael knelt there with her finger hovering, reading her sister's name in a book of iron, and Odile watched her face do something Odile had never seen it do in all the months they'd sheltered together: go completely, perfectly calm. The calm of a person standing at the exact center of the thing they've been walking toward their whole life.

"She's here," Rafael said. Her voice carried strangely in the cold, small and clear. "Fifteen years, and she's forty feet away, and the book says paid in full, and I am going to make that the biggest lie this mountain has ever bought."

The discordant clang faded. The hall's hum resumed. And Odile, pressed against her pillar with her tablet full of workings and her heart full of something that had no professional name, wrote one last line in the wax:

*Found her. Now find the way.*

Then she blew the frost off her fingers, tucked the tablet away, and started walking toward the dais—toward Rafael, toward the name, toward the nine chained pages of everything the winter had ever stolen—because the method was patience, yes, but patience had a limit, and hers had just been reached.

Behind her, Tam began to whistle.
