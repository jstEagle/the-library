## 13. Sera, As She Is Now

Rafael had rehearsed this moment for fifteen years without knowing it.

She had rehearsed it in the dark behind her eyelids, in the half-second before sleep took her: *I will find her. She will be cold and afraid and I will say* — what? *Sera. Sera, it's me. I came back.* And in the rehearsal, Sera would always turn, and her face would do something — crumple, or blaze, or break — and Rafael would deserve every version of it.

The rehearsal had not included chandeliers of frozen starlight. It had not included a hall the size of a cathedral's ambition, with courtiers gliding across black ice floors in gowns stitched from frost-ferns, their laughter falling like snow that never landed. It had especially not included the possibility that Sera would look at her with the pleasant, vacant courtesy one gives a stranger who has interrupted a dance.

"Madam," said Sera Marchetti, "you're standing on the pattern."

Rafael looked down. Her boots were indeed planted on an inlaid line of silver that wound through the floor like a frozen river, and she was, apparently, ruining it.

"I'm sorry," Rafael said. Her voice came out strange. Flat. As if it belonged to someone else watching from very far away. "I didn't know there was a pattern."

"There is always a pattern." Sera smiled, and the smile was Sera's smile — the exact one, the one that had once preceded the theft of Rafael's last honey-bun and the blaming of it, flawlessly, on a neighbor's goat — but worn slightly wrong, like a coat cut for someone taller. "That's rather the point of everything."

Everything. The word sat in the air between them like a held note.

Rafael had spent three days in this mountain getting to this moment. Three days of Tam's remembered corridors, of Brannic holding doors with his shoulders and his oath, of Odile muttering ward-mathematics into wax tablets like prayers to a god of geometry. Three days of walking past bottled screams on iron shelves — some of them, she was nearly certain now, in bottles she herself had blown glass for, twenty years ago, clumsy and young and greedy for coin. She had told herself, all three days, that whatever waited at the end of it would be simple. A cage. A cell. A sister chained to a ledger-page, hollow-eyed and waiting to be freed.

She had not told herself: *she might be happy here.*

That was the cruelty no one had warned her about. Not Brannic's grim records, not Odile's careful warnings, not even Tam, who knew better than anyone what the court did to a person, because Tam had been unmade in these halls and rebuilt out of kindness alone. They had all described horror. None of them had described *this*: Sera in a gown of pale blue frost-silk, her hair pinned up with combs of white metal, her hands — her hands, which had once been nicked and ink-stained from mending nets — smooth and idle and folded at her waist like a lady's in a portrait.

"You're new," Sera said, tilting her head. "I'd remember you. I remember everyone." A small pause, a flicker — the first crack in the porcelain. "I think I remember everyone."

"Do you?" Rafael managed. "Remember everyone?"

"It's my work." Sera said it with pride, gentle and terrible. "I'm the Keeper of Small Warmths. When someone new comes down from above — when they've made their bargain — someone has to hold what they leave behind. Their favorite song. The way their mother's kitchen smelled. Little things. No one wants the little things, so I keep them." She gestured, and Rafael followed the gesture to a low cabinet against the far wall, its drawers labeled in delicate script. "Would you like to see? Most visitors don't ask. They find it upsetting."

*Upsetting,* thought Rafael, whose entire trade was exactly this, whose pockets at this moment held eleven charms of other people's stolen moments, whose deepest bottle sat against her sternum like a second heart. "Yes," she said. "I would like to see."

Why? She couldn't have said. Perhaps because if Sera was going to show her the machinery of her own damnation, Rafael wanted to watch it with her eyes open. Perhaps because every second Sera talked was a second Rafael could study her — the way you study a lock — looking for the seam where the girl she'd sold might still be sewn in.

Sera opened a drawer lined with velvet. Inside lay dozens of small objects: a child's wooden bird, a ribbon gone grey with age, a tin whistle, a pressed flower that should by all rights have crumbled decades ago but hadn't, because nothing here aged, nothing here *moved*, everything here was preserved the way flies are preserved in amber.

"This one," Sera said, lifting the whistle with two fingers, reverent, "belonged to a man who traded his winters for a warm hearth. He left the sound of his brother laughing behind. He didn't want it anymore." She set it down. "Isn't that kind of him? To leave them for others. Everything here is a gift, really. People give and give and give."

"They don't know they're giving," Rafael said.

The flicker again. Faster this time. Sera's brow creased, smoothed, creased. "They sign," she said. "Everyone signs. If you sign, it's yours to give." And then, brightly, terribly: "You haven't signed anything, have you? You smell of the surface. Of salt and smoke and—" she leaned closer, and Rafael held still, held *very* still, as her sister's face came within a handspan of hers, those familiar grey eyes searching unfamiliar territory— "of old glass. Do you work with glass?"

"Bottles," Rafael said. "I make charms. Bottled memories. Harmless ones. For people who want to forget a bad year, or keep a good one."

Something moved behind Sera's eyes then — something quick and dark and drowning, a fish under ice, there and gone. She stepped back. Her smile reassembled itself.

"How useful," she said. "How *convenient*. We could use someone with your craft. The Ledger is always hungry, and feeding it by hand is such slow work." She laughed, a light bell of a sound. "Listen to me, recruiting. Odette will tease me. She says I collect strays."

"Odette?"

"The Lady of the Hall. You'll meet her, everyone meets her eventually, she's wonderful, she has your mother's—"

"*What?*"

"—smile," Sera finished, puzzled at the interruption. "Her smile. Why do you look like that?"

Because, Rafael did not say, because I watched a thing wearing my mother's face offer me tea in a dream I haven't woken from yet, because I know what wears faces here, because you just said *she's wonderful* the way a lamb describes the shepherd. Because, Sera, because —

"I look like that," Rafael said carefully, "because I'm looking for someone. Someone I lost. A long time ago. I thought — I hoped —"

"Oh." Sera's whole bearing changed; softness flooded in, genuine, unbearable. She touched Rafael's arm. Her fingers were cool as river stones. "Oh, you poor thing. Who?"

And there it was. The door. Fifteen years of locked grief swinging open onto the one conversation Rafael had never let herself imagine, because imagining it required deciding what she would say, and every possible answer was a knife.

*I'm looking for my sister. I sold her. I signed for both of us — well, I signed for me, and I let them take the signature as covering you, and I ran, and I built a life on top of the hole where you used to be, and I am here now to drag you out whether you want to go or not.*

Or: *No one. No one important. Just passing through.*

Rafael looked at her sister — at the Keeper of Small Warmths, the woman who filed away orphans' lullabies in labeled drawers and called it kindness — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that neither answer would work. The truth would shatter her, or worse, wouldn't shatter her at all; would slide off the borrowed calm like rain off glass. And the lie would be a betrayal so complete that Rafael would become the court itself, another thing that decided what Sera got to remember.

"The person I lost," Rafael said slowly, "loved honey-buns. Stole mine constantly. Blamed a goat."

Sera blinked.

"She was scared of deep water and pretended not to be. She could gut a fish in four seconds flat and once won a bet doing it. She had a scar on her left knee from falling off the sea-wall, and she cried — she'd deny it, but she cried — the night our mother first sang the salt-song to us, because she said it was too beautiful and it wasn't fair that beautiful things could also be sad." Rafael's voice was steady. Somewhere very far away, she was impressed by how steady. "Do you know her?"

The hall went quiet around them. Not silent — the courtiers still glided, the chandeliers still burned their stolen light — but quiet the way a room goes quiet when a string inside a piano snaps. Sera stood with her hand still resting near the drawer of small warmths, and her face did something Rafael had never seen it do in life or in memory: it tried to be several faces at once.

"No," Sera said.

"Yes," said something else, from somewhere underneath her.

"No," Sera repeated, firmer, and now there was fear in it, real fear, the first honest emotion Rafael had seen on her since the door opened. "I don't — I'm sorry, madam, I don't know any — why would you say those things to me? I don't have a scar. I don't have a—" her hand drifted, without her permission, toward her left knee. Stopped. Drifted back. "I don't cry at songs."

"No," Rafael agreed softly. "Not anymore."

"I serve the winter." Sera's voice had gone thin and high. Around them, several courtiers had turned to watch, their borrowed faces tilted with mild, hungry interest. "The winter provides. The winter keeps us. I signed. Everyone signs. If you sign, it's yours to—"

"Sera."

"Don't call me that!"

It rang off the black ice walls, and every courtier in earshot went still, and Sera stood trembling with both hands pressed over her mouth as though the name had come out of her own throat and frightened her. Above, the chandeliers dimmed, just slightly, the way a fire dims when someone feeds it something bitter.

"I don't know that name," Sera whispered through her fingers. "But it knows me. It's been sitting in me for years, hasn't it, like a stone at the bottom of a river, and I've been walking around it, and walking around it, and everyone tells me the water is fine, the water is fine—"

"Sera, listen to me—"

"You should go." Her eyes were wet and furious and terrified. "You should go right now, before she comes, before Odette sees you making me feel things, we aren't supposed to feel things, feeling is how the pages get torn, feeling is how—" She stopped. Breathed. And when she spoke again, her voice was the courtier's voice, smooth and pleasant and dead, and somehow that was the worst thing yet. "Madam, thank you for your visit. The hall hopes you enjoy your stay. Please mind the pattern on your way out."

"Sera—"

"*Mind the pattern.*"

Rafael minded the pattern. There was nothing else she could do — not here, not now, with the whole glittering hall watching and Odile's plan still unassembled and Brannic holding a door somewhere below and Tam whistling his way through corridors that remembered him. She backed away, one step, two, her eyes never leaving her sister's face, and Sera watched her go with tears standing perfectly still in her eyes, unmoving, unfalling, preserved in amber like everything else in this place.

At the edge of the dais Rafael stopped, because there was one more thing, and she would hate herself forever if she left without saying it.

"When I find what I came for," she said, "I'm coming back for you. And I'm not going to drag you anywhere. I'm going to stand in front of you and tell you the whole truth, every ugly piece of it, and then you're going to choose. That's the difference between me and this place, Sera. This place takes your choices and files them in a drawer. I'm going to give yours back. Even if you use it to tell me no."

For a moment — one suspended, crystalline moment — Sera's composure cracked clean through, and beneath it Rafael saw her: seventeen years old, smoke in her hair, screaming Rafael's name across a burning village green while their mother's face watched from the treeline and did nothing, *nothing*—

Then the frost closed over, smooth as poured glass.

"Enjoy your stay," said the Keeper of Small Warmths.

Rafael walked. She did not run, though every nerve in her body screamed for it; running drew attention, and attention here was a currency she could not afford. She kept her pace even past the courtiers, past the cabinet of small warmths, past a pair of doors carved with winter wheat, and it was only when she reached the shadowed colonnade where Odile was waiting, tablet clutched to her chest like a shield, that her legs finally informed her they were done pretending.

Odile caught her elbow. "You found her."

"I found her."

"And?"

Rafael opened her mouth to answer and discovered that the answer was too large to fit through. Her sister was alive. Her sister was a jailer. Her sister was a prisoner. Her sister had screamed *don't call me that* in a voice full of drowning, and then offered her a courtier's courtesy, and both of those women were real, and only one of them could be saved, and Rafael did not know how to save either.

"She remembers honey-buns," Rafael said finally. "She doesn't know why."

Odile studied her for a long moment. Then, instead of sympathy — which would have broken Rafael open on this cold marble, in this enemy's house, at entirely the wrong hour — she said, briskly: "Good. That means the memories aren't destroyed. They're stored. Stored can be stolen back. That's practically my whole profession." She tapped her tablet. "Which brings me to the problem, because of course there's a problem. The Ledger holds the debts. But the debts aren't the only entries. Every soul down here has a page, and every page is written in the same ink, and I need to see that ink up close before I can tell you whether we can write on it — or only read."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning we need a sample." Odile glanced back down the colonnade, toward the hall, toward the cabinet of small warmths with its labeled drawers, and her expression did something complicated. "And I think I know where the court keeps its stationery."

Behind them, faint across the black ice, a drawer slid shut. And in the silence after it, Rafael heard — very clearly, unmistakably, in a voice she had not heard in fifteen years — her sister begin, quietly and wonderingly, to hum the salt-song.
