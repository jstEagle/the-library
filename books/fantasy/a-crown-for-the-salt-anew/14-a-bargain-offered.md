## 14. A Bargain Offered

The sovereign did not send for us. That was the first thing I noticed, and the thing that frightened me most.

In Corvel, power announced itself—wardens with their brass badges and louder boots, guildmasters who made you wait three days for an audience so you'd know your place. But the court beneath the mountain simply *arrived*, the way cold arrives under a door. One moment Sera had led me down a corridor of black ice polished so fine I could see myself in it, a hundred thin Rafaels walking beside me, all of them looking as sick as I felt. The next moment the corridor opened into a small receiving room, warm as a kitchen, and someone was already sitting there.

Someone wearing my mother's face.

I want to tell you I reacted with dignity. I want to tell you my hand went to a weapon, or that I spoke a ward-word, or that I thought of Odile and Brannic waiting somewhere above with a plan. What actually happened was that I made a sound like a kettle taken off the heat too late—a small, wounded, domestic noise—and stopped walking.

"Hello, little wren," the sovereign said, in my mother's voice, in my mother's accent, with the particular softening she'd always given the second word. My mother had called half the village *little wren*. It had never once felt like being singled out. It had felt like belonging to a flock of one.

"You're dead," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Both of you died in the fire. I watched the roof come down."

"And here I am," the sovereign said, spreading hands that were my mother's hands—the burn scar along the left thumb where the bread oven had bitten her, the crooked knuckle from the winter she'd punched a tax collector—and rising, and crossing the room, and taking my face in those hands with a gentleness that was worse than any blade. "Fifteen years, and this is how you greet me. No wonder you bottle other people's love instead. Yours has gone sharp at the edges."

Up close, the disguise was flawless and therefore wrong. My mother had laugh lines. This face had the *idea* of laugh lines, drawn on by something that had studied grief from the outside. The eyes were brown where Mama's had been nearly black. And when the sovereign blinked, the blink came a half-second too slow, like a clock winding down.

I stepped back. The hands let me go. That, too, was a kind of cruelty—my mother would have held on.

"Where is Sera?" I asked.

"Nearby. Safe. Comfortable." The sovereign settled back onto a chair that hadn't been there before and gestured to a second one that also hadn't been there before. "Sit. We have business, you and I, and I find I dislike conducting it with you standing there like a heron deciding whether to stab a frog."

Against every instinct I owned, I sat. The chair was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything here was engineered to feel like home, because home was the sharpest knife they had.

* * *

"I'll assume you've worked out most of it," the sovereign said, pouring tea from a pot I had not seen enter the room. The tea smelled like chamomile and woodsmoke, like the specific blend Mama bought from the peddler who came through twice a year. I did not drink it. The sovereign didn't seem offended. "The court feeds on souls. Souls are, at their essence, memory given weight. A soul without memories is just meat with ambitions—we've all met those, usually at parties. So we collect. Carefully. Consensually, even, if you squint at the contracts sideways."

"Sera didn't consent. She was eleven."

"She consented through you." The sovereign's smile was small and terrible. "You signed. Your hand. Your name. And hers, appended, as minors' bargains are. You are, technically speaking, her guardian of record in our Ledger. Which makes this conversation delightfully simple."

The tea steamed between us. Somewhere beyond the walls, faint as a pulse under bandages, I could hear the hall: the courtiers moving, the frozen starlight chiming against itself in the chandeliers, and underneath it all a sound I couldn't name until suddenly I could—it was the sound of a great many people not quite sighing. Contentment, mass-produced.

"You brought down the wards," I said. "You blinded the city."

"The city was due." The sovereign shrugged with Mama's shoulders, and I hated it, I hated every borrowed inch. "Corvel hoards. Its charter-memory alone could feed the court through two more winters. A fat town behind fat walls while the rest of the world goes hungry—that's not virtue, Rafael, that's a pantry. We only opened the door."

"And the stars?"

"The stars were a bonus." For just a moment the mask slipped—not off, but aside, the way a shopkeeper lifts a corner of cloth to show you the real goods. Underneath my mother's face there was nothing. Not darkness; nothing. A hollow shaped like a woman, wearing what it could carry. "Starlight keeps. Bottles beautifully. We're bottling the sky, and when the sky is empty, the world below will be very dark and very quiet and very *manageable*, and no one will remember summer well enough to miss it."

I thought of Tam. Tam, who remembered nothing but kindness, who had walked out of this mountain with his name scraped out of him and had still chosen, with the blank slate they'd left him, to be gentle. They'd have done that to everyone. They'd have done it to the whole sky.

"So," I said, because my mouth has always moved faster than my sense. "What's the offer? You didn't dress up as my dead mother to chat about agricultural policy."

* * *

The sovereign laughed. It was a good laugh. That was the worst part. It was Mama's laugh, the one that came from low in the chest, the one that made strangers in markets turn around and smile without knowing why.

"There she is," the sovereign said. "The girl who sold a soul and kept receipts. Very well. The offer."

It stood, and the room rearranged itself around the motion—walls sliding back, the little kitchen dissolving into something grander, a receiving hall hung with icicle-lamps, and through an archway I could see a glimpse of the great hall proper, the Soul-Ledger on its iron lectern like an altar to arithmetic.

"Item one," the sovereign said, ticking fingers with Mama's scarred hand. "Your sister walks free. Tonight. Unwound from the Ledger, her debt burned, her contract void. She leaves this mountain whole."

My heart did something stupid and hopeful, and I despised it.

"Item two: you keep your craft. Every charm you've ever bottled stays yours—your hoard, your collection, fifteen years of stolen sunsets and first kisses and soldiers' courage, all of it intact, all of it *yours*, which I know matters to you more than you admit even to yourself."

"Item three," and here the sovereign leaned in, and the false laugh-lines creased with false warmth, "you take a place at court. Master of Acquisitions. A title older than Corvel. You'd keep your skills sharp, your sister near, and—" the voice dropped to the register Mama had used for telling me secrets in bed, the fire-crackle register— "you'd never feel it again. The guilt. We can take that out, Rafael. We invented taking things out. Fifteen years you've carried that night like a stone in your boot. Imagine setting it down. Imagine waking tomorrow and remembering that you loved your sister, and that she is happy, and that nothing bad ever happened in a burning village, because the memory simply won't hurt anymore. Not gone. Just... painless. Like it happened to somebody else. Like it should have."

The silence stretched. In it, I heard everything the offer wasn't saying.

"That's not mercy," I said quietly. "That's just theft with better manners."

"It's *peace*," the sovereign said, and for one flickering instant something in the hollow behind the face sounded genuinely puzzled, genuinely wounded, as if I'd told a fish that water was overrated. "You've spent fifteen years numbing yourself piecemeal, one stolen feeling at a time, charging coin for it like a charlatan with a wagon. We offer you the cure wholesale. Your guilt, excised. Your past, filed. Your sister, returned. And all it costs is a book and a talent." It straightened, brisk now, Mama doing sums at the kitchen table. "The founding charter-memory of Corvel. And your craft—your *method*, taught to our people, so we needn't rely on clumsy instruments like the ones upstairs in the stairwell. Teach us to steal gently, and we will stop breaking what we take. Everyone wins."

"Except the city."

"The city will forget it lost anything." The smile again. "Which is rather our specialty. And yours."

And there it was. There was the hook under the bait. Because the sovereign was right, and it knew it was right, and it wore my mother's face specifically so I couldn't pretend otherwise: I *had* spent fifteen years selling forgetting by the dram. I had rules—I never stole from the desperate, never bottled my own past—but the rules were just the price of sleeping, and the trade itself had been the dream. I had made a comfortable living off the gap between what people felt and what they could bear. This court was that gap, given a throne.

The seduction wasn't Sera's freedom. I'd have traded anything for that, and the sovereign knew it, so it wrapped the rest of the bargain around that gift like sugar around a pill. The seduction was the morning after. Waking with a full hoard and an empty conscience. Being able to look at Sera without the burning village standing between us. Being, at long last, *light*.

I looked at my mother's face offering me absolution at a fair price, and I understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was exactly how Sera must have felt every day here. Painless. Manageable. Warm.

"No," I said.

The word came out small. I said it again, bigger.

"No."

* * *

The sovereign tilted its head. Mama's head, at Mama's angle, the way she'd listened to me argue curfew at fourteen, amused and patient and utterly certain of winning.

"No?" it said pleasantly. "May I ask why? Genuinely. I collect reasons."

"Because you got her face wrong." I stood. My legs shook. I let them. "She had laugh lines. You drew them on. She had eyes like spilled ink, and yours are tea-colored, because you took the color from the cup you offered me. You studied her from the Ledger, from *my* memory of her, filtered through fifteen years of guilt, and guilt is a bad mirror—it makes everyone look like an accusation." I swallowed. "Also, you called me little wren correctly four times out of five, and Mama never got it wrong. Not once. Not even the day the village burned."

Something shifted in the hollow behind the face. I don't know what—wounded pride, perhaps, or recalculation, or hunger discovering a new flavor.

"And the rest?" the sovereign asked. "The guilt we'd lift? The stone in your boot?"

"The stone tells me where the ground is." I backed toward the archway, toward the great hall, toward wherever Brannic's plan and Odile's wards and Tam's half-remembered maps were converging on the iron book. "Keep your peace. I've seen what your peace does to people. It makes them set tables for masters and call it family."

"Sera chose this," the sovereign said softly, and the softness was the cruelest cut yet, because it was true. "Ask her. She's happy."

"She doesn't remember enough to be unhappy. That's not happiness. That's anesthesia." I reached the archway. "Here's my counteroffer. You give me back my sister's soul, and I don't pour fifteen years of stolen moments into that Ledger of yours until it bursts like a rotten barrel."

The sovereign went very still. Stillness, in something that hollow, is loud.

"You wouldn't," it said. "You'd destroy your own hoard. Your collection. The only proof you were ever anything but a coward with a corkscrew." My mother's lips curved. "Sentiment. It always betrays the amateur."

"Good thing I'm a professional, then."

I turned my back on it—which took more out of me than the whole descent down the Stair of Teeth had, walking away from my mother's face twice in one lifetime—and stepped through the arch into the great hall, where the frozen starlight chimed overhead and the Soul-Ledger lay open on its iron lectern, waiting.

And there, standing at the base of the lectern with his head tipped back and his eyes gone wide and wet, was Tam.

He wasn't looking at the Ledger the way the rest of us did—as a target, a lock, a problem. He was looking at it the way a man looks at a house he grew up in and hasn't seen since the fire.

"Rafael," he said, and his voice was wrong, layered, doubled, a boy's voice with a man's grief underneath it. "This is where I died. I remember. Rafael, I remember my name."
