## 15. Tam Remembers

Tam had never been anywhere that smelled like this before.

That wasn't saying much. Tam had never been most places. He'd told Rafael that once, on the second night he'd known her, when she'd asked him where he was from, and he'd said, "Here," and she'd looked at him with an expression he hadn't had the words for then but which he now understood to mean: *of course you're from here, boy, you are a creature made entirely of here.* She hadn't meant it unkindly. Rafael almost never meant things unkindly, even when they came out of her like splinters.

But this hall — this hall smelled of iron and cold candles and something older underneath, something like snow that had never once melted off a roof. It smelled like the inside of a promise nobody had ever meant to keep.

He knew that smell.

He didn't know how he knew it. That was the whole trouble with Tam. He knew things without knowing them, the way you know the shape of your own bed in the dark even after you've moved houses. Odile said it was because memory wasn't only in the head; it was in the hands, the feet, the spine. Brannic said it was because Tam's head was empty the way a drum is empty, which is to say not at all — everything inside it just waiting to be struck.

Right now, standing on marble so cold it ached through his boots, Tam felt very thoroughly struck.

The hall stretched out before them like a cathedral someone had built by accident. Chandeliers hung overhead, each one a knot of frozen starlight, and their glow was wrong in a way Tam couldn't name — bright but giving no warmth, the way a painted fire gives no heat. Courtiers drifted between columns wearing faces that fit them beautifully and badly at once, like gloves tailored by someone who had only ever heard descriptions of hands. And at the far end, open on its iron stand beneath the sovereign's dais, lay the Soul-Ledger.

Nobody had explained to Tam what the Ledger would feel like. Nobody had thought to, because nobody knew, because nobody had stood in this room twice — once to be unmade, once to come back.

Tam felt it before he saw it. It felt like his name.

Which was ridiculous, because Tam didn't have a name beyond Tam. He'd checked. He'd spent years checking, the way other boys checked their pockets for coins they might have forgotten. *Tam,* everyone called him, because it was short and friendly and required nothing of anyone. When people asked what it was short for, he smiled his smile — the one Rafael said could talk a baker into free bread — and said he didn't remember, and people laughed kindly, and the moment passed, and the small hollow space behind his ribs stayed exactly as hollow as it always was.

But the Ledger pulled at him anyway. Gently. The way a hook pulls when the fish has only just noticed the line.

"Stay close," Brannic murmured beside him. The old captain wore his warden's coat turned inside out, the silver threads hidden against his chest, and he kept one broad hand hovering near Tam's shoulder without quite touching, the way you steady a lantern in wind. "Don't look at the courtiers' eyes. Don't touch anything that glitters. And if anything starts to feel strange—"

"Tell me when we're out of it," Tam finished. "I know."

"No," Brannic said quietly. "I was going to say: tell me anyway. Strange feelings are worth more down here than gold."

Tam nodded. But it was already too late for strange. Everything had been strange since the stair — since the bottled screams in their niches, since Rafael had gone white as tallow recognizing her own handwriting on the ward-seals. Strange was the water they were all swimming in now.

What was happening to him was different.

* * *

It started with bread.

Of all the things it could have started with, bread. They were moving along the edge of the hall, hugging the shadow of the columns while Odile counted ward-nodes under her breath like a woman counting stairs in the dark, and Tam passed a frozen banquet table, and there — preserved mid-feast under a skin of ice — sat a loaf of dark rye, split and buttered, butter melting into the crevices in little golden rivers, except nothing here melted, so the butter simply hung there, mid-melt, forever.

His mouth flooded. His chest did something worse.

Because he had eaten that exact bread. Not that loaf — another loaf, the same loaf, a hundred mornings of the same loaf, torn not cut because tearing was faster and his mother said cutting was pretentious — and she had —

*Mother.*

The word arrived in him like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it fall and fall and never hit bottom, and then, long seconds later, the splash came up to meet him.

"Mama says the crust is where all the goodness lives," a voice said, from inside his own skull, in his own smaller voice, "which is why she always gives me hers."

Tam stopped walking. His hand found the table's edge. The ice under his palm was so cold it burned, and he was grateful for it, grateful for any sensation that belonged to *now* instead of *then*.

"Tam." Rafael's voice, low. She'd doubled back; of course she had, she was already three steps away from wherever she was supposed to be, that was simply Rafael's natural state. Her hand closed over his wrist — not pulling, just holding. "What's happening?"

"I don't—" He swallowed. "Bread."

"Bread," she repeated, with the gravity of a physician.

"My mother used to give me her crusts." Saying it aloud cracked something, or maybe fixed something; he genuinely could not tell which. "Rafael, I don't think I lost my memories in an accident. I don't think I fell, or got sick, or any of the stories I've been telling myself. I think I was *sold*."

The word landed between them and neither of them picked it up, because they both knew who in their little company had expertise in such transactions, and Rafael's face did the thing it did — went smooth and still, like a pond deciding not to be a pond.

"Sold," she said finally, very softly.

"To this place." His eyes went to the Ledger despite himself. Iron covers, iron clasps, pages that were somehow both paper and metal, and a hum coming off it that he felt in his teeth, in the bones behind his ears, in the place where his name should have been. "It's calling me. Like — like a dog hearing a whistle it forgot existed."

Rafael's grip tightened. For a moment he watched her wrestle with something — he knew that look too, he'd seen it every time she approached her own deepest charms, the ones she wouldn't sell at any price — and then she leaned in until her forehead nearly touched his, and said, "Then let's go get your name back. After Sera. But we *will* get it back, Tam. I'm very good at taking things that belong to other people."

"That's the first honest thing you've said about yourself since I met you," he whispered, and she laughed — a tiny, startled laugh, quickly smothered — and the sound of it steadied him enough to take his hand off the table.

He didn't look at the bread again. He would remember it later, though. He would remember it forever, which was, he was beginning to understand, a new and astonishing privilege.

* * *

The memories didn't come in order. That was the cruelest part, and also, eventually, the kindest.

They came like guests arriving late to a party, each convinced it was the first. Bread and crusts. Then — halfway across the hall, crouched behind a column while a courtier in a borrowed alderman's face glided past close enough that Tam could hear the faint creak of a smile held too long — a kitchen. Copper pots. A woman humming off-key on purpose because she claimed songs sounded better slightly broken.

Her name surfaced whole, unasked-for, in the middle of counting his own heartbeats:

*Marisol.*

Marisol Vane. His mother, Marisol Vane, who mended nets and sang flat and had a scar on her left thumb from a knife fight with an eel, an actual eel, she'd won, mostly. Who had hands that smelled of salt and tar. Who had carried him on her shoulders through the fish market in a town whose name was—

*Tarnholt.* On the coast. North of Corvel, two weeks' walk, a town where the gulls were criminals and everyone pretended otherwise.

Tam gripped the column and breathed through it, because Brannic had once taught him that feeling, fully felt, passes through you in about ninety seconds if you don't fight it, and Brannic was usually right about pain even when he was wrong about everything else.

Ninety seconds. Then the next wave.

He remembered being seven. He remembered being nine, and proud of it, because nine seemed impossibly grown. He remembered a winter that had come early and stayed rude, crops black in the fields, his mother's face getting thinner around the smile. He remembered strangers coming to Tarnholt in fine coats — courtiers, though he hadn't known the word then, he'd thought they were lords — offering bargains. Debts forgiven. Warm rooms. A future.

He remembered sitting on his mother's knee while she listened to one of those strangers in their doorway, and he remembered the stranger saying words like *apprenticeship* and *opportunity* and *a better life*, and he remembered thinking it sounded boring but the man's coat was very shiny.

And he remembered — this came last, and it came like a blade finding the gap in armor — his mother saying no.

She said no. He remembered her saying it. Clearly. Firmly, but politely, because Marisol Vane was polite the way cliffs are polite to the sea. *"We'll manage. We always manage. Thank you kindly, but the boy stays with me."*

The deal he never agreed to. The deal she never agreed to either. They had simply taken him anyway, some night soon after, and whatever story Tarnholt had been told, whatever story his mother had lived the rest of her life inside — Tam would never know it, because the court had reached into his head and scooped out eleven years like the flesh of a fruit, leaving the rind standing upright and walking around, smiling its smile that could talk a baker into free bread.

They hadn't even needed his consent. Consent was for bargains. This had been harvest.

Something went hot behind Tam's eyes and colder, much colder, in his chest, and he realized distantly that he was furious — not the flash-fury of being cheated at cards, which burned off in an hour, but a deep structural fury, the kind you could build things out of. Or burn things down. Either, really. It was generous like that.

"Tam." Odile, this time, appearing at his elbow with her tools wrapped and her eyes sharp. She studied his face for exactly one second, which was all she needed; ward-smiths read cracks the way farmers read clouds. "You're remembering."

"Yeah."

"Is it bad?"

"It's my mum's name," Tam said, and his voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Odile. My name isn't Tam."

"What is it?"

He reached for it. It was right there, behind the Ledger's pull, glowing like a coal under ash. He reached and reached and—

The iron book *pulled*. Deliberately. He felt it happen, felt the memory slide backward like a coin snatched off a table, felt the whole vast cold attention of the thing swivel toward him the way a librarian looks up when someone touches the wrong shelf.

*No,* Tam thought, with all eleven stolen years behind it. *Mine. You've had your share of mine.*

He grabbed the name and *held*.

"Ezren," he said out loud, into the frozen hall, into the humming silence, loud enough that Rafael's head snapped around and Brannic's hand went to his sword. "My name is Ezren Vane. My mother is Marisol Vane of Tarnholt. And I wasn't lost, and I wasn't given away, and I never agreed to anything. You took me. And I've come to take it back."

The hall heard him. Whatever the hall was — and Tam suspected it was mostly the Ledger wearing architecture — it heard him, and the chandeliers of frozen starlight flickered, all together, like a held breath.

Somewhere above them, on the dais, something began to rise from the sovereign's empty throne.

* * *

"Well," Brannic said into the ringing quiet, in the tone of a man reviewing troops before a battle he expects to lose slowly. "That was either extremely brave or extremely foolish, lad."

"Both," Tam — Ezren — said. "Mum always said I was economical."

Rafael appeared at his side, pale, gripping his arm hard enough to bruise. "Your name. You got it back?"

"Some of it." He found he was grinning, which made no sense, and made perfect sense. "Enough. Enough to want the rest." He looked down the length of the hall, past the drifting courtiers who had all, he noticed now, stopped pretending to drift and were watching with their borrowed eyes, and at the iron book gleaming on its stand, and at the figure rising from the throne wearing a face he hadn't yet recognized but suspected, horribly, that Rafael had.

"And Raf?" He said it quietly, just for her. "Whatever you're planning to do to that book — whatever it costs — I want to help. I know things about being emptied out that none of the rest of you do. You'll need that. Let me carry some of it."

For a moment Rafael looked at him the way she looked at her oldest charms, the ones she'd never sold — like something precious she'd been guarding without admitting she was guarding it.

"All right, Ezren Vane of Tarnholt," she said. "Together, then."

Above them, the sovereign stepped down from the dais wearing his mother's face, and smiled.
