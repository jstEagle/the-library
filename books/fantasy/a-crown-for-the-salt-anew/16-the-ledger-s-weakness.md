## 16. The Ledger's Weakness

Odile had never trusted books that didn't want to be read.

She'd felt it the moment they'd crested the gallery above the winter court and seen the thing: an iron volume the size of a cellar door, chained open on its lectern of black ice, its pages turning themselves in a wind that did not exist. Every other object in the court was beautiful the way a lie is beautiful—chandeliers of frozen starlight, courtiers gliding in borrowed faces—but the Ledger was honest. It was ugly, and heavy, and hungry, and it sat in the middle of that glittering hall like a toad in a jewel box.

Now, wedged into a dead miner's cache three levels up from the hall, with Brannic snoring in the manner of a man who claimed he was merely resting his eyes, Odile sketched it from memory. She was not much of an artist. Her charcoal rendering looked like a coffin propped open on a dinner table, which, she reflected, was not far wrong.

"Stop looking at it like that," Brannic said without opening his eyes.

"Like what?"

"Like you're about to take a hammer to something expensive."

"I am always about to take a hammer to something expensive. That is my entire character." She tapped the charcoal against her teeth. "But no hammer this time. Something stranger."

Brannic opened one eye. In the blue light of the bottled-scream wards down the passage, his face looked carved out of old weather. "Go on, then."

"The pages," she said. "Did you notice? They turn when someone new is brought before the throne. When a bargain's struck, the book writes itself—the entry appears in ink before the sovereign finishes speaking. I watched it happen with the fishmonger's boy. The pen wasn't moving. There *was* no pen. But the letters came up through the page like frost on glass."

"So it's magic. We're shocked."

"Don't be dense, it's unbecoming in a captain." She pulled the sketch closer. "It's not just magic, it's *fed* magic. I've built wards for thirty years, Brannic. Nothing holds for free. A ward-stone drinks power. A charm eats memory. Everything that binds does so because something pours into it. That book doesn't record bargains. It *is* one—a bargain held in place by being continuously fed."

"Fed with what?" Brannic asked, though she could hear in his voice that he already knew. He was a warden; he'd spent his life around the arithmetic of sacrifice.

"Memories. Souls are made of them—that's why the court harvests them whole. But look at the entries themselves. I got close enough to read one, over Rafael's shoulder. *'One daughter's name, freely given, against one winter's passage.'* Freely given. You know why contracts say *freely given*, Brannic? Because a gift carries. It has weight. The Ledger isn't holding Sera Vasquez's soul in a cell somewhere—it's holding the *memory of the transaction*. The debt lives in the book as remembered words. Feed the book a different set of words—" She stopped. Her hands were shaking slightly, which annoyed her.

Brannic sat up fully now, snoring forgotten. "You're saying Rafael could write in it."

"I'm saying Rafael's whole cursed craft is putting memories where they don't belong and making them stick. She bottles first kisses. She seals lullabies in glass. If the Ledger runs on stored memory, then it isn't only a ledger." Odile heard her own voice drop, the way it did when she stood at the base of a wall everyone else called unclimbable. "It's an empty bottle the size of a mountain. And we know the finest bottler in Corvel."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat through stone, the winter court played its music.

"It won't be that simple," Brannic said finally.

"Nothing is ever that simple. That's never once been a reason not to try."

"No," he agreed. "But it's a reason to plan like pessimists and pray like fools. Come on. Wake the others. And Odile—" he paused at the cache's entrance, "—when you tell Rafael, tell her gently. She'll do it anyway. She's been walking toward this thing since the day we met her. But she'll need to believe you think she can."

"Why me?"

"Because you've spent two weeks deciding whether to forgive her," Brannic said. "Whatever you decided, she'll trust the verdict more than mine. Mine's compromised."

"How?"

"I like her," said Brannic, with the air of a man confessing to a crime, and went to wake Tam.

* * *

Brannic had held a great many doors in his life. Garrison doors, courthouse doors, the door of a burning tenement with a family behind it and smoke ahead of him. He thought about doors now, while Odile laid out her theory to the others in the low firelight, because a plan needed a door-holder, and he already knew it would be him.

He listened with half an ear—he'd learned long ago that listening with your whole ear made your face show things—and watched Rafael instead.

She sat with her back against the rock, turning a blank glass charm over and over in her fingers, the way other people worried a coin. Tam leaned against her shoulder and was asleep, or pretending to be; the boy slept anywhere, instantly, like a cat, and Brannic had privately begun to suspect it was a skill left over from whatever the court had taken from him. Some part of Tam knew how to go somewhere else when things got bad. Brannic recognized it because he had the same scar, just differently shaped.

"—so the debt isn't a chain," Odile was saying, pacing the length of the cache, four strides each way, "it's an *entry*. Ink and iron. Which means it can be overwritten, if the hand doing the writing knows how to make memory hold. Rafael, you seal recollections in glass so they can't decay. What would you do if you wanted to change what a person remembers? Not erase—change."

Rafael was quiet for a moment. In the dimness her face was all angles and shadow, and Brannic thought, not for the first time, that she had the kind of face that would still look guarded on its deathbed.

"Layering," she said at last. "You don't delete the true memory. True memory roots deep; pull it and everything around it tears. You lay a new memory over it, thick enough and true-*shaped* enough that the mind accepts the graft. Like whitewashing a wall. The wall's still there underneath. It just reads different."

"And the Ledger?"

"The Ledger's worse than a mind. A mind forgives small inconsistencies. Iron doesn't. Whatever we write would have to be—" she searched, "—*paid* for. Real. The book takes memories as payment; it would know thin coin."

Odile stopped pacing. "How real?"

"You saw what it took to make one entry. 'A daughter's name, freely given.' Fifteen years ago Rafael paid a soul for passage." Brannic heard his own voice come out flatter than he intended. "To overwrite a debt that size, we'd need to feed the book something of equal weight. Not trinkets. Not a stolen kiss or a soldier's courage in a bottle."

The silence that followed had a texture to it. Tam opened his eyes but didn't move from Rafael's shoulder.

"I have fifteen years of charms," Rafael said quietly. "Everything I ever stole and couldn't bear to sell. First kisses. Last words. Lullabies. Whole afternoons that belonged to people who never knew they were happy until I took the proof away." Her thumb moved over the blank glass. "I've been carrying them like a magpie carries shiny guilt. I always told myself I kept them safe for the owners. To return someday."

"And now?" Odile asked.

"Now you're telling me there's a furnace downstairs that runs on exactly what I'm hoarding." Rafael laughed, short and joyless. "Of course there is. Of course the way to buy Sera back is to give up every stolen thing I have." She looked up, and Brannic met her eyes and didn't look away, because some things owed the looking. "It's almost funny. It's priced exactly right. The court always prices things so the paying hurts."

"That's how you know it's fair," Tam said, muffled, into her sleeve. Everyone turned to look at him. He shrugged. "My mother used to say that. About medicine. I just—I remembered that. Just now." His voice cracked on the last word, and Rafael put an arm around him, and Brannic studied the ceiling very hard.

* * *

Later, when Tam had gone back to sleep for true and Odile sat sharpening a ward-knife that would be useless against anything down here but made her feel better, Brannic took the watch by the entrance. Rafael came and stood beside him. Neither of them spoke for a while. This was, Brannic had found, the foundation of their entire friendship: two people willing to stand next to each other and say nothing with real commitment.

"You're going to ask me to hold the door," Rafael said eventually.

"I am going to *tell* you to let me hold the door. There's a difference, and it matters to me."

"Brannic—"

"There are four of us," he said. "The girl who makes walls, the boy who remembers the halls, the thief who writes in souls, and the old man who used to be a captain and is good for exactly one more fight. You're the thief. Odile keeps the wards standing while you work—if the wards fall, we all become courtiers wearing borrowed faces, and I will shoot myself before I let that happen, which would spoil the plan considerably. Tam guides. And I stand between that door and the three of you, and I hold it, and that is the whole of my contribution, and I have never in my life been happier about a job assignment."

Rafael looked at him sidelong. "You've been planning to die since the Archive."

"I've been planning to *spend* myself since the Archive. Different ledger entirely." He smiled at his own joke; Rafael didn't. "I burned my honor to cover our tracks, Rafa. Forged the record. Thirty years of clean service, ended with my own handwriting on a lie. Whatever's left of me is worth more as a door than as a man. Let me be worth it."

"No," Rafael said.

That surprised him. He'd expected argument, or worse, gratitude.

"No," she said again, turning to face him fully, and even in the dark her voice had the particular steel he'd heard exactly twice before—once over Odile's questions, once at the mouth of the mine. "You don't get to pay my debt for me. Nobody does. That's how I got here—someone decided what she was worth, and someone decided what her sister was worth, and nobody asked either of us. If anyone spends themselves in that hall, it's me, on the Ledger, and it costs memories, not blood. Your job is the door. Your job is also, specifically, to *survive* the door. Can you manage that? Or shall I find a less dramatic captain?"

Brannic opened his mouth. Closed it. Found, to his considerable irritation, that his eyes were hot.

"Hold the door *and live*," Rafael said. "That's an order. I'm stealing your rank. It's mine now."

"Yes, Captain," Brannic managed.

"Good." She turned back toward the fire. Then paused. "And Brannic. The forging. I never thanked you. I should have."

"You paid me back by being worth the lie," he said gruffly. "Now go sleep. If we're going to rob a book, I want everyone rested enough to run."

* * *

They went over the plan eleven times. Odile insisted on eleven; she'd once told Brannic that anything rehearsed fewer than ten times fell apart and more than twelve fell apart *confidently*, and he had learned not to argue with her about numbers.

The shape of it was simple, the way an avalanche is simple.

Tam would lead them down the servants' vein he remembered—the one corridor in all the mountain's throat where the bottled screams hung sparse enough to pass. Odile would carry the ward-frame she'd spent two days building from salvaged stone, a portable silence that could hold a circle of floor calm for perhaps four hundred heartbeats while Rafael worked. Brannic held the door. And Rafael—

Rafael would pour fifteen years of hoarded humanity into the Ledger and write, in her own craft, in her own hand, the only sentence that mattered: *debt paid, debt paid, debt paid*, layered thick and true-shaped and bought with real coin, until Sera Vasquez's entry drowned under it.

"She'll feel it," Odile warned on the final repetition. "Your sister. Whatever the court's done to her memory, the debt is tied to her. When it burns, she'll feel fifteen years of her own name coming back. It may break her."

"It may," Rafael agreed. Her voice was steady. Her hands, Brannic noticed, were not. "Then I'll be there to catch the pieces. I owe her a catch fifteen years late."

"And if the sovereign intervenes?" Brannic asked. "A creature that wears your mother's face doesn't sit idle while its meal ticket gets rewritten."

"Then you hold the door," Rafael said, "longer than four hundred heartbeats."

"Ah," said Brannic. "Yes. The door."

Odile rolled the ward-frame onto her shoulder. Tam checked and rechecked the chalk marks of his remembered route, muttering street names to himself like prayers. Rafael packed her charms into the satchel she'd carried since Corvel—all of them, every bottle, every vial, the whole magpie hoard clinking softly against its neighbors like a bagful of small warm hearts.

"One thing," Odile said, as they formed up at the mouth of the cache. Her voice had gone quiet, the way it did when she'd found a flaw and hated it. "If the Ledger takes the memories as payment—truly takes them, eats them—then whoever feeds it will lose them too. Not the bottles. The originals. You can't sell a man his own shadow twice, Rafael. Once those memories go into the book, they're gone from you. All of them. Fifteen years of other people's moments—and everything you ever stole to survive on." She hesitated. "Possibly everything you ever loved."

The passage breathed. Down below, the winter court's music wound on, patient and cold.

"I know," Rafael said, and stepped past them both into the dark, satchel singing against her hip, and started down the stair of teeth toward the hall where her sister waited and the great iron book sat open, hungry, and—Odile was suddenly, terribly sure—already beginning, in its slow frost-script across a fresh white page, to write theirs.
