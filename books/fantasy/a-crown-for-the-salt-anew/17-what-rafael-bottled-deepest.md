## 17. What Rafael Bottled Deepest

The plan, such as it was, had been Odile's. Odile's plans always sounded simple in the telling, the way a cliff sounded simple when described from the bottom.

"The Ledger holds its entries with memory," she'd said, chalk in hand, drawing on the flagstones of the mine-shaft shelter like a woman sketching a kitchen renovation. "Every bargain in that book is fed. Memories go in, debts stay written. Which means—" she'd tapped the chalk against the stone, leaving a small white asterisk, "—it can be *fed* by anyone who knows how to pour. Rafael. You know how to pour."

"I know how to pour," Rafael had agreed, because it was true, and because the alternative was to say the rest of the sentence out loud.

The rest of the sentence was this: to overwrite Sera's debt, the Ledger would need to be fed something heavier than fifteen years of court-cruelty. Something that outweighed a soul. And the only thing Rafael owned that weighed that much was everything she'd ever stolen.

Every charm. Every bottle. Fifteen years of other people's first kisses and last words and lullabies and courage, the whole hoard she'd told herself was inventory and knew, in the hourless dark of three in the morning, was armor.

* * *

They made camp in a hollow off the Stair of Teeth, a pocket where the screaming-wards had gone quiet—Brannic said even cruelty got tired—and Odile drew the warding circle while Tam, bless him, produced from some impossible pocket of his coat a small tin of honey-cakes and distributed them with the solemnity of a priest.

"Eat," Tam said. "Falling down mountains is hungry work. So is falling out of them, probably. We'll find out."

Brannic took two. Rafael took one and held it without eating, turning it over, and Tam watched her with those clear, unburdened eyes of his and said, gently, "You're doing the thing where you hold food and don't eat it. Odile does it when she's doing arithmetic in her head. You do it when you're doing arithmetic in your heart."

"Is that so."

"It is so. What's the sum?"

Rafael looked at him. Tam, who remembered nothing but kindness, who had lost his name and his mother and his whole first life to the court and had come out of it *sweet*, somehow, sweet the way a candle is sweet after a storm—Tam was looking at her like the answer mattered, like she mattered, and it was almost unbearable that he did.

"The sum," she said, "is that I have to give up everything I've spent fifteen years keeping."

"Oh," said Tam. "That's a big sum." He thought about it, chewing. "Is it the bottles? The ones you carry in the inner coat pocket? The ones you never sell, even when the buyer offered the stupid fortune, even when we were hungry that winter?"

"You noticed that."

"Tam notices things," he said. "It's all I've got. Names go, faces go, but noticing stays." He leaned forward, honey-cake crumbs on his chin. "What's in them?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Not *what do they cost*—she'd have answered that easily, easily, with a joke about overhead. But *what's in them.*

"Moments," she said. "Stolen ones. Good ones, mostly. A baker's daughter learning her mother's bread-song. A warden's first snow. A man dancing with his wife at their daughter's wedding, and the wife laughing at how badly he dances." Her voice did something traitorous on the last one. "I bottled them because—"

She stopped. Because the true reason was shameful and simple: because she had sold her own moments, the ones that mattered, and had spent fifteen years collecting everyone else's like a woman who'd burned down her kitchen collecting other people's recipe cards.

"Because you're a magpie," Tam said comfortably. "It's all right. Magpies are very loyal. They just like shiny things."

"Tam," Brannic rumbled from the edge of the circle, "let the woman have her crisis in peace."

"I'm giving her company in her crisis. There's a difference."

* * *

Rafael waited until the others slept—Odile curled around her chalk like a knife, Brannic sitting up out of habit even in sleep, Tam starfished and smiling at nothing—and then she took out the inner coat pocket's contents and laid them in a row on the stone.

Forty-one charms.

She'd never counted them before. That had been deliberate; counting was a kind of knowing, and she had made a life's discipline of not-knowing. But now she counted, and the number sat there in the ward-light like a verdict.

Forty-one bottles, each no bigger than her thumb, each holding a moment that belonged to someone else and had become, over fifteen years of carrying, something that belonged to her too. She picked up the first one—a green glass stopper, the baker's daughter's bread-song—and uncorked it a hair's breadth, just to smell it, the way you'd lift the lid off a pot.

The song came out in a thread: flour on hands, heat on faces, a mother's voice going up and down like a tide. Rafael corked it fast. Her eyes were wet. That was the trouble with the hoard; it wasn't inventory. It was a family she'd assembled one theft at a time, because she couldn't trust herself with the real kind.

*Look at you,* said the voice in her head that sounded like her sister at fourteen, sharp and fond. *You stole a whole city's worth of love because you couldn't face the one person you actually owed.*

"Sera," Rafael said aloud, to the dark, "if you can hear me from whatever gilded cage they've got you in—I'm coming. And I'm bringing the only currency they can't counterfeit."

The dark said nothing, but the mountain hummed, low, the way it had hummed since the Stair, and Rafael chose to believe it was listening.

* * *

The problem—the real problem, the one under the problem—was this: she did not know who she was without the hoard.

She'd built Rafael Vasquez out of stolen timber. The charm-seller with the rules—*never steal from the desperate, never bottle your own past*—the woman with the wry line for every buyer and the exit for every room. But strip away the forty-one moments and what was left? A girl from a burning village who had stood in the smoke with her sister's hand in hers and a court-broker's offer in her ears, and had chosen herself. Had said *yes, take her, let me go.* Fifteen years of anesthesia, and under it, the same girl, the same smoke, the same yes.

Without the hoard she was only the woman who sold her sister.

She said it out loud, there in the hollow, testing the weight of it, and found it did not actually crush her. That was the surprise. She'd expected it to crush her. Instead it just sat there, terrible and true, like a stone you finally pick up and find you can carry—not because it's light, but because you've been carrying it all along and calling it something else.

"Odile would say the stone was load-bearing," she muttered. "Brannic would say something about soldiers and pack-weights. Tam would offer me a honey-cake."

She looked at the forty-one bottles.

"They'd say give it up," she said. "They'd say it's not even a question. That's the whole point of them, isn't it. That's what they're *for*."

Because here was the thing she'd finally understood, somewhere on the Stair of Teeth with the bottled screams whimpering in the dark: the moments in the bottles weren't diminished by being given. The bread-song didn't stop being sung. The wife didn't stop laughing at the dancing. A memory poured into the Ledger to burn away a debt didn't vanish—it *landed*. It became part of the book, part of the mountain, part of the reason Sera could walk out. The moments would stop being hers, yes. But they had never really been hers. They'd been *hers to carry*, and carrying, it turned out, was not the same as keeping.

Her rules. Never steal from the desperate. Never bottle her own past.

She'd never written down the third rule because she'd never had to: *never spend the hoard*, because the hoard was the wall between her and the smoke. But rules, Odile said, were just wards you drew around yourself, and every ward had a failure mode, and the failure mode of Rafael's whole life was a girl in a burning village choosing herself.

Not this time. This time she chose the other way around. This time the burning thing was the Ledger, and she was going to feed it until it choked.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Near dawn—assuming the mountain kept a dawn, which was anyone's guess—Odile stirred, saw her sitting upright with forty-one bottles in rows before her like a merchant's stall, and came over without a word, sitting down cross-legged, chalk still dusted on her fingers.

"You're going to do it," Odile said.

"I'm going to do it."

"You understand the mechanics. Once they're in the Ledger, they're in the Ledger. No unpouring. And the pour itself—" Odile hesitated, which Odile never did. "Pouring your whole stock through your own craft, all at once, into an iron book that eats memories. It will hurt. Not might. Will."

"I'd be disappointed if it didn't," Rafael said. "It should hurt. It's fifteen years of cowardice coming due."

"That's not what I meant." Odile picked up the green bottle, the bread-song, turned it in her scarred smith's fingers. "I meant that it should hurt the way a burn hurts. Not as punishment. As *proof*. That it was real, and that you gave it anyway." She set the bottle down with strange gentleness. "I fell from grace in the ward-smiths for a mistake I actually made. You fell for one you made at fifteen, in a fire, with a knife at your whole world. I've been angry at you since the ward-stones. I'm not anymore. I want that on record, because I'm bad at saying things twice."

Rafael's throat was tight. "Noted for the record."

"Good." Odile stood, brisk again, armor back on. "Then when we get to the hall, you pour, I hold the wards, Brannic holds the door, Tam holds—" she paused. "What does Tam hold?"

"Everyone together," Rafael said. "Tam holds everyone together."

* * *

Brannic found her last, at the mouth of the hollow, watching the dark of the Stair. He stood beside her the way he did everything—solidly, at parade rest, as if the abyss might be intimidated by good posture.

"Captain," she said.

"Not a captain. Burned the last of that." He was quiet a moment. "In the wardens, before a hard entry, we had a custom. You told your shield-brother what you were afraid of, and they told you it was stupid, and then you went in anyway. It was stupid, usually. Fear of dogs. Fear of small rooms." He looked at her. "Yours isn't stupid."

"No."

"But it's wrong in one particular, and I'd be failing you as shield-brother not to say it." He turned, and in the faint ward-glow his old scarred face was almost kind. "You think that without the hoard, you're only the woman who sold her sister. I've marched with that woman for weeks now. The woman who sold her sister doesn't walk back *down* a mountain of teeth to fix it. The woman who sold her sister doesn't collect strays—a disgraced smith, a boy with no past, a captain with no honor—and feed them honey-cakes and lie to them about the danger to keep them steady. The woman who sold her sister doesn't have a single person in this hole who'd follow her into that hall."

He let that land.

"Empty the coat, Vasquez. You were never the bottles. You were the one who carried them."

He walked back to his post. Rafael stood at the mouth of the hollow a while longer, and then she did the thing she'd been avoiding since the plan was drawn: she took the last bottle from the innermost pocket, the one wrapped in cloth, the one charm she'd told herself she'd never spend—the memory of Sera at nine, both of them on the sea-wall, salt wind, Sera laughing with a whole mouthful of missing teeth, saying *when we're big, Rafa, we'll live where the salt is new every day.*

She'd kept it back. One charm. A reserve, a ration, a coward's tithe.

She looked at it a long time. Then she put it back in the innermost pocket, against her heart, where it had lived for fifteen years.

"Not that one," she said. "Not that one. That one's not for the book. That one's for *her*."

The mountain hummed. Above, impossibly far, the starless dark pressed down like a held breath, and Rafael Vasquez, charm-seller of Corvel, thief of moments, sister, coward, magpie, began to pack her forty-one bottles with the care of a woman packing for the only journey that mattered.

Somewhere below, iron pages waited to be fed.

She was ready to starve them with love.
