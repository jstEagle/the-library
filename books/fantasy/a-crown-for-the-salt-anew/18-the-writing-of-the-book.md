## 18. The Writing of the Book

The Ledger lay open on its iron stand like a mouth waiting to be fed.

Rafael stood before it with her satchel heavy against her hip—fifteen years of stolen moments, every charm she had ever made, each one a small glass stopper against the dark. The hall of the winter court stretched around her in impossible directions, chandeliers of frozen starlight burning cold and blue overhead, courtiers in borrowed faces watching from the edges with the patient hunger of moths.

"Odile," she said. "How long can you hold it?"

"How long can you hold your breath?" Odile didn't look up from the ward-circle she was scratching into the marble with a stylus made of salt-iron. Her hands were steady. Her voice wasn't, quite. "Same answer. Don't make me hold it long."

Brannic planted himself at the great doors with his old warden's halberd—the one he'd sworn he'd melted down for scrap fifteen years ago and had, apparently, lied about—and rolled his shoulders like a man settling in for weather. "Door's mine," he said. "Whatever comes through it comes through me first."

"That's not comforting," Tam said from his place at Odile's shoulder, where he'd insisted on standing because, as he put it, *I know what this room does to people and I'm the only one who's already survived it.* He was pale as candle wax. His hands shook. But he stood there anyway, and Rafael thought—not for the first time that night—that courage came in stranger bottles than any she'd ever sold.

"It's not meant to be," Brannic said. "It's meant to be true."

Rafael knelt before the Ledger.

Up close, the iron book smelled like the inside of a locked drawer—dust and metal and something older underneath, something like the air in a house where no one has spoken in years. Its pages were not paper. They were thin sheets of slate, and on them, written in lines of frost that never melted, were names. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every bargain the court beneath the mountain had ever struck, every soul traded for a wish, a passage, an escape from fire.

She found Sera's name on the third page she turned.

*Sera Marchetti,* the frost read. *Sold by Rafael Vasquez. Price: one life, safe passage, all memory of the transaction withheld. Term: perpetual.*

*Perpetual.* Fifteen years, and the word still went through her like a nail through the palm.

"You don't have to do this," Tam said quietly behind her. "There might be another way. There's usually another way. It's just worse."

"There isn't." She touched the page and felt the debt humming under the frost, a thread running down through the mountain's roots to wherever they kept her sister's soul in its little jar of winter. "This is the way. It's just mine."

She unslung the satchel.

Inside, the charms caught the frozen light—hundreds of small glass vials, each one holding a moment someone had paid her to take away or carry for them. A first kiss from a baker's daughter. A lullaby sung in a language dead two hundred years. A soldier's last morning of peace before the war. A fisherman's memory of his father's hands on a tiller. Fifteen years of other people's precious things, hoarded because she could never quite bring herself to destroy them, because some part of her had always known—had always *hoped*—that someday they'd be worth more than coin.

She had told herself they were inventory. She had told herself a lot of things.

"Rafael." Odile's voice, sharp now. "The wards are waking up. Whatever you're going to do, do it."

Rafael pulled the first stopper.

The lullaby came out like smoke, silver-gray, curling toward the Ledger with the sweet lost sound of a mother's voice singing in a kitchen that no longer existed anywhere but in that bottle. Where it touched the slate, the frost hissed. The line of Sera's name flickered.

"Oh," Tam breathed. "Oh, it *drinks* it."

"It drinks everything," Rafael said. "That's rather the problem."

She unstopped the second charm. The third. The fourth. Each one poured out its moment—a kiss blooming red-gold, a morning of peace spreading green and quiet, a father's hands warm as bread—and each one fed the frost-lines, and each one made the debt thinner. She could feel it working, feel the ledger rewriting itself around the intrusion, the old bargain fraying like rope under a knife.

And she could feel herself emptying.

That was the part no one warned you about, giving memories away. They didn't leave clean. They left the way blood leaves a body when you finally open a wound you've kept pressed shut for years—all at once, and taking pieces of you with it. With the lullaby went the smell of her own mother's kitchen. With the soldier's courage went the taste of fear she'd bottled alongside it, and without the fear she felt strangely naked, like walking outside without skin.

"Halfway," Odile reported. Her salt-iron circle was glowing white now, holding back the pressure of the hall itself—the whole winter court pressing inward like deep water. Sweat ran down her face and froze in her eyebrows. "Also I want it noted that if we die here, I am going to be extremely annoyed about it forever."

"Noted," Brannic rumbled from the doors.

Something hit the doors. Once. Twice. The boom of it rolled through the hall like a drum the size of the mountain.

"They've noticed us," Brannic said, with the calm of a man who had spent thirty years being informed of terrible things in exactly this tone. "In case anyone was wondering."

"We were wondering," Tam called back.

"Good. Stay wondering. Wondering keeps you behind me."

Rafael unstopped faster. The kisses and the mornings and the griefs and the joys, all of it pouring out of her satchel and into the book, and the book drinking, drinking, the frost around Sera's name retreating line by line like a tide going out. She was crying and hadn't noticed when she'd started. The tears froze on her cheeks and she wiped them away and kept pouring.

Because here was the thing she'd understood only when she'd opened the deepest bottle, the one she'd carried fifteen years unopened: the sale itself was in there too. The burning village. The stair down into the mountain. The voice that had offered her a choice, and the moment—the unforgivable, unforgettable moment—when she had chosen. Herself. Over Sera. Over everyone.

If she poured out everything else and kept that, she'd be standing in this hall with nothing left but the worst thing she'd ever done, polished and alone.

So she unstopped that bottle too, and let the fire and the choice and the shame pour into the Ledger with all the rest, and the book drank her worst night along with her best stolen moments, and somewhere in the drinking, the frost around *Sera Marchetti* began to burn.

Not melt. *Burn.* White fire crawling up the slate, eating the bargain, eating the price, eating the word *perpetual* letter by letter until—

The doors burst open.

Courtiers poured through—or the things wearing courtier-faces did, dozens of them, beautiful and hollow-eyed, moving with the wrong-jointed grace of puppets whose puppeteer had many hands. Brannic met them like a cliff meets the sea. The halberd sang. Two went down. Three. He gave ground slowly, terribly, each step bought and paid for, and Rafael heard him grunting out the old warden's cadence—*hold, turn, strike, hold*—the rhythm of a man who had done this in a hundred doorways and intended to do it in a hundred more.

"Almost!" Odile screamed over the din. "Rafael, whatever's left, NOW—"

Rafael looked into the satchel.

One charm left. One. Small, plain glass, no ornamentation. She had saved it back without deciding to, the way you save back the last bite of something without admitting you're doing it. Inside it glowed a single memory, warm as a hearthstone: a summer evening, two girls on a seawall, salt wind, a shared orange, Sera laughing with her head thrown back at some joke neither of them would remember the words to tomorrow. The oldest charm she owned. The first one she'd ever made—for herself, not for sale, back when she still believed her own past was something she was allowed to keep.

Her rules. Never steal from the desperate. Never bottle her own past.

She'd broken both, once, just this once, and it was the only thing in fifteen years she'd never regretted.

The courtiers broke past Brannic's guard—one, two, three of them, flowing toward the stand, toward the book, toward her—and Rafael looked at the last charm in her hand and understood, with terrible clarity, what the sovereign had known all along. This was why the trade had been offered. Not the charter-memory. Not her craft. *This.* Because a person who gives away everything else can survive. But a person who gives away the one warm thing, the one true thing, the single proof that she had ever been loved and been capable of loving—

That person becomes hollow. That person becomes a courtier. That person becomes *them.*

"Rafael!" Tam's voice, cracking. "They're coming!"

She thought about Sera on the seawall. She thought about Sera in the hall above, wearing her memories like fine gloves, serving a winter because she remembered nothing else. She thought about what Odile had said, days ago, salt on the sill between them: *You can't buy a family, Rafael. You can only build one, and building means putting things in that you can't take back out.*

She tipped the charm over the Ledger.

The summer evening poured out—salt wind and orange peel and laughter, all of it, the whole golden evening of it—and where it fell, the white fire roared up the page and took the last of the frost with it, and the name *Sera Marchetti* burned free of the iron book like a bird off a wire, and far below them, down in the roots of the mountain, Rafael felt—she *felt*, the way you feel a hand let go of yours—a debt die.

Sera's soul, released. Fifteen years of winter-bargain, undone by a shared orange on a seawall.

Rafael laughed. It came out cracked and sobbing and it was the best sound she'd ever made.

Then the light changed.

Every chandelier in the hall guttered at once. The frozen starlight—the harvested stars, centuries of them, hanging in their cages of ice—flickered like candles in a draft. And from the high throne at the end of the hall, the sovereign rose, wearing Rafael's mother's face, and Rafael saw something new in those borrowed eyes.

Fear.

"No," the sovereign said, and the voice was her mother's voice, and it shook. "*No.* Do you know what you've done? Do you know what feeds this place? What holds the winter together?" It raised one hand toward the ceiling, toward the chandeliers, toward the last caged starlight in the world. "A ledger must balance. You have emptied one side of the scale. So I will empty the other."

Its hand closed.

Above them, the greatest chandelier—the one hung directly over the throne, dense with captured stars beyond counting—cracked down its length like an egg. Light bled from the fissure, wild and blinding, and the sovereign *pulled*, hauling the sky's last starlight down out of its cage, gathering it into its fist like a fistful of burning snow.

"The winter doesn't end tonight," the sovereign said, in her mother's voice, with her mother's smile gone wrong. "The winter goes *everywhere*. I will spend every star I have ever stolen to snuff out what you've lit. Starting with you."

The light in its fist flared white.

Behind Rafael, the Ledger lay open and half-empty, its pages scorched, its remaining debts trembling—and she saw, in the space of a heartbeat, that the book was still hungry. Still drinking. Still *open*.

The sovereign stepped down from the throne, dragging a fallen sky behind it.

"Odile!" Rafael shouted. "How much can your circle take?"

Odile was bleeding from the nose, shaking, the salt-iron ring around them blazing like a captive dawn. "One more hit! Maybe! Ask me again never!"

"Then hold it for one hit." Rafael turned back to the Ledger, to the hungry pages, to the sovereign striding toward them wrapped in the light of every star it had ever stolen. "Because I just thought of something, and it's either brilliant or it kills us all."

"What?" Tam demanded.

"The book drinks everything." Rafael spread her hands over the scorched slate, over the empty pages where a sister's debt had died. "So let's give it something worth choking on."

* * *

The sovereign came on like winter itself, and Rafael reached for the only thing left in the hall big enough to feed an iron book built on hunger: the harvest. Centuries of stolen stars, caged in ice above their heads, every one of them torn from some sky by some bargain—and every one of them, she realized now, *memories*. The stars' own light, the sky's own remembering, bottled the way she bottled, hoarded the way she hoarded, by a court that had learned cruelty from thieves like her.

The court had made one mistake. It had taught her its craft first.

"Odile," she said, "when I say *now*, drop the circle."

"Drop the—" Odile stared at her, then at the chandeliers, then at the sovereign, and Rafael watched understanding arrive on her friend's face like weather. "You want to pour the *sky* into the *book*."

"I want to give it a meal it can't digest."

"That's insane."

"Yes," Rafael agreed. "Get ready."

The sovereign raised its fist full of dying starlight. Brannic went down under three courtiers at the door with a roar that shook the rafters. Tam grabbed Rafael's arm, and she grabbed the Ledger's edge, and above them all the cracked chandelier groaned like a ship breaking up in a storm.

"Now, Odile!"

The salt-iron circle shattered outward. The chandelier came down. And Rafael held the iron book open to the falling sky, and the stars came home to be drunk.
