## 20. Sera, As She Might Have Been

The first thing Sera Marchetti learned about being free was that it itched.

Not the body—the body was fine, better than fine, fed and bathed and wrapped in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's cooking. The itch was underneath. It sat behind her eyes like a word on the tip of the tongue, like a room she'd walked through a thousand times and could not, now, draw from memory. She kept reaching for herself and finding only smooth walls where doors should have been.

She was sitting in a chair by a window in Odile's workshop, because Rafael had said *sit down before you fall down*, and Sera had discovered in the last two days that when her sister spoke in that particular voice—flat, tired, immovable as a sea wall—it was easier to obey than to argue. Not because she remembered obeying it. Because some part of her, deeper than the hollow, recognized the tone.

Rafael was across the room, pretending very badly to be busy with a tray of tea things.

Sera watched her. That was most of what she did these days: watch Rafael Vasquez move through the world like a woman carrying something breakable in both hands, careful of every surface, careful of everyone, and never once setting the thing down.

"You're staring," Rafael said, without turning around.

"I'm told I used to do that."

"You did." A pause. The kettle ticked as it cooled. "You used to stare at me until I confessed to things I hadn't even done yet. You'd say—" Rafael's voice caught, smoothed itself over like a hand passing over a wrinkle in cloth. "You'd say, *out with it, Rafa, I can wait longer than you can lie.*"

"Did it work?"

"Every time." Rafael brought the tea over. Two cups. She set them down with the exaggerated care of someone handling live coals, and Sera understood, distantly, that the coals were her—that her sister had spent fifteen years learning exactly how much weight this chair, this cup, this conversation could bear.

"I don't remember you," Sera said. It wasn't cruel. She'd practiced the sentence in front of the little mirror by the washstand, trying to find a way to say it that didn't land like a knife. There wasn't one. So she said it plainly, the way you'd tell someone the bridge was out. "I want to be honest about that. Everyone keeps looking at me like I'm about to remember, like it's all going to come flooding back if they just love me hard enough. And I can feel them waiting. All of you. Tam does it too—he knows what it's like, so he's kind about it, but he waits."

Rafael sat down across from her. Up close, her face had new lines in it that no one had mentioned, and her hands were scarred in ways that suggested fifteen years of work Sera knew nothing about.

"I'm not waiting," Rafael said.

"No?"

"No." Her sister looked at her own tea instead of at her. "I spent a long time believing memory was the same thing as love. That if I could just get yours back to you, whole, everything would... reassemble. Like the wards. Like a broken thing put back in its groove." She shook her head slowly. "But Tam doesn't have his memories either, and he's still Tam. Kindness survived the taking. Whatever else they stripped out of you, they couldn't get at whatever made you *you*. I've watched you for two days. You gave your blanket to Brannic last night because he was shivering and too proud to say so. That's not borrowed. That's not the court's. That's just you, with nothing but yourself left."

Sera looked down at her hands. They were a courtier's hands, or had been—smooth, uncalloused, elegant. But there was a small burn scar on the left thumb she had no story for, and when she'd first seen it she'd felt something turn over inside her, faint and stubborn, like a fish under ice.

"The court gave me memories," she said. "Did you know that? Beautiful ones. Dances and dinners and centuries of winter light. I wore them like gloves, someone said—I heard Odile use that phrase. Fine gloves. And they fit perfectly, and none of them were mine, and I *knew* that, even then. Do you understand? Down there, in the dark, wearing their beautiful borrowed life, some part of me knew it was all secondhand. I used to stand in those halls feeling homesick for a place I couldn't name." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "It was this. Apparently. A drafty workshop that smells like solder and burnt tea."

"That's the tea. I burnt it."

"You always burnt it?"

"How would I know?" Rafael said—and then, unexpectedly, grinned, quick and crooked, and for one unguarded second Sera saw the girl under the woman, the one who'd been worth selling a soul for, or damning one, depending on how you counted. "Ask me again in a year. Maybe we'll both find out together."

The itch behind Sera's eyes flared. She pressed her knuckles against them until sparks swam.

"I keep trying to decide who to be," she said. "That sounds mad, doesn't it. Most people just *are* someone and get on with it. But I'm standing in an empty house deciding which furniture to buy. Do I become the person you all seem to remember? Do I start fresh? Tam says the trick is to stop asking who you were and start noticing who you keep choosing to be. He says it like it's easy. He says most things like they're easy."

"He would." Rafael's voice went soft. "He earned that."

"And here's the worst part." Sera leaned forward. "Here's the part I haven't said out loud. Sometimes I'm glad. Isn't that horrible? The court took everything, and my sister bled fifteen years and poured out her whole life to get me back, and some mornings I wake up and feel *light*. No debts. No past. Nobody's grief in my pockets. Just a blank page." She forced herself to keep looking at Rafael while she said it, because she owed her that much honesty at minimum. "And then I hate myself for feeling light, because I know what it cost you."

For a long moment Rafael said nothing. When she spoke, her voice was steady in the way of things that had been tested to breaking and re-forged.

"When I was your age—when we were girls—I sold a memory once. My own. The day our mother taught us to salt fish. I sold it because I was hungry, and afterward I thought: good riddance. Who needs it. Then for years afterward I'd catch myself salting things wrong and not know why it made me sad." She turned her cup a slow circle on its saucer. "A blank page isn't freedom, Sera. It's just a page somebody else gets to write on. The court wrote on you for fifteen years. I'd rather you write on yourself, even if the first things you write are ugly. Even if the first thing you write is *sometimes I'm glad.*"

"It doesn't hurt you? Hearing that?"

"It should," Rafael said honestly. "It does, a little. But I've had fifteen years of practice hurting quietly, and I'd rather have your truth than your performance. We've both done enough performing to last several lifetimes."

* * *

They came for her in the afternoon—all of them, in the loose, shuffling way people had of arriving when they'd clearly coordinated in advance and wanted to pretend they hadn't.

Brannic first, filling the doorway like weather, holding a loaf of bread he presented with the gravity of a man surrendering his sword. "From the baker on Tanner's Row. He owes me. Don't ask why." Then Odile, who looked at Sera the way she looked at ward-stones—assessing, respectful, already drafting improvements—and said, "Your sister tells us you have questions. We're going to answer the ones we can. The others we'll sit with. Sitting with them is most of the work; I've found."

Tam arrived last, upside down, having come in through the window for reasons known only to Tam. "Evening," he said, righting himself. "I brought nothing. I considered bringing a rock, but it felt symbolic in the wrong direction."

They arranged themselves around the workshop's big table, and Sera realized, with a strange lurch, that this was a family. Not the word anyone used—these people were far too proud and too recently assembled for that—but the shape of it was unmistakable: four people who had rearranged their lives around an empty chair because she was sitting in it.

"We're not going to tell you stories about yourself," Odile began, businesslike. "That was the debate. Brannic wanted to give you a full briefing. I wanted to let you grow into it. Rafael threatened us both with the teapot."

"It worked," Brannic rumbled.

"So instead," Odile continued, "we're going to offer you choices. Small ones. What you eat. Where you sleep. Whether you want to learn ward-work, or baking, or absolutely nothing for a month. The court decided everything for you for fifteen years. Our entire strategy is: nobody decides anything for you ever again."

"Except whether you eat the bread," Brannic said. "You have to eat the bread. I fought a man for that bread."

"You bargained for that bread," Odile said.

"I fought him *with* bargaining. It counts."

Sera laughed—really laughed, the first time since the sky had come back—and felt something shift in the empty rooms of herself, a window opening somewhere. Not memory. Just... air.

Tam slid onto the bench beside her. Up close, she could see what the others perhaps missed: the places where his emptiness showed, the seams. He'd been hollowed too, years ago, and had built himself a person out of kindness and audacity, and it had held.

"Can I tell you the one useful thing I know?" he said quietly.

"Please."

"For the first year after I got out, I tried to reconstruct myself. Interviewed everyone, collected every story, tried to assemble a Tam out of other people's pieces. And you know what happened? I built a stranger and then had to live with him." He shrugged. "The memories came back on their own, eventually—some of them, anyway, the ones that mattered. They came back when I stopped reaching for them. Turns out the mind's like a cat. Chase it and it hides. Sit still and be interesting, and it comes to you."

"Be interesting," Sera repeated.

"Or failing that, hold bread. Cats and people both respond well to bread."

* * *

It was later—much later, the others gone, the lamps turned low—when Rafael came to her room and stood in the doorway holding something small.

Sera knew what it was before she saw it. She'd seen the charms on Rafael's belt for days, dozens of them, glass stoppers winking in the light, each one a stolen moment. But this one Rafael held apart from the rest, in her palm, the way you hold a bird you've rescued.

"What is it?" Sera asked.

"The only one I kept." Rafael crossed the room and sat on the edge of the bed, and her hands were shaking, and she let Sera see that they were shaking, which seemed to cost her more than the shaking itself. "When I poured everything into the Ledger—every charm, fifteen years of stolen moments, all of it—I kept this one back. Hid it in my boot like a coward. Told myself I'd need it to fuel the last of the working. I didn't. I just couldn't let it go."

She opened her fingers. On her palm lay a small glass charm, no bigger than an acorn, and inside it moved a warmth like summer light, like dust motes in an afternoon, like something alive.

"It's ours," Rafael said. "One memory. Just one. The river behind the village, the summer before everything burned. You were teaching me to skip stones and I was terrible at it, and you laughed so hard you fell in, and you came up spitting water and said—" her voice finally broke, all the way through, no smoothing it over— "you said, *well, now the river knows your name too, and it'll tell everyone.* And we laughed until we couldn't breathe. That's it. That's the whole thing. Nothing important. Everything important."

Sera looked at the charm and felt the fish under the ice turn over again, harder this time, thrashing toward the light.

"I don't remember it," she whispered.

"I know."

"If I take it, it's not remembering. It's being told."

"I know that too." Rafael set the charm gently in Sera's hand and closed her sister's fingers around it. "So here's what I've decided, and you can refuse, and I will never mention it again. It's not a restoration. It's not me demanding you be the girl from the river. It's a gift. One true thing, from someone who loves you, about who you were before anyone took anything. Take it or leave it in a drawer. Wear it or open it or give it back. It's yours either way. That's the whole point of it. For fifteen years I owned your past and it made me a thief. I'd rather you own one minute of it and be free."

Sera held the charm up to the lamp. Inside, the summer light moved, and she thought she could almost hear water, almost hear laughter, and the hearing was its own kind of ache—not the ache of loss, exactly, but the ache of a door standing open into a warm room.

"If I open it," she said, "will it make me her? The girl you lost?"

"No." Rafael wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, like dealing with rain. "Nothing will make you her. She's gone, the way I'm gone, the way everyone's gone from who they were. But maybe it'll show you the shape of the door. And maybe someday you'll walk through it on your own feet, and it won't be going backward. It'll just be going home."

Sera thought of the blank page. Of the court's fine gloves. Of Tam's cat, and Brannic's bread, and Odile's chairs, and this fierce scarred woman who had traded her entire self to buy back a sister who couldn't even remember her.

She pulled the stopper.

The memory rose like breath in winter—small, warm, ordinary, golden. The river. The stones skipping flat and bright across the water. A girl's laugh, high and delighted, that Sera realized with a jolt was her own voice, younger, unguarded, *hers*. And beside her another girl, solemn and clumsy, throwing a stone that sank like a sin, and Sera-the-elder watching from fifteen years away felt her heart crack open along a seam she hadn't known was there—

—and did not close again, and did not need to.

When it faded, Rafael was watching her with an expression like a person awaiting a verdict.

"Well?" she managed.

Sera considered. The memory was settling into her now, not replacing anything—there was nothing to replace—but nesting, a single warm stone laid in an empty field. The first stone. Others might come. Others might not. Either way, the field was hers.

"The river knows my name," she said. "Apparently it's been telling everyone."

Rafael made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and Sera reached out and took her sister's hand—scarred, thieving, steadfast—and held on.

Outside the window, above the rebuilding city, the stars were coming out one by one, and far to the north, where the mountain's throat still smoked with the wreckage of a broken court, something stirred that was not quite finished with any of them.
