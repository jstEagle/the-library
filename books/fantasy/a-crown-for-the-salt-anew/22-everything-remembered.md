## 22. Everything, Remembered

The shop on Tanner's Row had been a candlemaker's before the Long Winter, and it still smelled faintly of beeswax and old smoke, which Rafael considered an improvement over the smell of her old trade, which had been nothing at all — forgetfulness has no scent, that was rather the point of it.

She'd spent three weeks scrubbing the place. Odile had spent three weeks telling her she was scrubbing it wrong. Brannic had spent three weeks standing in the doorway with his arms crossed, offering commentary of the sort retired wardens' captains offer, which is to say: *the lock is bad*, *your sign will blow down in the first storm*, and *in my day shops faced away from the wind*. Tam had spent three weeks being delighted by everything, including the scrubbing, which he declared better than any festival he could remember, which, granted, was not much.

Now the sign hung above the door — Brannic had reinforced the brackets twice — and it did not say CHARM SELLER or FORGETTINGS BOUGHT AND SOLD or any of the elegant, secretive little phrases Rafael had once painted for herself. It said:

**VASQUEZ & SISTERS — HEAVY THINGS CARRIED**

The & Sisters had been Tam's idea. He'd painted the letters himself, slightly crooked, enormously proud. Sera had stood beneath it for a long time the day it went up, saying nothing, and then had said, "I'm not your sister," in the flat voice she still used sometimes when the hollow places in her showed through — and then, ten minutes later, had come back inside and corrected the sign's grammar without being asked, because apparently you could not be someone's sister and also let them hang a misspelled word over their own door.

Rafael had counted that as a victory. She counted most things as victories now. It was a habit the last six months had taught her, the way fifteen years had taught her the old one.

* * *

Tuesdays were quiet days, by design. Nothing good ever happened on a Tuesday, Odile maintained, and Rafael had made it her business to prove her wrong weekly, out of spite, which was as solid a foundation for a family as any.

On this particular Tuesday — the first anniversary of the sky coming back, though Rafael hadn't planned it that way; the calendar had simply arrived there while she wasn't watching — the morning brought exactly one customer.

Mrs. Anselm, the widow from the tannery, who climbed the stairs slowly and settled into the chair by the window with the sigh of a woman who had been carrying something a long way.

"You're the memory woman," she said. "The one who used to sell the forgetting."

"I'm the one who used to," Rafael said. "I don't anymore."

"So I heard." Mrs. Anselm looked at her hands. "My husband died four years ago. Before all this. And everyone kept telling me — the priest, my daughter, the neighbor with the loud opinions — they all said I should let him go. Put him down like a heavy pack at the end of a march." Her jaw tightened. "And then your wards went up over the city, the ones fed on — what was it? Small loves? — and I thought, well. If the whole sky can run on remembering, maybe I'm allowed to keep mine."

Rafael set down the cloth she'd been using to polish empty bottles. Empty bottles were most of her stock these days; she liked how they waited.

"What do you want, then?" she asked. "Because I don't take memories out anymore. I don't put them in bottles so people can walk around lighter and emptier."

"I know what you do now. My daughter told me. She said you help people *carry*." Mrs. Anselm lifted her chin, defiant, as if Rafael might argue. "I want to carry it better. That's all. I don't want less of him. I want — I don't know. A handle."

A handle. Rafael thought about that for a long moment. Fifteen years ago — no. Fifteen years and some months, now, since the burning village, since the stair of teeth, since the iron book and the sovereign wearing her mother's face — fifteen years ago, a woman like Mrs. Anselm would have walked into her old shop and Rafael would have sold her a charm that made the grief go soft and distant, like weather happening to somebody else. And the woman would have left lighter. And poorer. And with a hole in her shaped exactly like her husband, that she'd stop noticing, which was the cruelest trick of all: not losing the memory, but losing the *missing* of it.

"A handle," Rafael repeated. "Yes. I think I can do that."

It took her most of the hour. The craft was the same craft — memory into glass, feeling into form — but the direction of it was reversed entirely, the way a river is the same water whether you're floating down it or swimming up. Instead of drawing the grief out and sealing it away, she wove it tighter into everything around it: the smell of tanning oil on his hands, the way he'd hummed off-key at weddings, the argument about the fence they'd never finished and never needed to. Not a charm that removed the weight. A charm that gave the weight somewhere to sit, so Mrs. Anselm could pick it up in the mornings instead of waking up already under it.

When it was done — a small brown bottle, plain as bread — Mrs. Anselm held it in both hands like something warm.

"How much?"

"Nothing today. Pay me when it works." Rafael smiled. "If it doesn't work, tell me, and I'll fix it, and *then* pay me. I'm trying out a new business model where I'm only paid if I'm actually useful."

"Revolutionary," said Mrs. Anselm dryly, and Rafael laughed, and the widow went down the stairs lighter and heavier at once, which was, as far as Rafael could tell, the entire secret of being a person.

* * *

Sera came at midday.

This was not unusual — Sera came most days now, usually claiming it was on her way somewhere else, though nothing was ever on the way to anywhere from Tanner's Row — but today she came early, and she came carrying bread, and she stood in the doorway a moment too long before coming in, which meant something was on her mind.

"Odile says you've been up since dawn," Sera said, setting the loaf on the counter like an offering at a small domestic altar. "Tam says you've been up since *before* dawn. Brannic says you've been rearranging the same shelf for two hours and calling it work."

"All three are true. I'm a woman of many accomplishments."

"Mm." Sera looked around the shop. At the shelves of empty bottles. At the crooked sign visible upside-down through the front window, reflected in the glass. At the little workbench where the brown bottle from Mrs. Anselm had sat, now cleared and wiped, waiting.

"It's today, isn't it," Sera said.

It was not a question. Rafael found she didn't have the energy to pretend otherwise, and, more than that, found she didn't want to. Pretending had been her first trade. She was trying to retire from it the way she'd retired from the other.

"Yes," she said. "It's today."

Six months ago — no. More. The seasons had turned once fully since the Ledger cracked and the bottled stars spilled back into the sky, and Rafael had learned to measure time in ordinary units again, which was itself a kind of healing. Six months ago, in the wreckage of the winter court, with the sovereign crumbling into hollow shell-stuff and the courtiers waking and scattering like startled birds, Rafael had done the last thing on the list of terrible necessary things: she had taken the single charm she'd kept back from the hoard — one childhood memory, saved through fire and bargain and fifteen years of anesthesia — and given it to her sister.

The memory of the river. Both of them young. Sera teaching Rafael to skip stones, badly, and laughing so hard at Rafael's failure that she fell in, and pulling Rafael in after her, and the two of them soaked and shrieking and happy in a way that children are happy, which is to say completely, without reservation, without knowing yet that happiness was a thing that could be lost.

Sera had held the bottle for a long time. Then she'd unstopped it herself, without asking what it was, which Rafael would love her for until she died.

And afterward Sera had cried — great ugly sobs, the grief of someone meeting a ghost who turned out to be alive — and had said, "I don't remember any of it, Rafa. I remember that it *happened*, because the bottle told me so, but it's like reading about sisters in a book. I know the words. I don't know the river."

"I know," Rafael had said. "I know."

"And you took everything else. You poured out every stolen thing you had to buy me back, and now you're — what, empty? Just the woman who sold me?"

"No," Rafael had said. "Not just that. But also that. Both. All of it."

They'd agreed, eventually, over weeks, in the slow careful language of two people rebuilding a bridge from opposite banks: Sera wanted the truth. All of it. Not the softened version, not the version where Rafael was brave and the village burned heroically and the sale had somehow been noble. The real one. The night, the fire, the court's agent in the road, the exact moment Rafael had chosen herself — and the fifteen years after, every bottle, every rule, every lie of comfort Rafael had told herself about why she'd never gone back.

"You'll hate me," Rafael had said.

"Probably," Sera had said, with a small terrible honesty that was itself proof she was her sister. "But I'll hate the true you. I'm tired of hating guesses."

So Rafael had spent months doing the hardest work of her life, harder than the stair of teeth, harder than pouring her hoard into the Ledger while the sky tore open: she had lived her own past forward again, deliberately, piece by piece, writing it down — not bottling it, never bottling it, that door was closed forever — but setting it into a new kind of charm, one of her own devising, one Odile had helped her ward and Tam had helped her name and Brannic had gruffly pronounced *sound, structurally sound, I checked it twice*.

A memory given freely holds differently than one stolen. Rafael had known this theoretically for years. Now she knew it in her bones: a stolen charm is a wound in a bottle. A gift is a hand extended in the dark.

* * *

They sat across the workbench from each other, the way they had sat across a hundred tables in the last year — meals, arguments, maps, the long patient project of learning each other's faces.

"You don't have to do this," Rafael said, one last time, because some rituals deserved their full length.

"I know," Sera said. "That's why I'm doing it."

Rafael set the bottle between them. It was larger than her old charms, and plainer — no gilt, no seal, just glass and cork and a thread of blue ribbon, because Tam had insisted every important thing needed a ribbon and had been, infuriatingly, right.

"The sale," Rafael said. "All of it. And then the rescue, all of that too. The Ledger, the hoard, your name burning off the page. Everything I did to you, and everything I did for you, and none of it sorted into a flattering order. It's just — the truth, in the sequence it happened."

Sera picked up the bottle. Turned it in the light. Through the glass, faintly, images moved like fish under ice: fire, a road, a mountain, a river.

"Fifteen years," Sera said quietly. "I served them willingly because I remembered nothing else. And the whole time, my sister was three days' ride away, selling first kisses to strangers and pretending she didn't have one of her own." She looked up. "You know what I felt, when the stars came back and I woke up on that cold floor with nothing of my own except one afternoon of skipping stones?"

"Tell me."

"Angry," Sera said. "Enormously angry. At them, mostly, but some at you, and I couldn't even aim it properly, because I didn't have anything to aim it *from*. No before. No context. Just fury with no address." She smiled, thin and real. "I'd like my address back."

Rafael nodded. Her throat was too tight for words.

Sera pulled the cork.

The memory rose out of the bottle the way heat rises off summer stone — invisible, undeniable, everywhere at once. And because it was a gift, freely given, freely taken, it did not seize her or swallow her; it simply *arrived*, the way a letter arrives from someone you thought was dead.

Rafael watched her sister receive fifteen years.

She watched the flinch at the fire. The stillness at the road. The long, long pause at the moment of choice — Rafael had written that part honestly, had refused herself even the mercy of ambiguity, and watched now as Sera's face went through horror and grief and landed, unexpectedly, somewhere more complicated.

"You *hesitated*," Sera said at last, voice shaking. "In the road. You hesitated. The agent asked you to choose and you — you reached for me first."

"I reached for you," Rafael said, "and then I took my hand back. I know which part matters. I've had fifteen years to know which part matters."

"Yes," Sera said. "Well." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, roughly, the way their mother used to. "I've had about ninety seconds. Give me a minute to catch up."

Rafael gave her a minute. Outside, Corvel went about its business — carts on Tanner's Row, children shouting, the wards humming overhead their long patient hum, fed on a thousand small loves. Somewhere across the city, Odile was probably yelling at a ward-stone. Somewhere, Brannic was probably inspecting something that did not need inspecting, thoroughly. Somewhere, Tam was almost certainly making a friend.

"Sera," Rafael said finally. "Say whatever you need to say. Hate if you need to hate. I'll still be here tomorrow. That's — that's the whole point of the shop, really. Of everything. I'm staying put. Whatever you find in there, you won't lose me finding it."

Sera was quiet for a while. When she spoke, her voice was steady, and it was hers — not the courtier's smooth borrowed voice, not the hollow girl from the winter floor, but the voice of the woman who had fallen in the river laughing.

"There's a moment," Sera said, "near the end. After the Ledger cracks. You're on your knees, and you're empty — truly empty, I can feel how empty you were — and the stars are coming back, and you look up, and you say—" she stopped. Started again. "You say, *let her remember it however she needs to. Even if she needs to hate me. Let her have something.*"

"That's true," Rafael whispered. "I said that."

"And then," Sera went on, and here her voice did break, just slightly, gloriously, "and then the very last thing in the bottle, underneath everything, the oldest layer — it's not the fire. It's not the sale. It's the river. It's you looking up at me from the water, right before I pulled you in, and you were *laughing*, Rafa. You trusted me enough to laugh while falling."

"I always did," Rafael said. "Right up until I didn't. And then — for fifteen years — I didn't trust anyone. And then four idiots broke into my shop and ruined everything."

"Terrible people," Sera agreed solemnly. "Absolutely no respect for privacy."

"They stole my entire lifestyle."

"Rebuilt it into something better, though."

"Well." Rafael came around the workbench, and Sera stood, and the two of them embraced in the middle of the little shop that smelled of beeswax and old smoke, holding on with the specific ferocity of people who had learned exactly how much holding-on costs, and decided to pay it anyway.

"Thank you," Sera said into her shoulder. "For the ugly parts especially."

"That's the strange thing, isn't it," Rafael said. "I spent fifteen years believing the ugly parts were the ones to hide. Turns out they're the ones worth giving."

* * *

Evening brought the others, because evening always did now; they converged on Tanner's Row the way rivers converge on the sea, without discussion, without arrangement, drawn by some gravity none of them had ever voted on.

Odile arrived complaining about a ward-stone on Cooper's Lane that hummed a quarter-tone flat and had been driving her mad for a week ("It's not *broken*, it's *sulking*, stones sulk, nobody believes me"). Brannic arrived carrying, inexplicably, a ladder, which no one questioned anymore; the ladder was simply part of Brannic now, like his scowl and his opinions about signage. Tam arrived with two new friends, a baker's apprentice and a girl who repaired clocks, both of whom had heard about the shop that carried heavy things and wanted to see, and both of whom stayed for dinner because Tam had invited them before asking anyone, which was exactly how Tam did everything.

They ate Sera's bread and a stew Rafael had been tending since dawn — since before dawn, fine, Odile, *fine* — and the talk was small and warm and utterly unremarkable: the flat-humming stone, the price of salt, whether Tuesday was cursed (it was not, Rafael had now proven this eleven consecutive weeks), whether the clock-girl could fix the shop's stubborn clock (she could, by Thursday).

Later, when the plates were cleared and the candles burned low, Tam stood up abruptly, which meant he'd been waiting all evening to do something.

"I got my name back a year ago," he said, to the room generally, in the way he addressed rooms generally, as beloved assemblies. "Tam. Short for Tammas. My mother called me Tammy when I was small and I hated it and I'd give anything to hear it again." He paused. "Anyway. That's not the announcement. The announcement is: I remembered something new today. Something that isn't from before. Something from *now*." He grinned around the table. "I remembered that a year ago I didn't know any of you. And now I do. And that's my favorite memory. It's the only one I made myself."

Nobody spoke for a second. Then Odile muttered, "Oh, that's *cheating*, you can't just *say* things like that at dinner," and dabbed at her eyes with her sleeve while pretending to scratch her nose, and Brannic cleared his throat approximately nine times, and Rafael laughed until she cried, which was becoming, she noticed, not an anomaly but a habit — laughter arriving easily, unguarded, the way it had by the river a lifetime ago.

* * *

Much later, when the others had gone home into the starlit streets — home, actual homes, plural, a word the city was relearning — Rafael stood alone in her shop with a fresh bottle in her hands.

It was small. Plain. Inside it, a single image moved: this evening. This table. Five people and two guests and a loaf of bread and a ladder leaning against the wall for reasons history would never adequately explain.

She wrote the label carefully, in her best hand:

*Tammas makes his own memories. Keep forever. Do not sell. Not for sale. Never for sale.*

Then she opened the little ledger she kept behind the counter — an iron-bound book she'd bought from a scrap dealer the month the shop opened, because irony, she had decided, was best handled head-on — and wrote beneath the entry for Mrs. Anselm, and beneath the forty-odd entries that had come before it, the day's closing line:

*Everything carried. Nothing erased.*

Above the shop, through the window, the stars were all present and accounted for. Rafael blew out the candles, locked the good lock — Brannic-approved, twice-checked — and stepped out into the night with the small bottle in her pocket, going home, going wherever home was now, which was less a place than a set of people, all of whom remembered everything.

Including her.

Especially her.

And that, she thought, turning toward the lamplit row where her sister's window glowed, was the whole of the wealth she'd spent fifteen years failing to steal, and one year learning to earn:

to be known, entirely, and loved anyway —

and to wake up tomorrow, on a Wednesday, which was, she had recently confirmed with Odile, statistically the luckiest day of the week.

**THE END**
