## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The bell above Edmund Ravensworth's shop had not rung in three days, and he had begun to talk to it.

"Not you as well," he told it, polishing the counter with a rag that had outlived several of his shirts. "Everyone else has abandoned me. The tide. My landlord. Mrs. Pellum, who swore she'd be back Tuesday for a fever-charm and has evidently decided to die of natural causes rather than pay me."

The bell said nothing, which was more than could be said for most of Greyharbor at this hour. Below the window, the salt-locked streets were waking the way they always did — slowly, resentfully, like an old man persuaded out of bed by the smell of frying fish. Gulls argued over something dead on the breakwater. A cart groaned past, its wheels singing that particular Greyharbor song: iron on salt-caked stone, a sound so constant that newcomers stopped hearing it after a week and locals stopped hearing it after never.

Edmund set out his wares with the care of a man arranging flowers on a grave.

That was not far from the truth of it, though he'd have bitten his own tongue before admitting so. The tray held eleven charms this morning. To any inspector from the Office of Purity — and one did come round every few months, polite as a cat at a mouse funeral — the tray held eleven pieces of carved driftwood, bone buttons, knotted string, and other harmless bric-a-brac suitable for tourists who believed superstition was quaint so long as it wasn't *real*.

Which was, in its way, the finest joke in Greyharbor: none of it was real. Not anymore.

Edmund picked up the first charm — a small disc of whalebone, scored with a spiral meant to turn away bad luck — and felt the familiar tremor pass through his fingers. Not a shake of the hand. Something deeper. Like a plucked string still humming long after the instrument had been smashed. Dead magic, the old crew used to call it, back when there was such a thing as live magic to compare it to. Twenty years since the gods went quiet, twenty years since every prayer in the world fell into a silence so total that people eventually convinced themselves the silence had always been there.

His hands remembered otherwise. His hands hummed.

He put the charm down before the tremor could show, and busied himself with the kettle instead. Routine was a kind of armor. He had learned that early. Wake. Boil water. Open shutters. Arrange the dead things on their tray. Whisper the morning office to a god who had not answered in seven thousand-odd mornings, if you counted properly, which Edmund made a point of not doing.

*Hallow the hour. Keep the hearth. Turn no traveler from the door.*

The words came out under his breath, half-swallowed, the way a man might mutter an apology to furniture he'd walked into. Old habit. Older than habit — older than the law that would see him hanged for saying them aloud in the square, though the law was less concerned with prayers than with what prayers used to *do*. The Salt Accords were very clear on the matter. No charms. No blessings. No invocation of the Quiet Ones, may their names be struck from every bell and book. Greyharbor had hanged four charm-sellers in Edmund's memory, and burned two, and drowned one — the drowning being considered merciful here, which told you most of what you needed to know about the city's relationship with water.

Edmund sold luck-charms anyway. Someone had to keep the trade alive until — well. Until. He'd stopped finishing that sentence years ago. *Until* was a word for younger men.

The bell rang.

He looked up so fast he nearly knocked the kettle arm into the coals, and there in the doorway stood a woman he knew by sight and sorrow: Widow Brack, née Tullamore, whose husband had gone down with the *Kindly Fortune* eight months back and whose grief had settled on her like the city's fog — everywhere, weightless-looking, and impossible to shift.

"Mrs. Brack," he said. "You're up early."

"I'm up at all," she corrected, coming in with the air of a woman doing him a favor. She was perhaps fifty, built like a bollard, with forearms that had hauled nets alongside her husband for thirty years and a face that suggested she found most of the world mildly disappointing but was willing to be proven wrong. "Couldn't sleep. The baby next door cried till midnight, then the gulls started, then I lay there thinking about money, which is worse than either."

"Money is the worst of the three," Edmund agreed. "Gulls at least stop when you throw things."

She snorted. It was, he suspected, the closest thing to laughter she'd permitted herself since the funeral. He poured her tea without asking — the good cup, the one without the chip, because some courtesies mattered more than crockery — and she took it and wrapped both hands around it and stared into it the way sailors stare at weather.

"You know why I'm here," she said.

"I can guess."

"Don't make me say it in the open." Her eyes flicked to the window, to the street, to the world in general. "Walls have ears in this town. Walls have ears and the ears have badges."

Edmund crossed to the door, checked the street both ways — empty except for a boy delivering fish, who was interested exclusively in the fish — and turned the little brass sign to CLOSED. Then he drew the curtain halfway, which gave the shop the dim, conspiratorial look of a confessional, and came back to the counter.

"Now," he said. "Tell me."

Widow Brack set down her tea. From somewhere inside her coat she produced a coin purse, and from the coin purse she produced coins, counted onto the wood one at a time with the grim ceremony of a woman paying a ransom. Six coppers. A seventh, hesitated over, added. That was a day's washing, Edmund knew. Maybe two.

"For my Tam," she said. Tam being her grandson, aged nine, who had been born with lungs like wet paper and a talent for finding every open cellar hatch in the district. "He's going to be like his grandfather, God rot him. Drawn to the water. I can't watch him every hour, and the sea doesn't take warnings." Her jaw tightened. "The priests say there's nothing to be done. Pray to the Quiet Ones, they say, as if—" She stopped herself, because even anger at the Church was dangerous, and finished differently: "They say there's nothing. I think you say different."

Edmund looked at the coins. Seven coppers, gleaming dully in the lamplight, each one a small accusation.

What he should have said — what the sensible man, the surviving man, the man he had spent twenty years constructing out of routine and small kindnesses and careful silence — what that man should have said was: *I sell carvings, Mrs. Brack. Pretty things for tourists. Nothing more.*

What he actually said was: "For Tam, I'll do better than a luck-charm."

Her breath caught. He held up a hand before hope could get fully dressed.

"It's not magic," he said quickly. "I want to be honest with you, because honesty is rarer than charms in this city and worth about as much. There is no magic anymore. Whatever I give you is wood and knot-work and an old man's knowing. But the old man's knowing includes which knots the deep-fishermen tied for a thousand years before the Silencing, and which words they said over them, and how to teach a boy to say them so they sit in his mouth like his own name. Is that worth seven coppers?"

Widow Brack studied him for a long moment. Outside, the cart-wheels sang. Somewhere a ship's horn mourned its way into harbor.

"My husband bought one of your charms once," she said. "Before. A wind-knot, for the *Fortune*'s mainsheet. He laughed at it. Kept it in his pocket anyway." Her voice went rough around the edges. "The boat sank, Mr. Ravensworth. But they found him with his arms around two of the younger crew, keeping them up against the piling till the rescue boat came. They lived. He didn't." She pushed the coins across. "I don't know if your knot did anything. I don't care. I'll pay for the *trying*."

Edmund's throat did something complicated. He covered it by turning to the shelf behind him, where the real stock lived — not the tourist tray, but the honest junk: bone needles, tide-tables forty years out of date, a jar of pickled something he preferred not to identify. And beneath a false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a small bundle of cord braided in the old pattern, three strands, the middle one dyed with kelp-black.

He had made it years ago, in the first mad season after the Silence, when he still believed craft alone might call something back. It hadn't worked. Of course it hadn't. But his hands had known what they were doing, and his hands had hummed while they did it, and he had kept it the way one keeps a letter never sent.

He laid it on the counter between them.

"Three strands," he said, and heard his own voice drop into the cadence of instruction, the old teaching voice, and was startled by how naturally it fit. "One for the shore she comes from. One for the deep she goes to. One for the man walking between. You tie it on the boy's wrist yourself — not his mother's, yours, because grief ties tighter than love and holds longer. And you tell him: *the sea keeps what it catches, so be too quick to catch.*"

"That's not a blessing," Widow Brack said slowly.

"No."

"That's just sense."

"The best blessings are," Edmund said. "The rest is decoration."

And then — because the shop was dim and curtained and closed, and because her eyes were wet and fierce and reminded him unbearably of faces he had buried in another life — he leaned across the counter, put two fingers lightly on the knot, and whispered the old words. *Hallow the hour. Keep the hearth. Turn no traveler from the door.* Not a spell. A prayer, the way a man might whisper at a headstone: addressed to no one, meaning everything.

His hands trembled. The tremor ran up through his wrists, his elbows, into his chest, that dead-string hum that had lived in him for twenty years, and for one absurd heartbeat he could have sworn the air above the counter grew *warm*, the way a room warms when someone enters it —

Nothing. Of course nothing. A draft from the door. The kettle cooling.

He straightened. Widow Brack was looking at him with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Take it," he said gruffly, pushing the knot toward her and sliding only five of the seven coppers back. "Two coppers off for the sermon. I talk too much when the shop's empty."

"You talk exactly enough," she said, and pocketed the knot, and for the first time in eight months she smiled — a small, cracked, seaworthy smile, the kind that had been through weather and meant to go out again. "My Tam will wear it till it rots. And I'll send you customers, Mr. Ravensworth. Quiet ones. The kind who pay."

"The best kind," Edmund said. "Though between us, Mrs. Brack, the loud ones tip better."

She laughed — actually laughed, short and startled out of her, as if surprised to find the machinery still worked — and let herself out into the grey morning. The bell rang behind her, bright and foolish, and Edmund stood in the settling dust of her departure feeling something he refused to name, because naming it would mean admitting how rarely he felt it.

Useful. The word was useful. Being useful to one widow and one asthmatic boy on a street that would drown him for a whisper of the old trade. It wasn't much. It was a candle in a cathedral-sized dark, and anyone could tell you a candle was nothing, statistically speaking.

But candles don't care about statistics. Candles burn where they're lit.

* * *

The afternoon passed the way afternoons did: a tourist bought a "genuine pre-Silencing amulet" (a button; Edmund felt only mildly guilty, since the tourist genuinely believed it, which was the whole engine of commerce), Mrs. Pellum finally returned for her fever-charm (boiled willow-bark and instructions, plus a carved fish she'd treasure precisely because it did nothing), and the light moved across the floorboards from west to gone.

At dusk, Edmund locked the till, banked the fire, and performed the closing office. This one was longer than the morning's, and he saved it for the dark, lips barely moving, hands cupped around the last warmth of the stove as if warming them at a smaller, older fire.

*Keep the hearth. Hallow the sleep. Guard the door I cannot guard.*

Twenty years of that. Twenty years of talking to a silence so complete it had become a presence of its own, the way an empty chair becomes a person if you stare long enough. His old crew had scattered or died or gone over to the Choir — men who once could raise weather with a word now selling insurance to fishermen, and calling it providence. Edmund alone had stayed in the trade, if you could call it that. Selling wood. Selling sense. Selling the *shape* of faith to people starving for it, and telling himself the shape was nourishment.

Some nights he believed it. Tonight, standing in his cold shop with his humming hands, he mostly believed it.

He climbed the stairs to his rooms above the shop, candle in hand, and was three steps from the top when the smell reached him.

Blood. Under it, stranger and worse: something burnt, but sweet, like incense burned in a closed room, like holiness gone septic.

"Candlelight," Edmund said to himself, conversationally, because his heart had begun to hammer and talking kept the panic in its box. "Just a man with a candle, smelling of blood and church. Perfectly normal. Happens every evening in Greyharbor."

It did not happen every evening in Greyharbor. It had never happened once in twenty years of evenings.

There was a man crumpled in his stairwell.

He lay wedged in the corner where the stairs turned, half in shadow, and even before Edmund's candle found his face Edmund knew he was dying — knew it the way you know weather coming, in the teeth, in the old scarred places. The man was young, or had been; now he was grey as ash, and his doublet was soaked black down the left side, and the wound beneath the soaking was *glowing*. Faintly. A dull gold light pulsed in the torn flesh like a coal refusing to die, and where the light touched, the blood hissed faintly, and the sweet burnt smell rolled off him in waves.

Holy fire. Edmund had seen wounds like that exactly once before, twenty years ago, on a night he spent the rest of his life failing to forget.

The man's eyes opened. Found Edmund. And — this was the part Edmund would remember all his life — the man *smiled*, with relief, with recognition, as if Edmund were the answer to a question he'd been carrying across the whole city.

"Ravensworth," the man breathed. "Knew it was... your door. Knew the bell."

"How do you know my—"

The man's hand shot out and seized Edmund's wrist with the desperate strength of the nearly-dead, and pressed something into his palm: a scroll, small, sealed with dripping black wax stamped with a sigil Edmund's body recognized before his mind did — a wave broken over a crown.

"Map," the man gasped. "To where it still... beats. You have to—" A cough wracked him, and gold light leaked from his lips. "He isn't silent, Ravensworth. He never was. He's *held*. And his name is—"

The stairwell seemed to lean closer. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"—*Vaelmora*," the dying man whispered, and spoke the true name of Edmund's god for the first time in twenty years.

Then his hand went slack, the gold light guttered out like a pinched wick, and somewhere below, out in the salt-dark streets of Greyharbor, a dog began to bark — and did not stop.
