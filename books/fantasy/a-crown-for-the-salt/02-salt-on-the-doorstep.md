## 2. Salt on the Doorstep

The trouble with living above your own shop, Edmund had long ago decided, was that you could never properly leave work behind. The trouble with living above an *illegal* shop was that you could never properly sleep.

He'd managed three hours of it — a personal record for the season — before the knocking started. Not at his door. At the *street* door, the heavy one at the bottom of the stairwell that opened onto Brine Lane, and it wasn't knocking so much as a slow, arrhythmic thudding, like a man who had forgotten how hands worked.

Edmund lay in the dark and performed the ancient ritual of every Greyharbor shopkeeper: he pretended not to exist.

The thudding continued. Patient. Terrible.

"Go away," he told the ceiling, which had heard this prayer many times and never once granted it. "It's the middle of the night. Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying. Whatever you're buying, I'm not selling."

More thudding. Then — worse than thudding — silence.

Silence meant collapse. Silence meant someone had stopped being able to stand. Edmund stared at the water stain on his ceiling, the one shaped vaguely like a herring if you squinted and were drunk, and conducted a brief internal negotiation between the part of him that wanted to pull the blanket over his head and the part of him that remembered what it felt like to be lying somewhere no one would come.

The second part won, as it always did, because Edmund Ravensworth had never learned how to lose to himself gracefully.

He took the stairs carefully, one hand on the wall out of twenty years' habit — the third step from the bottom creaked like a betrayed widow and he'd learned to skip it — and unlatched the street door on its chain first, because even good Samaritans in Greyharbor checked chains.

"Who's there?"

Nothing. Then, from the dark at the base of the stairs, a sound like wet breathing through cloth.

Edmund looked down.

There was a man crumpled against the lowest step, half in and half out of the doorway, as though he'd been trying to crawl inside when his body had finally filed its resignation. He wore a courier's oilskin, salt-stiff at the shoulders, and there was blood on the stone beneath him — more blood than Edmund wanted to think about, black in the lamplight, spreading in a slow tide toward the drain grate.

"I'm coming in," Edmund said, mostly to himself, and unhooked the chain.

Up close the man was younger than the voice suggested — thirty, maybe, weathered by roads rather than years — and his face was the particular grey of someone who has already said goodbye to everything and is just waiting for the paperwork. Edmund got an arm under him and hauled, grunting, and the two of them staggered up the stairs together in a series of collisions that Edmund would later describe, generously, as "assisted ascent."

The third step creaked anyway. Of course it did.

"Easy. Easy. You picked a fine establishment to bleed on, friend. I'll have you know the floors are original."

The man made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a lung giving notice. His hand came up and clutched at Edmund's sleeve with shocking strength, and Edmund felt something wrong in that grip — heat, absurd heat, like holding a kettle that had been left on a fire.

"Inside," the man rasped. "Door. Lock it."

"I locked it."

"*Lock it.*"

Edmund locked it again, because dying men get priority on instructions, and then dragged him up to the shop and laid him out on the floor between the counter and the shelf of — well, technically they were decorative river stones with encouraging words painted on them. Perfectly legal. Edmund was very proud of his decorative river stones. They had saved him from three inspections so far, mostly because Inquisitorial clerks found them too pathetic to look at closely.

"Right." He knelt, already reaching for the man's oilskin to find the wound. "Let's have a look at—"

He stopped.

The wound was in the man's side, a long tear through cloth and flesh, and it was *glowing*. Not metaphorically. Not the way a bruise looks bad in certain light. It glowed a pale gold-white, the color of candleflame seen through closed eyelids, and where the light touched the edges of the wound the flesh was not bleeding so much as *burning*, slowly, cleanly, the way paper burns when you hold it to a lamp and watch the words disappear.

Edmund knew that light.

He had spent twenty years trying not to know that light.

"No," he said, to nobody, uselessly. "No, no, no."

"You know it." The courier's eyes had found his, and there was something terrible in them — not fear. Recognition. Relief, maybe, which was worse. "I can see it. Your face. You've *seen* it before."

"I've seen a lot of things." Edmund's hands were moving anyway, pulling bandages from under the counter, the good ones, the ones he kept for cuts that happened during illegal charm-work and couldn't be explained to a physiker. "Hold still. This is going to sting."

"It's been stinging since Tarnmouth." The man laughed, wetly. "Stinging is — hah — stinging is the polite word."

Edmund pressed the bandage to the wound and snatched his hand back. The cloth smoked. Actually smoked, a thin curl of white rising off the linen, and beneath it the glow pulsed once, twice, patient as a heartbeat.

Holy fire. Wound of judgment. There were a dozen names for it in the old books, the ones Edmund had burned or buried or sold to collectors who didn't know what they were buying, and all of them meant the same thing: this man had been touched by something divine, and divinity did not forgive quickly.

Except the gods were silent. The gods had been silent for twenty years. That was the whole architecture of Edmund's life, the foundation stone under everything — the Silencing, the Purges, the laws, the little painted stones, the careful small existence built on top of a grave.

So either the gods weren't silent anymore.

Or they never had been.

The thought arrived whole and cold, like a fish slapped against his chest, and Edmund pushed it away because pushing things away was a skill he'd practiced.

"Who did this to you?" he asked. "Who cut you?"

"Doesn't matter." The courier's breath was going ragged. "They'll come here. Following me. That's why — listen. Listen to me." He grabbed Edmund's wrist again, that impossible heat, and Edmund let him, because there was nothing else to do. "I was told to find a charm-seller on Brine Lane. A man who used to be a soldier. Who used to be — other things. They said you'd know what this is."

"They said wrong. I sell river stones. Very soothing. People love them."

The courier smiled. It was a horrible smile, blood-flecked and fond, the smile of a man who has been lied to by professionals and recognizes amateur work when he sees it. "You pray," he said. "When you work. Under your breath. My sister bought a luck-charm from you last month. She said she heard you whispering over it, and it wasn't a spell. Spells don't sound like that."

Edmund's mouth went dry. Twenty years of whispering into cupped hands, twenty years of keeping his voice below the threshold of the world, and some dockworker's widow with sharp ears had—

"She said it sounded like prayers," the courier went on. "Old ones. To the Tide-Father."

And there it was. The name. Not spoken aloud in Edmund's hearing for two decades, not spoken above a whisper, not spoken anywhere the walls might be listening, and now lying in the middle of his shop floor like a dropped knife.

"Don't," Edmund said hoarsely. "Don't say that name in here. Names carry. Names *travel*."

"He's my god too," the courier said simply. "Was. Before. Everyone pretended, after, but everyone still — you all still—" He coughed, and the cough cost him something vital; Edmund watched the color change in his face, watched the tide go further out. "Take it. Take it *now*. I haven't time to argue with a coward, God love him."

"That's slander."

"That's *affection*." The man's hand fumbled at his own chest, inside the oilskin, and came out with a scroll — a tube of oiled leather, sealed at both ends with wax stamped with a mark Edmund hadn't seen in twenty years and had never once stopped seeing when he closed his eyes: a wave breaking over a crown.

His hands shook. He noticed them shaking with a kind of distant irritation, the way you notice rain starting when you're carrying something heavy.

"What is it?"

"A map." The courier pressed it into Edmund's palms and folded Edmund's fingers closed over it with his own burning ones, and the heat of it sank through skin and knuckle into the bone, and Edmund thought, absurdly, *this is how it feels, this is what I've been missing, this warmth, oh, oh no.* "To where his heart still beats. Where they put it. Where they've kept it all this — all this time while we starved and called it peace."

"The Choir silenced them. Everyone knows the Choir silenced—"

"The Choir *locked the door*." The courier's voice cracked into fury and grief in equal measure, and the wound flared bright enough to throw shadows up the shelves. "But the gods walked in on their own feet, charm-seller. Or so the ones who remember say. It doesn't matter now. What matters is the map, and the seal, and you. They said you'd know what to do. They said—" and here his voice failed him entirely, and what came out was very quiet, almost a child's voice, "—they said someone finally would."

Edmund held the scroll and could not speak.

Twenty years ago he had been a different man with the same name, a mercenary with a god on his tongue and lightning in his fists, and he had stood on a ridge above the Salt Road on the night the world went quiet, and he had felt his god leave him the way you feel a tooth leave your jaw — sudden absence, shocking space, the tongue going again and again to the gap. He had spent two decades telling himself it was mercy. That the silence was rest. That the small life was enough.

The small life was currently kneeling in its own shop at midnight, holding a burning man's last hope in shaking hands.

"Tell me your name," Edmund said. "If I'm to — whatever this is. Tell me your name."

"Dannel." A ghost of a grin. "Dannel Ostray. Courier, fourth rank, sworn to the Vigil of the Deep Water. Which you'll pretend you've never heard of."

"Never heard of it."

"Liar." Dannel's eyes were losing their focus, drifting toward the ceiling, toward whatever comes next. "Listen. The wax. Don't break the wax until — until salt water. Only reads true in salt water. And Edmund—" his grip tightened one final time, and the heat of it was almost tender now, banked coals, "—they'll tell you he's dangerous. The hunters, the Choir, everyone. They'll tell you the world is safer quiet. And they'll be right. It *is* safer. Safer isn't the same as *alive*."

"I don't want any of this," Edmund said. "Do you understand? I sold charms. Little ones. Luck, mostly. I kept my head down for twenty years. I have a *routine*."

"I know." Dannel's smile was fading along with everything else, but it went last, the way smiles do. "That's why it's you. The loud ones all died screaming in the first year. It had to be someone who knew how to be small." His breath caught. Held. "Someone who knows... how much it costs..."

The light in the wound dimmed. Not quickly — gently, the way a lamp gutters when the oil runs low, flickering down and down through shades of gold to amber to a last ember-bright point, and then nothing. Just a wound. Just a dead man on the floor of an illegal charm-shop on Brine Lane, in a city where the tide came in twice a day and had not once, in twenty years, brought anyone anything but salt.

Edmund sat back on his heels.

He sat there for a long time. Long enough that the candles burned down a thumb's width. Long enough that the sounds of Greyharbor's night — gulls arguing over the midden, the harbor bell counting the hour, some drunk on Cordwainer Street singing a song about a woman named Bess with more verses than sense — rose and fell around him like water around a rock.

Then, because routine was all he had, he did the practical things. He fetched the canvas drop-cloth from the back room. He found Dannel Ostray's purse — eleven coppers and a carved bone whistle, which he set aside; whistles deserved better than the evidence pile. He cleaned the blood off the stairs, badly, and decided the stain on the third step could plausibly be wine, and resolved to start calling it wine in his own head immediately.

And all the while the scroll lay on the counter where he'd placed it, and he did not look at it, and looking away from it took most of his strength, the way not looking at the sun does.

At some point near dawn he realized he was whispering. The old words, the morning words, the ones he hadn't missed a day of in twenty years — *Tide-Father, keeper of the deep ledger, count me among your debts forgiven* — and his hands were cupped around the sound out of habit, and for the first time in twenty years the habit felt less like madness and more like practice.

Because the gods were silent. That was the law of the world, written in ash and statute both.

But a man had bled holy fire onto his floor, and holy fire required a source, and Edmund Ravensworth had been a soldier long enough to know that when you find fresh tracks, the thing that made them is not far away.

He looked at the scroll. The wax seal caught the candlelight, the crowned wave gleaming like something wet and alive.

Somewhere out in the dark of the city, he was quite sure, people were already asking questions about a courier's route. About a stairwell on Brine Lane. About a shop with its lights burning past midnight.

Edmund blew out the candles, sat down in the dark with his back against the counter, and began — quietly, under his breath, out of twenty years of stubborn habit — to plan.

* * *

The knock came at midmorning, brisk and official, three raps exactly.

Edmund, who had slept perhaps an hour sitting up, looked at the scroll, looked at the loose floorboard under the rug where eleven coppers' worth of courage said to hide it, and then — because hiding it was the first thing they'd search for, and because he was apparently a coward with instincts — slipped it into the one place no Inquisitor in Greyharbor would ever willingly put her hands: the basket of decorative river stones marked *BLESSINGS, 2 COPPERS*.

The knock came again. Polite. Patient. Terrible.

He unlocked the door.

A woman stood on his step in the grey-and-silver of the Inquisitorial service, hands clasped neatly at her waist, a smile on her face like frost forming on a window — beautiful, intricate, and a sign of very cold weather coming.

"Master Ravensworth?" she said. "Inquisitor Serah Vole. May I come in? I'm afraid there's been a death nearby, and you're the only soul on this lane who doesn't seem surprised."
