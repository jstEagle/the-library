## 3. What the Tide Refuses

The map sat on Edmund's worktable all night like a guest who refused to leave.

He had tried everything short of violence. He'd turned it face down. He'd put a ledger on top of it — *Charms, Assorted: Inventory and Debts*, which was mostly debts — as though paper could shame paper into behaving. He'd even gone so far as to make tea, because there were very few problems in Greyharbor that could not be at least postponed by tea, and the map had sat there through the whole ritual of it, wax seal glinting in the lamplight, patient as tide.

At some point past midnight he gave up pretending and unrolled it.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing, and the cruelest. The ink had gone the brown of old blood, but the lines beneath it were sure and fine — coastlines drawn in a hand that had loved what it drew, the little waves sketched along the shore of the Saltreach like a child's idea of the sea, except that every wave was different, and every difference meant something. Depth-marks in an older script. Tide-tables keyed to moons that no longer rose where they used to. And there, inland from the drowned cliffs south of Greyharbor, a small careful symbol he knew the way you know a scar: a circle broken by a vertical line, with three dots below.

The mark for a sealed holy place. The mark his own teachers had used, before using it became a way to die.

Edmund sat back and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

"Right," he said to the empty room, because saying things aloud helped, and because at this hour nobody was left to hear him be foolish. "You're going in the fire."

* * *

The stove in the back of his shop was a squat iron thing that had heated charms and porridge and, once, memorably, a smuggled goose, and it took a mercifully long time to die when fed bad fuel. Edmund built it up hot anyway. He fed it the good coal, the black shiny stuff Maren Quill used to call *widow's candy*, until the grate glowed orange and the air above it shimmered like the surface of the harbor in July.

Then he held the map over it.

He expected it to catch quickly. Old parchment, dry as a sermon, twenty years in someone's coat. It should have gone up like a sinner's reputation.

It did not catch.

He held it closer. The flames licked up around the edges of the parchment, curled around it, touched it — and slid off. Not as water slides off oil. As hands slide off something they are not permitted to hold. The fire bent away from the map the way a crowd bends around a funeral, respectfully, without quite knowing why.

Edmund's arms began to shake. Not from the heat.

"No," he said. "No, you don't. You're parchment. I have burned parchment. I have burned *books*. I once burned a love letter from a woman in Tarnwick who turned out to be married to my sergeant, and it burned beautifully, and I felt terrible about it for a week, and it *burned*—"

The fire continued to decline the invitation.

He set the map down on the table with exaggerated care, the way you set down a sleeping animal you have suddenly realized might bite, and stood there breathing while the stove ticked and cooled behind him.

Refusal of flame. He knew what that meant. Every charm-seller in Greyharbor knew, in the way that fish know about hooks: consecrated things did not burn. Blessing clung to matter like salt to skin, and fire would not take what had been given to a god. It was one of the first tests the witch-hunters ran, actually — hold a suspect's goods over a candle, watch what flinches. Edmund had spent twenty years making certain that everything he owned would burn cheerfully, right down to the prayer-beads he kept hidden in a false bottom of his tea tin, which he had personally singed at the corners to give them honest, deniable scorch marks.

And now here was a map that would not burn, sitting on his table, glowing faintly — he hadn't noticed before, or hadn't let himself notice — with a light like moonrise on water.

"Consecrated," he said. His voice came out strange. "To *him*."

There it was. The name he had not said in twenty years, not once, not alone, not drunk, not weeping, not in the fevered middle of the marsh-fever nine years back when he'd cried out every other name he'd ever known. The courier had said it with his last breath, blood on his teeth, and Edmund had heard it the way you hear your own name called across a crowded market — sudden, total, unmistakable.

Ossuar. Tide-Father. The Drowned King. The god whose silence Edmund had mourned the way other men mourned wives, and grieved over the way other men grieved over nothing at all, because you were not permitted to grieve gods in Greyharbor, only to forget them.

The map lay on the table, refusing to burn, and Edmund Ravensworth — forty-six years old, proprietor of a failing charm shop, keeper of a dead man's secrets and a silent god's grief — put his face in his hands and, for the first time since the Silencing, allowed himself to remember.

* * *

They had been camped above the Vale of Corryn, nineteen years and some months ago, and it had been raining the way it always rained there, sideways and without conviction.

Edmund had been twenty-five and already tired, though he hadn't known yet that the tiredness would last. He'd been a battle-chaplain's assistant, which was the army's polite term for what he was: a hedge-priest with a sword, licensed to bless the men before a charge and bury them after. He'd carried Ossuar's sign on a leather cord around his neck, and when he pressed his thumb to it he could feel the god the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall — distant, steady, real. The whole company could. That was the thing people forgot now, the young ones who'd been born after: everyone had felt them. All of them. The gods had been as ambient as weather.

That night, Sergeant Brakka had won four coppers off him at dice and then immediately lost two back buying him beer, which was Brakka's entire philosophy of friendship. Corporal Ives had been showing anyone who'd stand still a sketch of a girl in Millbrook he intended to marry if she'd have him, and if she wouldn't, he said, he intended to stand outside her window looking tragic until she reconsidered. And the chaplain, old Father Aldous, half-deaf and wholly stubborn, had led the evening prayers in the rain with his voice cracking like the fire, and Edmund had mouthed the responses and felt the Tide-Father turning over in his sleep somewhere far under the world, slow and vast and warm as deep water.

He remembered thinking, with the specific stupidity of the young and happy: *this will always be here.*

Then the bells started.

Not real bells. There were no bells within thirty miles. But every man in the camp heard them — heard them *inside*, was the only way Edmund ever managed to describe it afterward, in the few conversations he'd risked having about it. A ringing like the largest bell in the world being struck once, far away, under the sea. And then a second toll. And then —

Nothing.

Not quiet. Quiet is a sound; it has texture; it can comfort. This was absence. Edmund had reached for Ossuar the way you reach for a stair in the dark, with total confidence, and found no stair. He had reached again. And again. Around him, the camp was coming apart — men screaming, men laughing horribly, men standing perfectly still with rain running down their faces. Father Aldous stood in the mud with his arms raised and his mouth open, and Edmund watched the old man age ten years between one breath and the next, watched the faith go out of him like heat out of a corpse.

By morning, one man in seven was dead. Not killed — *dead*, dropped where they stood or wandered into the river walking, because whatever part of them the gods had been holding up had quietly been let go. The company never fought again. Half the kingdoms didn't. The priests who survived either went mad or went quiet themselves, and the ones who went quiet learned fast that going quiet was the only safe thing left to be.

Because it didn't take long for the questions to start, and then the answers, and then the answers with hoods and pincers attached. If the gods abandoned us, the new authorities asked, whose fault was that? Someone's. Obviously someone's. And the Hollow Choir rose out of that question like scum rising on a boiled pot, gentle-voiced and clean-handed, preaching that mankind had been spared, *saved*, delivered from the capricious tyranny of things too large to love safely — and that any man who still whispered to the empty sky was not grieving but *conspiring*.

Edmund had buried Father Aldous in the Vale of Corryn with no prayers, because prayers were now contraband, and had walked south until he hit the sea, and had stayed where the sea hit back.

Twenty years. Twenty years of selling luck-charms made of nothing but copper and confidence, of whispering half a prayer into his pillow each night and hating himself for it, of keeping his head down and his hands still and his heart locked in a box at the bottom of him marked DO NOT OPEN UNTIL THE TIDE RETURNS.

And now a dying stranger had pressed a burning-less map into his hands and said the name, and the box was open, and everything he'd packed away so carefully was spilling out onto the floor of him, and it smelled exactly like rain in the Vale of Corryn.

* * *

Dawn came up grey over Greyharbor, the way it did, seeping through the shutters like something apologetic. Edmund discovered he was still sitting at the worktable. His tea, made sometime in the lost hours, had gone cold enough to develop opinions.

The map lay where he'd left it. In daylight it looked less haunted and more heartbreaking: the work of a cartographer who had loved the coast, who had dotted the little fishing villages along the Saltreach with tiny careful churches, each one marked with the broken-circle sigil. Pre-Silencing work. Drawn by hands like his own — by someone licensed, ordained, trusted, someone who had believed the stairs would always be there.

One of the villages had a name written beside it in faded brown: *Brackwater*. And further along, at the edge of the parchment where the cliffs marched south toward the drowned country, the cartographer had drawn the vault itself — or the entrance to it — a staircase descending into a stylized sea, with the broken-circle mark at its foot, and beneath it, in the old liturgical shorthand Edmund hadn't read in two decades, a single line:

*Where the heart keeps time, the tide returns.*

His eyes were stinging. He told himself it was the coal smoke.

"You're not a message," he informed the map. "You're a trap. Some trap with a lovely hand and a sentimental streak, laid by people who want me dead, and the correct response is to fold you up, put you in the stove — which doesn't work, fine, the *harbor* then, weighted and sunk — and go back to selling luck to widows until I die of old age, which at this rate will be Thursday."

The map did not dignify this with a response. Maps rarely do.

But his hands were already moving, almost without him, doing the thing they'd done in another life: smoothing the parchment flat, tracing the tide-lines, reading the depth-marks. Because here was the problem, the ugly practical problem underneath all the grief, and it was this: Edmund could not read the tide-tables. They were keyed to the old moons, the pre-Silencing skies, and the arithmetic of them had been priest-craft, and his priest-craft had rotted out of him like a tooth left too long in saltwater. He could see *that* the map said come, and roughly *where*, and even *when* — the next great spring tide, six weeks hence, when the sea would draw back from the cliffs like a held breath.

But the exact path, the safe hours, the marks and countersigns — those needed someone who still thought in drowned time.

And there was exactly one person in Greyharbor who navigated by the old moons out of pure spite, who kept her grandfather's tide-almanac wrapped in oilcloth under her bunk like a relic, who had crewed with Edmund back when he'd still been worth crewing with, and who owed him nothing and would laugh in his face and possibly charge him for the laugh.

Maren Quill.

Edmund looked at the map. The map, insufferably, declined to burn, glow softly, and wait.

"Oh, I hate this," he told it sincerely. "I want you to know that. Whatever happens next, I hate it, and I am going to complain the entire way."

He folded it along its old creases — gently, the way you handle a letter from someone dead — and went to find his boots, and his coat, and the last of his courage, which he checked the pocket of and found, as usual, slightly lighter than advertised.

Outside, Greyharbor was waking up to gulls and gossip, and somewhere across the rooftops a witch-hunter's bell rang the hour, clean and indifferent. Six weeks to the spring tide.

Edmund pulled his collar up against the salt wind and went to knock on the door of the most dangerous woman he'd ever trusted.
