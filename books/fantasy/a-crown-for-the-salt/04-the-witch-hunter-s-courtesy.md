## 4. The Witch-Hunter's Courtesy

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which was wrong on two counts.

First, nobody respectable knocked on Edmund Ravensworth's door before noon, because nobody respectable had any business in Saltcandle Row until the fish stink had burned off and the pawnbrokers opened their shutters. Second — and this was the count that mattered — the knock was polite. Three measured raps, evenly spaced, like a metronome. His usual visitors pounded. Debtors hammered. The widow Ashka, who bought sleep-charms for her nightmares, scratched like a cat asking to be let in.

Polite knocking meant someone who wanted him to know they could wait.

Edmund set down the charm he'd been wiring — a small brass bell meant to ward off damp-rot, worth perhaps four coppers and a lie — and looked at the map.

It lay where it had lain for three days now: wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard by the cold hearth, radiating its refusal to burn like a stone in his shoe. He had tried the fire twice more since that first night. The parchment had drunk the flame both times, glowing a faint gold at the edges, unbothered, almost smug. He had stopped trying after that, partly because it felt like arguing with something older than fire, and partly because the smoke had smelled — briefly, terribly — of incense. The kind they'd burned in the war-camps. The kind he hadn't smelled in twenty years.

Three more raps. Patient as tide.

He crossed the shop, past the shelf of luck-knots and the drawer of saint-medallions filed down to look like ordinary coins, and opened the door with the expression of a man mildly inconvenienced by life itself.

Inquisitor Serah Vole stood on his step in a grey coat cut clean as a blade, hands folded at her waist, two witch-hunters behind her wearing the bored faces of men who had kicked in a hundred doors and were disappointed not to be kicking in this one.

"Master Ravensworth," she said. Her voice was warm the way a hearth is warm when you're standing too close to it. "I hope I'm not early."

"You're not open," Edmund said. "Shop's closed till noon."

"I'm not here to shop." She smiled. It was a good smile. It had been built carefully, brick by brick, out of exactly the right amount of kindness. "May I come in? It's about a courtesy, actually. Mine, and one I'd like to extend to you."

*There it is,* Edmund thought. *The noose, gift-wrapped.*

He stepped aside. It never once occurred to him not to; refusing an Inquisitor at your door was like refusing rain permission to fall.

* * *

She moved through his shop the way water moves through a wreck. Slowly. Thoroughly. Her gloved fingers trailed along the shelf of charms without touching any of them — a hunter's discipline, because everything she touched became evidence — and her eyes did the touching instead. Luck-knots. Damp-bells. A jar of river-stones painted with protective sigils so badly that even a gull would have been embarrassed.

"Charming," she said, and seemed genuinely amused by her own pun, which unsettled Edmund more than anything else she'd done. "You sell these openly?"

"Openly as anything gets sold in Greyharbor."

"Mm. And the Wardens don't trouble you?"

"The Wardens don't trouble anyone who pays their market fee and keeps the strong stuff out of sight." He shrugged. "I sell trinkets, Inquisitor. Knots and bells and painted rocks. Superstition for people who can't afford religion."

"And do they work?"

It was the question under every question, the one that separated the merchants from the criminals in Greyharbor's long ledger of the illegal. Edmund had answered it a thousand times with a shrug and a grin. This morning the shrug came slower.

"They work the way a mother's lullaby works," he said. "Whether or not there's anything listening."

Something flickered across Vole's face — quick, quickly buried. Interest, maybe. Or recognition. She turned away from the shelf and looked at him directly, and up close her eyes were the pale grey of weather coming.

"Then you'll understand why I've come." She produced a folded paper from inside her coat — not a warrant, Edmund noted, just paper, which was somehow worse. "Two nights ago, a man died in a stairwell on Saltcandle Row. Bled out from a wound my physicians describe as—" she consulted the page, though Edmund was certain she knew it by heart— "*cauterized from within.* Do you know what that means, Master Ravensworth?"

"It means he was stabbed with something hot."

"It means he was stabbed with something *holy*." She said the word the way other people said *plague*. "Holy fire is Choir work. The Hollow Choir has been quiet in Greyharbor for years, and now their handiwork turns up dying on your doorstep, clutching—" she paused delicately— "well. Clutching nothing, according to the watch report. But the man walked a very particular route before he died. Through the fish-market. Past the tannery. Down streets that don't lead anywhere useful except—" she smiled again, that careful, constructed smile— "here."

"My street's on the way to everywhere. It's Saltcandle Row. Everything leads through here eventually, mostly rats."

"Mostly," Vole agreed pleasantly. She drifted toward the cold hearth, and Edmund's heart performed a slow, deliberate act of sabotage against his chest. She crouched, examined the ash, the loose floorboard — glanced at it, only glanced, but hunters don't glance — and rose. "Master Ravensworth, may I speak plainly?"

"I'd be honored."

"The Church of the Ninefold Silence offers protection to citizens who cooperate. Full protection. Whatever this man was running from — whatever found him on your stairs — it will not find *you*, if you stand behind the Inquisition. We are very good at standing between people and the things that hunt them." She tilted her head. "All we ask in return is honesty. And whatever the honest man is holding."

"And if the honest man isn't holding anything?"

"Then the offer stands empty, and I'll have wasted a perfectly good morning, and I'll apologize for the intrusion, and I'll leave." She said it lightly, easily, and then let the lightness drain out of her voice like water out of a cracked cup. "But Master Ravensworth — and I say this with genuine respect — you have the hands of a charmer. I watched them while you talked. You keep them still the way soldiers keep still, because stillness is a skill you learned for a reason. Men with nothing to hide fidget. Men with everything to hide go quiet." Her pale eyes didn't blink. "You went very quiet just now."

Edmund looked at his hands. They were, indeed, perfectly still. Twenty years of practice, holding dead magic the way a man holds a dead bird — gently, uselessly, remembering when it flew.

"Inquisitor," he said slowly, "I'm going to tell you the truth, and then I'm going to ask you to leave, because I have inventory to finish and you're bad for business."

"Please."

"A man died on my stairs. I found him. He was bleeding and burning and beyond saving, and I held his head while he went, because that's what you do for a dying man even when he's a stranger. He never gave me anything. He never said anything I understood. If he was carrying something, the tide took it, or the watch took it, or it burned up with the rest of him." He met her eyes and lied — flat, clean, whole, the first true lie he'd told in six years of selling false truths. "I have nothing of his. I wish to every silent god that I did, because a man who bleeds holy fire deserves better than to die anonymous on a stranger's steps, and I'd like to know what killed him."

Silence. The two hunters by the door shifted, waiting for a signal that didn't come.

Vole studied him for a long moment. Then, softly: "To every silent god."

"That's what I said."

"Yes." She straightened her coat. "It's an odd habit, isn't it — praying to gods who don't answer. Half this city still does it. Out of habit, they say." She moved toward the door, and the hunters snapped upright. At the threshold she paused, back to him, and Edmund saw her shoulders settle into some private resolution. "For what it's worth, Master Ravensworth, I believe you. That's rare, for me. I believe perhaps one man in twenty, and I've never once enjoyed being right about the other nineteen." She turned, and the constructed smile was back, and behind it, faint as a lamp behind a curtain, was something that looked almost like regret. "My offer stays open. Protection for cooperation. Any hour. Any day."

"And if I never take it?"

"Then I'll hope you never need it." She stepped down onto the cobbles, then paused once more. "Oh — one small thing. The wound that killed your visitor. Holy fire leaves residue. It burns the air around it for days. My sensitives can taste it in a room the way you'd taste smoke." She looked back at him, mild as milk. "Your stairwell tastes *terrible*, Master Ravensworth. Like a cathedral on fire. Whatever he was carrying passed through those stairs, and it was soaked in divinity." A beat. "Good day. Do mind the damp — I hear your bells help with that."

And she was gone, grey coat swallowed by the grey street, leaving Edmund standing in his own doorway with his heart hammering a war-drum rhythm and the taste of his own lie souring beautifully on his tongue.

He closed the door. Leaned against it. Laughed once — a short, cracked sound with no humor in it.

Then he went to the floorboard, lifted the map, and stared at it as if it might, at last, explain itself.

It didn't. It just lay there, patient as tide, smelling faintly of incense and old wars.

* * *

Serah Vole sat in the carriage and did not write anything down, because writing things down meant admitting uncertainty, and Serah Vole had built a career on the absence of uncertainty the way other women built careers on marriages.

"He's lying," said Brother Cassel from the opposite seat. He was young, earnest, and shaped like a question mark, and he had been Vole's shadow for two years on the strength of a talent for sensing residue that bordered on miraculous. "The stairwell's drenched in it. Whatever the courier carried, Ravensworth touched it. Held it, maybe. He's got trace on his *hands*, Inquisitor — I could smell it from the doorstep. Old divinity. Deep. Like—" he groped for it— "like a chapel that's been underwater."

"I know," Vole said.

"You *know*?" Cassel's voice climbed. "Then why did we leave? Why did we let him—"

"Because, Brother, if I arrest a charm-seller on suspicion of residue, I get a frightened tradesman, a sealed room, and a magistrate telling me I've overstepped. And somewhere across this city, the people who put a hole of holy fire through that courier go on walking." She looked out the window at Saltcandle Row sliding past, at the crooked shops and the salt-eaten gables, at the small gray lives stacked on top of each other like wet kindling. "I don't want the map, Cassel. I want the people who killed to protect it. The map is bait. Ravensworth is the hook. And the fish—" she exhaled slowly— "the fish are already circling. Did you see the window across the lane? Third floor, the shuttered one."

Cassel blinked. "No."

"There was a man behind it. He watched our entire visit, and he wasn't watching me." She tapped one gloved finger against her knee, once, twice — the only tic she permitted herself, and only in private. "He was watching the door. Counting. Learning the rhythms of the house." Her voice cooled several degrees, settling into the register her subordinates called *the deep water*. "The Choir knows where the map is. They've known for days. Which means our charming charm-seller is a dead man walking unless someone stands between him and them."

"So we protect him. Arrest him for his own safety—"

"We *watch* him. Quietly. Lom and Ferris stay on the street in shifts. If the Choir moves, we move faster." She was quiet a moment, watching the city scroll by, all its ten thousand secrets going about their morning business. "He prayed, Cassel."

"Sir?"

"'To every silent god.' He said it naturally. Not like a man mocking. Like a man *keeping* something." She turned her pale gaze from the window and found her junior watching her with that open, worried face. "Twenty years ago I took vows to the Ninefold Silence. I was told the gods abandoned us — that they went quiet out of indifference, or death, or disgust, and that the Church remained to guard mankind in the dark. I have burned a great many people on the strength of that story." Her finger tapped her knee. Stopped. "Lately I keep meeting men who pray to the silence like it's a grave they visit. Not an absence. A *grave*. You don't bring flowers to an absence, Brother."

"You think the gods didn't leave."

"I think," Vole said carefully, "that I have spent twenty years hunting heretics for worshipping gods who may not have gone anywhere at all. And I think the Hollow Choir agrees with me, which is the most frightening part, because the Choir kills to keep that truth buried." She looked back out the window. Somewhere behind them, in a crooked shop on Saltcandle Row, a man was lying to the Inquisition with steady hands and a soldier's stillness, and somewhere ahead of him, men with knives and hymns were learning the rhythms of his door. "Set the watches, Cassel. And send word to the archive — I want everything on file about the Silencing. Everything. Including the parts the Church doesn't circulate."

"The restricted folios? Inquisitor, that requires a sanction from the—"

"I know what it requires." Her reflection in the glass was thin and grey and very tired, suddenly, in a way she would never permit her face to be. "Get it anyway. Tell them I'm investigating the courier's murder and I require full doctrinal context."

"And if they refuse?"

Vole watched the city pass — the harbor masts, the salt-crusted walls, the bell towers that had rung nothing but hours for twenty years.

"Then I'll consider," she said quietly, "exactly whose side the silence is on."

Behind them, Saltcandle Row disappeared into the morning fog, and in a shuttered room above a chandler's shop, a watcher finally moved — slipping out into the grey with a message to carry and a hymn stuck in his throat like a fishbone.

By nightfall, Edmund Ravensworth would have two shadows instead of one.

He would notice neither of them.

He would be too busy trying, for the fourth time, to make a piece of parchment burn — and this time, when the flames refused and the incense-smell rose thick as a memory, the map would show him something new: a second line of ink, surfacing slow through the gold-lit fibers like a body rising in dark water.

Not one destination.

Two.
