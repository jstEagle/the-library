## 5. Nine Bells for the Drowned

Maren Quill had three rules, and she'd kept all of them for eleven years: never drink before the second bell, never lend money to a man with clean boots, and never, under any circumstances, let Edmund Ravensworth back into her life.

She was breaking the third rule before noon, which meant she'd already broken the first, which meant the day was going sideways from the start.

The Saltgut Tavern smelled of tar, boiled fish, and the particular despair of men who had once sailed rivers and now mostly sailed gossip. Maren occupied her usual corner — the one with the cracked table, the view of both doors, and a chair nobody sat in because she'd once broken a man's nose for trying. She was on her second cup of something the barkeep called rum and she called an insult to sailors everywhere when the door opened and the light bent around a tall shape she knew better than her own handwriting.

*Well, hell,* she thought. *Eleven years, and he still walks like a man apologizing to the room.*

Edmund Ravensworth had aged the way driftwood ages — grayed, hardened, still recognizably what the sea had made of him. He'd been twenty-six the last time she'd seen him, all sharp angles and sharper tongue, the best tide-reader she'd ever crewed with and the worst liar this side of the Brack. Now he was forty-something and dressed like a respectable shopkeeper, which on Edmund was about as convincing as a fish in a waistcoat.

He spotted her. She watched him consider, briefly, pretending he hadn't. She raised her cup in a toast that said *I see you, you great coward, get over here.*

He came.

"Maren." He said her name the way men say the names of old debts.

"Edmund." She let it hang. "Sit down before someone recognizes you and I have to explain why I know you."

He sat. Up close he looked worse — hollowed under the eyes, hands not quite steady where they rested on the table. Maren had spent thirty years reading water, and men were just water that walked. This one was in flood.

"You look terrible," she said, because warmth had never once gotten her what she wanted.

"You look exactly the same."

"Flatterer." She pushed the rum toward him. "Drink. You've the look of a man who needs to be drunk before he says why he's here."

"I'm not here for—" He stopped. Took the cup. Drank it like medicine, which it was. "All right."

"All right what?"

"All right, I need to be drunk first."

Maren grinned despite herself. Eleven years, and the boy could still make her mouth betray her. She signaled for two more and settled in. "Then talk while it comes. Small talk. You remember small talk? Weather. Boats. The price of salt."

"The price of salt's criminal."

"See? You're a natural." She leaned back. "How's the shop? Still selling your little — what do we call them now — *commemorative medallions*?"

"Commemorative medallions," Edmund agreed solemnly, "of purely sentimental value, with no functional properties whatsoever, as attested by the Greyharbor Charter on Public Safety."

"Charmed, I'm sure." She meant it as a joke. He didn't laugh, and she watched the joke die on the table between them like something small and cold. "Edmund. What's happened?"

He looked at the door. Looked at the window. Looked at his own hands, which were trembling in a way she didn't like at all, because she remembered those hands steady on a line in a storm that had drowned four other ships.

"I need you to read tide-marks," he said. "Old ones. Pre-Silencing."

The tavern noise seemed to pull back from their table, like water before a wave.

"No," Maren said.

"You haven't heard the rest."

"I don't need the rest. *No* is a complete chart, Edmund. Pre-Silencing tide-marks means old water. Old water means the drowned places. The drowned places mean the vaults, or the wrecks, or whatever else the Silencing put under the salt, and every one of those roads ends the same way — with you dead, me dead, or worse, both of us *talked about*." She tapped the table. "I have a boat. I have a crew of two idiots and a boy who owes me money. I have a quiet life running cargo that's only slightly illegal, and I intend to die old and rich and unremarkable. That is the whole of my ambition, and you are sitting on it."

"I haven't even shown you the map."

"You don't need to show me the map. The map is a door, and doors are how trouble gets in." She drained her cup and stood. "It was good to see you. Genuinely. You look terrible, your shop is a felony, and I hope you die in bed at ninety. Goodbye."

She got four steps.

"Maren."

And there it was. That voice. Not loud — Edmund had never needed to be loud. He had the other thing, the thing she'd spent eleven years trying to forget: he could put a whole argument into a single word, the way a bell puts a whole morning into one ring. She'd crewed with him through the last years before the gods went quiet, back when his god still spoke to him, and she'd watched that voice talk a mutinous deck into calm twice. She'd sworn never to answer it again.

She turned around.

*Weak,* she told herself. *You're getting soft. The rum's gone to your principles.*

"The courier who brought me this," Edmund said quietly, "died on my stairs. Burned from the inside by something holy. He said a name before he went — the old name, the true one — and he put a map in my hands that won't burn, won't tear, and shows water that hasn't existed in twenty years." He held her eyes. "The witch-hunters are already asking after me. The people who killed him are already hunting me. I didn't come here to drag you in. I came because if I'm going to drown, I'd rather it be in water someone competent is reading."

Maren stood there with her hand on the back of a chair and hated him, in the old way, the way you hate a song you can't stop humming.

"You always were a sentimental liar," she said. "'Didn't come to drag me in.' You came to my tavern, in daylight, knowing I'd say yes. You counted on it, you wretched, careful man."

"Yes," Edmund said. "I did."

*There it is,* she thought. *Eleven years of quiet, and the first honest thing he says to me is a confession.*

* * *

They took the map to the back room, because Maren refused to open it at the table where Ollie the barkeep could see, and Ollie saw everything, and what Ollie saw, the whole harbor heard by supper.

It was worse than she'd expected. She'd expected a chart, yellowed and cracked. What Edmund unrolled across the crates was something else — the parchment had a give to it, almost like skin, and the ink had sunk so deep it seemed to come from *underneath* the surface rather than on it. The tide-marks were the old kind, the kind that hadn't been legal to draw since before Maren was old enough to steal a boat: concentric rings, depth-spirals, and at the center, a mark she'd only ever seen in her grandmother's forbidden stories.

The drowned bell.

"There," Edmund said, tapping it. "That's what I need you for. I can read the coast, the channels, the depths. But these—" his finger traced the rings around the bell, "—these aren't depths. I've been over them a hundred times. They're not measurements of anything."

Maren leaned close. The rings weren't rings, now that she really looked. They were *lines of a song.* Notes, written the old way, the way tide-priests had once written the water — because the old faith held that the sea was a hymn the gods were singing, and if you knew the tune, you knew where the water would go.

"Nine bells," she said slowly. "It's a tide-song. Nine rings, nine bells. My gran used to hum it. Said her mother learned it off a tide-priest before the Silencing." She hummed it without meaning to — three notes up, two down, a long hold — and the hairs on her arms stood up, because Edmund hummed the next phrase without thinking either, and the two halves fit together like a hatch and its coaming.

They stared at each other.

"You knew it," she said.

"I didn't know I knew it." He looked shaken. "It's just — in my head. It's always been in my head."

*Of course it has,* Maren thought, and didn't say. *You were his. Before. Everyone knew it, and you were the last to admit it.* Edmund Ravensworth had been a godsworn mercenary back when that still meant something — sworn to the Tide-Father himself, if the stories were true, though Edmund had only ever said *my god* the way a man says *my late wife*, with a grief too big to name. And then one night twenty years ago every bell in every temple in the world had rung nine times and gone silent, and every god with it, and half the godsworn had gone mad with the loss and the other half had gone quiet like Edmund, and the world had decided magic was a disease and started burning it out of people.

She'd watched him survive that. She'd crewed with him after — the smuggling years, the hungry years, running charms and relics past the witch-hunters while he smiled his shopkeeper's smile and slowly turned into a man who apologized to rooms. And then the quarrel, eleven years back. She'd said something unforgivable about his god. He'd said something unforgivable about her brother. Neither of them had spoken since.

"You could have come to me with this years ago," she said, and heard her own voice come out harder than she'd intended.

"There was nothing to come with. There was a silent god and a shop full of illegal trinkets. There was no *this*." He touched the map. "Now there's this."

"And what exactly is this, Edmund? What's at the end of your nine bells? Treasure? The vault? His *heart*?" She'd heard the rumors — everyone who'd sailed before the Silencing had heard the rumors — that the gods hadn't died, they'd drowned, and their hearts were still down there in the flooded dark, beating like a second tide.

"I don't know," Edmund said. "I know a man died to bring it to me. I know the Choir wants it badly enough to burn couriers. I know the witch-hunters want it badly enough to be polite about it, which is worse. And I know that for twenty years I've been praying every morning to a god who never answered, and this is the first thing in two decades that feels like an *answer*, even if it's a terrible one." He looked at her, and there it was — not the shopkeeper, not the smuggler, but the man from before, the one the water had made. "I'm going regardless, Maren. With you or without you."

"Without me you'll drown by the second ring. These marks are pre-Silencing water — the channels have shifted, the flats have risen, half of what's charted here is now a sandbar with a graveyard on it. You need someone who can read the *new* water and the old together." She sighed, long and theatrical, and held out her hand. "Half."

"Half of what?"

"Half of whatever's down there. Coin, relics, salvage, divine favor — I'm not particular, I'll take payment in kind. Half, plus my boat gets first refusal on any cargo we bring up. And you never lie to me again. Not even kindly. Especially not kindly."

Edmund took her hand. His grip was steadier than it had been an hour ago, which she suspected was the map's doing and not the rum's. "Half," he said. "And no more lies."

"You're still lying right now, by the way. You said you'd rather drown in water someone competent was reading. That was flattery. You came because I'm the only fool left in Greyharbor who can read those marks and the only fool left who'd say yes."

"I did consider those factors."

"I'm sure you did, you cold-blooded eel." But she was grinning, and worse, he was grinning, and eleven years fell away between them like a coat they'd both been tired of wearing.

* * *

They spent the rest of the afternoon in the back room with the map, two cups, and a bottle that Maren defended with her elbow. It was almost — almost — like the old days: Edmund's finger on the coast, hers on the water, the argument flowing easy as tide.

"The Brack channel's moved east," she said, marking it in charcoal. "Whatever this says, the real passage is here, past the Widow's Teeth. And this depth-spiral — third ring — that's not the vault floor, that's the *roof* of it. You'd come down through the drowned chapel above. Which is Choir territory, or was, before they went quiet themselves."

"The Choir's been quiet for six years."

"The Choir's been *quiet*," Maren said, "the way a knife is quiet. They stopped preaching and started digging. My cousin saw their barges going down-coast loaded with stone and chain. They're not done, Edmund. Whatever they did to the gods, they're still doing it."

Edmund was quiet a moment. Then: "The man on my stairs. The wound that killed him — it burned like the old liturgies. Holy fire. But wrong. Inverted."

"Inverted how?"

"Like a hymn sung backward." He said it lightly, but his hands had gone still on the map, and Maren had spent enough years beside him to know that stillness was his fear.

Outside, the harbor bells began to ring the evening watch. Maren counted without thinking, the way every Greyharbor child did — one, two, three — and then stopped counting, because the bells kept going. Four. Five. Six.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

They rang nine times, and then they stopped — not fading, not trailing off, but *stopping*, clean as a cut rope, in a way bells did not stop. The whole tavern went silent. Somewhere a glass broke. Out on the water, Maren could hear the gulls go up all at once, a thousand voices, screaming toward the sea.

Edmund was standing. He had the map against his chest, and he was looking at the window, and his face — Maren would remember his face for the rest of her life — was the face of a man hearing a voice he'd spent twenty years begging for.

"They rang nine," he whispered. "Maren. The bells rang nine."

"That's not possible," Maren said. "That's the old signal. That's *his* signal. That's—" She stopped. Because she'd heard the stories too, from her gran, from the old crews: nine bells for the drowned, rung when the Tide-Father called his people home.

Down at the harbor, a whistle went up — the witch-hunters' whistle, long and cold, and then a second one, and then a dozen, all across the waterfront, all moving toward the same quarter.

Toward Edmund's shop.

Edmund was already moving for the door. Maren caught his sleeve. "Edmund. *Edmund.* If that's your shop, it's a trap, it's—"

"I know," he said, and his eyes were terrible and bright. "I know. Stay here. Ring the map, hide it—"

"Like hell I'm staying here." She grabbed her coat and her knife and the bottle, because a woman had to have her priorities. "You just bought half a war, Ravensworth, and I'm not letting you drown in it alone. *Move.*"

They went out into the ringing dark together, toward the bells, toward the water, toward whatever had finally, after twenty years of silence, decided to speak.
