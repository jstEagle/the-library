## 6. A Debt Paid in Iron

The trouble with killing a man, Edmund had learned long ago, was not the moment itself. The moment was quick. It was all the hours after that took their time with you.

He thought about this while sweeping his shop floor at an hour when no honest shopkeeper swept — past midnight, past even the drunk hour, in that gray sliver of night when Greyharbor's gulls went quiet and the tide muttered against the pilings like an old man complaining about his knees. He wasn't sweeping because the floor was dirty. He was sweeping because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

Three days since the man in his stairwell. Three days since the map had come into his life like a debt collector, and still he hadn't burned it. He'd tried twice more. The parchment sat in its oilskin wrap at the bottom of a flour crock, refusing flame with the smugness of a cat that knows it owns the house.

"You're talking to a sack of flour," he told himself. "That's where we are now."

The broom whispered against the boards. Outside, rain had started again — Greyharbor's default weather, the sky unable to decide between drizzle and penance. He counted the sounds of the street out of habit: the creak of the fishmonger's sign two doors down, the drip from the eave, the far bell-boy at the Saltgate calling the half-hour.

And then, underneath it all, footsteps. Four sets. Unhurried. Spreading out the way water spreads when you spill it on a level floor.

Edmund set down the broom with exaggerated care, as though it were a sleeping child.

*Well,* he thought. *Either the Watch has developed an interest in my inventory of illegal trinkets, or someone worse has found me.* He looked around his little shop — the shelves of luck-charms disguised as fishing lures, the love-tokens dressed up as cheap jet brooches, the fever-knots hidden inside hollow candlesticks. Twenty years of small, careful, cowardly work. Every shelf a little apology for what he used to be.

He blew out the lamp. Darkness didn't bother him; he knew this room the way he knew his own regrets.

The door didn't burst open. That was the first wrong thing. Professionals never burst. Instead there came three soft knocks, courteous as a tax assessor, and then the lock simply *gave up* — a faint click, a sigh of tumblers surrendering their principles — and the door swung inward on oiled hinges.

"Closed," Edmund said into the dark. "Come back at dawn."

"We know you have it," said a voice. Young. Sincere in the way that frightened him most. Sincere people did not stop to negotiate.

"I have herring pickles," said Edmund. "I have a suspicious quantity of candlesticks. If it's the map to the drowned vault of the god whose name I have never heard, you've got the wrong shop."

A pause. Then, gently, almost kindly: "You just named it."

*Damn.* Twenty years of lying low, undone by one clever sentence. He could hear them moving now, fanning through the shop with a confidence that made his skin prickle — they weren't searching, they were *herding*. Two by the window. One at the door. One coming down the center aisle, and this last one's steps made no sound at all against the boards, which meant boots wrapped in felt, which meant they'd planned this, which meant someone had talked.

"The Choir pays well for silence," the voice continued, closer now. "But it pays better for delivery."

"I'm retired from delivery," Edmund said. "Bad back. Terrible references."

Something flared in the dark — a hand-lamp with a shuttered hood, the kind witch-hunters used, but this light was wrong. Not lamplight. It was the color of old bone, and it came from the speaker's open palm, and Edmund's stomach dropped through the floorboards into the harbor mud beneath.

They had magic. The Hollow Choir, who had spent twenty years preaching that magic was a plague to be stamped out, were carrying it in their fists like smuggled brandy.

"Oh, that's rich," Edmund breathed. "That is genuinely — do you hear yourselves? Do you ever?"

"The gods' power belongs to mankind now," the young one said, stepping into view. He couldn't have been more than twenty-five, smooth-faced, earnest, wearing the gray-and-white of the Choir beneath a plain fisherman's oilskin. His eyes were the eyes of a man who had found an answer early and intended to die inside it. "It was left to us. We only gather what was abandoned."

"Abandoned." Edmund laughed, and hated how thin the laugh came out. "Boy, I stood on the Salt Road the night they went quiet. There was nothing abandoned about—"

The light moved.

Later, he would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail, the way you fail to reconstruct a storm. What remained was fragments:

The bone-light swinging toward him, and behind it the second cultist rising from beside the counter where he'd hidden himself too well —

The knife — not toward his throat but his *hand*, they wanted the hand that held the map, they'd been told to take it intact —

And then something in Edmund's chest, something that had been sitting quiet as a banked coal for twenty years, opened one eye.

It did not feel like power. It felt like *recognition*. Like a dog hearing its name after decades. Heat poured up through his arms — not fire, exactly, but the memory of fire, the shape of it, the way a dead language lives on in the mouth that swears. The air went thick and tasted of lightning before the strike.

The knife-hand stopped. The whole man stopped — lifted, actually, a full inch off the floorboards, and thrown backward into the shelving with a crack of splintering pine and toppling candlesticks. The bone-light guttered. Someone shouted a word that was half prayer and half curse, and the third cultist lunged at Edmund from the blind side, and Edmund — moving on muscle laid down in another lifetime, in another war — turned inside the reach and put his own blade up under the man's jaw.

He'd kept the knife sharpened for twenty years. He had told himself it was for opening crates.

The man looked surprised. That was the worst part, Edmund thought later. Not the blood, not the weight of him going down — the surprise. As if he'd expected the world to be softer than it was. As if he'd been promised.

The fourth one ran. He heard the door bang, footsteps sprinting into the rain, and some distant part of him noted: *he'll report. You have until dawn, maybe less.*

The young one with the bone-light was groaning under the ruined shelf, alive, one arm bent wrong. Edmund stood over him with his knife wet and his hands burning with a heat that would not go out, and realized he was whispering.

"—the tide that remembers, the shore that forgets, keep him, keep him, oh you silent bastard, if you can even hear me, *keep him*—"

Old words. Battle-prayers. They came up from somewhere below memory, worn smooth as sea glass, and the horror of the moment doubled on itself: he had killed a man and prayed over him in the same breath, and the prayers had felt *true*, and the heat in his hands answered them like a second pulse.

The young cultist spat at him. "Witch," he said. "The Choir was right about your kind."

"Probably," Edmund said. His voice came out steady, which frightened him more than the shaking had. "Get out. Take your friend—" he nodded at the body, and could not look directly at it, "—and get out of my shop."

"He's dead."

"I noticed."

"You *killed* him."

"Yes," Edmund said, "I did notice that too, thank you, I was present." And the gentle humor cracked in the middle as he said it, because there was nothing funny anywhere in the room.

* * *

They left before dawn, the living carrying the dead between them through streets gone soft with rain. Edmund watched from the upstairs window until the gray swallowed them, then sat down on the stairs — the same stairwell where a dying stranger had bled holy fire into his hands three days ago — and put his head in his hands.

His palms still glowed. Faintly, like coals under ash, a warmth that pulsed when he breathed. He turned them over and stared at them as though they belonged to a stranger, and in a way they did — they belonged to a younger man, a mercenary of the Vigil companies who had once lit a campfire with a word and healed a fevered child with a touch, back when such things cost only exhaustion instead of exile.

Twenty years dormant. Twenty years of telling himself the silence was total, that whatever he'd been was burned out of him like a field after salt. And tonight, at the first real threat in two decades, it had woken up *swinging*.

"Why now?" he asked his hands.

They didn't answer. But the map did — he could feel it from here, through floor and crock and oilskin, a faint tug at the base of his skull, patient as a fisherman's line. As if the parchment had been waiting for exactly this. As if every step he took toward it woke something else up along the way.

"That's not how charms work," he told the empty stairwell. "Charms don't *pull*. I make my living knowing how charms work."

The stairwell, being a stairwell, declined to be convinced.

He went down and surveyed the damage. The shelf was beyond saving. Candlesticks everywhere, wax pooled and hardened like spilled moonlight. And in the middle of the floor, dark stain soaking into pine boards that had never asked for any of this.

Edmund fetched a bucket and rag and knelt to scrub, and found he could not. His hands hovered over the stain and shook, properly now, the delayed tremor finally arriving to collect.

He'd killed men before. He'd killed *many* men, in a life he'd packed away so thoroughly he sometimes doubted it had happened — except that his body remembered, apparently, better than he did. But this one was different. This one had been twenty years ago, in another country of the soul. This one had happened in his *shop*, among his careful lies, in the small safe life he'd built plank by plank and called peace.

Survival, it turned out, had a price, and the price was not guilt exactly. Guilt he could carry; he had a pack already shaped for it. The price was knowledge. He now knew, with certainty, that the small life had always been a coat worn over the other man, and coats come off.

The knock, when it came, nearly stopped his heart.

Then he heard humming — off-key, tuneless, unmistakably Maren Quill — and sagged with relief so profound it was almost laughter.

She let herself in the way she always had, without permission, and stopped dead in the wreckage of his shop. Her eyes went from the broken shelf, to the scrubbed-raw patch of floorboards, to the bucket of pink water, to Edmund standing in the middle of it all looking like a man who'd been pulled from the harbor.

"Well," she said. "You've been busy."

"Maren—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. She was wearing her oilskin and her hard expression, and beneath both, worry she'd deny to her grave. "Word's already down at the docks. Gray-cloaks hit a charm-shop off Rope Lane in the night. One dead. Owner unknown. And a certain old crewmate of mine sent a boy round at first light asking me to come quick, and the boy was *pale*, Edmund. Boys don't run pale unless it's bad."

"It's bad."

"How bad?"

He looked at her — at the woman who'd crewed with him across the Teeth, who'd seen him do things with a word that the law now hung people for, who'd watched the world's gods go out like lamps and never once asked him to explain the look on his face afterward. Trust was a currency he'd hoarded too long.

"They know I have the map," he said. "And I think... I think using it is the only thing that's keeping me alive now. Running isn't enough anymore. They don't stop."

Maren studied him for a long moment. Rain dripped off her oilskin onto his ruined floor, adding its small contribution to the mess.

"So," she said at last, cracking her knuckles, one by one, in the manner of a woman settling accounts. "We're doing this, then. Fine. First rule of running, same as sailing: you don't outrun a storm, you read it. Which means before we go one league downriver, somebody's going to teach me what in the deep dark hell these tide-marks on your impossible map actually mean."

Edmund stared at her. "You've seen it?"

"Saw it three nights ago when you slept like the dead and I don't trust sleeping men holding secrets." She shrugged, entirely unashamed. "Couldn't make heads nor tails of it. But I know who might."

"Who?"

Maren smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile.

"The Drowned Bell rings at nine tomorrow," she said. "Somebody very old owes me a favor. Get your coat, Edmund Ravensworth. And maybe say goodbye to the shop."

He looked around the little room — his twenty years of careful smallness, scattered across the floor in wax and splinters — and understood that she was right. Whatever walked out of this shop next would not walk back in.

He got his coat.
