## 8. Downriver, Running

The coal barge *Patient Widow* smelled of tar, wet dog, and a hundred years of honest labor, which was more than could be said for the two people crouched behind her cargo in the dark.

"Your plan," Maren whispered, "was to hide under a mountain of coal."

"It's not a mountain. It's a modest hill."

"It's forty tons of coal, Edmund, and you're wearing your good coat."

He looked down at himself. The coat was indeed his good coat — the one with the brass buttons he polished on festival days so that the tax assessors would believe he was prosperous and leave him alone. It was now black to the elbow and would remain black until the sun burned out.

"I was in a hurry," he said.

"You were in a hurry when you were nineteen and it was a tavern wench's husband. Some things don't change." Maren shifted against the coal, finding the particular angle that only lifelong smugglers can find, the one that makes forty tons of anything into a comfortable chair. "Hush now. Someone's coming."

Edmund held his breath and listened past the lap of water against the hull. Footsteps on the quay boards — two sets, unhurried, the walk of people who owned the night and knew it. Lantern light slid along the barge's rail above them like a hand feeling for a pulse.

"Inquisitor's men," Maren breathed. "Vole's hounds. See the grey cloaks?"

He saw them. He also saw, further down the quay, three figures who did *not* wear grey — tall shapes in robes the color of drowned things, moving without lanterns, and the water where they walked seemed to go briefly still, as if it were holding its tongue.

"And the Choir," he murmured.

"Well. Isn't that cozy. Two kinds of people wanting to kill us, and they can't even share a dock." Maren's grin flashed white in the dark. "I've missed this. I want you to know that. Twenty years of selling charms to grandmothers, and the first night out with you, we're smuggled cargo under a coal hill. It's like old times."

"It is nothing like old times. In old times nobody wanted us dead."

"Somebody always wanted us dead, love. We just hadn't met them yet."

Above them, a voice — Vole's voice, he was almost certain, though she wasn't visible; only her hounds, and her voice carrying across the water with that terrible courtesy of hers: "Search the *Widow*. Gently. The harbormaster says no one boarded, but the harbormaster also says a great many things."

Boots thumped onto the deck overhead. Edmund pressed himself flatter against the coal, which rewarded him by shedding a small avalanche down his collar. Somewhere below them, in the hold, the bargemaster — a vast, silent woman named Odji whom Maren had paid with coin and a charm against boiler-scalding — went on stoking her furnace as if the world above her were a mild inconvenience. The *Patient Widow* shuddered, gathered herself, and began to slide away from the quay.

The boots stopped. A lantern beam swept the coal hill, close enough that Edmund could see the dust motes dancing in it, close enough that he could smell the hound's dinner — onions, cheap sausage.

Then the beam moved on, because the tide had taken the barge, and Greyharbor's hunters had never learned that the sea does not wait for warrants.

They drifted. Then the current took hold properly, and then the river took the current, and Greyharbor began to slide backward past them — the salt-stacks, the bell towers, the witch-hunter garrison with its grey banner hanging limp in the windless night — all of it going soft and small and golden-lit, like something seen through tears, or through the bottom of a very good bottle.

Edmund found, to his considerable surprise, that he was laughing.

"What?" Maren demanded.

"I'm running for my life," he said, "under a coal hill, in my good coat, and I'm *happy*."

"That's not happiness, that's terror with better posture." But she was grinning too, and after a moment she laughed with him, low and rough, the laugh of two people who had once been young together on decks exactly like this one, before the world went quiet.

* * *

An hour downriver, when the city's lights had sunk behind the headlands and the banks had gone over to reeds and ruin, Odji sent her boy up from the hold with bread, hard cheese, and a look of profound disapproval aimed at both of them. They ate sitting on the coal like crows on a fence, legs dangling over the black water.

Maren had the map out. She'd had it out most of the hour, actually, turning it in the moonlight, frowning at it the way she frowned at bad charts and worse men.

"This ink," she said. "It moves."

"It doesn't move."

"Look at the tide-marks. Watch the third one." She held it steady. And Edmund watched, and hated himself for watching, because she was right — the little curling mark that meant slack water did shift, very slowly, the way a sleeping man shifts. Not alive. Remembering.

"Pre-Silencing work," he said quietly. "The old cartographers bound their maps to the tides the way we used to bind charms to breath. When the gods went quiet, the binding should have died with them."

"But it didn't."

"No. It didn't." He looked at the map and felt the old cold move through him, the cold he'd been carrying for twenty years like a stone sewn into his chest. Because there was only one reason a god-touched thing kept working after the Silencing, and that reason was currently beating somewhere at the bottom of the sea, in a vault that this map knew the way a son knows his mother's door.

Maren watched him with those eyes that had read weather and men for fifty years and been wrong about perhaps four of each.

"You know whose map this is," she said. Not a question.

"The courier did. Before he died in my stairwell, he—" Edmund stopped. His hands had started doing the thing they did, the tremor, the ghost-itch of magic that wasn't there anymore but still dreamed it was. He closed them. "He said a name."

"And?"

"And names have weight, Maren."

"Not out here they don't. Out here it's just me, the coal, and a woman at a furnace who'd sell us both for a new boiler. Say it or don't. But know that you've been carrying it shut up inside you for twenty years, and I've carried men before, and they're heavy."

The river ran. The stars did what stars do, which is watch without helping. And Edmund Ravensworth, who had spent two decades being careful, being small, being a shopkeeper with a tremor and a locked box of illegal luck-charms, heard himself say:

"His name was Alder-marrow."

The word came out of him like a tooth coming free — painful, and then shockingly light.

"Alder-marrow," Maren repeated. She tasted it. "That's not a name. That's a *tree*."

"He was called that because that's how the old folk named him. The alder grows in the flood-line, see. Where the water meets the land. Where things change." Edmund heard his own voice do something it hadn't done in twenty years — quicken. "He was the god of the in-between. Tides. Thresholds. Doorways. Every charm I ever sold illegally was one of his — luck at crossings, safe passage, open doors. I told myself I was just a criminal making rent. I wasn't. I was a priest who'd lost his congregation and kept lighting the candles anyway."

The words kept coming, twenty years of them, tumbling like a dam finally admitting defeat.

"The night of the Silencing, I was on the Salt Road with the company. We felt it go. All of it, every god, everywhere, at once — like a great door closing in a house you didn't know you lived in. Half the mercenaries knelt down and cried. The other half laughed and said good riddance. I did neither. I just stood there waiting for him to come back. Waiting for Alder-marrow to come back through whatever door had closed." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Twenty years, Maren. I've been standing in front of a closed door for twenty years, telling myself I liked the hallway."

Maren said nothing for a long while. She was looking at the map again, but Edmund suspected she wasn't seeing it.

"My gran prayed to him," she said at last. "Fisherman's family. We prayed to the in-between god because we lived our whole lives in-between — between sea and land, between feast and famine. After the Silencing she kept praying anyway. Said it wasn't for him. Said it was for *her*, so she wouldn't forget how." She looked up. "You've been praying for yourself, then. All these years."

"Every night. Like an idiot. To a door that doesn't open."

"Mm. Or to a door that's *locked*," Maren said, "which is a different problem entirely, and one that maps are famously useful for."

And there it was — the exhilaration, rising in him like the tide the map remembered. Because if the door was locked, then someone had locked it. And if someone had locked it, then it could be *unlocked*, and if it could be unlocked—

"We're going to die," he said happily.

"There he is," Maren said. "There's the madman I crewed with. Knew he was in there somewhere under the shopkeeper."

* * *

They made the first bend of the river by moonset, and that was when the boy came up from the hold again, white-faced, and said, "Missus Quill? There's a boat behind us. Been there since the last bend. No lamps."

Maren went to the stern rail and looked back the way sailors look — not directly, but slantwise, off the corner of the eye, where the dark things live. Edmund joined her.

Far back, where the river narrowed between black bluffs, something moved against the marginally-less-black water. No lanterns. No wake, either, which was worse. Boats make wakes. Whatever followed them had persuaded the river not to mention it.

"Choir," Edmund said.

"Or Vole's lot playing clever."

"Vole's lot would have lamps. Lamps mean law. That—" he nodded at the soundless shape "—that means silence. That's theirs."

Below them, faintly, through the deck planks, Odji's furnace roared, and the *Patient Widow* leaned into the current like a tired horse that has decided, out of stubbornness alone, to gallop.

Maren rolled her shoulders. "Right. Options. One: we outrun them. Two: we fight them. Three: we throw you overboard as bait and negotiate."

"I'm touched."

"Don't be. You float; I've seen it." She squinted astern again, and her jaw tightened. "They're gaining. Whatever that boat is, it doesn't row like a boat."

Edmund looked at his hands. In the dark, he couldn't see the tremor, but he could feel it — feel something underneath it, too, something that had stirred the night the cultists broke into his shop and died for their trouble. Something that had been asleep for twenty years and was now, slowly, like an old man waking before dawn, opening one eye.

"They want the map," he said. "They've wanted it since the courier bled out on my stairs. And they won't stop at the city limits, and they won't stop at the river mouth, and they won't stop, Maren, until either they have it or—"

"Or we reach the vault first." She turned to face him fully, and in the starlight her old scarred face looked suddenly very young, the way it must have looked the day she first signed aboard with a company of idiots and dreamers. "So we don't stop either."

Ahead, the river bent north, toward the hills, toward Brackwater and the ferry and everything the map promised lay beyond. Behind, the silent boat came on. And somewhere beneath the water, beneath the silt and the years and the great locked door of the world, Edmund could almost swear he felt it — faint as a heartbeat through a wall, patient as a tide.

Alder-marrow, waiting.

"Hold course," Edmund said, and his voice didn't shake at all, which frightened him more than the boat behind them ever could. "Whatever's following us — let it follow. We're done running from doors."

Maren gave him a long look. "That's either very brave or very stupid."

"It's usually both."

"Aye," she said, and spat ceremonially over the rail, which in her language was a blessing. "Both, then. Hold course."

Behind them, on the soundless water, the silent boat drew closer — and ahead, around the northern bend, the first grey light of morning found the ferry landing at Brackwater, where an old man waited with a lantern and a price that no coin could pay.
