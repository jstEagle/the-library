## 9. The Ferryman's Toll

Brackwater smelled of fish and rot and river-mud, which Maren Quill considered a considerable improvement over Greyharbor, which smelled of salt and fear.

The town clung to the riverbank the way barnacles cling to a hull — stubbornly, gracelessly, and with no intention whatsoever of going anywhere. Houses leaned against each other like drunkards holding one another upright. Laundry lines sagged between them, hung with shirts gone grey from years of river fog. Somewhere a dog was barking at something with tremendous conviction and no success.

Maren walked with her hood up and her hand resting on the knife at her hip, because old habits were the only kind she'd ever managed to keep. Edmund walked beside her, quieter than usual, which for Edmund was saying something. He'd been quiet since the barge. Quiet in the way a man is quiet when he's been rehearsing something in his head and hasn't found the nerve to say it yet.

She'd known him twenty years. She knew all his silences by name.

"There," he said, nodding toward the water.

The ferry landing sat at the end of a crooked wooden pier, and moored to it was the ugliest vessel Maren had ever seen, which was genuinely an achievement, because Maren had seen vessels that were technically shipwrecks still taking on passengers. This one was long and low and black-green with age, its planks swollen and mismatched, its single mast bare. No name painted on its hull. Of course not. You didn't paint your name on a boat like that any more than you'd tattoo it on your own grave.

At the pier's end stood the ferryman.

He was tall and thin as a heron, wrapped in an oilskin coat that might once have been grey but had given up on color sometime before the Silencing. His face was long and mild and utterly unremarkable, except for his eyes, which were the flat silver of a coin left too long in water. He was watching them approach with the patient expression of someone who had watched everything already and found it adequate.

"That's him," Maren murmured. "That's the one the coal-barge captain warned us about."

"He didn't warn us. He made a joke about it."

"The captain jokes about everything. It's how he prays." She squinted at the ferryman. "What's his toll? Coin?"

Edmund shook his slowly. "I've heard stories. Downriver talk. They say he doesn't take money."

"Everyone takes money. Money is the one honest thing left in the world."

"Not him." Edmund's voice had gone strange — careful, the way it went when he was handling something fragile. "They say he takes memories. A memory, weighed against the crossing. Something you can spare, or something you can't. He knows the difference."

Maren laughed, then stopped laughing when Edmund didn't join her. "That's superstition. That's dock-rat nonsense, Ed. Next you'll tell me the tide-marks on our map are drawn in mermaid tears."

"Maybe they are." He wasn't smiling. "We need passage upstream. The Choir will have riders on the salt road by now. Vole—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. Inquisitor Serah Vole, who had let them go at the vigil-house with a look on her face like a woman walking out of a burning building and deciding, mid-stride, not to look back. "Vole bought us time. We shouldn't waste it arguing about ferrymen."

"Fine." Maren rolled her shoulders. "Then I'll pay. I've got plenty of memories. Most of them are bad anyway. Half my youth is just hangovers with scenery."

"Let me do the talking first."

"You always want to do the talking first. That's how you got us thrown off the *Kestrel* in '61."

"We were thrown off the *Kestrel* because you cheated at cards with the quartermaster's wife."

"I cheated *for* the quartermaster's wife. There's a difference, and it's called loyalty."

But she let him walk ahead, because underneath the banter she could feel it — that carefulness in him again, that held breath. And when they reached the pier's end and the ferryman turned those silver eyes on them, Maren felt the humor drain out of her like water through a cracked hull.

The man looked at her the way a ledger looks at you.

"Two crossings," the ferryman said. His voice was soft and dry, like paper turning. "Upstream, past the weirs, past the drowned chapel, to where the cliffs begin."

"You know where we're going," Edmund said.

"I know what you carry." The ferryman's gaze slid to Edmund's chest, to the oilskin packet beneath his coat, where the map slept its stubborn, unburnable sleep. "And I know what it costs to carry it. So. My price."

"Coin," Maren said firmly. "Name a figure. I'll haggle you down to half and feel good about it."

"No coin." The ferryman smiled faintly, without warmth or malice — the smile of a man stating the weather. "A memory. One each. Weighed fair. Small things cross cheap. Large things cost what they're worth."

"This is ridiculous," Maren said. "Memories aren't goods. You can't—"

"Maren." Edmund's voice was very quiet. "Look at his hands."

She looked. The ferryman's hands rested on the rail, and now that she truly saw them, she saw that they were wrong — not scarred, not marked, but *smooth*, smooth as river stones, with no lines at all. No creases in the palms. No history written in the skin. Hands that had given away so much there was nothing left on them to read.

Her mouth went dry. "It's true. The stories are true."

"People rarely believe until the pier," the ferryman agreed pleasantly. "Belief at the pier is the traditional moment. Shall we proceed? Who pays first?"

Maren opened her mouth to volunteer — partly bravery, mostly the desire to get it over with and see how it worked — but Edmund stepped forward.

"Me first."

There it was again. That carefulness. That held breath. And suddenly Maren understood, with the cold clarity of twenty years of reading this man, that he hadn't been rehearsing a confession on the barge.

He'd been rehearsing a payment.

* * *

The ferryman produced a small brass balance, old and green with patina, and set it on the rail between them. On one pan he placed nothing at all. On the other pan he placed nothing at all either. Both pans sat empty and level, which seemed to defeat the purpose of a scale, but Maren had learned long ago that magic didn't care about making sense; it cared about making terms.

"Now," the ferryman said to Edmund, "you'll hold the memory in mind. All of it, edge to edge. Don't flinch from the middle. The middle is where the weight is."

"And if I flinch?" Edmund asked.

"Then I weigh the flinching too. Everything counts. That's the whole trouble with memory." The ferryman folded his smooth hands. "Begin when you're ready."

Maren watched Edmund close his eyes. Watched his breathing change. Watched his face do something she had never seen it do in two decades of knowing him — soften completely, without guard, without irony, without the wry armor he wore the way other men wore scars.

And she heard him speak, though she was fairly sure he didn't know he was speaking. People under deep memory did that sometimes; she'd seen it in sailors with fever. Words leaking out like light under a door.

"*White flowers,*" he murmured. "*Someone's mother grew them along the fence, and everyone said it was bad luck, out-of-season blooms, and she said luck could mind its own business.*"

Maren went very still.

"*The chapel was small. Not the big one in the city — the little one up the coast road, the one with the crooked bell. It rang wrong and everyone loved it for that.*" A breath, shaky. "*She wore blue. Everyone told her brides wear white, and she said white was for women who hadn't decided who they were yet.*"

Maren's heart had begun to beat somewhere up around her ears.

Because she knew. She knew that voice in his mouth. She knew the crooked bell. She knew—

"*Liss,*" Edmund whispered. "*Her name was Lissa.*"

The world tilted gently, the way a deck tilts.

*Maren Quill had crewed with Edmund Ravensworth for six years before the Silencing. She'd shared rum, watches, wounds, and card debts with him. She had counted herself among the three people alive who knew him whole. And in all those years, through all those nights, he had never — not once — mentioned a wife.*

The ferryman's scales began, very softly, to hum.

"Heavy," the ferryman said, with something almost like respect. "This is a heavy one. Are you certain? A smaller memory would cross you cheaper. Your mother's face, perhaps. A childhood winter. Men give up their mothers' faces all the time; they barely notice they're gone."

"No." Edmund's eyes were still closed. His voice came out steady, which somehow made it worse. "This one. Only this one."

"Why?" Maren heard herself ask. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Ed. Why that one? Why not—"

"Because it's the realest thing I own." He still wasn't looking at her. "Everything else I've spent. Twenty years of selling charms out the back of a shop, pretending the god I served never existed, pretending I was never anyone. I spent everything else keeping small. But I kept this. I kept it the way you keep a coal in ash." His jaw worked. "And I can't keep it anymore. Not if we're going upstream. Not if I'm going to stand in front of a god's heart and ask it questions. You can't carry a life you refused to live and expect the dark to take you seriously."

The pans of the balance trembled. Between them, in the empty air above the lower pan, something began to gather — a shimmer, faint as heat-haze, the shape of a day assembling itself out of nothing: the ghost-smell of white flowers, the far-off wrong-sweet clang of a crooked bell, a thread of music played badly on a fiddle by someone who didn't care.

Maren watched the wedding day of Edmund Ravensworth hover over a brass scale on a rotten pier, and she wanted to grab it. She wanted to snatch it out of the air like a moth and stuff it back into his head where it belonged, because some things weren't for spending, some things were load-bearing, some things were the last plank between a man and the water—

"Liss," she said. "Lissa. Ed, I crewed with you. I *knew* you. Why didn't you ever—"

"Because she died." Still that terrible steadiness. "Three years after. Fever, up the coast. And after the Silencing, after everything went quiet, I couldn't— grief needs somewhere to live, Mare. It needs a house. Mine burned down with everything else. So I moved the memory somewhere safe and boarded it up and told myself I'd visit." Finally he opened his eyes, and they were wet and clear and looking right at her. "I never visited. Twenty years. You don't forget a thing like that. You just stop going home."

"Oh, Ed."

"Don't." A ghost of his old crooked smile. "If you make it kind right now I'll lose my nerve, and then the ferryman will weigh my cowardice, and cowardice is heavier than you'd think."

"It's not," Maren said thickly. "Cowardice floats. Everything cowardly floats. It's the brave stuff that sinks."

The ferryman cleared his throat with the gentleness of a man who had done this ten thousand times and never gotten used to it. "The memory is weighed," he said. "Do you release it?"

Edmund looked at the shimmering shape of that day — the blue dress, the white flowers, the crooked bell ringing wrong and beloved — for one long last moment. Maren watched him memorize the memorizing.

"I release it," he said.

The pan dropped.

There was no flash, no thunder. The day simply poured upward out of the scale like smoke finding a chimney, streaming into the ferryman's cupped hands, into his lineless palms, and he closed his fingers over it with the care of a man pocketing a bird. Then it was gone, and Edmund swayed, and Maren caught his arm, and he blinked at her with polite, distant confusion — the look of a man waking from a nap in a room he didn't recognize.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry — what was I—"

"Crossing," the ferryman said briskly. "You've paid. Step aboard. Your friend pays next."

Edmund frowned slightly, rubbing his temple, and Maren saw it happen in real time: the hole where the day had been, and the edges of the hole, and the way his fingers drifted unconsciously to his chest, to the place a wedding ring would have sat, and found bare skin, and hovered there, puzzled, like a tongue seeking a missing tooth.

"What year did I marry?" he asked the air, lightly, testing. Then, more uncertainly: "Was I married?"

And Maren Quill, who had survived storms and smugglers and one memorable encounter with a customs cutter off the Silt Islands, discovered that her eyes were full and her throat was closed, because she was now the only person in the world who remembered Lissa at all. The blue dress. The crooked bell. The white flowers blooming out of season because luck could mind its own business.

She was the keeper of a dead woman now. Someone had to be.

"Yeah," she managed. "Yeah, you were, Ed. She wore blue. It suited her."

He smiled at her, warm and baffled and grateful, having no idea why his chest ached. "I wish I could remember her."

"I know," Maren said. "I'll remember enough for both of us."

* * *

Her own turn at the scale came quicker. She gave up the memory of her brother Tomas teaching her to swim — a small, bright thing, a summer thing, cheap at the price, or so she told the ferryman, and the ferryman's silver eyes said plainly that he knew she was lying about its cheapness but took it anyway, because fair was fair and lies were part of the weight.

She paid. She crossed. She sat on the ferry's wet bench beside Edmund as the ugly black boat pulled away from Brackwater and slid upstream past the weirs, past the drowned chapel with its roof just breaking the water like a sleeping whale's back.

Edmund was quiet, feeling along the edges of his own gaps. Then, abruptly: "Mare. Back at the pier. Did I say anything? Under the weighing? People leak, I've heard. Did I leak?"

Maren looked out at the brown water, at the drowned chapel sliding by, at the grey sky promising rain it wouldn't deliver.

"You leaked," she said.

"What was it?"

And here was the thing Maren Quill discovered about carrying someone else's dead: you couldn't put it down, and you couldn't share the weight, but you could decide *how* to hold it. She could tell him everything and watch him grieve a stranger. Or she could keep it, keep it the way he'd kept it — a coal in ash — and give it back to him someday, if the world ever got kind enough to have somedays like that.

"Flowers," she said finally. "You talked about flowers. White ones, growing out of season."

Edmund nodded slowly, and something in his face eased, some ache finding a nameless shape it fit. "Out of season," he repeated softly. "That sounds right. That sounds like something I'd have loved."

Ahead, the river narrowed, and the cliffs rose up dark against the afternoon, and Maren saw, moored in a hidden cove below the cliff-shadow, ships flying no colors at all — lean, silent ships, and figures moving on their decks in robes the grey of dead coals.

The Hollow Choir had reached the water first.

"Well," Maren said, standing, loosening her knife in its sheath. "Hope you didn't spend all your courage on the pier, Ed. Because I think we're about to need a refund."
