## 10. Ashes of the Vigil

The congregation found them before they found the congregation, which was the first sign that something strange was happening in the hills above Brackwater.

Edmund had been following Maren's lead up a goat track that pretended to be a road, his lungs full of cold air and his boots full of worse, when a boy stepped out from behind a boulder. He couldn't have been more than fourteen, wrapped in a fisherman's oilskin three sizes too big, and he looked at Edmund the way people in Greyharbor looked at the tide charts — with total, desperate attention.

"You're him," the boy said.

"I'm nobody," Edmund said automatically. It was a well-practiced line. Twenty years of selling illegal charms taught you to be nobody with real conviction.

"You're *him*," the boy repeated, with the unshakable certainty of youth and bad information. "The herald. Come on, they've been waiting since before I was born."

Maren looked at Edmund over the boy's head. Her expression was one he'd been cataloguing for two weeks now: half amusement, half warning, like a woman watching someone walk toward a cliff edge with excellent posture. "Well," she said. "Congratulations. You've been promoted."

"I didn't apply."

"Nobody ever does. That's how you know it's destiny." She shouldered her pack and followed the boy up the track, leaving Edmund no dignified option but to follow too, which was, he was beginning to understand, how Maren Quill made most of her decisions for her.

* * *

The vigil-house had been a chapel once, back when chapels were legal, and then a barn, and now it was something that refused to be either. The stone walls still bore the old carvings — waves, bells, a figure with its arms open — worn soft by weather and by hands that had touched them in the dark for twenty years. Someone had patched the roof with boat planking. Someone else had hung lanterns made from jam jars and prayer-book pages, which struck Edmund as either profound or blasphemous depending on how long you'd been hungry.

There were perhaps forty of them. Old dockworkers with salt-cured knuckles. Women who'd been young when the gods went quiet and were not anymore. Children born into the silence who had never heard a god speak and believed in one anyway, which was either the purest faith or the stubbornest, and Edmund suspected the difference didn't matter to them.

They came out of the woodwork the way mice come out when a house goes quiet — except these mice came out singing. Low, wordless, a hum that started somewhere near the floor and rose until Edmund felt it in his teeth. A hymn without words because the words had been outlawed, and so they'd kept the melody and thrown away everything dangerous about it.

"Twenty years," said an old woman, taking his hand in both of hers. Her palms were rough as whetstones. "We said the Vigil would end when the herald came. Some nights we thought we were fools. Most nights, honestly." She laughed, wet-eyed. "But you came."

"I came because my boat sank," Edmund said.

"Providence," she said, nodding, and would not be moved on the point.

He tried, over the next hour, to be moved off it. He explained, gently at first and then with increasing specificity, that he was a charm-seller of modest talent and considerable debt; that he had fled Greyharbor ahead of a witch-hunter and a knife; that the map in his coat was a matter of geography rather than prophecy. The old woman — her name was Doss, and she ran the place with the iron patience of someone who had raised nine children and buried four — listened to all of it with the serene attention of a woman listening to weather.

"Maren says you carried the name," Doss said.

"Maren talks too much."

"She does," Doss agreed warmly. "It's why I trust her. Liars economize." She poured him tea that tasted of pine needles and hope, and pressed his hand again. "You don't have to be anything tonight. Eat. Sleep. Tomorrow is soon enough to be the beginning of everything."

That night he lay on a pallet of straw in a room that smelled of tallow and lamp oil, listening to forty people breathe in the next chamber, and understood that he had made a serious error. You could lie to a witch-hunter. You could lie to cultists with knives. But you could not lie to people who had spent twenty years keeping a candle lit for you specifically, because their faith did not require your cooperation. It simply stood there, warm and enormous, like a tide coming in, and waited for you to decide whether to swim.

* * *

Morning brought the children.

This was the part he hadn't prepared for. The adults he could manage — adults wanted things, and wanting was a language he spoke fluently. But the children just *looked* at him. There was a girl of six or seven who followed him around the yard where the vigil-keepers grew turnips in soil that fought them every inch, and she didn't ask for anything. She just walked where he walked, at a respectful distance, the way you might trail a lighthouse.

"Her mother named her after the god," Doss explained, catching him staring. "The true name. We say it quietly, but we say it."

"That's dangerous."

"So is turnip farming, the way we do it." Doss smiled. "Everything worth doing is dangerous. That's what the silence taught us, if nothing else. We got comfortable, before. Comfortable people let the gods go out like lamps and called it peace."

Edmund crouched down to the girl's level, feeling profoundly fraudulent. Up close she had chapped cheeks and a scab on her knee and the ferocious directness of small children everywhere. "Hello," he said.

She studied him. "Are you going to wake it up?"

The question went through him like a fishhook. "I don't know," he said, because lying to this one seemed somehow worse than lying to Vole had been.

"Good," she said. "Mama says waking up is the scary part. But sleeping forever is scarier." And she went back to her turnips, having delivered more theology in four sentences than Edmund had managed in twenty years of whispered prayers.

* * *

He found Maren on the roof, ostensibly repairing a leak, actually drinking from a flask she pretended was medicinal.

"They've asked me to bless the seed potatoes," she said.

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them I'm a smuggler, not a priest. They said smugglers are close enough, given the company I keep." She passed him the flask. It was medicinal, in the sense that it medicated despair. "They're going to ask you, you know. To do something holy. Something visible. Word's already gone out to three other cells. By week's end there'll be pilgrims on that track, looking at you like you're the second tide."

Edmund drank. The flask burned agreeably. "I'm not what they think I am, Maren."

"No," she agreed. "You're worse. You're exactly what they think you are, and you can't stand it." She took the flask back and looked out over the valley, where the morning fog was peeling off the fields like old plaster. "I've watched you for two weeks, Ed. You talk like a man hiding, but you walk like a man answering a summons. You knew that courier's name before he died. You've known it for twenty years. Whatever you were before the Silencing — you're still it. It's just been asleep. Same as the god."

"And if I wake it?" He hadn't meant to say it aloud. "If I break whatever seal holds it, and everything the silence was holding back comes out with it? Forty people on this hillside, Maren. Children. What happens to them?"

Maren was quiet for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had lost its humor entirely, which was rare enough that Edmund sat up straighter. "My brother used to say the same thing. Right before he helped build the lock instead of breaking it." She climbed down off the roof without another word, leaving Edmund alone with the leak, the fog, and the distinct impression that he had just been handed one piece of a much larger puzzle, and that the piece was sharp on all sides.

* * *

Evening brought the candles.

It was a Vigil custom, Doss explained — every seventh evening, each keeper lit a candle and set it in the window, one for every year of silence, and spoke the god's name into the flame. Forty candles in forty windows, and the whole hillside glowing like a fallen constellation.

Edmund stood in the yard and watched them do it. Doss came to stand beside him, her candle cupped against the wind.

"You prayed once," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Every night. For years. Then I stopped counting."

"We never stopped. That's the whole trick of it. Not devotion — stubbornness. God knows the difference. God, presumably, appreciates it." She lit the candle from her neighbor's flame and set it in the sill. "You want to know something funny, herald?"

"I'm not—"

"When the gods went quiet, everyone said the faithful would fade away inside a year. Grief fades, they said. Even love fades. But here's what we learned, keeping our little fire on this cold hill: grief fades. Love doesn't. Love just gets quieter and meaner and more patient, until one day it's twenty years long and it's carrying a map to your heart's door." She patted his arm with a hand like a mooring rope. "Whatever you decide to do with that map — do it knowing that. Somebody loved you before you ever earned it. That's what faith is. That's the whole ugly, beautiful business."

Edmund stood in the yard a long time after she went in. Forty candles. Forty windows. Behind each one, somebody who had decided, without consulting him, that he was the answer to a question they'd been asking for two decades.

He thought about the girl and her turnips. About the boy on the track who'd been waiting since before he was born. About a dying man in a stairwell who had spent his last breath on a name.

Then he thought about the seal. About what slept behind it, and what the silence might have been holding back, and about Maren's face when she'd spoken of her brother — and the cold, quiet certainty underneath her words that some doors are locked for a reason.

He whispered the god's true name into the dark, the way he used to, the way he'd sworn he'd stopped. It felt less like a prayer than like knocking on a door he wasn't sure he wanted opened.

Somewhere below the hills, beneath the sea-cliffs, something that had been silent for twenty years did not answer.

But Edmund could have sworn — though he would deny it later, and did, repeatedly, to Maren's open delight — that the forty candles in the forty windows all leaned toward him at once, like grass bending before a wind that hadn't arrived yet.

And far down the valley road, past the fog line, a lantern flickered that was not one of theirs. Two lanterns. Three. Moving uphill through the dark, unhurried, in the manner of people who knew exactly where they were going and were quite sure no one could stop them when they got there.

Maren appeared at his shoulder, flask already in hand. "Pilgrims?" she asked.

"Pilgrims don't travel in formation," Edmund said, and went to find Doss, and to tell her that the Vigil was about to learn what its faith had cost.
