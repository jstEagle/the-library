## 11. Vole Descends

The report came to Inquisitor Serah Vole at the fourth bell of the morning, folded twice and smelling faintly of lamp oil and someone else's breakfast.

*Congregation sighted. Old tannery district, upriver bend. Vigil-keepers, by the look — candles in the windows, salt on the sills. The old signs.*

She read it three times, because the third reading was where the lie usually hid.

Vigil-keepers. Twenty years since the Silencing, and there were still people who kept vigil — who lit candles for gods that no longer answered, who marked their doorposts in salt the way you'd mark a grave you refused to accept was a grave. The Church called them sentimentalists. The Crown called them tax evaders. The Inquisition called them *leads*, and leads were how careers were made.

Ravensworth would go to ground somewhere like that. He had the look of a man the faithful would trust on sight — tired hands, honest slouch, the particular sadness that made old women want to feed him soup. She'd catalogued it herself during their interview. *He has the face of a man carrying something,* her notes said, which was the kind of observation that sounded profound and meant nothing, and she'd hated writing it.

She gathered four of her wardens, took the river road, and told herself the whole way that this was routine.

It was not routine. Routine did not make her check her pistol twice.

* * *

The tannery district had died slowly, the way districts do — first the trade, then the families, then the hope, leaving behind long low buildings with roofs like collapsed lungs. The congregation had taken the largest of them. From the outside it looked abandoned. From the inside, Vole knew, it would look like every vigil-house she'd ever raided: candle stubs arranged in patterns nobody living remembered the meaning of, a salt circle swept and reswept until the floorboards went white, and somewhere, always, an elderly person knitting with the serenity of someone who has already survived everything worth fearing.

What she did not expect was the smell.

She smelled it from thirty yards out. Smoke, yes — but under the smoke something sweeter and fouler, the smell of burnt hair and burnt fat, and beneath *that* the smell her nightmares kept in reserve: choir-incense. White ash and cold iron. The Hollow Choir's signature, the way they scented their work like a cat scenting territory.

Her wardens fanned out behind her without being ordered to. Good instincts, all four of them. None of them had ever seen a Choir site. She almost envied them.

The door wasn't locked. It hadn't been a door for very long, either — the hinges were torn, not opened, and the splinters pointed *inward*.

"Names," she said quietly, to the dark inside. "If anyone's alive in here, I need names and numbers."

Silence answered. Then, from deep in the hall, a sound she'd heard exactly once before, in a border chapel after the Choir passed through: someone humming. Small voice. Off-key. A child's lullaby with half its notes missing.

Vole went toward it the way you go toward a wound — fast, because slowing down is how you lose your nerve.

The main hall had been a vigil-keeper's dream two days ago. Now it was a vigil-keeper's answer. Candles crushed under boots. The salt circle gouged through in one long deliberate line, like a signature written across someone's face. Scorch-marks on the walls in the shape of the Choir's sigil — a mouth, closed. And along the far wall, laid out in rows with blankets over them, the ones who hadn't run fast enough or hidden well enough.

She counted nine before she stopped counting. Three of the blankets were small.

One of the small ones moved.

* * *

The girl was perhaps eight, maybe nine, hard to tell under the burns. Left arm wrapped in a blanket soaked with something — tallow, maybe, the crude field-dressing of someone who'd learned medicine from a butcher. Her hair had been singed down to a stubble that stood up like winter grass. She was humming because humming was what she had left, and when Vole knelt beside her the humming stopped and the girl looked at her with an expression Vole had never once received in eleven years of wearing the grey coat.

Not fear. Not pleading. *Assessment.* The girl was deciding what Vole was.

"Inquisitor," Vole said, and showed the badge, and immediately understood it was the wrong thing to show, because the girl flinched like she'd been shown a knife.

"I'm not — " Vole started, and stopped, because she didn't know how to finish that sentence honestly. *I'm not with them? I'm with the other ones? The ones who arrest people for praying?*

"My mum said if the grey coats come, don't run." The girl's voice was a rasp. "She said running makes them certain."

"Your mum's smart." Vole pulled off her own coat and put it around the girl's shoulders, which was against about six regulations and felt like the most regulation thing she'd done all year. "Where is she?"

The girl looked at the row of blankets.

Vole had interrogated smugglers, murderers, one truly memorable bigamist, and a man who claimed to be his own twin as a legal strategy. She had a professional face for every answer a human being could give her. She had no face for this. What she managed was a nod, and the nod cost her something she'd be finding pieces of for weeks.

"What's your name?"

"Tansy."

"Tansy. I'm going to pick you up now, and it's going to hurt, and I'm sorry for both of those things."

"Are you taking me to jail?" Tansy asked, with the practical tone of a child who has already priced out her options.

"No," said Serah Vole, and heard, arriving in her own voice, the sound of ice cracking. "No, I am absolutely not."

* * *

She found the rest of the survivors in the cellar: five adults and two more children, wedged behind barrels of ruined tanbark, armed with a hay hook, a boat oar, and an amount of faith that would have moved mountains if mountains were the sort of thing that could be threatened. An old man with forearms like dock rope stepped in front of the others and raised the hay hook with the gravity of a man drawing a legendary sword.

"Warden Vole," Vole said. "Greyharbor Inquisition. I'm alone in purpose if not in fact — my wardens are outside, and they'll stay outside. Who leads here?"

"You're looking at him," said the old man. "Doss Merrow. Vigil-keeper, twenty years, and before you write that down, girl, ask yourself what exactly you think we've been *keeping*."

"A record," Vole said. "I know. I've read the files." She shifted Tansy's weight on her hip — the girl had gone limp with exhaustion and trust in roughly equal measure, which undid Vole more than the burns had. "The Choir came through. When?"

"Night before last. Six of them, masked, singing that awful quiet song. They wanted names. They wanted to know who'd passed through, who'd sheltered anyone, whether anyone had come bearing 'the salt-mark'." Doss spat. "We told them nothing. They expressed their disappointment."

"The salt-mark." Vole's stomach performed a maneuver she associated with bad shellfish and worse news. "Describe it."

"Like a wave drawn wrong," said a woman behind him. "Or a crown, maybe. Drawn by someone who'd only had it described."

Edmund Ravensworth had walked into this house wearing that mark on his map, or his person, or his future, and the Choir had followed him here like smoke follows a draft. Two days ago. Which meant he'd come and gone ahead of them — or he was still —

"He was here," Vole said. It wasn't a question and she didn't pretend otherwise. "The charm-seller from Greyharbor. Edmund Ravensworth."

Doss Merrow studied her for a long moment, hay hook still raised, though it had drifted from *ready to fight* to *leaning on it thoughtfully*.

"And if he was?"

"Then he's a fugitive in possession of restricted materials, and my duty is to take him in chains." Vole heard the sentence leave her mouth and marveled at it, the way you marvel at a clock still ticking in a burning room. Perfectly formed. Perfectly dead. "And then I'd hand him to whatever's left of Greyharbor's law, which currently can't protect its own witch-hunters from the people we're all pretending aren't at war with us. So let me try again, as a person rather than a coat." She adjusted Tansy higher on her hip. "Where is he?"

"He went downriver," Doss said. "Two nights back, with the Quill woman. Before the Choir came." He lowered the hook entirely. "You've got burn-powder in that kit of yours, Warden, or just paperwork?"

Both, as it happened. She used the powder first.

* * *

She worked through the cellar by lamplight while the wardens — regulations be damned, and damn them too — carried water and held children and asked no questions with impressive discipline. One warden, Brakkus, a slab of a man who arrested smugglers the way other men petted dogs, sat with the youngest vigil-child on his knee and taught her a marching song with the violence carefully edited out. Another, Fenwick, wept openly over the row of blankets upstairs and pretended magnificently that he hadn't.

Tansy followed Vole from patient to patient like a small singed shadow, handing her bandages before she asked for them, having apparently decided — in the way of children, instantly and without appeal — that Vole belonged to her now.

"You're different than stories say," Tansy announced, somewhere around the fourth dressing change.

"Stories say what?"

"That grey coats eat prayers." The girl considered. "Mum said that was silly. Mum said fear tells bigger stories than truth does."

"Mum sounds like she should have been running this city."

"She said you'd come, someday. She said somebody in the grey would come who remembered why the vigils started." Tansy's voice caught on the tense of it — *said*, past, finished — and then steadied itself with the terrifying resilience of the young. "Was she right?"

Vole tied off the bandage. Outside, somewhere downriver, Edmund Ravensworth was carrying a map toward a drowned vault, hunted by men who burned children for standing near it. Inside her own chest, something she had built career by career, regulation by regulation, over eleven loyal years — the belief that the Inquisition was the wall between order and the fire — developed a crack that ran floor to ceiling.

"Yes," she said. "She was right. It just took me longer than it should have."

* * *

She wrote the report that night by lantern light, sitting on the tannery steps while the survivors slept in shifts inside. Four pages. She read them back and heard Inquisitor-Captain Hesse's voice in every line, and Hesse's voice had never once in her experience led anywhere but deeper into the machine.

*Site raided by elements of the Hollow Choir prior to Inquisitorial arrival. Nine dead, including three minors. Perpetrators masked, unmarked, departed upstream. Survivors relocated under protective custody pending —*

*Pending*. Everything in her life had been pending. Pending promotion. Pending certainty. Pending the day the job would start making sense again.

She fed page four to the lantern and watched it curl.

Page three went next. Then two. On page one she paused, because a warden's habits die hard, and then she burned that too, and sat looking at the ash thinking: *this is what defection actually looks like. Not a dramatic speech. Just paperwork, catching fire.*

Fenwick appeared beside her with two cups of something the survivors called tea and history would call an act of aggression against hot water.

"They'll ask where your report is," he said.

"They'll ask where the site is. I'll tell them the truth — the Choir got here first. Let them chew on that." She drank. Grimaced. Drank again. "Fenwick. If I told you I was considering a change of employment, how would you feel about it?"

He looked at her the way he'd looked at the blankets upstairs — grief held together with professionalism.

"Ma'am, I watched you give your coat to a burned child and lie about our orders to a man with a hay hook. I've been considering a change of employment myself since approximately the second blanket." He shrugged, enormous and gentle. "Wherever you're going, seems likely I'm going too. Brakkus will want to bring the dog."

"There's no dog."

"There's *always* a dog, ma'am."

Downriver, the current ran black and quiet toward Greyharbor, and somewhere on it a coal barge carried a man she'd once offered protection to, in exchange for the one thing he'd rightly refused to give her. She had called it duty then. Sitting on the steps of a burned vigil-house with a sleeping city around her and a child's coat-sized weight of guilt she couldn't set down, Serah Vole finally called it what it was.

She stood, buckled her pistol, and woke her wardens.

"We ride at dawn," she said. "Downriver. We're not hunting him anymore." She looked east, where the sky was doing its first gray thinking about morning. "We're catching up."

* * *

They caught the barge's wake two days later at a ferry-crossing town called Brackwater — or rather, they caught the *story* of it, told by a ferryman with silver eyes and a smile that made Fenwick's hand drift toward his sword hilt.

"Friends of yours?" the ferryman asked, when Vole described Ravensworth and Maren Quill. His gaze lingered on her grey coat with an interest she didn't care for at all. "Odd pair. The man paid me a strange toll coming through. Paid a *memory*, if you follow. And the woman — " he smiled wider, and Vole saw, with a cold sliding feeling, that he was enjoying this — "the woman bought passage twice. Once going up. Once coming back down."

Vole went very still.

"Coming back down," she repeated. "*Alone?*"

"Oh, not alone." The ferryman leaned on his pole, delighted, unhurried, a man holding the last card in the deck and in no rush to play it. "Alone, and in a hurry, and asking every soul on this river the same question: where does a body find the Hollow Choir?"

The ice that had been cracking in Serah Vole's chest since the tannery did not break so much as *reverse* — the whole frozen weight of it swinging around to point downriver, after Maren Quill, toward whatever a woman does with a secret she's decided to end.

"Well," said Vole, reaching for her horse's reins. "That answers *that*."
