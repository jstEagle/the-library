## 13. Storm Over the Salt Road

The salt flats began where the river forgot itself.

Edmund had heard old crewmen describe them — a country of white that went on until your eyes gave up and invented hills that weren't there — but hearing was nothing to walking it. By midmorning the world had been reduced to two colors: the blinding crust underfoot and the bruise-colored sky above, pressing down like a lid on a pot.

"Cheerful place," Maren said, spitting into the salt. "Reminds me of my first husband."

"You've never been married."

"Exactly."

Vole walked a few paces ahead, hood up against the glare, one hand resting on the hilt of her sword as though the horizon might owe her money. Three days out of Brackwater, and the three of them had settled into an uneasy rhythm: Vole scouting, Maren navigating by tide-marks only she could read in the map's second face, and Edmund carrying everything — the water, the food, the guilt, the map, and the increasingly alarming secret humming under his skin.

Because something was wrong with him. Had been wrong since the Choir's attack on his shop, worse since the ambush on the barge, and by now it wasn't a secret so much as a weather system. His hands itched. Not the skin — deeper. As if the bones themselves were full of bees.

He'd told no one. Which, he reflected, watching Maren's broad back sway ahead of him, was possibly the stupidest thing he'd done since agreeing to carry a map that made witch-hunters and death-cultists alike willing to kill for it.

The salt crunched under his boots like old snow. Somewhere beneath it, miles down, lay the drowned vault, and the heart that beat there like a second tide. He could almost feel it now — a slow pulse through the soles of his feet, patient as a lighthouse.

*Soon,* it didn't say, because gods didn't say things anymore. But he felt it anyway.

*Stop that,* he told himself sternly. *You're a charm-seller. Charm-sellers don't feel tides.*

*Charm-seller,* said the itch in his bones, with what sounded suspiciously like laughter.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a salt-ridge — a wind-carved wall of white crystal taller than a house, glittering faintly even in the dark. Maren built a fire from coal they'd bought off the barge-master, and the three of them sat around it eating dried fish and pretending the fish was food.

"I knew a man once," Maren said, "who ate nothing but salted cod for a year. When they found him, he'd turned into a pillar."

"That's not how salt works," Vole said.

"You don't know how Maren works," Edmund said. "I've known her twenty years and I don't know how Maren works."

Maren pointed a fish at him approvingly. "See? He's learning."

But Edmund's hands were shaking again, and he tucked them under his arms where neither woman could see. The itch had become an ache, the ache a hum, and the hum was getting louder every hour, like a kettle deciding whether to scream. Twice today the salt around his boots had melted into brief, perfect circles of clear ice, then crusted over again before anyone noticed.

Twice today, he'd noticed.

"Edmund." Vole's voice, quiet. She was watching him across the fire with those grey eyes that missed nothing, because missing things got people burned at the stake. "Your nose is bleeding."

He touched his upper lip. Came away red.

"Altitude," he said.

"We're at sea level."

"Low altitude, then."

Maren snorted. But Vole kept looking at him, and Edmund looked back, and for a moment the fire cracked between them and neither spoke, and he understood that she knew — had probably known since the barge, since the Choir scouts' boat had simply *stopped*, holed below the waterline by nothing any eye could see.

She said nothing. That was its own kind of mercy, or its own kind of trap. With Vole it was hard to tell the difference, and lately Edmund suspected she was still working it out herself.

"Sleep," she said finally. "We cross the open flats tomorrow. Twelve hours of nothing between us and the cliffs. If the Choir means to take us, it'll be there."

"Comforting thought," Maren muttered. "I'll treasure it all night."

* * *

The storm came out of the east at dawn, and it was wrong.

Edmund felt it before he saw it — the hum in his bones spiking into a shriek, every hair on his arms standing up like soldiers called to attention. He stopped mid-stride and stared east, where the sky had gone the color of a bruised plum, and lightning moved inside it in patterns lightning does not make. Spirals. Rings. Deliberate, patient geometry.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's not weather."

Maren squinted. "Looks like weather."

"It's *herded* weather. Storms don't march in formation." His mouth had gone dry. "The Choir has storm-callers. I'd forgotten. Twenty years ago we used to—" He stopped. Twenty years ago belonged to another man, one who'd stood in shield-lines while priests sang the sky into weapons. "They're driving it at us."

"Can you outrun a storm?" Vole asked, already unrolling the map against her forearm, checking their position with the calm of a woman reading a menu.

"No," said Maren.

"Not this one," Edmund said, and hated how steady his voice came out when nothing inside him was. "It's being aimed. It'll follow us to the cliffs if it has to."

"Then we fight it here," Vole said, drawing her sword, "on ground where I can see them coming."

And see them coming they did — out of the white glare ahead, dark figures resolving one by one, then ten by ten. Knights in lacquered grey armor, faces hidden behind masks shaped like closed mouths, moving in silence across the salt. No war cries. No drums. The Hollow Choir did everything in silence; it was rather their whole thing.

"Fourteen," Vole counted. "Fifteen. Sixteen."

"I count seventeen," Maren said, loosening her knives.

"You always count one extra. It's why nobody plays cards with you."

"It's why nobody *loses* cards to me."

Edmund barely heard them. The hum in his bones had become a roar, and the storm behind them answered it — he could feel the two voices calling to each other across the flats, the storm and whatever was waking in his chest, and somewhere very far below, the heart going *boom... boom... boom...* like a drum before a battle.

"Get behind me," he said.

Both women looked at him.

"Edmund," Vole said carefully, "you're holding a walking stick."

"I know what I'm holding." His voice cracked on the last word, and light cracked with it — a thin white seam running up the length of the stick, glowing like a crack in the world. He hadn't done that. He hadn't *meant* to do that. "Get. Behind me."

The knights broke into a run.

What happened next, Edmund would spend the rest of his life trying to remember honestly, and failing, because memory is a poor vessel for divinity.

The storm hit first. It came down on the flats like the fist of some enormous angry child, and Edmund threw up his hands — not a decision, a reflex, the way you flinch from a thrown stone — and the lightning *bent*. All of it, every bolt, curving away from his companions as though the air itself had grown a spine, and slamming down instead in a ring around them, a cage of white fire that turned three charging knights to smoke and glass.

He screamed. He couldn't help it. It was like trying to hold a river in your teeth.

The power didn't come *from* him, exactly. It came *through* him, the way floodwater comes through a broken window — using his shape, ignoring his wishes. He could feel it reaching for Maren, for Vole, for anything alive and warm, wanting to fill them too, wanting to pour itself into the whole world at once, and he clenched every muscle he had and *pushed back*, and the pushing hurt worse than anything the Choir had ever done to him.

"EDMUND!" Maren's voice, very far away.

A knight reached the ring of fire. Vole met him — he saw that much, glimpses through the blaze: Vole moving like water, like twenty years of guilt given a sword and permission. Maren's knives flashed. The storm screamed overhead.

And then a bolt slipped through. One stray thread of lightning, wild and blind, whipping sideways through the gap where the ring flickered — and Edmund saw its path, saw it end at Maren's back, saw it in the half-second that gods give you and mortals must live inside forever.

He moved without thinking. That was the mercy of it. Thinking would have stopped him.

He caught the lightning.

Not with his hands — with everything, with the whole ruined cathedral of himself, taking the bolt into his chest the way you'd catch a falling child, and it *burned*, it burned like holy fire, like the wound that had killed the courier in his stairwell, and he understood in one white-hot instant exactly what that poor man had carried and why he'd been dying, and then Edmund turned and *gave it back*.

The lightning left him in a single lance, crossed four hundred yards of salt flat, and struck the storm-caller on her distant ridge with a sound like the sky clearing its throat. The storm above them shuddered, lost its geometry, and collapsed into ordinary rain — huge, cold, blessedly stupid rain that fell on everything equally and meant nothing at all.

Then Edmund was on his knees in the flooding salt, and someone was shouting his name, and the world went white at the edges.

* * *

He woke to rain on his face and Maren's fist an inch from his jaw.

Not striking. Hovering. Trembling.

Her face was wet — rain, or not rain — and there was a mark on her cheekbone he hadn't put there, or rather he *had*: a thin white scar, branching like frost ferns, where the edge of the lightning's heat had kissed her as he pulled the bolt aside. Half an inch closer and it would have taken her eye. Half an inch further and it would have taken everything.

"You absolute," she said, and stopped, and started again. "You *absolute*—"

"Maren—"

"Don't." Her voice shook. "Don't you dare talk yet. You lied to us. Three days, Edmund. Three days of you melting salt behind your own boots like we wouldn't notice, like we're *blind*, and then you stand up in front of seventeen knights and turn yourself into a lightning rod without telling anyone the rod was live!"

"I didn't know it was live," he said. "I swear it. I felt it building and I told myself it was nothing, because if it was nothing I could keep being a charm-seller, and if I stopped being a charm-seller then everything I buried twenty years ago gets dug back up, and I—" His breath hitched. "I wasn't ready to be that person again. So I pretended. And pretending nearly killed you."

Maren stared at him. Rain ran off her chin.

"Yes," she said finally. "It did. Nearly." She held up her hand, and he saw the same frost-fern scar branching across her knuckles, delicate and terrible. "This is what 'nearly' looks like on me, Edmund. I get to wear it. Every morning, I get to look at it and remember that the man protecting me is more dangerous than the men hunting us."

The words landed like stones. He took them. He deserved stones.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"I know you are. That's the worst part." She lowered her fist slowly, and then — because she was Maren, and twenty years of friendship doesn't dissolve in one storm — she sat down heavily beside him in the mud and put her scarred hand briefly, hard, on his shoulder. "Forgiven. *Barely.* And only because you caught it. Only because you stepped in front of it for me, you great soft idiot, which was the single stupidest brave thing I've seen since you took an arrow for a mule in Kessrin."

"The mule was carrying our pay."

"The mule was carrying our *pay*," she agreed. "So. Rules." She held up fingers. "One: no more secrets about the magic. Two: when you feel it building, you say so, out loud, like a person. Three—" her voice gentled despite itself, "—you stop carrying everything alone. Water, food, guilt, map, apocalypse. Share the load or drop some of it. Pick."

Edmund laughed — a wet, cracked sound — and found he was crying a little, which the rain kindly disguised. "Three's the hard one."

"I know," Maren said. "That's why it's three."

Vole appeared out of the rain then, sword cleaned, expression unreadable as ever — except that it wasn't, quite, anymore. Something in her face had shifted since the battle, some load-bearing wall quietly removed.

"Thirteen dead," she reported. "Four fled east. The storm-caller is gone." A pause. "You should know what I saw, Edmund. From the ridge. When you took that bolt." Her grey eyes fixed on him. "For a moment, you were *lit up*. Like a lantern with a man inside it. I have spent my life burning people who glowed like that." Another pause, longer. "I find I no longer understand why."

"Marevelous," Maren said. "Everyone's having revelations. Anyone want fish?"

Nobody wanted fish.

Edmund got to his feet, swaying, and looked east toward the cliffs — invisible now behind the rain, but he could feel them, and beyond them, far below the sea, the slow patient *boom... boom... boom* of the heart. Closer than yesterday. Closer than it had ever been.

And beneath his feet, in the flooded salt, he saw it: a circle of clear water, perfectly round, perfectly still, reflecting the storm-light like a coin laid on the world.

Where he'd knelt, the salt had melted. Where he'd knelt, something green was growing — one blade of grass, impossible, absurd, alive, bending in the rain.

Maren saw it too. She said nothing. She just looked at him, and touched her new scar, and nodded once — forgiveness, barely, wearing its price openly.

"Come on," Edmund said hoarsely. "The cliffs won't wait. And neither, I think, will what's under them."

Behind them, unnoticed by all three, the single blade of grass put out a second blade, and a third — a small green stain spreading through the white, like a rumor, like a dawn, like the beginning of everything the salt had spent twenty years refusing to remember.
