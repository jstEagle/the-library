## 15. Twenty Years of Quiet

The priest's cell smelled of tallow and old stone, the way every holy place smells when the holiness has been left unattended for a generation.

Edmund had expected chains. He had expected, at the very least, bars — some theatrical arrangement befitting the man who had silenced every god in the world. Instead there was a low room carved into the stronghold's heart, a straw pallet, a water jug gone green at the rim, and an old man sitting cross-legged on the floor as though he'd been waiting for company the way other people waited for rain.

"You're bleeding," the priest said, by way of greeting.

"It's been a long week."

"Mm." The priest's eyes were pale as river ice, and they moved from Edmund to Vole to Maren with the unhurried attention of someone who had not had anything new to look at in twenty years. "Three of you. I'd have guessed two. The woman with the knife-hips is a surprise."

"Knife-*hips*?" Maren said.

"I've had a long time to develop descriptions," the priest said mildly. "You'd be astonished what I can do with adjectives now."

Vole's hand hadn't left her sword hilt since they'd descended the stair. "Brother Anselm of the Hollow Choir," she said. "Anchor of the Seal. Confessor to the Ninefold Silence."

"Formerly," Anselm agreed. "These days I'm mostly confessor to myself, and I'll tell you, he's in *terrible* spiritual condition."

Edmund stood in the doorway and felt something in his chest come loose and swing free, like a door off its hinge. This was the man. This was the name whispered through twenty years of salt and fear — the priest who had cast the Silencing, whose living body was the lock on the world's quiet. Edmund had imagined him vast, terrible, robed in the dark of his own deed. Instead he was small and dry and looked like he would enjoy a good stew.

"You did it," Edmund said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "You made them stop."

Anselm tilted his head. "Did I?"

"The gods went silent. Every one of them. The whole choir of heaven, quiet in a single night. Everyone knows it was the Hollow Choir. Everyone knows it was—"

"Everyone knows a great many things," Anselm said, "because everyone needed the story to have a villain, and I volunteered. It's easier that way. Villains are very restful for other people." He unfolded himself from the floor with the creaking slowness of old joints and gestured at the room's single bench. "Sit down. All of you. What I have to say takes longer than standing legs want to hear."

Nobody sat. Anselm shrugged, as if he'd expected nothing else, and lowered himself back onto the pallet instead.

"Twenty years ago," he said, "I was not the Choir's greatest servant. I was its most doubtful one. Do you know what my heresy was? I asked a question during evening prayer. A small question. I asked: *if the gods love us, why do they let us burn each other in their names?*" He smiled, and it was a tired smile, worn smooth like a stone in a current. "The Choir had answers, of course. They always do. But that night I didn't want an answer. I wanted to ask the gods themselves. And so I did something forbidden. I went looking for one."

Edmund's hands had gone cold. He knew this story. He had lived the ending of it without ever knowing the beginning.

"I found one," Anselm said simply. "A small god. A river-god, out past the marches — nothing grand, nothing with a proper name even, just a spirit of brown water and willow shade that the locals fed bread and asked for good fishing. I knelt on his bank and I asked him my question. And do you know what he said?"

Vole said, quietly, "No."

"He wept." Anselm's voice didn't change at all, which somehow made it worse. "He wept, and he said: *Because we cannot stop you. Because you gave us your prayers and your fear, and with them you gave us your wars, and now when you march under our banners we must either bless the marching or break the hearts of everyone who ever loved us. We have no hands, little priest. Only mouths. And a mouth cannot hold back an army.*"

The silence in the cell was total. Even the sea beyond the cliffs seemed to lean closer to listen.

"They weren't silent because we silenced them," Anselm said. "They were silent because they *chose* it. All of them, together, in one night — the night you remember, mercenary. The night the sky went quiet. They closed their own mouths. Because the war between gods and men wasn't coming. It had already begun, and it was being fought *through* us — every crusade, every burning, every village put to the torch in some heaven's name. The gods looked at what their voices had built and they were ashamed. So they stopped speaking. All at once. Like a household blowing out its candles so no one can find it in the dark and use it."

"No," Edmund said. It came out before he could stop it. "No, that's not—" He had spent twenty years whispering prayers into that silence. Twenty years of believing his god had abandoned him, or died, or never existed at all. Twenty years of shame about the shame itself. "I *prayed*. For years. Nothing answered. If they chose it, if it was mercy, then why did it feel like—"

"Like being left?" Anselm asked gently. "Yes. That's the cruelty of it. Mercy often looks exactly like abandonment from the outside. They knew it would hurt. They chose the hurt anyway, because the alternative was watching us slaughter each other until there was no one left to pray." He paused. "But they couldn't fully close the door. Grief doesn't work that way, even divine grief. Their hearts still beat. Their power still leaked through the cracks. And hearts, mercenary, can be found. Hearts can be *used*."

"And that's where the Choir came in," Vole said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a woman walking across ice and listening to it crack.

"That's where the Choir came in," Anselm agreed. "Not all of us. Not even most of us, at first. But enough. There were those who heard the silence and thought: *at last.* No more holy wars. No more miracles to fight over. No more gods using men as gloves for their fists. The silence was the first peace the world had known in three thousand years, and they meant to keep it. Permanently. So they hunted down the places where the divine still beat — the vaults, the wellsprings, the drowned hearts — and they sealed them. And because a seal needs an anchor, and an anchor should be someone who understands what he's holding shut..." He spread his thin hands. "They used me. The doubter. The one who'd spoken to a god and lived. I think they thought it was funny."

"You've been down here twenty years," Maren said, "holding the door shut on a god who locked *himself* in?"

"I'm holding the door shut on everything the silence contained," Anselm said. "That's the part your map didn't show you, and the part the Choir's own faithful don't understand anymore. The seal isn't just keeping the god quiet. It's keeping the god's * grief* quiet. Twenty years of a heart that broke the world once already, beating alone in the dark, with nothing to do but remember." He looked up at Edmund, and his ice-pale eyes were suddenly very old. "You want to free him. I can see it in your face; it's written there like tide-marks on a piling. So let me tell you what freeing means."

* * *

Edmund had killed men in the war. He had held friends while they died and burned villages he'd been ordered to burn, and he had thought, in the twenty quiet years since, that he had already seen the bottom of what the world could hold.

He was wrong. The bottom was deeper.

"Think of a man," Anselm said, "who has been screaming inside a sealed room for twenty years. Not aloud — he learned quickly that screaming changed nothing — but inside, where it never stops. Now open the door. What comes out?"

"Grief," Edmund said.

"Grief with *power*," Anselm corrected. "Twenty years of it, compressed. The Silencing didn't end the war between gods and men, child. It postponed it. It put the whole storm in a bottle, and the Choir has spent two decades telling itself the bottle was peace. But storms don't die in bottles. They wait. And the god beneath these cliffs — the one whose heartbeat you've been following across half the world like a lovesick fool — he has had twenty years to compose his answer to the question of what men deserve."

Maren had gone very still beside him. Edmund realized his own hands were shaking, and that the tremor wasn't fear. It was the dead magic in him, the thing he'd spent two decades drowning, stirring in its sleep like a dog hearing its master's boots on the stair.

"You're lying," Vole said. But her voice cracked on the word, and Edmund heard it, and he saw her hear herself hear it.

"Am I?" Anselm spread his hands. "Inquisitor. You've read the archives. Tell me — in all the Choir's records, in all the sealed testimonies you were permitted, did anyone ever write down *why* the gods went silent? Not how. Not when. Why. Was there ever a document?"

Vole opened her mouth. Closed it. Edmund watched twenty years of certainty rearrange itself behind her eyes like furniture in a flooded house.

"There wasn't," she said finally. "There was only... the event. And the doctrine. The doctrine says the Choir silenced them to save mankind."

"The doctrine says," Anselm agreed pleasantly, "and doctrines are wonderful things. They mean you never have to look at what actually happened. I wrote the first draft of that doctrine, Inquisitor. I know precisely how much truth it holds." He held up a thumb and forefinger, a hair's width apart. "This much. The rest was comfort."

Edmund sat down on the bench after all. His legs had decided the matter without consulting him.

"If what you're saying is true," he said slowly, "then the map — the courier — the people who died getting it to me—"

"Were carrying an invitation to open the bottle," Anselm said. "Yes. Though I suspect they believed they were carrying a resurrection. There's a congregation out there, isn't there? God-sworn survivors, keeping vigil, waiting for a herald." His gaze flicked to Edmund's face and something almost like pity crossed it. "Ah. You've met them. And they looked at you the way starving men look at bread."

"They think I'm going to bring their god back."

"Are you?"

"I don't *know*," Edmund said, and it tore out of him louder than he meant, echoing off the stone. "That's the whole — that's the entire problem, old man. I've spent twenty years praying to a silence and hating myself for it, and now you're telling me the silence was a *choice*, a mercy, a — a door the gods closed from the *inside*, and that opening it might drown whatever's left of the world in twenty years of divine grief, and I am supposed to stand in front of that heart tonight and decide. Me. A charm-seller. A failed mercenary with shaking hands and a dead wife and a—" He stopped. Swallowed. "And a friend who wants the seal destroyed forever, and a witch-hunter who followed me here instead of arresting me, and a courier's blood drying on my stairs in Greyharbor. That's who decides."

"Yes," Anselm said. "That's rather the point."

"What point?"

"The gods closed their mouths because they couldn't trust themselves with power over us," Anselm said. "And the Choir locked the door because it couldn't trust *us* with the choice. Someone always decides for someone else. That's the whole history of faith, mercenary, boiled down to one sentence. But you—" he pointed one crooked finger at Edmund's chest, "—you're the first person in twenty years to walk down to this door *carrying the choice instead of the answer*. The Choir sent executioners. The vigil-keepers send heralds. You — you don't even know what you are yet. Which makes you, as far as I can calculate, the only honest man to reach this room since I did."

Edmund laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, halfway to something else. "Honest. Gods help me."

"They can't," Anselm said gently. "That's sort of the situation."

* * *

Later — how much later Edmund couldn't say; the cell kept time like the inside of a bell — Vole stood by the door with her arms folded, staring at nothing, and Maren had drifted to the far wall where the shadows were thickest, and Edmund understood that both women were doing the same arithmetic he was, and arriving at different sums.

"So the Choir," Vole said at last, in the voice of a woman testing whether the floor would hold her weight. "The Choir that raised me. That taught me the silence was salvation. That sent me hunting charm-sellers and hedge-witches and children with candle-magic." She stopped. Started again. "The Choir has been guarding a prison the prisoners built for themselves, and calling it mercy, and no one above the rank of anchor even knows."

"Now you know why the anchors are kept alive and kept ignorant of each other," Anselm said. "Knowledge is a key. The Choir locks its keys in people."

"And if you die," Maren said from the shadows, "the seal weakens."

"If I die, the seal begins to fail. Slowly. Over months. Enough time, perhaps, for the Choir to raise a new anchor — though they haven't managed it in twenty years, because the ritual requires a man who has spoken with a god and survived the conversation, and the gods being silent, recruitment has been *terrible*." He smiled his tired smile. "Which brings us, I'm afraid, to the practical question. Three armed visitors in my cell, one of whom has been following a map to the god's heart, and one of whom serves the organization that chained me here. What happens now?"

What happened now, Edmund thought, was that the floor of the world had given way beneath him and he was still falling. He had come down into the dark certain of one thing — one clean, simple, load-bearing thing: the god was imprisoned, the imprisonment was wicked, and breaking it was right. He had carried that certainty across salt flats and through ambushes, and it had weighed nothing at all, because certainty weighs nothing. Doubt was the heavy thing. Doubt was the iron.

Free the god, and perhaps heal the world — or perhaps loose twenty years of grief with the strength to crack continents.

Leave the seal, and the quiet continued — the quiet that had hollowed him out drop by drop for two decades, the quiet that had made a widow buy luck-charms from a man who no longer believed in luck.

Kill the anchor, and the choice resolved itself slowly, badly, over months, without his hands on it.

Every road led somewhere he didn't want to go.

"Edmund." Maren's voice, from the shadows. Low. Careful in a way he'd never heard from her before — and Maren Quill had once talked her way past a customs blockade using nothing but a borrowed hat and a fictional husband. "Come here a moment."

He crossed the cell. Up close, in the tallow light, he saw something in her face he couldn't read — and Edmund Ravensworth had spent twenty years reading faces across a market stall, learning which customers would pay and which would report him, and he could read nearly everyone.

Nearly.

"Maren," he said quietly. "Whatever it is—"

"We need to talk about the second mark on the map," she said. "Before you make any decisions. Before anyone does." Her eyes flicked, briefly, toward Vole by the door — and then away, too quickly. "Alone."

Something cold walked up Edmund's spine, patient as tide.

"Maren," he said again. "What second mark?"

She smiled at him, and it was her old smuggler's smile, easy and bright and utterly unreadable, and for the first time in thirty years of knowing her, Edmund Ravensworth did not believe a single word of it.

"Walk with me," she said, "and I'll show you."
