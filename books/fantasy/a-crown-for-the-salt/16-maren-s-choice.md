## 16. Maren's Choice

The fire at the waystation had burned down to coals the color of old blood, and Maren Quill was finding it very hard to look at Edmund Ravensworth while holding a knife behind her back.

Not that he'd notice. Twenty years of knowing the man, and he still thought a quiet Maren was a contented Maren. He sat across the hearth turning the map over in his scarred hands like it was something holy instead of something cursed, humming under his breath — actually *humming*, the great soft fool — as if they were two smugglers on a coal barge with nothing worse ahead of them than weather and bad coffee.

They'd made good time since Brackwater. The vigil-keepers had fed them, prayed over them, and sent them east with more faith than provisions. Tomorrow, maybe the day after, they'd reach the sea-cliffs and the drowned vault beneath them. Tonight there was only this: a ruined shepherd's hut, a dying fire, and a knife.

Maren shifted her weight. The blade stayed hidden in the small of her back, flat against her belt where she'd kept it since the congregation — since the moment she'd realized the miles were running out and she'd run out of reasons to delay.

"Edmund," she said.

"Mm?" He didn't look up from the map.

"I need to tell you something, and I need you to not do anything stupid when I tell it."

That got his attention. He looked up, and there it was — the old wariness coming back into his face, the mercenary surfacing through the charm-seller like a shark through calm water. "Maren."

"Twenty years ago," she said, "my brother helped build the seal on that vault. And I've known about it for nineteen of those twenty years. And I came with you to destroy it forever."

* * *

She'd expected shouting. She'd expected the knife to come out of hiding before she finished the sentence. What she got instead was Edmund going very, very still, the way he did when a deal turned rotten halfway through the handshake, and saying, quietly:

"Corwin."

The name landed in Maren's chest like a fish-hook. "You knew his name."

"He crewed with us. Two seasons, maybe three. Big lad, always cold, always complaining about it." Edmund's eyes went to the fire. "He disappeared the night the gods went quiet. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought the gods took him. As payment. That's what we all told ourselves, wasn't it? Something was taken, so something must have been paid."

"That's what you told yourselves." Maren's voice came out flatter than she intended. "Corwin told me different. He came home one last time — grey as ash, hands burned to the wrist, wouldn't let me touch him. He said the silence wasn't a punishment, Edmund. It was a door. And he'd helped build the door, and lock it, and throw away the key, and he was proud of it." Her jaw tightened. "Proud. My own brother, and he looked at me with those burnt hands and told me the world was safer dark."

Edmund set the map down on his knee. Very carefully, the way a man sets down something that might go off. "And you've known this since when?"

"Nineteen years." She made herself hold his gaze. "Since before your shop. Since before Greyharbor. Since before every whispered prayer you ever said to a god who never answered, I knew why he never answered, and I never told you."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, wind worried at the loose shutters, and the hut creaked like an old ship, which was a comparison Maren did not need right now.

"You let me sell dead charms," Edmund said at last. "For twenty years. You watched me—"

"Yes."

"—I lit a candle for him, Mare. Every Salt-Night. In your mother's chapel."

"I know. I paid for the candles." Her voice cracked and she hated it. "What was I supposed to say, Edmund? That my brother was one of the men who gutted the sky? That every prayer you whispered was a letter to a prisoner? You'd have gone after that vault twenty years ago, alone, half-mad with grief, and the Choir would have folded you into the dark alongside him."

"And instead?"

"Instead you grew old small," Maren said. "Which broke my heart differently, but it did keep you breathing."

* * *

Here is what Maren Quill had learned about truth, in fifty-odd years of smuggling everything from silk to secrets across every tide-mark from here to the Spur: truth is cargo like any other. It has weight. It has a shelf life. And if you stack too much of it in one hull, sooner or later the whole ship goes over.

She had been carrying Corwin's truth for nineteen years, and she had told herself every year that next year she'd find a harbor for it. Tell Edmund. Let someone else share the tonnage. And every year she'd found a reason — he was finally sleeping through the night, he had the shop, he had his little rituals and his little prayers and who was she to take the last of his hope away?

Then a dying man had bled out in his stairwell with a map in his fist, and the whole rotten cargo had shifted, and now here she stood with a knife behind her back telling him the truth all at once because there was no other way to do what needed doing.

"Say the rest," Edmund said.

"There isn't a rest."

"There's always a rest with you. You talk like a smuggler — everything comes in holds." He hadn't moved. His hands lay open on his knees, which frightened her more than clenched fists would have. "Say the rest, Maren."

She exhaled. Brought the knife around from behind her back — slowly, openly, letting him see it — and pointed it at the map on his knee.

"The rest is that I'm not taking you to that vault to free anything. I'm taking you there to end it. Permanently. The heart, the seal, the whole drowned shrine — cut, sunk, scattered. Whatever it takes."

The silence stretched long enough that she could hear both their hearts.

"Put the knife down, Mare."

"No."

"Put it down and we'll talk about it like—"

"Like what? Like people who haven't been lying to each other since before your shop had a roof?" The knife didn't waver, though her hand wanted to. "There's nothing to talk about. You heard the same stories I did at the vigil. Those people think you're a herald. They think when the god wakes, the world gets its warmth back, the harvests come green, the dead magic in your hands starts singing again. It's a lovely story. It's also exactly the story the god would want told, if it wanted *out*."

"It was imprisoned, Maren. Against its will—"

"Was it?" She took one step forward, and the firelight made the blade shine wet. "Because my brother's hands were burned to the bone building that seal, and he was *proud*. And the anchor-priest — whatever's left of him upriver in that Choir cage — he'll tell you whatever keeps him breathing another day. Everyone in this story has a reason for their version, Edmund. Mine is simple. Nineteen years ago I held my brother's ruined hands and listened to him praise the darkness, and I decided that no power worth worshipping needs to be let out of a hole under the sea. Nothing good lives chained. But nothing that was good would stay chained either — it would rot. Whatever's beating down there, it's had twenty years to curdle."

"Or twenty years to forgive," Edmund said softly.

"Oh, don't you *dare* be hopeful at me with a knife between us."

A sound escaped him then — half laugh, half something sadder — and he rubbed his face with both hands, and for a moment he looked every one of his years and several he hadn't earned yet. "Fair. That's fair." He lowered his hands. Looked at her. "Maren. I'm going to stand up now, and I need you to know that whatever happens in the next minute, I am not going to take that knife from you."

"That's a foolish thing to promise."

"I've been foolish before. It's mostly how I've done everything worth doing." And he stood, slow as rising bread, hands open, and stepped toward her, and she hated him for it because she could not strike a man walking toward her with open hands, and he knew it, he'd always known exactly how to get under a blade—

"Stop there."

He stopped. A pace away. Close enough that she could see the tremor in his fingers — the dead-magic tremor, the one he thought he hid, the one she'd watched for twenty years and pretended not to see.

"You want to know the worst part?" Maren said, and her voice was not steady anymore and she'd stopped caring. "The worst part isn't that you lied to yourself for twenty years praying to a locked door. It's that I loved you enough to let you. Nineteen years I chose your comfort over the truth, every single day, and now the truth's come due with interest, and it turns out the interest is a knife and a choice, and I *hate* owing."

"I know," Edmund said. "I've owed the same debt. Different coin." He glanced down at the map, then back up. "Ask me the question, Mare. The real one. Not the vault question."

"Don't tell me my questions."

"The real one," he said, relentless as tide, "is whether you can trust me. Twenty years of friendship, one secret each, and now knives. So ask it."

Her arm was beginning to ache. She didn't lower the knife. "Can I trust you?"

"No," Edmund said.

She blinked. "Well. Thank you for your honesty—"

"Not entirely. Not blindly. Not after tonight." He said it simply, without flinching, and somehow that undid her more than any argument could have. "But here's what I can give you: I will not kneel to that thing down there. Whatever it offers me — and it will offer, Maren, it offered the courier, that's why he died carrying its map — I will not trade my own will for it. Not for magic. Not for the past. Not even for—" his voice caught, just slightly, on something she couldn't name, "—not for anyone I've lost. If the god wants out, it goes out through me, and I am a very difficult door."

"That's not a plan. That's a personality trait."

"It's served me longer than most plans."

And there it was — the absurd, maddening, familiar warmth of him, cracking through the standoff like sun through harbor fog, and Maren felt her knife-arm sagging and despised herself for it. This was the trouble with loving someone for twenty years. You couldn't even threaten them properly.

"If we do it your way," she said, hoarse, "and that thing wakes wrong — if it wakes angry —"

"Then we'll have started a war," Edmund agreed. "You and me and Vole, if she's still breathing somewhere behind us. Against whatever's left of the Choir and twenty years of silence." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the one from the deck of the *Gullwing* in the years before everything, crooked and reckless and certain. "But Maren — think what we'll have started it *for*. Nobody's fought a war for a god in living memory. Only against them. Maybe it's time somebody tried the other direction."

"You're a romantic idiot."

"My mother thought so too."

Maren looked at the knife in her hand. Nineteen years of cargo, and here was the harbor at last, and it wasn't a harbor at all, it was just a man with open hands and a map and no better idea than hope.

She turned the blade and drove it into the table beside the map — thunk, quivering, an inch from the parchment's edge, which was as close to a signature as she'd ever given anyone.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When we're down there, in the dark, in front of that heart — if I say the word, if I say *Corwin*, you stop. Everything stops. We walk out, and the vault stays drowned, and you never speak of it again. Swear it on the salt."

Edmund looked at the knife in the table for a long moment. Then he put his hand flat over his heart, the old crewman's oath, older than the Silencing, older than grief.

"On the salt," he said. "Sworn."

Maren let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for nineteen years. It didn't feel like relief. It felt like the moment after you cut a mooring line — terror and freedom arriving together, hand in hand, grinning.

She was retrieving the knife and her dignity from the tabletop when Edmund went rigid, head cocking toward the window.

"What?"

"Lantern light." His voice dropped flat and cold. "Down the slope. Three — no, four. Moving in a pattern."

Maren crossed to the shutter crack in three strides and put her eye to it. Below, in the dark, four lights moved through the gorse in perfect silence, closing on the hut in a slow crescent. Not patrol spacing. Not witch-hunter spacing either.

Choir spacing. She'd seen it once before, the night Corwin died.

"Well," she said, pulling the knife free of the table and checking its edge, "the good news is we're done having the hard conversation."

Edmund was already stuffing the map inside his coat, and the dead-magic tremor in his hands had gone strangely, terribly still.

"The bad news," he said, as the first shoulder hit the door, "is what's coming next."
