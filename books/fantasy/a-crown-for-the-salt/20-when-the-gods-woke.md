## 20. When the Gods Woke

The first thing the city of Greyharbor knew of the god's waking was the smell of bread.

Not burning bread — rising bread. In the cellar ovens of the Saltrow district, forty loaves that had sat dead and doughy for three days suddenly swelled and browned all at once, as if the yeast itself had remembered its purpose. The baker, a wide woman named Odile Fenn who had not prayed in nineteen years, stood in her flour-dusted doorway with her hands pressed to her mouth and watched the light come off her own hearth like something alive.

By noon she had sold out. By evening she had hung a small wooden bell above her door, carved from driftwood, the kind of charm that would have gotten her hanged a week ago. She did not take it down when the neighbor's boy pointed at it. She gave him a roll instead.

* * *

Edmund Ravensworth woke on the deck of Maren Quill's boat to the sound of gulls screaming in languages that did not exist yesterday.

He lay still for a long moment, listening. The sea off the northern cliffs was doing something it had never done in his lifetime: it was *singing*. Not waves against hull — waves against hull he knew like his own heartbeat — but beneath it, a low harmonic hum, patient and vast, like a choir warming up in a cathedral the size of the world.

"You feel that?" Maren said from the tiller. Her face was gray with exhaustion, salt-crusted, one arm bound where the storm-ambush magic had scarred her. But her eyes were bright, and frightened, and awake.

"I feel it."

"It's in my teeth." She flexed her bandaged hand. "And look at this." She held up a coil of rope. Along its length, faint blue light pulsed in time with the sea-song, traveling knot to knot like a thought moving down a spine. "That rope's been dead cordage since before you were born. Everything's *on*. Everything."

Edmund sat up slowly. His whole body ached — the vault, the water, Vole's face in the last light he'd seen of her, holding the line so they could run. He hadn't slept. He wasn't sure sleep worked anymore, or if it did, whether what came would be dreams or something else.

The map was buried now. The seal was broken. And the god —

The god had not commanded him. That was the part he kept turning over, like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. In the dark of the drowned vault, with the Choir hammering at the doors above and Vole buying them minutes with her life, the god had spoken to him in a voice like tide over stone, and it had asked. *Do you belong to me, or yourself?* And Edmund had answered: *Myself.* And the god had said — and this was the part that made him laugh, once, alone, an ugly wet laugh on a cold beach at dawn — the god had said, *Good. Then help me ask everyone else.*

"Where are we going?" Maren asked. "You've been steering us north since we cleared the cliffs and you haven't said why."

"Greyharbor," Edmund said. "I have to see it."

Maren looked at him for a long time. "They'll hang you. They'll hang both of us. Half the city thinks you broke the world, and the other half thinks you saved it, and both halves are armed."

"I know." He took the tiller from her gently. "But I left someone there. And I owe a debt I can only pay by looking at what I made."

She didn't argue. She went below and slept for eleven hours, and while she slept, Edmund sailed them south toward the city that had outlawed him, and the sea sang the whole way, and he found — to his enormous surprise — that he was humming along.

* * *

In Greyharbor, the laws died faster than anyone could enforce them.

It began, as most things in Greyharbor began, at the docks. A luck-charm — one of Edmund's old stock, sold years ago to a dockworker named Tam Brase who had died in a rigging accident two winters back — flared to life on the neck of Tam's widow, Sella Brase, while she was hauling nets. It had been a cheap thing, Edmund remembered later, hearing the story thirdhand: knotted twine and a fish-scale, barely worth the three coppers. For twenty years it had been exactly that. Now it glowed like a captive sunrise, and every net Sella touched came up full.

Within a day there were four hundred people outside her door wanting to know where she'd bought it. Within two days, half of them were wearing their grandmothers' wedding rings and finding that the rings *answered* — warming when danger neared, cooling when lies were told nearby. The witch-hunters' office put out a proclamation: all charms to be surrendered for destruction.

The proclamation was used, by nightfall, to wrap fried fish.

Inquisitor-Captain Dorn Hale, acting head of an office whose Grand Inquisitor had defected and then died holding a door against her own former comrades, read the situation with the cold clarity that had made him a decent hunter and was making him a terrible tyrant. He ordered arrests. His hunters went into the Saltrow and found that the woman they'd come to arrest had already healed a child's fevered lung with a touch, in front of thirty witnesses, and the thirty witnesses were standing in the street with arms crossed, and behind them stood sixty more, and behind *them*, the baker Odile Fenn with a bread paddle held like a halberd and an expression that promised kneading of a very personal kind.

Dorn Hale withdrew his men. He sat in his office until midnight, staring at the empty chair where Serah Vole used to sit, and at the single page she'd left in his desk — found after, in her handwriting, dated the day she'd gone downriver: *If I'm wrong about the Choir, burn my files. If I'm right, you'll know soon enough, and then burn the laws instead.*

He burned the laws at dawn. All of them. He did it in the courtyard, publicly, and when a junior hunter asked him what they were supposed to do now, Dorn Hale said, "I haven't the faintest idea. Fetch me tea. And find out who's organizing the bread distribution, because if the city starves while it's busy being free, none of the rest matters."

* * *

Not everything that woke was kind.

In the undercrofts of the Hollow Choir's chapterhouse — half of it collapsed now, the seal-anchor dead, the caged bell cracked open like an egg — the remnants gathered. There were perhaps sixty of them left in Greyharbor: canons and chanters, door-wardens and bone-counters, men and women who had spent their whole lives believing that silence was mercy, that the gods had been locked away the way you lock away a plague, and that the Silencing had been the kindest act in human history.

Their leader was a thin canon named Anselm Roke, who had never in his life raised his voice, and who now spoke very quietly indeed, which was worse.

"The seal is broken," he told them, in the candlelit ruin of the crypt. "The heart beats unbound. And you have heard what follows — the singing, the lights, the charms waking in the gutters like rats after a flood. You have heard them call it a blessing." He let the silence stretch. He was good at silence. It was, after all, his faith. "I ask you to remember what the silence *contained*. Twenty years ago the gods warred, and men burned as the grass between them burns. We did not imprison the divine out of hate. We imprisoned it out of love — love for every child who got to grow up without a god's whim deciding whether her village stood or fell. That work is not finished because the door is broken. The work has only changed shape."

"What shape?" asked a young chanter, a girl of maybe nineteen, whose voice shook.

Anselm smiled. It was not a comforting smile, but it was sincere, which in some ways was more frightening. "Now we do not guard the prison. Now we must remind the world why it was built. Gently, at first. And then, if gentle fails—" he folded his thin hands— "not gently."

Sixty people bowed their heads. Fifty-nine of them believed him completely.

The sixtieth was the young chanter, and she kept her eyes down and her doubt hidden, and she would matter later, though no one in that room knew it yet.

* * *

The god did not make war. This surprised everyone, including, Edmund suspected, the god.

Instead it *asked*. That was the only word for it. It had no priests yet, no doctrine, no name anyone agreed on — the vigil-keepers called it the Tidefather, the smugglers called it the Deep Mother, a philosopher in Brackwater published a pamphlet calling it *the Question Below*, which the god reportedly found hilarious, insofar as a tide could be said to find anything hilarious. What it did was speak — softly, in the space between wave-sounds, in dreams, in the sudden hush before rain — and what it said was always, always, a question.

*What do you need?*

*What did the silence cost you?*

*What will you build, now that nothing is forbidden?*

It never demanded worship. It never punished disbelief. When a Choir remnant in the fishing town of Marlow stood on the seawall and screamed denunciations at the water for six hours, the god listened to all of it, and then, witnesses swore, the tide came up gentle and cold and washed the man's feet, and a voice like deep stone said, *Tell me more. Why did you choose the silence? What did it give you?* And the man had sat down on the seawall and wept, and by all accounts was still sitting there, thinking, three days later.

"He's not what I expected," Maren said. They were a day out from Greyharbor, running easy before a following wind that felt less like weather than like courtesy. "Gods are supposed to want things. Thrones. Blood. Worship with the good incense."

"He wants conversation," Edmund said. "Twenty years underwater with no one to talk to. I understand that better than I'd like."

Maren snorted. Then, quieter: "Vole would have hated it. Or loved it. One of those. Probably both, loudly, at the same time."

"Yeah." Edmund looked at the horizon. "When we get to the city, there's a chapel on Lantern Row the vigil-keepers kept. She'd have called it sentimental. I'm going to put something of hers there anyway."

"What?"

"I don't know yet. Something true." He paused. "Maybe just the truth. That she broke like ice, but she chose which way to break."

Maren said nothing to that. She just put her hand briefly on his shoulder — the scarred one, the one that had hurt her, the one she'd forgiven barely — and left it there a while, and the sea sang underneath them, and neither of them told it to stop.

* * *

Greyharbor, when they reached it, was unrecognizable in the way that a person is unrecognizable when they finally start smiling.

Charms hung from every lintel. Not hidden, not smuggled, not sold in whispers in back rooms — *hung*, openly, swinging in the harbor wind, hundreds of small lights and knots and bells and bones, each one humming faintly with returned power. The anti-magic statutes were gone. The witch-hunters' office had become, through a bureaucratic transformation no one could quite explain, the Office of Charitable Registration, on the theory that Dorn Hale was very good at forms and the city was going to need forms. Children ran through the streets making sparks for pennies. Old women who had hidden their gifts for twenty years were teaching them, badly and joyfully, in tavern yards.

And everywhere, threaded through it all, the questions. Written on walls in condensation. Heard in the hush between bells. *What do you need? What will you build?*

Edmund walked through it in a borrowed coat and a hat pulled low, anonymous, and felt something in his chest crack open that he'd nailed shut twenty years ago.

At the corner of Saltrow and Rope-Walk, he stopped. There was a shop there — his shop, or its shell. The windows were broken from the Choir's attack. But someone had cleaned the glass out, and swept the floor, and in the window someone had placed a sign in careful charcoal letters:

**CHARMS MENDED. QUESTIONS ANSWERED. TEA IF YOU'RE POLITE.**

Below it, smaller: *Proprietor rumored dead. Rent negotiable.*

Edmund stood there a long time. A girl of about ten came around the corner, saw him looking, and said, "That's the charm-seller's shop. He broke the seal. Mam says he's a hero. Da says he's a fool who'll get us all killed. Gran says he owes her a refund on a luck-charm that didn't work for fifteen years and now works too well."

"What does your gran want refunded?" Edmund asked, before he could stop himself.

"She says it keeps answering her *before she asks*. Wakes her up at night, glowing." The girl shrugged with the magnificent indifference of youth. "Gran says if the god wants to chat that badly, it can pay rent like everyone else."

Edmund laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the vault — and the sound startled him, and the girl grinned at him like she'd won something.

Then, across the square, he saw them.

A dozen figures in gray, moving through the market crowd with the terrible patience of people who believe themselves righteous. No livery, but he knew the walk. Bone-counters. Door-wardens. Choir remnants, and at their center a thin man with folded hands and a quiet mouth, speaking softly to a knot of frightened stallholders, and even at this distance Edmund could see the crowd's fear changing shape — hardening, organizing, becoming something with edges.

Anselm Roke had come to Greyharbor. And he was not screaming at the sea. He was knocking on doors.

The girl followed Edmund's gaze. "Who's that?"

"That," Edmund said quietly, "is a man who thinks kindness is a cage." He pulled the borrowed hat lower. "And I think I'm done hiding."

Behind him, faint and vast and patient, the harbor sang its question: *What will you build?*

Ahead, the gray figures turned as one, as if they'd heard the answer coming.
