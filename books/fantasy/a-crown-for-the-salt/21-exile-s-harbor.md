## 21. Exile's Harbor

Greyharbor smelled the same. That was the cruelest part.

Edmund had braced himself for ash and ruin — for a city broken open by the waking of gods, its salt-locked streets cracked like old pottery. What he found instead was fish and tar and frying onions, the same morning stink that had greeted him every day for twenty years, as though the world could remake itself overnight and still remember to feed itself breakfast.

He pulled the hood lower and joined the queue at the Salt Gate.

The gate guards weren't checking papers anymore. They were checking hands. A bored sergeant in the grey-and-white of the city watch held out his palm to each traveler, murmuring the new words everyone had learned in the last three weeks: *Show me your light.* And one by one, farmers and fishwives and pilgrims opened their hands and let their small magics bloom — a candleflame, a curl of frost, a bead of light like a firefly caught mid-thought. The guards didn't arrest anyone. They just wrote names in a ledger, because even miracles need bookkeeping.

Edmund showed them nothing. He'd spent two decades learning how to be empty, and it turned out that skill kept. The sergeant squinted at him, saw a tired man with a limp and a farmer's coat, and waved him through.

*You used to sell charms under this very wall,* Edmund thought, passing beneath the arch where he'd once paid a boy a copper to whistle when the watch came round. *You used to be somebody here.*

Now he was nobody, and nobody was the safest thing a man could be in Greyharbor. Mostly.

Because there was one name on everybody's lips that morning, chalked on walls and shouted from tavern doors and printed in cheap broadsheets sold by children too young to know what they were selling: **MAREN QUILL, TRAITOR OF THE VAULT.**

* * *

The trouble with heroes, Edmund reflected as he threaded through the Fishmarket, was that people needed villains to balance the books.

The story had traveled faster than he had, mutating with every telling. In the version sung outside the Anchor & Chain, Edmund Ravensworth was a martyr — the brave charm-seller who broke the seal and died holding back the Choir, bless his memory, drink up. Vole was already a saint; they'd given her a statue in the Temple district, or the start of one, scaffolding and wet stone and a face that looked nothing like her. But Maren Quill had come home alive, and she had come home with her brother's name attached to hers — *the seal-builder's sister*, the woman who'd put a knife to the liberator's throat — and grief needs somewhere to go, and a smuggler with a scarred jaw was easier to find than a dead man.

They were going to give her a trial. Everyone knew what kind of trial.

Edmund bought a meat pie he didn't want, mostly to have an excuse to stand still, and listened.

"—heard they're keeping her in the old customs house, not the gaol—"

"—her own brother built the cage, and she guarded it, my nan says—"

"—should hang her off the harbor chain and let the tide do the rest, that's what I say—"

"Charming town," said a voice at his elbow. "I've missed it terribly."

Edmund did not jump. Twenty years of selling illegal charms to frightened people had taught him never to jump, no matter who materialized beside you. He turned his head a careful inch and found a woman in a fishwife's shawl, gray-streaked hair tucked under a bonnet, chewing on a candied ginger like it owed her money.

"Maren," he said quietly. "You're supposed to be in custody."

"I'm supposed to be *hanged*, by popular demand." She popped the last of the ginger into her mouth. "Custody is what happens between now and Saturday. I walked out the customs house window last night. Third floor. There was a drainpipe."

"You're fifty-one years old."

"Fifty-two in the spring, and the drainpipe knows it better than I do." Her eyes, sharp and brown above the shawl, flicked across the market, cataloguing exits out of habit. "I came to find you before you did something noble and stupid."

"That's my whole character."

"Aye, and it nearly got us both killed on the salt road, so we're retiring it." She took his arm — a fishwife steering her husband, nothing more — and turned them both toward the harbor. "Walk. Don't look at the notice board. There's a drawing of me on it that makes me look handsome, which is how I knew it was inaccurate."

* * *

They talked in the lee of a coal shed, out of the wind, while gulls argued overhead about something neither of them would ever understand.

"They'll search the coast roads," Maren said. "Every cart, every ferry. They've got watchers at Brackwater and the toll bridges. Your face is known now, Ed. Not just mine. The martyr's ghost, walking around."

"I've been a ghost before. It's restful."

"It's not funny."

"I wasn't laughing." He was, a little. He couldn't help it. Something had loosened in him since the vault, since the seal, since the moment the god's heart had beaten against his palms like a second tide and he had chosen — chosen freely, kneeling to no one — to let it wake. Grief and release had gotten tangled up inside him like fishing line, and some days he couldn't tell which end he was pulling. "Maren. Why did you come to me? You could be over the hills by now. North, past the flats, anywhere they don't sing songs about you."

She was quiet long enough that a gull landed, considered them, and left, offended.

"Because you buried Vole," she said finally. "On the cliff. Alone. And then you walked into the sea-town and gave away everything we fought for, and you never once said her name out loud where anyone could hear it break your voice." She shrugged, rough and awkward, the way she did with anything soft. "That's the sort of fool I sail with. That's the only sort."

"She saved us," Edmund said. "She held the stair for eleven minutes. I counted."

"I know. I was behind her." Maren looked out at the water, at the masts swaying against a sky the color of unpolished pewter. "So. We leave tonight, we leave together, and we leave by water, because the roads are watched and the tide isn't. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Then, because honesty had become a habit he couldn't shake: "There's one more thing I have to do first."

She groaned. "There it is. Noble and stupid, right on schedule."

"It's small," he promised. "It's very small."

* * *

The map had been the beginning of everything — a dying man's blood-warm weight in his hands, wax seal and tide-marks and a drowned vault drawn by pre-Silencing hands. He had tried to burn it once, in another life, three weeks and a hundred years ago. It had refused the flame then.

It didn't refuse now.

Edmund stood in the bow of Maren's borrowed ketch, *Kestrel's Regret*, half a mile off Greyharbor's outer lights, and watched the parchment catch. Firelight played across his knuckles — his hands, which no longer trembled, which hadn't trembled since the vault — and the map burned the way ordinary paper burns, curling and browning and letting go, as if whatever had made it refuse flame had been released along with everything else. As if its work was done.

He'd thought about keeping it. That was the truth he'd carried out of the city like a stone in his boot. He could have kept it, hidden it, hoarded it — proof, relic, a piece of the story with his name on it. Museums were springing up all over the coast, people said. People collected things now. People remembered things now, out loud, in daylight.

But some things should get to be finished.

When the last corner went to ash, he tipped them over the rail. The sea took them without comment. Twenty years of silence, one courier's dying breath, a witch-hunter's fall, a friend's knife at his throat, a god's question — *do you belong to me, or yourself?* — all of it, gone into the grey water, weightless.

Maren leaned on the rail beside him. "Feel different?"

"No," he said honestly. Then: "Yes. Lighter. Like setting down a pack I forgot I was carrying."

"You'll want to watch that. Men who set down packs tend to pick up worse ones. Causes, usually." She said it dryly, but her hand found his shoulder and stayed there a moment, brief and hard, which from Maren Quill was practically an embrace. "Come on. Tide turns in an hour, and I want to be past the chain before the harbor watch remembers it owns a boat."

* * *

They cleared the harbor chain at dusk, riding the last of the ebb, and Greyharbor shrank behind them into a scatter of lamplight and smoke.

From the water, at evening, with the first stars coming out over the Temple district — over Vole's unfinished statue, though he couldn't see it and didn't try — the city looked almost gentle. He let himself look anyway. Let himself feel it: the ache of leaving a place that had never been kind to him but had been *his*, the strange gratitude of it, the grief with nowhere to sit down.

"You'll go north," Maren said. It wasn't a question. She'd been watching him steer for two days, and Maren Quill read helmsmen the way other people read weather.

"The northern shore," he agreed. "Past the flats. Where the magic-born are turning up with no one to teach them, burning their own fingers trying to make candlelight." He smiled faintly. "Someone has to tell them power doesn't need a crown. Someone told me, eventually. Took a god, a war, and a funeral, but someone told me."

"And you'll write letters to a dead woman."

"Occasionally."

"Address them to me instead, sometimes. I'll read them aloud to the sea. She liked the sea." Maren turned in toward the cabin, then paused at the companionway, silhouetted against the lantern glow. "Edmund. For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much, coming from a wanted woman on a stolen boat — I'm glad the map found your door and not mine. I'd have burned it. I nearly did anyway, at knifepoint, and you know it."

"I know it."

"But I'm glad," she said, "that it was you. Sleep. I have the middle watch."

He slept, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was its own kind of mercy.

* * *

Dawn found him at the rail with tea that tasted of tar and freedom, watching the coastline slide northward. Behind them, far off, Greyharbor's morning bells rang out — all nine of them, unhurried, unsilenced, ringing for no god and no law and no reason except that it was morning, and bells ring in the morning.

Edmund raised his cup to the sound.

Then he frowned, and lowered it, and leaned forward against the rail.

Far up the coast, where the cliffs bent west toward the northern shore — *his* shore, the shore he'd spoken of like a promise — a light was burning. Not a beacon. Not a signal fire. It rose off the headland in a slow golden column, steady as a held breath, and even at this distance Edmund felt it against his skin like warmth from an open oven door. Magic, raw and untrained and enormous, the kind of flare a frightened child makes without knowing.

Maren came up the steps two at a time, squinting. "What in the deep salt is *that*?"

"I don't know," Edmund said. His heart was doing something complicated — fear and recognition and, underneath it all, a fierce and unfamiliar hope. "But it's on our heading."

He took the wheel from the pin and brought the *Kestrel's Regret* about, north, toward the light.
