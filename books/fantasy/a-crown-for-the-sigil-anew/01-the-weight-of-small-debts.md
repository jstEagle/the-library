## 1. The Weight of Small Debts

The bottled storm had been sitting on Kestrel's appraisal table since dawn, grumbling.

It was a small thing, no bigger than a pear, blown glass the color of a bruise, and inside it clouds turned in slow, sulky circles. Every so often a thread of lightning would unspool against the glass and tap at it, the way a bored prisoner taps at a cell wall. Kestrel had appraised forty-one impossible things since coming to the guild, and they had developed a private taxonomy of the trade's hazards: cursed rings were tedious, haunted mirrors were vain, but bottled weather was *moody*.

"All right," Kestrel murmured, keeping their voice low, because the walls of the Guild of Impossible Things were old and thin and had opinions about who talked to merchandise. "Let's see what you are."

They turned the bottle in the lamplight and consulted the ledger open beside them. Column one: provenance. The storm had come in overnight with a fishwife from Lower Marsh who swore her late husband caught it off the jetty in a net meant for herring. Column two: condition. Column three: danger classification, which was the column Kestrel liked least, because it was the one where honesty cost money.

A storm like this—a genuine squall, not a conjured imitation with a hair of graveyard fog in it—could sell to any of three buyers. The College of Natural Philosophers would pay well for study. Lady Corvane, who collected catastrophes, would pay better for display. And the war-ministry man who came through every few months wearing a civilian coat that never quite fit his shoulders would pay best of all, and Kestrel did not let themselves think about what a ministry wanted with weather.

"Provenance, honest," they said, writing. "Condition: agitated but sound. Danger classification—" They held the bottle up. Inside, a tiny bolt struck the glass directly opposite their eye, as though it knew where to aim. "—moderate. You're all bark, aren't you. A little thunder, a little drama."

The storm swelled itself darker out of what Kestrel could only assume was spite.

"Yes, yes." Kestrel set it down in the padded crate and laced the ties. "Everyone in this building has a temperament. It's a wonder we get anything done."

Downstairs, the guild was waking up the way it always did: not gradually, but in stages of increasing chaos. First came the smell—Tansy's porridge, which was either burnt or miraculous depending on an alignment of factors no one had ever successfully charted. Then footsteps overhead as the apprentices claimed the good attic bunks before Marta could beat them to it (Marta always beat them to it; she slept in her boots). Then, inevitably, the argument.

"—saying we *have* it, I'm saying we don't have it *yet*, which is a different problem entirely—"

"That's exactly the kind of distinction that gets us audited, Fenn."

Kestrel descended into the common room to find Fennimore Quill, the guild's acquisitionist, standing on a chair for reasons that would become clear and probably regrettable, while Odile Voss, guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things, stood below him with her arms folded and the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she already knew the sum of.

"The Sable Compass," Fenn said, "is *nearly* ours."

"It is nearly ours the way I am nearly tall," said Odile.

"You are tall," said Kestrel, taking their seat at the long table. "For a woman who once threatened a duke."

"I didn't threaten him. I described consequences. There's a legal difference, which is precisely my point, Fennimore." Odile pulled out her own chair and sat, and the chair complained the way everything in the guild eventually complained. She was sixty-something by the records, which the guild kept, and ageless by the face, which the records did not. Her hair was iron-grey and pinned with whatever had been nearest to hand that morning; today it appeared to be a bone stylus worth more than the street outside. "Kestrel. The ledger."

"The ledger." Kestrel opened the great book—the second-great book, technically; the first-great lived in Odile's office behind a lock that had never been picked, and several people had tried—and began the morning's accounting aloud, because Odile liked it aloud, because numbers spoken in a room full of people couldn't be quietly changed later.

"We hold, in escrow, the bottled storm, appraised moderate, pending sale. We hold the dowry letters of the Widow Asche, redeemable at month's end. We owe the Lamplighters' Benevolent Fund four silver, which Tansy keeps forgetting and I keep remembering, which is why I do the books. We are owed by Captain Harn two marks and an apology, and I've given up on the apology. And we took in last night, from Mrs. Ock of Lower Marsh, one storm, provenance honest, payment rendered in advance: a winter's coal and her husband's fishing boat stored dry until spring, if it ever stops being haunted."

"It's haunted?" said Marta, arriving with her boots on and her hair like a crow's nest after weather.

"The boat. Not the widow."

"Can boats be haunted?"

"This one is. It sighs. Mrs. Ock says it started the week after the funeral and it isn't malicious, it's just lonely, and she can't keep it at the jetty because lonely boats attract things, and she'd rather we kept it than the things got it." Kestrel looked up. "I said yes before I checked with anyone, so if we're voting retroactively, I vote yes."

Odile's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling, which was a small tightening at one corner that the whole guild knew how to read. "A haunted boat costs us nothing but space. Yes. Write it in under mercies, small."

That was the third column in the second-great ledger. Relics, rumors, mercies. The Guild of Impossible Things traded in the first two to afford the third, though if you asked Odile—which no one did twice—she'd tell you the order was reversed.

* * *

Midmorning brought the deliveries, and with them the part of the day Kestrel loved most and was most careful to love quietly.

The guild's cart-horse was named Bonfire, on account of a coat the color of embers and a temperament that was mostly smoke. He was old, or old-ish, or perhaps simply dignified—he refused to clarify—and he had come with the guild building itself, so far back that even Odile referred to him as *original fixtures*. His stall stood at the end of the courtyard, and every morning he received a apple, a brushing, and whatever gossip Kestrel could carry out to him.

This morning Kestrel carried the apple and the storm story both.

"She wants eight gold for it," Kestrel said, leaning against the stall door with the ledger balanced on one knee, pretending, in the way they had perfected over eleven years, to be checking inventory. "The storm. Eight gold, and I told her six was generous, and she told me my arithmetic was an insult to her husband's ghost, and honestly? Fair. It's a good storm. Old-fashioned workmanship. They don't bottle them like that anymore."

Bonfire lipped the apple from their palm with the delicacy of a bishop handling relics, and chewed, and fixed Kestrel with one dark, liquid, deeply unimpressed eye.

"I know," Kestrel said. "You think everything in this courtyard is overpriced. You think that about the chickens too."

Bonfire snorted. It was, to any passerby watching from the lane, merely a horse being a horse: ears flicking, hide twitching, the ordinary dumb music of an animal. But between the ear-flick and the hide-twitch there ran a rhythm, and Kestrel read it the way other people read handwriting.

*I heard,* the rhythm said. *About the storm. Rats in her wall were talking about it before her cart reached the gate.*

"The rats have opinions on bottling now?"

*Rats have opinions on everything. It's why cats hate them.* A shift of weight, a toss of the grey mane. *The little ones say the ministry man was asking at the tollhouse yesterday. Asking who does our appraisals.*

Kestrel's quill paused above the ledger for precisely one heartbeat, then resumed. This was the discipline, and they practiced it the way other people practiced scales: pause without stopping, look without staring, let the fear pass through you like weather and leave your face untouched.

"The ministry man," they repeated, mildly, to the air, to the ledger, to no one. "Good. He pays promptly and doesn't haggle. I'll set the storm aside."

Bonfire stamped once, and the stamp meant *be careful*, and Kestrel scratched the warm grey velvet of his nose and said, softly enough that even the rats would call it a murmur, "I'm always careful."

Which was true, and also the loneliest sentence they owned.

Because here was the shape of Kestrel Okonkwo's life, worn smooth as a river stone by eleven years of daily wear: they could speak to beasts. Had been able since before they had words for it, back when talking to the yard sparrows seemed as natural as talking to their mother, back before their mother knelt down one evening with her hands on their shoulders and explained, in a voice gone quiet and terrible, that the world burned people for exactly this. That the boy three doors down, Tam, sweet Tam who sang to the pigeons, had been taken by Inquisitors in the spring, and no one spoke of him anymore, and when Kestrel asked why, their mother had said, *because speaking his name is like holding up a sign*. That the gift—if it was a gift; some days it felt more like a debt inherited from someone reckless—must never, ever surface. Not in a glance held too long on a falcon. Not in a question answered before it was asked. Not in comfort offered to a creature in front of witnesses.

Invisibility wasn't cowardice, their mother had said. Invisibility was a craft. Do it well and you could live a whole life.

Kestrel had done it very well indeed. They had a place here—had a *seat* here, a ledger with their name on the spine, a hook for their coat, a bowl at Tansy's table—and none of it, not one brick of it, knew what they were. The guild loved them. The guild did not know them. Some nights those two facts sat together in Kestrel's chest like strangers sharing a bench, and some rare black nights they swapped places.

But this morning there were apples and ledgers and a horse who worried about them in fluent equine, and Kestrel found, as they found most mornings, that the warmth outweighed the ache. Contentment built on concealment is still contentment. It just has to be swept daily, like a floor.

*Ministry man,* Bonfire pressed again, insistent. *Careful means more than a still face.*

"I know." Kestrel closed the ledger. "Thank you. Now tell me honestly—does the new chicken have something wrong with it, or is it simply like that?"

*Both,* said Bonfire.

* * *

The afternoon went sideways at half past three, in the person of Master Hollis of the College of Natural Philosophers, who arrived flushed and important and wanting to see the storm.

"Hollis," said Kestrel, meeting him in the entry hall with the professional smile they kept for buyers, the one that showed exactly as many teeth as courtesy required. "You received our notice, then."

"Received it, received it. Eight gold, your note said."

"Our note said *appraisal available upon inspection*. The number comes after the inspection." Kestrel led him back to the storeroom, walking at the pace of a funeral so that Hollis's enthusiasm would have time to exhaust itself against their unhurried back. Behind them, Fenn fell into step, drawn by the gravitational pull of any transaction larger than a chicken.

The storm was sulking in its crate. When Kestrel lifted it into the light, it brightened obligingly—storms liked attention, this one especially—and Hollis made the noise he always made, a sort of reverent wheeze.

"Genuine. Genuine wild-caught. Look at the convection cells—look at the *rotation*—"

"Six gold," said Kestrel.

"Four."

"Six. It's honest work, Hollis, no graveyard fog, no curse-residue. Your instruments will love it and so will your funding committee."

"Five, and I'll take it today."

From the doorway, Odile's voice arrived without her body, the way it often did: "Six, Master Hollis, and the guild will store the empty bottle for you afterward. Glasswork that fine shouldn't be broken."

Hollis, who had been coming to the guild for nine years and had never once won an argument with a woman he couldn't see, sighed and paid six gold.

Afterward, the common room smelled of victory, which at the guild meant tea and Tansy's seedcake, which was dry but traditional. Kestrel counted the coins twice into the strongbox, entered the sale in the ledger under *relics, atmospheric*, and set aside the tithe for the Lamplighters' Fund before Odile could catch them skipping it. Around the table the evening gathered itself: Fenn recounting the negotiation to Marta with himself cast as the decisive figure; Tansy humming; Odile in her corner chair with the first-great ledger open on her knees, reading entries that nobody else was permitted to read, her thumb moving slowly down columns of ink gone brown with age.

It was, Kestrel thought, watching steam curl off their cup, a good day. A whole day, start to finish, with nothing in it stranger than a moody storm and a horse's opinion of chickens. Eleven years of careful living, and the craft of invisibility had paid its wages again: a seat, a hook, a bowl, a family assembled from strays and misfits who needed no explanations because everyone at this table was somebody's unsolved mystery.

"Kestrel." Odile hadn't looked up from her ledger. "You're smiling at your tea. Either it's spiked or you're content, and I inventory the spirits weekly."

"Just balancing the day," Kestrel said. "It balances."

"Mm." Odile turned a page, slow as a tide. "Days that balance are the ones I distrust. But drink it anyway."

Later, banked firelight, the guild settling into its night-noises—the tick of the beams, Tansy's distant snore, the sigh of the haunted boat in the yard, audible even from here, patient and sad. Kestrel climbed to their bunk past Marta asleep in her boots as prophesied, and lay in the dark doing the last accounting of the day, the one that never went in any ledger.

Six gold. Coal secured for the widow Asche. One boat, lonely, stored dry. One secret, intact. One family, unaware.

It balanced.

They were nearly asleep when the knocking started—not at the street door, which had a proper iron knocker and civilized hours, but at the storeroom shutter, the low one, the one that faced the alley where nothing with good intentions ever stood. Three blows, uneven, weakening. Then a sound Kestrel knew from the market slaughterhouses and had hoped never to hear at the guild: a body sliding down wood, and staying down.

And beneath it, threading through the walls from the gutter and the cellar and every rat-run in the old bones of the building, the small urgent voices of the rats, all saying the same thing in the same ragged rhythm:

*Bleeding. Bleeding. The man in the alley says a word we don't know, and the word is burning him.*
