## 3. What the Dying Press Into Your Hands

The grey coats did not stop at the guild.

Kestrel watched them from behind the curtain, breath held, counting their steps the way they counted coins — one, two, three, four — until the rhythm of boots passed the door without slowing and faded toward the fishmongers' row. Not for them. Not yet. The dogs had been following a scent, but scents in Lowmarket tangled like fishing line, and whatever trail the hounds held tonight belonged to someone else's sin.

Still, Kestrel did not breathe properly until the last echo of those boots dissolved into the river fog.

They turned back to the storeroom.

The stranger lay where they'd dragged him, on the folded sailcloth between the casks of pickled eel and the crate of bottled storms that would not stop muttering to itself. He was younger than he'd seemed at the door — the blood and the raving had aged him, the way mud ages a road. Up close, in the light of the single lamp Kestrel dared to burn, his face was ordinary. A courier's face. A face built to be forgotten on purpose.

He was still talking.

Not raving now. The fever-words had burned down to embers, and what came out instead was low and steady and terribly clear, as though he were reading from a ledger only he could see.

"Vhalcrest," he said. "You have to understand. It isn't gone. They only wrote it out of the books. You can't kill a country with ink. You can only make it lonely."

"I know," Kestrel said, because it seemed crueler to argue. "I know. Hold still."

"Hold still." He laughed, and the laugh cost him something; it came out wet at the edges. "That's what my mother used to say. Hold still, Tam. Bees only sting what flails." His eyes found Kestrel's, and they were too bright, the way lamps are too bright just before the oil runs out. "Are you a bee?"

"No," Kestrel said gently. "I'm an accountant."

"An accountant." He said it the way some men say *saint*. "Good. Good. Then you'll keep it. You'll enter it properly. Debts must be recorded or they're never paid."

"What debts?" Kestrel asked, kneeling, pressing the heel of their hand against the worst of the bleeding, which was everywhere and therefore nowhere in particular. "Tell me who owes you, and I'll write it down. That's true. That's the guild's whole trade. Impossible things, honestly priced."

But Tam the courier was no longer listening to the question. He was listening to something else — something under the floor, perhaps, or under the world. His head turned toward the wall where the rats lived, and Kestrel felt it happen: the small cold attention of the house's creatures turning all at once, like weathervanes in a changing wind.

*He's speaking,* the oldest rat said from inside the wainscoting, in the wordless way rats say things. *He's speaking our language.*

Kestrel went very still.

Because it was true. The man's next words were words — Kestrel heard them as words, shaped them as words — but they were also *not* words. They arrived the way the horses spoke, the way the gulls spoke, the way the storm in its bottle grumbled: meaning first, sound after, sound merely a coat thrown over the shape of the thing. And no human tongue should have fit that coat.

"*Ashkarath,*" the courier said.

The rats fled. All of them, at once, a soft rain of claws retreating into the deep dark behind the walls, and Kestrel understood that whatever *ashkarath* meant, it was not a word you said in front of small creatures unless you wanted them to remember it forever.

The man's hand shot out and caught Kestrel's wrist with a strength that had no business remaining in him. His other hand came up between them, trembling, holding something folded — leather? paper? It was hard to tell. It was blackened along one edge, scorched in a pattern that looked less like fire damage and more like a bite.

"Take it," he said. "Take it take it take it—"

"I can't—"

"You're the accountant." His grip tightened. Fever-bright, those eyes. Terribly awake. "Everything given must be received. Everything received must be kept. Everything kept—" and here his voice cracked open into something that was half prayer and half apology, "—must be *delivered*, Kestrel Okonkwo."

Kestrel's blood stopped moving for a moment.

They had never told him their name. They had never told anyone in this city their full name; they gave the ledger-name, *Kess*, to customers, to the watch, to the tax assessor, to everyone. *Okonkwo* was a word that existed only in one place now: a dead woman's mouth, twenty years gone, saying it over a cradle in a language the Crown had since made illegal to speak aloud.

"How do you know my—"

"He told me your name," the courier whispered. "Before I crossed the water. He said — he said *find the one who keeps accounts with animals.* He said you'd know what to do with it." The scorched thing pressed into Kestrel's palms, hot as a fresh loaf, though there was no fire near it. "I don't know what to do with it. I ran. I'm sorry. I ran so far and it wasn't far enough, and now it has to be you."

"Who told you? Who is *he*?"

But the answer, when it came, was not an answer. The courier's whole body arched once — a bow, almost, courteous to the end — and then he sagged back against the sailcloth, and the terrible brightness went out of his eyes all at once, like a shutter closing on a lit window.

The storeroom was very quiet.

Above, faintly, the bottled storm muttered in its crate. Somewhere across the city a clock Kestrel could not see struck the hour anyway, out of habit.

Kestrel knelt there for a long moment with a dead man's gift warming their hands and a dead man's word ringing in their skull in a language that had no business living in a human throat.

*Ashkarath.*

When they thought it, even silently, the silence around them changed texture. The eels in their brine shifted. The moths at the lamp glass beat their wings in a new rhythm. As if the word were a stone dropped into a pond whose surface was everything.

"Well," Kestrel said aloud, to nobody, in the flat voice they used for balancing columns that refused to balance. "That's going in the ledger."

* * *

They did not sleep. There was no time for sleep, and besides, Kestrel had learned long ago that fear and bookkeeping could not occupy the same mind at once, and bookkeeping was safer.

First: the body. This was the part Odile would need to know about, and the part Odile would hate most, because bodies brought questions and questions brought the watch and the watch brought forms, and forms, in Kestrel's experience, were how the Crown counted people it intended to spend later. They covered the courier with the good sailcloth — the waterproof one, worth real money, which felt like the kind of extravagance grief permits — and noted the expense in the margin of their memory for later penance.

Second: the object.

They carried it to the counting room and set it on the desk beneath the good lamp, and made themselves look at it properly, because whatever else it was, it was inventory now, and inventory deserved respect.

It was a map.

Or it had been a map, once. It was made of something between leather and vellum, soft as skin, and someone had tried very hard to destroy it — the scorched edge, the water stains, a long diagonal scar that might have been a blade or a claw. But maps are stubborn things. So are debts. Across the surviving surface, in faded ink the color of old tea, coastlines curled. Mountain ranges hunched. A river system spread like the veins of a leaf, and at the center of it all, where a capital city ought to be, someone had drawn a spire — a single tall stroke of ink rising out of a valley like a needle out of cloth.

There were no labels. No names. No compass rose. And no border, which was the strangest thing of all, because every map Kestrel had ever appraised — and they had appraised maps from nine kingdoms and three that claimed to be more than one — was mostly border. Borders were what maps were *for*, if you asked the people who paid for them.

This map had no borders because, Kestrel slowly understood, everything on it was inside.

And then the coastline moved.

Just slightly. Just at the edge of vision, the way you think you saw a cat move in a dark room. Kestrel bent closer, breath fogging the vellum, and watched the western shore ease itself a finger's width further west, unhurried, like a sleeper turning over.

The map was breathing.

"Oh no," Kestrel said. "No, no, no. I am a *bookkeeper*. I deal in fixed quantities. You cannot appreciate a thing that changes while you count it—"

The map, being a map, did not apologize.

Kestrel sat back. Pressed their palms flat against the desk. Made themselves think in columns, because columns were calm, and calm was survival:

*Column one:* a dying man knew their true name, given by someone called *he*, someone who spoke beast-tongue or something adjacent to it, someone who had known about Kestrel — about *them specifically* — before ever sending a courier across the water.

*Column two:* the word. *Ashkarath.* Spoken in a register that moved rats and moths and possibly the eels. Which meant either the courier had the gift — unlikely, or the Inquisitors would have taken him apart looking for it — or the word itself carried the gift the way a bell carries ringing.

*Column three:* the map was alive, illegal, hunted, and currently sitting on their desk like a debt come due.

*Column four:* three grey coats had walked past the guild not four hours ago, following dogs that followed a scent.

Kestrel looked at column four for a long time.

Then they blew out the lamp.

* * *

Hiding a thing well, Kestrel had learned from Odile, is mostly a matter of hiding it among things nobody wants. The guild's cellar shelves held forty years of unlovable inventory: cracked reliquaries, love potions past their efficacy, a taxidermied goose alleged to be haunted but actually just badly stuffed. Nobody searched the boring shelves. Boredom was armor.

But the map was warm through its wrapping, and it hummed — very faintly, very low, a frequency Kestrel felt in their sternum rather than heard — and Kestrel did not trust any shelf to hold it. Shelves were for things that stayed put.

In the end they wrapped it in oilcloth and bound it with the plain twine used for eel casks, and they put it where Kestrel put the things that mattered most, which was not a shelf at all but the false bottom of the ledger chest itself — beneath eleven years of meticulous, dull, defensible, *boring* accounts. Let the Inquisitors dig through columns of pickle-brine expenditures if they wanted it. Let them die of arithmetic.

The chest closed with a click that sounded, in the dark, unnaturally loud.

And that was when the knocking started.

Not at the guild's front door. At the *street door*, the heavy one, the one with the guild's sigil — a key, a candle, and a question mark, wrought in iron — and beneath the knocking came voices, official and tired and carrying that particular authority of men who get paid whether or not they're right.

"Guild of Impossible Things! Open in the name of the Watch!"

Kestrel stood in the dark of the counting room, heart hammering a rhythm no ledger could hold, and took stock the way they always took stock: hands steady? Yes. Face arranged? Working on it. Story?

The story assembled itself the way stories did when Kestrel needed them, smooth and sad and entirely composed of true things, which was the best kind of lie.

They lit the lamp. Smoothed their hair. Opened the street door on the chain, one careful hand's width, and blinked lamplight and sleeplessness at the two watchmen and the sergeant standing on the step with the fog curling around their boots.

"Sergeant Hobb," Kestrel said. "It's past midnight. Someone had better be dead."

"As it happens." The sergeant removed his hat, which was how Sergeant Hobb delivered all bad news, as though the hat were the responsible party. "We've reason to believe a man came through your district tonight. Hurt, by all accounts. Courier, maybe. Grey coat's got the hounds on it, and the trail goes cold somewhere near your corner."

"Near," Kestrel repeated. "Near is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, Sergeant."

"Near is all I've got at this hour, Master Kess." Hobb peered past them into the dim entryway, and Kestrel let him peer, let him see exactly the right amount: lamplight, ledgers, the honest clutter of a business that ran on margins. "Man answering to no particular description. Might be wounded bad enough to be dead by now. You'd tell me?"

"If a dying man stumbled into my shop, Sergeant, I'd tell you, the priest, and probably the fishmongers' association." Kestrel leaned on the door and produced, from somewhere deep and well-rehearsed, the small weary smile of a tradesman whose night is being ruined. "As it stands, the only impossible thing in my inventory tonight is the idea that I'll be paid on time this quarter. Goodnight, Sergeant. Do give my regards to the hounds."

Hobb held their gaze a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then he put his hat back on, which meant the news was delivered, and the watch withdrew into the fog, and Kestrel closed the door, and slid down against it until they were sitting on the floor of their own entryway like a child hiding from thunder.

Their hands were shaking now. They'd waited until it was safe to shake. That was the trick of it — you scheduled the fear, you gave it its appointed hour, and it kept the appointment like anything else in a well-run ledger.

From the kitchen doorway, a soft whicker. Brindle, the cart-horse, had pushed her head through the gap, ears forward, eyes reflecting lamplight.

*The man,* she said. *The one who smelled of smoke and far away. He is done?*

"He's done," Kestrel said quietly.

*His word is still in the walls,* Brindle observed. *The small ones won't come out. They say the word is still walking around in the wood.*

Kestrel closed their eyes. *Ashkarath* sat in their own skull the same way — pacing, patient, waiting to be understood.

"Yes," they said. "I know. Go back to bed, old girl."

*You will not go back to bed,* Brindle said, with the gentle accuracy of horses.

No. Kestrel would not. Because tomorrow — no, not tomorrow, *within hours* — Odile would come down the stairs with her keys and her morning face, and she would find the sailcloth, and she would ask questions, and Kestrel would have to decide, standing in front of the woman who had raised them, how much of the truth a found family could safely hold.

And somewhere beyond the world's edge, in a country erased from every atlas, something vast was waiting for a delivery that had just changed couriers.

Kestrel got up off the floor, picked up the lamp, and went to sit with the body until dawn — partly out of respect, and partly because the dead man's hands, folded now under the sailcloth, still seemed to be waiting for a receipt.

*Delivered,* Kestrel promised him silently. *Whatever you were carrying. Whatever it costs. Entered, received, and — eventually — delivered.*

Upstairs, unaware, the guild slept on.

And in the grey hour before morning, Guildmaster Odile Voss woke from a dream of her sister calling her name from across a burning field, and lay staring at the ceiling, knowing — the way she knew weather and bad debts — that something had entered her house in the night and would never entirely leave it.
