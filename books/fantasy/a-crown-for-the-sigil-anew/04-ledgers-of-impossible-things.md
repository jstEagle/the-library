## 4. Ledgers of Impossible Things

Guildmaster Odile Voss had three rules for the Guild of Impossible Things, and she recited them every morning to herself in the same order, because order was the only luxury that never raised its price.

One: everything that comes through our doors is appraised before it is believed.

Two: no one asks where anyone came from.

Three: if it bleeds on the floorboards, salt it before dawn.

She was thinking about rule three at half past five in the morning, standing in the south storeroom with her hands folded into her apron, looking down at the stranger who had bled out overnight among the crates of bottled weather and mislabeled saints' fingers. He lay on his side now, curled like a comma, as though he'd been interrupted mid-sentence by something larger than death. Which, Odile reflected, was more or less the truth of most people's endings.

The floor around him had been salted. Someone had done it carefully, generously, working the coarse grey crystals into the seams between the boards so that not a single dark thread of stain remained to tell stories. That someone had also dragged a tarpaulin halfway off the shelving to cover him—no, not dragged. Folded it over him. Tucked it under his chin.

Odile stood very still and let the arithmetic finish itself.

Kestrel had closed last night. Kestrel always closed on Thursdays, because Kestrel had decided—without ever saying so, without ever doing anything so legible as deciding—that Thursdays were when Odile's knees bothered her, and therefore Thursdays were theirs. And this storeroom, with its crooked lock that only opened for someone who had lived in the guild longer than two winters, was where you would put a dying man if you did not want the apprentices to find him over breakfast and turn him into a legend before the porridge cooled.

Salted floor. Tucked tarpaulin. Crooked lock turned correctly from the outside.

"Ah," Odile said quietly, to the room in general. "Well."

The room, being a storeroom full of impossible things, said nothing back, which she appreciated. Discretion was the guild's chief export.

She crossed herself the way her mother had taught her—wrong, according to three separate priests, but consistently wrong, which was its own kind of orthodoxy—and then she crouched down, knees cracking like green kindling, and looked at the dead man properly.

He was young. Thirty at the outside, though courier work aged people in dog years. His boots were good ones, resoled twice, and the second sole was stitched with a pattern she recognized: the double-running-stitch of the Vessan post-roads, which meant Crown service once, and not long ago either. His coat had been fine before it became a rag. Under the dried blood at his collarbone there was a brand—not a tattoo, brands were faster and didn't wash off—that she chose not to look at too closely, because looking closely at things was how you ended up knowing things, and knowing things at six in the morning was a heavy way to start a day.

But Odile Voss had been carrying heavy things for sixty-one years and had learned to distribute them across both shoulders evenly.

There was something else. A smell under the blood and the salt and the storeroom's usual perfume of candle-wax, old paper, and the storm-in-a-bottle that had been leaking faintly since 1809. Ozone, almost. Scorched iron. The smell of a page held too near a flame.

Odile's stomach performed a slow, professional descent.

*Oh, you foolish man,* she thought, looking down at his tucked-in tarpaulin. *You didn't.*

*You didn't bring it here.*

* * *

By seven, she had managed three accomplishments: she had sent Tam and the twins off to the fish market with instructions to haggle badly and take all morning about it; she had informed Mrs. Hetch that the south storeroom was "flooded again" and to keep everyone out of it on pain of mopping; and she had located Kestrel, who was in the front room doing what Kestrel did between six and eight every morning, which was pretending to dust while actually eavesdropping on the building.

That was the thing about Kestrel Okonkwo that Odile had understood within a week of taking them in—a thin, watchful child of eleven who arrived at her door with two shirts, no surname she'd admit to, and eyes like someone perpetually counting exits. The child listened to the building. Not the way lonely people talk to houses; Odile did that herself and considered it a virtue. No. Kestrel would go still, head tilted a degree, and the rats in the wainscoting would go still with them, and the pigeons on the roof would rearrange themselves, and once—only once, and never again—the cart-horse in the stable below had answered a question nobody had heard asked, and Odile had walked past the stall very quickly and thought about other things very loudly.

Rule two existed for reasons. *No one asks where anyone came from.* It was the kindest rule and the most necessary one, and for eleven years it had kept them all alive, and Odile intended for it to keep them alive for at least eleven more.

"You're up early," Kestrel said, not looking up from the shelf they were dusting with great theatrical diligence and no actual results.

"I'm old. Being up early is the whole job." Odile lowered herself into the good chair by the counter—the one with the hidden compartment, which currently held nothing more dangerous than liquorice—and studied her bookkeeper. "Sleep well?"

"Mm."

"Mm," Odile agreed. "Me neither."

Kestrel's duster paused for exactly one quarter-second. To anyone else, nothing. To Odile, who had watched this child grow up translating entire conversations into the movement of an eyebrow, it might as well have been a signed confession read aloud at market.

"There was a man," Odile said, conversationally, examining her nails. "Came in after closing. Died in the south storeroom. Terribly rude of him—right on top of inventory week."

The duster resumed. "That's unfortunate," Kestrel said. "Should I enter him in the ledger? Under losses, or—"

"*Kestrel.*"

And there it was. The child—no, twenty-two now, but the word stuck, and Odile suspected it would stick until one of them was in the ground—looked up, and their face did the thing it almost never did: it showed something. Fear, mostly. Underneath the fear, something stubborn and grieving and very young.

Odile felt the familiar ache open behind her ribs, the one shaped exactly like rule two. She had taken in a hundred strays in her time and buried nine of them, and every single time she swore she would stay appraising instead of loving, and every single time the ledgers won out, because what were ledgers for, if not keeping track of what you couldn't afford to lose?

"Before you lie to me," Odile said, gently, "I'd like you to remember that I've been lying professionally since before your mother was born, and that I salted that floor myself this morning and found your work very tidy and very telling. So. Did he give you anything?"

The silence stretched. Outside, the cart-horse stamped twice, and Kestrel's eyes flicked toward the sound and back, quick as a card sharp.

"No," Kestrel said.

"Did he say anything?"

"No."

"Is anything he owned currently hidden somewhere in this building that rats could find?"

Another flicker. Rats. Of course. Odile filed that away next to the cart-horse and the pigeons and eleven years of small unexplained mercies, and smiled the smile she saved for customs officials.

"All right," she said, before Kestrel could build a better fortress out of no. "You don't have to tell me. Rule two. But hear an old woman's appraisal, freely given, no charge: a man died within thirty feet of my bed last night, smelling of burnt paper, and men who smell of burnt paper are followed by men who smell of ink and boot-polish. When those men arrive—and they will arrive, they always arrive—I intend to know exactly what story we are all telling, and I intend for us all to be telling the same one. Do you understand the difference between what I'm asking and what the Crown would ask?"

Kestrel's throat moved. "Yes."

"Good. Think about your answers. You have until they kick the door in, which historically gives us about four days." She hauled herself out of the chair, joints filing their customary complaints, and paused at the doorway. "And Kestrel. Whatever he gave you—whether it's a map or a message or a cursed spoon—it is not yours to carry alone. That's not rule two talking. That's me."

She left before Kestrel could arrange their face into anything, which was a mercy for them both.

* * *

The oldest ledger of the Guild of Impossible Things lived in a box under Odile's bed, which was the safest place in the city, because no thief alive would rob a woman who wore her hair like that. It was bound in hide that no tanner would confess to having supplied, and its first hundred pages predated the Sigil Wars, and Odile had read all of it exactly twice in forty years: once when she inherited the guildmaster's chair, and once more, later, on the worst night of her life, looking for something that wasn't there.

She took it out now and set it on her bed, and sat beside it for a long moment with her hands flat on the cover, the way you'd steady a nervous animal.

Vhalcrest.

Even thinking the word felt like stepping off a stair that turned out not to exist. Her tongue knew the shape of it. Her mother's tongue had known it. But the maps had no room for it, the atlases had burned it in the great sanctioned reprintings, and the Crown's historians—those dear, diligent, frightened men—had been given a generation to unremember it, and had done beautiful work. If you said *Vhalcrest* aloud in any market in the kingdom, you would get blankness, then puzzlement, then—within the hour, depending on the district—a knock at your door.

Unless you were a woman of a certain age with no living heirs and a guild full of contraband, in which case you got visited by a memory instead. Twenty years ago, on a Tuesday, her sister Maren had packed one bag and kissed her cheek and said, *I know where the erased country is, Odile, I found the route in a song, can you imagine, it was in a *lullaby*—* and had walked out through the north gate whistling, and had never come back, and the watch reports listed her under *unresolved*, and the world had proceeded to proceed.

Odile had spent twenty years telling herself that Maren was dead in a ditch, because a ditch was survivable grief. A ditch had a shape. What Odile had never once let herself think—what rose in her now, hot and enormous, behind her sternum, as the smell of scorched paper drifted up from the dead man's coat still hanging in the south storeroom—was that Maren might have made it. Might be *there*. On the far side of whatever border the Crown had drowned in ink.

She opened the ledger to the marked page.

It was not a grand entry. Ledgers abhor grandeur; that was why she loved them. It was dated a hundred and ninety years back, in the crabbed hand of some guildmaster whose name history had also misplaced, and it recorded, in the flat commerce-of-everything cadence of the guild:

*Received, one map, unsellable, being a chart of a country no buyer will acknowledge exists. Seller wept and would not take payment. Filed under V. Note: three subsequent inquiries for same. All sellers wept. Note appended by Guildmaster Corvin: the Crown pays forty crowns per copy recovered and burns them in front of you, which is the only honest transaction in this trade, and I will have no part of it.*

Below that, in a different ink, a later hand—Corvin again, older, shakier:

*Note to whoever keeps this book after me. The country is real. I saw a man from it once, before the Wars, and he had fire in his lantern that needed no oil. They did not erase it because it is dangerous. They erased it because it is true, and truth is the one cargo the Crown cannot tax. Burn this page if they come for you. Better the book than the keeper.*

Odile sat with that for a while. Sunlight crawled across the floorboards. Somewhere below, Kestrel was conducting the morning's business with the low, even voice that made merchants trust them against all sense, and the guild breathed around her the way it had for forty years—settling, sighing, holding its impossible things close.

Then she took the ledger to the hearth, because Corvin had been right about one thing in a century of things: better the book than the keeper. Better, too, the page than the girl. Because Captain Vess or some other grey-coat with clean nails was going to come through that door within the week—Odile had run enough contraband to smell a net being drawn—and grey-coats did not read gently. They read with fire. And if they found *Vhalcrest* written in a hundred-and-ninety-year-old hand in the guild's own book, then rule two would die in this room along with everything else, and Kestrel Okonkwo—who had come to her door at eleven years old with fear worn into them like a seam—would learn what happened to people the Crown decided to read.

She fed the corner of the page to the coals.

It caught reluctantly, the old vellum-flax fighting the flame the way old truths do, and Odile held it with a poker's steadiness and watched the words go. *The country is real.* Curling brown, then black, then gone. *They erased it because it is true.* Ash. *Better the book than the keeper.* Nothing.

Her hands were steady the whole time, which surprised her. Grief, she had found, was excellent for steadiness. It cleared out everything tremulous and left only the load-bearing parts.

When the last flake lifted up the chimney, she spoke to it—quietly, in the direction of the north gate, in the direction of twenty years of Tuesdays.

"If you're there, Maren," she said, "hold on. Something's coming. I don't know yet what it is, but it came through my doors bleeding, and my girl salted the floor for it, and in my experience that's how miracles usually arrive."

The ash went wherever ash goes.

* * *

She spent the rest of the morning being aggressively ordinary, which was the guild's finest art. She argued with a spice merchant about an invoice until he apologized for existing. She paid the glazier. She told Tam that no, they could not keep the "definitely a mermaid tooth" they'd bought at the fish market for two pennies, and then let them keep it, because hope needed exercise like any other muscle.

At noon she called Kestrel up to the office, set out two cups and the bottle she kept for deaths and weddings, and poured with the gravity of a woman administering sacrament.

"We're going to play a game," Odile said. "It's called the story we tell the grey-coats. In this game, a sick man knocked on our door Thursday night seeking shelter, we gave him soup and a blanket because we are soft-hearted fools, and he died of his wounds in the night despite our soup, which was excellent. We buried nothing, we kept nothing, we noticed nothing unusual, because noticing things is above our station. You will deliver this story with the same face you use on the spice merchant. Can you do that face?"

Kestrel wrapped both hands around the cup. For a moment they just looked at it—an old soul in a young face, wearing grief like an unfamiliar coat—and then they lifted their chin, and the face settled into pleasant, harmless blankness, and Odile could have wept at how well they'd learned it. At who had taught them.

"I can do that face," Kestrel said.

"I know you can. That's what worries me." Odile raised her cup. "To the guild of impossible things. May our books balance, may our lies be boring, and may every door in this building hold."

Kestrel touched their cup to hers, and above the mantle, in the silence that followed, the little brass bell over the shop door gave one soft, unmotivated ring—the kind of ring it made when the air pressure shifted, or when a storm-bottle dreamed, or when something vast and patient had finally, after years of searching, fixed its attention on a particular address.

Odile looked at the bell. Then at Kestrel, who had gone very still, listening to something no one else could hear.

Four days, she thought, and drank. Perhaps three.
