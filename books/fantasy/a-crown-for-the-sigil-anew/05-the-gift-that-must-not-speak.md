## 5. The Gift That Must Not Speak

Sleep did not come easily to Kestrel Okonkwo, and on the night after a man died in their storeroom, it did not come at all.

They lay in the narrow room above the guild's counting-house, staring at the ceiling beams, listening to the building settle around them the way old buildings do—creaks and sighs like an arthritic grandmother rearranging herself in a chair. Below, the guild slept. Odile's lamp had gone dark two hours past. The twins who worked the receiving dock had stopped arguing about dice somewhere around midnight. Even the mice in the wainscoting had gone quiet, which was unusual, because the mice of the Guild of Impossible Things were chatterers by nature, and lately they had been chattering about blood.

Kestrel pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes.

*The word.* The dying courier's last word. It had not been meant for them—not exactly—but it had landed in them all the same, the way a thrown stone lands wherever gravity decides, and it was still sitting there behind their ribs like a swallowed coal. A word in no human tongue. A word that the rats had flinched from, that the cart-horse Bramble had turned her great head away from as if it smelled of something too large for a stable.

They knew what it was. Of course they knew. They had known since they were seven years old, sitting on their mother's knee in a kitchen that smelled of onions and tallow, learning the first and only lesson that mattered.

*Some words are doors,* their mother had said. *And you, my heart, are the kind of person doors notice.*

Kestrel rolled onto their side and tried very hard not to be the kind of person doors noticed.

It did not work. It never worked. The gift sat in them like a second heartbeat, patient and insistent, and tonight—of all nights—it wanted to talk.

* * *

Their mother had been a poultry-woman before she was anything else, and even then she had been more than a poultry-woman, though she would have boxed Kestrel's ears for saying so. She kept forty hens behind the tenement on Cobbler's Row, and every one of those hens adored her with the single-minded devotion that hens usually reserve for the invention of the worm.

"They follow you," six-year-old Kestrel had observed one morning, watching a procession of brown hens trail their mother through the yard like a train of small feathered courtiers. "Why do they follow you?"

"Because I feed them."

"You feed me and I don't follow you."

"You're a child, not a hen. Though I begin to wonder." Their mother had crouched down then, taken Kestrel's small face in her flour-dusted hands, and looked at them with an expression Kestrel would spend thirty years learning to read. It was not fear. It was something sadder than fear—the look of a woman measuring a distance between her child and the world, and finding the distance already growing.

"Listen to me, little bird," she had said. "The hens come when I call because I ask them kindly, and asking kindly is a small magic, a kitchen magic, a nothing-magic. But you—" her thumb had traced the line of Kestrel's jaw, gentle as anything, "—you will do more than ask. You will *hear*. And hearing is a door that swings both ways. Do you understand me?"

Six-year-old Kestrel had not understood. Six-year-old Kestrel had nodded anyway, because their mother's voice had gone quiet in the particular way it went quiet when she was frightened, and frightened mothers made children nod.

"The Sigil Wars ended before you were born," she had continued, "but wars don't end, little bird. They just get quieter. The people who won them wrote the rules, and the first rule is this: beasts speak to no one, and no one speaks to beasts. Anyone caught between—that's a heresy, that's a crime, that's a rope or a pyre depending on the mood of the magistrate. So here is what you'll do. When the animals talk to you—and they will, oh, they will—you will smile and say nothing. You will let them think you're simple. Simple children live long lives. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you, Mama."

"And Kestrel—" and here her voice had cracked, just slightly, a hairline fracture in a wall of certainty, "—never answer them where anyone can see your lips move."

That had been the whole of the law, handed down in a chicken yard: *smile, say nothing, keep your lips still.* Kestrel had carried it through childhood like a stone in the shoe—always present, always shaping the way they walked.

* * *

The neighbor boy's name was Tam. He had lived two doors down from the tenement on Cobbler's Row, in a house that always smelled of boiled cabbage, and he had been the closest thing Kestrel had to a friend before Kestrel learned that friends were a risk.

Tam was eleven the summer his gift surfaced, and it surfaced the way gifts do in children—all at once, catastrophically, without any instruction manual. One afternoon he had stood in the middle of the lane screaming at a dog, except he wasn't screaming *at* it; he was screaming *with* it, his voice and the dog's grief tangled together so thoroughly that passersby thought the boy had gone mad, and perhaps in a way he had, because no one had taught him what Kestrel's mother taught Kestrel over onion stew: *smile, say nothing, keep your lips still.*

No one had told Tam that the Inquisitors kept lists.

They came on a Tuesday. Kestrel remembered it was a Tuesday because market day, and their mother had sent them out for a knob of butter, and Kestrel had been standing at the corner of Cobbler's Row with the butter sweating in its cloth when the grey coats came up the street.

There were four of them, walking in step, and they moved through the market crowd the way a knife moves through bread—without hurry, without resistance, everything parting ahead of them. Behind them came the hounds. Not ordinary hounds. These were lean, pale-eyed things on silver chains, and they held their heads low and swept them side to side like lamps searching a dark room.

Kestrel had known, even at nine, what the hounds were for. Everyone knew, in the way everyone knows things they have agreed not to discuss. The hounds didn't smell lies. Lies were safe; lies were everywhere. What the hounds smelled was *listening*—the particular scent, if scent it was, of a mind tuned to another species' frequency. A beast-speaker standing still among a crowd rang like a struck bell, apparently. Or so the Inquisitors believed, and their belief was backed by fire, so belief hardly mattered.

But Kestrel had learned early a trick their mother never named, because their mother never needed it: Kestrel could close the door. Could fold the listening part of themselves down small, small, smaller, until it was a grain of sand in a desert of ordinary thought. It hurt, the way holding your breath hurts. But it could be done, and Kestrel had done it now, standing at the corner with the butter, folding themselves shut while the pale-eyed hounds swept their lamp-heads across the crowd.

The hounds passed within arm's reach of Kestrel. One of them paused. Turned its head. Looked directly at them with eyes like chips of winter sky.

And Kestrel, nine years old, heart hammering, had done the bravest thing of their young life: they had thought, deliberately, loudly, in the direction of the hound, *I am thinking about butter.*

The hound's ears twitched. Its head tilted. And then—it had sneezed, shaken itself, and moved on, because even a hound bred to hunt listeners can be thrown off by a child projecting dairy products at it with the full force of mortal terror.

Kestrel had walked home slowly, butter clutched to their chest, and watched from the safety of their mother's window as the grey coats led Tam out of the cabbage-smelling house by both arms. He wasn't struggling. That was the worst of it. He was looking around at the street, at the faces in the windows, at Kestrel's own face in the glass—though he couldn't have seen Kestrel, couldn't have known—and his expression hadn't been terror. It had been confusion. The confusion of a boy who had only wanted to grieve with a dog and couldn't understand why the world had decided that was a crime.

His mother ran into the street after him. One of the grey coats simply put a hand flat against her sternum and pushed, not hard, almost gently, and she sat down in the dust of the road and stayed there, and the procession walked on, and nobody in the crowd said anything, because the first rule of surviving the Crown's peace was that crowds see nothing, hear nothing, remember nothing.

Tam was eleven. The Inquisitors' wagons, Kestrel learned much later, from a drunk ex-warder who liked to weep into his ale at the Drowned Rat, went to a place called the Quiet House, and the Quiet House did not return children. Whatever they did there to make the listening stop, it stopped permanently, along with other things.

Kestrel's mother had held them that night, hard enough to bruise, and said nothing at all, which said everything.

After that, Kestrel had practiced. Every day. Folding the gift down, closing the door, becoming a grain of sand. They practiced until the folding became reflex, until they could stand in a stable full of chattering animals and hear nothing but the ordinary sounds of stables, until silence was not a rule they followed but a shape they lived inside—a fortress with walls built brick by brick out of everything they had lost.

Grief, Kestrel had discovered, makes excellent mortar.

* * *

Now, thirty years later—or near enough, time being slippery in memory—Kestrel lay awake above the counting-house and realized, with a cold clarity that felt like falling, that the walls of the fortress had cracks in them.

The courier's word sat behind their ribs, glowing.

They had spent a lifetime keeping their lips still, and the world had rewarded them with a warm room, steady work, a guildmaster who pretended not to notice that the ledger margins sometimes contained notes in a script that was suspiciously good at mimicking hoofprints, and a cart-horse who thought Kestrel was the finest creature ever to walk upright. Small mercies. Careful mercies. Mercies paid for daily, in silence.

And now a dying stranger had pressed a map into their hands and spoken a word that no human mouth should know, and the walls Kestrel had built were ringing like a struck bell.

Bramble had heard it too. Down in the stable, when the courier breathed his last, Kestrel had felt the horse go rigid with attention three walls away, had felt her listening the way one listens to distant thunder—already knowing the storm is coming, already counting the seconds.

And the rats. The rats in the storeroom walls had gone silent as graves, and rats are never silent unless something frightens them more than hunger does.

Kestrel sat up in the dark.

Here was the arithmetic they could not escape, the ledger that balanced against nothing: a man had died in their care carrying a country that officially did not exist, and the Crown's hounds were already sweeping the city for traces of him. Captain Vess would come—grey coats and pale-eyed dogs and polite, terrible questions. And when the hounds came within arm's reach of Kestrel Okonkwo, they would need to project thoughts of butter with a steadiness they had not required since they were nine years old, because the courier's word had changed something. Had *opened* something. Kestrel could feel it, the way one feels a tooth that has shifted in its socket—everything slightly wrong, everything slightly loose.

For thirty years, silence had been survival. Smile, say nothing, keep your lips still.

But the map under their floorboard was not silent. It hummed. Faintly, patiently, in a register below hearing, it hummed with the same frequency as the word behind Kestrel's ribs, and Kestrel understood—with the terrible clarity of three o'clock in the morning—that the two of them together, the map and the word, were going to make noise. Eventually. Inevitably. Noise that hounds could smell and grey coats could follow.

The choice, then, was not whether to break the silence. The choice was whether to break it on their own terms or have it broken for them.

Kestrel got up, dressed in the dark, and took the map from beneath the floorboard where they'd hidden it. It lay in their palms, light as ash, humming its low patient note. Outside the window, the city slept, unaware that somewhere in its midst a dead man's errand had found its keeper.

Down in the stable, Bramble lifted her head as Kestrel approached, ears forward, and asked the question horses ask with their whole bodies: *Well?*

"I don't know yet," Kestrel whispered, lips barely moving, an old habit worn smooth as river stone. "But I think the quiet days are over."

Bramble blew out a long breath through her nostrils, which in horse language could mean anything from agreement to profound skepticism, and Kestrel chose to believe it was agreement, because tomorrow the grey coats would come to the market district asking questions, and Kestrel Okonkwo intended to be very, very good at lying.
