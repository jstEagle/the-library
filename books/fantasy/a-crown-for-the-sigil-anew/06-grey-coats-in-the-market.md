## 6. Grey Coats in the Market

The morning the grey coats came to Saltrow Market, Kestrel was haggling over eels.

Not for pleasure — nobody haggles over eels for pleasure, unless they are a very particular sort of person whom Kestrel had met exactly twice in their life and hoped never to meet again. No, this was business. The Guild of Impossible Things owed Mistress Halloway of the eel-barge four silver marks, and Odile's ledger did not forgive debts any more than it forgave crooked handwriting, which Kestrel knew from painful personal experience.

"Three marks," Kestrel said, "and I'll throw in the storm."

Mistress Halloway, a broad woman whose forearms could crack walnuts by reputation alone, squinted at them over her barrel. "That bottled thing you lot keep behind the counter? The one that rattles?"

"The very same. Certified genuine tempest. Caught off the Teeth during the equinox gale. It has opinions about Tuesdays."

"Everything in your shop has opinions about something," Halloway grumbled, but she was already reaching for her scale-weights, which meant she'd been beaten and knew it. Three marks and a bottled storm for four marks' worth of eel. Kestrel smiled the small, modest smile of a person who has just won without appearing to try, which is the only kind of winning the guild approved of. *Be forgettable*, Odile always said. *A memorable merchant is a merchant who gets robbed.*

Kestrel was very good at being forgettable. It was, if they were honest, their second-greatest talent.

Their first was the reason it needed to be.

* * *

Saltrow Market on a market-day morning was a creature with a thousand voices: fishwives crying the dawn catch, a knife-grinder's wheel singing its long bright note, someone two stalls down arguing with a goat about the price of turnips. The goat was winning. Goats usually were. Kestrel moved through it all with their ledger-satchel snug under one arm and their hood up against a drizzle that couldn't decide whether to commit, greeting people by name in the easy way of a person who belonged — a nod to Old Tam at the rope stall, a coin flipped to the pie-boy, a word of sympathy to the flower-seller whose chrysanthemums had frozen overnight into something that looked less like flowers and more like tiny glass sculptures.

"Pretty, though," Kestrel offered.

"They're dead is what they are."

"Dead things can still be pretty. Ask anyone who's ever been to a funeral with an open bar."

The flower-seller laughed despite herself, and Kestrel moved on, collecting debts the way a magpie collects buttons: steadily, without fuss, leaving no trace but a lighter satchel and a trail of mildly annoyed creditors who nonetheless liked them well enough.

It was halfway through the rope stall that Kestrel noticed the dogs had gone quiet.

This deserves explanation. Saltrow Market had eleven dogs, not counting the ones that merely visited, and every one of them had opinions about Kestrel. Not hostile opinions — nothing so dramatic. But dogs always knew. Dogs always gave them *that look*, the tilted-head, ears-up look of an animal trying to work out why the tall quiet ledger-keeper smelled faintly of *something else*, something old and feathered and vast that lived underneath the world. Kestrel had spent years cultivating those dogs. A dried liver strip here, a scratch behind the ear there, until the whole canine population of Saltrow regarded them as a harmless fixture, like a lamppost that occasionally produced bacon.

Now all eleven dogs were silent. Sitting perfectly still. Watching the north end of the market with the flat attention of soldiers awaiting orders.

Kestrel followed their gaze and felt the morning go cold around the edges.

They came through the fish-gate in file, six of them, in coats the grey of old ash and rainwater. Not watchmen — watchmen wore the city's blue and slouched. These walked like men who had never once slouched in their lives. At their head rode a woman on a dun mare, and even before Kestrel saw the sigil stitched at her shoulder — the crowned eye, lidless — they knew what she was.

An Inquisitor. In Saltrow. Asking after a courier, if the whispers already rippling ahead of her were anything to judge by.

"Captain Vess," breathed Old Tam, going pale around the mouth. He was too old to remember the Sigil Wars himself, but he remembered the stories his mother told, and he told them the way you tell stories about floods and plagues. "Crown's hounds. What's the Crown want in *Saltrow*?"

Kestrel didn't answer. Kestrel was doing arithmetic.

A courier had died in their storeroom three nights ago, raving about a country that wasn't on any map. A scorched piece of that country now lay wrapped in oilcloth beneath the loose floorboard under Kestrel's bed, beneath a layer of genuinely boring tax receipts that Kestrel had placed there specifically because no one in history had ever voluntarily disturbed tax receipts. And now the Crown's grey coats were walking the market asking questions.

Coincidence was possible. Kestrel believed in coincidence the way they believed in dragons: theoretically, and with a strong suspicion that anyone claiming to have seen one was lying.

"Tam," they said lightly, "I've just remembered I'm needed elsewhere."

"You're needed here, boy, these ropes won't—"

But Kestrel was already moving, unhurried, hood up, a forgettable figure drifting toward the pie-stall as though pies had suddenly become the most important thing in their life. Behind them, the grey coats fanned out. One took the fish-gate. One drifted toward the rope stalls. Two began working the middle rows, and Kestrel heard the first question asked aloud, mild as milk:

"—a courier, dark-haired, rides a bay mare with a white sock. Might have passed through in the last week. You'd remember the horse."

*You'd remember the horse.* Clever. People forgot faces; horses stuck. Kestrel bought a pie they had no appetite for and ate it anyway, because a person eating a pie was a person with somewhere unimportant to be.

And then the captain turned her horse, and looked directly at them.

* * *

She was younger than Kestrel expected. That was the first unfair thing about her. Stories made Inquisitors out of granite and old leather, grey-faced and greyer-eyed, but Captain Vess was perhaps thirty, with a broad honest face and a small scar through one eyebrow and eyes the color of a winter sky deciding whether to snow. She sat her mare like the horse was furniture she happened to be using. Her gaze went across the market in slow sweeps, methodical, patient, and when it snagged on Kestrel — a hooded figure eating a pie beside a pie stall, the least remarkable sight in creation — it stayed.

One heartbeat. Two.

Then she dismounted, handed her reins to a soldier, and crossed the mud toward the pie stall with the unhurried stride of someone who had never once been told *no* by the world.

"Good morning." Her voice was pleasant. Genuinely pleasant, which was worse than cruelty. Cruelty you could brace against. "Is that the mutton or the mystery?"

"Mutton," Kestrel said. "The mystery's extra."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, but a neighbor of one. "I'll pass. I'm Captain Vess, Crown Inquisitory." She said it the way other people said *good morning*, a fact worn so smooth with use it had stopped feeling like a threat to the person saying it. "We're looking for a missing man. Courier. Dark hair, bay mare, white sock on the near hind."

"Haven't seen him." Easy. Smooth as poured cream. Kestrel had been lying since before they could write, and their lies came out with the same effortless neatness as their ledger columns. "Courier traffic comes through Saltrow twice a week, mostly grain manifests and love letters. Nobody memorable lately. Well — except a fellow last week who tried to ship a live goose as a parcel without declaring it. That was memorable. The goose was memorable. It bit the weighmaster."

Vess listened to all of this with her head slightly tilted, and Kestrel realized, with a slow sinking feeling, that she was listening *well*. Not the bored listening of authority humoring the small folk. Real listening. Cataloguing.

"A goose," she said.

"A heroic goose. It escaped. Last I heard it holds the alley behind the tannery."

"I'll add it to my list." She glanced back at her soldiers, then at the market — the stalls, the crowds, the eleven unnaturally silent dogs. "Busy place, Saltrow."

"Best market in the district. Worst weather. We consider it a fair trade."

"Mm." She looked at Kestrel again, and this time the look had weight to it, the specific weight of a person reading a document and finding one line that doesn't balance. "Your name?"

"Kestrel. I keep accounts for the Guild of Impossible Things, down on Candle Lane." Names freely given, occupations cheerfully stated — the oldest trick. Liars hide; honest-seeming people volunteer. "Relics, curiosities, appraisals. If you're ever in need of something valued, we're very reasonable."

"Inquisitors don't need things valued."

"No? Then what do you do with all the confiscated goods? Give them back?" Kestrel let a beat pass, then smiled apologetically. "Forgive me. Ledger-keeper's humor. Everything's a transaction if you do it long enough."

There it was again, that almost-smile, gone as fast as it came. Vess studied them a moment longer — and then, casually, terribly, she whistled.

Two hounds detached from the rear of the column. They were big animals, mastiff-crossed with something colder, muzzled and grey-coated to match their handlers, and they came at the captain's heel like shadows on a string. Every child in Saltrow knew the Crown hounds. There were songs about them, the kind mothers sang quietly so children would stand still at checkpoints and not run. Runners got the hounds. Everyone knew that.

Kestrel stood very still and did the only thing they could do, which was nothing at all, while every muscle in their body screamed otherwise.

The hounds reached Vess's side. She gestured at Kestrel — a small, idle motion, testing rather than accusing, the gesture of a woman flipping over a stone to see what lived under it.

Both hounds sat down.

They didn't growl. They didn't bristle. They didn't do any of the things Crown hounds famously did to liars and smugglers and, according to the songs, beast-speakers, whose scent supposedly drove them to frothing madness. Instead the nearer hound — a big female with a scarred muzzle — leaned forward and pressed her great blunt head, briefly and unmistakably, against Kestrel's hand.

The market seemed to hold its breath. Kestrel certainly did.

"Well," said Kestrel, because silence was worse, "I like dogs."

"I can see that." Vess's voice had changed. The pleasantness remained, but underneath it something had gone very still and very sharp, like a blade being held perfectly level. She crouched, looked at the hound, looked at Kestrel's hand where it rested on the scarred muzzle. "Odd. Bree here doesn't care for strangers. Took a month to tolerate my own sergeant."

"Dogs know a pie-buyer when they smell one."

"Mm." Vess rose. For a long moment she simply looked at Kestrel, and Kestrel looked back, and between them stretched the whole length of the lie — the storeroom, the dying man, the word in a language only beasts understood, the map breathing softly under a pile of tax receipts — all of it balanced on the tip of a moment like a coin on its edge.

Then a soldier called out from the far row — *"Captain! Tannery hand says he's seen the mare!"* — and the moment tipped, and rolled away, and was gone.

Vess turned. Paused. Glanced back once over her shoulder.

"Enjoy your pie, Kestrel of Candle Lane."

"It's a good pie," Kestrel said, and watched the grey coats move off down the row toward the tannery, toward a goose that did not exist and a mare that might, and felt their heart hammering so hard they were half afraid the hounds would hear it.

* * *

They did not go straight home. Going straight home was what a guilty person did, and Kestrel was innocent, officially, publicly, in every way that could be proven. So they finished their rounds. They collected two marks from the cooper and one from the widow Prentiss and endured twenty minutes of the widow Prentiss's theories about her late husband's ghost, which Kestrel privately suspected was a drafty window but which paid well enough in goodwill not to correct. They nodded to neighbors. They complained about the drizzle. All the while, underneath the pleasant ledger-keeper surface, their mind ran the morning over and over like a tongue finding a cracked tooth.

The hounds hadn't growled.

That was the thing that wouldn't sit still. Crown hounds were trained — bred and broken and trained — to mark certain scents. The songs weren't exaggeration; Kestrel had seen it once, years ago, at a checkpoint outside Marrowford: a drover boy, sixteen maybe, trembling, and the hounds going wild against their leads, and the grey coats closing in, and the boy's mother screaming, and Kestrel's mother gripping their shoulder hard enough to bruise and saying, later, in the dark, *that could be you, that will be you, if you are ever anything less than perfect at being nothing.*

But these hounds had pressed their head into Kestrel's palm like they'd found a friend. Like they'd found—

Like they'd found family.

Kestrel stopped at the corner of Candle Lane, in the shadow of the cooper's awning, and let that thought arrive fully, the way you let yourself look at a wound.

Hounds marked beast-speakers as prey. Unless. Unless the blood they were smelling wasn't the small wild gift at all — the hedge-magic, the animal-whispering that got boys taken at checkpoints. Unless it was something older, upstream of all of it, and the hounds' training simply didn't cover what to do when a dog met a wolf's ghost wearing a person.

The courier's last word surfaced in their memory, unbidden. That syllable in the language-without-language, the one that had come out of his ruined mouth and landed in Kestrel's bones like a struck bell. The rats in the walls had gone silent when he said it. Every rat. Every mouse. As if the whole hidden nation of the guild's walls had stopped to listen.

And now hounds bowed to them in a market square, and an Inquisitor with winter-sky eyes had written their name down — Kestrel had *seen* her do it, a small note, a pencil moving — and the arithmetic that had started cold at the fish-gate finished itself now with a click, like a lock turning.

She hadn't been looking for the courier, not really. Or not only. She'd been walking the market the way a fisherman walks a riverbank, watching the water for the one ripple that doesn't fit. And Kestrel — careful, invisible, perfectly boring Kestrel — had made a ripple the size of a hound's devotion.

"Stupid," they murmured to themselves. "Stupid, stupid—"

From the gutter above, a rat peered down at them. Then another. Within moments there were five of them along the drainpipe, still as carvings, whiskers twitching, all facing Kestrel with the same fixed attention the dogs had shown the grey coats.

Waiting. Listening. As if word had already spread through the walls of Saltrow, faster than any Inquisitor could ride, that something had woken up in the ledger-keeper from Candle Lane — and that the waking had a name, or the beginning of one, and that names, once spoken, could not be stuffed back under floorboards no matter how many tax receipts you piled on top.

Kestrel looked up at the rats, and the rats looked down at Kestrel, and somewhere across the rooftops a crow began to laugh — a dry, knowing, horribly human sound.

"Fine," Kestrel told them all, quietly. "Fine. But if any of you talk to the grey coats, I'm telling everyone you carry fleas."

The crow laughed harder. And Kestrel pulled their hood low and went inside to tell Odile that the morning's collections were complete, and that they needed to talk about the floorboards, and possibly about running.
