## 8. An Offer From the Rebels

Serah Vale had knocked on a great many doors in her life, and she had developed a private taxonomy of the sounds they made.

There was the door that opened too fast — guilt behind it. The door that opened slow and heavy — suspicion. The door that never opened at all but breathed through its seams — fear, doing arithmetic.

The Guild of Impossible Things answered on the fourth knock, which put it somewhere between suspicion and good manners, and the woman who opened it was built like a ship's figurehead carved by someone with strong opinions about ships. Grey hair bound back tight. An apron dusted with ledger chalk. Eyes that performed a full inventory of Serah in under two seconds — boots, coat, sword-hilt, the particular way ex-Inquisitors stood even when they'd stopped being anything — and arrived, visibly, at *trouble*.

"You're not buying," the woman said.

"That depends what you're selling."

"We're not selling."

"Then we'll get along fine," Serah said, "because I'm not buying either. I'm here to make an offer."

The woman did not move out of the doorway so much as she rearranged herself within it, becoming somehow more doorway than person. "The guild doesn't take offers after dark."

"It's barely past dusk."

"The guild," the woman repeated, with the patience of someone explaining weather to a child, "doesn't take offers after dark." And she began to close the door.

Which was when Kestrel Okonkwo appeared behind her shoulder like a cat deciding whether the thing in the garden was worth interrupting a nap for, and said, "Odile. It's raining."

It was, in fact, beginning to rain — a thin, needling drizzle off the harbor, the kind that got into boot-seams and grudges alike. Serah looked up at Kestrel with the expression she'd spent three years perfecting since leaving the Inquisition: honest, tired, faintly amused, and completely unreadable underneath.

"I'll wait in the rain if I have to," Serah said. "But I came a long way to talk to your bookkeeper, and the rain and I both know how to be patient. One of us is better company."

Kestrel and Odile exchanged a look that contained an entire committee meeting — motions tabled, objections raised, one abstention from whatever ghost haunted the storeroom — and then Odile sighed the sigh of guildmasters everywhere, the sigh that was really a tax levied against the world.

"Ten minutes," Odile said. "In the front room. Where I can see you. And the kettle is not for you."

"Naturally not," Serah agreed, and stepped inside.

* * *

The front room of the Guild of Impossible Things was exactly the sort of place Serah had heard about in Inquisitor briefings and never quite believed: shelves of bottled storms labeled by vintage; a taxidermied something-or-other wearing a tiny hat, presumably to signal that it was friendly; a wall of keys for doors that no longer existed, each tagged in careful handwriting. It smelled of lamp oil, old paper, and — beneath it, faintly — horse.

She catalogued all of it without appearing to, because some habits don't leave when the uniform does.

Kestrel sat across the table from her, hands folded, face arranged into the pleasant blankness of a shopkeeper enduring a customer. Up close they were younger than their ledgers suggested, and warier, and there was a small fresh scratch along one wrist that Serah's eyes noted and declined to comment on. Rats, probably. Or the courier. Word traveled fast in certain circles that a man had died in this building four nights ago, and word traveled faster still about what he'd been carrying.

"You're Serah Vale," Kestrel said.

"I'm flattered."

"Don't be. You're also ex-Inquisitorial Order, discharged rather than resigned, which means you left angry or you were pushed. You run cargo on the *Merry Adelia* out of Saltreach, and half of what you carry was legal last year and isn't now." Kestrel tilted their head. "Did I miss anything?"

"My mother's maiden name," Serah said, "and my opinion of the tea here, which is that it hasn't been introduced to the water yet." She let the smile fade by degrees until what remained was just true. "I'm not here on Crown business. That's first, because I know how it sounds coming from me. I'm not here because anyone sent me. I'm here because six weeks ago I carried dispatches for a man named Corren Halloway, and Corren Halloway is dead, and before he died he walked into this guild carrying something the Crown will burn cities to get back."

Kestrel's face did nothing at all. It was genuinely impressive work. But Serah had spent nine years learning to read people who were trained not to be read, and beneath the blankness there was a flicker — not of guilt. Of *weight*. The look of someone carrying something heavy and hoping nobody asks them to set it down where it can be seen.

"I don't know anything about a courier," Kestrel said.

"No," Serah agreed mildly. "I imagine the rats know more than you do."

Something moved in Kestrel's eyes then — quick, involuntary — and Serah filed it away and hated herself a little for how easily the old instruments still played.

"Here's my offer," she went on, before the silence could set like mortar. "The Crown's Inquisitors are in the market district asking questions. Captain Vess, grey coats, hounds that smell wrong. They'll find this guild inside a week, maybe less. When they do, everyone under this roof answers for harboring a dying courier — and for whatever he gave you." She held up a hand before Kestrel could speak. "I can move you out. Tonight, if you want. I have routes the Order doesn't know exist anymore, papers good enough to fool Vess herself, and friends in low places who owe me. All I ask in return is the map."

The lamp flame leaned sideways in a draft from under the door. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked, deliberately or otherwise.

"And if I say there's no map?" Kestrel asked.

"Then I'll believe you meant it," Serah said, "and I'll still offer the protection, because I didn't come here to trade with you. I came here to trade with the truth. Those are different negotiations." She leaned forward. "But let's pretend for one moment that there is a map. A map to Vhalcrest. Do you know what's in Vhalcrest, Kestrel?"

"Vhalcrest isn't anywhere. It's a story drunk sailors tell."

"Vhalcrest is a country the size of Caelmoor with two hundred years of history, and every record of it was burned in a single season by order of the Crown, and every person who remembered it was made to forget, and every person who couldn't forget was—" Serah stopped. Steadied. "Was handled. There are people alive right now, Kestrel, whose names appear in no roll, whose families were signed into erasure, who have been sitting in Crown cells for decades for the crime of remembering a country that officially does not exist. Some of them are Speakers. Some of them just had the bad luck to love someone who was." Her voice stayed level. It took work. "I've seen the transfer orders. I signed some of them. That's why I'm here, and that's the whole of why I'm here."

For a long moment Kestrel simply looked at her, and Serah let herself be looked at, because the offer only worked if it could survive examination.

"Why would you help them?" Kestrel said finally. "Them being us. Strangers. What's in it for Serah Vale?"

"Nothing," Serah said. "That's usually how people know I'm telling the truth. My lies always come with a profit margin."

And Kestrel laughed — short, surprised, quickly strangled — and Serah thought, with a sensation she hadn't planned for and didn't enjoy, *oh no*, because the laugh was warm, and warmth was not something she had budgeted for in this negotiation.

"I'll consider it," Kestrel said, which was a lie, delivered kindly, and they both knew it, and Serah nodded as though she believed it, because that was how civilized people lied to each other.

* * *

Odile was waiting in the corridor with her arms crossed and her face doing an excellent imitation of stone, which fooled neither of them.

"Ten minutes," she said, "became forty."

"Time flies when you're threatening someone's family," Serah said.

"You threatened no one. That's what worries me." Odile walked her to the door at a pace designed to communicate that the visit was over, had been over for some time, and would remain over indefinitely. At the threshold she stopped. Rain hissed on the cobbles beyond. "You were Order," Odile said. Not a question.

"Nine years."

"And now you sell people escape routes."

"Now I sell people *choices*," Serah said. "The routes come free."

Odile studied her with those figurehead eyes, and Serah had the distinct feeling of being entered into a ledger — date, description, valuation pending.

"If you come back," Odile said, "come at noon. With bread. Nobody trusts a visitor who arrives empty-handed and dry."

It was, Serah realized halfway down the steps into the rain, possibly the strangest invitation she had ever received, and she found herself smiling at the wet dark like an idiot.

* * *

She did not go back to the ship. She went up instead, to the widow's walk of the counting-house across the lane, because she had learned long ago that the most important part of any offer was watching what people did immediately after you made it.

Through the rain-blurred window of the guild's front room she saw Kestrel and Odile arguing. She couldn't hear a word of it, which didn't matter; argument has a grammar all its own. Kestrel's hands said *no, absolutely not, we are not getting involved*. Odile's posture said something else entirely. Odile had gone very still in the specific way of a woman listening harder than she wanted anyone to notice — the way Serah herself had gone still, once, in a briefing room in the capital when a superior read aloud the list of names to be erased, and hers had been on it.

*Careful,* Serah told herself. *You're projecting.*

But she kept watching anyway, because Corren Halloway had died pressing a scorched map into somebody's hands, and his last words to her — delirious, half-drowned, three days before the end — had been: *find the bookkeeper, Vale. The quiet one. The beasts already trust them.* She hadn't understood it then. Standing in the rain above the lamplit window, watching Kestrel gesture sharply at nothing while a moth battered itself against the glass *toward* them rather than away, she was beginning to.

Below, the guild door opened a crack. A cart-horse's head emerged from the stable side, ears forward, and Kestrel leaned out into the drizzle and murmured something to it — something that made the big animal blow out its lips and bob its head like a gossip agreeing to keep a secret.

Serah exhaled slowly.

*Oh, Corren,* she thought. *You impossible, brave, stupid man. You found one.*

She turned up her collar and went down into the rain to write her report to no one, which was to say to decide what she would do next — knowing, with the certainty of a woman who had already broken one oath and found the air breathable, that whatever Odile had heard tonight through that closed door, the guildmaster had listened longer than caution allowed.

And that meant the offer was no longer Serah's alone to refuse.
