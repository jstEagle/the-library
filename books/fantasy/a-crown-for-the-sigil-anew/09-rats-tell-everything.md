## 9. Rats Tell Everything

The thing nobody tells you about keeping secrets for a living is that you end up knowing everyone else's too.

Kestrel Okonkwo had learned this lesson early and thoroughly, the way one learns anything in the Guild of Impossible Things: by accident, at an inconvenient hour, from an unlikely teacher. In their case, a rat named Duchess who had once lived behind the wall of a tax collector's office and had opinions about arithmetic.

Duchess was dead now — rats live fast and die faster, another truth of the trade — but her successors carried on the tradition. The guild's walls were honeycombed with them, and the walls of half of Copperlane besides. Rats heard everything. Rats remembered most of it. And rats, unlike people, could be paid in cheese rinds and old bread crusts and considered themselves handsomely compensated.

Which is why, three days after the stranger died in the storeroom with a word like a struck bell on his lips and a map that wasn't paper hidden beneath Kestrel's floorboard, Kestrel went down into the cellar before dawn to hold court.

* * *

The cellar of the Guild of Impossible Things smelled of tallow, dust, and the particular sour sweetness of a bottled storm that had been sitting on a shelf since before Odile bought the building. Kestrel lit no lamp. They didn't need one; they'd counted the steps down a thousand times, and besides, the dark made it easier to listen.

They sat on an upturned crate — their crate, gnawed at every corner into something resembling a throne — and waited.

It didn't take long. They never did, when there was news worth trading.

The first arrival was a small brown female with a torn left ear whom Kestrel had privately named Marguerite, on account of her habit of grooming mid-conversation like some duchess at a scandalous salon. She came down the wall in a series of confident little drops and settled on Kestrel's boot, whiskers twitching.

"Good morning to you too," Kestrel murmured. "I take it the market district was interesting last night."

Marguerite washed her face.

"I'll take that as a yes." Kestrel reached into their coat pocket and produced the heel of yesterday's loaf, still faintly warm where they'd kept it against their ribs. A bribe, yes, but Kestrel had long ago stopped pretending the arrangement was purely transactional. There were worse things in the world than being beloved by rats. "Go on, then. Who's asking questions?"

Marguerite took her share with delicate greed, and then she *told* them — not in words, never in words, but in the press-and-flow of images and impressions that lived behind the gift like fish beneath ice. Grey coats. Boots on cobbles. The smell of hound and oiled leather. A woman with a voice like a ledger line: even, precise, leaving nothing extra.

Kestrel closed their eyes and let it come.

The market. Yesterday afternoon, though Marguerite had been under the fishmonger's stall and her view had been mostly of ankles. The grey coats moving stall to stall. The hounds — two of them, big black things with breath like wet stone — snuffling along the baseboards of Copperlane while their handler asked questions in that flat, patient voice. *A courier. Tall. Brown hair going grey at the temples. Carries a leather satchel with a broken buckle.* Had anyone seen him? Had anyone taken him in? The Crown thanked the loyal citizens of Copperlane for their vigilance.

And then, threaded through the images like a wire through wax: the hounds passing close to a certain shopfront with a sign shaped like a question mark, and pausing. Not growling. Just — *pausing*. Noses working. Ears forward.

Kestrel's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

"They stopped," they said aloud, to the cellar, to Marguerite. "Outside the guild."

Marguerite nibbled bread and offered, unhelpfully, an image of a beetle she'd eaten earlier. Rats prioritized. It was one of the things Kestrel loved about them.

But the pause outside the door stayed in the thread of her memory, bright and unmistakable as a coin dropped in mud. The tall grey-coated woman — Vess, the neighbors' gossip had already supplied the name, Captain Vess, mind your tongue around her — had looked up at the guild's crooked sign. She'd stood there perhaps ten heartbeats. Then she'd written something in a little book and moved on.

"Ten heartbeats isn't much," Kestrel told the darkness. Their voice sounded steadier than they felt, which was, after all, its whole job. "People look at the sign all the time. It's a remarkable sign. Odile paid good money for that sign."

The sign in question depicted a question mark being interrogated by a smaller, more suspicious question mark. Odile maintained it discouraged frivolous customers. Kestrel had never had the heart to point out that it mostly encouraged them.

Still. Ten heartbeats.

* * *

By full light, Kestrel had assembled the morning's intelligence in the back room, and it was worse than the cellar session had suggested.

The mice of Copperlane were less reliable correspondents than the rats — flighty, easily distracted, prone to reporting crumbs rather than conspiracies — but they had numbers, and numbers mattered. Three separate mice reported grey coats at the harbor gates. Two more reported the same at the north road tollhouse, which meant the roads were being watched, which meant leaving was already complicated. And a sparrow — sparrows gossiped shamelessly and expected payment in suet, the extortionists — had seen the courier's description nailed to the watch-post boards, fresh ink, with a reward figure beneath it that made Kestrel whistle low.

Two hundred crowns. For a dead man's description.

"The Crown doesn't pay two hundred crowns for a lost satchel," Kestrel said.

Odile looked up from the great ledger, her reading spectacles pushed into her iron-grey hair, her expression arranged in its usual architecture of mild disapproval over deep calculation. Around her, the morning guild went about its business with the studied obliviousness of people who had survived thirty years of impossible commerce by knowing exactly when not to listen. Tam the appraiser was arguing with a client about whether a jar of captured thunder had gone flat. Young Pip was dusting relics nobody would ever buy. The hearth crackled. Everything was ordinary.

Everything was ordinary, and Kestrel knew better than to trust it.

"No," Odile agreed quietly. "The Crown pays two hundred crowns for a message it didn't receive. Which raises the question of where the message went." Her eyes, pale as winter water, rested on Kestrel with a weight that was almost gentle. Almost. Odile's gentleness was always load-bearing. "You wouldn't know anything about that, would you, love?"

"I know he died in our storeroom," Kestrel said, which was true, and carefully not everything that was true. "I know the watch took the body. I know I didn't see any satchel."

Every lie Kestrel had ever told Odile sat in their chest like swallowed stones. This one was the largest yet, and it had only been three days.

"Mm," said Odile, and returned to her ledger, which was how she said *I am letting this go for now, but I am writing it down in the part of my head that has its own ledger*. "Well then. Mind the stock room today. And Kestrel—" she didn't look up, "—if a message should turn up somewhere inconvenient, I trust you'd bring it to me before it brought trouble to my door."

"Of course," said Kestrel, and hated how smoothly it came out, and went to mind the stock room.

* * *

The stock room was where the guild kept the things too dangerous or too embarrassing to display: the bottled storm (still sulking), a crate of left-handed candlesticks, the taxidermied wyvern that Pip swore blinked when nobody watched, and the trapdoor to the cellars. Kestrel spent the morning doing inventory badly, because their mind kept returning to the same image: Marguerite's memory of grey boots pausing outside the guild door, ten heartbeats, a pen scratching in a little book.

What did she write?

He needed to know what she wrote. And there was exactly one way to find out, and it involved the boldest thing Kestrel had asked of their informants in six years of quiet, careful, invisible life.

At noon, Kestrel took their lunch — bread, hard cheese, and a knuckle of ham they'd traded two hours of ledger work for — to the alley behind the watch-house on Tanner's Row, where the market district's grey coats took their meals. They sat on a barrel in the shadow of a drainage arch, ate slowly, and listened.

The rats of Tanner's Row were fat and cynical and utterly unafraid of anything except the watch-cat, a monstrous orange tom named Brutus whose reign of terror had unified rodent politics across four streets. Kestrel had bought Brutus's tolerance years ago with dried fish; the cat now regarded rodents as colleagues. It remained one of Kestrel's proudest diplomatic achievements.

"Well?" they murmured, scattering crumbs. "Who's been inside today?"

The answer came back in fragments, stitched together from a dozen small witnesses. The watch-house kitchen: grease, shouting, terrible coffee. The duty room above it: boots, maps, the flat-voiced woman — Vess — standing over a table. And on the table:

Kestrel held very still.

On the table, a list. Paper, official cream, Crown watermark. Names in a column, some crossed out, some circled. And the list itself was old — the edges soft, the ink of the header faded — but the names at the bottom were new. Fresh ink. Days old at most.

Kestrel let the rats show him what they'd seen, reading upside-down glimpses through the eyes of creatures who cared nothing for letters but noticed everything about ink. Names. A hedge-witch from Weft Street. A fowler whose hawks obeyed him too well. A child, gods help them, a child from the lamplighters' quarter, circled twice.

And at the bottom, the newest entry of all, the ink still sharp enough that the youngest rat on the wall could taste it:

**Okonkwo, K. — beast-speaker (unconfirmed) — surveil.**

The bread turned to chalk in Kestrel's mouth.

*Unconfirmed*, said the list. *Surveil*. Not arrested. Not yet hunted. Watched. Which meant Vess suspected but didn't know — the hounds' silence had been a question, not an answer, and Kestrel's fluent lies at the market had bought doubt instead of belief. But lists like that didn't stay at *surveil*. Lists like that moved. Kestrel had seen a boy dragged away over exactly such a list, fifteen years ago, and had never forgotten the sound his mother made.

"How long," Kestrel whispered to the rats, to no one, to the cold space behind their ribs where safety used to live. "How long does she give it?"

The answer came in the indifferent language of vermin: the rhythm of patrols doubling. New faces in grey arriving on the morning coach. Vess asking the harbormaster about ships north. A week, perhaps. Perhaps less. The ink was days old and the net was closing at the speed of bureaucracy, which was slow right up until it wasn't.

Kestrel sat on the barrel in the alley behind the watch-house and finished their lunch, because a person sitting calmly eating a knuckle of ham attracted no attention whatsoever, and because if they stopped eating they might be sick. Above them, the drainage arch dripped. Somewhere inside, Vess's voice gave orders in that even ledger-line tone, and Kestrel thought, with a strange detached clarity: *she wrote my name with the same hand she writes everything with. Steady. No hurry. My life is a column in her book.*

Then they folded up the remaining ham, tipped the rats their wages, and went home to decide what to burn.

* * *

"You're doing the face," said Serah Vale, from the doorway of the stock room.

Kestrel jumped approximately a foot, knocked a left-handed candlestick off its shelf, caught it with the reflexes of six years' practice, and turned with as much dignity as the situation allowed — which was none. "What face?"

"The one where you've decided something terrible and are pretending it's inventory." Serah leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, her ex-Inquisitor's stillness making even lounging look like surveillance. She'd been haunting the guild for two days now, ostensibly waiting on a reply to her offer, actually — Kestrel suspected — refusing to leave until she understood why a dying courier had walked forty blocks to die in this particular storeroom. "Also, your count of the candlesticks is wrong. You've written 'twelve' next to eleven sticks."

"That one's a decoration. It doesn't count."

"It's identical to the others."

"It has *aspirations*, Serah."

Something flickered across the smuggler's face that on anyone else would have been called a smile. On her it was more of a controlled release of one. Then it faded, because Serah's eyes were good — too good — and whatever she saw in Kestrel's posture drained the humor out of the room.

"It's bad," she said. Not a question.

Kestrel set down the ledger. Looked at the trapdoor to the cellars. Looked at the floorboard beneath which a map that rearranged itself like a sleeping animal lay wrapped in oilcloth, humming its distant dragonsong into their bones whenever they let themselves touch it.

"There's a list," Kestrel said. "An old one. Beast-speakers, suspected ones, going back years. And my name's at the bottom. Fresh ink. *Surveil.*"

Serah didn't gasp or curse. That was the worst part — she simply went very still, the way soldiers do, and Kestrel understood in one cold flash that she'd known such lists existed. Had probably helped maintain them, once, before whatever had happened to make her an *ex*-Inquisitor.

"A week," Kestrel went on, because if they stopped talking the fear would get out and start running the conversation. "Maybe less. Patrols doubled this morning. She's watching the roads and the harbor. When she confirms — and she will confirm, she's thorough, I've watched her work — they won't come with questions. They'll come with the wagon." Their voice did something humiliating on the last word, a small crack, and they hated it, and pressed on. "So I need to move the— the stock. The item. Out of the building. Tonight."

"And go where?" Serah asked softly. "North road's watched. Harbor's watched. You have a week and nowhere to run."

"I have a map."

"The map." Serah straightened off the doorframe. "The one you're still pretending doesn't exist."

"I'm not pretending anything. I'm maintaining plausible deniability, which is different, and it's a discipline." Kestrel rubbed their eyes. Behind them, muffled through two floors, the guild went on being the guild: Pip laughing at his own joke, Tam still haggling over the storm, the hearth-song someone was humming — the old tune Odile sang at closing time, the one about the fox who out-traded the moon. Six years of warmth. One week left of it, maybe.

"Here's what I can't stop thinking about," Kestrel said. "That list is old, Serah. Older than me. Someone's been collecting names of Speakers for years — decades — and mostly just... holding them. Why now? Why does the ink suddenly go fresh? Why does Vess come to *my* market with hounds and a reward poster?" They looked up. "The courier. He's why. Whatever he was carrying, whoever he was carrying it *for* — the Crown thinks it reached Copperlane. They think it reached *someone*. And they're right."

Serah was quiet a moment. "You could give it to them," she said. "Whatever the map leads to. Hand it over, tell them the story, maybe they cross your name off."

"Do you believe that?"

"No," Serah said immediately. "I've seen what happens after the wagon. I told you that already."

"Right." Kestrel laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and bright as spun glass. "So. One week, one impossible map, one name on a list, and absolutely nowhere safe to stand." They picked up the candlestick and put it back on the shelf with exaggerated care. "You know the truly ridiculous part? Yesterday I was worried about the quarterly accounts."

"Yesterday you were a different person," Serah said. "That happens fast. Faster than you'd think."

Below them, faintly, the cellar rats began to stir — a ripple of movement in the walls, urgent, purposeful, the particular texture of *news coming, news coming now*. Kestrel felt it through the soles of their feet before Marguerite's successor even arrived, whisker-first, breathless with borrowed images.

Grey coats again. But not patrolling this time. Assembling. At the corner of Quill Street and Tanner's Row, out of sight of the guild but not far — a dozen of them, and the black hounds, and a wagon with a covered bed.

Not for tonight. Not yet. But positioned.

Kestrel looked at Serah, and Serah looked back, and between them passed the whole unsaid arithmetic of the next seven days.

"Get Odile," Kestrel said. "Tell her we need to talk about the cellars. All of them. Right now."

Somewhere beneath the floor, wrapped in oilcloth, the map hummed — patient, ancient, and utterly indifferent to the fact that the quiet invisible life it had cost Kestrel was already burning down around them like a wick.
