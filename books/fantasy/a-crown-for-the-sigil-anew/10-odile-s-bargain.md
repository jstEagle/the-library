## 10. Odile's Bargain

Odile Marchetti had run the Guild of Impossible Things for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned that the true measure of a person was not how they behaved at their best but how they behaved over an unpaid invoice. Kings could be charming. Heroes could be generous. But show Odile a man confronted with a ledger entry for *one (1) bottled storm, medium, slightly leaky* and she would tell you everything about his soul.

So it was with some expertise that she watched Kestrel Okonkwo scrub the same spot on the appraisal table for the fourth time that evening, and thought: *ah. There it is. Guilt.*

The guild had closed an hour past. The storm in its bottle—sold that morning to a nervous young widow who wanted to hear thunder because her late husband had snored like it—sat empty on its shelf, and the shop below was quiet except for the tick of the great orrery and the small industrious sounds of Kestrel pretending very hard that nothing in the world had changed.

Odile let them scrub for another minute. Then she said, "The table's been clean for ten minutes, love. You're going to wear through the varnish, and then I'll have to bill you, and then you'll be guilty about *two* things."

Kestrel started badly. The rag went into the air, was caught, was folded with terrible precision, set down as though it were evidence. "I'm not—" they began.

"You are," Odile said, "and worse, you've been bad at hiding it all week, which frightens me more than the guilt itself. You once hid a live griffin hatchling in the coal cellar for nine days without so much as a feather out of place. Now you can't even lie properly about scrubbing." She jerked her chin toward the back stairs. "Upstairs. The good kettle. If we're going to have a reckoning, we'll have it with tea."

"Odile—"

"Upstairs," she repeated, and because she was the guildmaster, and because there are tones that brook no argument, Kestrel went.

* * *

The upstairs room of the Guild of Impossible Things was Odile's kingdom entire: a low-beamed room smelling of lamp oil, dried lavender, and forty years of pipe smoke soaked so thoroughly into the rafters that even the fire couldn't have gotten it out. Maps papered the walls—not the pretty ones sold downstairs, but working maps, corrected in her own hand, annotated with the kind of knowledge cartographers charged fortunes to unlearn. A map of the Ambercoast with three bays that officially did not exist. A trade route through the Thornwood marked with a note reading *trolls reasonable if fed first.*

Kestrel sat at the round table with their hands wrapped around an untouched cup of tea, looking, Odile thought, exactly as they had at eleven years old, when they'd arrived on her doorstep thin as a winter branch with a stolen coat and a talent they didn't understand and eyes that watched every door.

"I know about the stranger," Odile said.

Kestrel's knuckles whitened around the cup. "He died, Odile. In our storeroom. I hid him from the watch. I know what that means, I know what I've put on you and the guild, and if you want me gone I'll go tonight, quietly, I won't even take my wages—"

"Stop." Odile held up a hand. "Breathe. You've rehearsed that speech; I can hear the rehearsal in it. Sit back down."

Kestrel sat.

"First," Odile said, "the dead man in the storeroom is the fourth dead man this guild has hidden from the watch since you came to us, and the second this year, and I have never once regretted it, so spare me the bit where you confess like I'm a magistrate. Second—" she fixed them with the look she usually reserved for merchants who tried to pass off brass as gold, "—I know he gave you something. Because I watched you carry it up to your room wrapped in your spare shirt, and I have known you since you were small enough to sleep in the appraisals drawer, and you have never in your life wrapped your laundry that carefully."

Silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below, old Bramble shifted in his stall, and Odile felt—as she always faintly did, standing near Kestrel when the guild was quiet—that the building itself was listening.

"It's a map," Kestrel said finally, which was, Odile noted, only about a third of the truth, offered the way one offers the smallest coin in one's purse. "It leads somewhere called Vhalcrest."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Odile kept her face still. Thirty-one years of negotiation had taught her that. But inside her chest something old turned over, the way a stone turns over in a riverbed, showing the pale underside that hasn't seen light in twenty years.

"Say it again," she said quietly.

"Vhalcrest? He said it before he died. It's not on any of the atlases, I checked—all of them, even the ones you keep locked in the—"

"I know it isn't on any atlas," Odile said, perhaps more sharply than she meant. She took off her spectacles, polished them on her apron, put them back on. Bought herself a moment. "Kestrel. What do you know about why I founded this guild?"

Kestrel blinked. "You—you always say it's because impossible things need someone to vouch for them. That the world is full of true things nobody will certify, so somebody has to keep honest books for dishonest reality."

"That's the speech I give at weddings." Odile leaned back in her chair. The pipe on its shelf behind her caught the lamplight, the one carved with the little bird she'd never explained to anyone. "Here is the other version.

"Twenty-three years ago—I was younger than you are now—I had a sister. Wren. Two years older than me, cleverer than me by half, and completely incapable of leaving a mystery alone. She worked for a cartographer in the capital until she got sacked for asking too many questions about a coastline that appeared on one old chart and none of the others." Odile's voice stayed level. It had to; she'd practiced level for two decades. "She found references. Old ones. A country that had been in every atlas in the world, and then wasn't—in any of them, all at once, and no one remembered it but a handful of cranks and madwomen. Everyone else looked at the empty space on the map and saw nothing strange about it at all. That was the part that frightened people who noticed. Not the absence. How comfortable everyone was with it."

"Wren went looking for it," Kestrel said softly. It wasn't a question.

"Wren went looking for it." Odile turned her teacup a quarter-turn, a pointless gesture, something to do with her hands. "She wrote to me three times from the road. Cheerful letters. Terrible poetry. The last one came from a river town north of here that doesn't appear on maps anymore either—funny how that happens—and then nothing. Twenty years, Kestrel. I hired trackers. I bribed priests. I wrote to the Crown archives under three false names and got back three polite letters telling me no such person as Wren Marchetti existed in their records, which I found more damning than any refusal." She looked up. "And when the trackers came back empty, and the grief had finished eating everything it could reach, I took the money left to me and opened a shop where things that had fallen out of the world's memory could be kept safe, catalogued, and believed in. Bottled storms. Deposed seals. Rumors with receipts." Her mouth twisted. "The Guild of Impossible Things is a twenty-year-long argument with the Crown, love. I just never told anyone what we were arguing about."

Kestrel had gone very still—the particular stillness they got when the beasts were talking to them, Odile realized, or when they were fighting not to let it show. Their eyes were wet.

"The courier," they said. "He said Vhalcrest was real. That it's still *there*, Odile. Burning. Something's burning to keep it alive, and it's failing, and the map—" They stopped. Swallowed. "The map wants to be followed. I know how that sounds."

"It sounds like my sister's letters," Odile said. "*The country is calling me, Ollie. Isn't that the most wonderful thing you've ever heard?*" She let the old words sit between them a moment. Then she stood, because sitting was unbearable, and went to the third floorboard from the hearth—the loose one, the one Kestrel had never known was loose despite being the guild's finest lockpick, because some secrets a guildmaster keeps even from family—and pried it up.

From the cavity beneath she lifted an iron box, unlocked it with a key she wore next to her heart and never spoke of, and carried it to the table.

"What I'm about to do," she said, "you are going to argue with. I'd think less of you if you didn't. But you're going to lose, so save your breath for the packing."

* * *

She laid it out on the table the way she laid out any transaction: precisely, item by item, because love without logistics was just sentiment, and sentiment had never once found her sister.

"Item one." Coin. Good coin, the heavy old-mint kind that crossed borders without questions—sixty crowns in mixed gold and silver, plus a letter of credit drawn on a name that didn't exist yet but would, by morning, thanks to a forger in Saltrow who owed Odile a favor dating back to a certain incident involving a bishop's mule. "That keeps two people fed, stabled, bribed, and moderately drunk for four months. Longer if you're careful, shorter if you're proud. Don't be proud."

"Two people?" Kestrel said. "Odile, I'm not taking anyone into—"

"Item two." She laid a ledger page beside the coin—a fair copy, fresh-inked in her own hand, showing a fictitious sale route: bottled relics bound for the northern river towns, dated forward three weeks, with toll receipts to match. "When Vess comes—and she'll come within days, Kestrel, the hounds wouldn't growl at you, do you understand what that means, the *hounds knew*—this tells her you ran north by the river road. It's plausible. It's detailed. And it leads her through the Thornwood, which will slow her down beautifully, because the trolls there are only reasonable if *she* thinks to feed them, and she won't."

Kestrel stared at the false route. "You're lying to an Inquisitor. For me. That's—it's not just ruin, Odile, it's the *rope.*"

"It's a fair copy of a route I invented while you were upstairs wrapping your laundry," Odile said. "And the rope only matters if she proves it, and she won't, because by the time she's done thrashing the Thornwood you'll be beyond her reach or beyond everyone's, and I am an old woman who misremembers things. Grief does that." She met Kestrel's eyes. "Item three."

It was small. A flat tin locket, dented, green with age, on a leather cord. Inside—she opened it before handing it over—two curls of hair, one chestnut, one grey, and a scrap of paper gone soft as cloth with handling, bearing a line of Wren's terrible poetry.

"She carried mine," Odile said. "Seems only right you carry hers. If you find her—or whatever there is to find—you bring it back. If you find Vhalcrest and it's half of what that dying fool said, you bring word of that instead. And if you find nothing, come home anyway, because the guild needs someone who can tell a forged griffin egg from a true one, and I'm not training anybody else. Do you understand the terms?"

"This isn't a bargain," Kestrel said, voice cracking. "This is you giving me everything and pretending it's a trade."

"Yes," Odile agreed. "That's how the best bargains work. Nobody writes them down." She pushed the locket across the table. "Take it."

Kestrel took it with both hands, the way they'd taken the courier's map, and Odile watched them press it briefly to their sternum, and had to look at the fire for a moment.

"There's more," she said, when she trusted her voice. "Not item four. Just—this." She sat down again, heavily, and for once let herself look as tired as she was. "Twenty years ago I would have gone after her myself. I nearly did. What stopped me was a rumor that whoever erased Vhalcrest didn't just delete a country—they hunted down anyone who remembered it, one by one, politely, permanently. And I chose to stay and remember quietly instead of loudly and briefly." Her jaw tightened. "It was the right choice. I built something worth defending. I raised a stubborn, brilliant, *impossible* child who scrubs clean tables out of guilt. But I want you to know, before you go, that running is not cowardice and staying was not courage. They were just the two halves of the same failure, and I've had twenty years to make my peace with mine, and you don't have to make peace with anything tonight. You just have to pack."

"I could stay," Kestrel whispered. "Fight. We could hide the map, we could—"

"And wait for Vess to find it, or the rebels to steal it, or Ashvael-knows-who to burn the guild down around it? Love, listen to me." Odile reached across and took their hand—callused, ink-stained, trembling slightly, twenty-six years old and carrying the weight of a country. "Some things can't be defended from a counter. Some doors only open from far away. Your stranger died pressing that thing into *your* hands, out of every pair in the city. I don't believe in destiny—bad accounting, no receipts—but I believe in evidence, and the evidence says this is yours to carry. So carry it. And—" her voice betrayed her, just slightly, just at the end, "—come back. That's the whole price, Kestrel. Everything I've given you tonight, the coin and the lies and my sister's bones' worth of hope—the entire bill comes due in one payment. Come back."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then Kestrel came around the table and hugged her, fierce and brief and bone-crushing, the way they'd hugged her at fourteen after the affair of the griffin hatchling, and Odile held on and let herself shake exactly twice before stopping.

"Thank you," Kestrel said into her shoulder.

"Don't thank me, settle your accounts," Odile said thickly. "You still owe me for the varnish."

* * *

They left before dawn, of course. Odile heard the cellar door at the black hour, heard Bramble's hooves on the cobbles—slow, steady, the mare Kestrel had been whispering to for years carrying them north like she'd been promised extra oats and meant to collect—and stood at the upstairs window with her pipe unlit, watching the dark swallow the road.

Then she went downstairs, lit the lamps, and opened the oldest ledger to the page she'd burned a week prior, and began, in her best book-hand, to write it anew:

*Entry the first. One (1) country, presumed lost, now claimed found. Value: incalculable. Collateral: one apprentice, one map, one sister's locket, and every coin I have left. Terms: return of goods or return of bearer. Either will do.*

*Witnessed: O. Marchetti, guildmaster, who remembers.*

She blotted the page, sanded it, and closed the book.

Outside, far to the north, the sky had begun — very faintly, in a way she would swear later she had imagined — to glow.
