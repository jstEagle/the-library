## 11. Burning the Guild Behind Them

The night the world ended—which is how Kestrel would come to think of it, though at the time it felt mostly like a Tuesday gone badly wrong—Kestrel was in the counting room doing what they did best: making impossible arithmetic behave.

The ledgers were balanced to the copper. The bottled storm from Chapter Nine of the acquisition logs—Kestrel thought of their life in chapters now, a habit picked up from the guild's resident failed poet—sat in its lead-lined case on the shelf, grumbling thunderously every few hours like an old man who'd lost his argument with the weather and couldn't let it go.

"Quiet," Kestrel told it, without looking up. "You'll be sold by month's end and then you can rumble all you like."

The storm rumbled. Kestrel chose to interpret this as agreement.

It had been six days since the rats brought news of the list. Six days since Kestrel had learned their name sat freshly inked on Captain Vess's roll of suspected beast-speakers, wedged between a suspected rat-charmer who turned out to be innocent and a suspected crow-speaker who turned out to be extremely guilty. Six days of Odile moving through the guild with a particular set to her shoulders that Kestrel recognized from years of reading her the way one reads weather: *storm coming, batten everything.*

They had a plan. Odile had made it herself, walking Kestrel through it twice with the patience of a woman teaching a child to tie knots: if the grey coats came, Kestrel would go down through the wine cellar, along the old smuggler's tunnel under Tallow Street, and out through the cooper's yard where a horse would be waiting. The map would go with them sewn into the lining of their coat. Everything else—all the ledgers, all the impossible inventory, all twenty-two years of Kestrel's life—would stay exactly where it was, because things that stayed put looked innocent, and innocence was the guild's whole business.

"You'll come too," Kestrel had said, the second time through.

"When there's a reason," Odile had said, which was not a yes, and Kestrel had been careful not to notice that.

So: the counting room, the balanced books, the grumbling storm. Kestrel had just finished entering the day's small mercies—a widow's roof patched for the price of one true rumor, a runaway apprentice returned unharmed for the price of an apology—when the cart-horse, Bramble, spoke into their mind from the stable across the yard, in the way horses do: not words exactly, but a picture and a feeling, the way you might remember a bad dream.

*Grey coats. Many. Coming up Candlemaker's Row. Fast.*

Kestrel's pen kept writing for three more words before their hand understood what their heart already knew. Then they stood, knocked the stool over, and did not bother to pick it up.

"Odile," they said, loud enough to carry through the floorboards. "Bramble says—"

"I heard her the first time, dear." Odile appeared in the doorway already dressed, already shod, her silver hair pinned as neatly as if she were expecting company rather than catastrophe. Behind her came Pell, the guild's factotum, a round gentle man who was holding the good cheese, which under the circumstances said everything about how frightened he was. "Cellars. Now."

"The tunnel—"

"Is your business. Go." Odile took Kestrel's face in both hands, briefly, firmly, the way she used to when they were nine and about to lie to a tax assessor. "The map?"

"Sewn in my coat. Where it's always been."

"Good. Then the only thing they can take from you tonight is time, so stop spending it on me." She pushed them toward the back stairs, and Kestrel went, because twenty-two years of obedience has a momentum all its own.

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of the front door being broken.

* * *

The guild had rules about raids, written down in the oldest ledger in Odile's careful hand, and rule one was *don't watch.* Watching got you seen; getting seen got you caught; getting caught got everyone else caught. So Kestrel went down the back stairs two at a time, through the kitchen where the cook's boy Tammy was standing frozen over a pot of tomorrow's porridge, and shoved him toward the cellar door ahead of them.

"Smoke holes," Kestrel said. "All of them. Go."

Tammy went. He was fifteen and sensible, and Kestrel loved him the way you love people whose entire job description is eating breakfast near you without asking questions.

The cellar was cold and smelled of the wine Odile watered down and resold and the wine she didn't because some customers deserved honesty. Kestrel hauled aside the rack against the far wall—it moved easier than it should have, oiled recently, prepared recently, and Kestrel felt another one of those not-noticing feelings about Odile and filed it away—and pulled open the trapdoor to the tunnel.

Cold air came up out of the dark, smelling of river and old stone. Above, muffled through floors and ceilings, came voices. Boots. The particular cadence of an Inquisitorial announcement being read aloud, which always sounded like someone reciting a grocery list for a funeral.

Kestrel put one leg through the trapdoor.

Then stopped.

Because the thing about the plan—the thing Kestrel had noticed and declined to notice—was that it had one exit and two people who knew the route, and Odile was upstairs, and upstairs was filling up with grey coats, and Odile was fifty-nine years old and walked with a stick on damp mornings and had never once in her life run from anything, which was a virtue right up until the moment it wasn't.

From above came a crash, and Pell's voice raised in protest—"That is a *licensed* relic, sir, that is *bottled weather*, you'll let it *out*—" and then, horribly, the sound of glass.

Thunder filled the guild from attic to cellar.

Kestrel stood in the dark with one foot in escape and heard the storm they had appraised just that morning go shrieking through the halls above, and thought, with a clarity that felt almost holy: *they will blame that on us forever, and it isn't even the worst thing that will happen tonight.*

Then Odile's voice came down through the floorboards, pitched to carry, pitched the way she pitched it when she wanted the neighbors to hear and testify later:

"This is a licensed house! We keep honest books! You may search them page by page, Captain, but if you want them burned you'll have to burn me with them!"

And beneath those brave words, in the pause after, Kestrel—who spoke beast and bird and the language of rats in walls—heard something no Inquisitor could have heard, because Odile had learned it from her mother the way Kestrel had learned their own silence: three sharp knocks on the floor joist.

*Go now. Don't argue. I love you.*

Kestrel went.

* * *

The tunnel under Tallow Street was dug, according to guild legend, by smugglers, improved by counterfeiters, and finished by the guild itself during a tax dispute everyone had agreed to stop discussing. It ran forty yards under the street and came up in the cooper's yard through the floor of an abandoned smokehouse. Kestrel knew its every turn in the dark, had memorized it at eleven years old as part of an education that also included forgery detection and the proper way to bribe a harbormaster, and they counted the turns now the way you count breaths, or heartbeats, or the seconds between lightning and thunder.

Except the thunder didn't stop.

It should have been minutes by now—the search, the shouting, the performance of procedure. Instead the air coming down the tunnel grew warm, then hot, then acrid with a smell Kestrel knew from candle-making and feared from stories: pitch. Tar. Old dry timber going up.

And underneath it, faintly, impossibly, singing.

Not dragonsong. Not the hum in the bones that the map gave off on bad nights. This was human singing, ragged and defiant, a dozen voices or more, and it took Kestrel until the third verse to recognize it because they had never once heard it sung at full volume: the hearth-song. The guild's song. Sung on midwinter and on naming days and very quietly on the nights when somebody's luck had run out, the song about the door that opens for the honest and stays shut to the thief—

*She's burning it,* Kestrel realized, halfway up the smokehouse ladder, lungs full of smoke-flavored grief. *She's burning it herself. That's the diversion. That's the bargain. A fire big enough to bring every bucket in the district running, so the Inquisitors can't seal the streets, so nobody looks in a cooper's yard, so—*

Bramble stood saddled in the dark yard, patient as a saint, and behind her a second horse Kestrel didn't recognize, loaded and ready, held by a boy Kestrel did recognize: Tammy again, who should have been home, who had apparently decided that following instructions applied to everything except the instruction to leave.

"She said to give you this." He thrust out a wrapped bundle. "And she said—" His voice cracked, fifteen years old and trying to be a man about it. "She said the accounts are settled. She said you'd know what that means."

Kestrel knew. Twenty-two years ago a starving child had been carried through the guild door with nothing, and Odile had entered them in the ledger as a debt owed to the world, payable on demand. *The accounts are settled.* Paid in full. Released.

It was the cruelest kind thing anyone had ever done to them.

"The north road?" Kestrel managed.

"She said never the north road. She said the north road is what they'll watch. She said—" Tammy squinted, reciting. "'Tell them the ledger route, and tell them I'm sorry about the sister.'"

Kestrel stared at him. The fire over the rooftops painted his face orange. *The ledger route* meant nothing, and *the sister* meant less—Odile never talked about her family, except once, drunk on festival wine, a sentence about a girl who chased maps the way other girls chased soldiers—and there was no time, the sky was full of sparks and the bells of the district were beginning to ring fire-alarm, which meant minutes, not hours.

"Well?" Tammy said. "Aren't you going?"

"Yes," Kestrel said, and swung up into the saddle, because the alternative was standing in a yard crying where a child could see, and Villalobos help them, they had inherited Odile's spine along with everything else.

They rode south, away from the glow, away from the singing, which had stopped now—or perhaps Kestrel had simply ridden out of earshot, and of the two possibilities they chose the one that let them breathe.

* * *

An hour out of the city, on a farm track with the lights of the capital smudged behind them like a wound gone septic, Kestrel stopped at a stream to water the horses and unwrapped Odile's bundle with hands that shook more than they wanted anyone to know.

Inside: coin, real coin, the old minting before the Crown put faces on money. A travel pass in a name that wasn't Kestrel's but fit them like a coat. And a single page, torn cleanly from a ledger—the oldest ledger, Kestrel knew the paper, knew the watermark—covered in Odile's precise hand.

It was not an account. It was a letter, dated twenty years ago, addressed to no one.

*Sister mine [it began]. If you are reading this then either you finally came home, or someone else is chasing your map, in which case I am sorry twice over—for your loss and theirs. Vhalcrest is real. I know because you proved it, and because the Crown paid us a fortune to forget you proved it, and because I kept one page out of the fire anyway, because I am a sentimental old woman and sentiment is the only crime they haven't outlawed. If whoever holds this page is chasing you now: the fire there burns lies. Remember that when you meet what waits at the end of her map. And forgive me, both of you, for what I am about to do to keep this page safe.*

Below that, a route. Not the north road. A smuggler's chain of waystations running south and then east, through country Kestrel had never had cause to visit, toward a blank space on every map they had ever appraised.

At the bottom, added recently, the ink barely browned: *The accounts are settled, child. Now go be somebody's debt worth owing.*

Kestrel sat by the stream for a long moment, doing sums. Behind them, the city glowed—not just the guild now; the fire had jumped, or been helped, and half of Candlemaker's Row was alight, and somewhere in that light was a woman on a stick who had chosen to stand in her own doorway and be arrested for arson rather than let grey coats follow one child out of the dark.

Grief arrived, huge and wordless. Kestrel let it sit beside them, because Odile had taught them that debts unpaid poison the debtor, and this one—this one they intended to pay.

"Right," Kestrel said aloud, to the horses, to the dark, to the map sewn against their ribs humming its low dragon-note like a second heartbeat. "South, then. The ledger route."

Bramble flicked an ear. Somewhere across the stream a nightbird started up, and Kestrel—outlawed gift or no, hunted name or no—listened to what it had to say.

*Roads clear,* the bird reported. *For now. But riders on the north road, many, carrying nets. And one rider alone on the southern track, waiting near the stone bridge, wearing no colors at all.*

Grey coats behind. A stranger ahead. Kestrel put the letter inside their coat next to the map, next to their heart, mounted up, and rode south toward whatever was waiting at the bridge—because Odile had taught them the arithmetic of debts, and the first payment was due.
