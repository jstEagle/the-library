## 12. Road of Hanged Signs

The north road smelled of rain and cut wood, and Kestrel could not stop counting the stumps.

They had left the city before dawn, when the smoke from the guild was still a grey bruise on the southern sky and the watch was too busy hauling buckets to check travelers. Odile's false ledger route pointed them north like a finger bone: three days to Millbrook, then the river road, then—according to a map that rearranged itself when no one was looking—the drowned borderlands, and beyond them, a country that officially did not exist.

Kestrel walked because walking was invisible. Horses drew eyes. Coaches drew eyes. A person in a hooded cloak with a satchel and a tired shuffle drew exactly as many eyes as a fencepost, which was to say none, and Kestrel had spent twenty-six years perfecting the art of being a fencepost.

The stumps, though. The stumps kept catching their eye.

The first one was at a crossroads outside Harrow Fen, where the road split toward the coast and the hills. A signpost stood there, honest and oak-grown, pointing the usual ways: HARROW FEN, 2 MILES. COAST ROAD, 9. And where a third arm should have pointed north, there was only a sawed stub, pale and fresh as a wound, weeping sap in the rain.

Someone had cut it off recently. Someone had climbed a ladder or stood on a cart bed and sawn through a foot of solid oak because of the word that had been carved into it, and then—Kestrel crouched to check—someone had pried the severed arm out of the mud where it had been thrown, and carried it away. Burned, probably. Burned the way Odile had burned that page of the ledger, the way the Crown burned anything that remembered wrong.

*VHALCREST, 300 MILES*, the stump's ghost said anyway. Kestrel could almost read the absence.

"You're staring at a stick," Kestrel told themselves, and stood up, and walked on.

* * *

The second stump was worse, because it wasn't alone.

The village of Twosprings—named, presumably, for the springs that burbled cheerfully on either side of its single street—had a signpost at its well, and the signpost had been *thorough*. Not sawn. Hacked, with an axe, by someone in a hurry or someone in a fury, and then the whole north arm had been nailed flat to the post, face-down, like a corpse laid out for burial. Rain had swollen the wood so the nails had popped half out. Kestrel could see the edge of a letter—a V, maybe, or a Y—curling up from underneath like something trying to climb out of a grave.

An old woman was drawing water. She watched Kestrel with the flat, unhurried assessment of someone who had watched a great many travelers and been impressed by none of them.

"Lost?" she asked.

"Considering my options," Kestrel said, which was true in a way that felt safe.

"Hmph." The bucket came up dripping. "If you're considering the north road, don't. Nothing up there. Nothing at all." She said it with the particular firmness of a recitation, the way children recite the Sigil Litany or the names of the Nine Just Kings. *Nothing up there. Nothing at all.* It had the rhythm of something taught.

"That's convenient," Kestrel said. "I do my best walking toward nothing."

The old woman's mouth twitched, almost against her will. "You're a strange one."

"I'm a ledger-keeper," Kestrel said. "Strangeness is a professional hazard." They flipped a copper to her—payment for the water they hadn't drunk—and asked, casually, terribly casually, "What was on the sign? Before someone took an axe to it?"

The twitch died. The old woman's eyes went to the north arm, face-down and nail-loose, and something moved behind them that Kestrel recognized, because they saw it in mirrors: the look of a person carefully not knowing a thing.

"Nothing," she said. "It said nothing. It always said nothing." And she carried her bucket inside and shut her door, which was its own kind of answer.

Kestrel stood in the drizzle a moment longer. Around the well, the village went about its evening: a dog barking at a cart, a boy chasing a hoop, lamplight kindling yellow in windows. A perfectly ordinary village. Except that every house on the north side of the street had its shutters turned to face away from the north road, as if the whole village had arranged itself, over years, into a posture of not-looking.

Even the sparrows in the eaves were quiet on that side.

Kestrel's gift stirred, the way it always did around silence—the sense of a held breath, of small minds keeping small secrets. They didn't press. Pressing was how neighbors' sons got taken by grey coats. They touched their cap to the old woman's closed door and walked on, north, into the not-looking.

* * *

They camped that night in a hay barn that smelled of mice and honest labor, and took out the map by candle-stub, because they were apparently the sort of person now who did reckless things in barns.

The map lay quiet in their palms, scorched at its edges, the courier's blood long since rubbed to a brown shadow in one corner. In the candlelight the ink moved—not obviously, never obviously, but the coastline Kestrel was sure had been on the western edge yesterday had drifted a finger-width east, and a river that hadn't existed before now threaded down from the hills like a vein surfacing under skin. The country in the map's center—Vhalcrest, drawn in a hand that pressed hard enough to groove the paper—seemed to breathe.

When Kestrel touched it, the hum started in their sternum. Dragonsong, or the memory of dragonsong, a note too low for ears and too large for bones. It had frightened them the first time. It frightened them less now, which frightened them more, if that made any sense. It was becoming *familiar*. Like a hearth-song. Like home.

"Stop that," Kestrel whispered to the map. "You're a document. Documents don't hum."

The map hummed.

Kestrel put it away and lay back in the hay and listened to the mice in the walls, who were arguing about a crust of bread with the passionate precision of guild appraisers, and found the argument so comforting that they slept better than they had any right to.

* * *

The crow found them the next morning, three miles past Twosprings, where the road climbed out of the fen country and the stumps got stranger.

This one hadn't been sawn. This one had been *replaced*. Where the old sign had pointed north, someone had nailed up a new board, and the new board read: *NORTH ROAD — QUARRY — NO THROUGH TRAFFIC.* Official-looking. Guild of Roads letterhead, even, or a very good forgery of it. But the board was nailed over a stump that was too short for its arm, and the nails were too bright, and the whole arrangement had the desperate energy of a lie told by someone who had never practiced.

Kestrel was squinting at it, doing the ledger-math of forgery (ink too new, lettering too even, and the Guild of Roads hadn't used that seal since the tariff reforms, honestly, did the Crown employ *children*), when the crow landed on the post above their head with a thump and a rattle of feathers.

"Caw," said the crow, meaningfully.

"Good morning," Kestrel said, before they could stop themselves. Talking to animals in public was the first rule broken; talking to animals *aloud* was the second; and here they were, breaking both on an open road, because the last two days had apparently dissolved whatever discipline they had left.

The crow cocked its head. It was a big one, glossy-black with a scar across its beak and the bright, acquisitive eye of a creature that kept its own ledgers. It hopped down the post, closer. Closer. It stopped a hand's breadth from Kestrel's face and made a sound that was not a caw. It was a low, rolling croon, almost a purr, and it carried—Kestrel felt it before they understood it—a shape of meaning, the way the courier's dying word had carried a shape.

*North,* the croon said, in the wordless grammar of birds. *North, and left at the dead oak, and do not take the quarry road, the quarry road is watched.*

Kestrel went very still. "You can *speak*?"

The crow made a sound that, in any language, was a scoff. *Speak. Everyone speaks. The question is whether anyone listens.* It hopped in a tight circle, feathers ruffled with what Kestrel could only describe as professional indignation. *Two days I've watched you. You talk to sparrows. You apologize to mice. You walked past my cousin in Twosprings without so much as a nod. Rude.*

"I—apologize to mice?"

*Constantly. It's embarrassing.*

Kestrel looked up and down the empty road. The rain had thinned to mist. There was no one to see, and the grey coats were—somewhere else, for now, though "for now" had a way of shrinking. And the truth was, the crow was right. They had spent their whole life listening in whispers, catching fragments through walls, pretending not to understand. Never once, not since their mother died, had they simply stood in the open and *talked*.

It felt like unclenching a fist they hadn't known they'd made.

"You're right," Kestrel said. "That was rude. My name is Kestrel."

*Falcon-name,* the crow said, with grudging approval. *Small hunter. Adequate. I am called by the sound of a bell over a market stall, but your throat can't make it, so you may call me Bell.*

"Bell," Kestrel said solemnly, and the crow—Bell—ruffled its feathers in a way that was unmistakably preening.

Then Bell hopped onto the false sign, tapped it twice with a scarred beak, and fixed Kestrel with that bright ledger-keeper's eye. *Now. You want the hidden country. The one under the sawn-off words.*

Kestrel's heart performed a small, disciplined panic. "I want the quarry road. For business. Quarry business."

*Caw,* said Bell, which was clearly crow for *liar*, and not an unkind liar at that. *Everyone wants the hidden country eventually. They saw off the signs, but the birds remember. The birds always remember.* It tilted its head. *I can take you as far as the drowned lands. Three days, if you walk like a person. Two, if you walk like a falcon.*

"What do you want for it?"

And there it was: the gleam. Bell sidled along the signpost, one step, two, and stopped directly in front of Kestrel's satchel, and looked at it, and looked at Kestrel, and looked at it again, with the subtlety of a falling anvil.

*Shiny,* Bell said.

"Of course it's shiny."

*The button,* Bell specified. *The brass one, second pocket, the one with the ship stamped on it. I have a collection. There is a gap in the collection where that button should be. The collection is incomplete and it keeps me awake at night.*

Kestrel thought of Odile, who haggled over everything and paid over odds for anything that mattered. They thought of the guild's oldest rule, chalked over the storeroom door: *EVERY DEBT IS A DOOR.* They thought—briefly, painfully—of a burning roofline against a southern sky.

They took out the button. It was a good button. It had come off a smuggler's coat in their second year at the guild, and Odile had sewn it onto Kestrel's satchel strap as a joke about anchoring them to the trade, and Kestrel had carried it through six years of appraisals and near-misses and quiet, contented, invisible days.

They held it out on their open palm.

Bell looked at it. Looked at Kestrel. And then, instead of snatching it, the crow did something that undid Kestrel more than any dragonsong: it bowed. A real bow, head dipped, one wing half-spread, the formal courtesy of one ledger-keeper acknowledging another's payment.

*Debt witnessed,* Bell said. *Debt paid. Directions follow, and my word on them, and my word is iron.* It took the button—gently, delicately, like a priest taking an offering—and then it lifted off the signpost and circled once overhead, black against the grey.

*North,* it said, and the word rang in Kestrel's bones like the map's hum, like the courier's dying syllable, like something inherited waking up. *Left at the dead oak. The quarry road is watched by men in grey, so we go the shepherd's way, over the ridge. Keep up, falcon-name. I fly, you walk, and I am not a patient bell.*

* * *

They walked, and Kestrel listened, and listening turned out to be a kind of navigation all its own.

Bell called out the road ahead in croons and rattles: *cart coming, hide; dog ahead, friendly, but the dog's man is not; stream crossing, the stones are slick, trust the stones, they have been there longer than your country.* And Kestrel found that when they stopped straining—stopped *trying* to understand the way a scholar tries, with the thinking mind—the meanings simply arrived, the way the meanings of a mother's tone arrive in a child before any words do. The gift wasn't a trick. It wasn't even, really, a gift. It was a relationship. It had been waiting all this time for Kestrel to stop apologizing to mice from behind walls and simply stand in the open and *talk*.

By midday they had passed the dead oak—lightning-split, huge, its dead arms full of other crows who watched them pass in silence that was somehow respectful—and turned onto the shepherd's way, a track that wound up over the ridge through gorse and sheep-cropped grass. From the ridge top, Kestrel looked back once, south, toward the fen country and everything burned and left behind. The mist had swallowed it. There was nothing to see. Nothing at all.

They looked north instead.

The land fell away in long green folds toward a grey shimmer on the horizon that might have been sea, or might have been the drowned borderlands, or might have been the heat-haze of a country the world had agreed to forget. Somewhere out there, a dragon was dying. Somewhere out there, Odile's sister had walked twenty years ago and never come back. Somewhere out there was a door that opened from both sides, and Kestrel was one side of it, walking toward the other with a brass button poorer and a crow for a guide.

*You've gone quiet,* Bell observed, landing on a gorse bush. *Quiet is for hiding. You're not hiding anymore.*

"I'm deciding whether to be afraid," Kestrel said. "The fear has been very reliable. I'd hate to lose it before I know what replaces it."

Bell considered this with a tilt of the scarred head. *What replaces it,* the crow said at last, *is worse. It's hope. Hope is a debt you take on with no idea what the payment will be.* It launched off the gorse, circled, and headed north. *Come on, falcon-name. We're burning daylight, and I know for a fact you're the sort who hates unpaid debts.*

Kestrel laughed—actually laughed, out loud, alone on a ridge in the middle of nowhere, which was possibly the most reckless thing they'd done yet—and followed.

They were two miles along the ridge, Bell riding the wind above them like a small black banner, when the crow stopped laughing with them and went rigid in the air.

*Falcon-name,* Bell said, and all the humor had drained out of the croon. *Down. Get down. There's a hawk on the shepherd's way ahead, and it isn't a hawk, and under it on the road there are men in grey, and one of them is holding something.*

"Holding what?"

*Paper,* said Bell. *A list. And they're asking the shepherds about a ledger-keeper.*
