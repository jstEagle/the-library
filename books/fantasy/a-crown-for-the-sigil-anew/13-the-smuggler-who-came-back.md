## 13. The Smuggler Who Came Back

Serah Vale had been waiting three days in the ditch outside Millbrook, and she had opinions about ditches.

They were damp. They smelled of pig and older sins. The mud had found its way into places mud had no business being, and she was fairly sure a family of voles had filed a claim on her left boot. But the ditch had one virtue: it sat exactly where the north road bent around the alder copse, out of sight of the tollhouse, and anyone traveling north from the city without papers would come down that bend at a walk, tired and careless.

She'd bet her last decent knife it would be Kestrel Okonkwo.

The intelligence had cost her two favors and a bottle of plum brandy, which in Serah's experience was the going rate for anything true. A tinker who supplied the guild had mentioned, in the roundabout way tinkers mention things, that the Guild of Impossible Things had burned on a Tuesday — a clean fire, too clean, the kind Inquisitors wrote receipts for — and that Odile Voss had been taken alive, which meant someone had run, and that someone had gone north.

North was where the map pointed. Serah knew because she'd spent four years carrying things to and from a country that wasn't there, and every road she'd ever memorized ended at the same drowned borderlands. She also knew because Tam had died pressing that map into somebody's hands, and Tam did not waste his last breath on people who couldn't use it.

So: the ditch. Day three. Rain coming in off the hills like it held a grudge.

She heard them before she saw them — not footsteps, Kestrel walked quiet as an apology, but the cart-horse they'd apparently acquired, because of course they had. Kestrel Okonkwo could not walk past an animal without adopting it. She'd watched them do it once with a one-eyed gull outside the guild's loading door, holding a full conversation in shrugs and herring scraps while pretending to inventory crates.

The horse came around the bend first, ears swiveling. Then Kestrel, hood up, leading it, looking exactly like what they were trying to look like: nobody. A person made of ledger-dust and gray wool, forgettable as Tuesday.

Except the horse stopped dead when it reached the copse, turned its enormous head, and looked directly into Serah's ditch.

"Traitor," Serah muttered.

Kestrel froze. Their hand went under their cloak — toward nothing, she'd have wagered; Kestrel carried knives the way most people carried regrets, theoretically — and Serah decided the ambush portion of the evening had gone on long enough.

"Don't," she said, standing up out of the mud in one motion, hands open and empty. "It's me. It's Vale. If you stab me, I'll be very embarrassed, and my ghost will haunt you specifically."

* * *

To her mild surprise, Kestrel didn't stab her. They stood very still instead, rain dripping off their hood, and studied her with the flat assessing look she remembered from the guild's back room — the look of someone appraising a relic of dubious provenance. An ex-Inquisitor, presumably, rated somewhere between cursed mirror and haunted locket.

"You've been in that ditch how long?" Kestrel asked.

"Three days."

"Why?"

"Because you're predictable."

"I'm famously unpredictable. Ask anyone at the guild."

"The guild is ash, Okonkwo." She said it gently, or as close to gently as her voice came, which wasn't very. "I'm sorry. I know what it was to you."

Something moved behind their eyes and was filed away, unopened. Ledger-keepers, Serah thought. They put grief in columns and balanced it later.

"They took Odile," Kestrel said. "Alive."

"I know."

"And you know because—"

"Because I still drink with people who used to be mine, and some of them hate the Crown more than they hate me." She shrugged. "It's a small market, but it exists."

The horse chose this moment to nose Serah's shoulder, thoroughly, checking her pockets. She let it. There was a dried apple in her coat from a farmwife outside Harrow Fen, and the horse extracted it with the delicacy of a pickpocket.

"Her name is Bess," Kestrel said. "She doesn't like most people."

"She likes apple people. We're a trustworthy demographic." Serah wiped her hand on her cloak, which accomplished nothing, and jerked her chin northward. "You're making good time, but you're going to die at the Salt Stair if you keep this route, and I'd rather you didn't, so let's talk before the argument starts. Because it's going to start. I can hear it winding up in you like a clock."

"I don't trust you."

"No," Serah agreed. "You shouldn't. Not yet. Walk anyway. My knees are done with the ditch."

* * *

They walked. Bess clopped along between them like a chaperone who approved of neither party, and the rain settled into a fine persistent drizzle, the kind that got inside decisions.

Serah gave Kestrel the shape of it in pieces, watching their face sidelong. That there were patrols on the north road now, real ones, not toll-collectors playing soldier — grey coats moving in pairs, hounds on loan from the Inquisitor kennels, working a grid pattern that meant someone upstairs believed the courier's map had survived him. That the grid's western edge lay across the Salt Stair, the only dry crossing over the ravine for twenty miles. That Kestrel, walking alone with a cart-horse and no papers, was a lantern looking for a moth.

"You could go around," Kestrel said. "West, through the fen."

"The fen takes nine days and eats horses. Also there's something in the fen." She said it lightly. Kestrel's head came around anyway.

"What kind of something?"

"The kind the border stones used to mark. You'll meet it eventually if the map's real. Better to meet it rested." She let that sit, then added, because honesty was a currency she was trying to rebuild her account in: "I've crossed twice. Both times it let us pass. I don't know why. I try not to ask questions of things that big."

Kestrel was quiet for a long stretch of road. When they spoke again, their voice had the careful evenness of someone reading figures aloud. "In the storeroom. Before he died. Tam said a word I didn't understand, and the rats understood it, and the horse outside understood it, and I understood it, and I have never told another living soul that, so understand what it costs me to say it to you."

"I know what it costs," Serah said. "I signed papers about people like you for six years."

Kestrel stopped walking. Bess stopped with them, martyred.

"There it is," Serah said. "The clock striking. Go on. Ask."

"Why are you here, Vale? Truly. Not the smuggler's answer, not the brandy-and-favors answer. Why does an ex-Inquisitor spend three days in a ditch for a stranger with a stolen map?"

"Because he wasn't your stranger." It came out of her rougher than she'd planned. She kept walking so she wouldn't have to do it facing them. "Tam Wren was my partner. Four years. We ran the Vhalcrest line together — supplies in, letters out, people sometimes, when we could manage it. He taught me which border stones sang and which ones just echoed. I taught him how to forge a Crown seal well enough to fool a customs man who'd invented half his own seals." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "He was the best liar I ever met, and he lied like a man building something, you know? Every falsehood load-bearing. And then the reorganization came, and the new commissioner wanted the erased countries erased harder, and the Vhalcrest line went from tolerated to treasonous overnight, and Tam wouldn't stop running it. So they turned on him. And I—" 

She stopped. The rain filled the pause.

"I signed the order that put his name on the watch-list," she said. "Not willingly. Under threat, with a knife at considerations I won't bore you with. But my signature is on it, in my handwriting, and I've read enough confessions to know that 'under duress' is a coin that spends badly. He ran anyway. I helped him run, after — fed him the patrol rotations, got him out of the city. And then he went back in, because there were prisoners, because there's always prisoners, and the Crown caught him at the tollhouse on Corver Street, and everything he was carrying should have gone into an evidence vault and been forgotten." She turned and looked at Kestrel straight on, rain running down her scarred jaw. "Instead he spent his dying breath giving it to you. So either he saw something in you worth a country, or he was delirious, and I've decided to bet on the first one, because the second one means I'm standing in the rain for nothing."

Kestrel had gone very still, the way they'd gone still in the guild's doorway the night Tam bled out on the storeroom floor. Serah wondered, not for the first time, what the beasts heard in a silence like that.

"He talked about you," Kestrel said finally. "At the end. Not by name. He said *tell her the ledgers balance*. I thought he was raving."

The words went through Serah like a splinter finding old wood. She laughed, once, wetly. "That's — yes. That's a thing he'd say. We kept double books, him and me. One for the Crown, one for the truth. He'd say it every time we closed a run safe." She scrubbed a hand down her face. "Gods. *Tell her the ledgers balance.*"

Bess leaned her great head over Kestrel's shoulder and breathed warm horse-breath onto Serah's arm, which was either sympathy or a demand for more apples.

"So," Serah said, when she trusted her voice again. "Here's my offer, and I'll make it plain because you're a ledger-keeper and you'll want it in columns. I'm not asking for the map. Burn it in front of me if you want; I've got the roads in my skull and half of them in my handwriting. What I'm asking is to come along. I know the crossings. I know which villages will sell bread to strangers and which will sell strangers. I know what Inquisitor grids look like from the inside, because I built three of them, and Captain Vess is running mine — the Corver grid, the one I drew on a slate six years ago, and she hasn't improved it, because the Crown doesn't promote imagination." She paused. "And I want to finish what Tam started. That's the whole of it. Mutual damage, mutual direction. You don't have to trust me. You just have to find me useful until trust catches up, if it ever does."

Kestrel looked at her for a long time. Behind them the rain moved across the hills in gray curtains, and somewhere far off a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You saved me from a patrol tonight," Kestrel said slowly. "Except you haven't yet. There's no patrol."

"Not tonight." Serah tipped her head back toward the bend in the road, where the alders stood black against the sky. "But there's a picket posted at the tollhouse ahead — two men and a hound, rotated at moonrise, sloppy on the changeover. If we stand here talking much longer, we walk right into their lantern light, and you get to experience the ambush live." She smiled, thin and crooked. "Or we cut through the copse, cross the stream at the mill weir, and let them guard an empty road. Your call, Speaker. You're the one holding the impossible thing."

Kestrel's eyes widened fractionally. "*How* did you—"

"Vale," Serah said. "Ex-Inquisitor. We notice things; it was the one part of the job I never hated." She was already moving toward the trees, Bess's lead looped over her arm. "Also your horse told me."

"My horse did *not*—"

"She's been angling her ears at the tollhouse for ten minutes. Animals gossip terribly. You'd know." Serah glanced back, and let the smile soften into something truer. "Come on, Okonkwo. The ledgers balance. Let's go make them true."

For a heartbeat Kestrel didn't move — weighing her, the way they weighed everything, entry against entry, risk against risk. Then they pulled their hood down, letting the rain hit their face like a decision, and followed her into the dark of the copse.

Behind them, at the bend of the road, a lantern swung into view, and a hound began to bark at nothing at all.
