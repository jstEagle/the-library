## 14. Two Liars, One Fire

The fire was Kestrel's idea. The honesty was not planned by anyone.

They had walked since the grey hour before dawn, following the crow's directions through hills that grew stonier and less certain of themselves, and by the time the light went amber and then bruise-colored, Serah's limp had become a pronounced editorial comment on the day's events. Kestrel had watched her favor the left leg for three hours without saying anything, which was its own kind of lie, and finally Serah had stopped at a hollow between two granite shoulders and said, "We're camping here, and you're going to pretend that was your idea, and I'm going to let you."

"That was my idea," Kestrel said.

"Thank you," said Serah, and sat down with the grace of a felled tree.

Kestrel gathered wood while Serah saw to the practicalities — checking the road behind them with the flat, unhurried attention of a woman who had once been paid to notice things, then unrolling two bedrolls that smelled of river-witch and old wool. The fire, when Kestrel coaxed it up, was small. Deliberately small. A fire you could cup in your hands and lie about.

"There's a hollow behind that rise," Serah said, nodding north. "If a patrol comes up the road, we've got maybe forty seconds of warning. I'll take the first watch."

"You're limping."

"I'm *resting strategically*."

"You were limping since the ford."

"I was limping since the ford," Serah agreed, with the serenity of the incorrigible, "because I took a blade through the thigh at the Salt Stair, and flesh, unlike pride, takes time. First watch is mine."

Kestrel opened their mouth to argue and then closed it, because arguing with Serah Vale was like arguing with a well-made lock: technically possible, deeply unwise, and likely to leave you feeling foolish. They sat instead, and fed a twig to the fire, and listened to the evening arrange itself around them — a wood pigeon somewhere, the small industrious sounds of beetles, the wind combing through gorse with a sound like a whispered secret being passed down a long line of people who all promised to keep it.

It was the wind that did it, later. Or maybe the fire. Or maybe just the particular quality of dark that comes when you are two liars and one flame and the nearest town is a day behind you and the nearest enemy is an unknown number of hours ahead.

* * *

Serah took first watch as promised, sitting with her back to a boulder and her sword across her knees, and Kestrel lay in the bedroll and stared at the stars and did not sleep, which surprised no one. Around midnight Serah woke them — not with a touch, which would have gotten her a broken nose in the old days, but by saying their name once, quietly, the way you'd set a cup down on a table.

"Your turn."

Kestrel sat up. The fire had burned to a red heart with a skin of ash, and Serah looked like something carved out of the same material: tired, patient, glowing faintly at the edges.

"Nothing on the road," Serah said. "Owl went over twice. A fox screamed near the ford, but that was fox business, not people business."

"You can tell the difference?"

"Fox screaming has a certain *honesty* to it." Serah settled into her bedroll with a grunt. "People screaming is worse. It has words."

Kestrel took the watch. This was the arrangement, and the arrangement was comfortable, and comfortable things were the most dangerous things Kestrel knew, because comfort made you forget you were running.

The hours passed. The fire needed feeding. Kestrel fed it.

And somewhere in the smallest hour, with Serah's breathing gone slow and even and the stars wheeling with their ancient indifference, Kestrel heard the field mouse.

It lived under the gorse to the east, and it was having a bad night — a shadow had crossed the moon in a way it didn't like, and it was telling the darkness about it in the small urgent grammar of mice, which is mostly fear and inventory: *wing, wing, wing-shape, wrong, wrong, big, cold, mine, mine, my seeds, my life.* Kestrel had been listening without listening, the way you listen to rain, and then the mouse's alarm sharpened and Kestrel felt it land in their chest like a pebble dropped down a well.

*Wing. Big. Circling.*

Kestrel went very still.

There was nothing in the sky. There was nothing in the sky, and the mouse was right, and both of these things were true, and Kestrel sat with the contradiction for a long moment, hands around their knees, watching the dark where a dark thing circled a darker sky, and thought: *I am going to tell her.*

The thought arrived the way most catastrophic thoughts arrive: calmly, reasonably, at an unreasonable hour, dressed in sensible clothing. *I am going to tell her, because she saved my life at the ford, because she took a blade at the Stair that was meant for me, because tomorrow or the day after we will be in a place where my secret gets us both killed and she deserves to know what's in the luggage.*

Also, less nobly: Kestrel was tired. Tired the way you get tired of carrying something heavy in the same arm for twenty years. The arm doesn't stop working. It just starts to *dream* about putting the thing down.

The mouse's shadow-thing passed over, or didn't, and the field went back to its small honest noises, and Kestrel fed the fire one more stick, and waited for dawn, and rehearsed.

* * *

Serah woke at first light looking like she'd slept exactly as much as she had. She built the fire up, produced hard bread and harder cheese from her pack, split both with a knife that had seen honest work, and handed Kestrel the slightly larger portion without comment, which was the closest thing to tenderness Serah Vale permitted herself before noon.

They ate. The cheese was a war crime. The bread was only a misdemeanor.

"Serah," Kestrel said.

"Mm."

"I need to tell you something, and I need you to not do the thing where your face goes pleasant."

Serah's face, which had been pleasant, went carefully neutral instead. Progress, of a kind. "Go on."

Kestrel looked at the fire. It seemed wrong to say it to her face, somehow — too direct, like a blade with no hilt. The fire was a better audience. The fire already knew; fire had known since the guild burned, probably. Fire kept no secrets and told no tales, which made it the ideal confessor.

"You remember the hounds," Kestrel said. "In the market. Captain Vess's hounds."

"They wouldn't growl at you." Serah said it flatly, factually. She'd been turning it over too, then. Good. Bad. Both.

"They wouldn't growl at me because hounds don't growl at their own." Kestrel turned the bread crust over in their fingers. "They don't growl at anyone who speaks to them first. Politely. In their own tongue."

The silence that followed had texture. Kestrel counted its layers: one, two, three heartbeats of pure incomprehension, then a longer stretch in which Serah Vale, ex-Inquisitor, did the arithmetic and did not like the sum.

"Say it plainly," she said. Her voice was very quiet. Not soft. Quiet the way a drawn bowstring is quiet.

"I'm a beast-speaker." The words came out of Kestrel like teeth. "Have been since I was six. My mother found out when I told her the neighbor's cow was going to calve early, and she didn't speak to me for two days, and then she sat me down and explained that I was never, ever, *ever* going to say anything like that where anyone could hear, and that the cow was simply a clever cow, and that clever cows were a family tradition. And I have been lying ever since. To everyone. About everything. For twenty years." Kestrel looked up. "The rats in the guild walls told me you were bleeding before you ever reached the door. The crow on the north road gave me directions because I paid it in a button and flattery. The mouse under that gorse spent half the night telling me something with wings was circling the camp, and I have been sitting here since midnight trying to decide whether telling you was the bravest thing I'll ever do or the stupidest, and the cheese isn't helping me decide."

Serah had not moved. Not one muscle. She sat with her hands around her tin cup and her eyes on Kestrel, and her face had gone past neutral into something older and more carefully built, like a wall you only see the top of until you're standing next to it.

"You're telling me," she said at last, "that everything I've watched you do for two hundred miles — every lucky guess, every shortcut, every time you knew the patrol was coming before I did —"

"Wasn't luck."

"— was *them*. The animals. Talking to you."

"Talking to me. Mostly complaining. You would not believe how much of it is complaints. The horses alone —" Kestrel heard their own voice start to climb toward humor, that old reflex, the joke as trapdoor, and made themselves stop. "Yes. Them. Talking to me."

Serah set down the cup. She did it very precisely, in the center of a flat stone, as if the cup might otherwise do something regrettable.

And then, instead of reaching for her sword — and Kestrel would be lying, later, if they claimed that possibility hadn't crossed their mind, hadn't crossed it with its boots on — she said:

"Tell me about the cow."

Kestrel blinked. "The cow?"

"The cow. The first one. What did it say?"

"It said —" Kestrel laughed, a short startled sound, entirely against their will. "It said the calf was coming at moonrise and it would appreciate some quiet and possibly that hay that wasn't mostly thistle, if it wasn't too much trouble. It was very polite. Cows are polite. It's the geese you have to watch out for."

"Geese."

"Geese are *fascists*, Serah. Ask any goose. They'll tell you at length."

And something happened to Serah's face then — a crack, a shift, a tectonic event measured in the corners of her mouth — and she laughed. It was a rusty sound, a sound with cobwebs on it, the laugh of someone who hadn't used the machinery in years and wasn't sure it still turned over. But it turned over. It ran for a good five seconds before she got it back under control, and when she did, her eyes were wet, and she didn't wipe them, and she didn't explain, and Kestrel didn't ask.

"Twenty years," Serah said finally.

"Twenty years."

"And your mother. She taught you to hide it."

"She taught me that the neighbor boy — Tomas, Tomas Vey, he could talk to bees, they swarmed for him like he was made of flowers —" Kestrel's voice did something complicated on the name and they let it. "She taught me what happened to Tomas. Grey coats came for him on a Tuesday. His mother screamed at them until they took her too, for obstruction. I was seven. I stood at the window and watched them put him in a cart, and he wasn't crying. He was looking at the bees. He was saying goodbye to the bees." Kestrel fed the fire a stick it did not need. "My mother held my hand so hard it bruised, and she said, *never, never, never*, and that was my inheritance. Not the gift. The hiding of it."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, the field mouse had resumed its day, which apparently involved seeds and grievance in equal measure.

"That's why the hounds," Serah said. Not a question.

"That's why the hounds. And why Vess noticed. And why we're on this road." Kestrel finally looked at her straight. "You were an Inquisitor, Serah. You hunted —"

"I know what I was."

"Then you know what they do. To speakers. To people like me. And you're sitting there with a sword in reach and a fire between us and I have just handed you the only thing the Crown wants more than the map, and I need to know —" and here Kestrel's careful voice finally cracked, twenty years of polish failing all at once, like glaze off old pottery — "I need to know what happens now."

* * *

What happened now was that Serah got up, limped to the edge of the hollow, and stood looking north for a while, at nothing, at the road, at the whole rotten architecture of the world. Then she came back and sat down across the fire from Kestrel, closer than before, and spoke in a voice stripped of everything except the facts.

"You want to know what they do to captured speakers."

"I want the truth. I've earned at least that much of it, I think."

"You have." Serah turned her cup in her hands. "All right. The truth. The Inquisitors call it the Quiet Protocol. It's not in any document you could find, because the documents don't exist — the Protocol is taught mouth to ear, in a room with no windows, and the first thing they tell you is that the Protocol does not exist. That's the first lie they make you swear to. It's a test. If you can swear to a lie that big and keep your face still, you're Inquisitor material."

Kestrel's hands had gone cold. "Go on."

"There's a drug. They used to call it hushwater. Made from something that grows in the salt marshes — I never knew what, and the apothecaries who mixed it were the only people in the Order who were never promoted. It doesn't hurt. That's the part they're proudest of. It doesn't hurt at all. It just — takes the voice. Not the speaking voice. The *other* voice. The one that reaches the animals. It takes it out of you the way you'd take a splinter out, clean, and the speaker stays alive, and healthy, and can't so much as make a dog sit. And they keep them. In a facility outside Corvane. There's a garden. They're treated *well*." Serah's jaw worked. "That's the official line. Treated well. I signed transport orders for eleven of them. Eleven. I checked the boxes. I initialed the margins. I told myself it was mercy, because the alternative, the old way, before the Protocol —"

She stopped.

"The old way," Kestrel said. Not a question either. The fire had gone quiet, as if it too were listening.

"The old way was fire," Serah said. "The old way was why the Protocol was invented. Someone in the Order, a long time ago, decided that burning speakers made martyrs, and martyrs made more speakers, and that a quiet garden was better propaganda than a pyre. So they built the garden." She looked up, and her eyes were terrible and steady. "Except. There's a thing about hushwater they don't put in the transport orders. The voice doesn't just go quiet. The speaker can still *feel* it. All of it. Every animal within a mile, every fear and hunger and grief, coming through like weather, with no way to answer, no way to close the door. Forever. The garden is full of people who can hear everything and say nothing, and the Order calls that mercy, and I *signed the orders*, Kestrel. Eleven times. In my own hand."

The hollow was very silent. Even the mouse had stopped.

"I didn't tell you that to make you run," Serah said. "Or to make you forgive me. I told you because you asked for the truth, and because you gave me yours first, and because —" and here her voice, too, finally failed at the job it had been doing for years — "because when you said *the rats told me you were bleeding*, I wasn't surprised. Do you understand? I wasn't *surprised*. Some part of me has known since the ford. Since before the ford. Since the night in the guild when you stood in the doorway with that dying man's blood on your hands and looked at me like you were reading me. I've been read by speakers before. I know what it feels like. And I let you read me anyway, and I didn't say anything, because I was a coward, and because —"

She stopped again. This time it looked like the stopping was the hard part, like there was a door at the back of her throat and she had to physically open it.

"Because I was already gone," she said. "From the Order. From all of it. I defected for a man who died on the Salt Stair, and I told myself that was the whole story, but it wasn't. It was also every transport order. It was the garden. It was eleven names I never let myself learn, and I have been walking north for two years telling myself I was a smuggler now, a new person, clean ledger — and then you sat down at that fire and told me the truth with your hands shaking, and you *trusted* me, and I realized I have never once in my life been trusted with anything that mattered. I was trusted with orders. That's not the same thing."

Kestrel found that their eyes were burning. They let them. The fire wouldn't tell.

"Tomas Vey," they said quietly. "That's one of the names. The one I know. He'd be — thirty now. Twenty-eight. He liked honey cake and he was kind to his little sister and he talked to bees." Kestrel swallowed. "If he's in the garden, Serah, he can hear everything. He's been hearing everything for twenty years."

"I know."

"And we're going the other way."

"I know that too." Serah looked at the fire for a long moment. "Vhalcrest first. The map, the door, whatever's burning on the other side of it. That's the debt in front of us. But —" she looked up, and something had settled in her face, something that looked almost like a decision being forged — "when this is done, whatever this turns out to be, I'm going back for the garden. You should know that about me now, so you can plan around it. I'm not asking you to come. I'm telling you what I am."

Kestrel thought about it. Honestly. Weighed it the way they weighed everything, the appraiser's habit, turning the thing in the light to see what it was truly made of.

"I'm a beast-speaker," they said, "walking toward the last living dragon with an ex-Inquisitor and a wyvern hatchling in my pack. I don't think either of us gets to plan around anything anymore." They reached across the fire and, very deliberately, turned Serah's cup right side up. It had been sitting crooked. It had been bothering them for an hour. "But I'll come to the garden. When it's done. If we're both still —"

"When it's done," Serah said, "we'll both still be. That's not hope. That's scheduling."

Despite everything — the cold, the fear, the eleven names, the twenty years — Kestrel laughed. And Serah almost did. And the sun came up over the granite shoulders of the hills, and it was, all things considered, a terrible morning, and one of the best of Kestrel's life.

* * *

They broke camp slowly, neither in any hurry to end the strange truce the fire had built, and it was Ember who ended it.

The hatchling had slept through the confessions in the bottom of Kestrel's pack, curled around the map like a dragon-shaped paperweight, and now emerged blinking and indignant, shook herself to full size — which was to say, the size of a furious loaf of bread — and did something she had never done before.

She spoke.

Not in images, not in the warm wordless press of hatchling-mind that Kestrel had grown used to. Words. Old ones. Heavy ones, in a voice like a bell rung underwater, and there was nothing childish in it at all:

*The fire is failing. The door is opening whether you walk through it or not. Hurry, little speaker. Hurry. She has begun the ritual early — and she has your blood.*

Kestrel went cold from the boots up.

"What is it?" Serah was already reaching for her sword, reading Kestrel's face the way she read everything, fast and without mercy. "What did it say?"

Kestrel looked north, toward a valley that every map called empty, and found their voice had gone very quiet.

"We have a problem," they said. "And it's three days closer than we thought."
