## 15. The Drowned Borderlands

### Kestrel

The borderlands announced themselves the way a debt collector announces himself: politely, and far too late to do anything about it.

Kestrel noticed the water first. It came up between the tussocks of grass like gossip rising through floorboards, seeping into their boots, silvering the hollows of the road until the road stopped being a road and became a suggestion. Ahead, the lowlands stretched out flat and drowned to the horizon, dotted with the tops of things that should have been much taller—chimneys, a bell tower's crown, the uppermost branches of an orchard tree still stubbornly bearing fruit.

"There used to be a border here," Serah said, wading forward without breaking stride. "The Salt March. The Crown flooded it after the Sigil Wars. Said it was to stop smugglers." She glanced back, one eyebrow raised. "You're wading through a nation-sized smuggling operation, for the record."

"I prefer to think of myself as a legitimate trader in impossible things."

"You're knee-deep in a drowned country."

"Legitimate," Kestrel said, "is a matter of ledger-keeping."

The truth was that Kestrel's legs had gone cold and their stomach had gone colder, because the beasts had gone quiet.

Not absent—Kestrel would have preferred absent. Quiet. All morning, as they'd left the hanged-sign villages behind, the small voices had thinned: the field mice chattering their tiny ledgers of seed-debts, the herons grumbling their long grey complaints. Now, as the water deepened, there was nothing. Not silence, exactly. Silence was an empty room. This was a room full of people all holding their breath at once, all looking at you.

Ember felt it too. The wyvern hatchling rode in the sling against Kestrel's chest, and where she had spent the last week chirping commentary at every beetle, frog, and interesting cloud, she had gone still as a stone, her small claws gripping Kestrel's shirt with the particular firmness of a creature pretending not to be afraid.

"I know," Kestrel murmured to her. "I don't like it either."

"What did she say?" Serah asked. She'd been teaching herself to read the pauses in Kestrel's one-sided conversations, the way one learns to follow a conversation in a foreign language by watching faces.

"She didn't. That's the problem." Kestrel waded on. Water to mid-thigh now, then to the hip, the pack held high. "Everything's watching us. Nothing's talking. It's like walking into the guild's back room when Odile's doing the accounts and you've forgotten to knock."

"Terrifying image."

"You have no idea. She once made a man apologize to a filing cabinet."

* * *

### Serah

Serah had crossed the Salt March once before, seven years ago, running the other direction, with a wounded courier and a bag of documents she was supposed to burn and hadn't. She remembered the water being empty then, too, but she had been too busy bleeding and panicking to notice the quality of the emptiness.

She noticed it now. It had a texture. The water lay flat as poured glass in every direction, no wind, no ripples, though the reeds on the far shore were visibly bending. Something was moving air that wasn't reaching them. Or something was holding the water still the way a hand holds a page flat.

And the birds. There should have been birds over water like this. Instead there was one heron, standing in the shallows a hundred yards off, facing them. Just facing them. It had been facing them for the better part of an hour, and it had not moved, and Serah had seen enough ambushes to know what a picket looked like.

"Kestrel."

"I see it."

"It's not hunting."

"No," Kestrel said quietly. "It's reporting."

Serah's hand drifted to her knife out of seven years of habit, and Kestrel shot her a look that she was beginning to know well—the one that said *you cannot stab your way through a diplomatic situation, Serah, we have discussed this.*

"Fine," she said, and took her hand away. "But if it turns out to be an Inquisitor in a heron costume, I reserve the right to say I told you so."

"That would be an impressive costume."

"Vess would find a way."

The name landed between them like a stone in the water, and Serah regretted it. She'd been doing that—dropping Vess into conversations to watch Kestrel flinch, some small spiteful part of her wanting company in her nightmares. Kestrel didn't flinch anymore, actually. That was worse. Kestrel just went careful, the way they did with the ledgers, all the warmth folded away and put somewhere safe.

"Sorry," she said.

"Don't be. She's following us. Pretending otherwise would be its own kind of lie." Kestrel shifted the pack higher and squinted at the drowned landscape. "There. Do you see it?"

Serah followed their gaze. Rising out of the water a half-mile out, tilted like a drunk, stood a line of stones. Old ones, waist-high, spaced at intervals marching off toward the horizon—border stones, she realized. The old border of Vhalcrest, the one that had been on maps before the maps were burned. The flood had swallowed the land but not quite managed the stones; their crowns broke the surface like the backs of sleeping animals.

"The map wants us between them," Kestrel said. They had the map out, wrapped in oilcloth, and even from a distance Serah could see it moving under the wrapping, coastlines rearranging like a sleeper turning over. "The channel between the third and fourth stones."

"Of course it does. Why would it want us anywhere sensible?"

"You have a gift for asking questions whose answers are 'because the map hates us.'"

"I'm very talented."

* * *

### Kestrel

The water deepened as they neared the stones, until Kestrel was swimming short desperate strokes with the map held overhead and Ember clinging to their head like a furious, scaly hat. Serah swam better, of course. Serah swam the way she did everything, with an economy that suggested she'd once been graded on it.

They hauled out onto the third border stone, gasping, and sat straddling it like children on a fence, the drowned world spread around them.

And that was when Kestrel heard the word.

Not with their ears. It came up through the stone, through their palms, through the bones of their legs—the same way the dragonsong had hummed in them that night with the map. A word in the language that only beasts seemed to understand, the same word the dying courier had pressed into their hands along with the map. It was being spoken somewhere far below, slow and vast, the way a mountain might speak if a mountain had a great deal to say and all the time in the world to say it.

"Serah."

"I feel it." Serah's face had gone pale. "The stone's humming."

"It's not the stone." Kestrel's mouth had gone dry. "Something's under us. Something big."

"How big?"

Kestrel considered the question honestly, the way they considered all questions of valuation. "You remember the guild's cellar?"

"The one with the casks."

"You remember how the whole cellar was one cask, and everything else was just stored around it?"

Serah went very still. "That big."

"Or bigger. It's hard to appraise something you can't see the edges of."

Ember had risen from Kestrel's shoulder, wings half-spread, and was making a sound Kestrel had never heard from her—a low, ringing note, almost like a struck glass. Not fear. Recognition. And beneath them, in the deep water between the stones, the light changed.

It was subtle at first. A darkness moving under the darkness, the way a cloud crosses the sun underwater. Then the water itself began to swell, gently, without waves, rising around their stone like a tide that had forgotten to be tied to any moon. The heron, still watching from the shallows, finally moved—turned its head with great ceremony, as if closing a ledger—and flew away.

"Kestrel," Serah said, in the voice of someone doing arithmetic very fast, "I think we're being measured."

"I know."

"For what?"

"I think," Kestrel said, "we're being weighed."

* * *

### Serah

Serah had faced down Inquisitor tribunals, a burning safehouse, and one memorable evening with a crossbow-wielding tax collector, and she had a professional appreciation for the mechanics of fear: the cold start, the hot middle, the strange clarity at the end. What she felt now didn't fit any of the old shapes. This was awe with the safety removed.

The water below them was rising into a shape.

Not surfacing—rising, the way a cathedral rises around you when you finally stop looking at your feet. She caught suggestions of it: a ridge like a spine the length of a harbor; something that might have been an eye, opening at a depth where eyes had no business working; a slow drift of scales each of which could have roofed the guildhall. The water didn't splash or part. It simply made way, courteously, the way a crowd makes way for someone it recognizes.

And in her chest, in the place where she kept the things she'd signed and the things she'd lost, Serah felt the vast attention of it settle on her specifically. Not Kestrel. Her. It looked at her the way Vess used to look at suspects—except that where Vess's gaze stripped you down to your worst act, this gaze seemed to hold all of her acts at once, the burned and the saved, the signed order and the smuggled children, and weigh them together, without hurry, without mercy, and—she realized with a jolt—without judgment. Just weighing. The way Odile weighed a relic. The way Kestrel weighed everything: *what is this, truly, and what is it owed.*

"I'm sorry," she said aloud, absurdly, to the water. Her voice cracked in the middle. "For what I did. Before. I've been—"

"Serah." Kestrel's hand found hers on the wet stone. "It's not a tribunal."

"It feels like a tribunal."

"It feels like the guild," Kestrel said slowly, and Serah heard the wonder in their voice, "on audit day. When Odile goes through everything and doesn't throw anything out. It's not deciding if we're guilty. It's deciding if we're *real.*"

The eye beneath the surface came nearer. This close, it was not the flat black of a fish or the gold of a hawk. It was the deep amber of old resin, and in it Serah saw reflected two small drowned creatures clinging to a stone, and a hatchling with her wings spread, ringing like a bell—and the reflection of the eye itself, looking back, and looking back, a corridor of gazes going down into amber forever.

Then the vastness beneath them did something she would think about for the rest of her life.

It bowed.

Not much. An inch of deeper shadow, a settling of the water, the way an old courtier inclines his head to a child wearing a crown he remembers being fitted. The humming word changed key—resolved, the way a chord resolves—and Kestrel made a small sound, half laugh, half sob.

"What," Serah whispered. "What is it saying?"

"It's not saying anything. It's—" Kestrel's voice wobbled. "It's greeting Ember. By name. The name Ember doesn't have yet. It's greeting her the way you greet someone you've been waiting for." A pause. "And it's telling us to go on. It says—" Kestrel's brow furrowed, translating, the way they translated the cart-horse's opinions on oats. "It says the water remembers being land. It says it's sorry about the stones. It says... it says the door is expecting us, and we shouldn't keep it waiting, because doors get lonely too."

"That's the strangest sentence I've ever heard."

"You once told me Inquisitors file their confessions alphabetically."

"That's not strange, that's horrifying. There's a difference."

* * *

### Kestrel

They crossed the rest of the drowned border in the thing's wake, and Kestrel would never quite be able to explain the passage, even to themselves. The water bore them. Not carried, exactly—there was no current, no push—but every stroke went twice as far, every footing held, as if the whole flooded world had been asked to be a road and had agreed. Once, Kestrel's boot came down on empty water where a stone should have been, and the water held, firm as flagstone, until they had passed.

Ember had stopped ringing and started humming instead, low and content, and the great darkness below hummed back, and the two notes wove together into something that made Kestrel's chest ache with a homesickness for a place they'd never been.

The far shore came up slowly: reed-beds, then shingle, then a slope of honest dry land with the bell tower's drowned crown behind them like a marker on a map that no longer existed. Kestrel looked back only once.

The border stones stood in a line behind them, and between the third and fourth, the water had gone still and deep and dark, and nothing at all could be seen there, which was its own kind of signature. You don't get to see a thing like that twice. You get to be *let pass* once, and spend the rest of your life understanding what it cost the other party to be gentle.

"It knew Ember's name," Kestrel said quietly. They were wringing out their socks, which had given up all pretense of being clothing and were now simply sad aquatic organisms. "Not the name we gave her. Her other name. The one she'll grow into."

"Dragons have true names," Serah said. She was standing at the top of the shingle, not looking back at all, looking forward, up the slope, at the thin line of woodsmoke on the horizon. "That's what the archives said, before the archives were burned. That's why the Speakers were dangerous. A true name in a loving mouth is—" She stopped.

"Is what?"

"Is a door," Serah said. "I read that once. I thought it was poetry."

Kestrel looked down at Ember, who was drying her wings in the pale sun, smug as a cat, humming her unfinished name to herself. And Kestrel thought of the courier's last word pressed into their palms, still warm there after all these miles, and of the map shifting in its oilcloth, and of a door, somewhere ahead, that was expecting them and lonely and could not open itself.

Humility, Kestrel decided, felt like this: not smallness, but the sudden correct sense of scale. They were not the hero of this story. They were, at best, the courier. Someone had carried this word a long way already, and it had been carried to them, and someday it would be carried further, and the most a ledger-keeper could hope was that the sums came out honest.

"We should move," Serah said. "Woodsmoke means people, and people mean either help or trouble, and in my experience the ratio is—"

She stopped. She had gone rigid in the way she went rigid, all at once, like a door slamming.

"Serah?"

"Kestrel." Her voice had dropped to the flat tone Kestrel had learned to dread. "Look at the mud. Here, at the tide line."

Kestrel looked. The mud below the shingle was printed with tracks. Boot tracks. Military issue, grey-coat pattern, dozens of them—fresh enough that water was still seeping into the prints. And beside them, pressed deep and deliberate into the clay where their owners had wanted it found, a single mark stamped over and over into the mud.

The Crown's sigil. And beneath it, in the Inquisitors' cipher that Serah had taught Kestrel to read on a night much like this one, a single word.

**FOUND.**

"They didn't follow us through the water," Serah said. "They went around. They've been waiting on this shore." She turned slowly, scanning the reed-beds, the slope, the woodsmoke. "Kestrel—the smoke. There's too much of it for a cookfire."

Kestrel gathered Ember against their chest and did the only arithmetic that mattered, which was the distance to the treeline and the odds of reaching it.

The reeds, all around them, began to move against the wind.
