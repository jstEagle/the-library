## 16. Vess Closes the Net

Captain Elara Vess had interrogated one hundred and forty-two suspects in her eleven years of service to the Crown's Inquisition, and she had developed a theory about them, which she kept written in the back of her field journal in small, orderly script: *Everyone lies, but no one lies well.* People lied with their hands, with their eyes, with the little hesitations before their yeses. A suspect was a book written in a language of tells, and Vess was fluent.

Guildmaster Odile Marchetti was not a book. She was a locked strongbox that had been painted to look like a book.

"Sit down, Captain," Odile said now, gesturing to the soot-stained chair across from her as though she were the hostess and Vess the guest. "The tea's gone smoky, I'm afraid. Everything has, lately." She smiled at her own inn with its blackened east wing, its smell of wet ash and charred rosemary—rosemary, of all things; the guild's drying racks had gone up like a festival—and her smile did not waver by a hair.

Vess sat. She did not take the tea. One hundred and forty-two suspects had taught her that tea offered during an interrogation was either a bribe, a delay, or a laxative, and she was unwilling to discover which this was.

"The fire started," Vess said, opening her journal, "in the storeroom."

"Fires generally start somewhere," Odile agreed. "That's the trouble with fires. Terribly unhygienic about boundaries."

"It started in the storeroom where your ledgers were kept."

"Our *inventory* was kept there. Ledgers are kept where I can see them, which is to say, under my nose, which is to say—" she tapped the side of it, "—here. The rest is ash and speculation."

Vess looked up from her notes. Outside the rain-shrouded window, two of her greycoats were interviewing the guild's surviving staff in the courtyard: a one-eyed locksmith who kept giggling nervously, an apprentice whose alibis contradicted each other so thoroughly that Vess suspected he simply enjoyed talking, and a cook who had wept continuously for forty minutes about a burnt pot of preserves and could not be induced to say anything else. None of them knew anything. All of them knew something. That was how guilds worked—a hive of small mercies and smaller crimes, everyone guilty of smuggling someone's cousin past a toll gate, no one guilty of anything worth burning a building over.

Except someone had burned it anyway. And someone had fled through the cellars the same night, according to the mud prints on the cellar stairs, and someone had left behind a scorched patch of floor exactly the size and shape of a missing map case.

And someone—the thought arrived, as it had been arriving all week, at inconvenient hours—had not growled at.

Vess shut the thought away. It did not go quietly.

"You had a guest," Vess said. "Three nights ago. After hours. Bloodied. Raving about a country called Vhalcrest."

Odile poured herself the tea after all, unhurried, steam curling up through the smell of smoke. "We get guests. That's what an inn-adjacent trade house is, Captain. Guests, goods, gossip, and the occasional rat with opinions."

"This guest died."

"That does happen to bloodied, raving men," Odile said gently, as if consoling her. "It's why we prefer the other kind."

"He pressed something into someone's hands before he died. A map."

"A dying man pressing things into hands." Odile sipped. "In my experience they press mostly apologies and unfinished debts. Maps are rarer. Though I once knew a man who tried to will me his boat, and it hadn't floated since his grandfather's time, so perhaps maps would have been kinder."

Vess wrote nothing. This was deliberate. Some of the most useful silences were the ones you filled with the scratch of nothing.

"The courier's name," Vess said at last, "was Tam Weller. He vanished off the north road nine days ago. His last confirmed sighting was in this market district. The night watch found blood on your doorstep."

"Found it, did they? Good for them. Very thorough, our watch." Odile set down her cup. And then—for the first time in the entire interview—she leaned forward, and her voice changed, and Vess felt the strongbox shift as though a key had been fitted into it from the inside.

"Captain," Odile said, "I've run this guild for thirty-one years. I have fed smugglers and sheltered deserters and once, in a fit of sentiment, hidden a deposed margrave in my flour barrel for six days. If Tam Weller died in my storeroom—if he gave something precious to one of my people with his last breath—then that thing is beyond the reach of your warrant, your hounds, and your grey coats, and I will tell you precisely why." Her eyes, dark and dry as old ink, held Vess's. "Because everything that comes through my doors comes to me broken, and everything that leaves them leaves mended, and I do not mend things only to hand them back to the people who broke them. Now." She sat back, and the warmth returned to her face like lamplight. "More tea?"

*Everyone lies,* Vess thought, closing her journal, *but no one lies well.*

She had never met anyone who lied this well. It was, professionally speaking, fascinating. It was also, in some part of her she declined to examine, rather admirable, which was worse.

* * *

Outside, the rain had settled into the patient, businesslike drizzle that made the market district smell of wet stone and old fish. Sergeant Broch awaited her under the awning, holding the leash of a hound named Verity, who drooled with tremendous dignity.

"Anything?" Broch asked.

"She confessed to nothing, admitted to everything, and offered me tea."

"Did you take it?"

"I'm not an idiot, Sergeant."

"Only asking, Captain." Broch consulted his slate. "Courtyard interviews are done. The cook's still crying. The locksmith wants immunity for information about a smuggling ring operating out of the fishmonger's, which he's offered to every patrol that's come through since spring, so I'd take that with salt. The apprentice—"

"The apprentice is a waste of a good uniform. What about the hounds?"

Broch's face did something complicated. "That's the thing, Captain. We ran Verity along the cellar route, same as the trail from the north road. She tracked the courier's scent to the storeroom door, then to the cellar stairs, then—" he hesitated, and Verity, hearing herself discussed, sighed gustily, "—then she sat down."

"Sat down."

"Sat. Down. Wagging, Captain. Like she'd found what she was looking for and saw no further need of it." He scratched his jaw. "Hounds don't sit on a trail. Not trained ones. And she won't track the second scent at all—the one going out through the cellars. Won't put her nose near it. Just... looks at it."

Vess looked at Verity. Verity looked back with the frank brown eyes of an animal who knew something and considered it none of Vess's business.

*Not growling is also a tell,* she thought, and the thought had teeth.

She had noticed it first at the market canvass, days ago, before the fire. The Crown's hounds were bred and conditioned to signal on beast-speakers—it was the whole point of them, the doctrine said, the beasts themselves recoiling from the abomination in human skin. Every speaker they'd ever taken had been given away by a growl, a flattening of ears, a hackle. It was reliable. It was in the manuals. It was in the *hymns*, for pity's sake, sung at every Inquisitor's oath-taking: *and the beasts shall cry out against thee.*

But the Okonkwo person—Kestrel, the guild's ledger-clerk, quiet as dust and twice as easy to overlook—had walked past three hounds on three separate occasions, close enough to touch, and the hounds had done nothing at all. Not growled. Not signaled. One of them, a young bitch named Doctrine, had *wagged*.

Vess had written it in her journal: *No reaction. Anomaly. Possible faulty conditioning.* She had underlined *anomaly* twice. She had not written what she had actually seen in that moment, which was a young woman flinching—just barely, just at the edge of perception—as though walking past something holy and hoping not to be noticed by it.

Doctrine had wagged. Verity sat. And somewhere in Vess's chest, in the place where eleven years of hymns were stored, a very small voice observed that the beasts of the Crown had opinions too, and that perhaps the manuals had never once consulted them.

She told the voice to be quiet. It was becoming harder to obey.

* * *

The false trail, when she found it, was almost insultingly good.

It began—or appeared to begin—in the guild's cellar, where the fleeing intruder's muddy prints had paused beside a collapsed shelf. Wedged in the rubble, dropped in haste, was a page torn from a ledger. Vess recognized the format instantly: the Guild of Impossible Things kept meticulous books, and this was a shipping record. Consignment of "preserved storm-glass, six crates" sent north by river barge to a broker in Millhaven. Dated five days ago. Signed with a mark instead of a name.

Six crates of storm-glass. Vess had personally watched the appraisers carry in a bottled storm two weeks prior—one crate, heavy, humming faintly, worth more than her annual salary. Six crates moving north in secret, days before the fire? That wasn't smuggling. That was a treasure convoy.

"There," she said, when she laid the page before Odile for confirmation. "Your hand?"

"My ledger format," Odile conceded, examining it with theatrical care. "My seal-mark, even. Though anyone who's bought so much as a hairpin from us has seen both."

"Is the entry genuine?"

"Captain, if I told you, I'd have to start lying."

Which was, Vess reflected later, the most honest answer she got all day.

She rode for Millhaven with a squad of eight at first light, because the trail was good, because the warrants were signed, because doctrine said follow the evidence wherever it leads. She rode hard, and the whole way she was aware of a splinter working deeper under her certainty, and by the second day she had identified its exact shape:

The trail was *too* good.

Evidence did not behave like this. Real evidence was ragged, partial, contradictory. It smelled wrong and pointed nowhere and wasted weeks. But here was a torn page, perfectly preserved, positioned precisely where a searcher would look. Here was a barge-master in Millhaven with a conveniently vivid memory of a hooded passenger heading north. Here was a stable-boy who'd seen a tall figure buying a horse, and a ferryman who'd seen a short one, and a beggar who'd seen both, and every single witness along the route had a piece of the story and every piece slotted together like joinery.

Real fugitives left chaos. This trail left a *narrative*. Someone had built her a road, signposted it, and swept it clean, and the only reason to build a road for your hunter is—

"—if you want them going where *you're* going," Vess finished aloud, alone in her room at the Millhaven coaching inn, and then stood very still.

Somewhere north, a clerk with mud on her boots and a stolen map was running for a country that did not exist, and she had looked over her shoulder, and she had thought: *the hounds will follow. Let them follow* — *here.*

Vess took out her journal. She turned past one hundred and forty-two entries, past *everyone lies but no one lies well*, past the underlined *anomaly*, to a fresh page. Her pen hovered.

What she wanted to write was: *Suspect anticipates pursuit. Trail unreliable. Recommend redirection.* What her hand actually wanted to write, in the small orderly script that had never once betrayed her, was something else entirely, something that had no place in any manual or hymn:

*She is cleverer than I am, and she is kind to horses, and I sat in her guildmaster's chair and was offered tea by a woman who has buried a sister's name so deep even the Inquisition couldn't find it, and I cannot stop thinking about a hound wagging its tail.*

She wrote the professional sentence. Then, after a long moment, beneath it, in even smaller script, she wrote: *Note: consider the possibility that the beasts have been trying to tell us something for eleven years, and we are the ones who don't speak.*

She read it back. It was heresy. It was possibly the truest thing she had ever committed to paper.

A knock came at the door. Broch, breathless, rain streaming off his oilcloth.

"Captain—you'll want to hear this. Ferryman downstream swears the hooded passenger didn't cross the river at all. Says they went up the *old salt road* instead. Toward the drowned borderlands."

"The drowned borderlands are outside our jurisdiction."

"Yes, Captain." Broch shifted. "Also—the ferryman says the passenger had a bird with them. A crow. Riding on their shoulder like it was *paid to*." He paused. "And there's one other thing. Message came in from the capital overnight. Command's reassigning the Vhalcrest inquiry to a senior inquisitor. We're ordered to hold position until—"

"Hold position," Vess repeated, already reaching for her coat, "while the quarry walks into a country that officially isn't there, accompanied by a bird on retainer, and the file lands in the hands of someone who's never heard a hound wag." She buckled it with sharp, decisive movements, and felt—not dread, exactly, and not doubt, but the strange exhilaration of a woman stepping off the edge of her own map. "Sergeant, saddle the horses. We're crossing the borderlands."

"Captain," Broch said carefully, "the borderlands are flooded, cursed, and full of things the manuals say don't exist."

"Yes," said Captain Vess, pulling on her gloves. "I'm beginning to think the manuals have a great deal to answer for."
