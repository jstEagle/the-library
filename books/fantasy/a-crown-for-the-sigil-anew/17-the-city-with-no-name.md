## 17. The City With No Name

The city did not appear on any map, which was fitting, because it had made a career of appearing on nothing at all.

Kestrel felt it before they saw it—a change in the quality of the silence along the river road, the way the reeds stopped whispering and started listening. Even the waterfowl went strange, paddling in tight circles as though the marsh itself had asked them for a moment of discretion.

"There," Serah said, and pointed with her chin, hands staying where they were—crossed loose over the boat's gunwale, within reach of neither knife nor anything that looked like a weapon. Old habits. She'd been a soldier of the Crown once; she carried herself now like someone perpetually apologizing for the fact.

What Kestrel saw was, at first glance, nothing. Fog on grey water, a broken tooth of shoreline, the suggestion of piers. Then the fog shifted in a way fog does not do on its own, folding back like a curtain drawn by a considerate servant, and there it was: a city built of everything the world had tried to throw away.

Docks of blackened timber rose from the shallows in crooked tiers, and every one of them was crowded. Boats without flags. Boats with too many flags, stitched together from scraps, so that no single nation could claim them. Houses stacked atop warehouses stacked atop what looked suspiciously like a beached warship someone had decided would make excellent housing if you just turned it on its side and put doors in the hull. Above it all, on poles and rooftops and bridge-cables, hung lanterns of a dozen incompatible makes, burning colors that had no business coexisting—guild-light and witch-fire and honest tallow, all jostling together like strangers forced to share a bench.

"No name," Kestrel murmured. "But everyone knows where it is."

"That's the trick of it." Serah shipped the oars and let the current take them in. "You can't erase a place nobody will admit exists. The Crown's writ stops at that fog line. Has for sixty years. They call it a smuggling den in the reports and then they pretend the reports are exaggerated."

"And what do you call it?"

Serah was quiet a moment. Inquisitors were taught to call such places *infected*, Kestrel knew. She'd told them as much over a dying fire two nights past, in the flat voice she used for confessions that cost her something.

"Home, once," she said finally. "Briefly. Before I got respectable." The word came out with an edge that drew blood on its way past her teeth.

Ember shifted against Kestrel's chest, under the loose fold of their coat where the little wyvern had ridden since dawn, hidden and warm and heavier than any creature her size had a right to be. No—not yet named. Kestrel had been calling her *little one* in the privacy of their skull, and the hatchling had been answering in images instead of words: *warm, hunger, wrong-place, yours?* That last one came with disconcerting frequency.

They bumped the pier. A boy of perhaps ten, missing two fingers on his left hand, caught the rope without being asked and held out his palm with the weary professionalism of a customs official.

"Toll's three coppers, or something true."

Kestrel blinked. "Something true?"

"Something true you say out loud. Portmaster's rule. You can pay in coin or in honesty, but everybody pays." The boy shrugged, as if to say he hadn't written the rules, only survived them. "Coin's faster."

Kestrel fished out the coppers—and paused, because their fingers found only two, and beneath them the thin leather purse that had been Odile's parting gift, light as a dried leaf, holding the last of what she'd pressed into their hands on that terrible night with the guild burning behind them. Two coppers it was, then. They paid, and the boy waved them through with the air of a man waving through royalty.

Serah paid differently. She leaned down to the boy and said, quietly, "I was born about forty miles north of here, and I have not been home in eleven years, and I am afraid of it," and walked on before he could react.

Kestrel hurried to catch up. "That wasn't necessary."

"Neither was the money." Serah's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling—the small twitch at one corner, quickly arrested. "Portmaster's rule. Figured I'd test whether it was real."

Behind them, faintly, the boy said, "Huh," in the tone of someone updating a ledger.

*

The market streets of the unnamed city smelled of tar, frying onions, wet wool, and old paper—an odor Kestrel recognized in their bones, because it was the smell of the Guild of Impossible Things' back storeroom, and for one lurching heartbeat they were homesick enough to stumble.

Then they looked at the stalls, and the homesickness curdled into something more complicated.

There were books for sale that Kestrel had only ever heard of as *titles*, whispered like obscenities: *A Natural History of the Speaking Peoples*, its spine cracked, its pages bristling with ribbons left by some previous owner who had loved it enough to mark every argument. A whole barrel of sigil-charts, banned since the Wars, going for pennies to a woman who was using them as placemats. Glass cases held rings and seals of deposed kings—three dynasties' worth, at least, each tag written in a bored hand: *House Vellin, deposed, minor claims, make offer.* One case held nothing but a single boot, displayed on velvet, labeled simply *THE BOOT OF THE LAST MARGRAVE. DO NOT ASK.*

"Every erasure leaves debris," Serah said, walking half a step ahead, eyes moving over the crowd with the automatic sweeps of her old training even as her shoulders sat looser than Kestrel had seen them. "The Crown burns books, but books float. People drown, and their papers wash up. Whole villages get struck from the rolls, and somebody's grandmother kept a spoon." She gestured at a stall hung with hundreds of spoons, arranged like an army of small silver-faced witnesses. "This is where the debris comes ashore."

Kestrel touched a spoon, gently. It was engraved with a name in a script they didn't know, and beneath the name, a tiny stamped bird.

And then the birds around them went quiet.

Not all of them. Just the near ones—pigeons on a warehouse ridge, gulls wheeling above the fish-stalls, a mangy bantam rooster tied by one leg outside a cookshop. Every feathered head turned toward Kestrel at once, and Kestrel felt the familiar cold drop in their stomach, the old reflex of a lifetime spent hiding: *stillness, blankness, be no one.*

But this was not the market district of home, where a sparrow's attention meant danger. Here, the pigeons only watched, and then—one of them, bold as brass, hopped down the drainpipe and landed on Kestrel's boot and *asked*. Not in words. In the pressing, hopeful, image-language of birds: *speaker? speaker-warm? seed?*

"They know," Kestrel breathed. "They can tell, and they don't—they're not afraid."

"Why would they be?" Serah nodded at the banned books, the sigil charts, the boot of the last Margrave. "Look around. This is the one place in the world where being forbidden isn't the same as being alone."

The pigeon on Kestrel's boot cooed up at them with frank, greedy hope, and Kestrel—feeling reckless in a way that still frightened them, but less than it used to—crouched and answered in the silent way, *no seed, small friend, but you are seen,* and the pigeon fluffed itself into a sphere of pure satisfaction and stayed there while Kestrel walked, riding their boot like a figurehead, until a fishmonger's shout distracted it and it departed with a debt of goodwill Kestrel suspected they'd never be able to repay in grain.

They were halfway down the row of salvage stalls when they heard the sound.

It was not loud. That was what stopped them. In a market roaring with hawkers and gulls and argument, a small sound cut through like a pin through cloth: a high, thin, furious chirring, the sound of something very young trying very hard to roar.

Under their coat, the hatchling—unnamed, smuggled, purchased two days ago in a fit of insanity Kestrel had not yet finished regretting—chirred back.

*

The cage was at the end of a dead-end spur of the market, where the salvage gave way to live cargo, and the live cargo gave way, in the way of such places, to cargo that should not have been alive to sell.

Kestrel saw the seller first: a broad man in a coat made of layered aprons, each pocket bulging, his face professionally pleasant in the manner of men who have learned that pleasantness moves merchandise. Then the cages: songbirds in stacks, a terrapin the size of a dinner plate, a fire-lizard sulking in a mesh ball. And at the end, apart, under a tarp thrown carelessly across the top—

The hatchling.

She was smaller than the one sleeping against Kestrel's heart, if that were possible—all oversized head and enormous amber eyes and wings like wet leaves, pressed into the far corner of a cage built for a hunting dog. Her scales were the color of banked coals, dull with grime, and where the bars had rubbed her forelegs raw she had worried the wounds with her teeth. She was making the chirring sound continuously now, a tiny engine of rage that had never once, apparently, considered stopping.

Beside Kestrel, under their coat, their own hatchling went very still, and then began to press outward against the fabric with the patient, irresistible determination of something that has made a decision.

"*No,*" Kestrel breathed.

The coat bulged.

"*No.*"

A coal-colored snout emerged from Kestrel's collar, took in the scene—the cage, the kin, the seller—and produced a chirrup of absolute, ringing authority.

The man in the layered aprons turned around slowly. His professional pleasantness performed a series of maneuvers: surprise, calculation, fear, greed, and settled on greed with surprising speed.

"Well now," he said. "Well, well, well."

"Good morning," Kestrel said, with the bright, doomed fluency of a person who has balanced ledgers for liars and knows exactly how negotiations die. "I'm just browsing."

"You've got a wyvern in your coat."

"I've got a cat in my coat."

"It's a wyvern."

"It's an unusual cat."

The man's eyes moved to the cage. To the hatchling inside it. Back to Kestrel. Kestrel watched him do the arithmetic—he was quick about it, give him credit—and arrive at the sum: *two wyverns, one buyer, prices negotiable upward forever.*

Serah drifted closer with the unhurried menace of weather. "Friend," she said pleasantly, "what's the animal trade like in this port? Regulated?"

"Free port," the man said, not taking his eyes off Kestrel. "Everything's regulated by appetite."

"Mm," said Serah, who had spent nine years regulating things by appetite herself, in a grey coat, and whose voice still remembered how to sound like it. "And who sets your appetite's limits?"

The man looked at her then, properly, and something in her face must have translated, because his arithmetic visibly gained a new column. But greed is a stubborn animal, and his had already scented blood.

"Tell you what," he said. "You want the little one. Obviously. Cage and beast, forty marks."

Kestrel laughed before they could stop themselves—a short, appalled bark. Forty marks was four years of a laborer's wages. Forty marks was the guild's quarterly candle budget. Forty marks was—

Forty marks was a number designed to open a negotiation, and Kestrel knew it, and hated that they knew it, and hated more the thing they did next, which was look at the hatchling again. She had gone silent. She was watching them with those enormous amber eyes, and behind the rage in them was the other thing, the thing Kestrel had seen in the eyes of every animal ever brought to the guild's back door injured or lost: the question. Not *will you buy me.* Animals didn't know about money. The question was simpler and infinitely worse: *are you one of the ones who sees me?*

Under their coat, their own hatchling chirred once, softly. The image that came with it was unmistakable, absurd, and completely binding: *sister.*

"Oh no," Kestrel said aloud, to nobody, in the exact tone Odile used when reading a ledger entry she already knew would end in a loss.

*

They negotiated in the shade of a warehouse overhang, because the seller refused to conduct business in front of his other stock ("bad for the mystique"), and Serah stood slightly behind Kestrel's shoulder radiating the specific silence of a person counting exits.

"Thirty," the man said, third round. "Final. I've fed her a week and she's bitten me twice."

"She's bitten you twice because you keep her in a dog cage," Kestrel said. "Ten."

"Twenty-five, and I throw in the cage."

"The cage is worth nothing. The cage is an instrument of cruelty with hinges. Ten."

"Twenty, and—" he spread his hands, magnanimous, "—and I don't go asking around the port about the girl with the unusual cat."

The street went very quiet. Somewhere behind Kestrel, Serah's weight shifted onto the balls of her feet.

Kestrel held up one finger—*wait*—without looking away from the seller, and reached into their coat, past the indignant hatchling, and brought out Odile's purse.

They had inventoried it every morning since the burning. They knew it to the copper: eleven marks, six shillings, and the strange foreign coin Odile had told them never to spend unless the situation was *truly impossible*, a fat square thing stamped with a dragon rampant, worth either nothing or everything depending on which side of the world's forgetting you stood on.

Eleven marks, six shillings. Against twenty.

"Here's what happens instead," Kestrel said, and heard, with distant surprise, Odile's voice coming out of their own mouth—warm, unhurried, immovable, the voice of a woman who had talked down pirates and tax assessors with the same expression. "You take eleven marks, six shillings. You take the cage, which I am buying from you at a fair price so you can tell yourself you won something today. And you forget our faces—not because my friend behind me is frightening, though she is, and not because I'll ask the birds of this port to follow you for the rest of your life, though I could, and we both know the gulls here would do it for fun. You forget our faces because you are, I think, underneath the aprons, a man who knows exactly what that creature in the cage is, and knows exactly who buys wyverns and why, and has been lying awake hoping someone would come take her off your hands before those buyers found her."

The man's face did several things. The last of them was the truth.

"...She doesn't eat much," he said hoarsely. "Fish. Mostly fish. She likes the heads best."

"I know," Kestrel said gently. "I know what they like."

He counted the coins with shaking hands, and if his eyes went briefly to the strange square dragon-stamp and lingered there, he said nothing—which was, Kestrel understood, its own kind of payment, its own kind of honesty, dropped into the ledger between strangers.

The cage door stuck. Of course it stuck. Kestrel worked the latch while the hatchling inside pressed herself against the bars, chirring again, that furious little engine, until the door swung and she poured out over Kestrel's wrist and up their arm and into their collar beside her sister, and the two of them wound together into one trembling coal-and-ember knot, and the sound they made then was not a roar and not a chirr but something smaller and older than both—

—and every gull in the harbor, every pigeon on every ridgepole, every bird in every stacked cage along the dead-end row lifted its head and looked at Kestrel Okonkwo, and did not look away.

The seller crossed himself with the wrong hand, an old habit from a religion the Crown had also erased, and said, very quietly, "Get her out of the city. Please. Get them both out of the city."

"We're leaving tonight," Kestrel promised.

They were down to four shillings and a coin that shouldn't exist. They had bought a life and gotten a sister thrown in. Kestrel stood in the narrow street of a city with no name, holding the last of Odile's love in a light leather purse and two dragons against their heart, and thought: *this is what rebellion actually looks like. Not banners. Not fire. Just refusing, over and over, in small sums, to let the world finish its arithmetic.*

Serah appeared at their elbow carrying, inexplicably, a paper cone of fried fish heads.

"For the— " she began, and stopped, because both hatchlings' heads had emerged from Kestrel's collar and were staring at the cone with the fixed, ancient hunger of their kind. She sighed. "For the unusual cats."

* * *

They spent the afternoon preparing to leave, which mostly meant Kestrel spending their final shillings on dried fish and a better sling for two passengers instead of one, and Serah standing watch, and the hatchlings eating with a savagery that suggested the dog cage had been as short on meals as it was on mercy.

Toward dusk, Kestrel climbed to the top of a beached-hull tenement to look at the harbor and think about the map.

It rode against their ribs always now, wrapped in oilcloth, and even through the cloth it was restless tonight—coastlines sliding, the drowned borderlands rearranging themselves like a sleeper turning over. And there was something else. For three days, whenever Kestrel touched it, the dragonsong in their bones had changed pitch. Deeper. Closer. As if whatever burned beyond the world's edge had noticed the approach of something small and warm and kin.

Now, with two hatchlings asleep against their chest, the hum was nearly a chord.

Below them, in the lamplit maze of the market, a grey shape moved against the flow of the crowd. Grey cloak. Grey hood. Hounds on lead—or no. Kestrel squinted. Not hounds. Ferrets on leads, white ferrets, moving purposefully from stall to stall, and at each stall the traders shook their heads, and the grey figure paid them anyway, and moved on.

Searching. Methodically. The way Captain Vess searched—but Vess was days behind them, chasing the false trail through the eastern passes. Unless the false trail had failed. Unless—

One of the ferrets stopped. Raised its blunt little face directly toward the rooftop where Kestrel stood.

And spoke, in the thin whistle of its kind, a message meant for the man in grey but audible, oh so clearly, to anyone in the crowd who happened to have Kestrel's particular affliction:

*Two dragons. She carries two dragons. I smell them on the wind.*

Kestrel did not wait to climb down properly.
