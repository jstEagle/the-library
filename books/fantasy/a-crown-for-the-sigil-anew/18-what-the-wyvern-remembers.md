## 18. What the Wyvern Remembers

The hatchling ate three of Odile's coins before Kestrel learned to keep the purse out of reach.

Not swallowed, precisely. Sucked on, worried at with tiny needle teeth like a child gnawing a sweetmeat, then dropped—dented, sullen, and thoroughly licked—onto the floorboards of their rented room above the salt-house. The first coin had gone down whole and come back up an hour later polished to a shine that no moneylender in three cities could match. Kestrel supposed they ought to charge for the service.

"No," they said, for perhaps the ninetieth time that day. "We do not eat the currency. The currency is how we eat."

The hatchling blinked at them from atop the folded blanket that served as a nest, which was in fact Kestrel's blanket, which was in fact the only blanket. Its scales were the color of a banked fire—deep red shading to ember-orange along its belly—and when it breathed, a faint sulfur smell drifted through the room like someone cooking eggs badly one floor down. It was smaller than Kestrel had expected. Barely the size of a well-fed cat, wings still soft at the joints, eyes enormous and gold and fixed on Kestrel with an attention that made the back of their neck prickle.

Beasts looked *at* you. This one looked *into* you.

"I'm going to name you," Kestrel said, "because Serah keeps calling it 'the lizard,' and I can see you resent it."

The hatchling's tail lashed once, knocking a cup off the table. Point proven.

"Ember," Kestrel said. "For obvious reasons." They held out a hand, palm down, the way you'd offer it to a strange dog. "Ember. There. Now you're somebody."

The little dragon leaned forward and pressed its forehead against Kestrel's knuckles, and the world tilted.

* * *

Kestrel had been touched by beasts before. It was the ordinary currency of their gift: the horses' gossip, the rats' reports, the crow's mercenary commentary. It always felt like pressing an ear to a wall—a thin barrier, other voices on the other side, muffled and sideways.

This was not that.

This was the wall falling down.

Memory hit them like deep water. Not Ember's memory—the hatchling was eleven days old and had memories to fill a thimble: warmth, hunger, the smell of Kestrel's coat, the terrible glory of the first flame (a sneeze, mostly). No. These were older. Vastly, absurdly older. Memories worn smooth by passing down through generations like a river stone, carried in the blood the way songs are carried in families, until nobody remembered who first sang them.

Kestrel saw—

—fire, but not destruction. Fire as *architecture*. Fire holding up a sky.

—a valley ringed by mountains, green and terraced and full of people, and over it all a dragon so large that the clouds broke around her wings like water around a ship's prow—

—an archway of pale stone, half-drowned, humming—

—hands. A human woman's hands, scarred and ink-stained, clasping a dragon's claw the way you'd clasp a friend's hand at a funeral and a wedding both. And a voice, speaking words Kestrel somehow understood without hearing them clearly, words that meant something like *we will remember you* and something else like *you will remember us*, an exchange, a bargain, a—

Kestrel came back to themselves on the floor. They did not remember sitting down. Their nose was bleeding, a thin trickle over their lip, and Ember was standing on their chest making small anxious sounds like a kettle deciding whether to whistle.

"I'm fine," Kestrel croaked, which was true in the sense that they were alive and false in every other sense. "I'm fine. You—" They stared at the hatchling. The hatchling stared back, head cocked, and there was something almost apologetic in the gold gaze. "You're a door, aren't you? Not the door. But a door into the remembering."

Ember chirped.

Downstairs, the salt-house door opened and closed; Serah's boots on the stairs, two at a time, because Serah did everything two at a time when she could. She came through the door with bread under one arm and her hand nowhere near her sword, which for Serah counted as relaxed, and stopped dead at the sight of Kestrel sprawled on the floorboards with a bleeding nose and a dragon standing on their sternum.

"So," she said, setting the bread down with the careful calm of a woman who had defected from an organization that taught her exactly what panic looks like and refuses to perform it. "Either the room spun for you too, or something happened here."

"The room didn't spin," Kestrel said. "Something much worse. I remembered things."

"You're twenty-six. Of course you remembered things."

"Not my things."

Serah sat down on the floor across from them, cross-legged, the way she used to sit during Inquisitor briefings—which she'd told him about in the small hours by the fire, the confession chapter of her life still being written slowly and painfully. She waited. That was the thing about Serah Vale: she could be patient when patience mattered, and this, apparently, mattered.

Kestrel wiped their nose, took a breath, and tried to put the unputattable into words.

* * *

"It wasn't like speaking to the horse," they said, some while later. The bread had been broken and mostly eaten. Ember had claimed Kestrel's lap and fallen asleep in a coil of warm scale, radiating heat like a stove with opinions. "With animals it's—it's small talk. Where's the grain. Which man hurt you. What does the fox want. Little sentences. Little lives. Wonderful lives, but small." They flexed their fingers, which still tingled. "This was a cathedral. I walked into somebody's cathedral and the somebody was dead a thousand years before I was born."

"Ancestral memory," Serah said quietly. "Inquisitors have files on it. Fragmentary. Most scholars thought it was myth." Her mouth twisted. "The ones who didn't think it was myth were the ones who wanted to breed for it. There was a program. I signed the requisitions without knowing what they were for." She said it flatly, the way she always said the confessions—as if daring Kestrel to flinch. Kestrel had stopped flinching somewhere outside the drowned borderlands, mostly because Serah's flinches on their own behalf were worse than any Kestrel could manage for herself.

"Well," Kestrel said, "the myth is currently asleep on my legs and has eaten four coppers, so the files need updating."

"What did you see?"

"A dragon. Enormous. Over a valley full of people—fields, terraces, a city. Vhalcrest, I'd bet my remaining teeth. And fire being used to... hold something. Not burning anything. Holding. Like the fire was a roof beam." Kestrel frowned, chasing the image. "And an archway. Pale stone. And a woman—human—holding a dragon's claw, and words going between them. An agreement. A covenant, Serah. Not dragons guarding Vhalcrest like hired swords. Dragons *founding* it. Being the founding of it. The fire isn't protection bolted onto the country. The fire is the country's bones."

Serah was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the free-port went about its lawless evening: gulls arguing over fish heads, a fistfight starting and aborting, someone singing very badly about a lighthouse keeper's wife. A city where erased things washed up. Kestrel was beginning to understand why.

"And the woman?" Serah asked. "In the memory. The one holding the claw."

"Ink-stained hands. Scars." Kestrel's voice went strange in their own ears. "Serah, she was wearing guild colors. Or what guild colors became. And when the voice spoke the covenant words, I felt—" They stopped. Started again. "I felt my own mouth moving. Like the memory was checking whether I fit. Like it was trying me on."

The lamp guttered. Ember shifted in sleep, and a thread of smoke curled from one nostril, smelling of struck flint.

"There's more," Kestrel said. "There was a word in it. A title, maybe. *Speaker of the Royal Beast-Blood.* And it wasn't a description of somebody else, Serah. The memory looked straight through my eyes at me. It recognized me. Eleven days this creature has been alive, and its blood remembers my family better than I do."

* * *

They talked until the lamp died and relit it twice. Serah laid it out the way an Inquisitor would, because old habits were armor and Serah wore hers deliberately: facts in order, conclusions following like soldiers.

The courier had been carrying the map to someone. Not to anyone—to a specific kind of someone. He had pressed it into Kestrel's hands with his last breath and spoken a word in a language only beasts seemed to understand, and every beast within earshot had turned to look at Kestrel afterward. Kestrel had thought, at the time, that they were looking at the map.

"They weren't looking at the map," Serah said. "They were looking at you. You were already carrying whatever he gave you before the paper ever touched your hands."

"My mother's people were drovers," Kestrel said. "Before that, weavers. Before that—" They spread their hands. Nobody knew. The family tree lost its leaves somewhere in the great-grandparents' generation, pruned by silence, by the Sigil Wars, by the sensible survival policy of never writing down anything that could hang you. "My gift came from somewhere. Gifts don't come from nowhere. I just—I always assumed it was an accident. Bad luck, inherited."

"And now?"

"Now I think accidents don't come with titles attached." Kestrel looked down at the sleeping hatchling. Ember's claws flexed faintly, dream-running after something. "Speaker of the Royal Beast-Blood. If that's real, if that's what the door needs, then the courier didn't find me by chance either. Maps that shift. Words only beasts know. He was following the same river I am, upstream, toward the source." A laugh escaped them, ragged at the edges. "All those years learning to be invisible. Practicing small. Keeping my ledgers balanced and my voice down and my gift locked in a box so tight even I forgot where the key was. And the whole time I was—what? Designed? Bred? Set aside like a spare key in a drawer?"

"Do you hear yourself?" Serah said gently. "You just described yourself as a key."

"I know how I sound."

"How do you feel?"

Kestrel considered lying—they were, after all, extremely good at it—but Serah had this infuriating habit of simply waiting, and the firelight was warm, and the truth had been rattling around inside them since the nosebleed like a coin in a dry cup.

"Furious," they said. "Relieved. Terrified. Someone chose me before I was born, Serah. Chose my line, kept it, passed it down like a candle through dark rooms. My mother knew—or half-knew—and she taught me to smother it instead. All those years I thought she was teaching me cowardice. She was teaching me to survive until—" Until what? Until a dying courier fell through the wrong door? Until a hatchling in a cage in a free-port needed buying with the last of Odile's coin? "Until the door needed opening. And I don't get to be angry about it, because whoever lit this candle meant it kindly, and I don't get to be proud of it either, because I didn't earn it. I *inherited* it. My whole self is a bequest."

Serah reached across and took their hand. Ink-stained, Kestrel noticed. Hers were ink-stained too, from the ledger work she'd taken over since the burning.

"Here's what I know," she said. "I spent nine years in an organization that believed people could be designed. Assigned. Made into instruments of a purpose chosen by someone else. I signed papers that turned children into entries." Her thumb moved once over Kestrel's knuckles. "It's a lie, Kestrel. It was a lie when the Crown did it and it would be a lie if the dragons did it. Whatever your blood carries, it doesn't decide who you are. You decide what it's *for.*"

"That's a very comforting sentence."

"I have others. I've been saving them up."

Kestrel laughed despite everything, and Ember woke at the sound, lifted her head, and looked from Kestrel to Serah and back with an expression of profound draconic judgment, as though reviewing a performance and finding it adequate but overwrought.

Then the hatchling did something new. She climbed up onto Kestrel's shoulder, wrapped her tail around their neck like a scarf with intentions, and breathed out—not flame, not smoke, but a slow warm current of air that carried images the way her blood carried memories:

*A door. The pale archway, closer now, moss-hung, standing in a drowned field. On one side of it, darkness and forgetting. On the other side, the valley, dimming, grey at the edges, beautiful and fading like a photograph left in the sun.*

*And a keyhole shaped, unmistakably, like a human throat.*

"She's showing me where," Kestrel whispered. "She's showing me what's happening there. It's dying, Serah. Whatever's behind that door is going out like a lamp."

"Then we go," Serah said, already reaching for her boots, because of course she was; Serah processed grief and revelation at roughly the speed of a drawn sword. "How far?"

"Three days' walk. Two if the crow will talk to me, and the crow will talk to me, because I owe it a shiny debt and it knows where I live." Kestrel stood, knees protesting, hatchling riding their shoulder like the world's most dangerous epaulette. They crossed to the window and looked out at the free-port's lamplit chaos—at the banned books stacked in windows, the deposed kings' seals sold by weight, the city where everything forgotten came ashore eventually.

Somewhere beyond the world's edge, a country was guttering. Somewhere behind them, Odile was keeping secrets and possibly committing arson. Somewhere ahead, a door needed opening from both sides, and the blood in Kestrel's veins was apparently the hinge.

They had spent their whole life being nobody, meticulously, on purpose.

It was very difficult to be nobody when a dragon slept on your shoulder and your ancestors had signed a covenant in your name before the invention of the atlas.

"All right," Kestrel said softly, to Ember, to the memory-cathedral, to the ink-stained women dead a thousand years. "I'll carry it. But I'm carrying it my way."

Ember tightened her tail, once, like a handshake.

Behind them, Serah slung her pack and said, "There's a patrol at the east gate that knows my face, so we're leaving by the harbor, and Kestrel—you're smiling. Why are you smiling?"

"Because," Kestrel said, pulling their coat on over the hatchling, who made a pleased rumbling noise and settled in like she'd been doing it for years, "the crow owes me change."

They blew out the lamp, and went down the stairs together, out into the city of forgotten things, toward the door that had been waiting for them longer than either of their names.
