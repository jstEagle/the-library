## 19. Serah's Confession

The fire had burned down to that stage Serah privately thought of as *honest* — no longer performing cheerfulness, just doing its work, red and low and steady. Ember lay coiled at its edge like a cat who had discovered warmth and intended to sue for ownership of it, wings folded, one amber eye tracking the sparks that climbed. Beyond the firelight, the wild country they'd walked out of Vhalcrest's forgotten fringe was quiet in the way of places that had learned not to be noticed.

Serah cleaned her blades because her hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking.

Across the fire, Kestrel sat cross-legged with the map on their knees, though they weren't really looking at it anymore. They had that stillness Serah had learned to read the way she'd once read Inquisitor field-signals: the set of the shoulders, the particular angle of the chin. Kestrel was deciding something.

She should have known. She'd trained them to notice everything, in a sense — not literally, of course, but the whole shape of Kestrel Okonkwo was a person built by concealment, and people built by concealment always recognized other people carrying weight.

"You've been rehearsing something since noon," Kestrel said, without looking up. "You polish your second knife when you're avoiding a conversation. You did it four times today."

"I could have been thorough."

"You could have been thorough about the first knife too. You left it alone." Kestrel folded the map with exaggerated care, as if giving her time. "So. Whatever it is."

*Whatever it is.* As if there were one thing, contained, finite. Serah set the blade down across her knees and looked at the fire and found, to her complete lack of surprise, that twenty years of discipline offered absolutely nothing useful against this. She could face a patrol with a straight spine. She could lie to a captain with her pulse slowing. But this — this was the thing she'd been walking toward since the day she'd turned her back on the grey coats, and she had told herself all down the road that she would say it when the moment came, and now the moment had come and every word she owned had fled the province.

"Ember showed you things," she said. "About your bloodline. About why you."

"Yes."

"And you didn't ask me anything after. You just went quiet and made dinner."

"You make excellent decisions when you're quiet," Kestrel said. "I didn't want to interrupt."

It was gentle. That was the unbearable part. If Kestrel had pressed, if Kestrel had demanded, she could have armored up and held. Instead there was this patience, this terrible roomy patience, as if Kestrel were saying: *whenever you're ready, I'll still be here*, which was precisely what she did not deserve and precisely what she could not survive pretending away any longer.

"When I was nineteen," Serah said, "I lived in a village called Merrowdown. On the coast. You won't find it. That's rather the point."

Kestrel went very still. It was a different stillness than before. Serah kept her eyes on the fire because she could not do this and watch Kestrel's face at the same time; she only had courage enough for one impossible thing per sentence.

"It was a Speaker village. Not everyone — maybe one family in three carried some thread of the gift. My grandmother spoke to gulls. Actual conversations, gossip mostly, gulls are dreadful gossips. My mother spoke to whales. She'd stand on the headland in winter and talk to them while they went south, and they'd answer, and the sound would come up through her feet and out of her mouth in words, and I used to think—" Her voice caught. She let it. There was no point doing this halfway; a half-confession would be worse than none, an insult dressed as honesty. "I used to think it was the most beautiful thing in the world. And then I was recruited, and within two years I thought it was the most dangerous thing in the world, and both things were true, and nobody ever tells you that."

Ember stirred. The little wyvern lifted their head and looked at Serah with an attention far too old for their size, and Serah thought, absurdly, *good, witness this, someone should.*

"The Crown doesn't erase villages with armies," she went on. "Armies leave graves, and graves leave questions, and questions leave stories. They erase villages with paperwork. A census revision. A trade route redrawn so no one passes through anymore. And — orders of severance. Documents that unmake a place in law, so that officially it never existed, so that anyone who remembers it is either mistaken or mad or lying." She heard her own voice go flat and procedural and hated it and let it, because the flatness was the truest register she had; it was the voice they'd trained into her. "There were nine signatures required for a full erasure. Field officers, clerks of seal, a chaplain. I was junior liaison for the coastal district. My signature was the seventh."

The fire popped. Somewhere off in the dark, a night bird called and got no answer.

"They didn't ask me," Serah said. "That's the part I want you to understand, and the part I know doesn't matter, and I'm going to say both anyway. They didn't ask. They told. My commander put the document in front of me at a desk in Greyharbor with my mother's village name written on it in a clerk's tidy hand, and he said, *Sign, Vale*, and behind him stood two men whose job I understood without asking. And I signed." Her hands were steady. She'd never hated them more. "Because I was nineteen and afraid, and because I told myself a story in the space of about four heartbeats — that refusing would only get another name to sign it, that I could protect them better from inside the system, that my mother would want me safe. All lies. Good ones. The kind you can live on for years."

"Serah." Kestrel's voice was very soft.

"Let me finish. If I stop now I'll never start again." She swallowed. "Merrowdown wasn't destroyed. Nobody died. That's the mercy in it, if you can call it mercy — the people were relocated under settlement writs, scattered across six provinces, given new names in the rolls. My mother got a cottage and a stipend and a documented life that began at age forty-one, as if she'd been conjured out of nothing, and she never once spoke to a whale again, because the whales' waters were inside the erased boundary and speaking to anything inside an erased boundary is, by definition, speaking to nothing at all." The flatness cracked then, finally, and what came through underneath was worse than tears. "She wrote to me twice a year until she died. She signed her letters with her new name. She never once said *why*, Serena. Never asked me why. She knew. Of course she knew — she watched her own daughter wear the grey coat to the door the last time I visited. She just decided loving me didn't require the question. And I have spent eleven years trying to work out whether that was forgiveness or whether it was worse than condemnation, and I still don't know."

Silence. Ember crept closer, wing dragging, and laid their small head against Serah's boot with the grave solemnity of a creature who had understood every word, which — knowing wyverns — they probably had.

"And here's the rest," Serah said, "because you deserve the whole ledger, not the honest-sounding abridgment. When they gave me the erasure file afterward — we kept files, of course we did, the Crown erases countries but never its own paperwork — I read every name. Every relocated family. I memorized them. And for eleven years I told myself that was penance. That carrying the names was a kind of service to the dead memory of the place." She laughed, short and ugly. "It wasn't penance. Penance would have been signing my own name to something true. Carrying the list secretly was just — hoarding my guilt where no one could audit it. Even my defection. Even this. I tell myself I left the Inquisition because it was wrong. Some days I even believe it. But I walked into this guild hall the first time because the courier I failed — Tam, his name was Tam Weller, he deserved better than dying in a storeroom — because Tam had come to me with a map to a country the Crown deleted, and I thought, *there*. *There* is where the ledger gets balanced. I came to Vhalcrest for me, Kestrel. I've been traveling beside you because being near you feels like the closest thing to absolution I've ever touched. That's not love. Or — it is, but it's love with rot in the joists, and you deserve to know before you—"

"Before I what?" Kestrel said quietly.

*Before you stay.* She couldn't say it. She looked up instead, finally, and met Kestrel's eyes across the fire, and what she saw there undid her more thoroughly than any judgment could have, because Kestrel wasn't horrified. Kestrel was grieving. Grieving for her, apparently, which was so far outside the range of responses she'd prepared for that she had no drill for it at all.

"Do you understand what I'm telling you?" Serah said. "Every beast-speaker who ever stood in an Inquisitor hearing — every child taken in the night, every village dissolved — documents like mine made it legal. My hand is on one of them. Maybe more than one; I never dared look. You carry a gift that the paper I signed declared unreal. Your whole life of hiding exists downstream of desks like mine. And you're sitting there looking at me like—"

"Like someone who hid in a wall cavity for three days when she was nine years old," Kestrel said.

Serah stopped.

"I never told you the whole of it," Kestrel went on. "The neighbor boy. Jossen. They took him when we were children — you know that part, I've told you that part. What I've never told anyone is what happened before. The Inquisitors came for Jossen because a speaker's gift, when it surfaces young, calls to the beasts nearby, and the hounds hear it. And I heard it too. And I understood what it was, because my mother had taught me, and I could have warned him. Could have taught him to muzzle it the way I'd been taught to muzzle mine. I was nine and scared and a little bit jealous, honestly, because Jossen's gift sang and mine only whispered, and I said nothing. I stayed invisible. He was taken, and I stayed invisible, and it worked, and I have built my entire life on that single success." Kestrel's hands were folded in their lap, knuckles pale. "You signed a paper at nineteen with soldiers at your back. I did nothing at nine with nothing at my back but fear. We are both carrying the same debt, Serah. We're just wearing different uniforms for it."

"That's not the same."

"No," Kestrel agreed. "Yours is heavier. You were given power and it was misused against you. I was given none and chose cowardice. But we're keeping books in the same currency, and I will not sit here and let you present me a bill you refuse to present yourself." They rose, crossed the small space between bedrolls, and sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. Ember, sensing a shift in the weather, abandoned Serah's boot and poured themselves into Kestrel's lap like warm lead. "You want me to leave. I can see you've already composed my departure — you've probably got the route planned, you insufferable professional. You expect judgment, and you've arranged the scene so that receiving it will at least be tidy."

"It would be sensible," Serah said. Her voice came out wrecked.

"Sensible." Kestrel said the word the way Odile used to say it — with fond contempt, the word of ledgers and locked doors and lives measured in careful increments. "I spent thirty years being sensible. It got me a cot above a storeroom and a talent I wouldn't speak aloud to horses. Then a dying man put a word in my mouth that opened a door between worlds, and a dragon's blood turned out to be running in my veins, and I learned that the entire architecture of my sensible life was built on top of a fire somebody lit on purpose and meant me to tend." They shook their head slowly. "I'm done making decisions out of fear, Serah. Including the fearful decision to walk away from someone because they once, at nineteen, under threat, did a terrible thing and then spent eleven years paying for it in the only coin she had."

"It's not enough," Serah whispered. "Eleven years isn't enough. Nothing is enough. Merrowdown is still gone from the maps."

"Then it goes back on them," Kestrel said simply. "Not on your guilt. On us. On this." They touched the map case at Serah's hip, gently, as if it were a wound. "You keep saying you came to Vhalcrest to balance your ledger. Fine. Then help me do something no signature can undo. Help me put a country back into the world. And when it's back — write Merrowdown in yourself. In ink. With your own hand. I'll hold the lamp."

Serah hadn't known she was crying until Ember clicked their tongue at the drops landing on small scales, offended. She laughed despite everything — a broken, wet sound — and wiped her face with the heel of her hand like a soldier, because some habits kept a person upright.

"You should hate me a little," she said.

"Oh, I imagine I will, some mornings, over breakfast, for entirely different reasons," Kestrel said. "You fold maps wrong. You hum Inquisitor marching songs when you think no one's awake. You're unbearably smug about knots." A pause, and then softer, in the voice Kestrel saved for horses and frightened children and, lately, her: "But hatred requires me to believe you are only the worst thing you ever did. And I've watched you take a blade for a stranger's hatchling. I've watched you give your rations to Speakers you'd never met. I've watched you look at me like I was worth protecting when I hadn't earned it. I don't believe in a person made of one line in one ledger. Neither does anyone who's ever truly kept accounts. A ledger is a life, Serah — pages and pages of it. You don't judge the merchant by his worst entry. You see whether the debts are still being paid."

"They are," she managed. "Every day. Whether I want them to be or not."

"Then stay," Kestrel said. "Stay and pay them where they'll count."

They sat like that while the fire settled, shoulder to shoulder, the little wyvern snoring in Kestrel's lap with the shamelessness of the very young. Serah found that the thing she'd been carrying — not the guilt itself, that would never set down its weight, but the dread of being seen carrying it — had, somehow, been taken from her arms and set down between them, shared, halved, or perhaps more than halved. It was extraordinary what a person could survive once someone else knew the whole of it.

Later, when Kestrel slept, Serah lay awake and listened to the enormous silence beyond the fire and thought about her mother standing on the headland, talking south with the whales. *She never asked why.* She was beginning, at last, after eleven years, to suspect she'd finally worked out what that was. Not forgiveness. Not condemnation. Something larger than both: her mother had understood that love is not a verdict. Love keeps the account open.

Near dawn she slept, and dreamed of the sea, and woke to Kestrel shaking her shoulder with an expression she'd learned to dread.

"Serah," Kestrel said. "Wake up. Ember says there's smoke on the river road — and whatever's burning, the crows are calling it *grey coats*."
