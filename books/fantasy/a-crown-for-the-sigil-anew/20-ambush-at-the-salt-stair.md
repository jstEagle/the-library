## 20. Ambush at the Salt Stair

### Kestrel

The Salt Stair had been carved into the cliff face by people who clearly hated both travelers and themselves. Nine hundred steps, the ferryman had said, grinning through a mouthful of black teeth, cut by monks who wanted to be closer to heaven and further from everyone else.

"Nine hundred," Serah repeated as they stood at the bottom, looking up at the switchbacks stitched against the rock like pale scars. "And we're carrying what, exactly?"

"Supplies." Kestrel adjusted their pack. "Ember."

"I'm carrying Ember."

"You're carrying Ember *and* supplies. I'm carrying the map and my enormous guilt about making you carry everything else."

Ember, riding in the sling across Serah's back like a very smug infant, chirped agreement. The wyvern hatchling had grown in the weeks since the free-port—no longer small enough to fit in a coat pocket, now roughly the size of a well-fed cat, with wings that worked in theory and opinions that worked in practice. Mostly the opinion was hunger.

They had made it perhaps two hundred steps when Kestrel's stomach dropped.

It wasn't the height. It was the gulls—or rather, the absence of them. The cliff should have been loud with them, wheeling and screaming over the river below. Instead the air was silent in the specific, deliberate way of a room where someone has just stopped talking because they heard footsteps.

"Serah." Kestrel kept their voice low. "The birds are gone."

Serah didn't turn around. Her hand drifted to her sword hilt with the unhurried ease of someone who had spent years doing this for a living. "How long?"

"Since the last switchback. Maybe longer." Kestrel closed their eyes and reached out—not far, just a whisper, just enough. The rocks below held nothing but crabs and barnacles. But above—

Above, on the clifftop, something was holding its breath. Several somethings. Boots, mostly. And dogs.

"Hounds," Kestrel breathed. "A dozen men, maybe more. They've been following us since the ferry. They let us get on the stair first." Their mouth went dry. "It's not an ambush, Serah. It's a trap with better manners."

"Ambushes have manners too. That's what makes them ambushes." Serah was already moving, pressing Kestrel back against the cliff wall, scanning upward. The stair narrowed here, hemmed between stone and a drop that ended in white water. "Options?"

"We can't go up. We can't go down fast enough. We can't fly—" Kestrel glanced at Ember, "—*yet*, presumably, if you'd care to work on that."

Ember hissed, deeply offended.

"Then we fight," Serah said simply.

"There are twelve of them."

"Eleven, if one of them is Vess. She counts herself separately. Always has." Something flickered across Serah's face—not fear exactly, but the particular grimness of someone meeting an old colleague. "Kestrel. Listen to me. If it goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it goes wrong, you take Ember and you jump. There's a pool below the falls, I saw it from the ferry. Deep enough."

"You're coming with us."

"Kestrel—"

"You're coming with us or I'm not jumping, and then we'll *both* be inconveniently dead, and how would that look in the ledgers?"

Despite everything—the silence above, the drop below, the eleven or twelve soldiers preparing to descend upon them—Serah laughed. It was a short, rough sound, startled out of her, and Kestrel thought: *I would cross nine hundred more stairs to hear that again.*

Then the first crossbow bolt buried itself in the rock where Kestrel's head had been a heartbeat before, and there was no more time for anything.

### Vess

Captain Ilsette Vess had pursued heretics through burning granaries, smugglers through sewers thick enough to chew, and one memorable apostate through a wedding feast, during which she had been hit with a cake. She considered herself a patient woman. A thorough woman. A woman whose faith in the Crown's order was a load-bearing wall.

So it troubled her—not greatly, not yet, but it *troubled* her—that the beast-speaker they were hunting kept apologizing to the mules.

She had watched from the ridge above the ferry crossing while Okonkwo waited for the boat, murmuring to the ferryman's draft animals, and she had seen the mules' ears turn toward the speaker like flowers toward sun. She had told herself: *trickery. Trained animals. Coin changing hands.* The doctrine was clear. Beast-speech was not communion; it was corruption. The speaker did not befriend the animal. The speaker *bent* it.

But the mules hadn't looked bent. They'd looked *listened to.*

"Captain." Sergeant Brohm crouched beside her at the lip of the cliff, his grey coat dark with sea-damp. "Targets on the stair, third switchback. The deserter Vale is armed. Okonkwo has no visible weapon. The animal is—" he paused, visibly searching for the correct word, "—a lizard."

"A wyvern, Sergeant. Wyverns are fire-kin. Do not treat it as a lizard."

"Yes, Captain. It's wearing a sling."

"I can see that, Brohm."

Below, on the narrow stair, the fugitives had pressed themselves against the cliff face. Good position, Vess noted professionally. Bad odds. Eleven crossbows above them, the river below, and nowhere to go but down.

She raised her hand to signal the advance—and hesitated.

It was the hesitation itself that disturbed her most. In six years of service she had never once hesitated at a signal. Doctrine did not hesitate. Doctrine said: the gift is outlawed, the outlawed must be taken, the taken must be questioned, and the questioning was not spoken of afterward even among Inquisitors, which was how you knew it was necessary.

But she had read the courier's body before they burned it. She had seen the wounds and known they were not made by any blade the Crown issued. Someone had wanted the map badly enough to torture for it, and the courier had died without giving it up, and Vess had lain awake that night thinking: *the Crown's enemies do not torture. Only we are permitted to. So who—*

She put the thought away. She was good at putting thoughts away. It was practically a skill listed in her commission.

"Take them alive," she said. "Both of them. The animal too."

"And if they resist?"

"Then remind them of their options, Sergeant. Loudly. I want Okonkwo to hear every word." She started down the cliff path toward the upper switchback, boots finding the worn steps without looking. "Desperate people do desperate things when they think there's no way out. I'd rather they believed there was one."

Brohm blinked at her. "That's... merciful, Captain."

"It's *efficient*," Vess said, and did not examine why the correction tasted like a lie.

### Kestrel

The bolts came down like iron rain, and the stair became a lesson in arithmetic: eleven shooters, two of them, one lizard, and a vertical drop with a swimming pool at the bottom of questionable depth.

Serah moved like the answer to a question Kestrel hadn't finished asking. She caught the second volley's rhythm—reload, loose, reload—and pulled Kestrel along the wall in the gaps, her free hand hard around their wrist, her voice counting under her breath. "Now. Now. *Move.*"

A soldier rappelled down past them on a rope, sword out, and got exactly close enough to learn that Serah Vale remembered everything the Inquisitors ever taught her, including the parts about how Inquisitors parry. He went over the edge with a yelp, caught the rope, and dangled there, reconsidering his career.

"Sorry!" Kestrel called after him, because apparently their mouth had decided honesty was the policy today. "Nothing personal!"

"Stop *apologizing* to people trying to kill us!" Serah snapped.

"You apologized to the toll-keeper last week!"

"He was *charging us double* and he *knew it!*"

Another rope. Two more soldiers descending. Above, a voice like cold slate cut through the chaos: "*Hold positions. Alive, I said.*" Captain Vess. Kestrel had heard her only once, in the market, asking gentle terrible questions—but a voice like that you didn't forget. It was the voice of someone who had never once needed to shout.

"They're herding us down," Serah panted. "Toward the lower switchback. It dead-ends at a ledge."

"Wonderful. A ledge. My favorite."

"Kestrel—"

"I know. The pool. Jump. Take Ember." Kestrel's heart was hammering somewhere up near their teeth. "You first. You take Ember down, I'll follow—"

"I'm not leaving you on this stair with eleven crossbows—"

"*Ten,*" corrected a new voice, high and furious and entirely not human.

Everything stopped. Even the crossbows wavered.

Ember had climbed out of the sling onto Serah's shoulder, wings spread to their full and frankly unimpressive span, and was glaring up the cliff face with eyes like struck flint. And from somewhere inside the small scaly body came heat—a shimmer in the air, a smell of struck matches, a glow building in the hatchling's throat like a swallowed sunrise.

"Oh," Kestrel said faintly. "Oh, that's new."

"Ember," Serah said carefully, in the tone one uses near a lit fuse, "sweetheart, we talked about indoor voices—"

Ember was not, historically, a dragon. Wyverns were fire-*kin*, the books said, distant cousins twice removed, the sort of relations who showed up at funerals and ate all the cheese. Nobody had expected flame. Ember had never produced so much as a spark. Ember's greatest achievement to date was melting the handle off a soup ladle through sheer force of sulking.

But the hatchling looked up the cliff at the grey coats, at the crossbows, at the woman in the captain's insignia standing at the top—and something older than Ember woke up behind those flint eyes. Ancestral memory, Kestrel realized. The same current that had shown them the covenant. Blood remembering blood.

"*Ten,*" Ember repeated, and breathed.

Not much flame. Honestly, more of a concentrated belch of it—a cone of orange maybe three feet long, crackling and sputtering like a wet bonfire catching. But it caught the nearest rope full in the middle, and hemp burns, and the soldier on it descended considerably faster than planned, and the whole upper switchback erupted into shouting as men scrambled away from falling comrades and smoldering lines.

"The ledge!" Serah shouted. "Go, go, GO—"

They ran. Down the switchbacks, three at a time, Kestrel's boots skidding on salt-crusted stone, Ember clinging to Serah's shoulders and radiating heat and triumph. Behind them the stair filled with smoke and swearing. Ahead, the ledge jutted out over the falls, and beyond it the white water thundered down into the pool Serah had marked from the ferry.

Kestrel reached the ledge first, turned to help Serah—and saw the soldier.

He had come down the outside of the cliff, alone, quiet, a knife in his fist instead of a sword, and he had timed it perfectly. He stepped out of the rock shadow directly behind Serah, arm already swinging, blade angled for the space between her shoulder blades—

For the space where Kestrel stood.

There was no time to shout. There was no time for anything. Later, Kestrel would replay it a thousand times and find it lasted less than a breath: Serah turning, seeing, understanding—and stepping *into* the blow instead of away from it, putting her own body between the knife and Kestrel with the simple mechanical certainty of a door closing.

The blade went in under her ribs.

The sound she made was small. That was the worst part. Not a cry—just a small punched-out sound, almost surprise, like someone who'd been told the bill was less than expected.

Serah killed him anyway. Even with the knife in her, even folding around the wound, her sword took him across the legs and put him down hard, and then her knees went, and Kestrel caught her, and the world went narrow and ringing.

"No. No no no—hey—hey, look at me, that's cheating, you don't get to—" Kestrel's hands were pressing the wound and the blood was coming up between their fingers, hot and shocking, far too fast. "Ember! Ember, keep them off us, whatever you've got left—"

Ember planted themself on the ledge and hissed fire at the stairhead, thin and stuttering but enough, enough to make the soldiers recoil.

"Pool," Serah gritted. Her face was grey as her old uniform. "Jump. Both of us. Now."

"You can't swim like this—"

"Then you'll have to be *very* motivating."

Behind them, boots on the stair. Vess's voice, closer now, sharp with something that might have been alarm: "*Hold! HOLD, damn you—I want them ALIVE—*"

Kestrel got Serah's arm over their shoulders. The ledge ended in air. Below, the pool churned white and brown and promised nothing.

"Together," Kestrel said.

"Together," Serah agreed, and smiled—actually smiled, blood on her teeth, the absolute menace—and they jumped.

### Vess

Vess reached the ledge ninety seconds too late and stood looking down at the churning pool, at the two heads bobbing in the current, at the small winged shape circling overhead shrieking what could only be described as parental abuse.

"Sergeant. Report."

"Two in the water, Captain. The deserter's wounded—there's blood in the current. The animal attacked with... fire, Captain. Actual fire. I've written it down."

"See that you spell it correctly." Vess watched the river carry them downstream, past the falls, around the bend, gone. She could send riders along the bank. She could probably catch them by nightfall.

She found she was in no hurry.

"Captain?" Brohm was watching her sideways. "Your orders?"

Vess looked at the knife lying on the ledge—one of hers, unissued, unmarked, the kind of blade that didn't exist in any armory ledger. She picked it up and turned it over slowly.

Her men had orders to take the fugitives alive. She had given those orders herself, loudly, twice. And yet a man had come down the outside of the cliff, alone, with a knife that left no questions to be asked.

Someone in her company wanted Okonkwo dead, and it wasn't for the Crown's reasons. Which meant either her orders were being countermanded from above—which happened, quietly, sometimes, and was never spoken of—or someone feared what a living beast-speaker might say.

Or what a living ex-Inquisitor might say.

"Break camp," Vess said at last. "We follow the river. At walking pace."

"Walking pace, Captain? They'll—"

"They have a wounded woman, a lizard with indigestion, and a fugitive who apologizes to mules, Sergeant. They aren't going far." She pocketed the knife. "And Brohm—if anyone asks, we lost them at the falls. Let the paperwork say what keeps everyone comfortable."

Brohm stared at her. "Captain... is everything quite all right?"

"Everything is doctrinally sound," said Vess, and walked away before her face could disagree.

### Kestrel

The river spat them out half a mile downstream, bruised, soaked, and alive. Kestrel dragged Serah onto the shingle bank and got her out of her soaked coat with hands that wouldn't stop shaking.

The wound was bad. Not immediately fatal—Kestrel had grown up around a guild that collected impossible things, including a surgeon with strong opinions—but deep, and dirty with river water, and Serah's skin was already flushing hot around it.

"Fever by tonight," Kestrel muttered, tearing a shirt into bandages. "Infection by tomorrow. Wonderful. Excellent. This is fine."

"Kestrel." Serah's voice was slurred at the edges but her eyes were clear. "The knife. Did you see it?"

"I saw it."

"That wasn't a patrol tactic. That was an *assassination*. Vess doesn't work that way—she's rigid, she's insufferable, but she plays fair. Someone else wants you dead." Serah coughed. "Someone who knew we'd be on that stair."

Kestrel tied off the bandage and sat back on their heels, river roaring beside them, Ember curled against Serah's side radiating worried warmth like a feverish hearthstone. Somewhere upstream, pursuit regrouped. Somewhere ahead, a drowned borderland waited, and a door that opened from both sides, and a dragon guttering toward darkness.

And Serah, who had taken a blade meant for them without a moment's calculation, watching Kestrel with fading eyes and trying very hard to stay awake.

"Stay with me," Kestrel said. "That's an order. I'm told I give those now."

"Mm. Terrible at it." Serah's eyes were closing. "You apologize to mules..."

"I will apologize to *anything* if you stay awake. I'll apologize to the river. River, I'm sorry. See? Devastating authority."

But Serah was sliding under, the fever climbing, and the light was going gold and then grey, and Kestrel looked down the river at the gathering dark and understood, with terrible clarity, that they could not stop, could not rest, could not carry her alone—and that the stories said there was a witch who lived on this stretch of water, a healer who took payment in the one coin nobody wanted to spend.

Memories. True ones.

Kestrel looked at Serah's face, slack with fever, and at Ember, and at the road ahead, and began, quietly, to count what they could afford to lose.

The river, indifferent, carried the answer downstream ahead of them.
