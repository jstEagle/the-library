## 21. The Healer Who Charges Memories

The river-witch's house stood on stilts above the brown water like a heron that had decided, after long consideration, to become a building.

Kestrel saw it first from the reed shallows where they'd beached the stolen skiff, Serah burning in the bottom of it. Fever had painted her a color Kestrel didn't like—too grey at the edges, too bright at the center—and every bump of the hull against the water made her hiss through her teeth and grip the wound below her ribs as though she could hold herself together by force of stubbornness alone.

"Hold on," Kestrel said, for perhaps the hundredth time.

"Wasn't planning," Serah managed, "on letting go."

Ember rode in the crook of Kestrel's arm, small enough still to pass for an unusual cat, hot as a fresh loaf of bread. The hatchling's eyes were fixed on the stilt-house with an intensity that Kestrel had learned to read as *something is there*. Ember did not look at ordinary things that way. Ordinary things did not make dragons' blood sit up and take notice.

The house was built of driftwood and river-cane, hung with drying herbs and stranger things: bundles of feathers sorted by color, jars of river-mud labeled in careful script, a wind chime made of spoons. Smoke rose from a chimney that bent twice before letting go of its burden, as if even the smoke here moved cautiously.

A woman appeared on the porch without appearing to have walked there. She was old in the way rivers are old—not wrinkled so much as *worn smooth*, with white hair braided into ropes thick as mooring lines and skin dark as wet clay. Her eyes were the pale gold of shallow water over sand.

"You've brought me bleeding meat," she said.

"I've brought you my friend," Kestrel said. "She took a blade meant for me."

"Mm." The witch looked down at the skiff, at Serah. "Inquisitor."

"Former," Serah said, from between her teeth.

"They all say that eventually. Some mean it sooner than others." The witch turned and went inside without another word, which Kestrel chose to interpret as an invitation.

Getting Serah up the ladder was the worst quarter hour of Kestrel's life so far, which given recent history was saying something. Serah refused help until she physically could not, then hated accepting it, then stopped having the strength to hate anything. Kestrel half-carried, half-dragged her across a floor strewn with river stones worn round and smooth, and laid her on a low bed that smelled of crushed mint and something older underneath, something like rain on stone.

The witch knelt beside Serah without ceremony. Her hands hovered over the wound but did not touch it.

"Blade went deep. Missed the liver by a kindness. But it was Inquisitor steel?" She glanced up, and her gold eyes caught Kestrel's and held them. "I can smell it. They quench those blades in something nasty. It doesn't want to let go of what it cuts."

"Yes," Kestrel said.

"It will kill her by tomorrow night if it stays. I can pull it out." The witch sat back on her heels. "But nothing in this world comes free, child, least of all healing. Especially not healing. You know what I charge?"

"Heard stories," Kestrel said carefully. "Downriver. They said you take memories."

The witch smiled, and it was not a cruel smile, but it was not a kind one either. It was the smile of someone who had done this arithmetic ten thousand times and never once enjoyed it. "One true memory each. From each person who asks something of me. Not the worst memory—that would be easy, everyone has garbage they'd pay to lose. A *true* one. One you'd keep. That's the coin that actually costs."

"And if we refuse?"

"Then I'll give her poppy and clean bandages and my honest condolences, and you can bury her under the willow downstream. Nice spot. I'll waive the fee for the burial." She said it without malice, which somehow made it worse.

Kestrel looked at Serah.

Serah looked back. There was fever-brightness in her eyes now, and beneath it something steadier—the look of a woman doing sums. "Do it," she said.

"Not yet—" Kestrel began.

"Kestrel." Serah's hand found theirs, gripped with what strength remained. "I have spent eleven years being an Inquisitor. Do you know how few true memories I'm attached to? Take stock of yours first. Then decide. That's an order."

"You're not my commanding officer anymore."

"No," Serah said, and her mouth curved faintly despite everything. "I'm your partner. Same thing, better hours."

* * *

The witch—who gave her name only as Nen, and said her surname had been eaten by the river decades ago and she hadn't missed it—set her terms formally, because formality mattered, she explained. Magic was like a ledger. Sloppy entries came due with interest.

"You each choose while you're whole," she said. "You speak it aloud to me, once, in full detail, so I know exactly what I'm taking. Then I take it. You won't remember giving it. You'll just find a hole where it was, smooth-edged, like a tooth that came out clean. You may spend years poking at it with your tongue." She looked at Kestrel. "Some people regret. Most don't, in the end. The ones who regret are the ones who tried to cheat me with a false memory. Those come back worse."

"How do you use them?" Kestrel asked. "The memories. What do you *do* with them?"

Nen's expression flickered—grief, quickly smoothed. "I drink them," she said simply. "It's how I stay alive long past my season. Every memory is a little life, and I am very thirsty, and the river keeps asking me why I'm still here. So I answer it: because I remember other people's living." She shrugged, vast shoulders moving like a hillside settling. "It is not noble. It is not wicked. It is a price. Now. Choose."

Kestrel sat on a stool by the window and did the hardest inventory of their life.

They thought of the map, folded against their heart. Needed. They thought of Odile's face, the last time they'd seen it, lit orange by the guild's burning—no, that wasn't a memory they could give; it was a debt, and debts weren't currency. They thought of their mother's warning voice, *never let them hear you*, and flinched away from it. Too load-bearing. Take that beam out and the whole house of Kestrel Okonkwo came down.

They thought, unwillingly, of smaller things. The neighbor boy, Tovin, laughing with a squirrel on his shoulder the day before the grey coats took him. No. That one was a scar, but scars told you where not to touch.

And then, unbidden, it rose: the guild's hearth-song.

Every Impossible Things child learned it before they learned their letters. Old Marta had composed it, supposedly, though Odile claimed it predated the guild itself, that it had been sung in waystations and smugglers' dens for two hundred years—a song for people with no country, no family name worth writing down. Six notes rising, four falling, then a pause exactly long enough for someone to squeeze your hand. Kestrel had heard it on their first terrified night in the guild, age seven, sobbing into a blanket that smelled of cedar and lamp oil, and the whole common room had picked it up around them without stopping their card game or their mending or their argument about whether bottled storms appreciated in value. Six notes rising, four falling, a pause, and a hand-squeeze. It meant *you are among us*. It meant *no one here needs to know what you are to love you anyway*.

They had hummed it to themselves in cellars and ditches for twenty years. It was the smallest thing they owned and the truest.

"Oh," Nen said softly, watching their face. "You've found it."

"There's a song," Kestrel said. Their voice came out rough. "The guild's hearth-song. My—my mother found family sang it. Sing it." They closed their eyes. "Six notes going up, four coming down. And then a pause, and in the pause, someone holds your hand. It means you belong somewhere. It's the only place I've ever belonged. It's—" Their throat closed. They pushed through. "It's the truest thing I have."

Silence. When Kestrel opened their eyes, Nen was looking at them with an expression Kestrel couldn't parse—something old and tired and almost tender.

"That one hurts to take," Nen said. "Are you certain?"

"No," Kestrel said honestly. "But do it anyway. Before I get smart."

* * *

Serah's turn was harder to witness.

She lay propped against pillows, sweat-soaked but lucid now, the poppy Nen had given her for pain apparently sharpening rather than dulling her. She stared at the ceiling beams for a long time before speaking.

"I have to pick something true," she said, mostly to herself. "That's the joke, isn't it. I spent eleven years building a life out of lies and duty, and now I need one true thing to pay with, and I'm—" She laughed, a short ugly sound. "There aren't many candidates."

"Take your time," Kestrel said.

"The Order trained us to let go of attachments. Called it *clarity*. We memorized our families' faces specifically so we could report them accurately if they ever became suspects, and then we were taught to file them away." Her jaw worked. "My mother. Maren Vale. She baked bread on Thursdays and sang off-key and thought I joined the Inquisition because I wanted to protect people, and she was proud of me, gods help her, she was *proud*, and I never once corrected her because the truth would have broken her heart." Serah's breath shuddered. "I can see her face right now. Flour on her cheekbone. The crease between her brows when she worried, which was always. The exact way her smile went crooked when she lied about liking a gift." She looked at Kestrel, and her eyes were wet and furious. "If I give her up, I won't grieve her properly. I won't even know what I lost. Is that—is that a fair trade for staying alive? Because it feels like theft."

"From whom?" Nen asked quietly.

"From her. From the version of me that loved her."

Nen was silent a moment. "Child," she said at last, "I have taken ten thousand memories. Mothers' faces, wedding days, the smell of a dead man's coat. And here is what I know, and I'll tell you for free: love does not live in any single memory. It lives in the shape the memories leave behind. You'll forget her face and keep loving her, and it will ache worse and stranger, like a word on the tip of your tongue forever. That is the cost. I won't dress it up."

Serah wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Fine," she said. "Maren Vale. My mother. Her face. All of it—I want to describe all of it so you understand what you're taking." And she did, voice cracking, flour on the cheekbone, the crooked smile, the Thursday bread, the pride, until Nen raised a hand gently and said, *Enough. I have it.*

* * *

The taking itself was almost gentle.

Nen had them lie down side by side—Serah on the bed, Kestrel on a pallet dragged close, because she insisted the payments happen together, "so the ledger balances in one entry." She pressed one palm to each of their foreheads and began to murmur something in a language that sounded like water negotiating with stones. Ember, curled at the foot of the bed, grew very still and watched the witch with ancient, unblinking eyes.

Kestrel felt the six notes rise and the four fall somewhere far away, and felt a hand squeeze theirs in a pause that stretched—

—and then there was a hole.

Not painful. That was the horror of it. Smooth-edged, exactly as promised. Kestrel knew, intellectually, that there had been a song, that they had loved it, that they had just paid it. But reaching for it was like reaching for a stair that wasn't there in a house they'd lived in all their life. The foot swung out into nothing. Around them, dimly, they heard Serah make a sound—a small, lost sound, the sound of someone groping along a wall for a doorframe that had been removed—and Kestrel's own hand found hers and gripped it, hard, in a pause whose meaning they no longer knew but whose *shape* they still felt, like a fossil of feeling.

They lay like that for a long time, holding hands over a shared absence.

When Kestrel finally spoke, their voice was strange to them. "What was it? The thing we just—what did it feel like?"

Serah was quiet. "Warm," she said at last. "Whatever it was. It felt warm. And I think—" She frowned, chasing the toothless socket of it. "I think we used to do this. Hold hands. I think there was a reason."

"There was," Kestrel said. "We just can't afford to know it."

* * *

Nen worked through the evening, and her craft was genuinely astonishing to watch. She drew the poison from the wound in threads of grey light that she caught in a jar of river-water, where they writhed like drowned hair. She packed the cut with moss that glowed faintly green and sang—actually sang—in a register only Ember seemed to fully enjoy. By midnight Serah's fever had broken. By dawn her color was human again, and she slept the dreamless sleep of the truly mended.

Kestrel did not sleep. They sat by the window instead, watching the river carry the night away, and poked at the absence where the song had been, and found, to their surprise, that something had survived the extraction after all.

Not the melody. Not the words. But the *gesture*—the hand-squeeze in the pause—had been woven so deeply into them that even Nen's clean pull had left its shadow. When Serah stirred in the night, Kestrel reached over without thinking and squeezed her hand, and some animal part of them knew it meant something, knew it was a sentence in a grammar they'd forgotten, and the not-knowing hurt in a way that was almost bearable because it proved the meaning had been real.

*You are among us,* the ghost of the gesture said. *Whoever you are. Whatever you've done.*

Kestrel held onto that ghost all night like a coal carried against the wind.

Near dawn, Nen appeared beside them with two cups of something bitter and steaming, and sat down on the floorboards with a groan, suddenly looking every one of her impossible years.

"You'll want to know what I saw," she said. "In the memories. I always see them, as I take them. It's part of the drinking." She sipped. "Yours had fire in it. A big room full of people, and a song, and a girl with a burn scar teaching a child to whistle. Warm. Very warm. I'll keep it well." She glanced sideways. "Hers had bread. And guilt underneath the bread, layer after layer of it, like river silt. That woman forgave her daughter things the daughter hasn't confessed to anyone, including herself. Including, I suspect, whatever she's carrying toward you." Nen's gold eyes were unreadable. "Interesting cargo you two are hauling north."

"We're not hauling anything," Kestrel said automatically. "We're just traveling together."

"Mm," said Nen, in the exact tone Odile used when a customer swore a bottled storm was harmless. Then she set down her cup and leaned forward, and the humor drained out of her face entirely.

"One more thing, and this one's free, because I like you, and because you paid me honestly when cheating would have been easy. Last month, a grey boat came downriver. Inquisitors, hunting a beast-speaker. They asked after the drowned borderlands, and after a courier, and after—" she tapped her own temple— "*listeners*. I told them nothing, because I owe the Crown less than nothing. But they left a thing behind. A notice, nailed to the ferry post across the water." She reached into her shawl and produced a folded paper, soft with damp. "I kept it. Seemed like the sort of thing someone might eventually pay me for, and here you are."

Kestrel unfolded it. The paper was official Crown stock; the ink was precise and cold.

A woodcut portrait—outdated, but unmistakable. Their own face. Below it, the charges, and below the charges, a line that stopped Kestrel's breath:

*Approach with extreme caution. Subject believed capable of commanding beasts. Lethal force authorized. Captain Vess requests the subject be taken alive if practicable—for questioning regarding the Vhalcrest matter.*

"They know where you're going," Nen said. "Not the route—they're not that smart—but the destination. Someone talked. Someone high up." She nodded toward the sleeping form on the bed. "Your Inquisitor's old colleagues have been ahead of you since the ambush. That blade she took—it wasn't chance, child. They knew who'd throw herself in front of you."

Outside, the river ran brown and patient toward the drowned borderlands. Ember woke, stretched wings still too small for the body they dreamed of, and fixed their gaze north with a hunger that needed no translation.

Kestrel folded the notice and put it away, next to the map, next to the hole where a song used to be.

"Wake her," they said. "We leave within the hour."
