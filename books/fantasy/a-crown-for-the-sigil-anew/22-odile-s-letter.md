## 22. Odile's Letter

The cell smelled of wet stone and other people's despair, which Odile Vann considered, on the whole, an insult to her nose.

She had been in worse places. She had, in fact, been in this exact place before—thirty-one years ago, a girl of nineteen caught with a forged bill of lading and a smile too quick for the watch to trust—and she remembered thinking then what she thought now: whoever designed Inquisitor cells had never once needed to use a privy at night. The architecture of cruelty was always so careless with its own comforts.

They had taken her boots. That was the part that galled. Not the manacles, not the interrogation lamp with its smug little oil reservoir, not Captain Vess's voice like a ledger being read aloud by someone who hated ledgers. The boots. Her good boots. Oiled leather, double-stitched, bought from a cobbler in Lowmarsh who owed her a favor she'd never called in until that day.

"They'll want you to talk," said the guard on the second evening, a young man with chapped hands and the posture of someone who'd been told he was intimidating so many times he'd started to believe it.

"I know," Odile said pleasantly. "Everyone wants something from me. It's the story of my life."

He blinked at her, thrown off his script, and left without asking anything after all. Small victories. She collected them the way some people collected coins: obsessively, and against the day they might be spent.

* * *

The interrogations came twice a day, morning and evening, like meals she refused to eat.

Captain Vess was not what Odile expected. She had imagined a brute; she got a bookkeeper. Vess sat across the little table with her grey coat buttoned to the throat and asked questions in order, numbered, patient, as though Odile were a shipment whose manifest didn't quite add up. Where did the courier come from? Who else saw him? What did Kestrel Okonkwo carry out of the guild the night of the fire?

"You have my statement," Odile said for the eleventh time. "I wrote it out myself. You can check my penmanship against the guild ledgers—I'm very consistent."

"Your statement says the courier died of his wounds before speaking."

"He did."

"Your statement says you don't know what he carried."

"I'm sixty-two years old, Captain. I've watched four hundred people die in my storeroom or near enough to it, and I'll tell you what I've learned: the dying don't hand you things. They hold on. Clutching is the last luxury anyone can afford." She folded her hands atop the table, manacles clinking, and gave Vess the smile she used on customers trying to return items clearly marked *no returns*. "You're asking me to believe a dying man performed a delivery."

Vess wrote something down. She always wrote something down, even when Odile said nothing worth writing, which Odile suspected was itself a technique—the illusion that every silence was being converted into evidence.

"The fire," Vess said. "Origin undetermined. Your cellar stores show accelerant residue."

"Oil lamps fall over. It happens in buildings where people work late."

"Three lamps fell over in three separate rooms?"

"Clumsy building," Odile said. "It takes after me."

Something flickered at the corner of Vess's mouth—not quite a smile, more like a muscle remembering it had once been capable of one. Odile noted it the way she noted everything: filed under *assets*, sub-filed under *possibly exploitable*.

Because here was the thing about Odile Vann that the Crown had never understood, despite holding her in a cell with her name chalked on the door: she had built her life on the principle that everyone has a price, and the price is almost never money. The guard with the chapped hands wanted to be seen. The cook who brought her food wanted someone to complain to. And Captain Vess—Vess wanted, more than anything Odile could yet name, for the numbers to balance. For the world to be a ledger that reconciled.

Odile intended to give her a column that wouldn't.

* * *

On the ninth day, they moved her.

Not to a deeper cell but a higher one—a room in the tower above the old customs house, with a window. A real window, barred, but facing the harbor, and Odile understood immediately that this was either a reward for compliance or a test of it, and that the distinction mattered very little because either way there was a window, and through a barred window, if you were patient, if you were Odile Vann, you could conduct business.

The gulls found her on the second morning.

Harbor gulls are the merchants of the sky—greedy, shameless, fluent in exchange—and Odile had been feeding them scraps of her bread through the bars since the first week, flicking crumbs onto the outer sill, establishing herself in their ledger as a reliable account. A bird does not love you. A bird simply knows where the bread comes from. This was, in Odile's professional opinion, the only honest relationship in the world.

By the twelfth day, one gull in particular—a scarred old hen with one pale eye and the air of a woman who had survived three marriages—would land on the sill and wait. Odile talked to her. Not beast-speech; Odile had no gift, only the next best thing, which was thirty years of trading with creatures who wanted something. She told the gull about the bread. She told her about the tower. She held up strips of salt pork saved from supper and let the bird learn the arithmetic: sill, patience, pork.

Then, on the thirteenth night, by the light of the interrogation lamp she'd learned to coax into a low steady glow, Odile Vann sat down to write a letter.

Paper was the problem. They gave her none. But the Inquisitors had made one error, the same error institutions always make: they gave her *forms*. Confession forms, property inventories, statements of witness—all blank, all hers to fill out, all written on paper soft enough to fold small. She chose the inventory of the burned guild's recovered goods, because there was a certain poetry in it, and began drafting on the back in ink she'd been saving from her supper tray, thickened on the windowsill until it wrote dark.

She drafted three times. The first draft was angry. The second was weepy. Both went into the lamp flame, because a letter that makes the reader feel sorry for you is a letter that gets them killed. The third draft she wrote the way she'd taught Kestrel to write trade terms: short, clear, and impossible to misread even by someone hoping to misunderstand.

*Little Wren.*

*If you're reading this, then the gull found you, which means you're near water, which means you took the river road, which means you listened to me when I told you never to take the river road, which is the most proud I've ever been of you.*

*Kestrel. Listen now, because I will say this once and once only, and then we will both pretend I never said it, because sentiment is a currency that devalues fast.*

*Vhalcrest is real.*

*I know. Sit down. Have a biscuit. Then keep reading.*

*Twenty-three years ago my sister Maren stood in my office exactly where you used to stand, holding a map she wouldn't show me, talking about a country that wasn't on any chart. I told her she was chasing a rumor. I told her the Crown had burned every record, and things the Crown burns stay burned. She laughed at me—Maren always laughed, it was her worst quality and the reason I loved her—and she said, "Odile, you deal in impossible things. Why do you flinch at the biggest one?"*

*She took a boat north. I never got a letter. For twenty-three years I told myself she was dead, because dead is a thing you can put in a ledger and close the book on. Missing is a page that never stops turning.*

*Here is what I know now, from things said in your hearing and mine, from papers Vess waves about thinking I can't read upside down, from twenty years of buying rumors the way other women buy ribbon: the Crown didn't erase Vhalcrest because it was false. They erased it because it was TRUE, and truth, properly tended, is the most dangerous commodity there is.*

*There is a dragon there. Old. Older than the Sigil Wars, maybe older than the wars' causes. Its fire doesn't just warm—it BURNS AWAY LIES. That's what the old records say, the ones the guild kept locked in the iron chest under the floor of my office, third board, the one that squeaks. Yes, I had records. Yes, I knew. No, I didn't tell you, and if you're angry, be angry efficiently, because we haven't time.*

*Think about what such a fire would mean to a crown built on a lie. The Sigil Wars were not a noble defense—they were a land grab dressed in scripture, and every schoolbook in this kingdom teaches children otherwise. A fire that burns lies cannot burn in secret forever. So the Crown did what crowns do: they didn't kill the dragon. Killing it would make a martyr and a legend. Instead they erased the country around it. Starved it of memory. A fire needs tending, Little Wren, and you cannot tend a fire no one remembers exists.*

*But it's guttering. That courier died carrying word of it. The rebels want the map because they think Vhalcrest is a weapon. Ashvael—if that name reaches you—wants it because she thinks it's a birthright. The Crown wants it buried. Everyone wants to OWN the fire.*

*You must not let them. And you must not let yourself, either, though I suspect you of all people already know that.*

*Maren's map, the courier's map, your map—they're all the same map, I'd wager my remaining boot on it. And maps like that don't lead anywhere. Maps like that OPEN. From both sides. Which means somewhere in Vhalcrest there is a door standing ajar waiting for a knock, and the knock has to be made by someone the door recognizes.*

*I always wondered why a beast-speaker washed up on my doorstep twenty years ago with hollow eyes and a talent for numbers. I stopped wondering a long time ago. Some stock comes to a shop because it was ordered.*

*So here is my instruction, and I give it as your guildmaster, which outranks whatever tenderness is making my handwriting wobble: FINISH IT. Go through the door. Tend the fire. Speak whatever needs speaking. And when you get there, find out if a woman named Maren Vann ever arrived, and if she did, tell her her sister kept the books balanced and the shop open and waited, and that waiting was its own kind of ledger entry, and it was NEVER closed.*

*Practical matters: the gull knows your smell from the cloth tied to her leg. Trust her and no other bird. The iron chest, third board—take what's in it if you ever get back, it's yours. Vess suspects everything and believes nothing, which makes her slower than a believer would be. Use that. Eat something green occasionally; you forget.*

*That's all. I've said my once.*

*Balance the books, Little Wren. All of them. Even the ones the Crown tore out.*

*—O.V.*

*P.S. If a woman named Serah Vale is with you: I know what she was. I also know what she chose. In my experience the second thing weighs more. Mind her back and let her mind yours, and if either of you breaks the other's heart, I will hear about it eventually, and I am VERY difficult to visit in prison.*

*P.P.S. The boots were my favorites. Someone will pay for this.*

* * *

Folding the letter took an hour. She worked it small—smaller than a coin, smaller than a knucklebone—wrapped it in oilcloth boiled off the edge of her supper covering, and bound the whole with a strip of cloth she'd torn from her own shift and worn against her skin for two days, so it would carry her scent, so the bird would know it mattered.

In the morning, the scarred gull landed on the sill and waited, head cocked, one pale eye accounting for her.

"Now listen to me, you magnificent extortionist," Odile murmured, pressing the little bundle flat against the stone between the bars while the gull sidled close, feathers rustling with anticipation. "Under the binding. There. You feel it? That goes to a person who smells like woodsmoke and honesty and bad sleeping habits. Find them. Do this and I swear on the guild's oldest ledger you eat fresh fish every day of your greedy life."

The gull picked up the bundle, tested its weight with the seriousness of any merchant appraising goods, and looked at her.

"Yes," Odile said. "Payment on delivery. I run an honest shop."

The bird went off the sill into the wind like a thrown knife, and Odile watched it shrink over the harbor, over the masts, over the grey line of the sea, until it was a speck, and then not even that.

Then she sat back down on the cot, folded her hands, and prepared to be interrogated again at six o'clock, because the world did not stop for the sending of letters, and neither did Captain Vess.

* * *

Vess came at six, as scheduled. But today she did not open her notebook.

Today she stood by the window a moment, looking out at the harbor, and Odile watched her with the particular attention of a woman counting another woman's coin without seeming to.

"My men searched the guild ruins again," Vess said. "Third pass. They found the cellar."

"Cellars burn slowly," Odile said. "All that stone."

"They found an iron chest."

Odile's heart did not stop—she was far too old and too well-managed for theatrics—but it did, briefly, consult its ledger. "Did they. And what did they find in it?"

"That's the question." Vess turned. Her face was composed, but her hands were not; her thumb moved along the spine of her notebook, back and forth, a small restless arithmetic. "The chest was empty except for ash. As if papers had been burned inside it, recently. Except the guild burned nine days ago, and ash that fresh shouldn't—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "You knew we'd find it."

"I knew you'd find *a* chest," Odile said. "What you found in it is between you and your assumptions."

For a long moment Vess said nothing. Then, quietly, in a voice with no numbers in it at all: "What was in the chest, Mistress Vann?"

And Odile Vann, who had lied to kings' officers for forty years and slept soundly after, heard herself answer with the truth—or a piece of it, the piece that was a hook dressed as a gift:

"A page from the oldest ledger, Captain. The one entry the Crown never managed to buy or burn. It named a country. And it named what that country's fire does to liars." She leaned forward, manacles clinking, and smiled the smile she saved for closing a sale. "You've read the Sigil War histories. The official ones. Tell me—when you read them, did the sums ever quite come out right?"

Vess's thumb stopped moving on the notebook.

"There it is," Odile said softly. "That look. That's the look of a woman whose ledger won't reconcile. Welcome to the guild, Captain. We deal in impossible things. Lesson one is free."

She settled back against the wall, contented as a cat in a sunbeam, and began—in her head, because paper was dangerous—to draft her next letter.

Because the gull would come back. Gulls always come back for payment. And Odile Vann had a great deal more to say, and a prison full of guards who wanted to be seen, and time—she hoped, she prayed, she entered it in her mental ledger as an asset pending confirmation—enough time.

Somewhere north, past the drowned borderlands, her letter was flying toward a person who smelled like woodsmoke and honesty and bad sleeping habits. Somewhere north, a page that should never have been torn out was being returned to the book.

"Finish it, Little Wren," she murmured to the empty window, to the wind, to the whole indifferent grey sea. "And mind the boots situation. Someone will absolutely pay for the boots."

Far out over the water, a speck beat northward against the wind, carrying a country's name in a bundle smaller than a coin—and behind it, in a high stone room, an old woman began teaching her jailers to bring her better bread.
