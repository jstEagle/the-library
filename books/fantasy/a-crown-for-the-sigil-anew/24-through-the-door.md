## 24. Through the Door

**Kestrel**

The valley that wasn't there had a smell.

Kestrel had expected nothing—emptiness, absence, the olfactory equivalent of a blank page. Instead, as they crested the last ridge on the third morning of following a map that kept rearranging itself like a cat trying to get comfortable, they smelled rain on hot stone, woodsmoke, and underneath it all something green and old, like the inside of a forest that had never been logged.

"Do you smell that?" Kestrel asked.

Serah, walking a careful half-step behind with her hand resting on Ember's shoulder—the hatchling was now the size of a large dog and insisted on being treated like a small one—drew a long breath through her nose. Her face did something complicated.

"Bread," she said. "Someone's baking bread."

"Exactly. Nobody bakes bread in an empty valley."

Ember sneezed a spark of agreement and tugged forward on the rope lead Kestrel had fashioned mostly for appearances. The wyvern had never once been restrained by it. It was, Kestrel suspected, a courtesy the creature extended to make them feel better.

They descended through scrub pine into air that shimmered. Not heat-haze, though it looked like it at first glance—a trembling in the light itself, as though the valley were seen through the surface of very still water. Kestrel had walked through glamours before; the guild had once stored a cursed mirror wrapped in seven layers of them. This was nothing like that. Glamours pushed you away. This *invited*, gently, the way an open window invites a breeze.

The map in their satchel was humming. It had been humming since dawn, a vibration felt in the sternum more than heard, and now it climbed toward something like a struck bell.

At the valley floor, half-drowned in a tarn that mirrored the impossible sky, stood the arch.

It was older than anything Kestrel had ever seen. Grey stone gone black at the waterline, furred with moss, crowned with birch saplings growing straight out of the mortar as if the arch had decided, somewhere in its long patience, to become a garden. No wall attached to it. No ruin. Just the arch, standing alone at the water's edge like a sentence waiting for its verb.

Kestrel's legs stopped without consulting them.

"That's it," they said. Their voice came out strange, thin. "That's the door."

"You're sure? Because I've seen at least four things this week that were 'definitely the door,' and two of them were rocks."

"The map's sure."

The map, in fact, was coming apart. Kestrel drew it out with shaking hands and watched the scorched vellum begin to glow along its fault lines, gold light seeping up through the ink the way dawn comes up under a door. The coastlines that had shifted nightly were shifting now moment by moment, rivers running uphill, mountains folding themselves flat—and all of it, every line, flowing toward the shape of the arch, as if the map had always been less a picture than a set of directions written for someone who needed to walk the whole way before understanding.

"It's dissolving," Serah said, hand going to her knife out of pure habit. "Kestrel. Your map is dissolving."

"I know."

"Should we be worried?"

"I don't know." A laugh escaped them, high and unsteady. "I've been carrying this thing since a dying man pressed it into my hands in a storeroom that smelled of pickled storm. If it wants to become light, I suppose that's its business."

Ember chirped, imperious, and butted the arch's stones with her snout. Nothing happened. She looked back at Kestrel with an expression of profound wyvern accusation: *well, do something.*

Kestrel stepped closer. Up close, the arch's surface was carved—so worn it took a moment to parse—with interlocking spirals, and threaded through the spirals, worn nearly smooth, words in no alphabet Kestrel knew. Except that wasn't quite true. Their fingers found one groove, deep where others were shallow, and their bones recognized it before their mind did.

The word the courier had died speaking. The name-fragment. All these weeks it had lived in Kestrel's memory like a coin sewn into a coat lining, forgotten until needed.

*Ythir—*

The rest of the name was missing. Had always been missing. And Kestrel understood, suddenly and completely, why the door would not open from one side alone.

"Serah," they said quietly. "It needs a speaker."

---

**Serah**

Serah Vale had breached warded doors in three countries. She had picked locks, bribed gatekeepers, and once—in a career she tried not to think about too closely—talked a fortress garrison into opening their own gates by arriving dressed as their long-lost colonel. Doors, in her experience, opened for force, fraud, or money, in roughly that order of reliability.

She watched Kestrel stand before a mossy archway with a disintegrating map in their hands and realized none of those would work here, and that this was the whole point.

"What does it need?" she asked, because asking was easier than admitting her palms were sweating.

"A name. Half a name." Kestrel's thumb moved over the map's glowing surface. "The courier gave me the first part. But he died before—" Their voice caught. It still caught, sometimes, on the courier. Serah had learned not to smooth it over. "Before he could finish. The door opens when the name is whole. And the other half isn't written anywhere. It has to come from—"

"From the other side."

"From the dragon. From Ythirra herself." Kestrel looked up at Serah, and the morning light caught the exhaustion and the terror and—underneath both, stubborn as a spring bulb—hope. "It can't be forced. It can't be tricked. Someone over there has to want us through."

"Then we're trusting a country that forgot how to remember us."

"We're trusting a dragon who's been holding a door open for three hundred years on nothing but faith and a guttering fire." Kestrel smiled, crooked and small. "Seems rude not to knock."

Ember planted herself between Kestrel and the water, tail lashing, wings half-spread in what Serah had privately named the battle stance, though so far its only battlefield had been breakfast. The hatchling looked up at the arch, then at Kestrel, and made a low sound—not a chirp, not a growl. Something older. An encouragement, or an instruction.

"She says speak," Serah translated, surprising herself.

"You understand her?"

"No. But I've spent ten years learning to tell the difference between people telling me things and people telling me what they need. It's usually the same sentence."

Kestrel laughed—wetly, but they laughed—and turned to the arch. They raised their free hand, hesitated, and then laid their palm flat against the stone.

Nothing happened. Moss tickled their wrist. The tarn lapped at its own reflection.

Then Kestrel breathed in, and spoke the fragment.

*Ythir—*

The sound didn't echo. That was the first wrong-and-right thing. Sound in a valley should scatter; this sank instead, straight down through the stone, through the water, through the ground itself, and Serah felt it pass under her boots like a whale passing beneath a boat. The map flared white in Kestrel's hands and came apart—not burned, not torn, but *released*, every line of ink lifting off the vellum like midges off a pond at dusk, swirling into a ribbon of light that poured itself into the archway's spirals.

The carvings lit. One spiral, then another, then all of them, a slow constellation kindling across the ancient stone.

And the arch waited.

Ten heartbeats. Twenty. Serah counted the way she'd been trained to, and hated that she was counting, and counted anyway. Beside her, Ember had gone utterly still, which for a young wyvern was either catastrophe or prayer.

"He's not answering," Serah said. "Or she. Whoever."

"Give it a moment. It's been three hundred years. You'd want a moment too—"

The stone warmed under Kestrel's hand.

Serah saw it happen: saw Kestrel's eyes go wide, saw their mouth fall open, saw them sway toward the arch as if pulled by a hand at the collarbone. And through the link of that palm on stone, something came *back*—not sound, not quite, but a pressure in the air, a resonance, the second half of a bell-stroke.

A voice that was not a voice said the rest.

Serah couldn't hold the syllables afterward. They slid out of memory like water from a closed fist, and she understood dimly that this was mercy, or design: a true name could not be carried away by just anyone. Only Kestrel held it, and even Kestrel seemed to hold it the way you hold something hot—as briefly as possible, and forever.

But she heard Kestrel say it. Whole. Complete. Spoken not the way Inquisitors spoke binding-words, flat and coercive, but the way you'd greet someone across a great distance after a very long time. The way you'd say *you're still there* and *I'm here too* in the same breath.

The arch opened.

Not swung—there was no gate. The space inside the arch simply stopped being the far side of the tarn and became something else: light, and warmth, and the smell of bread unmistakably now, and green hills going up and up into a sky with two colors in it. Wind came through and stirred Kestrel's hair. Somewhere beyond, faint as a memory of music, something enormous breathed.

Ember shrieked joy and bolted through the door before either of them could move.

"EMBER—" Serah lunged, too late, and stood frozen at the threshold with one boot in each world, which was possibly the most accurate description of her entire life.

Beyond the arch, on a sunlit hillside, a wyvern the size of a hound rolled ecstatically in grass that had never known a boot.

"She's fine," Kestrel said faintly. "She's—she's rolling in it. In Vhalcrest. Like a dog in a fresh hayfield."

"Champion of the new world." Serah's throat was tight. She withdrew her boot from the threshold and looked at Kestrel—at this ridiculous, brave, ledger-keeping liar who had crossed a continent on a dead man's map and a stolen gift, and was now standing in a drowned valley crying at an open door. "After you, then."

"No." Kestrel held out their hand. "Together. I told you—it opens from both sides. I'm not crossing anything alone anymore."

Serah looked at the offered hand. Ten years ago she'd have catalogued it: gesture, motive, tactical implication. A year ago she'd have taken it and hated herself. Now she just took it.

Her palm fit against Kestrel's like a key turning.

"All right," she said. "Homecoming to a place you've never been. Let's see if it lives up to the rumors."

They stepped through together.

---

**Kestrel**

The first thing Vhalcrest did was check their pockets, apparently, because the wind that met them went through Kestrel's hair and coat with thorough, unhurried curiosity, the way a horse investigates a stranger. Then it settled. Then the sun—warmer than any sun had a right to be in early autumn—landed on their shoulders like a shawl.

Kestrel stood on grass. Ordinary, outrageous, emerald grass, dotted with tiny blue flowers that closed and opened in time with their own breathing, which Kestrel chose not to examine too closely. Before them the valley unfolded: terraced fields climbing the hillsides, orchards heavy with fruit, a river braiding silver through it all, and in the middle distance—so tall its peak wore cloud like a hat—a spire of pale stone.

Atop the spire, barely visible, a coil of darkness shifted. And around the spire's crown, like a halo seen wrong, the light flickered.

Guttered.

Steadied.

"Guttering fire," Kestrel whispered. The ledger-mind, bless it, was already itemizing: fields grey at the edges, orchards abundant but *tired*, the flowers breathing instead of swaying. Everything here was alive, and everything here was running out.

Serah had gone still beside her, in the way that meant she was seeing threats. "There are people on the ridge road. Two, maybe three, heading this way. Fast."

And there were—figures in travel-stained coats, one of them waving, one of them shouting something the wind brought in fragments. A language Kestrel shouldn't have understood. But the words arrived already translated, warm and urgent, and the first clear phrase that reached them across the grass of a nation erased from every atlas was:

*"You spoke the name! Who spoke the—"* The shout broke off. The lead figure stopped dead on the hillside, staring at Kestrel, at the arch behind them, at Ember—who had abandoned her hayfield to trot back, mud-flecked and triumphant—and said, in a voice that cracked down the middle:

"A Speaker. Oh, stars. Oh, stars preserve. A *Speaker*, and a wyvern, and—" The woman's eyes found the flickering halo above the spire, and her face did something that Kestrel would spend a long time afterward trying not to remember. "Come quickly. Please. Lady Ashvael must be told. Ythirra must be told. We thought—" She swallowed. "We thought the door would never open again."

Ember chirped and sat on the woman's foot.

Kestrel looked at Serah. Serah, hand on knife, ex-Inquisitor to the marrow, gave a single nod that meant *trouble ahead, go anyway.*

"Well," Kestrel said, and found they were smiling through the fear, through the grief, through the sheer unbearable strangeness of standing in a home they'd never seen while strangers ran to meet them. "Best put the kettle on. I imagine there's rather a lot of paperwork."

Behind them, in a drowned valley in a world that had forgotten, the archway glowed softly—and stayed open.
