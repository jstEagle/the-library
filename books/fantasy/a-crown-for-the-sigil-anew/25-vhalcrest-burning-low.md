## 25. Vhalcrest Burning Low

The first thing Kestrel noticed about Vhalcrest was the light.

Not sunlight—though there was sun, pale and honeyed, falling through a sky the color of old porcelain. It was something underneath the sunlight. A glow that came up out of the ground itself, out of the wheat and the hedgerows and the slate roofs of a village they could see in the valley below, as though the whole country were a lamp with someone's hand cupped around its flame.

"It's beautiful," Serah said beside them.

"Yes," Kestrel said. "And it's dying."

They stood on a ridge at the valley's mouth, Ember draped across Kestrel's shoulders like a scarf that had opinions. The wyvern hatchling had gone very still since they'd passed through the archway—a stillness Kestrel had learned to read as reverence, or grief, or both. Her claws tightened gently on Kestrel's collarbone, and she made a sound low in her throat, not quite a chirp, not quite a keening.

"I know," Kestrel murmured to her. "I know."

Below them, Vhalcrest spread out in waves of field and orchard and village, all of it touched by that strange under-light—and all of it, at the edges, going grey.

It was easiest to see at the borders of things. The outermost rows of the wheat stood silver-grey, stalks like old woman's hair. The farthest apple trees bore fruit that looked painted rather than grown, colors slightly wrong, slightly faded, as if the whole orchard had been left in a window too long. And beyond the fields, where the map—the shifting, sleeping, living map, which was now merely a warm weight in Kestrel's pack, its work done—had promised rivers and forests, there was only a soft white nothing, like fog that had forgotten how to move.

"The country is being erased from the inside out," Serah said quietly. She had her Inquisitor face on, which meant her jaw was set and her eyes were doing arithmetic. "From the edges inward. Like frost on glass."

"Like ink drying," Kestrel said. "Or being blotted."

Ember chirped once, sharply, and butted her head against Kestrel's jaw until they started walking.

* * *

The first village was called, according to a weathered sign at its crossroads, Brindlemere. The sign itself was an education: the letters had been carved deep and confident, then re-carved, shallower, then re-carved again, each generation of hands cutting the name less surely than the last, as if the word kept slipping out of memory even for the people who lived beneath it.

The people of Brindlemere were kind, and hospitable, and almost impossible to look at directly.

Kestrel couldn't decide, at first, what was wrong. The villagers moved and spoke and laughed—a woman selling bread from a cart laughed at something a child said, a full-throated, unguarded laugh—and yet looking at any of them felt like looking through a window when the lamplight inside was low. Their outlines wavered. Their eyes held a shine that wasn't quite reflection. When the bread-seller handed Kestrel a loaf, her fingers passed through the crust's warmth and Kestrel felt the heat arrive a half-second late, like thunder after lightning.

"Thank you," Kestrel said.

"You're welcome," the woman said, and smiled, and for one terrible moment Kestrel watched her struggle—as visibly as a person struggling up a steep hill—to remember what she had just been thanked for. "For... you're welcome," she said again, with relief, as though the second time had fixed it.

Serah bought three more loaves with coins that made the woman frown at them in puzzlement, turning the silver over and over, clearly trying to place what money was and why anyone would want it, before some older habit surfaced and she pocketed them with a curtsy.

"They're forgetting," Kestrel said, once they'd moved down the lane. They kept their voices low out of respect, the way one does near a sickbed. "Not just the world forgetting them. They're forgetting themselves. The erasure works inward."

"How long?" Serah asked. Not *how did this happen*—she'd already worked that part out, or thought she had. Just *how long*. Inquisitors counted in casualties; ex-Inquisitors, apparently, never stopped.

"I don't know." Kestrel looked back at the bread-cart, at the woman who had already turned to serve another customer and paused, mid-motion, a small crease between her brows, reaching for something just out of reach. "But I don't think it's fast. I think it's patient. I think it's been patient for twenty years."

Twenty years. The number sat in Kestrel's chest like a swallowed stone. Twenty years since Odile's sister had walked into the drowned borderlands and not come home. Twenty years since the Crown had taken its pen to the atlas. And all this time, a whole country had gone on living here—marrying, baking bread, laughing at children—while the world outside forgot it had ever existed, and the country itself slowly forgot along with everyone else.

A goose honked at them from a yard as they passed. Kestrel, out of lifelong habit, listened to what the geese were saying, and stopped dead in the lane.

"What?" Serah's hand went to her knife.

"She says—" Kestrel blinked. "She says the fire is thin this year. She says the *fire is thin*, and the geese have been saying it to each other for generations, and none of the young ones believe them anymore because the young ones have never known a thick fire." Kestrel swallowed. "Serah, the animals remember. The animals have been remembering this whole time. That's why everything out in the borderlands went silent and watchful—they weren't warning us away. They were mourning."

Ember pressed her warm flank against Kestrel's neck, and Kestrel realized their eyes were wet, and let them be wet for exactly four steps before wiping them dry and walking on. There would be time for weeping later, or there wouldn't, and either way bread needed eating and a road needed following.

* * *

They followed the road everyone in Brindlemere pointed to when asked about the Spire—which was everyone, eventually, because asking about anything else got you a kind smile and a long pause while the answer was fetched from somewhere increasingly dusty.

Everyone pointed the same way. Everyone used the same gesture, too: a hand raised toward the center of the valley, palm open, fingers slightly curled, the way you'd cup a candle flame against wind. Nobody seemed aware they were doing it. It was simply how you pointed at the Spire here—with your hand shaped like shelter.

The Spire rose from the heart of Vhalcrest like a needle of black glass, taller than anything Kestrel had ever seen built by hands, though the longer they walked toward it the less sure they became that hands had built it at all. It tapered to a point miles up, and at its crown burned the pyre.

Even from a distance, even through the honeyed air, Kestrel could see it: a great slow bloom of gold-white fire, and coiled around and above and through it, vast as a mountain range given ambition, the dragon.

Ythirra.

Kestrel knew the name the way they knew their own heartbeat—not because anyone had told them, but because the moment they'd crossed the archway, something in their blood had sat up straight and said *home*.

"She's beautiful," Serah breathed. They had climbed a low hill outside the second village—this one called, after much collective hesitation among its residents, Somethingmere, or possibly Meresomething—and the whole valley lay open below them, every road running visibly toward the Spire the way streams run toward the sea.

"She's starving," Kestrel said.

And it was true, and once seen it could not be unseen. The dragon's fire still bloomed, but the bloom was guttering. Great wings that should have caught the light like stained glass instead showed dull patches, grey spreading along their edges the same grey that ate the wheat. When Ythirra shifted—and she shifted rarely, each movement slow as continental drift—Kestrel heard it in their bones, a creak like a ship in heavy weather, and felt the pyre's flame dip, and saw, impossibly, at this distance, the nearest wheat field lose a row to silver.

"She's burning herself to keep them lit," Kestrel said. Their voice came out rough. "Look. Every bit of fire she makes goes outward. Into the land. Into the people. She's been feeding the whole country out of her own body for twenty years, and there's no one feeding *her*."

"The covenant," Serah said. "The founding covenant. What Ember showed you."

"A door needs two sides," Kestrel murmured. "A covenant needs two voices. She's been speaking alone all this time." They looked down at Ember, who had gone still and grave on their shoulder, hatchling eyes fixed on the distant pyre with an expression no creature that young should have been able to wear. "Alone for so long she's forgotten there was ever supposed to be an answer."

* * *

They came to the capital—if a city can be called a capital when its name has been eaten—near evening.

Kestrel had expected ruin. After the grey fields, after Brindlemere's translucent bakers, they had braced themselves for a ghost town, for empty streets and doors swinging. What they found instead broke them in a completely different way, and it took Kestrel several streets to understand why.

The city was alive. Thoroughly, stubbornly alive. Markets open, fountains playing, laundry strung between balconies like festival banners. Musicians on a corner—three of them, a fiddle, a drum, a pipe—playing a tune that snagged in Kestrel's chest like a fishhook, because it was almost, *almost* the hearth-song Odile used to hum over the guild's ledgers, and Kestrel had traded that memory away to a river-witch and hadn't expected to ever feel its shape again, and here it was, worn by a whole city, played on a street corner by strangers.

That was when Kestrel understood what was breaking their heart.

Nothing here was ruined. Everything here was *fading*. The city was a painting left in the sun: every color present but dimming, every detail intact but softening, love still functioning in every doorway but taking longer and longer to recall why. A man watered flowers on a balcony and paused, watering can tilted, staring at the flowers with polite bafflement, as if trying to remember whether he liked them. Two old women on a bench held hands without holding hands, fingers hovering a hair's width apart, contact lost somewhere in the years. Children played a game whose rules changed every third turn because no one could hold the rules in mind long enough to enforce them—and the children laughed anyway, and somehow that was the worst of it, the laughter, bright and unshadowed, ringing off buildings that were forgetting they had ever been built.

"They don't know," Serah said hoarsely. "Do they? They don't know it's happening."

"Some do." Kestrel nodded across the square, where an old man stood at a public notice board, pinning up a fresh paper with enormous care. His hands shook. The paper was covered in names—hundreds of names, columns of them, in a dozen different inks, some entries written over older, fading entries of the same names. "Look. He's writing them down. He writes them down so they last a little longer. He probably doesn't know why anymore. But he knows it matters."

The old man finished pinning his list, stepped back, and read the top name aloud to no one, twice, the way you'd water a plant.

Ember made a small broken sound against Kestrel's throat.

"We can't fix this tonight," Serah said. It wasn't coldness; Kestrel had learned to hear the care inside Serah's pragmatism, the way armor is shaped around the body it protects. "We can't fix it by crying in a square. We need to get to the Spire. We need to see her up close."

"I know." Kestrel wiped their face with their sleeve, unashamed. "I'm allowed five minutes. Then we go."

"Take ten," Serah said, and stood beside them in the fading golden light, shoulder to shoulder, while the fiddler played the almost-hearth-song and the old man read his names and the whole beautiful, dying city glowed softly around them like an ember deciding, stubbornly, not to go out.

* * *

They found lodging at an inn whose sign depicted a cheerful teapot and whose name, when Kestrel asked, produced in the innkeeper a long pause, a squint, and finally, triumphantly: "*The* Inn. Yes. This one."

The room was clean and warm and smelled of lavender, and the innkeeper's daughter—who was perhaps nine, and whose outline, Kestrel noted with a lurch, was the most solid thing they'd seen all day, children fading slowest—brought them stew and stared at Ember with the naked adoration only children and dogs are permitted to display.

"Is she yours?" the girl whispered.

"For now she's mine," Kestrel said. "Someday she'll be her own."

"That's what my gran says about me." The girl giggled. Then she leaned closer, dropped her voice to a conspirator's hush, and said: "You're going up to the Spire, aren't you. Everybody who comes here goes up to the Spire."

"Do they?"

"Mm. Pilgrims." She said the word carefully, tasting it, clearly recently learned. "They come and look at the fire and cry, and then they go talk to Lady Ashvael, and then they either stay forever or leave and forget us." She shrugged with the brutal economy of childhood. "Gran says the ones who leave forget fastest. Gran says write your name on your own arm before you go, if you want to come back."

She held out her own arm to demonstrate. Written on it, in childish capitals, worn nearly smooth by repetition, was a single word: *MIRELLE.*

Kestrel looked at it for a long time.

Then they took out their ink-stained ledger hand, borrowed the girl's stub of pencil, and wrote on their own forearm, in careful bookkeeper's capitals: *KESTREL OKONKWO, GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS.* Below it, after a moment, they added: *OWES MIRELLE A STORY.*

"There," they said. "Now I have to come back. Debts are sacred where I'm from."

The girl examined the arm, nodded gravely, pronounced the terms acceptable, and departed with the stew pot.

Serah waited until the door closed. "Guild of Impossible Things," she repeated softly.

"Best claim I've got to a family crest." Kestrel flexed the arm, watching the letters. "Besides. It's true. I'm standing in one."

Outside the window, the pyre at the Spire's crown pulsed once—slow, arrhythmic, a great heart missing beats—and the lavender-scented room dimmed with it, and brightened, and neither of them slept well, and neither of them was surprised.

* * *

Morning brought the summons.

Kestrel woke to find Serah already dressed and standing at the window, very still, in the particular stillness that Kestrel had learned meant *trouble wearing formal clothes*. Below in the street stood a dozen riders in livery the color of dawn-grey silk, and at their head a woman on a white mare, and even at this distance, even through the honeyed light, the woman radiated the specific gravity of someone accustomed to being obeyed.

An herald stepped forward and spoke to the innkeeper, and the innkeeper pointed up—at their window—with the apologetic shrug of a man caught between powers.

"Lady Ashvael," Serah said quietly, "Regent of Vhalcrest, requests the pleasure of the strangers' company at the citadel. Immediately, I'd wager." She turned from the window. Her Inquisitor face was fully on now, and behind it, visible only to someone who had learned to look, was fear. "Kestrel. I've seen that protocol before. The riders' spacing, the herald's phrasing, the—she's using Crown forms. Inquisitor forms. Here, in Vhalcrest."

Kestrel looked down at the riders, at the woman on the white mare who had not moved, who was waiting with the patience of someone who had waited twenty years and intended to wait precisely as long as she pleased.

Ember stirred on Kestrel's shoulder and hissed—a tiny, furious sound, smoke curling from her nostrils—and Kestrel, who had spent a lifetime listening to what beasts say beneath what beasts say, heard clearly the one word wrapped inside the hiss:

*Mine,* said Ember, of the woman below. *Mine. Mine. Mine.*

Which was strange, Kestrel thought, pulling on their coat with the ledger-hand arm and the written name, because Ember had never met Lady Ashvael in her life.

Unless, of course, she had—in the way that dragons remember things, backward and forward and sideways, in the blood.

"Come on, then," Kestrel said, and reached for the door.
