## 26. The Court of the Almost Forgotten

Serah Vale had been welcomed by royalty exactly twice in her life, and both times she'd counted the exits before the doors finished opening.

The first time had been a ceremony, early in her Inquisitor career—she'd stood at the back of a gilded hall while some minor duke received a commendation for services to the Crown's purity, and she'd memorized three doorways, two windows, and one suspiciously load-bearing tapestry. The second time was now, walking beside Kestrel Okonkwo through the gates of a citadel that officially did not exist, into a court that officially did not exist, toward a woman who officially did not exist either.

Old habits. She supposed you could take the girl out of the Order, but the Order stayed in the girl like a splinter working its way toward the heart.

"Stop counting," Kestrel murmured.

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting. Your shoulders do this thing."

"My shoulders do many things. They're very expressive shoulders."

Ember trotted ahead of them on the white gravel path, wings half-spread in what Serah had learned was wyvern smugness, and the guards lining the way—there were guards, of course there were guards, hospitality always traveled with an escort—watched the little dragon with expressions that ranged from reverence to hunger. Serah catalogued that too. Reverence she could work with. Hunger was a problem for later.

The citadel rose out of the dimming valley like a tooth from old gum: pale stone gone grey at the crown, banners hanging so long in the same windless air that they'd faded to ghosts of themselves. Everything here was beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful. Still. Waiting. Lit from somewhere that didn't quite reach the edges.

And everywhere, the people were translucent.

Serah had seen it since they crossed the border—the farmers in the fields whose hands went faintly see-through when they stopped moving, the children playing a game with a ball that passed through one boy's chest every third throw, and none of the children remarked on it. A woman watering her garden had turned to watch them pass and Serah had seen the hedge *through* her cheekbone, delicate as lace.

Kestrel saw it too. Serah felt it in the way their hand found hers—not romantic, not now, not with an audience, just anchor and anchored—and squeezed once.

*Later,* the squeeze said. *We get through this first.*

Serah had survived eleven years of Inquisitor service by being very good at *later*.

* * *

Lady Ashvael received them in a hall that should have been magnificent and instead was heartbreaking.

It wasn't ruined. That was the thing. Nothing in Vhalcrest was ruined—it was *fading*, which was worse. The hall's mosaics depicted dragons and harvests and a coronation Serah didn't recognize, and the colors were all still there, technically, but drained to the saturation of things remembered rather than seen. A chandelier hung overhead with perhaps a third of its candles lit. The rest had simply... stopped. Not burned down. Stopped, as if fire itself had grown tired here.

At the far end, beneath a canopy worked with silver thread that had once surely been brilliant, stood the regent of a country erased from every atlas.

Serah had expected grief. Twenty years alone remembering a nation—that leaves marks. She'd expected a woman worn thin as the banners, or hard as the border stones drowned in the lowlands, or mad in the quiet careful way that long solitude makes people.

What she got was warmth.

Lady Ashvael came down the steps herself—which no regent does, Serah noted, file it away—and she was tall and silver-streaked and moved like someone who had spent decades practicing grace until it became armor. Her smile arrived a half-second before the rest of her face agreed to it.

"The Speaker," she said, and her voice filled the hall without effort. "And the wyvern-child. And—" her eyes touched Serah, brief and weighing, "—a guest. You are all so welcome in Vhalcrest. Forgive us our dimness. We have been waiting a long time for light."

She said it beautifully. Every word placed like a stone in a garden wall.

And Serah's spine went cold, because she knew this. She *knew* this. Not the words—the architecture beneath them.

This was Protocol Nine. Reception of Assets. She'd drilled it as a cadet until it lived in her marrow: *the subject must feel chosen; the subject must feel safe; the subject must never feel surveyed.* Warmth deployed in graduated measures. Personal attention calibrated to the subject's evident loneliness. The offer of comfort before the request, so that refusal would feel like ingratitude.

The Order taught its people to welcome like this. Serah had done it herself, smiling at frightened beast-speakers in holding rooms, offering tea, saying *you're safe now* in the exact tone Lady Ashvael was using right now.

Nobody outside the Order talked like that. Nobody outside the Order *had* to.

Beside her, Kestrel beamed back at the regent with the open, artless warmth of a person who genuinely liked people, and Serah wanted to shake them and also never let go of their hand.

* * *

They were fed. Of course they were fed.

The meal was served in a smaller chamber off the main hall—a table set for six though only four sat down, because the place settings included two chairs that no one claimed, and when Serah asked about them with a raised eyebrow, Ashvael said only, "We keep places for those we hope will return," and the candle nearest her guttered as if in agreement.

The food was extraordinary and slightly wrong. The bread tasted almost like memory of bread. The wine was warm going down and left a faint hum behind the teeth, like biting foil. Ember ate an entire roast fowl and then looked offended that there weren't more roast fowls, which was the most normal thing that had happened since the border.

"So," Ashvael said, pouring wine with her own hands—again, no regent pours, file it away—"you crossed the drowned borderlands. Few manage it. The water remembers who belongs to the old world." Her gaze rested on Kestrel with an intensity that would have read as admiration to anyone else. To Serah it read as appraisal. Livestock appraisal. "And you carry the map. May I see it?"

"It dissolved," Kestrel said, around a mouthful of bread. They'd gotten better at lying since the road; they were still terrible at it. Their eyes flicked to Serah for exactly one heartbeat too long. "At the archway. Went up in light. Very dramatic. Sorry."

Something moved behind Ashvael's expression—quick, controlled, filed away—and Serah recognized that too. It was the look of an officer updating a ledger. *Subject claims asset destroyed. Unverified. Probable false.*

"No matter," Ashvael said warmly. "The map was only ever paper. What matters is what walked through the door carrying it." She reached across the table and took Kestrel's hand, turning it palm-up with a tenderness that made Serah's jaw ache. "May I?"

She didn't wait for permission. Of course she didn't; assets don't get asked. She traced one finger along Kestrel's palm, following lines Serah couldn't see, and murmured something under her breath in a language that made the candles lean.

Kestrel laughed—nervous, delighted, fooled. "What was that?"

"A blessing," said Ashvael. "An old one. We are so glad you've come home, Speaker."

*Home.* There it was. Protocol Nine, second clause: *the subject must feel chosen.* And underneath it, threaded through the word like wire through wax: *ours.*

Under the table, Serah put her boot on top of Kestrel's foot and pressed down, slow and deliberate.

Kestrel, bless them, didn't flinch. But they did stop smiling quite so widely.

* * *

After the meal, Ashvael gave them rooms.

"Rooms," plural, in a wing of the citadel called the Guest's Reach, which was beautiful and comfortable and had windows that overlooked a courtyard with exactly one exit, which Serah confirmed by leaning out of each window in turn while Kestrel unpacked a bag containing, essentially, three shirts and a great deal of guilt.

"They seem nice," Kestrel said.

"They're going to bleed you."

Kestrel paused, a shirt in hand. "That's a bit of a leap."

"Lady Ashvael welcomed us using Inquisitor reception protocol. Word for word. I could recite it along with her. The graduated warmth, the personal attention, the *home* language, the hand-taking without asking—" Serah ticked them off on her fingers. "I ran receptions like that. I know what comes after the welcome."

"What comes after the welcome?"

"The request. And the request is never small." Serah leaned against the window frame. Outside, dusk was gathering wrong—pooling in the courtyard corners like spilled ink instead of falling evenly. "Think about it. A country held together by one dying dragon's fire. A regent who's kept it alive twenty years alone. Along comes a beast-speaker whose gift, if Ember's memories are right, descends from the founding covenant itself. What would you want, if you were her?"

Kestrel sat down slowly on the edge of the bed. "Renewal."

"Renewal. On her terms. Controlled, permanent, *hers*." Serah crossed the room and crouched in front of them, taking their hands properly now, no audience but a wyvern who was pretending to sleep with one eye open. "Kestrel. Listen to me. I signed orders. I know how the sentence gets built. First it's *help us*. Then it's *help us or the country dies*. Then it's *you wouldn't want the country to die, would you?* By the time you notice the cage, you're already inside it, and the door was never locked because they never needed to lock it—you walked in and thanked them for the bread."

Kestrel was quiet a moment. Then: "You could be wrong."

"I could be wrong," Serah agreed. "I've been wrong twice in my life and both times it was about soup. This isn't soup."

A ghost of a laugh escaped them, tired. "What do we do?"

"Tonight? Sleep. Eat breakfast. Be charming and grateful and completely unremarkable. Tomorrow we find out what the request is, and we say we need time, and we watch what happens when we ask for time." She squeezed their hands. "People who mean well give you time, Kestrel. People who need something from you start moving the walls."

Ember opened both eyes and made a low sound, deep in the chest, that Serah had learned to translate as *the small one speaks sense.*

"Thank you, Ember."

"*Both* of you can stop ganging up on me," Kestrel muttered, but they squeezed back.

* * *

Serah didn't sleep. She hadn't expected to.

Instead she lay in the dark of her assigned room—soft bed, clean linens, a carafe of water that hummed faintly against the teeth even cold—and listened to the citadel breathe.

Buildings breathed differently than houses. Houses creaked and settled and argued with the weather. Citadels *counted*. Footsteps in corridors, measured and regular. Doors opening and closing at intervals too even to be organic. Somewhere below, a rhythm of movement that might have been drills, or might have been ritual, or might have been both, because in Serah's experience the difference between a drill and a ritual was mostly a matter of who was watching.

Near midnight, footsteps stopped outside her door. Paused. Moved on.

Two minutes later, they stopped outside Kestrel's door. Paused longer. Moved on.

Counting the assets. Checking the inventory.

Serah got up, dressed, and let herself into the corridor, because there was one more thing she needed to confirm, and confirming it required being an idiot, which had always been one of her more reliable skills.

The Guest's Reach was not locked. None of the doors were locked. That was the confirmation she'd come for, and it settled in her stomach like a swallowed stone: *they don't lock the doors because the guests aren't prisoners, they're investments.* You don't lock a treasury vault with the treasure walking around loose inside it unless you're certain the treasure doesn't know it's treasure.

She found the stairs down—unguarded, naturally—and followed the corridor rhythm to its source, keeping to shadows out of habit older than her defection.

The hall where they'd dined was dark except for the chandelier's stubborn third. And beneath it, alone, Lady Ashvael stood at the head of the empty table, both hands flat on the wood, head bowed, speaking to the vacant chairs.

Serah couldn't hear the words. But she could hear the shape of them—the rise and fall of a voice talking to people who weren't there, had maybe never been there, or had been there once and were names now, only names, kept alive by a woman who set places for them at every meal.

For one treacherous moment Serah's hatred wobbled. Grief did that. Grief wearing a crown was still grief, and Serah of all people knew what the Order could make a person do to survive.

Then Ashvael lifted her head, and across the dark hall her eyes found Serah's shadow in the doorway—found it instantly, precisely, without surprise—and she smiled the warm half-second-early smile.

"Couldn't sleep either?" the regent of Vhalcrest called softly. "Good. I hoped you'd come. There are things you should know about your Speaker—things better discussed before morning."

And Serah, standing in the dark with her heart hammering and her cover blown by her own curiosity, understood that she had just made her first move in a game whose rules she was only beginning to learn—and that Lady Ashvael had been waiting at the board the whole time.
