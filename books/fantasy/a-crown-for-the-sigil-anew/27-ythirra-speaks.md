## 27. Ythirra Speaks

The Spire had no stairs.

That was the first thing Kestrel learned about it, standing at its base in the grey pre-dawn light, craning their neck until it ached. The tower rose out of the heart of Vhalcrest like a needle pulled through cloth—black stone veined with something that glowed faintly amber, the way embers glow under ash long after you've sworn the fire is dead. It went up and up and up, past the low clouds, past what Kestrel had assumed were clouds but might have been smoke, past the point where necks gave out and faith had to take over.

And no stairs. No door either, at least none that Kestrel could find after circling the base twice, palms pressed to stone that was warm as a sleeping animal's flank.

"You're overthinking it," Ember said from somewhere around Kestrel's left shoulder.

Kestrel had stopped flinching at Ember's voice approximately two days ago, which by any reasonable measure was far too late. The wyvern hatchling—hatchling being an increasingly generous term; Ember had grown the size of a large dog since the free-port, and showed every sign of considering that merely a rough draft—was perched on a ledge of broken masonry, chewing thoughtfully on a piece of the Spire's fallen battlements.

"I'm *assessing*," Kestrel said. "There's a difference. Assessing is a professional activity. I did it for years. Bottled storms, cursed mirrors, one very memorable haunted spoon. You look for the mechanism before you commit."

"The mechanism," Ember said, "is that dragons don't build doors for people who are afraid to knock."

"That is not a mechanism, that's a riddle, and I don't get paid enough to solve riddles."

"You don't get paid at all anymore. You're a fugitive."

"Thank you, Ember. Truly. Your counsel is worth every coin I spent freeing you."

Ember snorted—a sound like a kettle achieving enlightenment—and a curl of smoke escaped her nostrils, smelling of cedar and hot iron. She'd been doing that more lately. The flame was coming easier now, fed on spoken memories the way other creatures were fed on meat. Kestrel had given her the smell of Odile's ledger-room, the taste of rain on the guild roof, the exact pitch of Serah's laugh when she forgot to be guarded. It felt less like spending and more like planting. Mostly.

Kestrel looked up at the Spire again. Somewhere near its crown, coiled around whatever passed for a summit, was Ythirra. The last dragon. The fire that sustained a nation that officially never existed. Kestrel had seen her from the ridge on the day they crossed into Vhalcrest—a shape like a mountain that had decided to move, dimming, guttering, her fire visible across the whole sky like a wound in the weather.

Lady Ashvael wanted Kestrel's blood for the covenant. Wanted it drawn, measured, bound into ritual on her terms: eternal, controlled, hers. Kestrel had spent one night in the Court of the Almost Forgotten being fed things too rich to name and smiled at too warmly to trust, and had understood, with the particular clarity of someone who has appraised cursed objects professionally, exactly what kind of cage courtesy can be.

So they had slipped out before dawn. Well—"slipped out" implied stealth. In truth Kestrel had walked out the postern gate past two guards who looked directly at them and then, with the practiced blankness of people who have decided not to see things for decades, looked away again. Vhalcrest's people were translucent with forgetting, but they remembered how to not-see. It was practically a civic art.

"You could just fly me up," Kestrel said to Ember. "You have wings now. Enormous ones. I've seen them knock over a market stall."

"I could," Ember agreed. "But she asked for *you*. Not you-and-luggage." A pause, and then, gentler than Ember ever managed on purpose: "Also, I think she wants to know if you'll climb. Dragons care about that sort of thing. It's very annoying."

"What sort of thing?"

"Whether you come to them under your own power." Ember looked up at the Spire, and her pupils went thin and ancient in a way that made her look suddenly much older than a creature who still chewed on architecture. "My mothers' mothers' memories say the old Speakers always climbed. Even the ones who could have flown."

Kestrel exhaled slowly. Put both palms flat against the warm black stone.

"Fine," they said. "Hello? I'm Kestrel Okonkwo. I keep ledgers. I'm told we have business."

For a moment, nothing. Then the stone beneath their hands *shivered*—not an earthquake shiver, but something more like a cat stretching, or a great door unsealing—and a seam of amber light opened in the Spire's face, spiraling upward in a slow helix. Steps. Rough-hewn, uneven, wide enough for comfort and narrow enough to make a point.

"Well," said Kestrel. "That's showing off."

* * *

The climb took hours.

That wasn't a complaint—it was, Kestrel came to understand, part of the design. Each turn of the spiral brought a change in the air, in the light, in the sounds of the country below. Vhalcrest spread out beneath them like a tapestry left too long in the sun: fields gone grey at their edges, villages where the rooftops were solid but the people moving between them had a certain quality of almost, orchards whose fruit was perfect and tasted of nothing. Beautiful decay. A painting of a country holding its breath.

And everywhere, threaded through all of it, the pyre-light from the summit, guttering.

Kestrel talked while they climbed, because silence on stairs this meaningful felt like bad manners, and because talking kept the fear at a manageable volume. They talked to themselves, mostly, and occasionally to Ember, who flew lazy circles alongside the open spiral and offered commentary of wildly variable usefulness.

"—and that's why you never appraise a relic while the seller is crying," Kestrel said, rounding another landing. "Sympathy inflates value. Odile taught me that. She said grief is the most common counterfeit in the trade, and also the only one that isn't a crime, which tells you everything about the trade."

Below them, faintly, a bell rang in some village. Or maybe it didn't. Maybe it was remembering ringing.

By midday—or what Kestrel guessed was midday; the sky over Vhalcrest held its light strangely, like a cup filled to the brim—the air began to change. Warmer. Thicker. It smelled of woodsmoke and myrrh and something older underneath, something like the inside of an egg, like the moment before hatching. And Kestrel began to hear it.

Dragonsong.

They'd heard it first through the map, weeks ago, a hum in the bones when they touched the shifting paper. But this was the source. This was the well itself. It wasn't sound so much as *pressure*—a vast slow note that lived below hearing and above endurance, and Kestrel's whole body recognized it the way a river recognizes the sea.

Their gift woke fully for the first time since crossing the border. Every beast-sense they had ever carefully, painstakingly, *disciplined* into silence came open at once like windows in spring. Kestrel staggered against the wall and gripped it, gasping, and the stone was warm and alive under their hands, and somewhere far below a field mouse going about its morning paused and looked up at the Spire and *knew*, somehow, that the speaker had arrived home.

"Oh," Kestrel said aloud, to nobody, to everybody. "Oh, that's what it's supposed to feel like."

* * *

The summit was a crater.

Not a violent one—an old one, softened by centuries, its rim worn smooth as a bowl's lip. And filling the crater, coiled in concentric rings like a written word, lay Ythirra.

Kestrel had prepared for enormous. They had prepared for terrible. They had prepared, in the small honest hours of the night, for a monster.

What they found was a grandmother.

Ythirra was vast beyond sense, yes—her smallest scale would have served as a shield, her eye alone was the size of the guild's round table—but the *quality* of her was not terror. She was the color of cooling charcoal shot through with veins of dying gold, and her fire, the famous fire, the fire that burned away lies and sustained a hidden nation, rose from her in threads so thin they were nearly transparent. Guttering. The word Ashvael used was accurate and cruel. Ythirra burned the way a candle burns in its last hour: faithfully, beautifully, and with an arithmetic everyone in the room understands.

Her head lifted as Kestrel stepped onto the crater's rim. One eye found them—molten, patient, ringed with lids that closed sideways like a book shutting.

"*Little ledger-keeper,*" said Ythirra.

Her voice did not arrive through the ears. It arrived the way the dragonsong did, through bone and blood, and it was tired in a way that made Kestrel's chest ache, the tiredness of someone who has been holding a door shut for three hundred years and has recently begun doing the math on how much longer they can manage it.

"Ma'am," said Kestrel, and then, because twenty years of guild training died hard, "I'd bow, but I'm carrying nothing valuable and I'm afraid of heights, so please consider the sentiment conveyed."

Something moved through the vast body—a ripple, a shudder—and it took Kestrel a full three seconds to identify it, because they had never once heard a dragon laugh.

"*Odile trained you well.*"

"You knew Odile?" Kestrel's heart did something complicated. "You know her?"

"*I knew her sister.*" The golden eye dimmed, briefly, like a lamp turned down in respect. "*Maren walked these steps forty years ago. She climbed badly, complained constantly, and spoke to my bones as if we were old friends within the first minute. She was meant to be the Speaker. She chose instead to go back out and try to bring others through. The border took its toll. Names fade, out there. Hers faded last of all, and even that was a long, brave fading.*"

Kestrel stood very still. Somewhere below, a whole city of almost-people moved through their almost-day, and one of them, in a cell or a chamber or a gilded cage, had spent twenty years remembering a sister the world was built to forget.

"She never stopped looking," Kestrel said quietly. "Odile. There was a page in her oldest ledger. About Vhalcrest. She burned it rather than let the Inquisitors find it, but she kept looking. She sent me here with coin and a false trail and her blessing, and she pretended it was logistics because that's how she says love."

"*Yes,*" said Ythirra. "*That is how love is said, in your family. In ledgers and provisions and doors left unlocked. It is why you are here, little keeper. Do you know why I called you?*"

"Because Ashvael wants my blood and you want... something else. I'm hoping."

"*Come closer. Sit, if your legs will permit it. Climbing is sacred but knees are mortal.*"

Kestrel sat on the crater's rim, legs dangling over a drop that would have made a fine ballad, and Ythirra lowered her head until the great molten eye was level with them, close enough that Kestrel could see their own reflection swimming in it—small, smudged, unmistakably present.

"*Your line descends from the first Speaker,*" Ythirra said. "*This is not a mystery. It is a record. When Vhalcrest was young, the covenant was made—not between dragon and crown, whatever the regent's rituals claim, but between dragon and person. Freely. A woman named Sefa Okonwe came up these same steps, out of breath and furious about it, and she did not ask me for fire. She offered me her name. Her true name, freely given, in friendship. And I gave mine in return. That exchange is the covenant. Everything else—the rituals, the blood, the bindings—is scaffolding built later by people who wanted the fire without the friendship.*"

"And the Speakers since? My mother? Her mother?"

"*Keepers of the exchange. Not owners of it. Your gift—your speaking—is not a tool you inherited. It is a relationship you inherited. The beasts answer you because your blood remembers being answered. That is why the Crown fears it. A beast-speaker cannot be lied to by the world. And Vhalcrest exists as a lie the world agreed to tell.*"

Kestrel looked down at their own hands. Ledger-keeper's hands, ink-stained, careful. Hands that had spent thirty years pretending they were ordinary.

"My mother told me to bury it," they said. "She caught me talking to the neighbor's hens when I was six, and she went white as salt, and she said—she said, 'Never where anyone can hear. Never. They took the Aldous boy for less.' And she was right. She was right, and I hated her a little for it, and I loved her completely for it, and I buried it so deep I half-believed it was buried." They laughed, short and unsteady. "And now it turns out it wasn't a curse to hide. It was a *post* to keep."

"*Yes.*" Gentle. Terribly gentle. "*Destiny is a chain only when it is put on you. Worn freely, it is a cloak. I will not chain you, Kestrel Okonkwo. Understand that before anything else. Ashvael will offer you chains dressed as crowns. I offer you a question.*"

"Ask it."

"*Will you renew the covenant? Not the binding. The friendship. Will you give me your name—not extracted, not ritually drawn, not surrendered—but given, the way one friend gives another a key? And receive mine in return? The fire answers only that. It always has. It cannot be forced, which is precisely why it is honest, and precisely why it is dying, because honesty requires two willing parties and I have been alone at this altar for three hundred years.*"

The wind moved through the crater. Below, the pyre-threads flickered, and somewhere in the grey fields a farmer straightened up and rubbed his eyes as if waking.

Kestrel found, to their surprise, that they were crying. Not dramatically—just leaking, the way old roofs do, steadily and without announcement.

"I spent my whole life," they said, "being very good at not mattering. Invisible on purpose. Careful ledgers, quiet rooms, conversations with horses conducted in whispers. And every single piece of it—the guild, the hiding, the discipline—I told myself it was survival. And it was. But some of it, if I'm honest, and you burn lies away so I suppose I have to be honest—some of it was because mattering *terrified* me. If I mattered, I could be taken. Like the Aldous boy. Like Maren. Like everyone who ever climbed toward something."

"*And now?*"

"Now I'm sitting on top of the world talking to a dragon, so I think the hiding has formally failed." Kestrel wiped their face with the heel of their hand and laughed wetly. "You should know that I don't give my name easily. It's the one asset I've never traded. Ask anyone. I once talked a smuggler out of a fortune rather than tell him my surname."

"*I know,*" said Ythirra, with unmistakable warmth. "*It is in your blood. Sefa haggled with me for a full hour before giving hers. She got a mountain renamed out of me. I regret nothing.*"

Kestrel laughed—really laughed, helpless, echoing off the crater walls—and Ember, circling below, looked up at the sound and let out a small confused puff of smoke, because in all their months together she had rarely heard that particular laugh.

Then Kestrel sobered. Looked into the molten eye. Felt the guttering fire, felt the weight of a nation balanced on a candle-flame, felt Ashvael below in her citadel preparing rituals and restraints, felt Serah somewhere in the city watching the doors, felt Odile in a cell writing love in the form of smuggled letters.

"One condition," Kestrel said. "One. Before I give anything."

"*Name it.*"

"When we do this—when the names are exchanged and the fire comes back—you don't make me a queen. You don't make me a saint or a symbol or a sword the country points at its enemies. I've met a crown. It tried to kill me. I want to go back to keeping ledgers. Big ones, now, admittedly. But ledgers."

Ythirra's laugh rolled through Kestrel's skeleton like distant, benevolent thunder.

"*Little keeper,*" she said, "*if the fire comes back, you may keep whatever ledgers you like. That, I swear on my name—which, I will remind you, I am about to trust you with.*"

Kestrel took a breath. Held it. Let it go.

"Then ask me," they said. "Properly. The way friends do."

And Ythirra drew herself up—all that vastness, all that age, gathering for one more act of faith—and the pyre-threads stilled, and the whole country below seemed to lean in without knowing why—

Which was when the bells began.

Not the soft, forgetful village bells. War-bells. Citadel bells, clanging down from the Court of the Almost Forgotten, and beneath them, rising on the wind, a sound Kestrel knew with sick certainty: the high ceremonial drone of Ashvael's ritual choir, beginning early, beginning *now*, and threading through it all, faint and desperate, a gull's cry with a letter tied to its leg that would never reach them in time.

Ythirra's eye flared gold.

"*She has felt you climbing,*" the dragon said, and for the first time in three hundred years, there was fear in her voice. "*We have less time than we thought, little keeper. So I must ask you now, quickly, before her ritual steals what was meant to be given—*"
