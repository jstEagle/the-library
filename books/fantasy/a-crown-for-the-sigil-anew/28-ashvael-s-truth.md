## 28. Ashvael's Truth

There were four hundred and eleven names in the Vhalcrest court registry, and Lady Ashvael had recited every one of them every morning for twenty years.

She did it in the old tongue, standing at the window of the Spire's guest wing with her hands folded behind her back like a woman inspecting troops, because a regent who wept was a regent who had lost, and she had not lost yet. She began with the king's name—her father's name—and worked downward through the dukes and the chamberlain and the mistress of the royal aviary and the boy who swept the aviary steps, whose name was Pell, who had once traded her a honey-cake for a look at the falcon eggs, and who had been thirty-one years dead for twenty years and did not know it.

That was the cruelty of being the only one who remembered. The dead stayed dead either way. But the living—the living went on sweeping their steps, tending their fires, singing their harvest songs inside a country that the rest of the world had agreed, all at once, on a Tuesday, had never existed.

* * *

She had been in the archives when it happened.

That was the detail that still woke her some nights—not the light, not the silence, but the sheer bureaucratic mundanity of it. She had been cataloguing treaty copies, ink-stained to the elbow, when the sound of the world changed. It was not an explosion. It was the opposite of an explosion: a great soft intake of breath, everywhere at once, as if the sky had drawn in its cheeks.

Then the bells stopped mid-peal. Not silenced—*unrung*, the way a word can be unsaid only in dreams. She ran into the courtyard and found the guards at their posts, blinking, and asked them what they'd heard, and Captain Dreyer—who had held her father's banner at Marrow Ford, who had carried her on his shoulders when she was six—looked at her with polite blankness and said, "Heard, my lady?"

"The bells," she said. "The noon bells."

"There are no bells in Vhalcrest, my lady." He said it kindly. He said it the way one humors a child or a madwoman, and behind him, faint as a candle seen through fog, the bell tower stood exactly where it had always stood, with the great bronze bell hanging in it, unrung, unringable, forgotten by the very metal it was cast from.

Only Ythirra's fire had kept the country real after that—a thin gold thread of dragonflame holding four hundred and eleven names and forty thousand souls against the tide of forgetting. And Ythirra was dying now. Ashvael had watched the pyre atop the Spire gutter from a bonfire to a beacon to a candle, and she knew what happened when the last of it went out. The country would not die. Countries do not get the mercy of dying. It would simply *stop having ever been*, and Pell's honey-cake, and her father's laugh, and the harvest songs, would dissolve into the general gray of things that never happened anywhere to anyone.

So yes. She would burn anything.

She had made that decision years ago, calmly, over cold tea, and she had never once regretted it, because regret was a luxury for people whose nations still existed.

* * *

The trouble with burning anything, she had learned, was inventory. What did you burn first? The Inquisitors' ships, when they came sniffing at the border-mist? The traitors within—there were always traitors within, men like Corvel who whispered that perhaps the world had forgotten Vhalcrest for a reason? Herself?

She had settled, in the end, on a hierarchy, which she kept written in cipher in the back of her father's psalter:

*First, whatever stands between the fire and renewal.*

Currently, that was a beast-speaker from beyond the border with kind eyes and a wyvern chick on their shoulder, and a defected Inquisitor with a sword callus and worse eyes, and—she consulted the report from the gate wardens with distaste—an increasingly large wyvern who had eaten two of the ceremonial orchard trees and appeared unrepentant.

"Unrepentant," muttered her steward, Master Ilven, reading over her shoulder. He was a thin man with the mournful face of a bloodhound and the loyalty of one too, which is to say absolute and slightly slobbery. "The trees were four hundred years old."

"So is our covenant, and it is dying faster than the trees did. Write to the orchard-keeper's family an apology and a stipend."

"They're translucent, my lady. Half of them won't remember owning an orchard by spring."

"Then write it anyway." She set down the report. "We keep accounts here, Ilven. That is the whole point of me. If Vhalcrest forgets itself, I will be the ledger."

Ilven bowed and withdrew, and Ashvael stood alone with the morning light—which came through the windows grayer than it used to, filtered through a sky that forgot how blue it was meant to be a little more each season—and permitted herself, for exactly sixty heartbeats by the water-clock, to grieve.

This was discipline, not weakness. Her mother had taught her that grief left untended grew wild as ivy and pulled down walls; grief kept to its plot fed the soil. Sixty heartbeats. She spent them on the bell tower, and on Pell, and on the specific unbearable image of her father's face smoothing into polite blankness the day he stopped knowing her name—no, that was not right, he had known her name, he had simply stopped believing there had ever been a kingdom for her to be lady of, and had recommended, gently, that she see the physicians about her fancies.

The physicians. Of course. There were no physicians in Vhalcrest anymore either. There was no anything. There was a country-shaped hole in the world with a candle burning in the middle of it, and she was the wax.

At heartbeat fifty-nine she straightened her spine, and at sixty she was Regent again, and the grief went back in its plot like a well-trained hound.

* * *

They had arrived three days ago, the speaker and the sword-woman, and Ashvael had welcomed them with everything the ruined court could muster: clean linens, hot food, the good wine from cellars that no longer remembered why they were deep. Hospitality was armor. Hospitality was also, if you did it properly, a cage with the door painted on.

She had watched Kestrel Okonkwo across the welcome-table with the attention of a jeweler assessing a stone. Young. Tired. Wary in the way of people who had learned that safety was seasonal. And underneath the wariness—she had seen it flicker when the doors opened onto the valley, when the dim fields and the pale-faced farmers came into view—grief. Recognition. The look of someone arriving at a funeral for a relative they never knew they had.

Good. Grief could be aimed.

It was the other one who worried her. Serah Vale. Ashvael had read the woman's file—or rather, she had reconstructed it, because the Crown's files on its own defectors were among the few documents about the outer world that still crossed the border-mist, smuggled in by the gulls, bless their thieving feathered hearts. Ex-Inquisitor. Disciplined. And defecting out of love rather than conviction, which meant her convictions were intact and merely outranked. A woman like that could be bought with nothing and sold for everything.

Love. Ashvael turned the word over like a coin she suspected of being clipped. She had loved Vhalcrest for twenty years with a devotion that would have made poets weep, and where had it gotten her? Alone in a country of fading faces, reciting names to a window. Love was fuel. Nothing more. Anyone who told you otherwise wanted something.

She had wanted something once too, though. She could admit that much in the privacy of her own skull. She had wanted, in the first terrible year, for someone—anyone—to cross the mist and say, *You're real. I remember you now.* She had imagined it so many times the imagining wore grooves: the stranger at the gates, the recognition, the weeping, the world tilting back into place.

And then the stranger had actually come, with a map pressed into their hands by a dying courier, and Ashvael had looked at Kestrel Okonkwo across her own hospitality and thought, with a clarity that frightened even her: *Ah. So this is what hope looks like when it finally arrives. It looks like a problem.*

Because the covenant did not want a believer. It wanted a Speaker of the old blood, freely given, freely speaking—and the moment Kestrel spoke Ythirra's name, the fire would renew, and the mist would burn away, and Vhalcrest would stand undeniable under the sun of the outer world again.

Unless the Crown got there first. Unless the Inquisitors crossed the door and did to Vhalcrest what they had done to the Speakers' villages—except this time there would be no erasure to hide behind, because erasure was precisely what was failing. No. When the door opened, it opened onto witnesses. Everything Vhalcrest did in that hour would be seen, and judged, and remembered forever.

Which meant the hour had to be hers.

* * *

"You don't trust them." Ilven again, that evening, in the map room where the charts of a country no atlas admitted to hung in careful rows. He said it without judgment. Twenty years of stewarding a ghost nation had burned judgment out of him like impurities out of steel.

"I trust their usefulness. Those are different animals." Ashvael traced the border on the great chart—the true border, the one beneath the shimmer. "The speaker carries the name in their blood. Whether they know it yet is immaterial. Blood knows what minds are slow to learn."

"And the Inquisitor?"

"Is a lockpick I haven't decided whether to use." She moved her finger north, to where the outer world lay like a sleeping dog. "Vess is coming, Ilven. The gulls brought word. A full company, following the false trail our guests left—no." She corrected herself, tasting the shape of it. "Following the trail someone *wanted* her to find. Our beast-speaker lies the way water flows downhill. Effortlessly, and toward a purpose."

"Does that trouble you?"

"It delights me." She smiled, and knew it was the smile that made hardened border-guards find reasons to be elsewhere. "A hunter with doubts, walking into a country her doctrine says cannot exist. Either she breaks, or she converts, or she dies. All three are useful. Two of them are tidy."

Ilven studied her for a long moment with his bloodhound sorrow. "My lady. May I say a thing I have wanted to say for nineteen years?"

"You may say anything. It's whether I listen that's rationed."

"When the fire renews—" He chose his words like a man crossing ice. "When the mist burns away and the world sees us again. What will you be to them? A regent? Or a woman who locked her guests in their rooms and bled their blood into a ritual?"

Ashvael went very still.

She had, in fact, already drawn the blood. A cup of it, taken while Kestrel slept off the road, by a physician who no longer remembered being a physician and therefore had no scruples to overcome. It sat in a warded jar in the ritual vault below the Spire, and it was the single most valuable object in a country full of dying treasures, and she had told herself it was insurance. Insurance against refusal. Insurance against the speaker's inconvenient conscience, their tiresome talk of *freely given names* and *love*, as if covenants were marriages.

Ythirra had told them that, she'd learned. The old serpent at the top of the Spire, spending her last strength on gossip. *A freely given name spoken in love.* As if love had ever held a border. Love had not kept her father from forgetting her. Love had not kept forty thousand people from going translucent at the edges like old fruit. Fire kept borders. Fire and will and a woman willing to be the villain of her own story so that the story continued at all.

"My lady?" Ilven prompted gently.

"I will be whoever history needs me to be," she said. "History has never once needed anyone to be kind."

* * *

She climbed the Spire that night, because she always climbed it on the eve of decisions, and because Ythirra was the only creature alive who shared her memories, and there are nights when a person needs to not be the only one.

The great dragon lay coiled around the pyre like a moored ship, scales gone from gold to the color of old brass, eyes half-lidded and banked as coals. The fire on the altar before her snout burned low and steady, and its light caught the mist-wall far below and made the whole hidden valley glow like a lamp under a shawl.

"You climb these stairs slower every year," Ythirra rumbled. Her voice was vast and tired, a landslide that had learned to whisper.

"You shrink every year. We are even."

"We are not even, little regent. You have your four hundred and eleven names. I have mine, and mine are longer." One enormous eye opened fully, ember-bright. "You've taken the speaker's blood."

Not a question. Dragons smelled everything, and presumably smelled guilt best of all.

"Insurance," Ashvael said.

"Mm." The eye regarded her. "I have eaten kings, you know. Before the Wars. Kings taste of fear and flattery. Do you know what the speaker tastes like, from up here, when the wind turns?"

"Ashvael did not answer.

"Like a door," Ythirra said, "that hasn't decided whether to open. And you—" the great head shifted, and the firelight ran along jaws that had shaped a nation's covenant, "—you smell like a woman standing in front of a door with a crowbar, telling herself it's a key."

"The ritual will work. Your own law says the blood of the founding line renews the flame. Their blood is the founding line. I have the scholars' proof, the genealogies, the—"

"My law says the flame is renewed by a name *given*. Given, child. Not extracted, not bled, not bartered. You cannot steal a gift and call it generosity." The dragon sighed, and the sigh stirred the pyre-flames, and for one instant the fire leapt high and gold and young, and Ashvael's heart leapt with it before it sank again. "But you know this. You have always known this. You simply cannot afford to know it."

"I cannot afford *anything else*," Ashvael said, and heard her voice crack on the last word, and hated it, and pressed on anyway, because a regent who wept was a regent who had lost. "Twenty years, Ythirra. I have recited four hundred and eleven names ten thousand mornings. I have watched my father look through me. I have buried a country that refuses to lie still. And now the instrument of our salvation walks my halls eating my orchards and talking about *love*, and you tell me I must wait for them to feel tenderly on their own schedule while your fire gutters like a tallow-end?" She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "No. If tenderness is too slow, I will use what works. Let history judge me. History owes me. I kept its books when everyone else tore out the page."

The dragon was quiet a long time. Below them, somewhere in the dimming fields, a farmer sang the harvest song to crops that were forgetting how to ripen, and the song drifted up the Spire thin and sweet and unbearably sad.

"Grief makes a fine sword," Ythirra said at last, gently, which was somehow worse than anger. "But you have been holding it so long, little regent, you have forgotten it is not your hand."

Ashvael turned and walked down the stairs and did not look back, because looking back was how regents lost arguments with dragons.

In the ritual vault, by lamplight, she checked the warded jar of blood, and the circle inscriptions, and the timing tables for the storm-front gathering over the northern peaks—three days, perhaps four, when the sky's own fury could mask the working from any Inquisitor scrying. Three days. She had waited twenty years. She could wait three days.

She told herself the small cold feeling in her chest was resolve.

She was almost successful.

* * *

On the second morning, the gulls brought her a message that changed the arithmetic.

It came tied to a gull's leg in the usual cipher, from her watcher at the drowned archway, and Ashvael read it twice in the map room while Ilven hovered, and then she sat down, which she almost never did, which made Ilven go the color of porridge.

"What is it, my lady?"

"The Inquisitor captain," Ashvael said slowly. "Vess. She's crossed the door."

"She—alone?"

"With a warrant, a company behind her, and—" Ashvael laughed, short and disbelieving, "—and according to my watcher, she stood at the archway for a quarter hour staring at our valley, and then took off her gloves before crossing. Took off her *gloves*, Ilven. Inquisitors wear those gloves like priests wear vestments. Something out there has already gotten its teeth into her certainty."

"That's good for us, surely. Divided command—"

"It is neither good nor bad. It is *fast*." Ashvael stood, and the old familiar cold filled her, the cold that had kept a country alive for twenty years, and she was grateful for it, the way one is grateful for an old coat. "Everything accelerates now. The speaker's friends march, the hunter hunts, the storm comes early. We begin the ritual tomorrow night, ready or not."

"And the speaker?"

Ashvael looked out the window, down at the guest wing, where a light still burned in a certain room—late hours, whispered conversations, a certain sword-woman's silhouette moving past the shutter-cracks. Planning something. Of course they were planning something. Doors that hadn't decided whether to open generally preferred to make up their minds in secret.

"Move them to the tower rooms tonight," she said. "For their protection."

Ilven wrote it down. He had long ago stopped asking whose protection, and Ashvael had long ago stopped pretending the answer wasn't *mine*.

Outside, the first fat drops of the early storm struck the windows, and far above, through the gray, the pyre-fire on the Spire flickered like a pulse counting down—and in the vault below her feet, in its warded jar, the blood of the last Speaker waited, patient as a debt, to be called due.
