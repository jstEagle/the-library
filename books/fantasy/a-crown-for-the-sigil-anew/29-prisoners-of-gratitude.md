## 29. Prisoners of Gratitude

### Kestrel

The cell they gave us had a window.

That was the first thing I noticed, and the first thing that should have frightened me, because prisons built by people who mean it don't have windows. Prisons built by people who want you to *like* them have windows—tall ones, arched ones, framed in pale stone that caught the light of Ythirra's guttering fire and scattered it across the floor like coins someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up.

"Guest quarters," Lady Ashvael had called them, with a smile that could have been carved by a very sincere sculptor.

"Cells," Serah had said, under her breath.

"Cells with excellent light," I'd said, because if you're going to be locked up, you may as well be accurate about the amenities.

The room was round, which I decided was a design choice meant to disorient, or possibly an architectural apology—the Spire's builders had run out of corners. There were two beds, a washbasin that filled itself from some clever pipe in the wall, a table, two chairs, and a door that never had a guard standing outside it because it didn't need one. The lock was a sigil-work seal that hummed when you touched it, a low note like a struck bell heard through water. Ember had tried biting it once. The sigil had hummed *louder*. Ember had taken this personally.

"They're feeding us," Serah said on our third morning, pushing her bread around the plate as though it might confess something. "Good bread. Fresh fruit. There was honey yesterday, Kestrel. *Honey.*"

"I know. I ate it."

"You ate my honey."

"There was one jar."

"And you ate it."

"We're prisoners of gratitude," I said, which was becoming our private name for the arrangement. "Gratitude is famously generous with its preserves."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she went back to interrogating the bread, and I went back to the thing I did every morning now: sitting cross-legged by the window with Ember draped around my shoulders like a scarf with opinions, telling him stories.

Not stories for comfort. Stories for *food*.

Because here was what we'd learned about wyverns, and about Ember specifically: he grew on memory. Not meat, not grain—memory. Speak something true and vivid and yours aloud, and he'd drink it in through whatever sense dragons used for such things, and his scales would come in a shade brighter, his wings a finger's width broader. Ashvael's people fed him meat out of courtesy. I fed him myself out of necessity, because we were going to need a dragon at some point—I didn't know when, and I didn't know how big, but every instinct I owned, all the borrowed instincts of rats and crows and cart-horses, agreed on this the way they rarely agreed on anything.

The trouble was that memories are not an infinite pantry.

"The guild kitchen," I said, and Ember's head came up off my shoulder, eyes whirling slow amber. "Midwinter. Odile had the whole guild mending nets by the hearth because there was nothing else to do, and she told the story of the bottled storm—we keep it on the third shelf, blue glass, it hums when rain is coming—and she swore to every god she didn't believe in that she'd once shaken it to see what happened, and it had hailed *inside the house*, only in the pantry, only on the cheese—"

Ember made a sound like a kettle deciding to be happy. Warmth spread down my spine where he lay. Somewhere behind my ribs, the memory went thin—not gone, but faded, like a page read too many times. I could still see the pantry. I could no longer quite smell the cheese.

"That one's getting expensive," I murmured.

"Then stop spending," Serah said without looking up. She'd developed a habit of listening while pretending not to, which I suspected was Inquisitor training and also just being Serah. "You've given him the hearth-song's cousin, the market day, the flood year, your mother's hands—"

"My mother's hands cost nothing. That one I'd give twice."

"—and half of Odile's sayings. You're going to arrive at this great confrontation you keep not describing to me and not remember why any of it matters."

"I'll remember why it matters." I scratched Ember under the chin, where his scales were coming in the color of new copper. "I just might not remember the cheese."

Serah set down the bread. She crossed the room—six steps; the room was not large—and sat across from me, and took my hands, which she did perhaps four times a year and always for cause.

"Tell me the plan," she said. "All of it. You've been carrying it alone since the cells, and I let you, because you looked like a man carrying water who doesn't want to spill any. But Kestrel. We're prisoners. The one luxury captivity offers is time, and you're spending ours on cheese anecdotes."

I looked at Ember. Ember looked at me with the serene confidence of a creature who believed, absolutely, that everything would work out, possibly because he'd eaten so many of my memories that he knew how most of mine ended.

"All right," I said. "But you're not going to like the shape of it."

"I never like the shape of anything. Tell me anyway."

### Serah

The plan had three parts, and every part of it depended on things going wrong, which Kestrel presented as though this were a feature rather than a flaw.

"One," he said, counting on fingers, "we learn the Spire. Every passage, every stair, every place the sigil-seals don't reach. Two, we find the other Speakers—Ashvael said herself there were survivors in the wilds, she said it like a threat, but it was a map. Three, we get to Ythirra before Ashvael's ritual does, and we do it *our* way. Freely. Or not at all."

"And the part I won't like?"

He had the grace to look slightly sheepish. It was a good look on him. It was also, I had learned, usually the look right before the worst sentence of the conversation.

"The part where I'm bait."

There it was.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. Any plan with you as bait is a plan I've already rejected, and I rejected it the first time in a smuggler's den in Greyharbor, and I'll reject it every time until one of us dies of old age surrounded by grandchildren who are sick of hearing about it."

"Serah." He said my name the way he said names generally—carefully, like each one was a small animal he didn't want to startle. "Ashvael wants me. Me specifically, my blood, my line. Everything she does bends toward getting me somewhere she can use me. If I walk into her ritual willingly, on my own terms, at a moment of *my* choosing—"

"She'll take the choice away the moment you're in reach. That's what people like her do. I was people like her. I know the trade."

He was quiet a moment. Through the window, Ythirra's fire pulsed above the valley—a heartbeat of gold, then dimmer, then gold again, like a lamp running out of oil in a house full of sleeping people.

"Then help me make it not-bait," he said finally. "Help me make it a door. You keep saying doors open from both sides. Fine. Teach me to be a door instead of a lamb."

I wanted to argue. I was, professionally speaking, extremely good at arguing; I'd done it for a living, in grey coats, in rooms with no windows, against people who had then been taken away. But he wasn't wrong, and worse than that, he wasn't reckless—he was the most careful person I'd ever met, careful the way a locksmith is careful, and the fact that his one reckless idea was also his best idea was the kind of irony I'd stopped expecting the world to spare me.

"Fine," I said. "A door. But we build it properly, and we build it together, and step one starts tonight."

"Which is?"

"Mapping." I stood and went to the washbasin, and pulled aside the panel I'd loosened two days ago with a hairpin and eleven years of professional resentment toward badly maintained plumbing. Behind it ran a service channel—dry, narrow, smelling of old stone and older dust. "Inquisitors are trained to memorize buildings. Corridors, sightlines, guard rotations, choke points. They taught us with models. I built models of forty fortresses before I was twenty-two." I glanced back at him. "Nobody ever thought to teach the prisoners the same trick."

* * *

We worked by the light of Ythirra's fire, which was fitting, though I didn't say so. Kestrel drew on the floor with charcoal stubs saved from supper—his idea, and a good one, because charcoal wipes away with a sleeve and looks like nothing more than a prisoner's boredom.

His gift did the listening. Mine did the remembering.

It worked like this: the Spire was full of small lives. Mice in the walls, moths in the rafters, a colony of bats in the bell-loft who had, according to Kestrel, "opinions about the acoustics and no respect for anyone's schedule." He'd sit with his eyes half-closed and his palm flat to the stone, and the building would tell him where it ached—which corridors the servants avoided, which stairwell echoed strangely, which stretch of the east wall was hollow. And I'd translate hollow walls and echoing stairs into architecture, into routes, into a model growing across our floor in charcoal and crumbs.

"The kitchens again," he said one night, frowning at the stone. "Something's wrong in the kitchens. The mice are upset. Well—" he amended, "the mice are always upset, they're mice, but they're *specifically* upset. Something below them. Big. Cold."

"Ashvael's ritual chamber," I said, marking it. "Has to be. Under the kitchens is under the Spire's root, and the root is where you put the thing you never want found." I drew the chamber as a circle, then hesitated. "How cold?"

"Cold like the drowned borderlands. Cold like—" He stopped. His hand lifted off the stone. "Cold like something that remembers being warm."

We left that room on the map unmarked except for a single X, and neither of us suggested going near it, and both of us knew we eventually would.

### Kestrel

On the ninth day of captivity, Ember outgrew the window alcove.

This was announced to us by a crash, a clatter of displaced masonry, and a wyvern the size of a large dog standing in the wreckage of his own sleeping spot looking profoundly betrayed by physics.

"You're growing too fast," I told him, hauling rubble. "You need to pace yourself."

He chirped at me, insufferably smug, and I felt the truth of it settle in my chest where our bond lived: he was nearly the size of a pony now, wings tight-folded, scales gone from copper to true gold at the edges. Nine days. At this rate—

At this rate, he'd be ready. Whatever *ready* meant. Ready for the Spire-top. Ready for a dying dragon and a regent with a ritual and a door that needed opening from both sides.

The cost sat in me like a stone I'd swallowed. I inventoried it that night, quietly, while Serah slept: the hearth-song, spent. The smell of Odile's ledger-room, spent. My mother's laugh—I'd given him the sound of it, and now when I reached for it I got the shape of the sound without the sound itself, a bell with the clapper removed. The taste of the honey. The exact blue of the bottled storm. Market day in the rain, spent. The neighbor boy's name—

No. I'd kept that one back. Some debts you don't pay even to dragons.

"You're doing the inventory face," Serah said from her bed, without opening her eyes.

"I don't have an inventory face."

"You have three faces. Inventory, stubborn, and the one you make at cheese. This is inventory."

I lay back and stared at the ceiling, where the firelight moved like water. "He has to eat," I said. "And I'm the only larder he trusts."

"Then we ration." She rolled onto her side, propped her head on her hand. In the firelight her scar—the one from Greyharbor, the one she'd never explained—caught gold. "Give him the cheap ones. Give him the ones you want to keep alive by telling anyway. That's the trick, isn't it? A memory spoken in love feeds him double. You said so."

"I said the healer's theory was—"

"You said it, and you tested it, and it held. So stop feeding him grief, Kestrel. Feed him hope. You've got more of that than you think." A pause, and then, drier: "You gave half of it to me on a riverbank in the borderlands. I'm holding it in trust. Consider this a withdrawal notice."

I laughed—actually laughed, in a cell, in a country that officially didn't exist, nine days into a captivity I was engineering my way out of. It startled both of us. Ember woke up, assessed the situation, decided laughter required participation, and set the curtains gently alight.

We put him out. Mostly. The scorch mark stayed, and after that the guards began leaving extra wood in our grate, which Serah insisted was a coincidence and I insisted was the Spire itself taking our side, and we agreed to disagree the way we agreed to disagree about everything: thoroughly, and while holding hands under the table where no one could prove it.

### Serah

By the twelfth day, the map covered half the floor, and I hated it, because it was good.

That was the problem with Inquisitor training: it worked. Give me a building's bones and I could tell you where it would break. And the Spire's bones told a story I didn't like—told it in load-bearing walls and service channels and one, exactly one, unguarded route from the guest cells to the upper terraces, winding through the bell-loft where the disrespectful bats lived.

"It's too easy," I said, crouched over the charcoal lines for the fourth evening running. "Ashvael's people built this seal on our door. They watch the stairs. They rotate patrols. And yet there's a servant channel from the laundry to the bell-loft with no wards at all."

"Maybe they missed it."

"Inquisitors don't miss things. I didn't miss things." I traced the channel with one finger. "Which means it's not missed. Which means it's *left*. Which means either it's a trap, or—"

"Or she wants us to move," Kestrel finished quietly. "Wants us loose in the Spire. Wants us to come to her, on her ground, so the taking of us looks like an accident of our own making." He sat back. "Gods. She's playing hostess even in the trap."

"Or it's Vess."

We both went still. Captain Vess's name had become, over the weeks, a strange kind of talisman between us—spoken carefully, like a coin of uncertain currency. Kestrel had sent the false ledger trail straight to her, months ago, and I'd argued even then that Vess was a hound who would follow any trail to its end, including this one, including through a door that shouldn't exist.

"If Vess came through," Kestrel said slowly, "she'd be asking questions. Loud ones. Ashvael would have to manage her—house her, watch her, keep her away from the parts of the truth that would crack her doctrine wide open." He looked at the unguarded channel. "You'd keep a dog like that near the kennels. Near the cells."

"Near *us*," I said. Because that was the other possibility, the one I liked least: that the open channel wasn't for us to escape by, but for someone else to reach us by—or for Ashvael to watch who did.

Ember chose that moment to sneeze, producing a small, apologetic flame that lit the whole map gold, and in that light I saw what I'd been staring past for four evenings: the channel didn't just pass the cells. It passed *above* them. Close enough that a person crawling through it—if a person were small enough, or determined enough, or angry enough at their own Crown for deleting a country—might hear everything said below.

Might already have heard everything said below.

"Kestrel," I said. "How many nights have the bats been restless?"

He closed his eyes. I watched him listen—to the walls, to the small lives in them, to whatever the Spire whispered through its stones. When he opened his eyes, he was smiling in a way I'd learned to distrust and love in equal measure.

"Three," he said. "Restless for three nights. And tonight—" he pressed his palm flat to the stone, and his voice went soft with wonder, "—tonight there's someone up there in the dark, lying very still, breathing like a woman who learned to breathe quietly in grey coats. And she's whispering to the bats."

"What's she saying?"

He listened a moment longer. Then he laughed again, that startled, unguarded laugh, and repeated it word for word:

"'Tell them the laundry channel is real. Tell them I count six guards, not four. And tell them—for the love of whatever's left—tell them somebody in this madhouse had better explain to me why my maps show an empty valley.'"
