## 30. Vess Crosses Over

Captain Ilse Vess had interrogated four hundred and eleven people in her nineteen years of service to the Crown, and she had a private theory that the four hundred and twelfth would be the one who finally told her the truth on the first try.

She was wrong. The four hundred and twelfth was Guildmaster Odile Ferrant, and she lied the way other women breathed—easily, constantly, and without any visible effort whatsoever.

"The ledger," Vess said for the sixth time, "records a shipment north in the month of Ashfall. Where does that road terminate?"

"Wherever the buyer lives," Odile said. She sat with her hands folded on the table of the interrogation tent as though it were her own kitchen and Vess were an inconvenient niece. Her wrists were bound. Her dignity was not. "I sell things, Captain. That is the whole of my business model."

"You sold relics. Rumors. Small mercies." Vess laid the recovered ledger page flat between them, the one that had survived the fire because Odile had kept it in a locked box under her bed like an idiot—or like a woman who wanted it found. "And this page records a route through the Greywater Fens to a terminus marked only with a symbol. A circle with a line through it."

"A decorative flourish."

"It appears forty-one times in your ledgers over thirty years."

"I'm fond of it."

Vess pressed her palms flat against the table and breathed. This was the discipline. This was what separated an Inquisitor from a thug with a warrant: the patience to ask the same question until the answer wore thin enough to see through. *Ask. Wait. Ask again.* Doctrine said every liar eventually circled back to the truth the way a dog circled back to its own sick.

But Odile Ferrant did not circle. She simply kept lying, fresh each time, like a woman laying out clean linens.

"Your guild burned," Vess said. "Your people are scattered. Your beast-speaker ran through your cellars while you set the fire yourself—we have the scorch patterns, we have the cellar door, we have a stable boy who saw Okonkwo leave with a satchel. You are protecting someone who has already abandoned you."

Something moved behind Odile's eyes then—not guilt, not fear. Something almost like pity, which was far worse.

"Child," the old woman said, "you think I'm protecting Kestrel from you. That's sweet. Sit with that a moment, and then ask yourself why a woman my age would burn down everything she owns unless she thought what was coming through that door was worse than fire."

"The Crown is not worse than fire."

"No," Odile agreed mildly. "The Crown isn't anything at all. It's a story men tell so they don't have to look at what's actually standing behind them." She leaned forward as far as her bindings allowed. "You've read the old doctrine. Tell me—what does the Codex say about Vhalcrest?"

The name landed in Vess's chest like a dropped coin.

"There is no such place," she said automatically.

"Mm. And where did you learn that there is no such place?"

"In the—the Codex of Boundaries. Chapter One. The Erasure Accord lists the territories removed from—"

"From record. Yes. And how many territories?"

"Three."

"And can you name the other two?"

Vess opened her mouth. Closed it. Somewhere in nineteen years of memorized doctrine, the other two names should have been sitting right beside Vhalcrest, tidy as beads on a string. They were not there. There was a blank space in her memory shaped exactly like two countries, and she had never once noticed the shape of it, because doctrine did not encourage you to notice blanks. Doctrine encouraged you to recite.

Odile watched her realize it, and smiled with genuine warmth, which was the cruelest thing anyone had done to Vess all year.

"There it is," the guildmaster said softly. "Go on, Captain. Chase your courier's ghost into the fens. Follow your little trail. But when you find whatever's at the end of it—and you will find it, because you're too stubborn to drown—I want you to remember that an old liar in a burning building tried to warn you."

* * *

The false ledger trail led through the Greywater Fens, which Vess suspected was deliberate, because nothing about the Greywater Fens suggested itself to a rational mind. It took her squad nine days. Two horses drowned. Sergeant Brakke developed a rash he described as "philosophical." And on the ninth evening, half-starved and map-blind, they came up out of the reeds into a valley that no chart in the royal cartography office acknowledged, and found the archway.

It was older than anything Vess had ever seen. Stone gone soft-edged with time, half-swallowed by moss and flood-silt, carved over its whole span with writing in no alphabet she knew—and yet, staring at it, she felt the absurd certainty that she could almost read it. That the letters were *waiting* to be read. That they were reading *her*.

"Captain," Brakke said quietly. His philosophical rash had spread to his voice. "The birds stopped."

He was right. The fens had been loud with herons and bitterns for nine days, and now the silence was total, and the air above the arch shimmered faintly, like heat off summer cobbles, except the evening was cold.

Vess dismounted. She did not know why. It felt like the sort of threshold one crossed on foot or not at all.

There were tracks in the mud. Boot prints—two sets, one long-striding, one shorter, favoring the left foot. And between them, a line of three-toed prints the size of dinner plates, already half-grown over with pale frost-ferns that bloomed wherever the creature had stepped. Wyvern spoor. Except wyverns were extinct outside the free-ports, and these prints were warm. She put her hand near one and felt heat rising off the mud like breath.

"Okonkwo came through here," she said. "Recently. Hours."

"With a dragon," Brakke said.

"With something," Vess said, and hated how unsteady her voice came out.

Doctrine, she reminded herself, climbing the mossy stones to the arch's center. Doctrine was a ladder. When the world tilted, you held the ladder. The Codex said erased territories were erased because they harbored covenant-magic, which corrupted, which spread, which had started the Sigil Wars. The Codex said the Erasure Accord was mercy—a surgical removal before the infection went septic. The Codex said—

The air parted.

It did not open like a door. It opened like an eye. The shimmer gathered itself, drew aside, and beyond it was not the far side of the valley but light—golden, low, slanted, the light of a late afternoon in a country that had been having late afternoons continuously for twenty years—and Vess walked forward because stopping had stopped being one of the available options.

She stepped through, and the world changed under her boot.

* * *

Grass. Real grass, deep green, knee-high, moving in a wind that smelled of woodsmoke and bread and something floral she had no name for. Rolling hills. A river, silver-bright. Terraced orchards climbing the slopes. And above it all, on a distant mountain, a Spire of white stone crowned with a pyre whose flame rose straight up into the sky like a second, thinner sun.

Behind her, the archway stood in the same hillside, mossy and ancient, and through it she could see the grey fens of her own country, dim as a painting seen through dirty glass.

"Captain?" Brakke had followed. So had six others. They stood in a cluster, weapons drawn, pointing them at an orchard.

"Put those up," Vess said. "You're aiming at apples."

"But Captain, the—the Codex says—"

"I know what the Codex says." She said it more sharply than she meant to. The truth was she didn't know what the Codex said anymore. The Codex said this valley was empty. The Codex said erased lands were blighted, corrupted, crawling with the aftermath of covenant-magic. The Codex had never once mentioned that erased lands might be *beautiful*. That was the crack, and she felt it widening with every breath of that impossible flowered wind: nobody had told her it would be beautiful, because nobody who wrote the doctrine had ever come here, and if nobody had ever come here, then what precisely had the Crown erased, and on whose testimony?

A figure was coming up the hill road. An old man with a scythe over his shoulder, unhurried, the way people walk when they have never once needed to hurry. He saw the soldiers, considered them, and raised one hand in friendly greeting.

"Afternoon," he called. "You'll be from over the water, then. Haven't had visitors in—" he counted on his fingers, gave up, "—a long while. Mind the third terrace, the wall's loose."

Vess's mouth was dry. "What country is this?"

The old man looked at her strangely. Then kindly, the way one looks at someone who has asked a question they've been avoiding themselves.

"This is Vhalcrest," he said. "This has always been Vhalcrest. What else would it be?"

*The Codex says there is no such place.* The words rose in her like a prayer and died in her throat like one, unanswered, because the evidence was standing in front of her offering directions to a loose garden wall.

"Captain," whispered Corporal Ennet, young, wide-eyed, her sword still half-raised. "Captain, the fire on the mountain—it's—it's going *out*, isn't it? Look at it. It's smaller than—"

"Smaller than what?" Vess asked. "Than when? You've never seen it before."

"I know." Ennet's voice shook. "I know I've never seen it. But I *remember* it bigger."

That was the moment. Vess would mark it afterward, in the way she marked all evidence: time, place, witness. Late afternoon, golden light, the hill road below the arch. Corporal Ennet remembering something she had never seen, and being frightened by the remembering, and Vess understanding—with the terrible clarity that comes when a structure fails all at once instead of gradually—that the erasure ran both ways. The Crown had deleted Vhalcrest from the world's memory. And Vhalcrest, fading, had been leaking back into the world's dreams ever since, because some things were too large to delete cleanly. Because the Crown's great surgical mercy had left a scar across the minds of everyone alive, and called the scar *doctrine*, and made the scar mandatory reading.

Her certainty did not break dramatically. It broke the way ice breaks on a thawing pond—one hairline, then another, then the quiet, catastrophic understanding that the solid thing you were standing on had been liquid all along.

"Captain," Brakke said. "Orders?"

Vess looked at the old man with the scythe, who was waiting patiently, because patience was clearly a national industry here. She looked at the Spire, and its guttering flame. She looked back through the arch at the grey fens, and thought of Odile Ferrant's pity, and heard again: *I tried to warn you.*

"Corporal Ennet," she said. "Sergeant Brakke. You will return through the arch and report to Colonel Marsh that the trail ended at a rock formation in the fens. No archway. No valley. Nothing to report."

"Captain, that's—that's falsifying a report—"

"Yes," Vess said. "It is. I'm going to go find out what the Crown was so afraid of. Someone has to write an accurate report eventually, and it clearly won't be the Codex." She paused. "Ennet. You remembered the fire bigger. Don't let anyone talk you out of that. Memory that arrives without permission is the only kind worth trusting."

The old man chuckled. "You'll do," he told Vess. "Come along, then. If you're hunting the Speaker and the smuggler, they went down toward the river towns two days past—but you won't catch them walking. And you don't want to, likely. Things are moving. The Lady's called in her guards, and her guards aren't from anywhere at all, if you take my meaning."

"I don't."

"No," he said cheerfully. "Give it a day."

* * *

She caught up with them at dusk, though "caught up" was generous; more accurately, a crow the size of a cat led her down the river road for two hours, pausing periodically to look back with an expression of insolent expectation, and Vess followed it because her alternatives were worse.

Kestrel Okonkwo stood at a crossroads shrine lighting a small candle, a slim figure in a travel-stained coat with a wyvern hatchling draped around their shoulders like a scarf that occasionally exhaled smoke. Beside them, hand on a knife, stood Serah Vale—former Inquisitor, traitor, and according to three separate warrants, dead.

Vess stopped ten paces away and did not draw. She noted, distantly, that her hands had decided this without consulting her.

"Captain Vess," Okonkwo said. Not afraid. Tired, and curious, and—she searched for the word—*sorry*, somehow. Sorry for her, which was insufferable. "You're a long way from your jurisdiction."

"My jurisdiction," Vess said, "appears not to exist." She held up both hands, empty. "I falsified my report. My squad is carrying the lie home. I have questions, Okonkwo, and I am formally renouncing my authority to demand answers, in favor of begging for them."

Serah's knife-hand lowered an inch. "That's new."

"Nineteen years of doctrine," Vess said, "and the first honest thing my training ever taught me was how to recognize a lie. I just hadn't understood that I was inside one." She looked at the little dragon, who regarded her with ancient, amused yellow eyes, and felt the last of the ice go. "The fire on your Spire is dying. The old farmer said it. The child corporal remembered it. Your whole country is forgetting itself, and the Crown that erased it would rather let it finish than admit the mistake. So." She squared her shoulders, a woman reporting for duty to the last authority she had left: the truth. "Tell me what you need. I know the Lady Ashvael's guard protocols better than anyone alive. I trained two of her current officers myself."

Okonkwo and Serah exchanged a look over her head—the particular wordless negotiation of two people who had learned trust the hard way.

"She's telling the truth," Okonkwo said quietly—to the dragon, Vess realized. To the *dragon*. Asking the beast to weigh her soul. The hatchling sniffed the air toward Vess, considered, and sneezed a small curl of smoke that smelled, absurdly, of cedar and approval.

"The crow vouches for you too," Okonkwo added. "He says you paid him a button."

"He extorted me," Vess said.

"He says it was a fair market rate."

Despite everything—despite the dying country and the guttering mountain and the wreckage of her life's certainties—Captain Ilse Vess laughed. It came out rusty, but it came.

"Then I suppose," she said, "we'd better move quickly. Because whatever the Lady Ashvael is planning with that blood she took from you—and I've read her requisition orders, Okonkwo, I helped draft their templates—she isn't planning to wait for anyone's consent."

Above them, far up the darkening sky, the fire on the Spire flickered—once, twice—like a candle in a doorway held by someone deciding whether to keep waiting.

Kestrel Okonkwo went very still.

"We ride tonight," they said.
