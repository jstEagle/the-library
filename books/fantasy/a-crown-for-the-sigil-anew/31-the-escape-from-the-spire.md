## 31. The Escape From the Spire

The key turned in the lock with the particular reluctance of a thing being done against its own better judgment.

"Up," said Captain Vess of the Crown Inquisition, in a voice pitched low enough that only Kestrel could hear it and flat enough that it might have been a weather report. "You have until the changing of the watch. After that I will be forced to pursue you with every resource at my disposal."

Kestrel sat up on the cell's narrow cot, blinking in the lamplight, and took stock of the situation the way they took stock of everything: carefully, ledger-first.

Vess stood in the doorway of the cell she had personally locked them into three days ago. She was still wearing her grey coat, though it had lost some of its creased authority — there was ash on one shoulder that hadn't been there before, and her left boot was wet to the ankle, which meant she'd come up through the flooded lower galleries again, which meant she'd been down there a long time, alone, looking at things.

"You're letting us out," Kestrel said.

"I am *escorting* you to an interrogation chamber," Vess said. "A more secure one. Downstairs. It requires that we pass through several unlocked corridors, and it would be *embarrassing* for me if you were to overpower me en route." Her jaw did something complicated. "I am a very skilled officer. It would take at least two of you."

From the shadows of the adjacent cell came Serah's voice, dry as old paper: "She's been practicing that speech for two days. I could hear her pacing."

"I have not—"

"The third corridor," Serah said, swinging her legs off her own cot. "You stopped at the third corridor and stared at the wall where they've hung the old border survey maps. The ones from before the Erasure. The ones showing Vhalcrest as a real place with real roads." She stood, rolled her shoulders, and looked at Vess with something that wasn't quite kindness but had stopped being contempt somewhere around the salt marshes. "You looked at them for a long time, Captain."

Vess's hand went to her collar — the old habit, straightening what didn't need straightening. "I was assessing their accuracy."

"Were they accurate?"

A long silence. Somewhere above them, through six floors of stone, the great pyre on the Spire guttered, and the light in the corridor dimmed and shivered like a candle in a draft. Everyone in Vhalcrest felt it when that happened. Even the stones seemed to hold their breath.

"They were accurate," Vess said finally, and it cost her visibly, the way paying a large debt costs a proud merchant. "My doctrine says this country cannot exist. My eyes say otherwise. I have decided—" she paused, recalibrated, tried again — "I have decided that the interrogation should proceed under field conditions. Outside. Where you cannot hide anything from me."

Ember, curled in the corner of Kestrel's cell in the manner of a creature who had grown from hatchling to small-pony-sized in a matter of weeks on a diet of spoken memories, lifted her head and made a sound that was half chirp and half laugh.

"She likes you," Kestrel told Vess.

"I don't care what the lizard thinks of me."

"She can hear you."

"I don't care what the *dragon* thinks of me," Vess amended, with the weary precision of a woman who had learned, over the past weeks, exactly how many theological revisions one journey required.

* * *

The escape itself was almost disappointingly easy, which Kestrel found more frightening than any alarm would have been.

Vess walked them down the Spire's back stair — the servants' stair, worn hollow by centuries of feet, though whose servants had climbed it since the Erasure was anyone's guess — past two guards who saluted their captain and conspicuously did not see the prisoners. At the third landing she stopped, produced a ring of keys, and unlocked a storeroom full of confiscated goods: Kestrel's satchel, Serah's blades, a coil of good rope, and — Kestrel's heart did a small, stupid leap — Odile's coin purse, taken off them at the Salt Stair, every last copper accounted for because of course Vess had counted it, because of course she had.

"You kept the ledger accurate," Kestrel said, hefting it.

"I keep *everything* accurate," Vess snapped. "It is my single redeeming quality and I will thank you not to make it strange."

They went out through the postern gate, where a fourth guard looked at his captain, looked at the three fugitives and one growing dragon, and developed a sudden intense interest in the horizon. Vhalcrest's garrison, Kestrel was beginning to understand, had spent thirty years guarding a country nobody outside remembered, answering to a regent who spoke in Inquisitor protocols, watching a fire die by inches — and whatever Vess had said to them, quietly, in the officers' mess or the guard rooms, it had landed like seed in fallow ground. You cannot explain a country your own Crown deleted. But you can stand in it. You can feel the pyre gutter and know, in your bones, that something true is dying.

The wilds took them within the hour.

* * *

Vhalcrest beyond the citadel was a country holding its breath.

They traveled north through hills that maps called empty, and the hills were full: terraces gone soft with moss but still terraced, orchards still bearing pale fruit no one harvested, waystones still standing at crossroads with their inscriptions worn to whispers. The people were harder to find. That was the cruelty of the Erasure — it hadn't killed anyone, not directly. It had made everyone *translucent*, thinned them toward forgetting, so that whole villages had drawn inward like snails, hiding not from soldiers but from the world's forgetting itself, keeping their own names alive by never speaking them aloud where the wind could carry them.

But Kestrel had a gift for finding hidden things. It was, after all, the family business.

The first sign came from a hedgehog.

They'd made camp in the ruins of a shepherd's cot, Ember dozing with her tail over Serah's feet — a development Serah pretended to find inconvenient and secretly treasured — when Kestrel felt the small prickling attention of something at the edge of the firelight. They opened the door of their gift the way they'd learned to open it: barely, politely, a crack.

*Small one,* they said into the dark. *What do you want?*

The hedgehog trundled into the light, sat up on its haunches with the dignity of a much larger animal, and said, in the wordless way beasts speak: *There is a woman three valleys east who talks to rivers. The rivers have been telling her about you.*

Kestrel sat very still.

*The rivers talk about me?*

*The rivers talk about everything,* the hedgehog said, mildly offended that this required explanation. *You crossed the drowned borderlands. The water remembers. She has been waiting since the ice broke for someone the water liked.*

Serah, who had learned to read the long pauses of Kestrel's conversations with small creatures if not the content, raised an eyebrow. "Good news?"

"A river-speaker," Kestrel said. "Three valleys east. Alive. Waiting."

Ember opened one eye. The eye was the color of the pyre-fire on the Spire, and just then it guttered, and both of them flinched.

"Then we shouldn't waste the night," Serah said, already reaching for her boots.

* * *

Her name was Maren Ostrow, and she lived in a mill that no longer milled anything, beside a river that had forgotten its own name and was bitter about it.

She met them at the door with a loaded crossbow, saw Ember, lowered the crossbow, saw Vess's grey coat, raised it again.

"That coat has done terrible things," she said.

"I know," said Vess.

"It did terrible things to my sister."

"I know that too." Vess stood very straight and did not look away. "I am not asking forgiveness. I am asking to stand behind your crossbow instead of in front of it, if that helps."

Maren considered this for a long moment. Then she stepped aside, jerked her chin at the doorway, and said, "River says you're the one. Says the water let you pass the drowned stones." She looked at Kestrel with fierce, exhausted hope. "Is it true? Can you really end it? The fading?"

"I don't know," Kestrel said honestly. "I know the fire needs a name spoken freely. I know Ashvael means to take it instead. And I know there are more of us than the Crown ever believed, because I'm starting to find them."

"Because of the animals," Maren said.

"Because of the animals."

Something in her face unclenched, just slightly. "Come in, then. All of you. Even the coat." She paused. "The river vouches for the lizard too. Says she's got her grandmother's temper and her grandfather's heart, whoever they were."

Ember preened.

* * *

One speaker became two became five became nine, the way embers pass flame along a fuse of dry grass.

Maren knew where to look, because the rivers knew where to look, and once the first door opened the others came easier — each hidden Speaker passing them along like a secret too heavy for one set of hands. There were the twins, Pell and Perrin, who spoke to stone and had spent twenty years listening to the border archway itself, mapping its moods, learning what it wanted. There was old Hask, a beekeeper whose bees brought him news from forty miles in every direction and who wept openly when Ember landed on his fence rail, saying he'd waited his whole life to apologize to a dragon for what his grandparents' generation had allowed. There was a boy of perhaps twelve named Tam, who spoke to moths, and whose mother gripped Kestrel's sleeve at the door and whispered, "They'll not take him like they took the others?" and Kestrel, who carried their own mother's voice in their chest like a coal banked in ash, said, "Not while I'm breathing," and meant it down to the marrow.

And there was Wren.

Wren was ninety if she was a day, tiny and sharp as a thorn, and she spoke to birds — all birds, casually, the way other people chatted across a fence. She received them in a treetop house held together with rope and stubbornness, looked Kestrel up and down, and said:

"So you're Ythirra's get. Hm. Smaller than I expected."

"I'm sorry?"

"The first Speaker," Wren said, settling onto her perch with a creak. "Your line. The dragon chose a shepherd girl, back when the covenant was cut, and the girl was small and stubborn and talked to crows. You have the look of her. Around the eyes." She waved a bird-boned hand at Kestrel's stare. "Oh, don't gape. My grandmother was there. Family keeps records. Birds especially — nothing gets forgotten up here, whatever the Crown does to paper."

"We're gathering Speakers," Kestrel said. "For the march on the Spire. Ashvael's begun the ritual preparations; we heard it from the bees, and the bees heard it from the moths in the citadel rafters. If she forces the covenant with stolen blood—"

"The fire will burn lies instead of burning them away," Wren finished. "Yes. We know. A country sustained by honest fire, lit by theft — it would curdle. It would become the worst lie of all: that the Sigil Wars were just, written in flame no one could argue with." Her eyes, bright and black as a wren's own, fixed on Kestrel. "That's why she must not win. And that's why *you* must not fail. Do you understand the difference?"

Kestrel thought of Odile, captive somewhere in the citadel's depths, unbroken. Of the guild burned behind them. Of a dying courier pressing a map into their palms with the last warmth in his hands.

"Yes," they said.

"Good." Wren hopped down, brisk as a sparrow. "Then listen, child, because the birds have been counting, and I have numbers for you."

* * *

They assembled in the mill over the following days, and it was — Kestrel kept turning the word over, unable to improve on it — it was like watching a ledger balance after years of red ink.

Nine Speakers became fourteen. Maren taught the younger ones how to listen without drowning; Hask's bees scouted the approaches to the citadel and returned with pollen-maps of guard rotations; the twins laid their palms on the millstone and pulled up, through stone, the deep routes beneath the Spire that even Serah's Inquisitor training hadn't known — passages older than the Erasure, older than the Wars, cut when the covenant was young and Speakers and dragon built together.

Even Vess found a place, though she fought it every step of the way.

It happened on the fourth evening, when Tam — the moth-boy, shy as his namesakes — crept up to her while she was cleaning her boots and asked, in the direct way of children, "Did you hurt people? Before?"

The camp went quiet. Serah's hand drifted toward her knife out of pure reflex. Vess set down her boot, folded her hands, and gave the question the same merciless accuracy she gave everything.

"Yes. I signed removal orders. I interrogated prisoners. I believed, completely, that I was protecting the realm from a corruption." She paused. "I was wrong. I am trying to learn how to be wrong properly, which turns out to be very difficult."

Tam considered this. "The moths say you haven't slept well."

"The moths are correct."

"They say you keep saying a name in your sleep. Someone you removed." His voice gentled, the way children's voices do when they're being kind without knowing the weight of it. "The moths think you should say it awake instead."

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at the fire, and her jaw worked, and finally, quietly, she said a name — a plain name, a village name, the name of someone erased — and said it into the open air where the wind could carry it, and the wind carried it, and somewhere far off a nightjar repeated it twice, and Vess of the Crown Inquisition put her face in her hands and wept without sound while a twelve-year-old patted her arm awkwardly and a dragon the size of a hay wagon rested her chin, very gently, on the top of her head.

After that, Vess drilled them. Because if she was going to defect, she informed the camp, she was going to do it *competently*, and a mob of civilians walking into a fortified citadel was not competent, it was a massacre with extra steps. She drew the Spire's defenses from memory on the mill floor in chalk. She organized watches. She taught Serah's ragtag scouts formation signals and taught Maren's river-speakers how to read troop movements in water-stains on stone.

"You realize," Serah said to Kestrel one night, watching Vess berate two farmers' sons for sloppy spear-drill, "that we've accidentally founded an army."

"We've founded a *guild*," Kestrel said. "Of impossible things. It's a family tradition."

* * *

On the sixth night, the bees brought bad news, and the moths confirmed it, and Wren's birds corroborated from three directions, because that was how certain you wanted to be.

Ashvael had moved the ritual forward. Not by days — by hours. Something had frightened her. The bees said the citadel's forges ran all night; the moths said the blood-sigils in the ritual chamber were already inked; the birds said Lady Ashvael had stood at her window at dawn, looking north, looking for them, and had ordered the gates sealed and the pyre fed with the last of the reserve timber, burning tomorrow what couldn't be spared, because she needed the fire *bright* for what she meant to do with it.

"She knows we're coming," Serah said.

"She's always known," Kestrel said. "Since the Salt Stair. Maybe since the door." They looked around the mill — at Maren sharpening a billhook, at the twins chalking route-marks on their forearms, at Hask feeding Ember strips of dried fish and telling her, softly, stories about her ancestors that made her scales flush rose-gold, at Tam asleep against Vess's grey coat with the captain sitting rigidly still so as not to wake him. "It doesn't change the arithmetic. The fire dies in a fortnight whether we march or not. She'll force the covenant either way. The only question is whether we're in the room when she tries."

Odile's voice, in memory, dry as her ledgers: *Love expressed as logistics, dear. Now go and be logistical.*

Kestrel stood, and the room quieted, and they felt the weight of every eye and hated it and accepted it, because someone had to say it and apparently that someone was them.

"Tomorrow at dusk," they said. "We march on the Spire."

Outside, far to the south, the pyre-light guttered against the clouds — and then, for the first time in living memory, flickered *back*, steadied, held, as if the fire itself had heard, and was waiting to see what they would dare.

Ember raised her head and sang one low note into the dark, and every beast in Vhalcrest, for fifty miles in every direction, answered.
