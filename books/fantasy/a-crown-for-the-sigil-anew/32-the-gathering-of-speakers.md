## 32. The Gathering of Speakers

### I. Maren of the Ford

The river had been telling Maren the same story for eleven years, and she had gotten very good at pretending not to listen.

*Cold today,* the water said as she knelt at its edge to fill the wash bucket. *Colder tomorrow. There are soldiers on the far bank, and one of them is thinking about his mother's stew, and another one is thinking about deserting, and a third isn't thinking about anything at all because he's dead inside his boots.*

"Thank you," Maren murmured, which was what you said to a river whether its news was useful or not. You thanked it the way you thanked a gossipy aunt — politely, and without encouraging it.

She had learned young that rivers talked. She had learned almost as young never to say so out loud. Her father had built their cottage three miles from any village for exactly this reason, though he'd told her it was for the fishing. Her mother had taught her the rules in a whisper while braiding her hair: *Never answer them where people can see your lips move. Never repeat what they tell you. And if anyone ever asks why you smile at puddles, say it's rain coming.*

For eleven years since they'd gone, she had kept those rules so well that she'd nearly convinced herself the gift was a small thing. A quirk. A way of being alone without ever quite being lonely.

Then the crows had come through the valley in October, all flying the same direction, all saying the same word in their rattling voices — *Speakers, Speakers, Speakers* — and one of them had landed on her wash line and looked at her with an eye like a wet black coin and said, in the tone of a bird delivering a summons it found personally tedious:

*"You're wanted. West. Bring nothing. The fire's waking up."*

Maren had stood in her yard with the bucket dripping down her apron and felt something in her chest crack open — not painfully. More like ice going out of a pond in spring.

She was on the road by dawn.

### II. The Twins Under the Hill

Bram and Sorrel Fenn had been arguing about the same rock for six years.

"It's *granite*," Bram said.

"It's *lonely*," Sorrel said.

"It can be both."

"It can't. Granite doesn't feel anything, that's the whole point of granite—"

"The point," Bram said, "is that it's been sitting in our turnip field since before Grandda was born, and last week it started *humming*, and when I put my hand on it, it said—" he stopped, because he always stopped here, because the next part sounded mad even to him, "—it said *soon*."

Sorrel went quiet. In the twilight the field stretched grey and stubbled around them, and the rock sat in the middle of it like a sleeping dog that had chosen its spot and would not be moved.

Twins who spoke to stone were not supposed to exist. Their grandmother had told them so, cheerfully, over supper, the way you'd tell children they were not supposed to pick their noses. Stone didn't have voices; stone had *patience*, and patience only made sound when something enormous was about to happen. Then she'd made them swear, hands on the hearthstone — which had, both twins privately agreed, felt like shaking hands with a very old, very tired friend — that they'd never speak of it.

They were seventeen now. They had kept the oath for nine years, mostly by talking about it constantly to each other, which they reasoned didn't count.

Last week the humming had spread. Not just their rock — the whole hillside. Fence posts hummed. The well stones hummed. The flat slab their grandmother was buried under hummed so persistently that Bram had taken to eating lunch on it, the way you'd sit with a chatty uncle.

And yesterday, a kestrel — an actual kestrel, a small brown hawk with a bandit's mask — had landed on their gatepost and spoken in a voice like dry leaves: *"West. Follow the drowned road. You'll know the others by how the ground greets them."*

"How does ground greet people?" Sorrel had asked.

*"Warmly,"* said the kestrel, and flew off, having apparently said everything it intended to say for the season.

Now they stood in the dark looking at their rock.

"Soon," Bram repeated.

"Soon," Sorrel agreed. She slung her pack over her shoulder. It contained two shirts, a heel of bread, and their grandmother's whetstone, which she could not explain bringing and refused to leave behind.

They set off west, and behind them, faint as a heartbeat, the hillside hummed.

### III. Kestrel

Kestrel Okonkwo had faced down Inquisitors, smugglers, a river-witch's prices, and Lady Ashvael's smile, and had come through all of it more or less intact. None of it had prepared them for a committee.

"They want names," Serah said, dropping onto the log beside Kestrel with the particular weariness of someone who had spent the morning mediating. "All of them. They've formed a — I don't even know what to call it. A delegation? They've elected spokespeople. There are *minutes*. Ember is taking them, or trying to. She keeps eating the charcoal."

"She's teething."

"She's the size of a pony and she ate the minutes, Kestrel."

The camp lay in a fold of Vhalcrest's forgotten wilds, in a hollow where the trees grew old and unafraid. Three weeks since the escape from the Spire, and word had traveled the way Kestrel had once only dreamed it could — beast to beast, crow to crow, river to river, stone to stone. *The Speaker has come. The fire is waking. Come west.* And they had come: forty-one souls now, and more arriving every day, trickling out of hedgerows and hillsides and the backs of shops where they'd hidden their gifts like contraband for a lifetime.

A woman named Maren who talked to rivers and apologized to them constantly. Twin teenagers from the east whose arrival had been announced three days early by every boulder between there and here. A goatherd who spoke goat, which everyone agreed was less impressive until they saw him calm a stampede with a cough. A fisherman who spoke gull. Two sisters who spoke moth, which turned out to be startlingly useful for scouting, moths being everywhere and suspecting nothing.

Forty-one people who had spent their whole lives being quiet, all looking at Kestrel.

"I'm not a leader," Kestrel said, for perhaps the ninth time that week.

"Mm." Serah picked up a stick and began drawing idle lines in the dirt. "You know what Odile would say?"

"Don't."

"She'd say that the Guild of Impossible Things didn't have a guildmaster because she was the bravest or the loudest. It had one because somebody had to sign for the deliveries." Serah glanced sideways, that crooked half-smile that still caught Kestrel off guard after everything. "Somebody has to sign for the deliveries, love."

"That's not — we're not a *guild*."

"We absolutely are. We trade in impossible things. Look around." Serah gestured with the stick. "A woman who negotiates with floodwater. Twins who get geological reports. A dragon the size of a hay barn who eats stationery. If that's not a guild, I'll eat the ledger."

"You'd eat anything. You ate that grey soup Maren made."

"The river vouched for it."

Kestrel laughed despite themselves, and then the laugh died, because across the fire Maren had risen and was looking their way, and behind her the others were gathering — not in a mob, thank all mercies, but in that loose, shuffling, hopeful way that people gather when they've decided something and are waiting for permission to say it.

Here it came.

"Kestrel." Maren's voice was the voice of someone used to being interrupted by water and therefore unhurried. "We've talked. All of us. Well — Tam talked to the goats about it first, and the goats were in favor, and after that it seemed settled."

"Wonderful," Kestrel said faintly. "Governed by goat consensus."

"The goats have excellent instincts," Tam said, wounded, from somewhere near the back.

"The point," Maren continued, with the gentle ruthlessness of a woman who had raised four sons, "is that we need someone to speak for all of us. To the regent's people, to the Speakers in hiding, to — well. To whatever comes. And everyone agrees it should be you. You crossed the borderlands. You opened the door. Ythirra knows your name." She paused. "We voted. It wasn't close. The twins abstained because they were arm-wrestling."

"We were establishing precedent," Sorrel called out.

"For what?"

"No idea," said Bram. "But it's established."

Kestrel stood. Forty-one faces turned toward them, and every single one of those faces belonged to someone who had done what Kestrel had done their whole life — swallowed the gift, lowered the eyes, made themselves small enough to survive. And every one of them had heard the crows anyway. Had walked away from everything anyway. Had come to a hollow in a forgotten country because a stranger with a ledger-keeper's hands had spoken a name into the dark and the dark had answered.

That was the thing Kestrel couldn't argue with, and had finally stopped trying to. This wasn't courage being asked of them. It was bookkeeping. Somebody had to keep the accounts. Somebody had to write down who came, and what they'd lost getting here, and what they were owed — because the world had erased these people once, and erasure was just a ledger with pages torn out.

Kestrel knew ledgers.

"All right," they said.

A rustle went through the crowd — surprise, almost, as if some of them had prepared arguments they were now disappointed not to use.

"But I have conditions." Kestrel held up fingers. "One: nobody calls me a queen, a general, a prophetess, or 'the Chosen Vessel.' I've met a woman who thinks she's all four and it hasn't gone well for anyone. Two: decisions get argued openly, in front of the fire, and the goats get a vote, apparently, since that's apparently how we do things now. Three—" and here their voice caught, just slightly, and they let it, because condition three deserved the catch, "—we keep a ledger. Every name. Every debt. Nobody in this camp gets forgotten again. Not by me. Not while I'm breathing."

Silence. Then Maren smiled the way rivers do when the ice goes out — slowly, all at once.

"Motion carries," she said.

"Carried," echoed the crowd, and Tam's goats, hearing the tone if not the words, bleated agreement, and somewhere overhead a crow said what Kestrel strongly suspected was a sarcastic remark, and Ember — who had been pretending to sleep — lifted her head, dropped the charred remains of the second set of minutes, and trumpeted something joyful and deafening that scattered every bird within a mile.

Serah leaned into Kestrel's shoulder. "Signing for the deliveries," she murmured.

"Shut up," Kestrel said fondly, and meant: *thank you.*

### IV. Sorrel

The stone spoke to Sorrel at moonrise, when the camp had gone quiet and she was supposed to be asleep.

It was the paving-stone under her bedroll, an unremarkable slab hauled up from some ruined courtyard, and it had been muttering contentedly all evening — stones muttered, mostly, about weight and frost and the indignity of being stepped on — but now its voice sharpened into something else.

*They're moving,* it said. *Down at the Spire. The tall ones with the iron shoes. They're carrying the lady's chains up the stairs, and the old fire is coughing, and the air tastes like a storm that hasn't decided yet.*

Sorrel sat up. Bram was already awake beside her — he always woke when she did, some twin-thing older than either of them.

"What is it?" he whispered.

She pressed her palm flat to the stone, and it told her again, patiently, the way you'd tell a child about weather: the ritual moved up, the storm undecided, the great dragon's breath gone thin as smoke.

"Bram," she said. "Get Kestrel."

Across the dying fire, Kestrel was already rising — of course they were; the whole camp's birds had probably known before the stones did — and Serah was buckling her sword with the brisk economy of a woman who had hoped for one full night's sleep and accepted long ago that she would never see one again.

"Well?" Kestrel said.

Sorrel took a breath. Her grandmother's voice was in her head — *patience makes sound when something enormous is about to happen* — and beneath her hand the stone hummed its humming, steady as a heart.

"The Spire," she said. "Ashvael's begun. And the mountain says we've got three days."
