## 33. March on the Spire

Serah Vale had marched in formations before. She'd done it in grey wool and polished boots, with a captain's voice at her back and a doctrine in her chest, marching toward villages whose names she would later wish she could forget. She knew what it was to move in step with hundreds of people who believed the same thing for the same reasons.

This was not that.

This was two hundred and eleven people walking through Vhalcrest's forgotten wilds in no formation whatsoever, arguing about lunch.

"The river-speaker says we should follow the waterline," Maren called back from the front, where she walked beside Ilsa of the Rivers, a broad-shouldered woman who had spent forty years talking to water and had absorbed some of its habits—chiefly, an inability to hurry.

"The stone twins say the ridge is faster," someone else countered.

"The stone twins are nine years old."

"The stone twins have been right every single time."

Serah adjusted the strap of her sword—a real soldier's blade now, not the ceremonial edge the Inquisition had issued her—and let the argument wash over her. Behind her, Kestrel was doing something to a blister on Ember's foot-pad while the young wyvern complained about it in a language that needed no translation. The complaint was universal. It was *ow*, and *why*, and *carry me instead*.

"You're growing," Kestrel said patiently. "Growing hurts. Ask anyone."

Ember blew a thin curl of smoke directly into their face. Kestrel coughed, laughed, and kept working.

*Two hundred and eleven people,* Serah thought, scanning them again because counting them calmed her the way inventory had once calmed her in the guild's storerooms. Speakers of rivers and stones and crows and bees. A man who spoke to moths and therefore knew everything that happened after dark. Twin sisters who spoke to horses and had brought half the wild herds of the eastern valleys along as outriders. An old woman named Bett who spoke to fire itself and walked at the column's center like a lit lantern, because everyone agreed that if things went wrong, they wanted Bett close.

And at the center of the center, kneeling in the mud with smoke in their hair, the person all of this was supposedly about, complaining about blisters.

"They don't look like a savior," said a voice at Serah's elbow.

She didn't startle. She'd stopped startling around Odile, and Odile wasn't here—but the gull had arrived three days ago with a letter written in a hand Serah recognized from a thousand forged ledger entries, and somehow Odile's voice had been following her ever since, dry as chalk dust.

"She escaped," Maren had said, when the letter came. "Your guildmaster. Walked out of an Inquisitor cell through a door the guards swear was locked, left a note thanking them for the soup." A pause. "The note also contained the complete garrison roster of the citadel, current as of Tuesday. We've been using it as a map."

So Odile was somewhere behind them, or ahead of them, or inside the citadel already, being Odile at it. Serah chose not to think too hard about which.

"She doesn't want to be a savior," Serah said now, answering the question honestly, because she'd learned that Speakers could smell a lie the way dogs smelled rain. "That's rather the point of her."

"Then what does she want?"

Serah watched Kestrel finish with Ember's foot, get knocked flat by an affectionate wyvern skull, lie there a moment in the mud, and get up still laughing.

"To go home," she said. "We're just making sure there's one to go home to."

* * *

They saw the storm before they saw the citadel.

It shouldn't have been possible—the Spire stood sixty miles off yet, past the salt flats where the ambush had nearly ended them all—but the sky above it was wrong in a way that pulled the eye like a hook pulls a fish. Clouds had gathered into a slow spiral centered on the peak, turning counterclockwise, and through the gaps between them came light that wasn't sunlight: a deep, guttering amber, pulsing.

Pulsing like breath. Like a heartbeat running down.

"Ythirra," Kestrel said quietly, coming up beside her. All the humor had gone out of their face. "Her fire. You can see it from here."

"I can see it's getting worse."

"It's getting worse faster than it should." Kestrel's hands moved as they talked, the old ledger-keeper's habit, counting invisible columns. "When we climbed the Spire, the fire had weeks in it. Maybe a month. That—" they pointed at the spiral of cloud, at the amber stuttering through it, "—that's days. Something is drawing on her. Hard."

"Ashvael."

"Ashvael started early." Kestrel's jaw tightened. "Because of us. Because we got out, and she knows what we know, and she can't risk waiting for a proper ritual. So she's forcing it." They looked at Serah, and Serah saw the thing underneath the fear, the thing that had been there since the cellar under the burning guild: Kestrel Okonkwo, terrified, doing the arithmetic anyway. "Blood drawn from a Speaker can hold a covenant open. It can't renew it. But Ashvael doesn't need renewal, does she? She needs control. And control you can take."

Serah had signed orders, once, that did exactly this kind of math with exactly this kind of cruelty. She knew the handwriting of it. *Collateral. Expedient. Necessary.* The Inquisition's favorite words, the ones that made atrocity sound like bookkeeping.

"Then we walk faster," she said.

* * *

They walked faster. It didn't help enough.

By nightfall they had reached the salt flats' northern edge, where the land began its long climb toward the Spire's plateau, and the sky overhead had gone fully strange. The amber pulse was visible even against the stars now, and each time it flared, the whole horizon flickered like a lamp about to die. The horses wouldn't graze. Ember kept stopping mid-stride to stare at the mountain, making a low sound in her chest that Serah had never heard from her—not the complaint-smoke sound, not the hungry-for-memories sound. Something older. Grief, maybe. Or recognition.

"She's calling," Kestrel said. They were standing apart from the campfire, which Bett was keeping alive through sheer stubborn conversation with the flames. "Ythirra. Can't you hear it?"

"No."

"Neither can I, properly. Not anymore—it's too far, and she's too weak. But Ember can. And the bees can." Kestrel nodded toward a hollow tree at the camp's edge where a swarm had settled, uncharacteristically quiet, all facing the same direction. Toward the Spire. "Everything that listens is hearing her run out of time."

Serah came to stand beside them. Below, the camp murmured—Maren reorganizing the march order for the morning, the moth-man reporting on patrol lights he'd seen on the plateau road, the stone twins asleep in a heap with their heads together the way they always slept. Two hundred and eleven people who had hidden their whole lives in holes and hedges and drowned valleys, about to walk up a mountain at the Crown's own erased heart.

"When this is over," Serah said, "what do you want?"

Kestrel glanced at her. "That's a strange time to ask."

"It's the only time it matters. People save the question for after, and then after comes and they've forgotten how to answer." She kept her eyes on the flickering mountain. "I signed papers once that decided what other people wanted without asking them. I'm not doing that again. Not even kindly. Not even for you."

Kestrel was quiet for a while. The fire crackled; Bett muttered something encouraging to it.

"A ledger," Kestrel said finally. "A clean one. No erased pages. I want to sit in a room that smells like ink and tea and write down true things, and have the true things stay true." A small, crooked smile. "And I want the room to have you in it. If you're asking what I think you're asking."

"I'm asking what I think you think I'm asking."

"That's very Inquisitor of you."

"I contain multitudes. Mostly guilt."

Kestrel laughed—short, surprised, real—and leaned into her shoulder, and for perhaps a minute they simply stood there, two liars watching a dying fire paint the sky, while two hundred and eleven people slept behind them trusting them to lead.

Then the sky screamed.

There was no better word for it. The amber pulse stuttered, flared white, and a sound rolled across the salt flats—not thunder, though thunder rode beneath it, but something vast and pained, a note so low Serah felt it in her teeth, in the fillings of her teeth, in the bones of the ground. Every horse in camp screamed back. The bees rose in a single dark ribbon and flew toward the mountain. Ember threw back her head and answered with a cry that cracked in the middle like a child's voice breaking, and Serah put her hand on the young wyvern's neck and felt her shaking.

"What was that?" Maren shouted, already awake, already armed.

Kestrel had gone white. "That was her name being used wrong."

* * *

They broke camp in the dark. Nobody argued about it, which told Serah everything about how the sound had landed. By moonless midnight they were climbing, two hundred and eleven people and a herd of horses and one growing wyvern moving up the switchback roads toward the plateau, and the amber light above them pulsed faster now, urgent, arrhythmic, a heart in fibrillation.

It was on the third switchback that the first scouts came back down at a run.

"Roadblock," gasped a horse-twin, Dara or Denna, Serah had never reliably sorted them. "Not soldiers. Chains. Great iron chains across the road, sigil-worked, and behind them—" she gulped air, "—behind them the whole plateau is warded. We sent a crow through. The crow didn't come back. Ilsa tried the river-water that runs down from the mountain and the water went *quiet*, Serah. Water doesn't go quiet."

Sigil-work. Of course. Ashvael had been regent of an erased country for twenty years; she had learned erasure the way other people learned prayer. And sigil-work meant—

"Inquisitor craft," Serah said grimly. "Old doctrine. I can read it. Some of it I can break." She caught the looks passing among the Speakers and added, flatly: "Yes. I helped write parts of it. Yes, I'm ashamed. No, shame doesn't break chains. Let me at the chains."

But it was Bett who solved the roadblock, in the end, in the way that fire solves most problems given sufficient encouragement and a grievance. The old woman walked up to the sigil-worked chains with her sleeves rolled, examined the warding for a long moment, and said, in the tone of a grandmother addressing a disappointing grandchild:

"You burn lies, do you? Well. So do I."

What happened next took four seconds and Serah's eyebrows a week to recover. The chains didn't melt so much as *reconsider*—the iron seemed to remember it had once been ore, free in the mountains, and found its current employment distasteful. The wards guttered out like candles pinched between wet fingers. And Bett stepped through the gap, dusted off her hands, and said, "Well? The mountain isn't getting any closer."

* * *

They came onto the plateau as the false dawn greyed the east, and Serah stopped breathing for a moment.

The Spire rose ahead of them, black stone veined now with dull amber, and coiled around it—visible at last, vast beyond sense, scales like a hillside of tarnished bronze—Ythirra. The last dragon. Even dimmed, even guttering, she was the largest living thing Serah had ever seen, larger than cathedrals, larger than the idea of cathedrals, and she was dying in public, her fire pulsing out of her in those terrible arrhythmic surges that lit the clouds from within.

And at the Spire's base, between the Speakers and the dragon, Ashvael's ritual was already underway.

Serah had seen Inquisitor workings before. This was not one. This was older and worse: a ring of standing stones around the Spire's root, each carved with the covenant-scripture of founding Vhalcrest, and between the stones ran channels cut into the rock, and the channels were full of light that moved like liquid and stank even at this distance of iron and salt. Blood. Old blood, drawn slowly, hoarded. And at the ring's center, on a dais of black glass, stood Lady Ashvael in robes the color of a closed door, arms raised, reading from a script that made the air around her bend.

"She's using the captive blood," Kestrel breathed. "All of it. She's not waiting for me. She's trying to brute-force the covenant with what she has." Their voice shook. "It won't work. It'll tear the covenant open instead of renewing it, and when a founding covenant tears—"

"What happens?" Maren asked.

"Vhalcrest doesn't come back," Kestrel said. "Vhalcrest ends. For real this time. Her, the dragon, everyone in it. Erasure with teeth."

Above them, Ythirra surged against the Spire and the sky flashed white, and this time the scream that followed had words in it, if you knew how to listen. Serah didn't. But Kestrel did, and whatever they heard made them close their eyes for a moment like a person absorbing a blow.

"She says," Kestrel translated, very quietly, "*Little speaker. Come quickly. I am almost out of time, and the woman below me is about to do a very stupid thing with my blood-kin's blood.*" A pause. "She also says the stairs on the north face are less guarded, and that she apologizes for the state of the weather."

Despite everything—despite the wards and the blood-channels and the dying god coiled around the mountain—Serah laughed. One short bark of it. Trust a dragon, even at the end, to apologize for the weather.

"All right," she said, and drew her sword, and turned to face two hundred and eleven people who had never in their lives been allowed to stand in the open and be what they were. The dawn wind came off the salt flats and carried the smell of iron and coming storm. "You all know what's up there. You all know what she's trying to do. I won't dress it up: some of us aren't coming back down. I did the arithmetic on the way up and the arithmetic is bad, and I'm telling you that because you deserve honest numbers—you've had enough of people lying to you about costs."

She looked down the line of them. River-speaker and stone twins, moth-man and horse-sisters, Bett with her singed sleeves, Maren with her jaw set, Ember with her too-big wings half-spread and her eyes fixed on the Spire like a compass needle finding north.

"But here's the number that matters," Serah said. "Every one of you is a voice the Crown spent thirty years trying to silence. And there is a country up on that mountain full of people going translucent with forgetting, and a dragon holding the last of them in the light, and she is asking us—politely—for help. So."

She raised the sword. It felt, for the first time since she'd taken it up again, like it was pointing the right way.

"Speakers of Vhalcrest. Let's go be loud."

And two hundred and eleven voices answered her—not a battle cry, nothing so simple, but a rising chord of every language they commanded, river and stone and crow and bee and fire and horse, a sound that had not been heard in the world for thirty years, rolling up the plateau toward the black glass dais where Lady Ashvael turned, mid-ritual, to see what was coming for her.

Behind her, in the channels, the stolen blood began to boil.

Kestrel was already running for the north-face stairs, Ember streaking ahead of them, and Serah went after—because whatever happened next, whatever the stupid brave beautiful ledger-keeper was about to do with a dying dragon's true name, Serah Vale had made a promise in a burned guild's cellar, and she intended to keep it:

Wherever Kestrel Okonkwo opened a door, she would be standing on the other side of it.
