## 39. Love Against Duty, Resolved

Serah Vale had killed men for less than what Kestrel Okonkwo was doing to the kitchen.

Not killing, precisely—she'd never actually stabbed anyone over cookware, though there had been a memorable afternoon in the drowned borderlands when Kestrel had nearly gotten them both eaten by a silent thing in the reeds because they *had* to ask it about its day. But watching them now, elbow-deep in a cupboard that had clearly been organized by someone with a grudge against logic, Serah felt the old itch between her shoulder blades, the one that meant *threat* and *act now*. Three years of running would do that. Some mornings she still woke reaching for a blade that wasn't under her pillow anymore, because Odile had confiscated it and replaced it with a note that read: *You are a guest. Guests sleep. Try it.*

"Kess," Serah said, leaning against the doorframe, "the spoons were fine."

"The spoons were anarchy." Kestrel didn't turn around. Their voice carried that particular ledger-balanced calm that Serah had once found infuriating and now found, embarrassingly, soothing—the sound of a mind putting small things in order so the large things couldn't sneak up on anyone. "They were sorted by size but not by function. There's no system. A household without a system is just a fire waiting for an excuse."

"We lived in ditches for four months."

"And I kept a ditch ledger. Ask me about the ditch ledger."

"I've heard about the ditch ledger." Serah crossed the kitchen, ducking under the low beam—rebuilt guild, rebuilt beams, same stubborn architecture of people who'd decided that if the Crown was going to burn their home down twice, they'd simply build it shorter and wider and more difficult to set alight—and wrapped her arms around Kestrel from behind. She felt the small hitch in their breathing, the one that even now, after everything, arrived before surrender. Then Kestrel relaxed by degrees, like a lock accepting a key it had been expecting all along.

"You're avoiding the courtyard," Serah said into their hair.

"I'm organizing spoons."

"You've organized these spoons three times this week."

"They keep rebelling."

Serah smiled against their temple. Outside the window, past the courtyard where the guild's hearth-stones had been laid back in a pattern that wasn't quite the old one but hummed truer, the morning light lay thick and gold across a patch of freshly turned earth. A small sack of something sat beside it. A trowel. And standing over the whole arrangement with her arms folded and her expression suggesting she was supervising the weather personally, Ember—who at two years old had gone from fist-sized hatchling to a creature roughly the size of a hay cart, iridescent and opinionated, who slept draped across the guild's roof tiles and terrified tax assessors.

"She asked me to help her," Kestrel said quietly.

"She didn't ask you anything. You're the only one here who speaks Wyvern, and she mostly says *food* and *mine* and, lately, *Serah-snores*."

"That last one is a real word. It has declensions."

Serah laughed, and felt the laugh do its work, loosening something in her chest that had been set there during the ambush at the Salt Stair and had never quite come out. The scar from the blade meant for Kestrel pulled when she laughed hard. She'd decided she could live with that arithmetic—a scar that ached only in proportion to joy seemed, on balance, a fair exchange.

"All right," she said. "Let's go see what she wants planted."

* * *

The courtyard smelled of turned earth and rain that hadn't fallen yet. Odile had said that about the rebuilt guild once—*it smells like a promise the sky hasn't kept*—and Serah had privately thought it was the most romantic thing the old woman had ever said, right up until Odile followed it with, *which is why we're installing gutters.*

Ember stood over the dirt patch with the gravity of a general reviewing troops. When Kestrel knelt at the edge of the soil, the wyvern made a low sound—not a word, exactly, more a chord—and nudged the sack with her snout until it tipped. Seeds rolled out. They were small, dark, faintly warm, which Serah supposed should have alarmed her more than it did; nothing in Vhalcrest or out of it stayed entirely cold these days, not with Ythirra's fire renewed and threading through the valley like a second bloodstream.

"What are they?" Serah crouched beside them.

Kestrel picked up one seed, held it to the light. Their eyes did the thing they did—distant, listening, the look of someone hearing a conversation happening two rooms away in a language older than borders. "Ashvael-bloom," they murmured. "No—that's the common name. The tree itself doesn't have a name yet. It's asking for one." A pause. "It says it grows slowly."

"How slowly?"

"It says—" Kestrel tilted their head, and Serah watched them translate, that gift inherited from a dragon and a founding covenant and a mother's whispered warnings, all of it braided now into something ordinary as breath. "It says some things can't be hurried without breaking them. It says it will take years. Maybe ten. Maybe fifteen."

"Fifteen years," Serah repeated.

"It says that's the point."

And there it was, Serah thought. The whole argument they'd been circling for weeks, delivered by a seed.

She sat down fully in the dirt, because if this was going to be the conversation, she wanted to be comfortable for it. Kestrel glanced at her sidelong—that quick, assessing glance that had once catalogued exits and now catalogued moods—and sat too, knees up, seed cupped in their palm like a coin they weren't sure they could afford.

"We've been doing this dance," Serah said. "For two months. Since the school opened and the envoys started arriving and your calendar filled up with other people's emergencies."

"I don't dance."

"You absolutely dance. You dance beautifully. You dance around every conversation that starts with *what happens next*." Serah kept her voice light, because that was how you talked to Kestrel about heavy things—you made room for them to leave, and then they didn't take the room. "So. What happens next."

Kestrel looked at the seed. At the courtyard—at the scarred stones, the new roofline, the kitchen window with its anarchic spoons. At the archway at the garden's far end, the old door between worlds, standing open now, tended, garlanded every season by children from the Speaker-school who treated it the way other children treated wells and wishing-trees: as furniture. As fact.

"When I was running," Kestrel said finally, "I used to think about what I'd do if I ever stopped. That was the fantasy. Not riches. Not revenge. Just—stopping. A room no one was looking for. Ledgers that balanced." A wry twist of mouth. "I got the ledgers. They balance beautifully. We're owed money by four governments and a river."

"The river's good for it."

"The river's *very* good for it. But—" They exhaled. Turned the seed over. "Stopping was always the end of the story. Nobody writes what comes after stopping. There's no chapter for it. I think I've been standing in the courtyard waiting for someone to tell me the plot."

Serah reached over and took the seed from their palm. Warm. Patient. Fifteen years of patient. "Then let's write it badly," she said. "That's my professional recommendation. I've read enough Inquisitor doctrine to know how stories written by certain people turn out. Badly plotted stories are safer."

Kestrel huffed a laugh. "Badly plotted."

"Terribly plotted. No outline whatsoever. Improvised ending. Characters who change their minds." She leaned her shoulder against theirs. "Here's my contribution to the plot: I want mornings. Not missions. Mornings. I want to learn your mother's hearth-song, except neither of us remembers it, so I want us to write a bad new one and argue about whose verse is worse. I want to teach the first-years footwork and watch you teach them patience and compete shamelessly for favorite-teacher status. I want—" Her voice caught, briefly, on the old grief, the memory-shaped hole where her mother's face used to be, paid to a river-witch to save her life. She'd stopped resenting the price a long time ago. Some days she still counted it. "I want to grow old enough that this scar stops pulling when I laugh. Which means laughing more, medically speaking."

"That's not medicine."

"It is now. I'm an ex-Inquisitor, I know how to make rules sound official."

Kestrel was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Ember crept closer, lowering herself into the dirt beside them both with the delicacy of a siege engine trying to be subtle, and rested her chin on Serah's knee. Warm as a hearthstone. Heavier than sin.

"I kept thinking," Kestrel said at last, very softly, "that choosing something meant the world would take it. That's the oldest lesson I have. Love something quietly or lose it loudly." They looked at their hands—the appraiser's hands, the speaker's hands, hands that had given a true name away and received one back and been remade by the exchange. "But you're asking me to choose loudly."

"I'm asking you to plant a tree that takes fifteen years," Serah said. "Loudly is optional. Stubbornly is mandatory."

And Kestrel Okonkwo—who had lied fluently to Inquisitors, refused a throne, spoken a dying dragon back to life with nothing but introduction and love—laughed, wet-eyed and helpless, and took the seed back.

* * *

They planted it together, which took longer than necessary because Ember insisted on supervising each shovelful and Odile came out halfway through to inform them they were digging in the wrong spot according to the original guild plans, and then examined the plans, and then declared that the original plans had been drawn by a coward and the tree should stay exactly where it was. This was, Serah had learned, how Odile expressed affection: through decisive reversal.

"There's a tradition," Odile said, watching from the doorway with her arms folded, "in the old country. My mother's country, before it was anybody's province. You plant a slow tree when you intend to stay somewhere. Fast trees are for tenants. Slow trees are a promise to people who haven't been born yet."

"Did your family plant one?" Serah asked.

Odile's face did the thing it did when her sister's name came near the surface—still tender, even now, even with letters arriving from a hill village full of pressed flowers and decades of careful questions being answered by post. "She did," Odile said. "My sister. Before she went looking for Vhalcrest. There's an orchard outside Thornmere with her tree in it. Almond. Takes nine years to fruit." She paused. "It fruited while we were burning, apparently. Kestrel, your rats tell you everything—tell the orchard I said thank you."

"I'll pass it along," Kestrel said, and Serah knew they would, knew the rats would carry it in their rat-way, knew the trees would lean a little closer to the walls of Thornmere that night. The world had gotten stranger since the fire returned, kinder-strange, conversational. Serah was still adjusting to living in a world that answered back.

When Odile went inside, Kestrel patted the earth flat over the seed, and Ember breathed on it once—a single careful exhale, the smallest flameless warmth a dragon could offer, a christening—and Serah put her arm around Kestrel's waist and they stood looking at a patch of bare dirt that was, somehow, the most legible document either of them had ever signed.

"Fifteen years," Serah said. "I'll be grey."

"You'll be devastating."

"I'll be *grey*, Kess."

"You'll be devastatingly grey, and I'll have ledgers proving it."

* * *

Evening came down soft over the guild. From the open windows came the sounds Serah had once believed belonged to other people's lives: students arguing about sigil theory in the dining hall, Odile threatening the wine shipment with an audit, Ember on the roof practicing her declensions of *Serah-snores* at a volume calculated for maximum dignity-loss. Down the lane, the lamplighter worked his way toward the market district where Captain Vess—retired, reformed, currently embroiled in a municipal election she was going to win by interrogating the potholes—was giving a speech nobody had asked for.

Serah and Kestrel sat on the courtyard steps, sharing a blanket that was technically communal property and practically theirs, and did the work of peace, which turned out to be much harder and much better than the work of war. Peace, Serah was learning, was not a place you arrived. It was a practice, like scales, like drills—done daily, imperfectly, in the teeth of every old habit that said *run*.

"So," Kestrel said, head on her shoulder. "Badly plotted. What's the next scene?"

Serah considered. "There's a rumor," she said, "from across the arch. One of the Vhalcrest hill villages. A child who talks to goats, and the goats are saying strange things. Things about the deep valleys. Things that don't map."

Kestrel went still. Not afraid-still. Listening-still.

"Goats," they said.

"Very credible goats."

A long pause. Somewhere beyond the world's edge, in a country that was on the maps again, a dragon's fire burned steady and honest, and something in the deep valleys stirred the way old doors stir when someone, somewhere, puts a hand on the handle.

"Well," Kestrel said, sitting up, eyes bright with the particular terror-joy Serah had learned to read better than any cipher, "we'd better sharpen the ledgers."

And Serah laughed, scar pulling, tree planted, home.
