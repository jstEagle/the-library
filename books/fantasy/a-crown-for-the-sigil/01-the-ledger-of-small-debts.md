## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

There were three hundred and eleven ways to be invisible in the city of Corvane, and Marguerite Voss had practiced most of them.

He had not learned them all at once. Invisibility, like bone-carving, was a craft of small patient cuts. You learned to walk at the hour when the lamplighters were between rounds and the shopkeepers were still arguing with their wives about the price of flour. You learned to dress in colors that made eyes slide off you the way rain slid off oiled canvas—mouse-brown, gutter-grey, the brown of a ledger nobody wanted to open. You learned, above all, never to owe anyone anything, because a debt was a hook, and a hook was a rope, and a rope meant someone pulling you into the light where they could look at your face and say, later, *I know that man.*

Marguerite Voss owed no one. It had taken him forty-one years to arrange, and he maintained it the way other men maintained their health: daily, cheerfully, and with a certain grim attention to detail.

So on the morning the frost came early to Corvane—the morning everything began, though he did not know it yet—he did what he did every morning. He rose before the cookhouse fires were lit. He ate his bread dry, standing, so as not to owe the cook even the courtesy of a chair. He took his ledger-key from around his neck, fit it into the lock of the counting room door, and went in to balance accounts for miracles.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a narrow building wedged between a chandler's and a house that had been condemned so long it had achieved a kind of civic respectability. From the street it looked like nothing—a tall thin slice of building with a sign so faded that most passersby read it as GUILD OF IMPOSSIBLE THINGS and assumed it was a joke, or a pawnshop, or both. This suited the guild fine. Impossible things kept best in the dark.

The counting room sat at the top of four flights of stairs, under the roof, where the rain could be heard thinking. Marguerite's desk filled half of it; the ledgers filled the rest. There were ledgers for flying carpets (depreciation schedule attached), ledgers for love potions recalled by their makers, ledgers for a wish granted in 1187 whose consequences were *still* trickling through the accounts like water finding its level. And there were the small ledgers, the ones he liked best, for the little miracles nobody thought of as miracles anymore: the candle that never burned down, the kettle that sang in tune, the grandmother's chair that always held one more person than it should.

Somebody had to keep track. That was the whole of the guild's business, when you stripped away the mystery people liked to imagine. Somebody had to write it down.

And somebody had to hear the dead complain while they did it.

"Complaint in the third register," said a voice through Marguerite's left hand, as he set pen to paper. The dead spoke through him the way cold spoke through iron—not all the time, and never politely. His fingers ached faintly, and then an old woman's voice was using his knuckles like a pulpit. "The memorial stone they put over me says I was generous. I was *not* generous. I want it corrected before anyone visits."

"You've been dead eleven years," Marguerite said mildly, writing. "Who's visiting?"

"My cat. She knows."

Marguerite added a marginal note: *Amend inscription. Generous → thrifty, but fair.* The voice in his hand subsided, satisfied, the way the dead always were once something was written down. That was the secret of them, the thing no one understood until they'd been dead a while: they didn't want much. They wanted the record straight. They wanted to exist somewhere besides other people's faulty memories.

Marguerite understood this better than most. He kept everyone's records but his own.

* * *

At midmorning there came a knock at the counting room door—two soft raps, then one, the client's knock—and he admitted a woman with flour in her eyebrows and grief worn the way you wear a coat too heavy for the season.

"Bonesetter," she said. Everyone called him that who didn't know better. It wasn't his trade, exactly, but it was close enough for a city that had stopped asking questions around the year of his birth.

"Memorial work?" he asked, because they always came for memorial work first.

"My husband." She set a parcel on his desk and unwrapped it with hands that shook only a little. Inside lay a shinbone, clean and pale, and a paper bearing dates. "Forty years a ferryman on the Graywater. The temple wants three lines and a dove carved on his stone, and he'd have hated it. He hated doves. Called them pigeons with good publicity." Her mouth trembled around what wanted to be a smile. "I want the bone done instead. Something he'd have laughed at. Can you...?" She gestured vaguely at her ear, then at his hands. People never knew how to ask. "Can you ask him?"

"I can listen," Marguerite said, which was the honest version. "What he says is between you and him."

She paid in advance—coins counted twice, because he counted them twice, and she understood without being told that this was part of the service. No debts. Not even the small warm kind. Especially those.

When she had gone, Marguerite took up his finest blade, laid the bone against the leather pad, and rested his palms along it the way a listener leans toward a door.

The dead came through slowly, the way heat comes through a wall. First the ache in his wrists, then a smell that wasn't in the room—river water, tar, wet rope—and then a man's voice, gravelly with age and current, saying:

"She's going to give my ferry money to that useless nephew."

"That's not what she asked me to carve."

"I know my wife. Write it down anyway. *Beware the nephew.*"

"I don't put warnings on memorials," Marguerite said. "It's unprofessional."

"It's *honest.*"

"What would you rather it said?"

A pause, long enough that Marguerite felt it in his thumbs. Then the ferryman's voice came again, quieter, almost shy, which was a strange thing for a dead man to manage:

"Tell her the crossing home was the easy part. Tell her I mean—tell her every morning she woke up next to me was a calm river. Forty years. Never once white water." A grunt, embarrassed. "Something like that. She'll know how to fix the grammar. She always fixed my grammar."

So Marguerite carved it, in letters small enough to require closeness, which was the point: *EVERY MORNING WAS A CALM RIVER.* And beneath it, smaller still, because he was after all a professional: *Beware the nephew.*

When the widow returned that evening and read it, she wept, then laughed at the last line, then paid a bonus he tried to refuse. He refused twice more and lost—it was that or cause a scene, and scenes were visible—so he tucked the coin into the guild's alms box, thereby converting debt into charity, which was the closest thing to a loophole his conscience allowed.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the ledgers.

This was the part of the day Marguerite loved, if love were the right word for the particular contentment of columns adding up. He worked through the small debts first, because the guild's founder had instructed—in a hand so cramped it might have been written falling downstairs—that small debts collected promptly keep large debts from forming, which was true of money and true of nearly everything else.

Item: Mrs. Ansel of Chandler's Lane, holder of a self-trimming wick (miraculous provenance, minor), owed the guild two candles per annum in perpetuity. Paid through spring. Balanced.

Item: the singing kettle of the Copper Kettle tea house. Owed one song per quarter, performed within the guildhall, for the entertainment of staff. Overdue by nine years, the current cook having declined to sing on the grounds that she was a serious woman. Marguerite made a note to negotiate the fee down to humming.

Item, item, item. The pen scratched. The frost ticked against the window. The dead murmured in his hands at intervals—an argument about a fence line, a confession about a borrowed shovel, somebody's grandmother wanting it known that she had *always suspected* the Hendersons—and Marguerite wrote it all down, and the world stayed balanced, and he was invisible in it, safe as a coin at the bottom of a well.

Once, years ago, his master had caught him at this desk and stood watching him with an expression Marguerite had never learned to read.

"You balance books like a man defusing something," Master Corvin had said.

"Aren't they?"

"They're just books, boy. Numbers don't bite."

"Numbers are teeth," Marguerite had said, without looking up, "if you let anyone see they're yours."

His master had gone quiet at that, in a way Marguerite had noticed but never understood, and had shuffled out of the room trailing the smell of pipe smoke and old miracles. Marguerite thought of it now, the way he thought of his master most days—briefly, at a slight angle, the way you look at the sun. Dead six years. Still owing the guild one song, technically, though Marguerite would never collect. Some debts you carried because collecting them would mean admitting they existed.

He was halfway through the third column when the pen stopped.

Not his pen. *A* pen. Across the room, in the stand where the guild's oldest ledger—the Black Book, the founding book, chained shut since before Marguerite had been born—lay sleeping, something had moved. A sound like a nib touching paper. Like a breath drawn through ink.

Marguerite sat very still. In his hands, the dead had gone silent—all of them at once, every murmuring ghost suddenly holding its breath, which had never happened before, because the dead did not hold their breaths, having none.

The sound did not come again. After a while, the murmur resumed, one aggrieved grandmother at a time, and Marguerite finished his column with a steadiness he did not feel, and locked the Black Book's chain himself, and checked the lock twice, and told himself it was the frost settling in the beams.

Frost settled. Frost did not write.

That night, in the cookhouse below, the orphan child Sefa said her prayers out loud, as she did every night, loudly, artlessly, thanking various powers by name for bread and blankets and the chandler's cat and asking them please, please, pretty please, for the thousandth time, to make her prayers actually work this time, and everyone smiled the way you smile at children, and no one listened.

But upstairs, in the counting room, Marguerite Voss dreamed of frost spreading across a window in letters, in columns, in a hand he almost recognized, writing and writing and writing—and when he woke before dawn with his hands aching like weather, there was a new entry in yesterday's ledger, in ink he did not own, recording a payment demanded by a name that had not answered a prayer in sixty years.

The dead in his bones were all facing east, every one of them, like compass needles.

Like witnesses.
