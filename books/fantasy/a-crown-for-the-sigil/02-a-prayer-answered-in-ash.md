## 2. A Prayer Answered in Ash

Marguerite Voss had three rules for living invisibly, and he recited them every morning the way other men recited prayers, which was ironic, because prayers were precisely the thing he intended never to have answered.

One: owe no one. Two: be owed by no one. Three: if a god offers you anything, check the ledger twice, then run.

He recited them while the kettle came to a boil in the little room behind the counting office, and he recited them while he trimmed his carving knife, and by the time he sat down at his desk with the morning's first entry open before him, he had recited them so many times they had worn smooth as river stones, which was the point. A rule repeated often enough stops being a rule and becomes a wall. Marguerite had spent forty years building walls out of sentences.

The guildhall of the Guild of Impossible Things was quiet at this hour. Down the corridor, Cook was arguing with her ovens, a daily negotiation she invariably won, though the ovens put up a spirited fight. Somewhere above, the archivists' footsteps crossed the ceiling like cautious mice. And in the counting office, Marguerite balanced the accounts of miracles.

That was the phrase the old ledgers used, *accounts of miracles*, and it was accurate in the way that most terrible things are accurate. People came to the guild when they wanted something the world would not give them, and the guild found it, and the guild was paid, and the payment was written down, because a miracle without a ledger is just a rumor with consequences.

Marguerite turned the page. The dead murmured through his hands.

They always did. It was the bone-carving that drew them, or perhaps it was Marguerite himself, though he preferred to blame the bone. His fingers rested on the day's work—a memorial rib, commissioned by a widow for a husband who had been, by all accounts, a difficult man—and the moment his skin touched it, the dead leaned in like neighbors at a fence.

*He never fixed the shutter,* said a voice like dry leaves. *Forty years. I asked about the shutter.*

"That was your husband's shutter, madam," Marguerite murmured, not looking up. "You may raise it with him directly. I'm told the afterlife has excellent acoustics."

*It has terrible acoustics,* the dead woman said, wounded. *Everything echoes.*

"Then you'll have plenty of time to practice projecting."

He set his hands flat on the desk and let the murmuring settle to a background hum, like bees behind a wall. This was the other thing Marguerite did, the thing no one at the guild fully understood and he took pains to keep it that way: he heard the dead. Not through séances or smoke, not with candles and theatrical voices. Through his hands. Bone remembered, and bone that had been carved by a bone-carver who listened remembered *loudly*, and Marguerite had learned to keep the volume of the afterlife at a civilized murmur the way other men learned to ignore a dripping tap.

The widow's commission was simple: her husband's name in the rib, the dates, and a single line of text. *He did his best,* the widow had said, and then, after a long pause, *write it exactly like that, no more than that.* Marguerite appreciated a client who understood the economy of epitaphs.

He was three letters into the name when the ledger moved.

Marguerite went very still.

It was the great ledger, the master volume, the one that sat chained to its lectern because it was older than the guildhall and had opinions about being moved. Its pages were supposed to turn only under his hand. Its ink was supposed to flow only from his pen. These were not mystical properties; they were simply the rules of bookkeeping, and Marguerite had spent his whole life believing that bookkeeping was the one force in the universe more reliable than gravity.

The page turned itself. The pen in its inkwell lifted, dripped once, and began to write.

Marguerite did not scream. Screaming was visible, and Marguerite had a reputation to maintain, or rather a non-reputation, which was harder to maintain and required considerably more discipline. Instead he sat in his chair with his hands flat on the desk and watched the pen scratch across the page in a hand that was not his hand, in ink that was not his ink, recording an entry he had not made.

*Received: one prayer, offered in desperation, by Maren Oduya, laundress, of Tanner's Row.*

*Answered by: the god who waits under the ice, called by the old name, which we will not write here because names are doors.*

*Terms: the child's fever, broken by dawn. Payment: to be collected.*

*Payment: to be collected.*

The pen underlined it twice, which Marguerite found gratuitous. Then it set itself back in the inkwell with a small, satisfied click, and the great ledger lay open and innocent, as if nothing had happened, as if the room had not just become a different room.

Marguerite sat for a long moment.

Then, because he was who he was, he reached for his blotter and checked the entry against the guild's standing rates. A fever, broken. That was a small miracle, a third-tier intervention, and under the old schedules—the schedules nobody had used in sixty years—the standard payment for a third-tier intervention was—

He stopped reading.

Under the old schedules, the standard payment for a third-tier intervention was *one memory of warmth, freely given, or equivalent.* And beneath that, in the older ink, in the hand of a guildmaster two hundred years dead, someone had written: *or whatever the god decides, which is why we stopped dealing with gods, you absolute donkeys.*

Marguerite closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping animal that might be pretending to sleep.

"Well," he said to the empty office. "That's fine. That's perfectly fine."

It was not fine. He knew it was not fine the way a man knows a storm is coming—not from any single sign, but from the particular quality of the silence before it, the way the birds go quiet, the way the air gets a taste to it. Sixty years, the gods had been silent. Sixty years, the ledgers had recorded only trades between mortals, which were manageable, negotiable, subject to the great leveling mercy of paperwork. And now, on an ordinary Tuesday, with the kettle not yet cold, a god had answered a laundress's prayer and filed the paperwork itself.

The dead had gone quiet too. All of them. Even the shutter woman. The silence of the dead was somehow worse than their murmuring, like the moment an argument stops next door and you realize they've both turned to look at *your* window.

Marguerite did the only sensible thing. He made tea.

* * *

"You're making tea at half past seven in the morning," said the ghost of Master Corvin Aldous, "which means either something wonderful or something appalling has happened, and given that you're making it in the *good* pot, I'm going to gamble on appalling."

Marguerite did not jump. He had gotten better at not jumping over the years, though the first dozen times his dead master had simply appeared in the counting office, sitting on the edge of the desk like a crow that had decided furniture was beneath it, he had dropped things. A lot of things. There was still a chip in the good teapot from the third manifestation, which Corvin referred to, whenever he wished to be insufferable, as "the teapot's little scar of grief."

"You could knock," Marguerite said.

"I'm dead, dear boy. Knocking is for the living, and the living knock *loudly*, which you'd know if you ever had visitors, which you don't, by design." Corvin drifted a half-circle around the desk, translucent and insubstantial and somehow still managing to look down his nose. In life, Corvin Aldous had been a small, sharp man with ink-stained fingers and a laugh like a door hinge; in death, he was the same, only bluer. "Something has written in the ledger."

Marguerite's hands paused on the teapot lid. "I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to. You made tea in the good pot, you haven't started your carving, and you've got the face you get when arithmetic has betrayed you." Corvin settled onto the lectern beside the great ledger, which was either very brave or very rude, depending on how one felt about ghosts sitting on guild property. "Show me."

Marguerite showed him. He turned the ledger around and watched his master's ghost read, and it occurred to him, not for the first time, that there was something deeply strange about his life, that he stood in a room before dawn reading the handwriting of a god with the ghost of the man who had taught him bookkeeping peering over his shoulder, and that he had somehow arranged things so that this was the *least* remarkable thing that would likely happen to him this year.

Corvin read the entry twice. His translucent face did something complicated.

"When," he said.

"Sometime between my first cup and the widow's rib. The pen moved on its own. The entry is filed under third-tier intervention, old schedules." Marguerite poured the tea, because his hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. "Payment pending. No terms specified."

"No terms specified," Corvin repeated.

"Which is irregular."

"Which is *catastrophic*, Marguerite. A god that specifies terms is a god doing business. A god that leaves the terms blank is a god leaving itself room." Corvin leaned over the entry, though of course he could not touch it, and his voice dropped into the register he had used, forty years ago, to explain why Marguerite must never, ever let anyone learn what his hands could do. "I told you once. I told you the year you came to me, all elbows and secrets, with a dead woman's name carved into your palm where nobody could see it. I told you: the gods went quiet, and we built the guild in the quiet, and the quiet is the only reason any of this works. Miracles traded between mortals are commerce. Miracles traded with *gods*—"

"Are doors," Marguerite said.

"Are doors," Corvin agreed. "When gods write back, doors open. I said it then and I'll say it now, and I'll keep saying it in whatever blue half-life they've stuck me in, because apparently I'm to be a warning forever, which I'd find insulting if it weren't so accurate."

Marguerite drank his tea. It was too hot. He drank it anyway, because the heat gave him something true and small to hold onto while the larger, colder thing settled over the room.

The frost was already forming on the window, he noticed. It was early autumn. Frost in early autumn was wrong, and the frost was forming in patterns, and the patterns were almost, but not quite, letters.

He got up and drew the curtain.

"That's not going to help," Corvin observed.

"No," Marguerite agreed. "But it's tidy."

* * *

The rest of the morning, he worked. This was his answer to most catastrophes: work. The guild had survived the dissolution, the purges, the three separate attempts to tax it out of existence, and the unfortunate Incident with the Weather Broker of '41, and it had survived all of them because somewhere in the building there was always one calm person doing the accounts while everyone else panicked, and Marguerite had appointed himself that person so long ago he no longer remembered it being a choice.

He carved the widow's rib. *He did his best.* Four words, in a hand small enough to be humble and deep enough to last. The dead husband drifted by at one point and read it over Marguerite's shoulder.

*Hm,* said the dead husband.

"Any objections?"

*I did fix the shutter,* the dead man said. *The year before I died. She never noticed.*

"I'll carve an addendum if you file a formal request."

*What's the procedure?*

"There isn't one. You're dead. That's rather the point of the whole arrangement."

The dead man considered this, and drifted off looking vaguely aggrieved, which Marguerite suspected was his eternal condition. Marguerite blew the bone dust from the finished rib, wrapped it in cloth, and set it in the delivery tray, and for perhaps an hour the world was ordinary: ink and bone and the smell of Cook's bread coming up through the floorboards, arguing successfully with the ovens.

Then the ledger moved again.

The pen lifted. The page turned. And Marguerite, who had moved his chair to the far side of the room specifically so he could pretend this wasn't happening, put down his knife and walked back over, because he was a bookkeeper before he was anything else, and a bookkeeper looks.

*Amendment to entry: prayer of Maren Oduya, laundress.*

*The child's fever broke at dawn. The mother has prayed a second prayer: that the child live to grow old.*

*Answered.*

*Terms: pending.*

*Terms: pending.*

"Pending," Marguerite said aloud. "You can't just keep a line of credit open with a *god*."

*The god can,* said the ledger, in the sense that the pen wrote, in fresh ink, beneath the entry: *The god can.*

Marguerite sat down slowly.

"That," said Corvin's voice from the doorway, "is new. Ledgers don't usually talk back. Ledgers are the last polite thing left in the world."

"Master. The laundress. Tanner's Row." Marguerite was already pulling the guild's address rolls, his hands moving on their own, because panic was for people without filing systems. "If a god is collecting on Tanner's Row, I want to know what else on that street has ever been traded, promised, or prayed for. I want every entry in forty years."

"Forty years is a great many entries."

"Then I'd best start now."

Corvin watched him work for a while, the ghost hovering at the window with his arms crossed, translucent and worried. Outside, the frost had melted off the glass, but the air still had that taste to it, cold and mineral, like the air over deep water. Somewhere far off—too far to be a real sound, too close to be a memory—a bell was ringing for no occasion Marguerite knew.

"Marguerite," Corvin said at last, gently, in the voice he used maybe once a decade. "The last time a god wrote in that ledger, I was a young man, and within a year the country was gone. Not defeated. Not conquered. *Gone*, the way a word is gone from the tip of your tongue. And the guild bought its survival with pieces of what was left, and we told ourselves we'd made a bargain, and we spent sixty years not looking at what we'd actually done." He drifted closer. "The gods going quiet wasn't peace, dear boy. It was a held breath."

Marguerite's pen had stopped. He hadn't noticed it stopping.

"Then what," he said, "is it doing now?"

"Exhaling," said Corvin Aldous. "Gods help us all. It's exhaling."

The pen, in the great ledger, wrote one more line, unbidden, in ink that gleamed like frost:

*The doorkeeper will be needed.*

Marguerite looked at that line for a long time. Then he did what he always did with entries he did not understand: he filed it, dated it, and told himself it could wait until morning.

Outside, on Tanner's Row, a child's fever broke, and a mother wept with gratitude, and somewhere beneath the city something old and patient made a note in its own book, and began, unhurried, to decide what it was owed.

The frost came back on the window that night, in letters this time.

Marguerite kept the curtain drawn, and did not sleep, and in the morning he would find the first entry in the new ledger already waiting for him, written in a hand almost exactly like his own.
