## 3. What the Guild Sold

There were three rules for auditing the deep vaults of the Guild of Impossible Things, and Marguerite Voss had learned all of them the hard way, which was the only way anyone learned anything down there.

Rule one: never audit alone. The vaults remembered being counted, and they liked to be counted, and a man who went down alone might find himself still counting at midnight, then at dawn, then long after dawn had stopped meaning anything.

Rule two: bring chalk. Not ink—chalk. Ink suggested permanence to things down there that took permanence personally.

Rule three: if something in a crate knocked from the inside, make a note of it and move on. Do not open the crate. Especially do not open the crate *apologetically*. Apology, in the vaults, could be construed as an offer.

"You talk to yourself," said Pellon Wray, who was his audit partner this quarter because Pellon had lost the coin toss and took the loss with the wounded dignity of a man twice passed over for head clerk. "Down here. You know that, don't you? Constantly. Like a kettle."

"I'm balancing," Marguerite said.

"You're muttering about candle tariffs to a shelf."

"The shelf has candle tariffs." Marguerite ran his thumb along the ledger spine in question—*Miscellaneous Illuminations, Vol. IX*—"and the shelf is forty years delinquent." He paused. "The dead also object to your cologne, incidentally."

Pellon sniffed his own collar, mortified. This was the great convenience of working with Pellon: he believed every strange thing Marguerite said, and apologized to invisible parties with real sincerity. It made the arithmetic go faster.

The deep vaults occupied the guildhall's cellar and, depending on the week, several cellars that belonged to other buildings entirely. Nobody had drawn a map since the dissolution, because maps implied borders and borders implied ownership and the vaults had opinions about both. So they worked by lamplight and habit: aisle nine held failed miracles, aisle twelve held successful ones nobody wanted to claim, and at the very back, past the crates that occasionally knocked, stood the Founding Archive, which no auditor touched.

Which was, of course, exactly why Marguerite was walking toward it with a lamp in one hand and his master's ledger-key warming against his chest like a swallowed coal.

It had started that morning. A faint heat under his shirt, where the old brass sigil hung on its chain—the key his master Oren Voss had pressed into his hands eleven years ago with the words *"You'll know when it matters, and you'll wish you didn't."* Typical Oren. The man had delivered prophecies the way other people delivered bad news about plumbing: reluctantly, and usually while doing something else.

But this morning the key had been warm. By noon it was hot. And by the time Pellon lost the coin toss, Marguerite had already decided that if the sigil wanted him in the Founding Archive, he would rather walk in on his own feet than be dragged.

"Founding Archive," Pellon said, reading the door. His voice did something complicated. "We're not—we don't audit *that*. That's not even in the charter. I checked once. Out of curiosity."

"Charter's wrong."

"Charter's *wrong*," Pellon repeated, in the tone of a man filing the phrase away to panic about later. "You're going in."

"I'm going in."

"And I'm—"

"You're holding the lamp," Marguerite said, and handed it over before courage could fail either of them.

* * *

The lock was not a lock so much as a courtesy. The sigil fit the plate by the door, and the plate recognized it, and the door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled recently by no one, which meant the door had oiled itself, which meant the door *wanted* visitors, which Marguerite chose not to think about.

Inside: dust, but polite dust, arranged in drifts like snow against a fence. Shelves of ledgers older than any he'd ever catalogued, bound in leather gone the color of tea left too long. And on the central table, laid out as though someone had stepped away mid-inventory sixty years ago and simply never come back—

—a border.

Not a map of one. The thing itself. A border, coiled like a grey ribbon, humming faintly with the smell of checkpoint tea and the sound—very distant—of a stamp coming down on paper. Marguerite knew what it was the way you know a face in a crowd you shouldn't: instantly, and with your stomach dropping ahead of your understanding.

"Pellon," he said carefully. "What does that look like to you?"

Pellon leaned in. "A ribbon?"

"What kind of ribbon?"

"A... bureaucratic one?" Pellon frowned. "Why do I want to file it?"

Marguerite's hands, which heard the dead through bone and brass and everything else the world made hard, were telling him the rest. The border was *asleep*, mostly, but it dreamed, and its dream was queues. Miles of them. Families with their papers folded just so.

He set it down gently and opened the nearest ledger.

The founding entries were written in three hands. The first was formal, guild-crisp: *Item: the western march, approx. forty miles, condition good, some erosion near the river. Sold to the Assembly of Free Cities. Price: grain allotment sufficient for 11,000 souls, winter term, renewable.*

The second hand was shakier, angrier: *They are buying us by the pound. We have no right. We do it anyway.*

The third hand appeared only once, at the bottom of the first page, underlined twice:

*Better a country sold than a country starved. Let whoever reads this judge us. We already have.*

Marguerite sat down on the stool, which was the only furniture, which suggested the founders had expected someone to sit for a long time.

"They sold the border," he said aloud.

"There's a border right there," Pellon said helpfully.

"No—I mean they sold it *first*. Before the anthems." He turned pages. There were the anthems: three of them, rolled into lead tubes, one still faintly audible if you held it to your ear—the second verse only, the part about the wheat, sung by no throats in particular. There was the national bird, stuffed, though *stuffed* was generous; it occasionally ruffled. There was the coat of arms, minus the motto, which had been sold separately to a collector in Marrowgate and was presumably hanging on someone's wall saying nothing at anyone.

And there, in a shallow drawer lined with velvet gone threadbare, a shadow.

A king's shadow. It looked like nothing—like a smudge of dark on dark—but when Marguerite's lamp swung, the smudge moved a half-second late, and it wore, unmistakably, the shape of a crown and shoulders and a man standing very straight because someone was always watching.

"Oh," Pellon whispered. He had gone pale. "Oh, that's—that's the *Regent's*—from the portraits, that's—" He sat down abruptly on the floor. "We sold the king's *shadow*?"

"*Sold*," Marguerite confirmed. His voice came out flat, which was how his voice behaved when feeling exceeded capacity. "Entry says the shadow was the last item. Everything else had gone. The buyers wanted a symbol of legitimacy for their new provinces. The guild wanted..." He read. He wished he hadn't. "Passports. Four hundred thousand blank passports. Guaranteed. For everyone who wanted out."

The dissolution had happened before Marguerite was born. Everyone knew the shape of it: the old country dissolved itself, officially, quietly, in a signing ceremony attended by nine people and one embarrassed photographer. The histories said the people voted for it. The histories said the transition was orderly.

The histories did not say that the country had been *sold for parts* to buy its own people passage out.

Four hundred thousand passports. Grain for eleven thousand souls, winter term, renewable. An anthem in three tubes. Forty miles of western march.

His safe little life—his ledgers, his candle tariffs, his careful invisibility, his name spoken by no one—all of it stood, floor by floor, on this table in a basement.

"Who bought the passports?" Pellon asked, very small.

Marguerite checked. "Everyone. Neighboring kingdoms. The Assembly. The Church of the Second Dawn." He kept reading because stopping would have been worse. "The guild didn't take payment for those. Look. *Price: forgiven.*"

*Forgiven.* As if forgiveness were theirs to give. As if the dead who hadn't gotten out—the ones whose prayers went unanswered for sixty years, the ones now murmuring complaints through Marguerite's hands at all hours—had signed anything.

The sigil burned against his sternum.

He pulled it out of his collar. In the lamplight the brass sigil—the doorkeeper's mark, the little gate-and-key emblem stamped on his inherited ledger-key—was glowing. Not metaphorically. Actually, softly, the way a poker glows an hour after the fire's out. And beneath the glow, engraved lines he had never seen in eleven years of handling the thing were surfacing like words rising through pond water:

*A DEBT OF COUNTRY. HELD IN TRUST. PAYABLE TO THE BEARER'S PEOPLE.*

"Marguerite," Pellon said. "Your necklace is doing religion."

"It's doing accounting," Marguerite said. "Worse."

Because he understood, suddenly, with the sick clarity of a man reading his own name in someone else's will, what the sigil was. Not a key to the archive. Not a badge of office. A *receipt*. The last unpaid line of the founding bargain, held by whoever carried it—and marked, in brass and older law than brass, as belonging to the bearer's people.

The trouble was that Marguerite's people no longer existed to be paid. They'd been dissolved, dispersed, sold, survived. The debt had nowhere to go. It sat. It warmed. It waited, patient as any entry in his ledgers, for circumstances to change.

For sixty years, nothing had changed.

This morning the key had begun to warm.

"Someone's called it due," Marguerite said quietly. "Or something has. Something's woken up on the other side of this receipt and asked for the country back."

Pellon looked at the border coiled on the table. At the anthem tubes. At the shadow in its drawer, standing very straight, wearing its crown, waiting the way shadows wait—for light, or for the thing that cast them.

"You can't pay back a country," Pellon said. It wasn't an argument. It was a hope, offered upward.

"You can't," Marguerite agreed. "But something down here thinks you can try."

The dead in his hands had gone silent. That was the worst part. Sixty years of murmured grievances, and now nothing—not quiet, but *silence*, the deliberate silence of a crowded room when the important person enters. Every unpaid soul in the world, holding its breath.

The sigil cooled by a single degree. Not settling. *Listening.*

From somewhere above them—three floors up, in the counting room where the ledgers lived in their neat rows—came a sound Marguerite had never heard the guildhall make.

A pen, writing.

No one was in the counting room. He had checked the roster himself; the night clerks had gone home at six. But the sound carried down through the floors the way sounds carry when buildings want you to hear them: scratch, scratch, scratch, unhurried, fluent, a hand that knew exactly what it was recording and had all night to record it.

"That'll be the books," said Pellon.

"Yes," said Marguerite, standing, pocketing the burning sigil, taking back the lamp.

"Books writing themselves?"

"Ink remembers, Pellon." He was already moving toward the stairs, faster than he liked, because dread had arrived in him fully formed, the way frost arrives on a window—not gradually, but all at once, in the shape of everything it intends to cover. "And lately, so does everything else."

Behind them, in the drawer, the king's shadow shifted its weight from one foot to the other.

It had been waiting sixty years. It could tell that waiting was nearly over.
