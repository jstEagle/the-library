## 4. The Child Who Prays Too Loudly

Sefa had three rules for living at the Guild of Impossible Things, and she had invented all of them herself, which she felt made them more official.

Rule one: never eat the gray porridge on Thursdays. Nobody knew what was in it, and the one time she'd asked Cook, Cook had gone very quiet and said, *Best not to name it, love,* which Sifa had decided was answer enough.

Rule two: the third floorboard from the hearth was hers. Not for sitting—for talking. More on that later.

Rule three: if anyone ever asked her where she came from, she was allowed to say *about,* and then look busy.

She was practicing rule three when Marguerite Voss came into the cookhouse that morning, all long bones and quiet feet, carrying his ledger like other men carried knives. He sat in his usual corner, which was the one with the best view of the door and the worst view of the fire, because Marguerite Voss was the sort of person who preferred to be cold and informed rather than warm and surprised.

"About," said Sefa, before he could ask, and looked busy scrubbing a pot that did not need it.

"I didn't ask," said Marguerite.

"You were going to. Your eyebrows asked."

"My eyebrows," said Marguerite, "are retired."

That was the thing about Master Voss. He talked like a man who had practiced having as little of himself as possible left over after each conversation. He never touched anyone. He never owed anyone. Cook said he balanced the guild's accounts so carefully that even his shadow probably paid its own debts. Sefa had checked once—watched his shadow very hard while he walked down the hall—and honestly she wasn't sure it kept up with him properly. It lagged, sometimes. Like it was doing sums.

The cookhouse was the warm heart of the guildhall, mostly because it contained the actual heart (the great hearth) and also most of the people who were kind to her: Cook, whose arms were like bread loaves and whose swearing was educational; Tam and Brava, the door-wardens, who played cards badly and let her deal; and old Fennick, who was technically dead but nobody liked to make a fuss about it, least of all Fennick.

But this morning the cookhouse held something new. This morning, over by the window, sat the strangest object Sefa had ever seen: a lantern, iron-caged, no bigger than a turnip, and inside it a flame that moved wrong. Fire was supposed to leap. This fire leaned. It bent toward the door of the cookhouse like a dog hearing its name called from another room.

"That's new," Sefa announced, because announcing things was one of her talents.

"It arrived last night," said Cook, ladling out the good porridge, because it was Wednesday. "No carrier. No note. Just set itself on the step like it owned the place."

"Things don't arrive here by accident," said Tam from the card table. "Nothing arrives at this guildhall by accident. That's sort of the whole point of us."

The flame leaned harder at the door.

Sefa finished her porridge — slowly, savoring, because Wednesday porridge was a gift and gifts deserved attention — and then went to sit on her floorboard.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about the third floorboard from the hearth.

It was warm. All the floorboards near a hearth are warm, but this one was warm in a way that got under your skin and settled, like being listened to. And when Sefa sat on it and talked — just talked, out loud, about whatever needed saying — the warmth answered back. Not in words. In feelings, mostly. A sort of up-swelling under her legs when she said something true. A flatness, a cooling, when she lied.

She had discovered it her first week, crying on the kitchen floor because an orphanage in Correnmark had turned her away and the streets had nearly done worse, and she had prayed the way she did everything: loudly, artlessly, without any of the proper words.

*"Please,"* she had said to nobody, to the floor, to the whole unfair world, *"please, I'm not picky, I'll take anything, I'll take a warm floor."*

And the floor under her had gone warm. Deliberately, specifically warm, like a hand.

So she stayed. And she talked. And every night since, she had told the floorboard about her day, and the floorboard had swelled and cooled and occasionally — she was almost sure — hummed.

Tonight she told it about the lantern.

"There's a fire in the front room that wants to go somewhere," she said. "It keeps looking at the door. Do you know about that?"

The board hummed. A low, rising hum, like a struck pot lid, and this time it didn't stop, and this time it wasn't only the board. The whole floor joined in, faintly, a chord coming up through the grain of the wood, through the stone beneath, down and down to somewhere deep under the guildhall where, Sefa realized with her whole stomach, something very old had just rolled over and opened one eye.

"Oh," said Sefa. "You're not a floor."

The warmth surged up her spine like sunlight given teeth.

She should have been frightened. She understood that later, when everyone told her she should have been frightened. But Sefa had spent four years being frightened on streets where nothing answered prayers, and now something enormous and patient and warm had finally, finally answered back, and what she felt was not fear.

What she felt was heard.

"Hello," she whispered.

And the floor — gently, deliberately, unmistakably — pushed up against her knees. Once. Twice.

Like a handshake.

* * *

The trouble started at breakfast.

Sefa said grace.

This requires context. At the Guild of Impossible Things, nobody prayed. Ever. There was a sort of collective understanding, unspoken but enforced by decades of pointed throat-clearing, that prayer was vulgar, dangerous, and above all *loud* — that gods were like bears, best avoided by making no noise and keeping your food sealed. Grace at the guild table consisted of a brief, secular silence in which everyone stared at their spoons.

Sefa, who had learned her prayers from street-preachers and a half-remembered grandmother and her own enormous heart, did not do secular silence.

"*Thank you for the porridge, thank you for the roof, thank you for Cook and Tam and Brava and Master Voss even though he's prickly, please send me a family, amen.*"

She said it at full volume, eyes shut, hands clasped, radiating sincerity like a small stove.

The spoon next to her hand rattled.

The fire in the hearth stood straight up, all of it, every tongue of flame at attention.

And outside the shuttered window, something vast exhaled — a breath that smelled of rain on old stones, of harvest fields nobody had planted, of incense and river-mud and the inside of bells.

Everyone froze. Cook's ladle hung mid-pour. Tam's hand crept toward the poker.

Then it passed. The fire slumped back down. The spoon stilled. Breakfast resumed with the desperate normalcy of people who had all, simultaneously, agreed not to discuss it.

Except Marguerite. Marguerite was looking at her across the table with an expression she had never seen on him before. His face didn't change much, generally — it was the face of a man who had arranged it that way — but right now something behind it had come unlocked. He looked at her the way he must have looked at ledgers, she thought. Like he was checking the figures. Like he had found a number in a column that should not exist.

"Sefa," he said quietly. "Where did you learn to pray?"

"About," said Sefa, and looked busy with her porridge.

But her floorboard was humming. She could feel it through the bench, through her boots, faint and rising, and she could feel it *answering*, and she saw Marguerite feel it too, see him go pale and still as paper.

He stood up so fast his chair fell over.

Nobody had ever seen Master Voss knock anything over. It was, in its way, the loudest thing Sefa had ever witnessed.

* * *

He found her that afternoon in the kitchen, because of course he did; she suspected Marguerite Voss could find anyone anywhere by following the trail of things they owed.

He sat down across from her, which he never did, and folded his hands, which he definitely never did, and said, "I'm going to tell you something, and then I'm going to ask you something, and I would like the truth, which is not a currency we usually trade in around here."

"Okay," said Sefa, who recognized gravity when it wore that face.

"The old gods are waking up," he said. "Sixty years quiet, and this week they're answering prayers again. One was answered two nights ago, across the city. A mother prayed for her sick son, and something answered, and the payment is already written in my ledgers in ink I didn't put there." His voice stayed level, but Sefa watched his thumb press white against the back of his hand. "Prayers are doors, child. Every time you pray, you knock on a very old door, and lately — someone has been home."

Sefa considered this.

"So I should pray quieter?"

"I need you to understand how serious—"

"Because I can do quiet. I've never *tried*, mind. Tam says I was born at full volume. But I could try."

"Sefa." He leaned forward, and his eyes were doing something complicated — fear and arithmetic and, buried way down deep where he probably thought nobody could see it, worry. For her. Actual, specific, pointed-at-Sefa worry. "Something answered you this morning. Something under this building. Do you know what that means? Things that sleep under guildhalls for sixty years do not wake for porridge-thanks. They wake because they've heard something they want. You're not praying *near* a beacon, child. As far as I can tell, you *are* one."

The kitchen went very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked. Under the floorboards, Sefa felt the warmth go still — listening, the way it always listened, but now listening to itself being discussed.

"He's not wrong," Sefa admitted to the floor.

"Don't talk to it," said Marguerite sharply.

"It's nice."

"It doesn't have to be nice to be dangerous. Bread mold isn't nice either." He pinched the bridge of his nose. "That was a bad example. My point—" He stopped. Started again, slower, the way men do when they're building a sentence out of materials they don't usually work with. "My point is that you are ten years old, and you are sitting on top of something ancient because it was warm, and every hungry god in a hundred miles can hear you the way I can hear Cook swearing. And I have spent forty years making sure nothing in this world could find me, and now the loudest creature alive lives in my cookhouse and prays like she's ringing a bell tower."

Sefa absorbed this.

"So you're worried about me," she said.

A pause. The particular pause of Marguerite Voss confronted with an accurate statement he did not wish to own.

"I am concerned," he said carefully, "that you are a liability to this guild."

"That's not what your eyebrows say."

"My eyebrows are retired."

"They came back," said Sefa. "They're worried too."

For one strange, suspended moment, Sefa thought he might laugh. Something happened at the corner of his mouth — a twitch, a ghost of a twitch, the memory of a smile belonging to a much younger man — and then it was gone, filed away, ledgered.

"Eat your supper," he said, standing. "From tonight, you pray silently. Or better yet, you don't pray at all. If you must give thanks—" he hesitated, visibly renegotiating his entire worldview, "—give it to Cook. Out loud. Cook is safe."

"Cook will be flattered."

"Cook will survive flattery. The alternative," and here his voice dropped, and Sefa heard under it the thing he never said aloud, the fear older than her, older than the guild, "is that the next thing to answer you won't bother being warm first."

He left. His footsteps faded down the hall, quick and quiet, a man retreating from a battlefield he'd been losing for years.

Sefa ate her supper. Then she sat on her floorboard, because rules were rules.

"I'll do it quieter," she promised. "But I'm not stopping altogether. You're the only thing that ever answered."

The warmth rose under her, slow and sad and gentle, and pressed against her knees — once, twice — and then held there, steady, like a hand that had decided not to let go.

And far away, at the edge of the city, in a smuggler's cold hands, a lantern-flame that had been leaning east for three days suddenly turned, strained against its cage, and burned toward the guildhall like a compass needle finding north.
