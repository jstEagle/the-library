## 5. Dragon-Fire, Guttering

Ysolde had carried the lantern through nine provinces, two civil disturbances, one wedding she'd been hired to disrupt and instead wept at, and the entire length of the Saltmarsh Road in the rain. In all that time, the flame inside it had never gone out. It had, however, developed opinions.

"Stop that," she muttered at it.

The flame leaned hard against the glass, the way a tired dog leans against a door, and guttered down to something barely bigger than a candle's promise. Then it flared again—petulant, defiant—as if to say *I heard you.*

Dragon-fire. The last of it. Not a coal from some hearth, not a spark kept sentimental in a reliquary, but the actual flame, the one that had burned at the heart of the old country for six hundred years before the country was dissolved like sugar in bad tea. When the borders were sold, when the anthem was auctioned off in lots, when the king's shadow went to a collector who nobody would ever name, the fire had been carried out the back of the burning temple by a nineteen-year-old priestess who was supposed to be dead by then, and technically was, depending on which order you asked and how generous they were feeling about paperwork.

Forty years later, that same priestess was standing on a hill outside a city she'd sworn never to enter again, watching a guildhall that looked, frankly, unimpressive.

It sat low and wide between a chandler's shop and a wall covered in ivy that had clearly won its territorial war decades ago. No sigil above the door. No guards. A cookhouse chimney smoking comfortably into the grey evening, and someone inside playing a fiddle badly enough that it looped back around to charming. If Ysolde hadn't spent three months and most of her remaining money tracking the ledger-keys through seven intermediaries and one very expensive dinner with a man who traded exclusively in rumors about other people's rumors, she'd have walked past it without a glance.

That, she thought grimly, was rather the point of the place.

The lantern flickered again, lower this time. Lower than it had ever gone. She felt the drop in her chest like a change in weather, because she'd been bound to this flame since she was nineteen and it had been bound to her, and when it died—

When it died, the boundary between the world as it was and the country as it persisted—the ghost of it, pooled and patient under forty years of forgetting—would thin to nothing. She'd seen what came through when it thinned locally. A village in the Greymarch where every well filled with seawater from a coast that hadn't existed since the dissolution. A town square where an entire brass band played at midnight, every night, for no one, until the townsfolk moved away rather than keep hearing a national anthem they'd all been legally required to forget.

And lately, everywhere: prayers getting answered. Old gods waking up hungry. That was new. That was very bad.

She shifted the pack on her shoulders and started downhill.

* * *

The door of the Guild of Impossible Things did not have a knocker. It had a bell-pull, a sign reading *WE ARE NOT ACCEPTING APPLICATIONS*, and beneath that, in smaller, more resigned lettering, *OR COMPLAINTS ABOUT MIRACLES ALREADY RENDERED.*

Ysolde pulled the bell. Nothing happened. She pulled it again, longer, with the authority of a woman who had once commanded three hundred voices in liturgy and now commanded exactly one mule named Duchess, who was currently tied up at the chandler's and eating something she shouldn't.

Eventually the door opened a hand's width, revealing a slice of warm lamplight and half a face belonging to a boy of perhaps fifteen with flour on his forehead.

"We're not—" he began.

"I need a doorkeeper."

"Applications are closed."

"I'm not applying for membership." Ysolde lifted the lantern slightly, and the flame inside chose that exact moment to flare, throwing light the color of old gold across the boy's astonished face. "I'm delivering the reason your guild exists."

The boy stared at the lantern. His mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"That's—" he whispered. "That's not real. That's a story they tell novices. That's what Sister Anselm said when I asked why the founding charter has a scorch mark shaped like a crown."

"Sister Anselm," said Ysolde, "was wrong about a great many things, including, if memory serves, which end of a censor holds the incense." She pushed the door gently. It opened the rest of the way. "Fetch whoever keeps your ledgers. The deep ones. The ones nobody reads."

"The ledgers don't have a keeper exactly," the boy said faintly. "There's just Marguerite. He does everything. He does everything and nobody knows what he looks like, which is honestly kind of impressive because he's *right there*, usually—"

"Then find him."

The boy fled down a corridor, shouting "MARGUERITE" in the tones of one announcing an apocalypse, which Ysolde privately considered accurate.

She stepped inside, out of the wind, and the warmth of the hall settled around her like a coat being put over her shoulders by hands she couldn't see. Cookfire smell. Bread. Tallow and ink. Somewhere, the fiddle player had stopped, possibly due to the shouting. The lantern brightened fractionally, drinking in the warmth, and Ysolde let herself lean against the doorframe for just one moment—one moment of forty years' exhaustion, allowed, rationed, immediately regretted—before straightening.

Obligation didn't care how tired she was. Obligation had never once cared, and she'd stopped expecting it to somewhere around the year the order stripped her name and the smugglers gave her a different one that fit better anyway.

* * *

They gathered in the guildhall's front room, which appeared to serve simultaneously as an office, a parlor, a shrine to somebody's grandmother, and a place where chairs went to be repaired or possibly to retire. Ysolde counted five people: the flour-faced boy; an enormous cook with forearms like a blacksmith's and an expression of profound maternal suspicion; two guild members she took for clerks by their ink-stained cuffs; and, at the desk, a thin man with careful hands who was looking at her lantern the way other men look at their own funerals.

He didn't introduce himself. He didn't need to. Everything about him screamed *invisible made deliberate*: plain clothes chosen to be forgotten, a posture arranged to occupy no space, eyes that catalogued exits and debts in the same glance. But his hands, when they finally rose toward the lantern, moved with the certainty of someone who had spent a lifetime handling things older and heavier than himself.

"You're the bookkeeper," Ysolde said.

"I'm the ledger-keeper," he corrected mildly. "There's a distinction. Books are objects. Ledgers are promises." He paused. "Marguerite Voss."

"Voss," repeated one of the clerks, surprised. Ysolde noticed that too—the way the name landed in the room like a coin dropped in church. So the invisible man had a name after all, spoken rarely enough that it startled people.

"And you," Marguerite said, "are wearing the sun-disc of the Order of the Flame Ascendant under your coat. Wrong shoulder. The right-shoulder version is for priests in good standing."

Ysolde huffed despite herself. "You're observant."

"It's cheaper than being visible." He turned the lantern toward him, studying the glass, the mechanism, the small scorched crown worked into the handle. "Where has this been?"

"Burning. For forty years. Mostly in my hands." She set it on his desk, deliberately, so there could be no misunderstanding of transfer. "It's dying. You can see it yourself. Three months ago it burned steady no matter the weather. Now it gutters every nightfall and takes an hour to come back. Whatever's feeding it is running out, and what happens when it runs out—" She looked around the room, at the clerks, at the cook, at the boy still hovering with flour on his face. "Your guild was founded during the dissolution. Your founders bought survival with pieces of a whole country. Somebody here must know what held the ghost of it back from spilling into ours."

Silence. The good kind, the working kind. Marguerite's fingers rested on the ledger at his elbow—an old ledger, spine cracked, corners rubbed soft—and Ysolde watched something move behind his careful face. Recognition. Reluctance. And underneath both, fear, worn smooth by years of handling.

"There's a name in the deep ledgers," he said slowly. "In the founding entries. A role, really. Doorkeeper. Singular. Hereditary by succession of keys, not blood." His hand drifted, seemingly without his permission, to the chain around his neck, where something under his shirt caught the lantern-light and warmed it. "I inherited a key when my master died. I assumed it opened the vaults."

"Doesn't it?"

"No," said Marguerite, with the calm of a man confirming his own diagnosis. "I don't believe it ever did."

The cook spoke for the first time, arms folded. "So open it and look, love. Only way to know what's rattling around in a locked room."

"It's not a room," Marguerite said. His voice stayed level but Ysolde heard the effort of it, the cost of the levelness. "I think—I've suspected for a while, since the prayers started getting answered—that the key isn't *to* anything in this building." He met Ysolde's eyes. "You said the boundary thins where the fire fails. Wells filling with sea. Bands playing for nobody."

"And worse, lately."

"Then you understand why I've been sleeping badly." He exhaled. "The key doesn't lock a door. I think the key *is* the door. Or the hinge of it. And the fire is what kept it shut."

The room had gone very quiet. Even the fiddle, resumed timidly in the far hall, had stopped again, as though the instrument itself wanted to listen.

Ysolde looked at this man—invisible by choice, careful by craft, holding forty years of inherited silence around his neck like a stone he'd never questioned—and felt something twist that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite anger. Exhaustion, meeting obligation, across a desk.

"So the ledgers name a doorkeeper," she said. "Is that you?"

Marguerite didn't answer immediately. Instead he drew the old ledger toward him, opened it to a page near the front, and turned it so she could read. The entry was written in a hand she didn't know, brown ink gone amber with age:

*Item: one (1) door, last of the country of Vhalen, sealed with fire and keyed to the bearer of the sigil. To be kept shut until such time as the debt of survival is paid in full. Keeper to be chosen among those who owe nothing and are owed nothing, that no creditor may move them.*

"You owe nothing?" Ysolde asked.

"Nothing," Marguerite said quietly. "That's been the whole design of my life. No debts, no favors, no attachments. My master built me like a lock." A pause, dry as paper. "He was rather better at construction than at asking whether the lock wanted the job."

The cook snorted. The flour-faced boy said, "That's the saddest thing I've ever heard," with the devastating sincerity of the young, and Marguerite blinked as if struck by a very small, very earnest weapon.

"The fire is dying," Ysolde pressed, because mercy could wait and the lantern could not. She tapped the glass. The flame inside stirred weakly against her knuckle, warm as a fevered brow. "When it dies, the door doesn't stay shut. It doesn't even stay *locked*. Everything the old country was—all of it, the streets, the songs, the things that were promised and never delivered—it starts coming through. And it won't come gently, because nothing that's been waiting forty years comes gently. And there are gods awake now, real ones, old ones, answering prayers and taking payment, and at least one of them wants what's behind that door badly enough that I killed a man in Corvassy to stop him learning your address." She set both hands flat on the desk. "I have walked nine provinces carrying this. I am tired in my bones in a way sleep does not fix. What I need—a doorkeeper. The one your own books say exists. And the books say it's you."

Marguerite looked at the ledger for a long moment. Then at the lantern. Then—at this Ysolde noted, and filed away, and would think about later at inconvenient hours—at the boy with flour on his face, and the cook whose arms were crossed, and the clerks who had not left, who could have left and had not.

"The ledgers name a doorkeeper," he said at last. "Fine. But ledgers record. They don't decide." He closed the book with a soft, final sound, like a breath held and released. "The guild votes tomorrow. And tonight—" his eyes came up to hers, and for the first time the invisibility around him seemed less like armor and less like a hiding place and more like a man standing very still in tall grass, deciding whether to stand up, "—tonight, priestess, you'll tell me everything. Starting with who's hunting this fire, and ending with why you flinched just now when the boy said the word 'novice.'"

Ysolde opened her mouth to object to being read that easily.

She never got the chance. Because the lantern—sitting quiet on the desk between them, guttering, patient, forty years old and nearly done—chose that moment to flare high and hard and gold, and in its light every shadow in the room bent briefly toward the door, as if something on the other side of the world had heard where the flame had come to rest.

And far off, beyond the city walls, beyond the hills, something answered it. Not a sound. A bell, maybe. Or a voice practicing being a bell.

Marguerite's hand went to the key at his neck. Ysolde watched it come away warm.

"Well," said the cook, into the ringing silence. "That's new."
