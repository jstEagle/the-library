## 7. The God-King Wakes

Ysolde had sworn off capitals the way other people swore off drink: sincerely, repeatedly, and always with a relapse planned for the far side of some crisis she hadn't invented yet.

The capital of Vellosse — not the old name, never the old name; the old name was a debt and nobody wanted to be holding it when the collectors came — had been rebuilt twice since the dissolution, once by committee and once by money, and both times it had come out looking like a city apologizing for itself. Wide pale boulevards that went nowhere in particular. Statues of statesmen whose accomplishments had been generously summarized. Fountains shaped like nothing, dedicated to no one, spraying water into basins labeled, in small brass letters, *Fountain*.

Ysolde hated it the way you can only hate a place that used to mean something to you. Because underneath the apology, if you knew where to kneel and look, the old city was still there. The cobbles under the asphalt ran in the old pattern — the wheel-rut pattern, laid by carts hauling salt from the coast roads of a country that officially did not exist. The cathedral had been renamed the Hall of Civic Assembly, but its stones still hummed at evensong, faintly, like a struck bell pretending it hadn't been hit.

She'd grown up humming back at it. That was the disgrace, in the end. Not the smuggling, not the relic-running, not even the incident with the Patriarch's miter and the river barge, which everyone always assumed was the disgrace because it was the funniest one. The disgrace was simpler: Ysolde of the Ninth Flame had been raised to sing to dragons, and when the last dragon-fire guttered down to a coal small enough to carry in a lantern, she had picked it up and run instead of kneeling beside it to die on schedule like a proper priestess.

Forty years of running, and tonight the coal had stood up in its lantern and pointed itself at the palace like a hound scenting blood.

So here she was, back in the capital after eleven years of careful avoidance, walking through streets that should have been empty at this hour but weren't, because nobody in Vellosse could sleep. They stood in doorways in their nightclothes. They clustered at windows. They looked south, toward the old processional way, toward the Hall of Civic Assembly that everyone still called the cathedral when they thought no official was listening, and their faces wore expressions Ysolde recognized from the oldest pages of her training: not fear. Not yet. *Hope*, which was worse. Hope was the match. Fear came later, when you found out what you'd hoped for.

"They're praying," said a woman beside her, unnecessarily, because everyone could hear it. It came through the walls of the houses, out of the dark interiors, a low mass murmur like the sound of a harbor at night. Sixty years of prayers going nowhere, all being said at once, because three days ago somebody's prayer had been *answered*, and word traveled, and hope is contagious in populations the way fire is contagious in hay.

Ysolde tightened her grip on the lantern's handle and kept walking against the current of the hopeful, hood up, shoulders rounded, every inch of her arranged into the posture of a woman who is nobody's business. She'd learned that posture in a seminary corridor at fourteen, from an old priestess who'd survived three purges, and it had never once failed her.

It failed her at the mouth of the processional way, where a line of municipal guards stood behind barriers that had been set up in a hurry, and one of them — young, frightened, radiating the terrible authority of the suddenly important — put a hand on her shoulder and said, "No closer, mother. Consecrated ground ahead."

*Mother.* She was forty-nine. She decided, in the interest of continued invisibility, not to take offense.

"I'm just looking for my son," she said, which was a lie so smooth it practically curtsied. "He works nights near the square."

"Everyone's saying the king's coming back." The guard's voice cracked on *king*. He meant the god-king, of course — there hadn't been a mere king in living memory — but sixty years of enforced forgetting makes even divinity hard to say out loud. His eyes were wet. "Is it true, do you think? My grandmother used to sing about him. Before they took the songs."

And there it was, the whole catastrophe in one boy's face: the hunger, held down for two generations, coming up through the cracks like frost heave. Ysolde looked at him and understood, with a priestess's clarity that she would have paid good money to be without, that it didn't matter what actually happened at the cathedral tonight. The country had already decided. You cannot un-pray a prayer. You cannot tell six million people that the thing they felt moving in the dark was only wind.

"He'll want your faith more than your presence," she said gently, and slipped a coin into the boy's hand — old coin, wrong coin, a *sela* stamped with the nine-flame sigil that should have been melted decades ago, and the guard stared at it like a man handed his own death warrant written in his grandmother's handwriting — and walked on before he could decide whether to arrest her or follow her.

The lantern swung at her knee. Inside the glass, the dragon-coal burned high and white, and it was *leaning*, the way flame leans toward a draft, except there was no draft. There was only the pull of the thing assembling itself eight hundred meters away, in the nave where Ysolde had been ordained, drawing toward it everything loose in the world that remembered being part of a kingdom.

Including, apparently, her.

"Don't you dare," she told the coal quietly. "I know what you're doing. I carried you out of that mountain and I will carry you back out of this city, and we are going to find a doorkeeper and *close accounts*, do you understand me? We are not participating."

The coal flickered in a way that might have been agreement and might have been laughter. Dragons, even the last ember of one, had never been respectful of her authority. It was one of the things she'd loved about them.

* *

The barriers ended at the edge of the old consecrated district, past which no municipal authority had ever had the nerve to post guards, and beyond that point the crowd simply stopped, the way water stops at a threshold it has agreed upon. Ten thousand people stood pressed together in the great square before the Hall of Civic Assembly, and none of them crossed the line where the old paving began. Ysolde didn't need anyone to explain it. The paving was the old processional way, the Road of Homage, and every child of the dissolved country had been taught before they could talk: *you do not walk that road unless summoned.*

She found a place at the crowd's edge, on the steps of a shuttered tea house, where she could see over heads and, more importantly, could leave quickly. Smuggler's instincts. You always choose your seat for the exit.

What happened next, she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe properly.

It began with the bells. The cathedral had been stripped of its bells during the dissolution — carted off, recast, made into factory gongs and war memorials — and yet the bells rang. The sound came up out of the ground, out of the stones, out of forty years of silence where the ringing used to live, and it was not an echo. Echoes fade. This gathered. Somewhere in the fabric of the city, every note those bells had ever rung was still stored, the way a ledger stores debts, and something had begun calling the accounts in.

Then the doors of the Hall of Civic Assembly opened from the inside — though Ysolde knew for a fact, from the whispering crowd, that it had been sealed since the funeral, since the state interment of the Unknown Ceremonial Remains, the bone fragments the commission had officially declared "of no particular significance," because declaring them significant would have meant admitting whose they were.

The procession came out.

Not people. Ysolde's professional assessment, delivered to herself with a smuggler's flat calm, was *not people*, and she clung to that assessment like a rail in a storm, because the alternative was to believe her eyes. The procession was made of ceremony itself — of incense smoke that had soaked into the walls over centuries and now walked upright; of folded hands; of the memory of trumpets; of the particular weight of ten thousand knees hitting stone in unison across four hundred years. They wore vestments made of the idea of vestments. Where faces should have been there was the general impression of having been looked at, reverently, by millions.

And at the center, assembling as it walked — bone finding bone, fragment seeking fragment across the whole breadth of the square, drawn out of pockets and reliquaries and museum cases and the state urn marked NO PARTICULAR SIGNIFICANCE — the god-king put himself back on.

He was not tall. That was the detail that broke her composure, that made her breath go ragged in the dark. She had expected vastness, terror at scale, some colossus of smoke and sovereignty. Instead he assembled into a moderate man, gray-robed, gray-bearded, with kind creases around eyes that had watched four hundred years of ordinary people trying to be good, and he looked tired, and he looked *home*, and the entire square went down onto its knees around her like wheat under wind.

Ysolde stayed standing. It took everything. Her knees wanted to fold; her body had been trained before her mind was, and the training was still in there, singing. She locked her legs and gripped the lantern and stood, the only upright figure in a field of bowed heads, and therefore perfectly, catastrophically visible.

The god-king turned and looked directly at her.

"It was here," he said, and his voice was not loud, and it reached her anyway, and it reached everyone, the way a mother's voice reaches a sleeping child through three closed doors. "You were told I was gone. You were told the country was dissolved — a word for killing that spares the feelings of the killers. But you prayed anyway. Sixty years, you prayed to a dead god, and I heard none of it, and I am sorry. And three days past, one prayer was answered—" he smiled, gentle as evening, "—and the door of the world came unlatched, and I remembered how to walk."

The square made a sound Ysolde had never heard from a crowd before: ten thousand people exhaling sixty years of held grief at once. It moved through her chest like a physical blow. Beside her, the tea house's shutters rattled.

"The kingdom will be restored," the god-king said. "Not conquered. Not imposed. *Restored* — every street, every song, every stolen grave given back its name. This I swear by the bone I stand in. But—" and here his voice changed, and the kindness in it did not so much vanish as reveal itself as having always been a form of gravity, impersonal and total, "—a kingdom is a house, and a house must have its door. Sixty years ago my country was sold, piece by piece, through a certain guild, and the price of that sale was recorded, and the record survives, and the key to that record survives. There is a doorkeeper."

He paused. In the silence, Ysolde heard the dragon-coal in her lantern give a single clear crack, like ice shifting on a spring river.

"Doorkeeper," the god-king said, to the whole city, to the whole night, to one specific invisible man in a guildhall she'd spent three days riding away from. "Open the way, and be honored beyond honor. Refuse, and be unmade — not killed. Killing leaves a ghost, and ghosts are citizens of somewhere. *Unmade.* As my country was unmade. Choose, and choose soon, for I am assembling, and assembly is patience, but it is not infinite patience."

Then he raised his hands in the old benediction — nine fingers of blessing, one for each flame — and the crowd wept, and sang, and began pressing forward toward the Road of Homage, and nobody was watching the woman on the tea-house steps anymore.

Which was fortunate, because Ysolde was already running.

* *

She ran the way she'd learned to run out of border towns with contraband in her pack: not fast, fast gets you noticed, but *smooth*, hood up, cutting through yards and laundry-lines and the gaps between municipal enthusiasm. Behind her the square roared like weather. Above her, in the sky over the old district, the stars were wrong — rearranged themselves, as she glanced up, into constellations that hadn't hung over any officially existing sky for sixty years, the Dragon, the Crown, the Nine Flames, all back in their places, all shining slightly too hard.

Her legs knew the route out of the capital the way her knees knew the benediction: without consulting her. Through the tannery quarter, along the canal, over the Weeper's Bridge where the toll had been free for children and priests since forever — she flipped the toll-warden her last wrong-era coin anyway, out of forty-year habit, and the warden stared at it, and she was gone before his wonder could become curiosity. At the north gate the watch was absent, every guard drawn to the miracle, and the road opened ahead of her, gray and familiar and utterly insufficient.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing, hooves or no hooves, road or no road.

Three days ago the gods had started answering prayers. Tonight the god-king had reassembled. Which meant whatever had unlatched the door of the world had done it *then*, and everything since was consequence arriving in order of size. And she had spent those same three days riding to a guildhall full of impossible debts, following a coal that leaned toward it, to find a doorkeeper the ledgers named.

The ledgers named one. The god-king had announced one. Two men hunting the same quiet clerk, and only one of them was offering honor.

She reached the livery yard where she'd stabled her mare — overpaid a hostler who was too busy staring at the southern sky to haggle — and got a saddle on the animal with hands that were steadier than she had any right to expect. The mare's ears were flat. Horses had always known things first.

"I know," Ysolde muttered, cinching the girth. "I know. He's waking up. The whole rotten beautiful country's waking up, and every soul that ever loved it is going to come running home to be devoured politely, and somewhere in the middle of it all there's a bone-carver who just wants to balance his books and never be looked at."

She swung up into the saddle. The lantern hung at her knee, and inside it the last dragon-fire in the world burned tall and white and steady, pointing north, pointing home, pointing at a guildhall and a man who did not yet know that he had been named aloud in front of ten thousand witnesses by a god.

The mare took the north road at a flat gallop, and Ysolde bent low over her neck and gave her the rein, and thought, with a clarity that felt almost like peace: *Terror is just purpose that hasn't found its work yet.*

Hers had found its work. Three riders' worth of distance between her and the guildhall, and a god assembling behind her, and a doorkeeper ahead who had perhaps three days to learn that hiding was over — and that the only question left worth asking was not whether he would open the door.

It was who would reach him first, and what they would ask him to do with the key.
