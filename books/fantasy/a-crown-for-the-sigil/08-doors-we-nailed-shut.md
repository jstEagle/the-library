## 8. Doors We Nailed Shut

The Guild of Impossible Things had voted on many strange matters in its long and disreputable history. Marguerite knew this because he had transcribed the minutes. There was the vote of the Year of the Wet Bells, when the guild debated whether a miracle could be repossessed (it could, with difficulty and much shouting). There was the vote on whether to accept payment in the form of a river's promise to flow uphill on Sundays (accepted, two votes to one, with one abstention from a member who had since been eaten by that same river). There had even been a vote on whether the guild's cookhouse should serve eel on feast days, which had lasted eleven hours and ended in a schism that produced the rival Guild of Perfectly Reasonable Things, which folded within the year for want of anything to do.

But Marguerite had never transcribed minutes like these, and he suspected no one ever had.

He sat at the long table in the guildhall's upper chamber, the ledger-key resting in front of him like something that had been placed there while he wasn't looking, which, in fairness, it had. The sigil engraved on its face was warm. It had been warm for three days, ever since the ledgers began writing themselves, ever since the word *god-king* had appeared in ink that remembered things Marguerite had never written. The warmth was not comfortable. It was the warmth of a hand resting on your shoulder when you are quite sure the room is empty.

Around the table sat the guild's senior members, all nine of them, and the air between them was thick enough to carve.

On Marguerite's left sat Mistress Ondrell, the guild's appraiser of unpayable debts, a small sharp woman who had once valued a widow's grief at three goats and been right. On his right sat Brother Cassim, who had joined the guild forty years ago and had never once been seen to blink. At the far end, old Wenna Thrice-Signed presided, her three signatures—each given under a different name, each legally binding in a different century—resting in their silver case before her like relics.

And at the middle of the table, between the water jug and the candle, lay the letter.

It had arrived that morning by means no one could explain: simply *present* on the doorstep, sealed in wax the color of dried blood, bearing a crest that had not existed for sixty years. The crown-and-door of the dissolved country. Inside, in handwriting so beautiful it was frankly threatening, were the god-king's terms.

*The door will be opened. The doorkeeper will open it, or the doorkeeper will be unmade. The guild may stand as intermediary and be rewarded, or stand in the way and be remembered as an obstacle. Payment negotiable. Eternity guaranteed.*

"It's generous," said Mistress Ondrell, "is what it is."

Marguerite looked up sharply.

Ondrell met his gaze without flinching. She was not a cruel woman; she was an *accounting* woman, and there was a difference that Marguerite, in his calmer moments, could appreciate. This was not one of his calmer moments. "Don't look at me like that, Voss. You of all people know what's in the deep vaults. You've spent a week down there with your lamp and your gloves, and you've come up looking like a man who's seen the foundation of the house. So you know. The guild's whole estate is mortgaged against a country that doesn't exist. Every favor we've ever banked, every miracle we've ever held in trust—it all comes due when that door opens or doesn't. The god-king is offering to *assume the debt*."

"He's offering to assume the debt," Marguerite said, "the way a flood assumes a debt."

"A debt assumed is a debt settled."

"A debt assumed is a debt *owned*, Ondrell. By him. By whatever he is now. You'd be trading sixty years of careful bookkeeping for a creditor who resurrects himself out of funerals."

"He resurrected himself out of a *state funeral*, Cassim," Ondrell said, turning. "Do you understand what that means? The country isn't gone. The country was *waiting*. All those prayers we've watched go unanswered for sixty years—there's something on the other side of them now, and it has a crown, and it has terms, and it is the only party at this table with a coherent offer."

"The offer is *or be unmade*," said Wenna Thrice-Signed quietly. "I've read a great many contracts, children. That is not an offer. That is a threat wearing an offer's coat."

"Then what's your alternative?" Ondrell's voice cracked, and Marguerite heard, beneath the crack, something that surprised him: fear. Real fear, the kind she usually converted into appraisal figures before it could reach her face. "The second option on the floor. Brother Cassim's motion. Destroy the sigil, seal the door by force, let the debts go unpaid. Do you know what happens to everyone in this room if we do that? Every soul the guild ever traded for, every family we bought out of the dissolution with pieces of the old country—their bargains void. Their protections void. The dead come collecting, and they are *owed*, Wenna. You've seen the ledgers writing themselves. You know the sums."

The chamber went quiet. In the quiet, Marguerite could hear the dead.

He heard them always, through his hands, through the bone he carved, through the floor of any building old enough to have buried anyone under it. Usually they murmured. Usually it was complaints, small and domestic: a man grumbling that his headstone faced the wrong way; a woman asking, eternally, after her sister. Tonight they were not murmuring. Tonight they were *gathered*, the way crowds gather before something happens, and the pressure of their attention came up through the guildhall's stones and into the soles of his feet like a tide coming in.

They were waiting to see what he would do.

That was the worst part. He had spent fifty-three years being no one. No name spoken in the guildhall above a whisper. No debts, no favors, no attachments. His master had bought that for him—bought his silence, bought his invisibility, with years of Marguerite's own life that Marguerite had only just learned were missing—and Marguerite had hated him for it and then, in the long gray hour before dawn, understood it. Invisibility was a wall. Walls kept things out. Walls kept *you* in, but that was the price, and Marguerite had paid it gladly for half a century.

Now the wall had a door in it, and the door had his name on it, and everyone was looking.

"Marguerite," said Wenna Thrice-Signed. "You've been quiet."

"I'm always quiet."

"Yes, dear. That's rather the problem tonight."

He looked down at the ledger-key. The sigil was warm under his palm, warmer than it had been, and through the warmth he could feel the dead pressing close, patient, terrible, hopeful. Somewhere below them, in the cookhouse, Sefa was supposed to be asleep. He had checked twice. The second time he had found her sitting up in her cot, whispering prayers in a voice pitched deliberately, artlessly loud, and he had stood in the doorway a long moment before saying, "Quieter, little one," and she had said, "But what if they can't hear me when I'm quiet?" and he had not had an answer, because the truth was that they could hear her perfectly at any volume, and the louder truth was that he would have burned the guildhall down before he let one of those hungry old things reach her.

And Ysolde was due back. The dragon-priestess with her guttering lantern, her exhausted eyes, her certainty that the ledgers named a doorkeeper and the doorkeeper was him. She was due back, and when she arrived she would find the guild either sold or shattered, unless—

Unless someone said something.

"Brother Cassim's motion is on the floor," said Wenna. "Destruction of the sigil. Seconded by myself. And Mistress Ondrell's counter-motion: acceptance of the god-king's terms. We vote in a quarter hour. Marguerite. You are the keeper of the ledgers. The floor will hear you."

*The floor will hear you.* He almost laughed. The floor had been hearing him his whole life; the floor was the only thing that ever had.

He stood. His knees objected. He was aware of every eye in the room, nine senior members plus Wenna, ten points of attention, and it felt like standing in a fire, and he understood suddenly, with terrible clarity, that this was what his master had spent those missing years protecting him from. Not the god-king. This. Being *seen*.

"The ledgers," Marguerite said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect, "record a debt of one country, incurred by the guild's founders, owed to every soul who lived in it at the time of the dissolution. The principal has never been paid. The interest, compounding for sixty years, is—" he stopped, because he had done the figures, and the figures were not numbers anymore, they were faces, they were the dead in the walls, "—incalculable. That is the debt both motions pretend to address. Selling it doesn't settle it. Destroying the sigil doesn't settle it. There is exactly one thing in this room that can settle it, and it is not for sale, and it is not for breaking."

He picked up the ledger-key.

The warmth of it ran up his arm like a recognition.

"Because it's mine," Marguerite said. "The sigil. The door. The debt. I'm the last doorkeeper of a country that no longer exists, and I am telling this guild, formally, for the minutes, that the sigil is claimed. Not guild property. Not an asset. *Mine.* And a man's own door—" his voice did crack then, and he let it, "—a man's own door is not something the guild gets to vote on."

The silence afterward had a texture. Marguerite had spent his life listening to silences; this one was the silence of a room recalculating everything.

Ondrell recovered first, of course. "You can't simply *claim*—"

"He can," said Wenna Thrice-Signed, and there was something in her voice that might have been relief, or might have been grief, or might have been both wearing each other's coats. "Under the founding charter, article one, the thing I signed my first name to. *The guild trades in what is given, never in what is claimed.* If the sigil is claimed by its heir, it leaves our jurisdiction entirely." She looked at Marguerite, and her old eyes were wet. "You've been hiding in this guild for fifty years, Voss. I always wondered what you were hiding *from*."

"Everyone," Marguerite said honestly. "It worked very well until recently."

A few of the senior members laughed—shaky, relieved laughter, the kind that comes after a storm passes or before one arrives, Marguerite wasn't sure which. Ondrell did not laugh. She looked at him a long moment, and then she inclined her head, one appraiser to another, acknowledging a valuation she couldn't argue with.

"The counter-motion is withdrawn," she said. "God help us all, Voss. You'd better know what you're doing."

"I haven't the faintest idea," Marguerite said. "But I know whose door it is."

He was still holding the key when the shouting started downstairs—cookhouse voices, Sefa's voice, high and bright and *afraid*—and Marguerite was moving before the thought finished forming, the sigil hot against his chest, the dead rising through the floor like a tide finally come all the way in.

Through the window, down in the yard, he saw the gate.

It was standing open. And on the far side of it, in the dark, something wearing the shape of ceremony was waiting to be let in.
