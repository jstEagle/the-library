## 9. Sefa's Bargain

Sefa had three rules for praying, which she had made herself and told nobody, because rules you tell people become rules people can argue with.

One: pray loud. The quiet prayers were like knocking on a door and then running away. Two: pray specific. "Bless everyone" was lazy. "Bless the cookhouse cat, who has an abscess" was a prayer a god could actually do something with. Three: never, ever pray after sundown, because that was when the floorboards answered back.

She had kept rules one and two beautifully. Rule three had lasted exactly as long as it takes a ten-year-old to get lonely after dark, which is to say: not at all.

The trouble started on a Tuesday, in the little alcove off the guildhall's east corridor where the brooms lived and where Sefa slept, because the cookhouse was warm but Mrs. Ivet snored like a landslide having opinions, and the alcove was quiet, and quiet was a thing Sefa was still learning to like.

She was lying on her pallet doing her evening inventory of grievances — the cat had absconded with her dinner roll, the second-floor apprentice had called her "the guild's stray" in a voice he thought she hadn't heard, and nobody, in the whole enormous creaking building full of people who traded in miracles, had ever once asked what *she* wanted to be when she grew up — when the floor under her began to hum.

Not the ordinary humming. The guildhall hummed all the time; Marguerite said it was pipes, and Master Oskar's ghost said it was the building dreaming, and Sefa privately suspected it was both and neither. This was different. This hum had a *shape*. It hummed the way a person hums when they want you to ask what the song is.

Sefa sat up.

"Hello?" she said, in her ordinary voice, and then, because rule one, "HELLO."

The floor hummed a little louder, pleased.

Now. A sensible child would have fetched an adult. Sefa considered this. The problem was which adult. Mrs. Ivet would feed the floor a bun and tell it to mind its manners. Master Oskar's ghost would sigh in the way he did, which was somehow louder than shouting. And Marguerite — Marguerite would go very still and very pale and start writing things in his ledger, and then everything would become *serious*, and Sefa had a strong personal policy about seriousness, which was that it was contagious and best avoided.

So she did what she always did with things that hummed at her.

"I'm Sefa," she said. "Who are you?"

The hum thought about it. She could feel it thinking, the way you feel weather thinking about rain.

*Old,* said the floor, in a voice like a bell rung under a blanket. *I am old. I am the one they prayed to before the praying stopped. I am the one under the floorboards of your little country, child, and I have been hungry for sixty years.*

"That's very sad," said Sefa, who meant it, "but I only have half a dinner roll left, and the cat already tried to steal it."

The floor laughed. It was a terrible sound, like a landslide being polite, but Sefa had the feeling the laughter was real, and real laughter, even from terrible things, was worth more than polite silence from good ones.

*I do not want your roll,* said the god under the floor. *I want a door.*

"Well, there's the front door," said Sefa helpfully. "It sticks. You have to lift the handle."

*I want a particular door. A door with a keeper. A door with a sigil on its key.* The hum deepened, went sweet, went the exact temperature of a fresh bun. *And I am prepared to pay for it, little Sefa. I am prepared to pay in the only coin you have ever truly wanted.*

Sefa's stomach did something complicated.

"And what's that?" she asked, in a voice she tried to keep casual and failed completely.

*A family,* said the god. *Not the cookhouse kind, where you are tolerated between shifts and called a stray. A real one. A mother who asks what you want to be when you grow up. A father who teaches you to whittle. Grandparents. Cousins. A dog. I am old, child; I remember how families are made. I made the first ones. I can make you one by Sunday.*

The alcove was very quiet. Somewhere above, the guildhall settled and creaked, and the pipes hummed their ordinary hum, and Sefa sat with her knees pulled up to her chin and let the wanting wash over her, because she was not a fool, and pretending she didn't want it would have been lying to the only being in the world who had ever asked.

She wanted it so much her teeth hurt.

"And what do you want from me," she said slowly, "for a whole family?"

*Only a small thing. A direction. You will point, and I will walk. Show me the doorkeeper, and your family will be waiting for you by Sunday morning, and I will never bother you again.*

Sefa looked at the floorboards. They looked back, in the way that floorboards do when there is something behind them doing the looking.

Now, here is the thing about Sefa that the guild never quite understood, because they kept thinking of her as a stray, and strays, people assumed, would follow anyone who fed them. But Sefa had been in the orphanage before the guildhall. She had been passed from the orphanage to a farm family and back again. She knew exactly what a family looked like when it was *made for you* instead of *grown around you*. The farm family had been very kind, in the way that people are kind to a chore they regret taking on.

Also — and this was the important thing — she had been listening to the god's offer very carefully, the way she listened to everything, and she had noticed something.

"You said 'show me the doorkeeper,'" she said. "But you didn't say what happens to the doorkeeper."

The hum paused. It was a very small pause, but Sefa had grown up listening to adults lie about whether there was more pudding, and she knew a pause like that the way a fisherman knows a bite.

*The doorkeeper is an adult matter,* said the god smoothly. *Adults and doors. You needn't worry your head.*

"Marguerite worries his head about me," said Sefa. "He thinks I don't notice, but he does this thing where he stands in the cookhouse doorway every evening and counts us, like a shepherd, except he'd be embarrassed if you called him that, so he pretends he's checking the stove."

*The doorkeeper,* said the god, with the patience of something that had waited sixty years and could wait sixty more, *is the last lock on a country that was sold. When the door opens, everything that was taken is given back. Everything. Including,* and here the hum went warm as hearth-smoke, *a certain ledger of debts, in which I believe your name appears as an entry marked 'orphan, loud, unclaimed.' Open the door, Sefa, and no one will ever call you a stray again, because there will be no such thing as strays.*

It was, Sefa had to admit, a very good speech. It had her name in it and everything.

But she had also noticed something else, something she'd been chewing on for days like a gristly bit of bread: the prayers. Lately, when she prayed loud and specific, the things that answered were *hungry*. The cat's abscess had been healed, yes, but the healing had left the cat afraid of shadows. The rain she'd asked for had come, and come, and come, until Mrs. Ivet had said "enough, Sefa, gods alive," and Sefa had thought: *that's just it, isn't it. Gods alive. All at once. Like people who've been starving at a feast.*

A hungry thing offering you a family was like a hungry cat offering to babysit a canary.

"I'll tell you what," said Sefa. "I'll think about it."

*Think fast,* said the floor.

"No," said Sefa, "I'll think at my own speed, because I'm the one holding the thing you want, and you're the one under the floorboards, which means between the two of us, I'm the one with the better view."

The hum went very still. Then it did something she hadn't felt it do before: it *laughed again*, but differently this time — startled, delighted, the way Master Oskar's ghost laughed when she out-argued him, which was often, because ghosts got set in their ways.

*Ohhh,* said the god. *Oh, you are a clever little morsel. Very well. You are a trader's child at heart, whether you know it or not. Name your terms.*

Sefa hadn't expected to get this far, and her heart was going like a rabbit's, but she was a girl with three rules and a lifetime of practice at wanting things she couldn't have, and wanting things you can't have teaches you patience whether you like it or not.

"Three days," she said. "I'll think for three days. That's fair. Marguerite says nothing in the guild gets decided same-day, because same-day decisions are how you end up trading a king's shadow for a bag of turnips."

*And after three days?*

"After three days, I'll answer you. But I want to ask you a question first, so I know what I'm dealing with. That's also a guild rule. Marguerite says you always ask what the other party means by the important words, because that's where they hide the knives."

*Ask, then,* said the god, amused.

Sefa took a breath. She had been turning the question over in her head all day, the way you turn a smooth stone in your pocket.

"What makes a family real?"

The silence that followed was long enough that she counted the pipes humming. When the god finally spoke, its voice had lost some of its hearth-warmth.

*Blood,* it said. *Blood and roof and name.*

"Wrong," said Sefa, with the tremendous confidence of a ten-year-old who has recently discovered she is right about something. "Because the farm family had all three, and they weren't real. Mrs. Ivet isn't blood and she is. Master Oskar's dead and he still tucks the blanket back up when it slips off me at night, and you can't get more family than tucking in a blanket, that's just physics." She leaned down close to the floorboards. "So here's my question, and you have three days to answer it, same as me: *can you make a family, or can you only make a family-shaped thing?* Because if you can't answer that, then what you're selling me is a lie with a dog in it, and I don't buy lies, not even ones with dogs."

The hum did not answer.

The hum *withdrew*, sinking down through the floor, through the foundations, down into whatever deep old dark it had been sleeping in for sixty years, and Sefa felt it go the way you feel a wave pull back from the shore — the sense of something enormous gathering itself.

But before it went, it left something behind.

Sefa saw it when she stood up to check the window: her shadow on the wall, thrown by the moon, had a mark on it. A small, neat, official-looking mark, like a wax seal, right over where the shadow's heart would be. She waved her hand. The shadow waved back, seal and all.

"Oh," said Sefa, to the empty room, in a very small voice. "That's collateral, isn't it. That's what Marguerite calls it. That's *collateral*."

* * *

She held out for most of a day, which for Sefa was a personal record.

The problem was that the mark didn't hurt. That was the horrible, clever part of it. It just *sat* there, in her shadow, warm as a hand, and whenever she felt lonely — and she felt lonely the way other people felt hungry, constantly and without warning — the mark warmed up, and the god's voice came, not from the floor anymore but from everywhere, gentle as a grandmother.

*The dog will be brown,* it murmured, while she peeled turnips in the cookhouse. *I have not decided on the name. You will decide the name. That is what mothers let you do.*

*Your grandmother will have flour on her hands always, even after baking, because that is what grandmothers are.*

*You will never again eat last.*

Sefa peeled turnips and did not answer, because answering felt like losing, and because she was saving her voice for the thing she'd figured out in the night, the thing that had made her sit bolt upright at dawn.

On the second evening, she found Marguerite in the counting room, doing what he always did: hunched over his ledgers like a heron over still water, carving a little bone button for some dead person's coat, his lips moving as the dead murmured their complaints through his hands.

"Marguerite," said Sefa. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to not go still and pale."

He went still. Then, with visible effort, he un-stilled himself, which was one of the things Sefa liked best about him — that he would try, when she asked, even though stillness was his whole personality.

"I'm listening," he said.

So she told him. All of it. The hum, the offer, the family with the brown dog, the riddle, the three days, and the mark on her shadow. She told it in the order it happened, because she'd learned that Marguerite liked order the way other people liked soup. When she got to the part about the seal, she turned sideways to the lamp so he could see it, and watched his face do the thing where it aged ten years in a second and then apologized for it.

"Three days," he said quietly. "You negotiated three days."

"And a riddle," said Sefa. "Two riddles. One each."

Marguerite put down his carving knife. He looked at her for a long moment, and Sefa braced herself for the lecture, the *you should have come to me immediately*, the grown-up music she knew all the verses of.

What he said was: "You asked it what it meant by 'family.' Nobody asks that. Grown men in this guild sign contracts without asking that."

"Well," said Sefa, examining her fingernails, "I'm not a grown man."

"No," said Marguerite, and something in his voice was strange and warm and a bit broken. "You are not. Sefa — the mark on your shadow. Has it asked you for anything since?"

"It doesn't ask. It just... advertises. Grandmother-flour. Never eating last." She scuffed her shoe. "It's very good at it. That's the worst part. It knows exactly what to say because it's been listening to me want things my whole life, probably. Through the floor."

Marguerite nodded slowly, and then he did something else she hadn't braced for. He reached across the counting table — carefully, the way he did everything, as if the world were a ledger he was afraid of smudging — and put his hand briefly on top of her head.

"Three days," he said. "Then we answer it together. And Sefa — whatever it offers, however real the dog sounds — you remember that you already have a family that is badly organized, frequently annoyed, and would burn this guildhall down around its own ears before it let anything collect on your shadow. It's just that nobody's said it out loud yet."

"Mrs. Ivet would burn it down for the insurance," said Sefa.

"Mrs. Ivet would burn it down *on principle*," said Marguerite, "and then bill the god for the turnips."

Sefa laughed, and the mark on her shadow flickered — just for a moment — like a candle in a draft, and somewhere far below them, something old and hungry heard that laughter, and heard the hand on the child's head, and understood with dawning, furious clarity that it had made a serious tactical error.

Because the riddle hadn't been a stalling trick at all. The riddle had been a *test*, and the child had already seen the answer, and the answer was standing in the counting room with flour-dust on his sleeve and a heron's patience and a ledger full of other people's debts, counting her, every evening, in the cookhouse doorway, like a shepherd who'd be embarrassed if you called him that.

* * *

That night, Sefa lay in her alcove and listened to the floor hum its offer one more time — *brown dog, flour hands, never eating last* — and patted her shadow the way you'd pat a horse you'd decided to keep.

"Two more days," she whispered to it. "And then you're going to learn what family means, and it's going to be so embarrassing for you."

Far below, the old god turned the question over in the dark, and could not answer it, and hated that.

And in the counting room overhead, Marguerite Voss sat down at his desk, opened a fresh page, and began — for the first time in his careful, invisible life — to write down a plan that involved leaving the guildhall, because three days, he suspected, was exactly how long they had before the floor came up to meet them.
