## 10. The Road Out of a Country That Isn't There

The city gates closed at dusk, which was why we left through the wall.

Not over it—through it. There was a door in the old customs house on Vellum Street that had been bricked up in the year of the Dissolution, and because the Guild of Impossible Things had sold that particular brickwork to a merchant who wanted his warehouse to be unburglarable, the door still technically existed in our ledgers. And what exists in our ledgers can be walked through, provided you carry the right key and don't think too loudly about walls.

"Is this legal?" Sefa asked, as I pressed the ledger-key into the mortar and the bricks went soft as bread.

"No," I said.

"Oh good." She stepped through first, because she was ten and had no sense of self-preservation, and also because she had been asking to go on an adventure since the day she arrived at the guildhall, and I had been saying no for exactly as long. Circumstances had overruled me. I resented circumstances considerably.

Ysolde came next with the lantern held out from her body like something that might bite her, which, in fairness, it might. The dragon-fire inside had burned low enough now that its light came more amber than gold, the color of lamplight behind a curtain in a house where someone is dying gently. She'd wrapped the lantern's cage in three layers of wool against the cold, and still the flame shivered.

I came last, and paused with one hand on the brick.

"You're doing the thing," said the ghost of Master Aldous Corvane, drifting at my shoulder like a draft with opinions. "The thing where you say goodbye to a building."

"I am not."

"You're saying goodbye to a building. You've done it to every room in the guildhall since dawn. You did it to the cookhouse twice."

"The cookhouse has been good to me."

"The cookhouse is a *kitchen*, Marguerite. Go."

So I went, and the bricks hardened behind us, and that was the last ordinary thing that happened for three days.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about roads that exist only on pre-dissolution maps: they are not hidden. They are simply unattended, like a garden left to itself for sixty years, and things left unattended grow strange.

We found the first one by accident—or rather, Sefa found it by falling down it. We had cut north through the tannery district's abandoned edge, following Ysolde's memory of an old survey map, when the ground simply opened between two warehouses into a lane cobbled in pale stone, running away between hedges that were neither wild nor tended but *remembered*—pruned into shapes that hadn't existed anywhere but here for six decades: leaping stags, spiraling roses, the double-curve of a crown.

Sefa picked herself up, spat out a hedge leaf, and said, "It smells like bread."

It did. That was the second thing nobody tells you. The old country smelled faintly of baking everywhere, all the time, as though some vast communal oven had been left on when everyone left, and no one had ever thought to turn it off.

"That's the harvest," Ysolde said quietly. "Lammas-tide. It would have been Lammas-tide." She said it the way you'd identify a body.

We walked. The lane took us past orchards where the trees bore fruit made mostly of suggestion—you could see apples, could nearly smell them, but when Sefa reached for one her hand passed through with a sound like a page turning. Past a well with a bucket that drew up nothing but the memory of cold water, which Sefa drank anyway and declared delicious. Past a milestone carved with distances to towns whose names I knew only from ledgers: debts recorded, never visited. Carrowmere, nine miles. The Ford of Small Promises, four.

"The Ford of Small Promises," Sefa repeated, delighted. "What kind of name is that?"

"A very honest one," I said. "Most fords promise nothing at all."

Master Corvane drifted along beside me, and I noticed—he would have denied it—that he kept looking around with an expression I had only ever seen him wear once before, the day he bought my silence and told me it was kindness. It was the look of a man seeing a place he had helped bury.

"You've been here before," I said. "On these roads."

"I audited them," he said. "In the last year. When the guild was selling the borders, someone had to walk them and count what was left." He gestured at a hedgerow stag mid-leap. "There was more, then. Whole villages' worth of leftover. We sold most of it in lots."

"To whom?"

"To anyone. To everyone. A shipping magnate bought the anthem. A widow bought her street. A man in the capital bought—" He stopped.

"The king's shadow," I said. "Chapter three of my nightmares. I read the ledger."

"Then you know the rest of the story already, and I needn't spoil the walk with it."

"I would like," I said, "for once in my life, to be spoiled."

But he only smiled his terrible accountant's smile and drifted ahead, and the road carried us on toward Carrowmere, or toward whatever Carrowmere had become.

* * *

We heard the festival before we saw it: fiddles, and feet, and laughter with no breath in it.

Carrowmere's square had kept its shape the way a footprint keeps its shape in ash. Around it stood houses that were mostly façades now, painted memories of houses, and the inn was a sign swinging over emptiness, and the church was a tower with no nave. But the square itself—the square was *occupied*. Figures danced in long lines through patterns I half-recognized from folk tunes the guild's cook used to hum: men with coats the color of turned earth, women with aprons full of light instead of grain, children—children like Sefa, except their faces were smooth as eggs, features worn away by sixty years of being remembered without being looked at.

They were dancing a harvest dance around a bonfire that gave off heat and no smoke, burning nothing, warming no one, and yet when Sefa ran toward them—I grabbed her collar; she wriggled; I held on—they turned as one and bowed to her, deep and courteous, the way you bow to a guest.

"They can see us," Ysolde breathed.

"We're on their road," I said. "Their whole country is a road now. I expect we're the closest thing to neighbors they have."

One of the dancers approached—a broad man with a fiddle under his chin and no face above the nose, though somehow you felt he was smiling. He played a phrase of music at Sefa, questioning. She, being Sefa, answered by clapping the rhythm wrong and then insisting her way was better. The faceless man considered this. Then he adjusted the tune to match her clapping, and the whole line of dancers turned and followed, and for perhaps ten minutes there was music in Carrowmere square that had never been played there before, invented by a ten-year-old with no sense of rhythm, performed by the dead remnant of a harvest festival, and it was—there is no other word for it—it was *lovely*.

That was the trap of the old country, I understood then. Not horror. Horror you can run from. It was this: the loveliness. The sense of a place holding its breath for sixty years, waiting for someone to come home, and the awful flattery of being mistaken for the person it was waiting for.

The dancers began to draw closer. Their hands, when they reached toward Sefa, were warm—I could see the warmth coming off them like off a stove—and their egg-smooth faces tilted toward her with such naked hope that my chest hurt.

"She can't stay," I said, to the man with the fiddle, to all of them. "She's not yours. None of us are."

The fiddle stopped. The hope didn't curdle into anger, which would have been easier. It just went patient, the way debt goes patient, and the whole square bowed again—deeper this time—and the fire dimmed to embers, and the dancers folded themselves back into the cobbles like figures sinking through water, until it was only a market square at night, empty, smelling of bread.

Sefa cried a little. I let her, and pretended not to notice, which is the Halloran family method of comfort, if the Hallorans had ever been a family, which they had not, which is possibly the whole problem.

"They were so happy," she said finally.

"They were lonely," I corrected. "Happy is different. Happy doesn't grab."

Ysolde lifted the lantern. In its guttering light, the cobbles where the dancers had been showed scorch-marks in the shape of footprints, dozens of them, all pointing inward, all facing the spot where Sefa had stood.

"We should keep moving," she said.

* * *

We walked through the night, because the lantern slept by day—dragon-fire, Ysolde explained, kept temple hours—and because none of us wanted to test whether the old country's nights were safer than its days. The bells solved that question for us around midnight.

They rang from everywhere at once. Steeples that had no bells rang anyway; the sound came up through the road itself, through the soles of our boots, a carillon playing changes I didn't recognize and Ysolde clearly did, because she stopped dead in the middle of the lane and stood with her hand over her mouth like a woman hearing her own name called across a crowded station.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Vespers," she said. "The night office. They're ringing for—"

"For whom?"

"For anyone." Her voice cracked on it. "Forty years I served the order, and they taught me the bells ring for God. But listen to it. Listen. It's not ringing *up*." She pointed at the sound, which is impossible, but the old country permitted it. "It's ringing *out*. Looking. Like a lighthouse."

Master Corvane floated up beside her, and for once he didn't correct her theology. "The priests who stayed," he said softly. "This is what they were ringing into. All those years. An empty country, listening."

"And nobody came," Ysolde said.

"Somebody's coming now," said Sefa, and pointed ahead, and we all looked, and there on the crest of the hill stood a bell tower with a rope hanging out of its window, swaying, though no wind moved the hedges, and the rope was pulling itself, hand over invisible hand, ringing and ringing and ringing, and I swear on my ledgers that the sound of it was glad.

We did not go up the hill. Some invitations you decline precisely because they are sincere.

* * *

By the third morning the lantern was the worst thing to look at in the world, so naturally we all kept looking at it.

The flame had shrunk to the size of a thumbnail. It no longer threw light so much as it emitted a sort of golden opinion, illuminating Ysolde's hands and a circle of road perhaps two feet wide and nothing else. She carried it cupped against her chest now, walking bent over it like a woman sheltering a candle in wind, and she had stopped speaking except to give directions, and her directions had gotten shorter each day, and I understood that she was rationing words the way the lantern rationed fire, and that both of them were running out.

"It needs feeding," she said at last, when the flame flickered in a way that flames shouldn't—inward, like a gasp.

"With what? There's nothing here to burn."

"Not wood." She wouldn't meet my eyes. "I'll tell you at the waystation. There's a ruin ahead, an old fire-post. It's meant to be fed there, properly, at a hearth. Not—not on the road."

"How far?"

"Half a day. Less, if the roads are kind."

"Are the roads ever kind?"

"In this country," said Master Corvane, "the roads are the politest thing left."

And they were, in their way. Where the lane forked, the signposts had rotated overnight to point the way we needed, which should have been sinister and was instead oddly touching, like a host turning the chairs toward the fire before guests arrive. Once, crossing a stone bridge over a river that was only the idea of a river—the water was audible, and the ripples were visible, but the wet was missing—we found a picnic laid out on the parapet: bread, cheese, a jug, two cups. Set for two. Waiting.

Nobody touched it. Sefa reached for the bread and I caught her wrist, and she glared at me with the full fury of a child denied a haunted sandwich, and I said, "When we're out of this country, I will buy you an entire bakery," and she said, "You don't have any money," and I said, "I have a guild," and she considered this and allowed it.

But as we crossed, I looked back, and the picnic had been cleared away. Tidied. Put back in its cupboard somewhere under the world, to wait for the next travelers, or for no one, forever.

That was the moment the homesickness hit me, and it hit sideways, which is the only way homesickness for a place you've never lived can hit. I grieved for that picnic. For the bread going stale in the dark of the world's cupboard. For the two cups set out for two people who had never come, in sixty years, and might have been anyone, and were therefore everyone. I grieved for a country I had spent my whole life helping to pretend had never existed, and the grief was real, and it was also, I suspected, exactly what the country wanted. That was how it survived. Not by fighting. By making you miss it.

"Marguerite." Master Corvane, at my elbow. "Your face is doing something."

"My face is fine."

"Your face is writing memoirs. Walk."

* * *

We came down to the waystation at dusk, and it was worse than ruins, because ruins have given up, and the waystation hadn't. It stood at a crossroads with its roof half-collapsed and its door standing open—still open, after sixty years, the hinges oiled by nothing, held wide in welcome—and inside, a hearth swept clean, logs stacked in the woodbox, a kettle hung on its crane, everything ready, everything waiting, everything patient as a debt.

Ysolde knelt at the hearth and unwrapped the lantern's wool with hands that shook.

"Now," she said. "Before it goes out. You need to understand what feeds it, because it has to be freely given, and it has to be given *knowing*, or it won't take."

"Fire needs fuel," I said. "Tell me something I don't know."

"It's dragon-fire," she said. "It doesn't burn wood. It burns belonging. A memory of home, freely surrendered—one true memory, given whole, and it eats the having of it along with the memory. You won't lose the fact of your life. You'll lose the *taste* of it. One taste. Forever." She looked up at us, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious with exhaustion. "I've fed it three times getting here. I no longer remember what my mother's kitchen smelled like. I know I loved her. I cannot remember why it mattered."

The silence in that ruined room was very large.

Sefa spoke first, because she always did. "What counts as home?"

"Anywhere you belonged," Ysolde said. "Truly. Even for a moment."

And I watched Sefa's face do arithmetic—ten years old, orphaned, belonging nowhere yet—and watched her arrive at the answer that there was nothing in her life yet worth the price, and watched her be frightened of that, and I decided, in that moment, that I would go first.

Because I had one. Of course I had one. I had spent forty years pretending I had none, keeping my accounts clean, owing nothing, owning nothing, invisible—but there was one memory I had never entered in any ledger, because it wasn't a transaction, it was just a fact, warm and useless and mine:

Aldous Corvane, thirty years ago, sitting up all night with a feverish apprentice he had no obligation to love, reading him the guild's founding charter in a voice made gentle on purpose, and at dawn pressing his own coat around the boy's shoulders and saying, "You're not a tool, Marguerite. Whatever I've taught you about ledgers—don't ever put yourself in one."

That was my home. That was the whole of it. One night, one coat, one sentence.

I opened my mouth to offer it—

And Master Corvane's ghost stepped between me and the hearth, solid-seeming for the first time since he'd died, arms spread, and said:

"*No.* Not that one. Marguerite—listen to me—not that one. Keep it. Whatever happens, whatever it costs, that one you keep."

"You don't get a vote," I said. "You're dead."

"I get every vote. I paid for that memory with thirty years of your life, you maddening boy, and I will not watch you burn the receipt."

And outside, far off across the fields of a country that wasn't there, a bell began to ring—not vespers, not a welcome, but fast and urgent, alarm-pattern, the toll the holdout priests had used to warn of riders—and down the crossroads, small in the last light, came a procession moving at speed, and at its head rode a figure in a crown of ceremony and bone.

Ysolde snuffed the lantern's last coal in her fist to hide the light, and whispered: "He found us."
