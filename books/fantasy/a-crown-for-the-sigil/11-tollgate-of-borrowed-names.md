## 11. Tollgate of Borrowed Names

Ysolde had crossed a great many borders in her life, most of them illegally, several of them on fire, and one—she preferred not to think about it—at the bottom of a wine cart while wearing a false mustache that kept slipping into her mouth. So she considered herself something of an expert when she said, with the weary authority of forty years' smuggling and eleven years' disgrace:

"That's not a tollgate."

"It has a gate," Sefa said helpfully. "And it's tolling. Listen."

She was right about the second part. Somewhere in the mist ahead, a bell was ringing—not the bright urgent clang of alarm or the fat bronze voice of a temple bell, but something thinner and more patient, like a spoon tapped against the rim of an empty cup.

"And I'm sure it takes tolls," Ysolde admitted. "But a *tollgate*, properly speaking, is a thing built by people who want your money. That—" she squinted through the fog at what waited on the road out of a country that wasn't there "—that wants something else."

The road had been narrowing for hours. It had begun, back in the living world, as a proper imperial highway, wide enough for six carts abreast, lined with milestones carved by masons who had believed in permanence. But each mile since they'd slipped across the border of unmapped memory had shaved it down: six carts to four, four to two, two to a footpath, until now it was barely wider than the lantern Ysolde carried, and the grass on either side grew in the stiff, gray-green way of grass that remembered being wheat.

The tollgate stood where the road gave up entirely.

It was not wood. It was not stone. It was, on close inspection—and Ysolde made herself look closely, because things you refused to look at always got worse—made of paper. Thousands upon thousands of sheets, pressed and layered and lacquered into posts and lintel and gate itself, all of them covered in writing. Forms. Ledgers. Permits. Declarations of goods. Petitions for travel. The whole structure had the faintly damp smell of a customs house, and when the wind moved through it, the papers rustled like a crowd murmuring *next*.

A window opened in the middle of the gate. There had been no window before. Behind it sat a figure that was mostly ink—a clerk-shaped absence, sleeves and spectacles suggested rather than drawn, holding a pen that wrote without touching anything.

"Good morning," said the clerk, in a voice like a stamp coming down. "Please have your documentation ready."

"We don't have documentation," Ysolde said. "We have a dying dragon-fire, a ghost, a bone-carver, and a child."

"Then you'll be paying in names," said the clerk, unsurprised. "One per traveler. Names are legal tender at this crossing." It produced, from nowhere, a ledger of its own—thick, swollen, its spine straining with entries. "Standard rates apply."

"What are standard rates?" Marguerite asked. He'd come up quietly on her left, the way he did everything, as though visibility were a debt he could still avoid if he were careful enough. The lantern hung from his hand; he'd insisted on carrying it after the third mile, when her arms started shaking, and hadn't made a single remark about it, which she appreciated more than she could say and would never say.

"Full names," said the clerk, "at full value. Titles and surnames accepted at partial value. Nicknames, pet names, and names given in love are assessed individually." Its pen hovered. "Who is first?"

"I am," Ysolde said, before anyone else could volunteer. She set the lantern down—no. She didn't. She'd promised herself she would never set it down again, not even to sleep, and she hadn't, and her shoulder screamed about it constantly, and that was fine. She shifted it higher instead and stepped up to the window.

Behind her, the master's ghost drifted forward with the air of a man who intended to supervise. He had been supervising all morning. Being dead had not improved his temperament; it had merely removed his ability to be argued with, which Ysolde suspected he'd always resented.

"Name," said the clerk.

"Ysolde," she began, "last flame-keeper of the—"

"Not that one," said the clerk quickly.

She stopped. Something cold walked down her spine. "What do you mean, *not that one*?"

The clerk adjusted its spectacles, which were also mostly suggestion. "That name is already in our records. It was surrendered sixty-one years ago, at the dissolution, along with the temples, the vestments, and the office of Dragon-Priestess entire. It is government property. We cannot accept what we already hold." A pause, and then, with what might have been sympathy, in the way a form can be sympathetic: "You've been carrying a name that isn't yours for a long time, priestess."

Ysolde stood very still.

She had thought, in the long gray miles behind them, that the worst thing the old country could do to her was remember her. She had braced for accusation—for the order's censure, for the god-king's claim, for the dead rising up to point their fingerless hands at the woman who ran. She had not prepared for *pity*. Not pity exactly. Something more bureaucratic and therefore worse: the flat administrative observation that her disgrace had been processed decades ago, stamped, filed, and closed, and that she had been grieving a verdict no one had ever rendered because the court itself had been dissolved.

"You mean I'm not disgraced," she said. Her voice came out strange. "Officially. I'm not officially anything. The office doesn't exist, so I can't have failed at it."

"The office exists," said the clerk. "It is simply in arrears. As are you." The pen scratched. "However. Your title remains valid collateral. If you surrender it freely, we will accept it at partial value toward your passage."

Her title. The last thing the order had given her, the thing she'd clung to through every smuggler's den and stolen hold and cold night on the water—the rank she wore like a scar, proof that she had once mattered enough to fall. And the gate wanted it. Of course it did. Gates always took the thing you carried in your teeth.

"If I give it up," she said slowly, "I'm just Ysolde. No order. No office. No flame-keeper of anything."

"Yes," said the clerk, and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible neutrality of paperwork. "You would be a person."

Sefa made a small sound behind her, half gasp, half cheer, and hastily swallowed it.

Ysolde looked back at them. At the ghost, who was watching her with an expression she'd never seen on him in life—something almost gentle. At Marguerite, whose face did its usual excellent job of revealing nothing and whose eyes revealed everything anyway. At the child, who nodded at her with the fierce encouragement of someone who had recently learned that titles were heavy and was delighted someone else might get to put theirs down.

The bell tolled again. Under her hands, the lantern guttered, and the gray country leaned in a little closer, listening.

"All right," Ysolde said. "Take it."

"Repeat it fully, please, for the record."

"I surrender the title of last flame-keeper of the Order of the Undying Flame"—her voice caught on the word *undying*, which had turned out to be a lie told by an entire civilization—"freely, and without reservation, to the toll of this crossing."

The clerk wrote. The pen made no sound, but she felt it anyway, a small clean tug somewhere behind her sternum, like a thread being drawn out. When it finished, the window darkened for a moment, and then the clerk looked up at her with its ink-blotted absence of a face and said, in the tone of a man closing a file he'd kept open too long:

"Processed. You may pass as Ysolde. Just Ysolde." A pause. "It suits you better than the other one did. You held it like it was burning you."

"It was," she said.

"Most of them are," said the clerk, and gestured her through—or would have, except that the gate did not open so much as *accept* her, the papers rippling aside like water, and she stepped through into a stretch of road that felt fractionally lighter, the way a pack does after you finally admit how much of it was rocks you'd picked up yourself.

She turned to watch the others come through. Which was when the trouble started.

* * *

Marguerite went second, because Marguerite would have gone last if the others had allowed it, and they had not.

"Name," said the clerk.

There was a silence. Ysolde knew that silence. She'd heard it from clients trying to talk their way past a customs inspection, from priests asked directly whether they believed, from herself, once, standing in the ruins of her temple being asked what she'd done with the fire. It was the silence of a person deciding how much of the truth they could afford.

"Marguerite," he said at last. Then, when the clerk's pen hovered expectantly: "That's all. Just Marguerite."

"The records disagree," said the clerk pleasantly. "Marguerite Voss. Bone-carver. Doorkeeper. Holder of the sigil of the Guild of Impossible Things, account current, debts outstanding." The pen tapped. "Full name, full value. That is the rate."

"No," said Marguerite.

Ysolde's stomach dropped. In eleven years of knowing him—had it really been eleven years? it felt like both a week and a lifetime—he had never once said *no* to anything with that particular flat finality. He bargained. He deferred. He vanished. He did not plant his feet.

"It's my name," he said. "I've spent fifty years making sure nobody used it. I'm not handing it to a gate."

"Names are currency here," said the clerk. "Without payment, you cannot pass. Without passing, the lantern does not reach the border. Without the border, the door stays open. Without the door—" the clerk consulted some inner page "—the god-king comes through. Shall I continue the causal chain? I have it written down. It's very thorough."

"I know the chain," Marguerite said. "I keep ledgers too."

"Then you know debts must be paid."

"And I know," said Marguerite, and here his voice changed, dropped into something older and harder than his usual careful mildness, "that you don't take a man's whole purse just because he's standing at your bridge. Take a portion. Take a surname. Take—" he stopped.

"Take what?" said the clerk.

"Nothing," said Marguerite. But Ysolde saw his hand move, quick and involuntary, toward the ledger-key at his belt, and understood, with a lurch, what he'd been about to offer instead. Not the name. The sigil. The last doorkey to a dead nation, offered to a tollgate made of filing cabinets, because he would rather lose the whole quest than be *Marguerite Voss* in front of witnesses.

Over her dead body. Or over the god-king's, whichever came first.

"He'll pay," she said loudly, stepping up beside him. "He's just bad at arithmetic. Marguerite—you gave me your name years ago. Don't look at me like that, you did. First night I came to the guildhall with the lantern, you signed the intake ledger, full name, in your own hand, because rules are rules. I've been carrying it around ever since. It's mine as much as yours now. So give the gate *my copy*."

"That's not how names work," said the clerk.

"It's how *guild* names work," said Ysolde, who had absolutely no idea whether that was true, and watched Marguerite's eyebrows rise a fraction, which from him was a standing ovation. "Everything in a guild ledger is jointly held. Look it up. You're made of paperwork; you must have the forms."

The clerk hesitated. Actually hesitated. Somewhere inside the gate, papers shuffled, consulting themselves.

And that was when Sefa stepped forward.

* * *

Later, Ysolde would try to reconstruct exactly what the child did, and fail, because the honest answer was that Sefa lied to a border made of bureaucracy and the bureaucracy *believed her*, and there was no accounting for it except the kind Marguerite did, in ledgers, in the margins, in tiny disbelieving handwriting.

"Excuse me," Sefa said, planting herself in front of the window with her hands behind her back, in the posture of a child about to recite something memorized. "I know the rule. My grandpa was a toll-keeper. Before. When there were countries."

"Sefa," Marguerite said warningly.

"There's a clause," Sefa said earnestly. "For children. Because children don't own their names yet—they're still being paid for, in food and shoes and bedtime stories, so the name is technically an investment, and you can't tax an investment before maturity. Grandpa said. It's in the old code. Section... twelve?"

The clerk's pen stopped moving.

Somewhere deep in the gate, the murmur of papers rose to a rustle, then a susurrus, then something approaching panic. Ysolde actually heard a distinct voice say, *"Is there a section twelve?"* and another answer, *"There was a section twelve, it was repealed in the fourth year of the Regency,"* and a third, shrill with bureaucratic terror, *"Repealed or amended? AMENDED OR REPEALED?"*

"The amendment," Sefa said clearly, into the noise, "says children pay in *promised* names instead. A name they're going to grow into. That's the fair rate. Everyone knows that."

Silence. Total, trembling, archival silence.

Then the clerk said, slowly, "State the promised name."

And Sefa—who had no grandfather who was a toll-keeper, who had been an orphan since before she could remember, whose loud artless prayers had dragged three hungry gods into their lives already and who knew, better than anyone Ysolde had ever met, exactly what it cost to want a family—Sefa looked straight into the ink-dark window and said:

"Daughter of Marguerite Voss."

The words went through the gate like a hot knife through wax.

Ysolde heard Marguerite make a sound she had never heard him make—not a word, not quite a breath, something between a laugh and the noise a man makes when a rib he didn't know was broken finally sets. The ghost put his hand briefly on the boy-man's shoulder, or tried to; his fingers passed through, and he settled, instead, for standing very close, which for him was an embrace.

"That name," said the clerk, in a voice that had gone strangely soft for a stack of paperwork, "does not exist in our records."

"No," said Sefa. "It's new. It's a promise. Promises count—that's the whole point of section twelve."

Another silence, longer. Then, from within the gate, the unmistakable sound of a stamp coming down, followed by many stamps, followed by what could only be described as applause from several thousand sheets of paper.

"Accepted," said the clerk. "Payment recorded: one promised name, maturing at majority, payable in full to the bearer." The pen scratched. "Marguerite Voss, you are assessed a dependent. Your passage is subsidized. Step through, please, before I discover I've invented a regulation, because I'm fairly sure I have, and I'd rather not examine it."

Marguerite stepped through. He did it slowly, and as he passed the window, he paused, and said—in a voice roughened at the edges, addressed not to the clerk but to the child—

"When it matures," he said, "it pays out to *you*. Remember that. I keep good books."

"I know," said Sefa. "I checked."

He went through. The ghost went through without ceremony—the gate apparently considered the dead pre-paid, which drew from the master a mutter about *typical* that Ysolde chose to find comforting.

Which left Sefa alone at the window.

"Your turn," said the clerk gently. "Though I confess I don't know what we'll do with you. You've already paid."

"I know," said Sefa. She was quiet for a moment, and Ysolde, watching from the far side, saw the child's shoulders do the thing they did when she was deciding whether to be brave or just tired. "Can I ask a question? Off the record?"

"Nothing is off the record here."

"Then on the record." Sefa took a breath. "The name I gave. Is it real now? Because I paid with it. Does that make it true?"

The clerk was silent for a long moment. Papers turned somewhere in its depths, unhurried, almost thoughtful.

"Child," it said at last, "we are a gate made of every form ever filed in a country that no longer exists. We have accepted bribes disguised as tariffs and tariffs disguised as gifts. We have processed grief under 'miscellaneous.' And I will tell you what forty years of this work has taught us." The pen lifted. "A lie told for love stops being a lie the moment someone believes it enough to build a life on it. That name is real. It entered the record. Records are how reality keeps its receipts."

"Oh," said Sefa, in a small voice.

"Now go. All of you. The bell doesn't toll for free forever."

They went. The road beyond the gate was wider—it had been waiting for them, Ysolde realized, the way roads in this country waited, patient and faintly hopeful—and the mist had thinned enough to show hills ahead, and on one hill, unmistakably, a temple roof, green with age and stubbornness.

Ysolde walked beside Marguerite, the lantern swinging between them, and found she couldn't stop turning the clerk's words over. *You held it like it was burning you.* She kept flexing her shoulders, waiting for the weight of the title to settle back where it had lived for forty years, and it didn't. There was just space there now. Strange, light, terrifying space.

"You okay?" Sefa asked, trotting up on Marguerite's other side, swinging her arms.

"No," Ysolde said honestly.

"Me neither," said Sefa cheerfully. "It's nice, isn't it?"

Ahead, the temple bells began to ring—real bells this time, bronze and cracked and human—and Ysolde stopped walking.

Because she recognized them.

She had rung those bells herself, at nineteen, with blistered hands, on the morning of her ordination. Which was impossible, because that temple had burned forty years ago with everything else.

Unless, said a cold, clear voice in her memory—the voice of the clerk, of the ledgers, of the whole patient machinery of the old country—unless it had never finished burning. Unless some fires, like some names, stay on the books.

"Marguerite," she said. "How much farther to the priests who stayed?"

He consulted the master's map, which was drawing itself as they walked, lines appearing in faded ink like something surfacing through water.

"Half a day," he said. "Why?"

"Because," said Ysolde, watching the smoke rise from a valley that should have been empty, "those bells are ringing the alarm for an ordination. And I never finished mine."
