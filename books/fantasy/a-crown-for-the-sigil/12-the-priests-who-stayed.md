## 12. The Priests Who Stayed

The temple appeared the way a lie appears in a good confession: suddenly, and only when you were ready for it.

One moment they were walking through hills that existed on no map made after the Dissolution—hills that had been sold off in parcels to three different provinces and so, by some bureaucratic miracle, belonged to none of them—and the next there was a road under their feet. Not a grand road. A cart-track, rutted, lined with waystones whose carvings had been worn to suggestions. And at the end of it, squatting in a fold of the land like an old dog that has decided not to move again: the Temple of the Embered Heart.

Ysolde stopped walking.

She had imagined this moment for eleven years. In the bad years—the smuggler years, the years of forged manifests and bribed harbor-masters and sleeping in the hold of ships that smelled of fish and worse—she had rehearsed it. She would come back in glory, or she would come back in chains, but either way she would come back *loud*, and they would all have to look at her.

Instead she came back tired, smelling of lantern-oil and three days of road dust, carrying a dying flame in a brass case, flanked by a bone-carver who moved like a man trying not to cast a shadow, a ten-year-old who was currently narrating her own footfalls ("*step*, *step*, *crunch*, that one was a beetle, sorry, beetle"), and a ghost who kept making disapproving noises about the state of the waystones.

"Well," said Sefa, squinting up at the temple. "It's smaller than I thought gods lived."

"It's not a god's house anymore," Ysolde said. Her voice came out flat. "It's supposed to be empty."

"It's smoking," Sefa said.

And it was. A thin gray thread rose from behind the temple's eastern wall, the thread of a cookfire, of laundry water, of somebody's supper. Forty years after the order scattered, forty years after the last dragon died and the last priest was told to go home and be nobody, somebody was home.

The master's ghost drifted up beside her shoulder. He had been quiet most of the day, which from him was either mercy or strategy.

"You could wait outside," he said.

"I'm a smuggler," Ysolde said. "I don't wait outside doors. Doors are where the money is."

"That is not what doors are for," Marguerite murmured, "in my experience."

"Your experience," Ysolde said, "is with doors that stay politely shut."

She walked up the track before she could talk herself out of it. The others followed, because that was the thing about found families nobody warned you about—they followed you into your worst rooms and stood there watching you open them.

* * *

The door of the temple was oak, older than the Dissolution, carved with the sigil of the Embered Heart: a flame within a circle within a flame. Ysolde had spent her childhood tracing that carving while the priests droned through evening offices. She had spent her adolescence resenting it. She had spent her exile dreaming of burning it.

Her knuckles hovered over the wood.

"Want me to knock?" Sefa offered. "I'm very good at knocking. I once knocked on the guildhall cookhouse door for an hour until Cook gave me bread just to make me stop."

"That," said Marguerite, "is extortion."

"That," said Sefa, "is *negotiation*. Tollgate rules."

Before anyone could knock, the door opened on its own.

The priest standing in it was old in the way that stone walls are old—not frail, exactly, but weathered down to essentials. He wore the gray robe of the order with the flame-sigil patched at the shoulder, patched so many times the original embroidery had probably given up decades ago. His eyes went from Ysolde's face to the lantern-case slung across her back, and something moved through his expression like wind through wheat.

"Sister Ysolde," he said.

"Not sister," Ysolde said. "Not anything. I'm just the courier."

The priest stepped aside anyway. "Then you'd better come in, courier. Supper's nearly ready, and Brother Anselm made too much again. He always makes too much. Forty years of cooking for a congregation of nine, and he still cooks for ninety." A pause, and then, with the ghost of a smile: "Hope, I suppose. Or habit. At our age they're the same thing."

* * *

The refectory smelled of barley stew and woodsmoke, and it was full.

Not full of ninety. Full of nine—eight priests and a lay brother, gray-robed, white-haired, arranged around two long tables built for a hundred. They had kept the big tables. That was the detail that caught in Ysolde's chest like a fishhook. Forty years, nine people, and they still set the long tables, still sat spread out along them as if the missing rows might fill in any Sunday now.

The priests looked up as the four of them entered—five, counting the ghost, though only Sefa seemed able to see him clearly, and she was busy waving at him to sit somewhere, which he could not, being dead, and which he pretended to consider anyway, out of politeness.

The old priest who had opened the door introduced himself as Father Oren. Then he went around the table naming the others—Brother Anselm of the too-generous stew, Sister Maren who was deaf in one ear and pretended not to be, Brother Tam who had been a novice of nineteen when the order fell and was now sixty and still technically a novice—and Ysolde stood there with her hood dripping meltwater and felt the strangest sensation.

None of them looked at her with anger.

She had prepared for anger. She had a whole speech prepared for anger, actually, honed over eleven years, beginning with *you cast me out for loving the fire more than the rule* and ending somewhere around *and I hope your incense gives you headaches forever.* It was a good speech. It had structure. It had a devastating closing line.

Nobody asked her to give it.

They asked if she was hungry.

"I—" Ysolde said, and found her voice had gone somewhere without leaving a note. "Yes. Fine. Whatever."

"She means yes please," Sefa translated, already climbing onto a bench. "She talks like that. It took me a week to learn her."

Marguerite sat at the end of the bench with the careful economy of a man who had decided the stew was safe but was reserving judgment on the bread. The ghost settled—or mimed settling—beside him, and Sister Maren, the deaf one, glanced directly at the empty air beside Marguerite and nodded courteously, as if greeting someone.

Ysolde stared at her.

"Maren sees them," Father Oren said mildly, ladling stew. "Always did. Why do you think we kept her? Someone has to know when the dead are complaining about the hymns."

"They are always complaining about the hymns," Maren said, with dignity. "Anselm sings like a landslide."

* * *

It was after supper, when the bowls were cleared and Sefa had fallen asleep sitting upright against Marguerite's arm mid-sentence (she had been explaining that beetles deserved apology, actually, because everything deserves apology, even beetles, *especially* beetles), that Father Oren finally said the thing.

He said it quietly, over the banked fire, while the others dozed or prayed or, in Anselm's case, began the dishes with the serene confidence of a man who believed washing-up was a sacrament.

"The lantern," Oren said. "You've carried it far."

"From the capital's black market to here," Ysolde said. "And before that, who knows. It changes hands. It's been changing hands since the last dragon died. Everyone who holds it can feel it going out, and everyone who holds it is too frightened to be the one holding it when it does."

"And so it travels." Oren nodded slowly. "We heard rumors. A flame in a box, moving north along the old roads. We prayed it would come here, and then we prayed it wouldn't, because answered prayers are dangerous these days." He looked at her across the fire. "Do you know why it must reach the border, Sister?"

"Don't call me—"

"Why must it reach the border?" he repeated, gently, relentlessly, the way he had probably asked penitents hard questions for forty years.

Ysolde looked at the lantern-case leaning against the wall. Through its seams, faintly, the light pulsed—slower than a heartbeat now, slower than breathing. Guttering. She had watched it gutter for three days and had told herself it was just a flame doing what flames do.

"Because it's dragon-fire," she said. "The last of it. And dragon-fire doesn't burn things. It burns *boundaries*. That's what the old rites were for—not sacrifice, not power. Maintenance. The dragons burned the borders of the world true, kept the real real and the unreal unreal. When they died, the borders started to... soften." She swallowed. "And the border of the old country never got sealed properly. It was never allowed to die properly. So it's been leaking for forty years. Ghosts. Roads that shouldn't exist. Gods answering prayers that should have gone to voicemail."

Oren almost smiled at that last part. Almost.

"And the god-king," he said. "You've heard."

"We've heard."

"He wants the door opened. Restoration. The country reborn, whole, with him on the throne of ceremony and bone." Oren's hands folded in his lap. "Here is what the order understood, in the last days, that the government never did. The final door of the old country—the true border, the one underneath all the maps—can only be sealed or opened by flame from the country's heart. Ordinary fire won't take the hinges. Foreign fire won't recognize the lock. Only the Embered Heart itself. The last dragon-fire *is* the heart of the country, still beating, still traveling, waiting for someone to carry it home."

"So whoever holds the lantern," Ysolde said slowly, "holds the key to everything."

"Whoever holds the lantern holds the choice," Oren corrected. "Seal the door, and the old country finally dies—a clean death, debts forgiven, ghosts laid to rest, the god-king locked out forever. Open it, and the country rises again—but every debt of the Dissolution comes due at once. Every promise broken. Every soul promised a homeland that was sold out from under them." He shook his head. "Resurrection is never free, child. It is always bought with someone else's grave."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dormitory wing, Sefa talked in her sleep about beetles.

"And why are you telling me this?" Ysolde asked. Her voice came out harder than she intended. "Why tell the disgraced one? The one they stripped of her orders for—" She stopped. Eleven years, and she still couldn't say it cleanly. "For what I did."

"For what you loved," Oren said.

"That's not—"

"Ysolde." He said her name the way he had said it when she was nine years old and had set fire to the vestry curtains trying to relight a votive that had gone out. Kindly. Without fear. "You were cast out for loving the fire more than the rule. Do you know what the rest of us were cast into? Loving the rule more than the fire. We stayed, yes. Nine of us, tending an empty temple, keeping the offices, lighting candles for a congregation of memory. And do you know what we tended?" He gestured at the cold hearth at the center of the sanctuary beyond, where the eternal flame had gone out forty years ago and had never, not once, been rekindled. "Ash. We kept faith with ash. You went out into the world and kept faith with the actual fire."

Ysolde's throat closed.

"You think we cast you out in anger," Oren continued. "Some did, perhaps. But I signed that decree myself, and I will tell you what I knew then: the order was dying, the dragons were dead, and the ones who stayed were staying out of grief, not strength. Grief makes poor priests. You were young and blazing and half-mad with loss, and we sent you into the world because the world needed someone half-mad enough to keep a dying flame moving. We just didn't have the honesty to say so. We called it punishment." His old eyes held hers. "It was the only ordination we had left to give."

"That's—" Ysolde's voice cracked and she hated it, hated the wet heat behind her eyes, hated that eleven years of rehearsed fury was dissolving in front of a bowl of barley stew. "That's not absolution. You can't just *decide* it was a blessing afterward. That's cheating."

"All theology is cheating," said Oren placidly. "It's cheating on behalf of mercy. Someone has to do it."

Across the room, the ghost of Master Ellery Voss—who had been listening, because of course he had been listening, the man had never once in life or death failed to eavesdrop—cleared his throat theatrically.

"For what it's worth," he said, though only Sefa (asleep) and Maren (nodding along) and Ysolde (barely) could hear him, "I told you something similar once. About inheritance. You didn't listen either. It's a family trait among the stubborn."

"Shut up, Ellery," Ysolde whispered.

"See?" he said to the room at large. "Family."

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She sat in the sanctuary instead, on the stone floor where novices used to sit for the night-vigil, and looked at the dead hearth.

Forty years cold. Nine keepers. Candles lit around it every morning anyway—she had seen Maren do it at dawn, lighting each wick with the unhurried precision of ritual, tending a flame-shaped absence.

Ysolde unslung the lantern-case and set it on the hearthstone.

Through the brass seams, the ember inside pulsed. Slow. Stubborn. Still refusing, in its dim animal way, to be finished.

"You're not going to make some dramatic speech, are you?" she asked it. "Because I've had enough speeches tonight."

The ember pulsed. Once. Twice. Like something listening. Like something patient.

And in the silence after, Ysolde heard—not with her ears, but in the marrow-deep way that priests of the Embered Heart were trained to hear, the training they'd tried to strip out of her and had failed, because you cannot strip out what someone loves—

She heard the dead.

Not one dead. Not the polite handful of ancestors that temples usually hummed with. A *crowd*. A procession's worth, gathering somewhere out past the temple walls, out along the old roads, drawn like moths to the last light of the country they had been promised. She felt them the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth.

They weren't angry.

That was the worst part. They were *waiting*. Patient as creditors. Patient as the poor.

Behind her, the sanctuary door creaked, and Marguerite stood in it, wrapped in a blanket, looking like a man who had hoped never to be visible at this hour. His face was pale.

"They're coming," he said. "My bones woke me up. All of them. Every dead person I've ever heard through my hands, and thousands more besides." He hugged the blanket tighter. "They're asking for someone. They keep saying—" He stopped, swallowed. "They keep saying they want their advocate."

Ysolde looked down at the ember, guttering, faithful, waiting on the hearthstone of a temple that had kept faith with ash for forty years.

"Then I suppose," she said, "we'd better figure out what they're owed."
