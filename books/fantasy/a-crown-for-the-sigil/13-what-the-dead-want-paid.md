## 13. What the Dead Want Paid

Marguerite had spent forty years learning the grammar of the dead, and he had learned it the way one learns any difficult language: slowly, painfully, and mostly by being shouted at.

The dead who spoke through his bones were, as a rule, a fussy lot. They complained about cold. They complained about being misremembered. One grandmother in his left wrist had spent three decades insisting that her grandson had buried her in the wrong shawl, and no amount of gentle explanation—that the grandson was himself long dead, that the shawl question was now, cosmically speaking, moot—had settled the matter. The dead did not want resolution. They wanted to be *heard*, which was a different industry entirely, and Marguerite was its sole, unwilling clerk.

So when the dead began rising at dusk on their third night out from the guildhall, he assumed at first it was simply more of the same. More complaints. More shawls.

He was wrong, and the wrongness arrived with a weight that buckled his knees in the middle of the road.

* * *

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen milestone—a pre-dissolution marker, its carved lettering worn to ghosts of ghosts—and Ysolde was feeding the lantern its measured thimble of oil while Sefa narrated her supper to it, because the child had decided the dragon-fire liked stories and Marguerite had not had the heart to say otherwise. The flame leaned toward her voice like an old man toward a fire. It was smaller than it had been. Everyone pretended not to notice.

Then Marguerite's hands began to hum.

It started, as these things did, in the small bones—the carpals first, then the metacarpals, a rising chorus like tuning forks struck one after another. He set down his bowl. "Oh," he said. "Oh, no."

"What?" Ysolde said, already reaching for her knife, which was her answer to most questions.

"They're all—" He flexed his fingers. The humming climbed his forearms, settled into his ribs, and became something less like sound and more like weather. "All of them. At once. I've never—there are *rules*. They queue. There's a queue. I built them a *queue*."

"Maybe they've finished waiting," said the master's ghost, drifting up through the bedroll he was technically haunting and had never once helped fold.

The road answered before Marguerite could.

They came up out of the ground the way frost comes up out of a field—not violently, not all at once, but everywhere at once, a pale blooming along the whole length of the old highway as far as the lantern-light reached. Figures stood in the ruts and the hedgerows and the doorway of a ruined tollhouse. Farmers. Soldiers. A woman still wearing the apron of a profession that had ceased to exist sixty years ago. A child holding hands with nobody, or with somebody only Marguerite could see. Hundreds of them. Then thousands, the dark between the lantern and the horizon filling with the faintly luminous patient shapes of the unpaid.

Sefa scrambled behind Marguerite, then, characteristically, peeked out. "Are those the ones from your wrists?"

"Some," Marguerite managed. His voice had gone thin. "And everyone else."

The dead did not advance. That was the thing that undid him. In forty years of hearing the dead complain, he had never once seen them *wait*. They pressed and muttered and interrupted; they were the living's worst dinner guests. But this host stood in perfect silence, and the silence had a texture to it, a held-breath quality, and when the nearest figure—a broad-shouldered man with a soldier's posture and a farmer's boots—finally spoke, it spoke the way a petitioner speaks at a counter, having taken a number.

"Doorkeeper," the man said. His voice came through the ground, through Marguerite's boot-soles, up through the very bones that had hummed. "We would file a claim."

Marguerite looked at Ysolde. Ysolde looked at Marguerite. Sefa whispered, "I think you're the counter."

"I'm not a counter," Marguerite said faintly. "I'm a *clerk*."

"Same thing out here," said the ghost of Master Aldous Vane, settling beside him with the air of a man enjoying himself enormously, which was, as ever, insufferable. "Congratulations. You've been promoted."

* * *

Here is what Marguerite learned over the next four hours, sitting on a fallen milestone with his ledger open across his knees and the dead of a dissolved nation queued before him in the freezing dark:

They were not angry. That was the second thing that undid him. He had braced for fury—he had, in fact, spent his entire adult life braced for fury, on the theory that anyone sold would eventually come looking for the seller—but what came out of them instead was something far harder to bear. Hope. Careful, rationed hope, the kind you keep in a jar behind a loose brick so it doesn't spoil.

The farmer-soldier went first, because queues have order even among the dead. His name had been Tam Brecht. He had been promised, in the last winter of the dissolution, a parcel of land in the new settlements—a country that would be rebuilt, the criers said, once the borders were sold and the debts paid and the danger passed. He had died in the waiting. So had everyone else who'd heard the criers. And the land had never been rebuilt, because the country itself had been sold off in pieces to buy the survivors' passage, and a promise made by a nation is void the moment the nation stops existing.

"We weren't told we were the payment," Tam Brecht said. Not accusing. Just stating the entry, the way Marguerite himself might state it. "We thought we were the ones being paid."

After him came a choir mistress whose anthem had been sold—literally sold, the melody itself, one of the guild's founding transactions—and who could no longer remember how it went, though she could feel the shape of it, the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth. After her, a midwife owed eleven children she had delivered into a country that evaporated before they could be registered. After her, a bell-founder. A tax assessor. An entire bridge club, still arguing about a hand played in 1943, who filed jointly.

Marguerite wrote until his hand cramped. Names, claims, amounts owed—not in coin, he understood quickly, but in things: a harvest never gathered, a song unsung, a grave unmarked, a homecoming deferred past all possibility. The ledger took it all without complaint, the ink flowing steady, and somewhere around the two-hundredth name Marguerite realized the book was warmer than the night air, warm as a body, as if it had been waiting its whole existence for exactly this work.

"You knew," he said quietly to Aldous, during the brief lull while the bridge club reorganized its filing system. "You knew they'd come."

"I suspected." The ghost's usual twinkle was absent. "Your master before me kept a page titled *Contingencies: Mass*. I never had the stomach to read it twice."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you'd have spent forty years dreading this night instead of forty years becoming the one man alive who could survive it." Aldous drifted closer, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "A clerk who fears the crowd hides from it. A clerk who knows the crowd is coming learns the forms. You learned the forms, boy. Every tedious one. I made sure of it."

Marguerite wanted to stay angry about the stolen years, the purchased silence, the whole architecture of protection built over his head without his consent. He found he couldn't, quite, not tonight. Tonight the anger felt like complaining about the shawl. He turned back to the queue instead.

* * *

It was near midnight when Sefa climbed onto the milestone beside him, wrapped in both blankets and Ysolde's spare cloak, and studied the endless pale line with the frank appraisal of a child at a market stall.

"Do they know you're scared?" she asked.

"Sefa."

"'Cause I can't tell. You're doing the face where you look bored, but your ears go red."

"My ears do not—"

"They're very red."

Marguerite exhaled. Down the line, the dead waited, and none of them rushed him, and that patience was somehow worse than shouting. "Yes," he admitted. "I'm scared. I've spent my whole life making sure nobody needed me for anything important. Needing gets people noticed. Noticed gets people—"

"Eaten by gods?"

"Sold, usually. Eaten occasionally. The guild has a pamphlet."

Sefa considered this. Then she said, with the terrible directness available only to ten-year-olds and the recently bereaved: "But they *do* need you. They walked all the way out of the ground. That's a lot of walking, when you're dead."

"I know."

"And you're helping them."

"I'm writing it down. Writing it down isn't helping. Writing it down is just... filing."

Sefa looked at him the way she looked at the lantern when it guttered—worried, and unwilling to say so. "My mama used to write everything down too. What we ate, what we owed, what I said, even the silly stuff. She said if you write someone down, they can't fully disappear." She shrugged. "Seems like helping to me."

Marguerite had no answer for that. He pulled the ledger closer and called the next name, and his ears, he was fairly certain, stayed red the rest of the night.

* * *

Dawn was graying the east when the nature of the request finally clarified itself, and it clarified, as most disasters in Marguerite's life had, through paperwork.

Tam Brecht returned to the front of the queue—he had, Marguerite noticed, been quietly maintaining order among the others all night, a foreman to the end—and laid a hand not quite touching the ledger's edge.

"Doorkeeper. We've discussed it. All of us." A ripple went down the thousand-strong line, a wind through wheat. "We know what you think we want. The god-king's men have been whispering along the roads. Restoration. Rebirth. A kingdom raised again with every soul returned to it." His voice roughened, just slightly. "Some of us were tempted. It's a fine word, *restored*. Makes you think of a house put right after a flood."

"But?" Marguerite asked.

"But we've had sixty years to read the terms." Tam Brecht's gaze was steady and terribly tired. "A restored kingdom means the dissolution undone means the sale undone means the debts called due in full—from whoever holds them. And doorkeeper, we *are* the debts. Every soul standing here. If that door opens wide, we don't get our country back. We get *collected*."

The words landed in Marguerite's chest like stones dropped down a well. He thought of the ledgers writing themselves in the guildhall, of the god-king assembling himself out of ceremony and bone, of a throne room waiting behind a door shaped like his own key. He thought of Sefa asleep against Ysolde's shoulder, her shadow flickering oddly in the lantern light, and made himself stop thinking about that, because dawn was not the hour for it.

"So what do you want?" he asked. "If not restoration."

Tam Brecht looked at him for a long moment. Around them the entire host of the dead leaned in—not menacingly, Marguerite realized, but the way a congregation leans in, the way people lean toward the one person in the room who might actually do something.

"Not a door," the farmer-soldier said. "We've had enough of doors. Doors get opened by kings." He knelt, slowly, the way men kneel when their knees are older than their pride, and the motion traveled down the line like a wave, hundreds upon hundreds of the dead kneeling in the gray morning light of a road that barely existed. "We want an *advocate*. Someone to carry our claims to the border. Someone to stand in front of whatever waits there and say: these souls were promised a country, and the country was sold, and the debt has never been paid, and we will not be collected—we will be *counted*. Someone to speak us aloud."

His eyes, pale as milk-glass, held Marguerite's.

"You hear the dead, doorkeeper. For forty years you've written us down so we couldn't disappear. We're asking you to finish the job. Not as a door. As a voice."

The silence afterward was total. Even the lantern seemed to hold its breath, the little flame standing very straight, listening.

Marguerite looked down at his hands—carver's hands, clerk's hands, hands that had spent a lifetime keeping themselves busy so no one would look at the rest of him. He thought about invisibility, and how comfortable it had been, and how it had always, underneath, been fear wearing a very well-tailored coat.

"I'm not brave," he said at last, because it seemed important to disclose this to his prospective clients. "I'm not a hero. I'm a bookkeeper. I hide. It's rather my defining feature."

"Yes," said Tam Brecht. "And yet here you sit, in the open, at dawn, with all of us watching. Funny thing about hiding, doorkeeper." Something that was almost a smile moved across the dead man's face. "Takes two. Somebody has to be looking."

Behind him, Sefa made a sound suspiciously like a snort. Marguerite chose, with great dignity, to ignore it.

"All right," he said. His voice cracked on the second word and he pressed on regardless. "All right. I'll carry your claims. Every name in this ledger goes to the border, and every claim gets spoken, and I will stand in front of whatever king or god or creditor waits there and I will *read them the accounts*. That's what I'm good at. Reading people the accounts." He swallowed. "But I set the terms. Nobody gets collected. Nobody gets spent without consent—including me. Agreed?"

The wave of kneeling figures bowed lower. Somewhere in the vast quiet, a sound went up that was not quite a cheer—it was softer than that, older, the sound a congregation makes when a prayer is finally, after decades, answered correctly.

"Aye," said Tam Brecht. "Agreed, advocate."

*Advocate.* Marguerite turned the word over as the dead began, gently, to sink back into the earth—not banished, just resting, the way workers rest between shifts. It fit strangely, like a coat cut for a bigger man. He had been a door his whole life, a thing others passed through, a thing defined by openings. Advocates got to choose which side of the argument they stood on.

Ysolde appeared at his elbow with tea that tasted of pine needles and iron, which was her way of saying she'd been awake all night worrying. "You realize," she said, "that you've just accepted power of attorney for several thousand souls in a dispute with a god-king."

"I realize I need more tea."

"And that the god-king will hear of this by sundown. Things that rise that loudly don't rise quietly. Everything on these roads felt it—the lantern's been burning brighter since midnight, have you noticed? Like something's *feeding* it. Or following it." She glanced at the eastern sky, where the gray was turning the color of old pewter. "He'll want to talk to you, Marguerite. Men like that always want to talk before they take. And he'll make you an offer. They always do."

Marguerite sipped his terrible tea and watched the last of the dead settle into the ground like sediment into still water. On his knees, the ledger lay open to a fresh page, and across its top, in ink he had not touched, in handwriting that was almost—almost—his own, a single line had already written itself:

*Claim No. 1: One country, promised and withheld. Payable to: everyone.*

"He can offer what he likes," Marguerite said. "I've read his books. He's insolvent."

But even as he said it, the ledger warmed against his knees, and far down the road behind them—back the way they'd come, toward the crossroads shrine he could see marked on Ysolde's impossible map—the air began, very softly, to ring with bells.
