## 14. The God-King's Offer

The crossroads shrine had been there before the road was, which Marguerite knew because the dead told him so, and they were insufferably smug about it.

It was a small thing—four posts of grey stone holding up a roof of slate gone green with moss, a niche where travelers once left bread and buttons and, according to one particularly chatty ghost, an entire wedding dress in 1147, for reasons no one had ever adequately explained. The shrine stood at the meeting of two roads that existed on different maps: the living road, wide and rutted and honest about its potholes, and the older one, the pre-dissolution map-road, which persisted only faintly now, like a word you could almost remember.

They had stopped to rest because Ysolde insisted the lantern needed tending and Sefa insisted she needed to pee behind a hedge, and Marguerite—who had learned over three weeks of travel that both insistences were usually cover for something else—had agreed with the weary alacrity of a man who has stopped asking questions.

The lantern sat in the center of the shrine's niche, where the bread and buttons used to go. The dragon-fire inside had burned down to something the size of a thumbnail—a coal, really, a stubborn orange secret in a cage of black iron. It did not flicker anymore. Flickering, Marguerite understood, required optimism.

"Three days to the border," Ysolde said, not looking at him. She was rewrapping the lantern's chain with the tenderness other people reserved for infants. "Two, if the old roads hold."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we walk through country that isn't there, and I don't recommend it. The last time I crossed unmapped ground, I came out with someone else's knees." She flexed her leg, considering. "Good knees, mind. But not mine."

Sefa returned from the hedge at a dead sprint, which was her only speed. "There's a man coming up the living road," she announced, breathless, delighted, in the tone of a child announcing cake. "He's wearing *so much gold*."

Marguerite's hands went cold before his mind caught up. He heard the dead shift inside his bones—the way a house shifts when something heavy moves upstairs.

"How much gold?" he asked, though he already knew.

"All of it," said Sefa. "I think he's wearing all of the gold there is."

* * *

The god-king of a dissolved nation does not arrive. He accumulates.

Marguerite watched it happen from the shadow of the shrine's slate roof, and some detached bookkeeping corner of his mind took notes even as the rest of him wanted very badly to be elsewhere. First there was a glint on the road, as though the afternoon sun had gotten lost. Then the glint resolved into a figure walking toward them at an unhurried pace, and the figure resolved into robes, and the robes resolved into ceremony—layer upon layer of it, coronation mantles and funeral shrouds worn simultaneously, state regalia and mourning cloth, because he was, after all, both a king and his own funeral. His face was bone under a skin of remembered light, and his crown was not a crown but the idea of one, hovering a respectful inch above his skull, the way titles hover above men who have lost the countries that issued them.

Behind him walked honor guards made of folded flags. They moved like men. They were not men. Marguerite's ledger-key, hanging against his chest, had begun to warm—not alarmingly, just warmly, the way a stone holds the memory of sun.

"Well," said Ysolde quietly. Her hand found the lantern chain. "That's inconvenient."

"He's smaller than I imagined," Sefa whispered.

"He's exactly the size of every prayer ever answered wrong," Marguerite said. "Which is to say: enormous, and dressed well."

The god-king stopped ten paces off, at what his ceremonial instincts clearly calculated to be the optimal distance for majesty without shouting. When he spoke, his voice was the sound a crowd makes before it decides whether to cheer or riot.

"Doorkeeper."

"I'm a bone-carver," Marguerite said. "I carve memorials. Small ones. Dogs, mostly."

"You are the last doorkeeper of Vhalen Maros, holder of the sigil, keeper of the ledger of debts, and you will open my door."

"There it is," Marguerite said. "Straight to business. No pleasantries. You'd think six hundred years of ceremony would teach a man to open with the weather."

Something happened to the god-king's face that might have been a smile on a face that had one. "You are afraid. Good. Fear is appropriate. Fear is the first tax any citizen pays."

"I'm not a citizen. My country doesn't exist. That was rather the point of dissolving it."

"It exists." The god-king spread his arms, and the folded-flag guards rustled like wind through wheat. "I exist. Every bell that rang for me this month exists. A nation is not its borders, doorkeeper. A nation is its ceremonies, its debts, its names—and all of those are gathered here, waiting for their king to lead them home." His voice dropped, and here was the terrible thing: it dropped into gentleness. "You are waiting too. You simply don't know it yet."

Marguerite felt the dead in his bones go very still, the way a congregation goes still when the sermon turns personal.

* * *

Here is what Marguerite knew, and had known since the ledgers began writing themselves: that the dissolution of Vhalen Maros sixty years ago had not been a war lost but a bargain struck. The guild had sold the country piece by piece—its borders to the neighbors, its anthem to a music publisher, the king's shadow to a collector who had never been identified—to buy its people passage out of history alive. And the price of that survival had been unpaid. Sixty years of unpaid debt, accruing interest in the dark, and the debtors were the dead: everyone who had been promised a country and handed a receipt instead.

And here is what Marguerite did not know until the god-king said it aloud, standing in the green-mossed shrine where travelers once left bread:

"I can give them back to you," the god-king said. "Your years."

"My what?"

"Do not insult us both by pretending ignorance." The bone face tilted. "Your master bought your silence when you were young. Silence has a price, and the price was paid out of your account. Fourteen years, doorkeeper. Fourteen years of your life, spent before you ever lived them, to keep you small and safe and unseen." The god-king smiled again, and this time it reached whatever he used for eyes. "You have wondered why your memories begin late. Why your childhood feels like a room with the furniture missing. I can restore every year. Every birthday. Every ordinary morning. I can make you whole."

The world went quiet. Not silent—the dead were never silent—but quiet, hushed, attentive, an audience leaning forward.

Marguerite became aware, distantly, that Ysolde had said his name. Twice. He did not answer. Because the truth was that he *had* wondered. All his life, in the way you wonder about a scar you've stopped looking at. There was a hole in him shaped like a boy, and he had built his entire careful invisible life around the hole, furnishing its edges, calling it privacy, calling it discipline, calling it anything but what it was.

"You could have him back as well," the god-king said softly.

"Don't," Marguerite said.

"Master Aldous Vey. Your teacher. The ghost who rides your shoulder and pretends he is merely a habit." The god-king gestured, and the air beside Marguerite shimmered, and there—unwillingly dragged into visibility, translucent and furious—stood the ghost of Aldous Vey, looking exactly as Marguerite had always imagined him alive: sharp-faced, ink-stained, with the permanent expression of a man reading a contract he intends to contest.

"Aldous," the god-king said, with the courtesy of one monarch to another's servant. "Tell him what you would give to breathe again."

"I would give nothing," snapped the ghost, "to a man wearing that much gold, and neither should you, boy—"

"I could make him alive," the god-king said to Marguerite, ignoring the ghost entirely, the way kings ignore things. "Not resurrected. Not haunted. Alive. Warm hands. A heartbeat. Forty more years of arguing with you about margins. And a country, doorkeeper. A country reborn. Bells that ring for someone. Borders that hold. An anthem sung by throats instead of sheet music. Everything the guild sold, bought back. Everything the dead were promised, delivered at last." He stepped closer, and the warmth of him was the worst part—it was hearth-warmth, home-warmth, the exact temperature of a kitchen you remember loving. "All I ask is the door. Opened willingly. By the hand that holds the key."

* * *

And here is the thing that Marguerite would carry, afterward, like a stone in his boot: it was tempting. Genuinely, shamefully, knee-weakeningly tempting.

He wanted the years back. He wanted them with a hunger that frightened him—wanted the missing birthdays, the missing mother whose face he could not retrieve, the missing decade-and-a-half of being a person before he became a function. He wanted Aldous alive. He wanted, God help him, the bells. He had spent his whole life pretending not to want things, and now want had arrived on the road wearing all the gold there was, and it knew his name.

Beside him, Sefa tugged his sleeve. "Mister Marguerite," she whispered. "Is he lying?"

And that—that artless, loud, praying-too-loudly child's question—was the frost-water down his back. Because Marguerite was, before all else, a bookkeeper, and bookkeepers do not ask whether a number is sincere. They ask whether it balances.

"No," he said slowly. "That's the horror of it. He's not lying. He can do everything he says." He looked at the god-king, and his voice steadied as the arithmetic did. "You can restore my years. You can raise my master. You can rebuild the country. Every promise is real."

"Then we are agreed—"

"But a restored country means the debts are called due in full." Marguerite's hand closed around the ledger-key, warm against his palm. "That's what restoration *is*. You don't forgive the arrears; you collect them. Sixty years of promises to the dead, called in at once, at full value. The guild sold the country to keep its people alive. To un-sell it, someone has to pay the difference, and the difference is—" he met the bone face steadily— "*them*. The dead. Every soul promised a homeland gets collected to populate yours. My fourteen years come back out of somebody's account. My master breathes because something else stops. You're not offering me a gift, Your Majesty. You're offering me a loan, secured against everyone I've ever spoken to through my hands."

The dead in his bones murmured—not in protest. In agreement. He felt them pressing close, hundreds of them, thousands, the unpaid creditors of a dissolved nation, and not one of them said *take the offer*. What they said, in the wordless way of the dead, was closer to: *we have waited sixty years; we can wait for justice, but not for resurrection.*

"They'll rest either way," the god-king said. "Rest or citizenship. Both are deaths of a kind."

"Yes," Marguerite said. "But only one of them is theirs to choose."

Silence, then. The folded-flag guards rustled. Somewhere far off, on a road that existed, a cart creaked past carrying turnips, gloriously indifferent to empire.

"You mistake me for a negotiator," the god-king said at last, and the gentleness went out of his voice like a lamp going out of a window. "I did not come to persuade you, doorkeeper. Persuasion is for equals. I came to inform you. The door opens at the border in three days—with your hand or through your absence. If you will not turn the key, I will take it from your cooling fingers, and I will build my country on top of your grave, and the grave will be a tax office." He turned, robes pooling and reforming around him like spilled ink deciding to stand up. "Consider my generosity while it lasts. Generosity is also a debt, doorkeeper. And I always collect."

He walked back down the living road, and the gold of him diminished gradually, glint by glint, until he was only a rumor of shine and then not even that.

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Sefa said, "He had a wedding dress in his pocket."

"What?" said Ysolde.

"In his robes. I saw it. White lace. Why does a god-king carry a wedding dress?" She frowned. "Do you think it's for someone?"

Marguerite looked at the shrine niche, at the little stone hollow where travelers had once left bread and buttons and, in 1147, an entire wedding dress, for reasons no one had ever explained. Something cold walked down his spine on small, patient feet.

"Sefa," he said carefully, "when you prayed under the guildhall floorboards. The thing that answered you back. Did it ever say what it wanted?"

She thought about it with the ferocious seriousness she brought to all questions. "It said it was lonely," she said. "And that it had been waiting a long time for somebody to talk to. And once—" her brow furrowed— "once it hummed. Like a song. But slow. Like it was remembering it wrong."

Ysolde and Marguerite looked at each other. Neither said *the anthem*. Neither had to.

"Right," said Ysolde briskly, in the voice of a woman refusing to think about a wedding dress. "Lantern's tended. Border's three days. We move."

"One moment." Marguerite knelt at the shrine niche, and—because he was a bone-carver, and because his hands needed to do something while his heart finished its argument—he took out his knife and a scrap of antler and began, quickly, roughly, to carve. A small shape. A doorstop, actually, though he didn't let himself think about why. He set it in the niche where the offerings used to go.

"For the shrine," he said, when Sefa peered at it. "Travelers used to leave things. It seemed rude to take shelter and leave nothing."

"That's not how shrines work, mister Marguerite. You're supposed to *ask* for something."

"I know what I'm asking for." He stood, brushed off his knees, and did not look at the place beside him where a ghost stood watching him with an expression Marguerite refused, for the next three days at least, to examine. "I'm asking for time enough to finish the accounts."

They took the old road north, the four of them and the coal of a dying fire, and behind them the crossroads shrine held its little carved doorstop like a held breath.

And on the road ahead, faint but unmistakable, drifting from a country that wasn't there yet: someone—or something—was humming an anthem, slow and wrong, getting it righter with every mile.
