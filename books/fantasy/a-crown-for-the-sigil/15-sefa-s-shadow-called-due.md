## 15. Sefa's Shadow Called Due

Sefa knew something was wrong when the cookhouse woman looked straight through her and asked the empty air if anyone wanted more porridge.

"You made porridge," Sefa said. "You always make too much. You said so this morning. You said, *Sefa, child, you eat like a starling and I cook like a woman with no sense of portion.*"

The cookhouse woman—Marda, her name was Marda, it had been Marda for the whole year Sefa had lived at the guildhall—frowned at the ladle, then at the pot, then, briefly, at a spot roughly two feet to the left of Sefa's head.

"That's funny," Marda said. "I do feel like I said something like that. To somebody." She shrugged her enormous shrug and went back to stirring, and Sefa stood there with her bowl getting cold in her hands, and under her feet, beneath the floorboards, beneath the cellar, beneath the deep vaults where the guild kept its impossible inventory, something chuckled.

It was not a nice chuckle. It was the chuckle of a thing that had been waiting politely for three days and had run out of polite.

"You said three days," Sefa said to the floor, quietly, so Marda wouldn't hear her talking to the boards again. People already gave her looks for praying out loud. Talking to floorboards was worse. Talking to floorboards *that answered* was the kind of thing that got you sent to the priests, and the priests, as Ysolde had explained with the particular flatness she used for important things, were mostly useless and occasionally flammable.

*A riddle,* the god said, and its voice came up through the soles of her feet like cold water through a boot. *Three days, little loudmouth. You bought three days with your clever question, and the three days are spent. Time to pay.*

"You haven't answered the riddle," Sefa said. "You can't collect until you answer it. That's the rules. *Your* rules. You said so."

*I said I would consider your terms,* the god said, *and I have considered them, and I have decided that gods are not bound by the rules of children. This is, historically, how most of our best work gets done.*

Sefa set down her bowl. She was ten years old and small for it, with elbows like doorknobs and hair that Marda cut with kitchen shears, and she had learned in her short and much-shuffled life that the world ran on exactly two things: people who kept their promises, and people who were bigger than you. She had hoped, foolishly, that gods might be in the first category.

"What are you doing?" the god asked, because she had put her hand flat on the floorboards.

"Calling Marguerite," she said.

*The doorkeeper will not remember you by tomorrow noon.*

"Then I'll talk fast."

* * *

Marguerite came down to the cookhouse within the hour, because Sefa had run up three flights of stairs and pounded on his door with both fists and shouted through the wood, "IT'S URGENT AND ALSO I BROKE SOMETHING," which was, she felt, the only reliable way to get a bone-carver to open a door. He came down pale and neat as always, sleeves buttoned to the wrist even though it was a warm morning, and he listened to her explain the shadow, and the riddle, and the three days, with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he already dreaded the answer to.

"Show me," he said.

She showed him. She stood in the cookhouse doorway where the morning sun came through, and she looked down at the flagstones, where everyone's shadow lay honest and dark, and hers lay—

Thin. That was the only word for it. Thin, like tea steeped twice. Like a shadow that had been copied from a shadow. And when she moved her hand, the shadow's hand moved a half-beat late, the way an echo trails a shout.

Marguerite went very still. Sefa had learned that stillness was his version of screaming.

"Ysolde," he said at last. "We need Ysolde. And the master. And every ledger in the vault that mentions collateral." He paused. "Sefa. What exactly did you promise this god?"

"Nothing," Sefa said. "That's the thing. I didn't promise *anything*. It offered me a family and I said I'd think about it and it got huffy and marked my shadow as, um. Collateral. For good faith." She scuffed her thin shadow with her toe. "I thought collateral was the thing you *kept*, not the thing it took."

"It's both," said a voice from the doorway, dry as old paper. The master's ghost drifted in through the wall, because he had never once in death used a door when a wall was available, and Marguerite pretended not to see him, because pretending not to see him was their whole arrangement and it worked fine. "Collateral is the thing that stands in for you when you can't pay. It *is* you, in the ledger's eyes. That's what makes it collateral." The ghost looked down at Sefa with his faded, fond, appalled expression. "Child. You offered a god *yourself* as a down payment."

"I offered it a *riddle*," Sefa said. "The shadow was its idea."

* * *

Ysolde took the news the way she took most news, which was to swear in three dead liturgical languages, put on her coat, and start planning a fight. "Which god," she demanded. "Name it. Names are handles. You can carry a thing by its name."

"It wouldn't say," Sefa said. "It said names were for *smaller* gods."

"Wonderful. An anonymous deity with a grudge and a collection agency." Ysolde paced the length of the cookhouse, boots ringing. "What was the riddle? The one you stalled it with?"

Sefa had turned the riddle over in her head maybe four hundred times since she'd spoken it, mostly at night, mostly wondering how she'd ever been brave enough to say it. She said it now, out loud, and felt the floorboards listen.

"*What makes a family real?*" she said. "That's it. That's the whole riddle. Blood? It said no, blood was just an accident of geography. Duty? It laughed. Love? It said love was a word humans used to dress up hunger. And I said, fine, then you tell me, and it couldn't, and I said, *then you don't get to collect until you know what you're taking.*" She lifted her chin. "And it got *so mad*."

The ghost made a sound that in a living man would have been a laugh and in a ghost was more like wind through a keyhole. "You held a god to its own ignorance. That's the oldest trick in theology, and it usually only works once."

"It's working *less* now," Sefa said, and held up her hand, and they all watched the shadow's hand lag behind like a lazy twin.

Marguerite had gone to the corner where he did his thinking, which was anywhere with a table and no people, and had spread out a ledger and was writing in it with the fast, small strokes he used when the dead were dictating. He looked up.

"The fading," he said. "It's moving through people in order. Marda forgot you first. Then the scullery boy. Then the lamplighter on the corner, who only knew you as 'that loud child.' It's eating outward from the edges." His pen stopped. "Sefa. When did you last see your parents?"

Sefa thought about it. She thought about it honestly, which was hard, because when she reached for the memory it was like reaching into a pocket she'd sewn shut herself.

"I don't," she said slowly. "I don't remember their faces. I remember that I *had* them. I remember the word *mama* the way you remember a word in a song. But the face—" Her throat did something complicated. "I thought that was just what happened. When you're little and then you're not."

The room got very quiet. Ysolde stopped pacing. Even the floorboards held their breath, if floorboards could be said to hold anything, which in this building they could.

"It's not just collecting," Marguerite said softly. "It's been collecting for years. Every place you were shuffled through, every family that gave you back—some of that forgetting was ordinary. But not all of it." He closed the ledger. "The mark on your shadow isn't new, Sefa. It's just been *called due*."

* * *

By evening, the forgetting reached Ysolde.

Sefa knew it was happening because she came into the common room where Ysolde was cleaning her lantern—the dragon-fire lantern, the little caged coal of the old country's heart that they were supposed to be carrying to the border of unmapped memory, the whole reason any of this was happening—and Ysolde looked up at her with the polite, blank courtesy of a stranger.

"Can I help you, small person?"

"It's me. Sefa."

"Is it." Ysolde's eyes went to the lantern, to the door, back to Sefa. "And you'd be... the cookhouse girl's niece? The starling one?"

The words went into Sefa like a needle. Not because Ysolde was cruel—Ysolde was never cruel, she was flinty the way a hearth is flinty, warm only if you knew where to stand—but because Sefa had spent a year learning the exact shape of Ysolde's attention, the gruff way she said *eat something* and *tuck your shirt in* and *if a god offers you anything, the answer is no, say it twice*. Losing that attention was like losing a wall she'd been leaning on.

"It's me," she said again, and her voice cracked in the middle, which she hated, because she had decided long ago that she was the kind of person who didn't crack. "Ysolde. You carried me on your shoulders through the tollgate marshes. You taught me the prayer that isn't a prayer, the one that just says *thank you* over and over because you said the old gods can't stand gratitude, it gums up their machinery. You said I was the loudest thing you'd ever loved and you'd deny it under oath."

Ysolde's hand had stopped on the lantern. Something moved behind her eyes, deep down, like a fish under ice. "I said all that?"

"You did. You said it the night before the crossroads shrine. You said it and then you told me to stop smiling about it."

The fish under ice moved again. Ysolde looked down at her hands, at the lantern, at Sefa, and her brow folded slowly, like paper being creased the wrong way and then forced back. "I remember a marsh," she said at last, carefully, like a woman testing ice. "I remember small boots on my shoulders. I remember being *annoyed* about it and not knowing why I remember it fondly." She looked up, and her jaw set in the way Sefa had learned meant *I have decided something and the something is dangerous*. "I don't know who you are, girl. But I remember loving you, and I'm not giving that back to anybody. Sit down. Eat something."

And Sefa sat down, and ate something, and cried a little into the bread where nobody could prove it, and understood, in the way children understand things sideways and all at once, that this was the answer to the riddle. Or the start of it. *What makes a family real?* Not blood, not duty, not the word love. This. The remembering. The *refusing to stop* remembering, even when the remembering was taken away and you had to dig it back out of your own bones.

The trouble was, one person half-remembering wasn't going to beat a god. She needed all of them. And there was one left who hadn't started forgetting yet, and he was the one the forgetting would hit hardest, because Marguerite had spent his whole life not letting himself love things, and things you refuse to love are the easiest things to take.

* * *

She found him at midnight in his workroom, carving. He carved when he was frightened; she'd learned that too. The bone shavings curled off his knife like little sleeping things, and the dead murmured through his hands in their usual discontented way—*the dates are wrong, the dates are always wrong, who taught this boy bookkeeping*—and he didn't look up when she came in, but he said, "You should be asleep," which was how he said *I'm glad you're here* in Marguerite.

"It's going to take you next," she said. "It's working outward. Marda, then the lamplighter, then Ysolde. You're the last one because I met you last. That's the order. It's eating me backward through my own life."

His knife stopped. "Then we solve it tonight."

"You can't solve it alone. That's the whole point. That's what I figured out watching Ysolde." She climbed onto the stool across the workbench from him, the one she wasn't supposed to climb on, and put her elbows on the bench, which she was also not supposed to do. "It's a god, Marguerite. One person's memory isn't enough to beat a god. But the riddle—my riddle—*what makes a family real*—it can't answer it, and I couldn't answer it alone, and I think—" Her voice wobbled and she pushed it straight. "I think the answer has to be said by everybody. Together. Honestly. That's what *real* means. It means more than one person remembers you."

Marguerite set down the knife. For a long moment he looked at her, and she watched him do the thing he always did, the terrible careful arithmetic of a man who had spent sixty years owing nothing to anyone, adding up what a child was worth, and she watched him arrive at a number too large to write down.

"The master," he said. "He'll have to be included. He's family too, though he'd rather die again than hear it."

"He's standing right there," Sefa said. "He heard it. He's doing the keyhole-wind thing."

"He does that when he's pleased. It's insufferable." Marguerite stood, and blew out the lamp, and did something she had never once seen him do: he took her hand. His fingers were cool and carved-feeling, a craftsman's fingers, and they held on. "Cookhouse," he said. "Wake Ysolde. We'll need the lantern. And Sefa—" He paused at the door, and in the dark his face was mostly shadow, but his voice wasn't. "When this is done, you and I are going to have a long conversation about collateral, and gods, and why you negotiate with things that have more lawyers than you do."

"That's a *later* conversation."

"It's a *later* conversation," he agreed, "which means there has to *be* a later. Come on."

* * *

They did it in the cookhouse, because that was where the floor was thinnest between the world and the thing underneath, and because Marda's porridge pot was the biggest container of warmth in the building, and Sefa had a theory—unprovable, unshakable—that gods hated warmth.

They stood in a circle around her: Marguerite with his ledgers, Ysolde with the lantern's guttering coal casting long unsteady light, the master's ghost hovering in the middle of the group because nobody had the energy to pretend he wasn't there. Under the floorboards, the god rose like a tide. Sefa felt it in her teeth. Her shadow lay thin and late on the flagstones, and around its edges, where the lantern light touched it, it *frayed*.

*Last chance, little loudmouth,* the god said, and its voice filled the room the way cold fills a room. *Give me the doorkeeper, and I'll return what's mine and more. A family. A real one. I'll build you one from scratch. Mothers who never shuffle you off. Fathers who stay. Every loneliness answered. You know I can do it. You know what you are to them—a stray they feed out of guilt. I'm offering you what they never could.*

And here was the thing Sefa had figured out on the stool, the thing that had to be said first, because it was the keystone and she was the only one who could say it honestly: "You're offering me a family that doesn't know me. That's not a family. That's a *performance*. Marda forgets me and still cooks too much. Ysolde forgets me and still says *eat something*. That's the answer. That's the riddle. A family is the people who keep feeding you after they've forgotten why."

*Sentiment,* the god said, but the tide under the floorboards hesitated, because a riddle answered is a hook set, and it had agreed—three days ago, in its huffy, superior way—that the answer would be heard and honored.

"It's not sentiment," Marguerite said quietly. "It's testimony." And then he said the hard thing, the Marguerite thing, the thing that cost him: "I have spent my whole life making sure I owed no one and no one owed me. It was the safest way to live and it was a small, cold way to live. This child broke it in a month. She is in my ledgers now. Not as a debt. As a *line I refuse to close*."

"She is," Ysolde said, "the loudest thing I've ever loved, and I'll deny it under oath, and I said that when I couldn't even remember her face, so it counts double." The lantern coal flared at her shoulder like it was agreeing.

"She is," said the ghost, "the granddaughter I never stole, and I regret the oversight," and Marguerite made a strangled noise but did not argue.

And Sefa, standing in the middle with her thin shadow fraying in the lantern light, said the last of it, the truest of it, the part she'd been carrying since she was small enough to be shuffled: "I prayed too loud because nobody was listening. That's the whole reason. That's the ugly root of it. And you listened, and you were *worse than nobody*, and these three—" her voice went and she let it, "—these three are the answer, and you can't have them, and you can't have me, because a family isn't something you can *build from scratch*, you fraud, it's something you *refuse to forget*, and we refuse, we all refuse, we refuse out loud—"

The lantern flared.

Not guttered—*flared*, the old dragon-fire standing up in its cage like something waking, and the light went through the cookhouse, through the floorboards, down into the dark where the god was, and the god made a sound like a ledger slamming shut.

*Honored,* it said, from very far below, in a voice gone small and sulky and, Sefa thought with fierce satisfaction, *beaten*. *The riddle is answered. The collateral is... released.* And her shadow poured back into her like ink returning to a pen, whole and dark and a half-beat *early* now, if anything, and Marda came stumbling down the stairs in her nightshirt saying "Sefa? Sefa, child, why are we all standing in the kitchen at midnight, sit down, I'll make porridge, I always make too much—"

* * *

They ate porridge at one in the morning. It was, Sefa thought, the best porridge of her life, and she'd had a lot of porridge.

Ysolde kept looking at the lantern with a frown. "It burned brighter," she said. "When we answered. It's never done that." She turned the little cage in her fingers. "Dragon-fire feeds on freely given things. I told you that. Memory, mostly." Her frown deepened. "But tonight it fed on something else. Something I didn't know it could eat."

"Refusal," said the master's ghost, from the middle of the table, where he was pretending to inspect the porridge. "Refusal is a kind of gift. Given freely, held honestly." He looked up, and his faded face did something almost like worry. "We'll want to remember that. At the border. When the fire runs low and the only thing left to feed it is the thing each of us can least spare."

Marguerite set down his spoon.

"How low," he said, "are we talking?"

Ysolde looked into the lantern, and the coal looked back, and Sefa watched the color go out of Ysolde's face by degrees. "A day," she said. "Maybe two. If we walk fast and don't sleep." She looked up at Marguerite, and then at Sefa, and her voice went quiet and flat the way it did for important things. "And the shrine where the god-king found us is half a day behind us. Which means he's half a day behind *us*."

Under the floorboards, very faintly, something laughed—but it wasn't the collector. It was older, and bigger, and it was coming up the road.
