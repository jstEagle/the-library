## 16. The Last Hearth

The waystation had been a tollhouse once, back when there was a country to collect tolls for. Ysolde could read its bones the way she'd been trained to read altars: the wide hearth meant for warming travelers' hands and their horses' breath both, the iron hook where a kettle had hung for sixty years until rust ate it, the low bench worn smooth by generations of people who were tired in exactly the way Ysolde was tired now.

She set the lantern on the hearthstone with the care of a priestess handling a relic, which was what she was, technically, though her order would have flayed her for saying so.

The fire inside was down to a single coal. Not even a coal, really—a bead of light the size of a currant, sunk in ash, breathing. When it swelled, the whole ruined room filled with a warmth that had nothing to do with heat: the smell of bread baking somewhere you couldn't find, the sound of a crowd singing slightly out of tune, the feeling—Ysolde knew it intimately, had built her whole disgrace around losing it—of being *from* somewhere.

"Is it supposed to look like that?" Sefa asked, crouched at the hearth's edge with her chin nearly on the stone. "Like it's dying?"

"It's not dying," Ysolde said. "It's hungry."

Marguerite stood by the door with his ledger-key in his hand, the way he'd held it for three days straight, as if he expected someone to come take it from him. The ghost of his master hovered at his shoulder, translucent as breath on glass, wearing the expression of a man watching an apprentice make a mistake he'd made himself forty years ago.

"You knew this would happen," Marguerite said to Ysolde. It wasn't an accusation. That was the worst part about him; even now, hunted across half a dissolved nation, he delivered everything like a line item. "At the temple. The priests told you what the fire eats."

"I suspected," Ysolde said. "They confirmed it." She knelt beside the lantern. The coal pulsed again, weaker, and the smell of that unfound bread thinned almost to nothing. "I was hoping we'd reach the border before it came to this."

"And now?"

"Now it's come to this."

* * *

Here is what Ysolde had never told anyone, not the smugglers she ran cargo with, not the holdout priests who'd taken her back in with more kindness than she deserved, not the three unlikely souls currently sheltering in a ruin with her:

She had fed the fire once already.

Forty years ago, when the dissolution was fresh and the dragon-priests were scattering like sparks off an anvil, a dying acolyte had pressed the lantern into her hands and told her the truth of it—that the last flame of the heart-country could not live on wood or oil or anything so ordinary as fuel. It burned on belonging. It needed memories of home, freely given, and every memory given to it was gone forever from the one who gave.

Her teacher, Mother Vess, had chosen starvation instead. Had sat in the empty temple holding the guttering lantern and refused, on principle, to feed it, because *a flame bought with forgetting is a flame that forgets what it's for.* Ysolde had watched the old woman grow dim along with the fire, and had thought, with the cold clarity of the very young: *that is a stupid way to die.*

So when Vess finally slept, Ysolde had knelt at the lantern, cupped her hands around the little coal, and given it the only thing she had worth burning.

She had given it the memory of her mother's kitchen. The specific one—the yellow light, the smell of cardamom, her mother humming off-key while she kneaded dough, the warmth of the stove against Ysolde's bare feet. She remembered giving it. She remembered that it had mattered enormously. She remembered crying.

She did not remember the kitchen itself. Someone had told her, since, that she'd loved cardamom. It sounded right. It sounded like hearing about a stranger who shared her name.

That was the price. That was what she would ask these people to pay.

* * *

"The fire doesn't burn wood," she told them, when they'd eaten what passed for dinner—hard bread, dried plums, the last of the priests' honey scraped from its jar. "It hasn't for centuries. It burns home. Memories of home. Given freely—it can't take them, that's the one mercy in the design—and once given, they're gone. Permanently. You won't remember what you gave. You'll know you gave something. You'll feel the hole where it was. But the memory itself—" She spread her hands. "Gone."

Sefa looked up from her plum. "You've done it."

Not a question. Children asked questions; this child made observations, which was far more dangerous.

"Yes," Ysolde said.

"What was it?"

"I don't know. That's rather the point."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Does it hurt?"

"No. That's the trap of it. It feels like setting down something heavy. It feels like relief. Then later you reach for it and your hand closes on nothing, and *that* hurts, but by then it's too late to want it back." She looked at the coal, breathing its slow breath. "It has maybe a day left. Less, if the wind picks up. If it dies before we reach the border, the door can't be sealed—or opened. Either way, we lose. Everything we've walked through—the tollgate, the priests, the dead marching behind us—all of it was for a lantern that goes dark in a ruined kitchen."

Silence. The wind worried the broken shutters. Somewhere outside, faintly, bells rang for no congregation, as they had rung for three days.

"I'll do it," Sefa said immediately.

"No," said Marguerite and Ysolde, in unison, with such identical ferocity that the girl blinked.

"You're a child," Ysolde said. "Your home is the guildhall cookhouse and it's four years old. There's hardly enough there to feed a candle, let alone this."

"That's mean," Sefa said, wounded.

"It's arithmetic," said Marguerite, and Ysolde could have kissed him for it, which was a disturbing development she filed away for later examination.

* * *

The ghost spoke first, after the silence had gone on long enough to develop opinions.

"I should tell them what I know of it," Master Corvin said. His voice was like paper turning pages. "When I was young—before you, Marguerite, long before—I carried this same lantern a mile. One mile. The priest who owned it fell ill, and I volunteered, thinking it an honor." He drifted closer to the hearth, and the coal brightened faintly, the way a dog lifts its head at a familiar footstep. "It recognized me. It wanted. And I understood what it wanted, and I was a coward, and I gave it nothing, and I have regretted it for sixty years in ways I could not name until tonight."

"You're dead," Marguerite said gently. "What home would you even give it?"

"A man keeps his home after death. That's most of what death *is*, for the lingering kind." The ghost's smile was thin and sad. "My mother's garden. The first room that was ever mine alone. Forty years of evenings in the guildhall, listening to you scratch your pen and pretend you couldn't hear me talking to you." He looked at his student. "I am owed nothing and owe everyone. Let me be the first to pay."

"No," Marguerite said.

"Marguerite—"

"You've lost enough. Whatever's left of you, you don't get to spend it on my account." His voice cracked on *my account*, and Ysolde saw him hear it crack, and hate it, and continue anyway. "Find another way to be noble. You're a ghost; you have eternity."

"I have," Corvin said softly, "rather less than that, actually, and less still if that door opens. But we'll set that aside, since you're being dramatic." He turned to Ysolde. "How much does it need? Precisely."

"One memory each," Ysolde said. "From each of us who means to cross. Four travelers, four memories. It's not about quantity—it's about the fire knowing us. A flame of the heart-country won't carry strangers. It has to have tasted our homes. Our belonging. Then it will burn for us all the way to the border and give itself up sealing the door."

"And if someone refuses?" Sefa asked.

"Then the rest of us feed it double-deep, and the ones who gave lose more than they planned. Or we turn back." Ysolde met each of their eyes in turn. "No one has to do this. I want that said plainly, aloud, witnessed. This isn't a debt and it isn't a duty. The fire takes gifts and nothing else. If any of you would rather keep what's yours, say so now and I'll think of something. I always think of something. It's my worst quality."

* * *

Sefa went first, because of course she did.

"I want to," she said, before anyone could arrange a vote about it. "And I know what you'll say, so don't say it." She planted herself in front of the hearth like a small general. "Everyone thinks I don't have a real home because I'm an orphan and the cookhouse is new. But I *do*. I know exactly which one I'm giving, and I've thought about it, and it's fair."

"Sefa—" Marguerite began.

"My home is the night you found me," she said, "sleeping in the guildhall doorway during the frost. Before that I slept in doorways all over the city and none of them were home, they were just places it didn't rain. But that doorway was warm because the guildhall breathed heat through the cracks, and in the morning you came out and looked at me like I was a problem, and then you brought me soup anyway, and you grumbled the whole time about how the cookhouse wasn't a charity, and I decided right then that wherever you lived was where I lived." Her chin trembled slightly. She ignored it with tremendous discipline. "That's my home. The doorway and the soup and the grumbling. And I'm giving it to the fire, because a home you keep secret isn't doing its job, and because—" she jabbed a finger at the coal— "*you* are going to carry me to the border and keep me safe, and that's what homes are *for*."

The coal flared. Light poured up out of the lantern like water finding its level, gold and deep, and for one moment the ruined waystation was full of the smell of soup—poor soup, grumbled-over soup, the best soup in any world—and Sefa's face went slack with the particular peace of someone setting down a weight they'd forgotten they were carrying.

Then it was done. She sat back on her heels, blinking.

"Huh," she said. "I feel lighter. Is that bad? Everyone looks upset. Why does everyone look upset?"

"Because we remember it," Ysolde said quietly, "and you don't."

"Well, that's silly of you." Sefa crawled over to Marguerite and leaned against his side, entirely without permission, entirely without being pushed away. "Tell me later what I gave. I'll probably cry about it. I'd like to know why."

* * *

Ysolde went second, because she would not ask of others what she hadn't paid herself, and because she wanted to choose before fear chose for her.

She knelt at the hearth and let the fire look at her, and it looked back, patient as a bank.

"I gave it my mother's kitchen, forty years ago," she said aloud, for the record, for the witnesses. "Tonight I give it the temple. My order's temple, before I disgraced myself. Dawn services, the smell of incense and scale-oil, the dragons humming in their sleep under the mountain, Mother Vess's voice going through the liturgy like a river going through stones. All of it. Every morning of it." Her throat tightened. "I spent forty years being ashamed of leaving. Apparently I kept the shame and threw away the thing I was ashamed of leaving. That's a very priestly trick and I'm done performing it."

She cupped her hands over the coal, and gave.

The light took it the way the sea takes a dropped coin—swiftly, totally, without malice. Warmth flooded the room. For a moment Ysolde heard dragons humming, felt stone under her knees, smelled incense—

—and then it was gone, and she was kneeling in a ruin next to a stranger's hearth, weeping without knowing for what, knowing only that she had paid, and that the payment had been *worth it*, and that she would spend the rest of her life reaching for the shape of the hole in her chest and finding only its outline.

"Ah," she said, wiping her face. "Yes. That. I'd forgotten how much that part hurts."

"You hadn't," said the ghost. "You couldn't have. Now you can."

"Thank you, Master Corvin, you're a great comfort."

"I'm told it's my function."

* * *

Corvin tried to go third. Marguerite physically stepped between the ghost and the hearth, which Ysolde suspected was the first time in recorded history a man had blocked a spirit with his body and expected it to work.

It worked, mostly because the ghost stopped out of courtesy.

"We can argue about this," Corvin said, "or you can notice that your hands are shaking and decide whether you want to give the fire your memory while you're like this, or after you've calmed down and thought clearly about what you're about to lose."

"I'm not giving it anything," Marguerite said. "There has to be another way. There's always another way; I've spent my life finding them. We could—"

"Marguerite." Ysolde kept her voice level. "I've watched you for two hundred miles. You audit everything. You found the hidden road, you negotiated with a bureaucratic tollgate, you out-bookkept a god-king. So audit this. What are the options?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Ysolde could practically see the ledger behind his eyes, columns filling and striking themselves out.

"There isn't one," he said at last, in the voice of a man reading his own bankruptcy. "Four memories or the fire dies. The fire dies and the door stays unsealed, and the god-king opens it himself, and every debt comes due at once." He looked down at his hands. They had gone still. That was somehow worse. "I know the arithmetic. I've known it since the temple. I was hoping someone would invent a fifth option before we got here."

"People die waiting for fifth options," Ysolde said. "Ask any priest."

* * *

He knelt at the hearth a long time before he spoke.

Ysolde watched him and understood, suddenly, completely, why the man had spent thirty years making himself invisible. It wasn't cowardice. It was inventory. He had so few things that were truly his—one sigil, one ghost, one habitually loud child, one ledger—and he guarded them the way misers guard coins, not from greed but because he knew exactly what each one cost and exactly how irreplaceable it was.

"I only have one," he said finally. "One true memory of kindness from him. Everything else is ledgers and lessons and corrections. He was hard because the world was hard and he was trying to armor me before I got hurt. I understand that now. I forgave it years ago. But there's this one—" His voice did something Ysolde had never heard it do: it simply stopped, mid-word, and he had to stand there breathing until it restarted. "There's this one evening. I was nineteen. I'd failed at something—not badly, just failed, the ordinary human amount—and I was sitting in the vaults feeling sorry for myself, and he came down and didn't lecture me. He sat down next to me and said, 'My father told me once that a man who has never failed at anything has never attempted anything either, and I have decided to believe him, because the alternative reflects poorly on both of us.' And then he produced two cups of terrible wine from absolutely nowhere, and we drank it, and he told me about his own failures until midnight, and I laughed until my ribs hurt, and I thought: *this is what having a father is.*"

The coal was pulsing fast now, leaning toward him like a plant toward sun.

"He's standing right there," Marguerite said. "He's been dead twenty years and he's standing right there, and I can talk to him, and if I give this away I'll look at him tomorrow and feel nothing but the shape of a hole. And he'll know. He'll know what I paid and what it bought and he'll watch me not-remember, and that's—" He laughed, wet and awful. "That's the cruelest part. He gets to keep it. He gets to remember being kind to me, and I get to be a stranger he was kind to once."

"Marguerite," the ghost said.

"Don't. Don't tell me it's all right. If you tell me it's all right I'll believe you, because I always believe you, and then I'll do it, and I don't trust my judgment when you're being gentle. You taught me that too."

"Then I won't say it's all right." The ghost drifted close, close enough that his translucence caught the coal-light. "I'll say this instead. You are about to hand a piece of yourself into a fire so that a child you've known four years and a woman you've known three weeks can reach safety. That is the single most Corvin thing you have ever done, and I include the time you forged my signature to stop the guild selling the winter stores." His voice frayed. "I have spent twenty years wondering if I armored you too well or not enough. Tonight I have my answer. Give it the wine, boy. Give it the terrible wine and the laughing. Some things aren't diminished by being spent. Some things are only finished when they're spent."

Marguerite bowed his head.

Then he cupped his hands over the coal, and gave.

* * *

The light that rose out of the lantern then was unlike the others. It was amber and low and it smelled—Ysolde would swear this to her dying day—of terrible wine, and for a moment the ruined waystation was warm in the specific way of a vault at midnight, two cups deep into a conversation that mattered, and everyone in the room felt, briefly, utterly, *at home*, though not one of them could have said whose home.

When it faded, the coal burned steady and strong, a proper flame now, tall and gold in its lantern, fed by four hearths it would carry to the border and beyond.

Marguerite knelt where he was, head bowed, hands flat on the cold stone. After a moment Sefa wriggled under his arm the way she did, proprietary as a cat.

"Do you remember it?" she asked him.

"No," he said.

"Does it hurt?"

"Yes."

"Good," she said, with ten-year-old logic that Ysolde, unexpectedly, found herself agreeing with. "It should. Things that don't hurt to give weren't worth much." She patted his ribs twice, a banker confirming a deposit. "I'll remember it for you. Both of you. I'm very good at remembering things; it's my best quality after praying."

"That's a horrifying offer," Marguerite said, "and I accept it."

* * *

Later, when the others slept—Sefa against Marguerite's shoulder, the ghost keeping a sentry's vigil he no longer strictly needed—Ysolde sat by the lantern and watched the flame burn tall and steady on its meal of other people's belonging.

She reached, experimentally, for her mother's kitchen. Found the outline. Felt the ache fit itself to the ache already there, the temple-shaped one, until she was mostly hollow in the middle, held together by duty and spite and a flame that hummed faintly, contentedly, like a dragon after feeding.

Worth it, she told herself, and was surprised to find she believed it.

She was still telling herself so when Marguerite sat up sharply in the dark, one hand flying to the ledger-key at his belt—which had begun, very softly, to glow.

"It's writing," he said. "The key. Something's coming through the ledgers." He looked up at her, and the firelight showed her his face, and she saw that whatever the key was telling him, it had drained every ounce of the night's hard-won peace clean out of him. "Ysolde. The god-king knows where we are. He's known since the fire was fed—he says the flame called to him like a bell. And he says—" Marguerite swallowed. "He says he's bringing the debts to us. All of them. Tonight."
