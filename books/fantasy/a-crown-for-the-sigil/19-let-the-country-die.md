## 19. Let the Country Die

The god-king's creditors did not shout at him. That was the remarkable thing. They simply stood in the great hall of his half-remembered palace—streets that had pooled up out of unmapped memory like water finding its old channels—and presented their claims in order of seniority.

Marguerite had spent forty years keeping ledgers. He knew what a queue looked like when it meant business.

The dead came forward one by one. A woman who had been promised a pension by a nation that sold its own borders before she could collect it. A soldier whose anthem had been traded away mid-verse, so that he had died not knowing how the song ended. A whole choir of schoolchildren who had been assured, in writing, that their town would always be there, and whose town was now a clause in a contract nobody could find.

The god-king stood on his throne-dais, and he was magnificent, and he was coming apart. Ceremony is a kind of cloth, Marguerite thought, watching the man's edges fray into procession and incense-smoke and the memory of trumpets. And cloth can be repossessed.

"You see," said the ghost of Master Corvane, drifting at Marguerite's shoulder with the particular satisfaction of an accountant watching arithmetic resolve itself, "a kingdom is just a debt structure with flags."

"I know," Marguerite said. "I've been keeping its books for years without knowing it."

* * *

It took until well past what would have been midnight, if this place had kept honest hours, for the hall to empty. The dead filed out through doors that hadn't existed an hour ago, going—to rest, to whatever waited past the last page of a ledger. Some of them paused to look at Marguerite as they passed. One old woman pressed something into his hand: a button, brass, worn smooth. He didn't ask what it had belonged to. Some debts you carry without itemizing.

And then it was quiet, and there were four of them left in the throne room of a country that no longer legally existed: himself, Ysolde, Sefa, and a ghost, standing before a door shaped exactly like his ledger-key.

The door was small. That was the thing nobody warned you about. All this—the palace, the god-king, the sixty years of haunted aftermath—and the door itself was barely taller than Sefa, set into a wall of pale stone, with a keyhole that fit the sigil hanging against Marguerite's chest like a second, heavier heart.

"Well," said Ysolde. She had her arms crossed and her priestess posture on, which meant she was frightened and refusing to let it show. "Two options, then. We open it, the kingdom comes back, everyone gets their country restored, and—" she gestured at the empty hall, at the fading smell of incense, "—all of *that* comes due. In full. Forever."

"Or?" said Sefa.

"Or we spend the sigil," Marguerite said. "Seal the door. Let the fire do what fire does to paper." His hand found the lantern at his belt. Inside it, the last dragon-fire burned down to a single stubborn coal, guttering like a candle in a draft, like a heartbeat someone was deciding whether to keep.

"Let the *country* die," Sefa said quietly.

"Yes."

* * *

They sat down on the steps of the dais to argue about it, because Marguerite insisted. If you're going to make a choice this large, you make it sitting down, with everyone heard, and you write it in the record. That was guild doctrine. It had gotten him through every hard decision of his life, mostly by ensuring he never made any.

"The dead wanted a country," Ysolde said. "You heard them. Sixty years of petitioning. Doesn't sealing the door cheat them?"

"They wanted what they were *promised*," Marguerite said. "A home. A future. Their names remembered. The god-king offered them resurrection instead, because resurrection is cheaper—it costs him nothing but ceremony, and it puts them right back where they started, holding IOUs from an institution that has already defaulted once." He turned the brass button over in his fingers. "Restoration isn't payment. Restoration is refinancing."

"That's very cold," said Sefa.

"It's very true," said Master Corvane's ghost, and there was something in his voice Marguerite had never heard before—not the dry correction, not the fond exasperation. Something like pride, arriving too late to be enjoyed properly. "I taught him that. I also taught him never to be seen, never to be owed, never to choose. The first two lessons were good ones. The third was cowardice wearing a ledger's coat."

"Master—"

"Don't interrupt an apology, boy, they're rare enough as it is." The ghost settled beside him on the step, translucent hands folded. "Here is what I should have told you years ago. An inheritance is not a weight you're handed. It's a question you're asked. *What will you do with what you were given?* Most people never answer. They just carry the question around until they die, and hand it to someone else, still sealed."

Marguerite looked at the sigil in his lap. Warm. Patient. Shaped like a key and heavy like a verdict.

"And if my answer is to end it? To let it all go dark?"

"Then," said his master, "you will be the first Voss in living memory to pay a debt in full instead of passing it down. And I think—" the ghost's voice caught, or did the ghostly equivalent, a flicker, a thinning, "—I think the dead will finally be able to sleep."

* * *

Sefa was the one who raised the objection that mattered.

"But it's *home*," she said. She was hugging her knees, ten years old and fierce-eyed, and Marguerite felt the objection land somewhere under his ribs. "Not for me—I never lived here. But for them. For Ysolde. You'd be burning the only copy."

Ysolde was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the cadence of liturgy in it, the old dragon-liturgy, the one that got her disgraced.

"When I was a girl," she said, "the temple taught us that a flame is alive. That letting it gutters is killing it. I believed that so hard I stole the last dragon-fire and ran, and I have carried it across three borders and forty bad roads, feeding it, protecting it, terrified of being the priestess who let the sacred flame die." She looked at the lantern. "But fire isn't alive, child. Fire is *generous*. It exists only by giving itself away. The moment it stops giving, it's just heat and light with nowhere to go." She smiled, tired and crooked. "Maybe the kindest thing you can do for a flame is let it finish what it was burning for."

"And what was it burning for?" Sefa asked.

"To keep a promise," Marguerite said. "That's all any of this ever was. A country is a promise people make each other. This one broke. You don't honor a broken promise by pretending it still stands. You honor it by telling the truth about it, and paying what you honestly owe, and letting the rest go."

He stood up. His knees cracked. Forty years of careful invisibility, and now his whole body announced him at every movement, as if it had been waiting for permission.

"All right," he said. "Then we need witnesses. A seal means nothing without a record."

* * *

The dead came back when he called them. Not all of them—he hadn't expected all of them—but hundreds, filling the hall again, silent, patient, the way creditors are patient when they know the debtor has finally come to the table.

Marguerite opened his ledger on the throne-dais steps and knelt, which put him below most of them, which felt correct.

"I'm not going to restore your country," he said. His voice carried further than it should have; the hall was built for proclamations, and it took his plain bookkeeper's voice and gave it stone acoustics. "I'm not going to bring it back. What was sold was sold, and buying it back with interest would only put you in debt to it again, forever, and call that salvation."

Murmuring moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. He pressed on.

"What I can give you is this: the truth, entered into the record. Your claims are valid. Your losses are real. Your country failed you—not the other way around. And when I seal this door, you will not be trapped behind it, waiting, hoping, haunting. You will be *paid*. In rest. In release. In the one currency that can't be devalued." He swallowed. "I know it isn't what you were promised. But it is everything I honestly have, and I am offering all of it."

Silence. Long enough that Marguerite began composing his own epitaph in his head—*he balanced the books, badly, at the end*—and then the woman with the pension claim stepped forward, the same one who'd given him the button.

"My name was Ilvetta Sorrows," she said. "Nobody has spoken it in fifty-one years." She looked around at the assembled dead. "If we take his offer, none of us get our country back. But if we refuse it, none of us get to stop waiting either." She turned to Marguerite, and her eyes were the eyes of every client he'd ever served, tired and hopeful and done with being managed. "Carver. Do you swear it? On your sigil, on your guild, on whatever you love?"

Marguerite thought of a cookhouse smelling of cinnamon. Of a smuggler sharing watch. Of a ghost who had lied to him for forty years to keep him safe, and a child who prayed too loudly and loved without art.

"On all of it," he said. "I swear."

Ilvetta Sorrows nodded once. And then, one by one, the dead began to kneel—not to him, he realized, but toward the door, the way you face Mecca, the way you face the altar. Consent, given freely, by a nation's worth of ghosts.

"Right," said Marguerite, and his voice shook, and he let it. "Then let's settle accounts."

* * *

The key fit the way it had always been going to fit, because it had been shaped for this lock before Marguerite was born, before his master was born, back when the guild first decided that survival was worth any price and never read the fine print.

He knelt at the little door with the lantern open beside him. The coal inside had gone from orange to a deep, determined red—the color of a thing that intends to finish.

"Marguerite." Ysolde crouched beside him. "Once the fire touches the mechanism, there's no un-burning it. No appeal. You understand that? The guild will lose its founding asset. You'll lose—" she touched the sigil at his throat, gently, "—whatever this has been to you. Your invisibility. Your armor."

"I know."

"You'll be *visible*, Marguerite. Permanently. To everyone. Every debt you ever dodged, every name you never gave, all of it comes due the moment this door seals, because the seal has to be paid for, and the only currency you have left is yourself."

He almost laughed. Almost. "Ysolde. I've spent my whole life being nobody. Do you know what it's like? Being nobody is a full-time position with no benefits. I'm *tired*." He looked at her, and found her smiling at him, fierce and sad. "Besides. Somebody has to sign for the delivery."

"Sefa," he called. "Bring the record."

She brought the ledger, and a pen, and stood beside him while he wrote the final entry aloud, because some things must be witnessed:

"*Entry the last. Received: one nation, dissolved, in poor condition, sixty years in arrears. Paid out: one door, sealed; one fire, spent; one inheritance, discharged in full. Remaining balance: nothing. Nothing at all.*" He hesitated, pen hovering. Then, in the space marked *settled by*, he wrote his name. His whole name. *Marguerite Voss.* The letters looked strange under his hand, like a stranger's handwriting, like a door opening.

"Oh," said Sefa softly, reading upside down. "Oh, that's *you*."

"That's me," Marguerite agreed, and turned the key.

* * *

The door opened inward, the way honest doors do, onto darkness—not the darkness of a cell but the darkness behind your eyelids, the darkness of finished sleep. And Marguerite lifted the lantern and tipped the last coal of the last dragon-fire through the doorway, into the dark, and spoke the words Ysolde taught him, the old closing words, the ones that mean roughly *go home, go to rest, the watch is ended.*

The fire went out in his hands.

Not with a roar. With a sigh. The way a tired person lies down. The light traveled once, briefly, through the gathered dead—touching Ilvetta Sorrows' face, touching the soldier who finally heard how the anthem ended, touching ten thousand others, a benediction written in the only ink that mattered—and then the door closed, and the sigil in the lock crumbled to ash, and the ash blew away, and the wall was just a wall.

The hall began to fade around them, streets unpooling, songs unwinding, the palace remembering itself back into possibility. Marguerite knelt on the steps with an empty lantern and an empty ledger and an emptiness in his chest that hurt in a way he suspected was the good kind, the way a healed wound itches.

Master Corvane's ghost stood at the top of the steps, thinner now, more suggestion than presence.

"You could stay," Marguerite said. "The door's shut, but—you could stay. Haunt the guildhall. Complain about my margins."

His master smiled, and for once it wasn't wry. "And miss whatever comes next? Boy, I spent two lifetimes teaching you to hide from the world. I'd like to stick around long enough to find out what you do with it." He began to thin at the edges, unraveling into something gentler than smoke. "Balance your books. Feed the child. And Marguerite—"

"Master?"

"Your name suits you. It always did. I'm sorry it took me this long to say so."

And he was gone, cleanly, the way accounts close when both parties are satisfied.

* * *

They walked out of the fading country together through streets that were already mostly memory, Sefa holding one of Marguerite's hands and, after visible deliberation, one of Ysolde's, so that neither adult could pretend they weren't a unit. Behind them the last bell rang—for no one, for everyone, for an ending done properly.

Ahead, at the border of the possible, the ordinary world waited: gray morning, real weather, a road that appeared on maps.

"Guildhall's a week's walk," Ysolde said. "The cookhouse will need airing. Half the membership will want to know what happened to the founding asset, and the other half will want to sell *you*."

"Mm," said Marguerite. He flexed his empty hands. They felt strange—light, unarmored, *visible*. Somewhere out there, every debt he'd ever dodged was waking up and looking for him, and for the first time in forty years, he found he didn't mind being findable.

Then Sefa stopped walking.

"Um," she said, in the small, careful voice she used when a god was nearby, or when she'd broken something expensive. "Marguerite? There's a letter on the road."

There was. Lying in the middle of the border-stones, addressed in handwriting he knew better than his own, though the hand that wrote it had been dust for thirty years and a ghost for twenty more:

*To M. Voss, Guild of Impossible Things — personal, urgent, and posthumous.*

"Master Corvane said goodbye," Marguerite said slowly. "I watched him go."

"Then who," asked Ysolde, "has been mailing letters ahead of you?"

Marguerite picked it up. The wax seal was intact. The sigil stamped in it was one he had never seen before—and yet his thumb fit its groove exactly, the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

He broke the seal.
