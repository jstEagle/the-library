## 20. Ashes on the Ledger

Sefa had a theory about the road home, and the theory was this: the world had been washed.

Not scrubbed, exactly. Not like Cook scrubbed the guildhall pots, all elbows and fury and lye-water to the wrists. More like the way rain washed a window — you didn't notice how dirty it had been until you could suddenly see the street through it, clear as glass.

She tested the theory every mile of the way.

At the first village they passed through — a place called Little Harrow that Sefa was fairly sure hadn't existed last month, or at least hadn't existed *confidently* — she knelt at the well and prayed out loud, the way she always prayed, loud enough to be heard by anything listening in the general vicinity of the sky.

*Please let there be bread,* she said. *Warm bread. And maybe honey.*

Nothing happened.

No god leaned down. No floorboards rumbled. No ancient hunger stirred in the dark under things and offered her a family in exchange for her shadow, which, honestly, rude of them not even to try, but also fine.

Instead, a woman came out of the nearest house carrying a tray of rolls, fresh from the oven, and said, "You look half-starved, child. Here. Take two. Take four, you're skin and bone."

"Thank you," Sefa said, and then, because honesty mattered: "I prayed for these."

"I know," the woman said placidly, and went back inside.

Sefa chewed on that along with the roll. It wasn't that prayers went unanswered now. It was that they were answered by *people*. By ovens and neighbors and women who kept trays of bread near windows on the off chance someone hungry walked past. The gods had gone quiet again — kindly quiet, the quiet of a parent stepping out of the room so the children could sort their own argument — and what rushed into the silence was ordinary kindness, which turned out to have been there all along, waiting its turn.

"It's better this way," Ysolde said, when Sefa reported her findings that evening over a cookfire. "Believe me. I spent forty years serving something that answered."

"But don't you miss it?" Sefa asked. "Being heard by something enormous?"

Ysolde considered the fire for a long moment. She'd been doing that a lot since the border — looking at flames, touching the cold lantern where the last dragon-fire used to live, carrying it now empty the way other people carried ashes of the dead. Which, Sefa supposed, was exactly what it was.

"When you're small," Ysolde said finally, "and someone enormous hears you, it feels like the most important thing in the world." She looked up, and her eyes caught the firelight. "Then you grow up and realize being heard isn't the same as being loved. Enormous things hear everyone. It's the small ones who listen."

Marguerite, sitting on his bedroll with the ledgers spread around him like a fortress, made a sound that might have been agreement or might have been indigestion. He'd been making a lot of those sounds lately. Since the door sealed. Since the fire went out in his hands and he'd stood there in the dark holding nothing, absolutely nothing, no sigil, no debt, no invisibility — just himself, suddenly visible to every eye on the road, flinching like a man expecting a blow that never landed.

People saw him now. Sefa watched them do it. Farmers nodded to him. Toll-keepers remembered him. A child in Little Harrow had pointed and said, "That man has kind hands," which was true but also strange, because how would a stranger know that?

Marguerite hated it. Sefa could tell he hated it, the way he straightened his collar whenever anyone looked directly at him, the way he angled his shoulders away from crowds. But she'd also noticed — because noticing people was her one real talent, honed by years of watching doors to see if anyone was coming back for her — that he'd stopped checking whether anyone was following him.

For twenty years, apparently, he'd checked. Every corner, every reflection, every shadow. Now he just walked, and the not-checking sat on him like a coat that still fit wrong but fit.

"You're staring," Marguerite said, without looking up from the ledger.

"You're writing your name," Sefa said.

He paused. Looked down at the page, where *M. Voss* sat in his careful clerk's hand — initials only, the way he'd signed everything for as long as Sefa had known him, which was only weeks but felt longer, the way important things did.

"Habit," he said.

"You wrote the whole thing yesterday. In the toll-book at Ferrow's Crossing. *Marguerite Voss*, big as anything. The toll-man read it out loud."

"He read it out loud," Marguerite repeated, in the tone of a man cataloguing a fresh grievance against the universe.

"And then he gave you directions to the good inn," Sefa said, "and told you about his daughter's wedding, and asked if you'd bless the marriage, because he thought you were a priest, because you have *kind hands*—"

"That was a misunderstanding."

"You blessed them anyway."

Marguerite's ears went pink. "It seemed rude not to."

Sefa grinned and lay back on her bedroll, full of stolen bread and victory, and listened to the scratch of his pen. Above them the stars came out, ordinary stars, answering nobody, shining on everybody, which struck her as the correct arrangement of things.

* * *

The guildhall smelled different when they got home.

Sefa noticed it before she even reached the door — standing on the cobbles of Undertaker's Row with her hand on the gate, breathing in. For years the guildhall had smelled the way old paper smells, and dust, and under that something colder, like the air in a room where a conversation stopped when you entered. Now it smelled like soup.

"Cook's been busy," Ysolde said.

Cook had been more than busy. Cook had, in the intervening weeks, apparently decided that if the Guild of Impossible Things was going to keep existing, it was going to exist *well-fed*, and had expanded operations accordingly. There were tables in the courtyard now. There were people at the tables — actual living people, petitioners and pensioners and a surprising number of children, eating stew and arguing about the weather.

"What happened?" Sefa whispered.

"The guild trades in possible things now," Marguerite said. He sounded dazed. "I left instructions. I didn't think they'd—" He stopped. A woman at the nearest table waved at him cheerfully, and he waved back, stiffly, like a puppet learning the motion. "Hello, Marta."

"Marta's here about a disputed inheritance," he explained to Sefa, in a low voice. "A perfectly ordinary disputed inheritance. There's a will. There are lawyers. There is nothing impossible about it whatsoever."

"So why come here?"

"Because we're cheap," said a voice from the doorway, "and because Marta's my cousin, and because somebody has to test the new ledger system, and you weren't here."

The speaker was a round, brisk woman with ink-stained fingers and Cook's exact frown, wearing an apprentice's sash that Sefa was quite sure had never existed before. She looked Sefa up and down with frank appraisal.

"So you're the girl," she said.

"I'm lots of girls," Sefa said cautiously. "Which one?"

"The one who prays too loudly. The one who stalled a god with a riddle. The one whose shadow got called due and won back." The woman ticked each item off on her fingers. "Cook talks about you constantly. You've been canonized, more or less. There's a soup named after you."

"There's a *soup*?"

"The Shadow-Won-Back. It's got beans in it. Don't ask me the connection; Cook works intuitively."

This, Sefa learned, was Perrin — Cook's niece, hired during the reshuffling, possessed of strong opinions about margins and stronger ones about punctuation. She marched them inside, past the cookhouse (loud, fragrant, full), past the front hall (the great ledger-desk now faced the *door*, so visitors could be greeted instead of processed), and up the stairs to the scriptorium, where the new ledgers waited.

They were beautiful. Sefa didn't care about ledgers, particularly, but even she could see these were beautiful — cream paper, generous margins, and on the first page of each, written in a fair copy of Marguerite's own hand:

*The Guild of Impossible Things. We no longer trade in miracles. We keep accounts of debts unpaid, promises broken, and griefs unrecorded, and we hold them safe until someone comes to settle them. Nothing is owed forever.*

"Where did you learn to write like that?" Sefa asked.

"I didn't," Marguerite said quietly. "It wrote itself. Once. Just once, after the door sealed, to set down the founding words. Then it stopped." He touched the page the way other people touched relics. "I've checked every night since. Ink stays put. It just... remembers what it's for."

Perrin cleared her throat. "Speaking of which. There's mail."

"Mail?"

"A letter came for you. While you were away." Perrin's briskness faltered, just slightly, and Sefa saw that underneath the efficiency she was nervous. "It's the second one."

Marguerite went very still. "The second one."

"Same handwriting. Same seal. Addressed the same way." Perrin produced it from her apron pocket, holding it at arm's length like something that might bite. "*To M. Voss, personal, urgent, and posthumous.*"

Sefa watched Marguerite take it. Watched his thumb find the groove in the wax seal — that unfamiliar sigil that fit him like a key fits the lock it was cut for — and watched him not break it.

"Not yet," he said, and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where Sefa noticed he'd been keeping the first one too. "Tonight. Tonight I'll read it tonight."

"You said that about the first one," Perrin said. "Three weeks ago."

"Yes," said Marguerite. "And I meant it then, too."

* * *

The temple took some finding, because it wasn't in the city anymore.

Ysolde had announced, somewhere around the third day home, that she was going back to the holdout priests' order — the one that had cast her out, the one whose empty temples she'd smuggled past for forty years — and Sefa had assumed she meant to argue with them. Possibly set something on fire. Ysolde had a way of walking when she talked about her old order that suggested arson was never entirely off the table.

But when Sefa tagged along (nobody stopped her tagging along anymore; it had been formally established that she belonged to the company, though the paperwork on this consisted of Marguerite saying "oh, *fine*" in a resigned voice), the argument never happened.

What happened instead was this: they arrived at the temple of the Flame Eternal — the one the holdout priests had tended for forty years, sweeping floors for a dragon that was dead and lighting lamps for a country that was gone — and Ysolde stood in the doorway with the empty lantern in her hands, and the oldest priest looked at her, and neither of them said anything for a very long time.

Then the old priest said, "We heard the fire went out."

"It did," Ysolde said. "Freely given. Fed on memories of home, all the way to the border. It sealed the door." Her chin came up, defiant, braced for judgment. "Your forty years of tending — it worked. That's what it was *for*. You kept the flame alive long enough to die properly."

The old priest's face did something complicated. Then he laughed — a rusty sound, like a hinge unused for decades — and said, "Forty years of sweeping, and the punchline is that we were hospice workers." He wiped his eyes. "Come in, apostate. The benches need moving and my knees are done with it."

That was how it started.

It shouldn't have worked. Sefa knew it shouldn't have. The temple was empty, the god was dead, the flame was ash — there was nothing left to worship, and worship was the whole business of temples. But word got around, the way word does, especially when Cook's niece is related to half the city's clerks. People came. First out of curiosity. Then out of habit. Then — and this was the part that made Sefa's chest go tight and funny — out of want.

Because Ysolde didn't preach. She'd tried it once, the first week, standing at the altar with forty years of liturgy behind her teeth, and she'd gotten three sentences in before her voice cracked and she'd thrown the whole service away and just... talked. About the flame. About feeding it memories. About how the worst thing she'd ever done was love something enormous, and the best thing she'd ever done was carry its last light to the border and let it rest.

"People kept asking me what they should pray to now," she told Sefa afterward, on the temple steps, while congregants streamed out around them nodding goodbye. "So I told them: pray to each other. Pray *at* each other. It turns out that's mostly what prayer ever was. The gods were just eavesdropping."

"Do you miss it?" Sefa asked. "The enormity."

"Every day," Ysolde said, without hesitation. "And then someone brings me their grief to hold, small and ordinary and real, and I remember which one I'd rather have." She bumped Sefa's shoulder with hers. "You'll understand when you're older."

"I'm ten."

"Then you've got time to practice."

Sefa practiced. She practiced on the walk home, composing sermons in her head that were mostly jokes. She practiced at dinner, where Cook had instituted a ritual of saying one thing you were grateful for, and Sefa always said the soup, and Cook always pretended to be offended, and everyone always knew she wasn't.

And she practiced, most seriously, in the scriptorium, three evenings a week, where Marguerite taught her the craft of the ledger.

It was not what she'd expected. She'd expected numbers. It was mostly listening.

"A debt entry," Marguerite said, "is a promise that someone was failed. Your job is not to judge the failure. Your job is to write it down truly enough that whoever reads it later can feel what it cost. Can you do that?"

"Can I write down what it feels like when nobody comes back for you at the orphanage gate?" Sefa said. "Yes. Extremely yes."

Something crossed Marguerite's face — quick, quickly mastered — and he said, "Then you'll do," in a gruff voice that fooled absolutely no one, least of all Sefa, who had watched him secretly commission a smaller chair for the scriptorium desk within the week.

Her first solo entries were small ones. A widow's debt of gratitude to a neighbor, recorded so it wouldn't be forgotten. A boy who'd promised his dying father he'd finish a boat, logged with a note that the guild held the promise safe until he was ready. Small debts. Human-sized ones. The kind that used to get eaten by gods and lost.

"This is better," Sefa said one evening, pen scratching, lamplight warm. "Than the old way. Isn't it?"

Marguerite was quiet long enough that she looked up.

He had the letter out. The second one. He was holding it the way he held everything precious — loosely, ready to put it down, as if wanting things too firmly was how you lost them.

"I don't know yet," he said. "There's a question I haven't answered. The letters keep asking it." His thumb moved over the intact seal. "And I'm beginning to suspect that whoever's writing them already knows my answer, and is simply waiting for me to say it out loud."

"What's the question?"

Marguerite looked at her — really looked, the way people could do now that seeing him didn't cost anything — and smiled, tired and rueful and a little afraid.

"*Who inherits the doorkeeper's office,*" he said, "when the door is sealed and the office refuses to die?"

Outside, the evening bells rang — ordinary bells, rung by ordinary hands, for no reason except that the hour had turned. And on Marguerite's desk, in the ledger that remembered what it was for, the ink stirred.

Just one line. Fresh, black, in that handwriting he knew better than his own:

*Ask the girl.*
