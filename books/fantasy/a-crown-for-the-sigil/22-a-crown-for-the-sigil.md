## 22. A Crown for the Sigil

The guildhall smelled of beeswax and sawdust on the morning Marguerite Voss finished the crown.

Not a king's crown—he wanted that understood from the first cut of the saw. He had carved kings' crowns before, or rather he had carved the bones of men who had worn them, and there was nothing in that work he cared to repeat. This crown was smaller, humbler, and it had no points at all. It was a ring of bone with a flat base, weighted along its lower edge so that whatever it held down, it would hold honestly.

A doorstop's crown. Which was, when you thought about it, the only crown worth making.

He worked at the long table by the eastern window, where the light came in thick and golden with dust motes, and around him the guildhall went about its morning business—which these days meant possible things. That had been the great reformation, the one nobody had planned and everybody now pretended they had always intended: the Guild of Impossible Things had quietly become a guild of very difficult but achievable things. Lost rings found. Estranged sisters introduced to one another over soup. A widow's roof mended by a carpenter who owed the guild three favors and paid two. The ledgers filled with entries like *one apology delivered after forty years' delay, terms satisfied* and *seven pounds of honey promised against a winter's firewood, interest waived.*

Marguerite found he did not miss the impossible things. They had been beautiful the way lightning was beautiful—wonderful to witness, terrible to owe.

The dead, too, had quieted. Not gone; he would never be quite alone again, and he had made his peace with that. But the murmurs through his hands as he carved were ordinary murmurs now, the dead equivalent of neighbors chatting over a fence. An old woman wanted someone to know her recipe for plum brandy had always used twice the ginger. A drowned fisherman kept repeating the name of a cove until Marguerite wrote it down and sent a boy with a shovel, who came back grinning with a box of coins under his arm and sand in his boots. Small debts. Settleable ones.

"Master Voss." Pell, the youngest ledger-clerk, appeared at his elbow with a cup of tea and the particular expression of someone delivering news they hoped would be taken well. "The door's sticking again."

"Which door?"

"The front one."

"The front door is *supposed* to stick," Marguerite said. "It sticks because Sefa wedged the hinge last spring to make it squeal in E-flat, so she'd know when certain people came in without knocking. It's a musical feature."

"Yes, well. It's also currently not closing at all, and the wind is coming in off the street, and Madge says if her dough rises crooked one more time because of the draft she's putting a brick against it, and then where will your crown go?"

Marguerite set down his knife and considered the ring of bone in his hands. Nearly finished. One more pass with fine sandpaper, one more hour of polish, and it would be ready for what he'd been avoiding being ready for.

"Tell Madge," he said, "that if she puts a brick against my door I will enter the brick into the ledgers as an unauthorized structural intervention, and it will take her forty years to pay it off."

Pell grinned and went. Through the floorboards came the sound of Sefa laughing in the counting room, which meant her morning lesson had reached the part where the arithmetic got ridiculous. She was sixteen now—or seventeen, depending on which lie about her age the tollgate had accepted, and they had all agreed never to audit that particular transaction. Her handwriting had improved from the wild scrawl of a child praying too loudly to something almost elegant, though she still dotted her i's with little flames, out of loyalty.

Ysolde's temple stood four streets east, and it was full these days—not with old gods, who had gone back to their long sleep once their debts were called in, but with people. Living ones. Ysolde preached on the first day of every week about tending fires you could actually keep lit, and afterward everyone stayed for bread, and somehow between the sermon and the bread the congregation had grown to the point where they were building an addition. She still wore her smuggler's coat to services, on principle. She said absolution sat better in the pockets of a coat with hidden seams.

Marguerite picked up the sandpaper and worked the crown's base smooth, and let himself think about the sigil, which he had not let himself think about directly for some time.

It lay in its box in the vault below—the old ledger-key, warm once, cold now, its purpose spent. He had gone down on the anniversary of the sealing, the first year, and the second, and found nothing changed each time: no hum, no pull, no whisper of a country pressing at the other side of anything. Just a key to a door that no longer existed, which is either the saddest object in the world or the most reassuring, depending on the hour.

This year he had decided to give it a proper resting place. Every relic deserved one. And every relic, he felt strongly, deserved better than a vault—it should live where people could see it and be unimpressed by it, because unimpressed was the correct attitude toward the machinery of old catastrophes.

Hence the crown. Hence the door.

* * *

At midday, Sefa came up from the counting room carrying her ledger with both hands, the way she carried everything important, which was everything.

"It's done," she said. "My first solo entry. Full cycle—intake, negotiation, fulfillment, closure. No supervision."

"No supervision," Marguerite agreed, setting down his work. "I was in the room."

"You were *asleep* in the room. In a chair. With your hat over your face. That's not supervision, that's furniture."

"I heard every word."

"Then what was the debt?"

"A left-handed promise," Marguerite said promptly. "A baker's apprentice swore on her mother's grave to deliver bread to a sick friend, then took a better job across town and didn't. The guild's role wasn't enforcement—we don't enforce anymore, we arbitrate. We arranged for the bread to be delivered by a third party, the apprentice to pay costs, and the sick friend, who mostly wanted company anyway, to receive a visit. Closure requires all three entries."

Sefa stared at him.

"You weren't asleep."

"Furniture," Marguerite said, "with excellent ears. Read me your entry anyway. I want to hear how you wrote it."

She opened the ledger on the table beside the half-finished crown and read aloud, and Marguerite listened with his eyes closed, partly to concentrate and partly because some pleasures were easier to admit to with the face at rest. Her entry was precise where precision mattered and generous everywhere else. At the end she had added a line no instructor would have suggested: *Debt closed. Nobody was made smaller by the settling of it.* 

"That last line," he said. "Where'd you get it?"

"Nowhere. I just think it's the whole point, isn't it? Everything we used to do—the impossible things—they made somebody smaller. The person who bought the miracle, or the person who paid for it later without knowing. You could get anything if somebody else got less." She tapped the page. "Possible things are harder. But nobody gets eaten."

Marguerite looked at her—at this girl the gods had tried to use as a beacon, a bargaining chip, a doorway, and who had stalled a hungry divinity with a riddle about families and then, years later, helped answer her own riddle by refusing to be forgotten and refusing to forget. Who had come back from fading with her shadow restored and her prayers turned from shouts into something quieter and stranger: questions, mostly, asked politely, expecting no answer, needing none.

"When you're ready," he said, "I have something to show you. Downstairs."

Her eyes went wide. "The vault?"

"The vault."

"You've never let me in the vault. You said—" she dropped into a fair imitation of his voice, low and dry— "*the vault keeps itself, and so should you.*"

"I've been known to say things for effect," Marguerite said. "Fetch your coat. And bring a candle. The lamps down there died years ago and I keep meaning to fix them and meaning, as my master used to say, is not wick nor oil."

From the corner of the workshop, faint as breath, came the ghost of a chuckle—and then nothing, which was right. The master's ghost had lingered only moments after the door sealed, long enough for goodbye, long enough for Marguerite to say the thing he'd needed six decades and one catastrophe to say. What remained of him now lived where ghosts ought to: in habits, in sayings, in the way Marguerite held a knife and the way Sefa dotted her i's. Some inheritances were burdens. Some were fuel. You got to choose which, if you chose out loud.

* * *

The vault was exactly as he'd left it: shelves of retired ledgers, their spines cracked and faded, recording debts no living soul owed; the deep alcove where the founding trade had once been documented—borders sold, anthems bartered, a king's shadow pawned against a nation's survival—all of it inert now, historical, safe the way ruins are safe. He lit Sefa's candle at the stub by the stairs and led her to the small iron box on its pedestal.

"This," he said, opening it, "is the sigil. The last doorkeeper's mark. It was given to my master, who passed it to me with several crimes attached, most of which I've forgiven and all of which I've entered into the record. It opened the border of a country that no longer exists. Six years ago I spent it sealing that border instead of crossing it, and the country was allowed to die with dignity, and the dead were allowed to rest, and I was allowed to become visible, which was the most expensive item on the whole invoice."

Sefa looked at the sigil for a long moment. Up close it was unimpressive—an old key of dark metal, worn smooth, its sigil-mark a simple gate. She reached out and touched it, gently, the way you touch a gravestone of someone you liked.

"It's cold," she said.

"Cold as an honest coin," Marguerite said. "That's the best thing about it."

"What are you going to do with it?"

"That," said Marguerite, "is where you come in. Bring the candle."

They climbed back up through the guildhall, past the kitchen where Madge was singing to her bread and threatening it simultaneously, past the counting room where Pell was showing a client the new public rate-board—no miracles, no mysteries, prices posted like any honest shop—and out to the front door, which stuck, and squealed in E-flat, and let in a slice of ordinary street: cobblestones, a fruit cart, two children arguing over an apple, the late sun lying gold across everything.

Marguerite knelt and set the crown of bone against the base of the door.

It fit perfectly. Of course it fit; he had measured it against this door and no other. The ring of bone settled onto the threshold stone, flat side down, holding the door open a hand's width—a crack of street-light and market-noise and living air, permanently admitted. Then he took the sigil from its iron box, and set it inside the crown, where it rested snugly, doing what it was made to do one last revised time: standing in a doorway. Holding open instead of shut.

"There," he said, sitting back on his heels. "Crowned."

Sefa crouched beside him, studying it. "People are going to ask what it is."

"People are going to trip over it, is what people are going to do, and then they're going to complain, and then I'm going to explain that it's load-bearing history and they should respect their elders." Marguerite stood, knees announcing themselves as usual. "Let them ask. I'm done with answers that can't survive daylight."

"Master Voss?" Pell called from inside, holding out the ledger. "Client for the afternoon slot. Widow Ammer's fence—her late husband promised the neighbor's orchard a share of the fall apples and died before picking season, and now there's a dispute about whether promises transfer."

"Everything transfers," Marguerite said. "That's the entire finding of my career. Book him. Sefa takes it solo."

Sefa's head came up sharply. "Solo-solo?"

"Solo-solo. I'll be in the chair. With my hat over my face. Furniture has excellent ears but no authority whatsoever—you'll have to sign your own name."

For a heartbeat she just looked at him, and he watched her understand it—not the task, but the trust, the whole shape of it, the fact that a man who had spent sixty years being invisible had built his final monument out of handing things away. Then she straightened, took the ledger, and said, in the clear voice she'd once used to pray too loudly:

"Marguerite Voss's apprentice takes the case. Entered into the record."

"Entered," Marguerite agreed, and went to put the kettle on.

Behind him the front door stood open its careful hand's width, propped by a crowned king of nothing, letting the ordinary world in. The street noise mingled with Madge's singing and the scratch of a young pen in a young ledger, and if any dead murmured approval through the floorboards, they murmured it kindly, and quietly, and asked only to be remembered—which, by now, was the easiest debt in the book to pay.
