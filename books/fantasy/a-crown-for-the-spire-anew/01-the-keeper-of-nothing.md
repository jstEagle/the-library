## 1. The Keeper of Nothing

The snow came down like a verdict, and Quentin Ashgrove swept it from the gate as they had every morning for ten years.

Not that anyone would notice if they stopped. That was rather the point.

The Thornfell gate was not, in truth, much of a gate anymore. It was a seam in the mountain's face, thirty feet of black stone set with ward-stones the color of old bruises, sealed behind masonry laid by masons who had died believing their work mattered. Above it, the rock rose sheer for half a mile. Below it, the pass fell away into pine and shadow and the long white silence of the borderlands. The kingdom had built a road to this place once—wide enough for armies—and then had thought better of it, and let the forest eat the road back. Quentin was what remained. A keeper without an order, a garrison of one, a name struck from the rolls so thoroughly that even the ravens seemed uncertain whether to land on their roof or fly on.

They swept the snow anyway. Rituals were how you stayed human.

The broom was birch, cut from the lower slopes where the trees grew crooked and stubborn, and Quentin worked it in slow arcs along the gate's threshold, pausing—as always—at the third ward-stone from the left. The stone was warm. It was always warm, even in the deepest cold, even when the frost climbed the other five stones and left this one bare, sweating a thin meltwater that froze again by dusk in patterns almost like script. Quentin had spent years pretending not to wonder about it. They had gotten quite good at pretending. It was, after sweeping, the second thing they did best.

"Morning," they said to the stone. Their breath fogged and vanished. "Still there? Good."

The stone did not answer. It never answered. But some mornings—the cold, clear ones, when the wind came down off the high passes carrying nothing but its own emptiness—Quentin could have sworn the mountain hummed. A note below hearing, felt in the teeth more than the ears, like a plucked string buried in the world's bones. They had mentioned it once, in their first winter, in a report sent east with the supply caravan. The reply had come back in spring: *Keeper Ashgrove is reminded that the gate is inert. There are no anomalies at Thornfell. Further reports of this nature will be considered evidence of unsuitability.*

There had been no further reports. There had been no reports at all, after that. The caravans came twice a year instead of four times, then once a year, then not at all, and Quentin had stopped writing and started living, which was either surrender or freedom depending on how the light fell.

Today the light fell gray and sideways through the storm, and the humming, if it existed, was lost under the wind.

* * *

The smugglers came up from the south three days later, as they always did around the dark of the moon, six of them with mules and oilcloth bundles and the particular quietness of people whose profession required them to be weather rather than men. Quentin heard them before dawn—not the mules, but the absence of sound where sound should have been, the birdsong stopping in a moving line through the pines.

They found Quentin waiting outside the hut with the stove lit and the kettle on, because that was how it was done.

"Mistress Ashgrove." The speaker was Corla Venn, silver-haired, sharp-faced, wrapped in a coat that had been expensive two owners ago. She said *mistress* because she'd asked, years ago, what Quentin wished to be called, and had accepted the answer with the gravity of a treaty signing. The smugglers respected rules the way soldiers respected flags: not out of love, but because everyone hanging from the same rope needed to pull the same direction.

"Not mistress. Just Ashgrove."

"Just Ashgrove." Corla smiled with half her mouth. "Tea first. Business after. I know the liturgy."

The liturgy was simple and had taken three winters to build. Quentin traded eggs from the four hens, dried herbs from the garden plot, and—once a year, at midsummer—a jar of honey from the bees nobody could explain this high up the mountain. The smugglers traded salt, flour, lamp oil, needles, news. No coin changed hands; coin left records, and records were the one commodity neither party could afford. Nothing was written down. Names were given freely but never asked for. If either party was ever questioned by authority, the other party had never existed.

"You've gone grayer," Corla observed, cradling her tin cup. "Or the hair's gone whiter. One of those."

"Both. It's efficient."

"And you're talking to the stones again." Corla nodded toward the window, toward the gate's black bulk half-buried in blowing white. "My youngest saw you at it, last summer, from the ridge. Thought you were praying."

"Maybe I was."

"To what?"

Quentin turned the cup in their hands. "To the only thing that's been here longer than I have."

Corla studied them for a moment too long, and something moved behind her eyes—not pity; smugglers didn't deal in pity, it ruined margins—but recognition. The look of one invisible person taking the measure of another. Then she shrugged and began the news, which was the real currency, and Quentin listened the way they always did: hungrily, and pretending not to be.

A new margrave in the east, young and ambitious. Grain prices up. A royal inquisitor seen in the lowland towns asking questions about the border roads, which was strange, because no one asked questions about the border roads; that was the entire architecture of the arrangement. And—here Corla paused, and her voice dropped into a register Quentin had never heard her use—"there's sickness walking the high passes. Not fever-sickness. Fear-sickness. Folk coming down out of the Thornfells from the west, from beyond the map's edge, saying the mountains are making noise. Singing, some of them say." She laughed, but it was a thin sound. "Old women's talk."

"Old women," Quentin said, "have been wrong less often than kings."

"Aye." Corla drained her tea and stood. "Well. We'll be gone before light. Same terms as ever?"

"Same as ever."

At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back. For a moment she seemed about to say something else—to break her own rule, to ask a question with a name in it, something personal, something dangerous. Then the discipline closed over her face like water over a dropped stone.

"Keep well, Ashgrove."

"You know I will. Nobody's looking."

That was the joke between them, ten years old now, and it still landed, and it still hurt, and Quentin stood in the doorway until the storm swallowed the mule-train, feeling the warmth of the hut behind them and the vast cold indifference of everything else, and thought—as they did most nights, though never aloud—that peace was perhaps just loneliness that had learned to hold still.

* * *

The stranger came the next evening, out of the heart of the storm.

Quentin heard nothing—which should have been impossible, because Quentin heard everything on this mountain, every fox-step and falling icicle, every shift of wind against the hut's north wall. But there was no warning at all, only the dog barking—Quentin kept a dog, a shaggy yellow bitch named Bristle who was more alarm than animal—and then Bristle was barking like the world was ending, and Quentin had the hatchet in hand and the door open, and there was a figure crawling up the path through knee-deep snow.

It came in stages. Crawl, collapse, crawl. The storm drove sideways, erasing it and revealing it, and Quentin stood on the threshold doing arithmetic with dread: no traveler had any reason to be here; no traveler *could* be here, unless they had crossed the high passes from the west, from beyond the map's edge, from the country that appeared on no chart the kingdom printed.

"Inside," Quentin shouted into the wind, when the figure was close enough to hear. "Can you hear me? Inside!"

The figure raised its head. Even at ten feet, even through the white chaos, Quentin could see that its lips were cracked black and its eyes were burning. Fever. The bad kind, the kind that comes from wound-fever or worse.

Getting them inside took the strength of both of them and cost Quentin a soaking to the waist. The stranger was a man, young, maybe twenty-five, dressed in courier's leathers gone gray with road-dirt, and his left side was soaked dark from hip to ribs, and the smell that came off him under the snow-smell was blood and something sweeter and fouler beneath it. Wound-rot. Quentin had smelled it once before, on a smuggler who'd waited too long to see a surgeon, and knew exactly how much time it bought.

Bristle pressed against the man's legs the moment he was down on the cot, whining, licking his frozen hands, which dogs do not do for strangers, which meant something, though Quentin hadn't the leisure to think about what.

The work was quick and ugly. Quentin cut away the sodden leather, cleaned the wound—a slash, deliberate and precise, made by someone who knew how to keep a man alive and hurting—and packed it with the moss-and-honey paste that had pulled the smuggler through, all those years ago. The man thrashed, muttered, gripped Quentin's wrist with a hand hot as a stove-lid, and through it all he held one thing against his chest with the stubbornness of the drowning holding driftwood:

A leather tube, sealed with wax, clutched in both arms like a child.

"It's yours," Quentin said, prying at his fingers gently. "Whatever it is, it's safe. Let go. Let me work."

He let go only when the fever took him fully under, and then his hands kept moving anyway, grasping at air, at blankets, at the shape of a thing that wasn't there.

* * *

He surfaced near midnight, lucid for perhaps a hundred heartbeats.

Quentin was sitting beside the cot—they had not intended to sit, but the night had drawn them down into the chair and held them there—and the man's eyes opened and fixed on them with terrible clarity, the way the dying sometimes look straight through flesh into whatever stands behind it.

"Thornfell," he rasped. His voice was gravel in a sack. "You're the Thornfell keeper."

"I'm the keeper of nothing," Quentin said. It was an old joke, worn smooth. "Who sent you?"

"The archive. The old archive, under the—" He coughed, wet and deep, and Quentin's stomach tightened. "Listen. Listen, there's no time, the fever takes me either way, I can feel it." He fumbled for the tube; Quentin put it in his hands, and he held it out, arms shaking. "Six gates. Six doors in the Thornfells. Your kingdom thinks there's one. Your kingdom *wants* to think there's one. The others were walled and drowned and forgotten on purpose—"

"On purpose by whom?"

"Doesn't matter. Matters that five are marked fresh. Fresh ink, keeper. Someone's opened the ledgers. Someone's *walking the line*." His grip crushed Quentin's wrist. "I watched the second one breathe. The stone moved, it moved like a chest, in and out, in and out—"

"You're delirious. You're wound-sick and half-frozen—"

"I'm dead," he said, with a calm that was worse than screaming. "I've known since the ford. So listen." He pulled Quentin closer, close enough that Quentin could smell the rot and see the burst vessels in his eyes. "Take the map. Don't burn it—burning's what they want, they can't use what's destroyed but neither can we, do you understand, they win either way if it burns. Take it *east*, take it to someone with a title and a spine, or don't, I don't care anymore, just don't leave it here to die with me."

"Why me?" Quentin heard their own voice go strange. "I'm no one. I'm erased. There's no one to send it to."

The man smiled. It was the worst thing Quentin had ever seen, that smile—gentle, knowing, pitying.

"That's why you," he whispered. "No one looks for a message carried by no one."

Then the clarity went out of him like a lamp guttering, and he sank back into muttering, into fragments—*the doors, the doors, they're not opening, they're waking*—and Quentin sat with him through the small hours while the storm beat on the roof, and somewhere past the deepest hour of night, the muttering stopped, and the breathing stopped shortly after, quietly, between one rise of his chest and the next, as though he'd simply decided he'd delivered the parcel and was done.

Quentin closed his eyes and covered his face with the leather tube, which he had not yet put down.

* * *

Toward dawn, the storm broke. Quentin buried the courier above the treeline, in the frozen ground, badly, with a cairn of unnumbered stones because names on graves were for people who existed. Bristle sat watch the whole time and would not come when called, and stared at the grave for hours after, ears up, as though listening to something underneath the earth.

And Quentin stood before the gate with the tube in their coat, and did not burn it, and did not open it, and did not decide anything at all—only pocketed it, the way one pockets a debt.

The sun cleared the eastern ridge and struck the black stone full-on, and the frost steamed off the ward-stones, all six of them, even the third, even the warm one.

And beneath the wind, beneath the birdsong, beneath the whole audible world, Quentin heard it clearly for the first time in ten years:

Humming. Low and patient and rising, like a voice remembering a song.

Like a door, singing.

They stood there a long time, hand pressed flat against the warm stone, an erased keeper on an unmapped pass, holding a dead man's map and listening to the exact sound they had spent a decade being told did not exist.

Then, faint but unmistakable—from the west, from beyond the map's edge, from the direction of the second door—

an answering note.
