## 3. The Inquisitor's Road

The road out of Vael Corrin was paved with the capital's confidence, and it ran out of both by the third day.

Serah Vane rode alone, as her orders required. The writ against the thief Quentin Ashgrove sat in a leather case against her ribs, sealed with the Chancellor's own black wax, and she had read it so many times on the journey that she could recite it in the dark: *Stolen property of the Crown. One map, six seals. Holder to be detained for questioning and executed upon confirmation of theft. No trial. No record.*

No record. That phrase had snagged in her mind like a burr in wool from the first reading. Inquisitors were the kingdom's memory—she had spent eleven years making sure nothing the Crown did was ever forgotten, because forgetting was how tyrannies began. And here was an order that demanded she become the instrument of forgetting.

She told herself it was simply the sensitivity of the matter. Old war business. Gate business. Things better left unremembered.

She did not entirely believe herself, but she was good at riding with discomfort, so she rode.

* * *

The Thornfells announced themselves long before they appeared—a gray bruise on the horizon that deepened hour by hour until it swallowed half the sky. Serah had crossed mountains before. She knew their grammar: switchbacks, scree, weather that changed its mind like a guilty witness.

These mountains spoke a different language.

On the fourth day, her horse refused a ford that her eyes told her was knee-deep and calm. It took her two hours of patient work to get the animal across, and when they reached the far bank she looked back and saw the water running fast and white, high as the saddlebags. She checked the sun. She checked the current upstream. Nothing explained it.

On the fifth day, she lost the road. Not wandered from it—lost it. She had been following a smuggler's track marked in charcoal signs she'd been taught to read years ago in another life, and between one bend and the next the track simply ended, dissolving into unbroken pine. She backtracked. Found it again. Lost it again at the same place, as though the forest were a page being rewritten behind her.

She camped that night under a rock overhang and took out the copy of the map she'd been given—not the original, which had never left the Chancellery vaults until it was stolen, but the surveyor's transcription, inked onto oiled linen. Six gates threaded through the range like stitches through cloth. Five of them had been circled in fresh red ink by someone in the capital, with dates beside them. Dates within the last season.

Her orders said the map had been stolen three weeks ago.

The red circles were older than the theft.

Serah sat with that for a long time while the fire burned down to coals. There were explanations. The circles could have been added to the transcription after the theft, during the frantic first days. The dating could be sloppy. Chancellery clerks were many things, and some of them were sloppy.

But the ink of the circles had browned at the edges. Aged. Settled into the linen the way ink settles over months, not weeks.

She folded the linen away and did not write the observation in her log. That, too, was unlike her. Her logs were famous—or infamous—for their completeness. She had once reported her own tactical error at the cost of a commendation, because a record that flattered its author was worthless.

Tonight she wrote only: *Progress slow. Terrain hostile. Continuing.*

Then she sat listening to the wind move through the pines, and thought it sounded less like wind than like breath.

* * *

Smuggler country began where the king's road ended, which was to say it began everywhere. Serah had worked these margins before—inquisitors always did, chasing debts and deserters and documents—and she knew the rules of invisibility the way the smugglers knew them. Don't ask names. Don't ask origins. Pay fair, take fair, leave no trace, and the mountains will pretend you were never here.

But invisibility, she discovered, was not extended to her.

At the waystation called Hollow Bridge—a trestle over a gorge, a stove, a woman who sold stew and silence—the room went quiet when Serah entered, and stayed quiet in a way that had weight. She had expected hostility. What she got was something closer to grief, or warning. The stew-woman took her coin without meeting her eyes and said, unprompted, "Whatever you're hunting up there, it kept our rules ten years. Longer than most of us managed."

"I'm hunting a thief," Serah said.

The woman finally looked at her. She was old in the way mountain people got old—young face, ancient eyes—and what lived in those eyes was pity. "That's what your paper says."

"That's what the truth says."

"Mm." The woman ladled more stew into Serah's bowl without being asked. "Truth up here runs downhill different. You want the thief, follow the charcoal marks past the split crag. But I'll tell you what we tell everyone who comes asking about Ashgrove." She leaned close, and her voice dropped to the register people used in chapels. "Ten years, that one climbed down to the low villages every midwinter and bought medicine for old Marta Kess with coin earned hauling stone. Ten years, never missed. Never said why. Never took thanks. You find your thief, inquisitor, you look at their hands before you look at their crimes."

Serah paid for the stew and the warning and walked back out into the cold, and found that her hands were not quite steady on the reins.

* * *

The mountains continued their quiet campaign.

A rockslide closed the pass she needed—an hour after she'd have been beneath it, or perhaps an hour before; she could never decide whether the timing was mercy or mockery. A shepherd gave her directions to the split crag that led her in a perfect circle back to his own fire, then looked embarrassed and insisted he'd said it right. Snow fell from a cloudless sky, fat flakes that melted on her sleeve and left no dampness, and when she mentioned it to a trapper he shrugged and said, "Thornfell does that near the gates. Keeps things out. Or keeps things in. Depends who's asking."

"Which am I?" she asked.

He studied her with the frank appraisal of a man pricing a pelt. "Don't know yet," he said. "Come back and tell me."

She laughed—it startled her, how easily it came—and he almost smiled, and for a moment the mountains felt like merely mountains, vast and indifferent and honest in their hostility. Then the snow stopped falling around her in a perfect circle six feet wide, and the trapper packed up his traps and left without another word, walking fast.

* * *

On the ninth night she made a cold camp above the tree line, within sight of the distant smudge of smoke that marked a smuggler waystation deeper in the high country—the one called, according to her intelligence, the Anvil. Ashgrove would come there eventually. Everything in the pattern she'd built pointed to it: the keeper's supply route, the midwinter circuit, the geography of habit. Ten years of routine made a man legible, even a man who wanted to be invisible. Especially such a man.

She built no fire. She wrapped herself in her cloak, set her blade where her hand would find it, and let the cold sharpen her thoughts the way it sharpened everything.

Her orders described a thief. She turned the description over now, examining it the way she'd examine a suspect's story, looking for the seams.

*A deserter of the Thornfell garrison, discharged by flight during the crisis of the old war. To be considered dangerous. To be approached with force.*

She had pulled the garrison records before leaving the capital—or tried to. The file existed, as files must, but it was thin in the wrong places. Desertion trials generated paperwork the way wounds generate blood: witnesses, testimony, sentencing, appeals. Ashgrove's file held a single sheet. Name, rank, date, one line of charge, one signature authorizing erasure from the rolls. No witnesses listed. No tribunal. No appeal. Just a name struck through in red ink, and the same hand that had circled five gates in aged ink on the surveyor's linen.

The same hand. She was nearly certain of it. Eleven years of studying documents had given her a connoisseur's eye for penmanship, and the pressure of that nib, the particular hook of its descenders, matched.

Whoever had erased Quentin Ashgrove from the kingdom's memory was the same person who had spent months marking gates for opening.

And her orders came from that person's chancellery.

Serah lay on her back beneath the wheeling stars and admitted it to herself at last, in the plain language she used for everything that mattered: she had been sent not to recover a stolen map but to bury a living witness. The theft might even be true—but if it was, it was the least important crime in the case. Somewhere ahead of her was a gatekeeper who had seen something ten years ago worth erasing, and someone in the capital who very much wanted that seeing to end with a blade and no record.

Duty said: proceed. Duty had carried her this far, through hostile country and quieter hostilities, and duty was the spine she had built her whole self around, because a girl from the river slums with no family and no name to speak of had nothing else to build it from. The Crown had taken her in, trained her, trusted her with its secrets. The Crown's word was the only belonging she had ever been granted.

But belonging granted, she thought, watching her breath fog and fade, was not the same as truth. And her oath—to the Crown, yes, but beneath that, older, sworn first at seventeen with a hand on the scales in the Hall of Records—her oath was to the record. To what actually happened. *Let the record be clean though the hands that keep it bleed.*

Somewhere below her, smoke rose from the Anvil's chimney, straight and thin in the still air.

Tomorrow she would descend and do her work. She would find Quentin Ashgrove, and she would observe before she acted, because that too was procedure, and procedure was the last honest thing she had left. And if the keeper's hands were what the stew-woman promised—

If they were, she would have to decide what an inquisitor owed when the Crown itself stood accused.

The wind moved down from the high passes, and beneath it—so faint she might have invented it, so faint she would spend the rest of her life deciding whether she had—Serah heard a sound from the heart of the mountains. Long, low, resonant, rising and falling.

Like humming. Like a door, very far away, learning to sing.

She sat up. Her hand found her blade.

The sound faded, leaving only wind and starlight and the thin thread of chimney smoke below, and Serah Vane understood that whatever waited for her at the Anvil, she would not be the only hunter on the road by morning.

Downslope, at the edge of her sight, a lantern moved through the dark where no road ran—hooded, unhurried, following the same track she was.

It was heading for the waystation too.
