## 4. Two Shadows on One Pass

*Serah*

The waystation announced itself the way all smuggler dens did: by pretending not to exist. From the ridge above the Greywater Pass, Serah Vane saw only a slump of stone and turf, a chimney smoking thin and grey, a stable roof sagging under three days of snow. No sign. No name. The kind of place that survived by being beneath notice.

She had been beneath notice once, too, in her first years with the Inquisition—small enough to slip through doors, quiet enough to hear what powerful people said when they thought no one was listening. She had traded that away for rank, for a warrant signed in red wax, for the right to be seen. Sometimes, climbing these mountains with frost in her boot seams and a dead man's description of Quentin Ashgrove folded against her heart, she wondered what she'd actually bought.

The warrant said *thief*. It said *murderer of a royal courier*, though the courier had been dying of a knife wound before Ashgrove could have touched him. It said *execute upon confirmation of possession*. What it did not say was why a sealed gate at the edge of nowhere mattered enough to spend an inquisitor on. Her orders described a man who had stolen something. They did not describe a keeper who had tended one door for ten years without pay, without pardon, without so much as a letter.

Thieves did not stay. That was the thought that had been following her down the pass for two days, patient as a wolf.

She went down through the trees with her hood up and her sword wrapped in sacking, because in smuggler country a drawn blade was a conversation-starter and she wanted the last word. The waystation's main room was low-ceilinged and amber-lit, thick with woodsmoke and the smell of tallow and wet wool. A dozen faces turned toward her and then, carefully, away. That was the etiquette here. You looked, you assessed, you forgot.

He sat in the corner farthest from the fire, where the shadows pooled deepest, and he was nothing like her file.

The file said *deserter*: a word that conjured a certain kind of face, shifty and soft. The man in the corner had a face like weathered granite, hands scarred from rope and frost and stone, shoulders that belonged to someone who had carried things a long way. He ate slowly, methodically, watching the room in the old soldier's rhythm—door, window, fire, door. And beside his elbow, wrapped in oilcloth but unmistakable in its length, lay a spear. Not a soldier's spear. A warden's: iron-banded, older than the kingdom, its head etched with sigils she'd only seen in the restricted archives.

Sigils meant to hold doors shut.

Her hand went to the warrant inside her coat. All she had to do was stand, speak the words, and let the room decide. Smugglers loved nothing better than a bounty settled among friends.

Then the outer door banged open, letting in a blade of cold and a figure in a deep grey hood, snow still clinging to its shoulders, and every instinct Serah owned went off at once—not at the stranger, but at the way the room *stopped*. Not the wary pause of men hiding their own business. The stillness of animals smelling something wrong.

The hooded figure walked to the bar, laid a coin down—an old coin, black with age—and said, in a voice like dry paper:

"Which of you has seen the keeper?"

*Quentin*

Quentin knew the voice before the coin hit the wood. Not the voice itself—he'd never heard it—but the *cadence*, the flat recitation of someone reading from a text older than any living tongue. He'd heard it once before, ten years ago, through six feet of sealed stone, on the night the Thornfell gate had tried to sing and he had refused to listen.

He kept eating. Spoon up, spoon down. His heart was trying to climb out through his ribs, and he made it sit back down the way you calmed a spooked horse: steady pressure, steady breath. The map was sewn into the lining of his coat, against his left side, warm from his body, and it felt less like paper every day and more like a second heartbeat.

The hooded one turned slowly, surveying the room, and Quentin used the turn to look properly. Under the hood's edge: a jaw too smooth, skin with the faint grey undertone of someone who hadn't seen sun in years—or wasn't using their own. At the belt, hanging among ordinary tools, a ring of small iron keys stamped with sigils. Ward-keys. Keys that should have been buried with the last generation of gate-wardens, because Quentin had personally watched them pour the burial cairn.

Someone had dug them up. Someone had opened a door.

*Five fresh marks on the map,* the dead courier's blood had shown him. *Five doors already opening.*

The hooded figure's gaze swept past him, caught, came back. Quentin felt it like a finger laid on the nape of his neck.

"You," the dry voice said. "You have the smell of the threshold on you."

The room's occupants were rising, drifting, finding reasons to be elsewhere—the smugglers' code of invisibility working exactly as designed, everyone vanishing from a situation before it became theirs. In ten years, Quentin had loved them for this. Right now he could have screamed at them.

His hand closed on the spear shaft. "I smell like every man in this room. We all stink of smoke and sheep."

"The keeper tends the Thornfell door." The figure drifted closer, and now Quentin saw that snow was not melting on its shoulders. Sitting there. Accumulating. "We only wish to talk. To offer what is owed."

Owed. The word went through him like a splinter, because he knew that word, had dreamed that word, had spent ten years telling himself he didn't want it.

And then a woman stood up two tables away, threw back her hood, and said in a carrying, official voice that had no business in a place like this:

"Inquisitor Serah Vane, acting on warrant of the Crown. Nobody move."

*Serah*

It should have worked. It always worked. The words were a mechanism; you wound them, they turned.

But the hooded figure simply rotated toward her, unhurried, and said, "Inquisitor," in a tone of mild interest, as if reading her rank off a list. "You are late. The sixth door opens in nine days, and we have so much to carry through."

"I said nobody—"

The figure raised one hand, and the sigils on its key-ring began to glow a dull, bruised red, and the temperature of the room dropped so fast the ale in the barrels frosted, and Serah understood with a lurch that she had drawn her sword against the wrong enemy entirely. Her orders had sent her after a thief. Whatever this was, the Crown's wax had not prepared her for it.

The keeper moved first.

She would think about that later, in the dark, turning it over like a coin she couldn't read: that Quentin Ashgrove, deserter, murderer, target of her warrant, stepped between her and the thing in the hood without hesitation, spear coming around in a guard so old it looked ceremonial until it didn't.

"Down!" he barked at her, and she dropped—pride swallowed whole—as the air above her split with a sound like a hinge the size of the sky.

What came through wasn't quite visible. It was more a *pressure*, a shape of absence, and where it passed, the fire went out, the lamps died, and frost bloomed across every surface in feathering white ferns. The keeper's spear-head flared with pale light, sigils blazing, and the shape recoiled with a sound like wind through a throat.

"Back door," the keeper snarled at her, jerking his chin toward the kitchen passage while the hooded thing gathered itself for another reach. "Now, Inquisitor. Unless your warrant covers this."

It didn't. Nothing in her training covered this. She ran.

* * *

They went out through the meat cellar and up a drift-choked goat track, the mountain swallowing them the way it had been swallowing her pursuers' tracks for days—she noticed, distantly, that the snow seemed to close behind the keeper's boots, erasing him, the Thornfells themselves conspiring in his favor. Behind them the waystation glowed with a light that was not firelight, and then did not glow at all.

They climbed until the sounds stopped, then climbed more, and finally ducked into a shallow overhang where the wind couldn't find them. He set the spear across his knees and sat with his back to the stone, breathing hard, watching her the way she was watching him.

"So," he said at last. "The Crown finally remembered me."

"The Crown sent me to arrest you." She kept her sword drawn anyway. Old habits. "For theft and murder."

"Of the courier." Something flickered across his face—not guilt, exactly. Grief wearing guilt's clothing. "He was dying when he reached me. Knife in the back, done miles east. All he gave me was a tube and thirty seconds of nonsense about singing doors." His eyes narrowed. "Which I'm guessing your masters know all about, since they sent an inquisitor instead of soldiers. Soldiers chase thieves. Inquisitors chase *knowledge*."

She said nothing, which was answer enough, and hated herself a little for how much he read in it.

"And you," he said. "Vane. You drew steel on that thing without knowing what it was. Bad trade."

"It spoke of a sixth door opening in nine days." She held his gaze. "Tell me what that means, Ashgrove, and maybe I'll believe you didn't kill anyone."

He laughed, short and bitter, breath steaming. "Belief. That's rich, coming from the woman holding a death warrant on me." But he shifted, just slightly, and she saw the moment he decided to give her something—not everything, never everything, but a coin's worth of truth to buy passage. "There are seven gates in the Thornfells. Seven doors on the old war's prison. One I've kept shut for ten years. The rest—" His jaw tightened. "The rest are marked open. Fresh ink on a dead man's map."

The map. Her orders had named it: *a leather tube containing documents of state security.* Documents. As if it were a treaty. As if it were anything so simple.

"And your masters," Quentin went on softly, watching her the way he'd watched the room, door-window-fire-door, "sent you to burn it. Or bring it home. Which?"

She thought of the archive seals, the restricted shelves, the way her superior's hands had shaken slightly sealing the wax. She thought of the hooded thing saying *we have so much to carry through*, and the terrible calm of it, the sense of a schedule being kept.

"I don't know anymore," she said, and was surprised to find it was true, and more surprised at how much it cost to say aloud.

Something in his posture eased—a fraction, a hair's breadth. Not trust. Trust was a currency neither of them had. But recognition, maybe. Two people discovering they were both standing on ice over the same black water.

"The sixth gate," he said. "Where does your map say it is?"

"I don't have your map, Ashgrove. I have my orders." A beat. "But the archive fragment I was given referenced the Spire Anew."

He closed his eyes. When he opened them, she saw ten years of careful invisibility cracking down the middle, and underneath it something that looked terrifyingly like resolve.

"Nine days," he said. "Then we'd best stop trading half-truths on a mountainside and start moving. You can arrest me when we're done. I'll even come quietly."

"That's not how warrants work."

"No?" He rose, shouldering the spear, and nodded back down the pass toward the dark where the waystation had been. "Then explain it to whatever that was. Because it knew my name, Vane. It knew what I was owed. And if your Crown is dealing with things like that behind our backs—" he looked at her, and for the first time there was no bitterness in it, only a bleak, level honesty, "—then the only person in either realm I can half-trust is the one sent to kill me."

The wind came down the pass, carrying with it, faint and impossible, a sound like distant humming—from below the stone, below the snow, below the world.

Serah pulled her cloak tight and followed the deserter into the dark.
