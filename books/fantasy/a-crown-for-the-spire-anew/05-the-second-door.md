## 5. The Second Door

They climbed for two days through country that had forgotten the shape of roads.

Quentin led, because only Quentin knew the smugglers' marks—faded chisels on boulder faces, a cairn stacked wrong on purpose, a birch blazed with three cuts that meant *water past, but don't trust it*. Serah followed in silence that was not peace. She walked like a woman counting her steps, as if the mountain might subtract one when she wasn't looking. Her inquisitor's coat had lost its authority somewhere on the second pass; now it was just wool and mud, and the sigil of the Crown at her collar looked less like an emblem than a wound.

The humming had not stopped.

It had been there since the night Quentin pocketed the map—a thread of sound below hearing, felt more than heard, rising through the boot-soles and up the spine like water climbing a wick. Quentin had spent ten years listening to the Thornfell gate and knew every voice it owned: the groan of frost in the mortar, the wind's low moan through the sealed arch, the tick of stone cooling at dusk. This was none of those. This was *singing*, the stranger had said, dying in the snow with blood on his teeth, and Quentin had wanted to call him a madman.

But the stone was singing, and it grew louder with every mile east.

"You hear it too," Serah said on the second morning. Not a question. She had stopped pretending she didn't, somewhere around the hour when she'd caught herself humming along without knowing the tune.

"I hear the mountain," Quentin said. "The mountain doesn't sing."

"Then what's singing?"

Quentin didn't answer. The map, wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, seemed to pulse with each heartbeat, and Quentin was no longer sure which rhythm belonged to whom.

* * *

They found the first ward-stone at noon, and Quentin knew before touching it that something was wrong.

The old boundary stones were the kingdom's oldest honest work—each one cut with the sigils of the Binding, the same marks Quentin had spent a decade maintaining at Thornfell. You fed them a drop of blood at midwinter and a prayer at midsummer, and they held. They had held for three hundred years.

This one lay on its side in the scree like a pulled tooth. And the sigils had not been weathered or broken. They had been *altered*—each mark of sealing scored through with a single clean stroke, and beneath it, cut fresh, a new symbol Quentin had never seen: a circle opened at the top, like a door ajar.

Serah crouched over it. Her fingers hovered above the fresh cuts without touching, the way one hovers over a body.

"This is capital work," she said quietly. "The stroke pattern. The depth. Masons in the Citadel quarter cut like this—I've seen it on the new monuments." She looked up, and Quentin watched something die in her face. "This isn't defacement. It's *revision*."

"Someone re-cut the wards to open instead of close."

"Someone with a royal chisel." She stood too fast and had to steady herself against the stone. "No. No, that's—there are protocols. The gates are sealed by treaty, the keys are split between the Crown and the Spire's remnant, no single office—"

"Serah." Quentin kept their voice level, the way they'd learned to speak to frightened smugglers in bad winters. "You were sent to kill a thief. Ask yourself who told you the map was stolen."

She stared at them. The wind came down the pass and filled the silence, and it carried the humming, clearer now, almost sweet, almost like a lullaby, and Quentin saw her hear it and understand it at the same moment, the way one understands a betrayal: all at once, and forever after.

* * *

The gate itself sat in a fold of the mountain where two ridges met like cupped hands—a place the maps called Hollowmere, and the smugglers called Nothing, because nothing that went in came out changed.

Quentin had never been here. Ten years at Thornfell, and they had believed—had needed to believe—that Thornfell was the door, the one door, the whole burden of it on their shoulders alone. The map's second mark had felt like an accusation. Now, standing before it, Quentin understood it was something worse. It was a *pattern*.

The gate was a twin of Thornfell's: the same black arch, the same seamless stone, older than the kingdom that claimed to have built it. But where Quentin's gate wore its seals like armor—ward-stones ranked before it, sigils bright with a decade of careful blood—this one stood *opened*. Not broken. Opened. A crack down the arch's center, wide enough for a hand, then a shoulder, then a man, and from it the singing poured like heat from a forge door.

The ward-stones lay in a neat row to one side. Not toppled. *Arranged*. Someone had carried them aside with care and set them in order, the way a workman stacks tools at the end of a shift.

"They didn't fight it," Quentin said. Their voice came out strange, flat. "The wards would have fought. Three hundred years of binding doesn't just—step aside. Someone unmade them. Properly. With the right words."

Serah had drawn her sword. Quentin hadn't seen her do it; the blade was simply there, and her knuckles were white around the grip, and she was staring into the crack in the arch as if it were staring back.

"I have authorization codes," she said. Barely a whisper. "Every inquisitor does. Sealed orders, countersigned, for emergencies. If someone used codes like mine to unbind a gate—" She stopped. Started again, and her voice cracked on the second try. "The counterseal on those orders would be the Lord Inquisitor's own."

"Your master's hand."

"My master's *ring*." She turned, and her eyes were wet and furious in equal measure. "You don't understand what you're asking me to believe. He swore the oaths. He taught the oaths. He had me memorize the Binding litany when I was nineteen, he said it was the only prayer that mattered—"

The singing swelled, and they both went silent, because beneath it now there was something else. A rhythm. A slow, vast pulse, like a heart the size of the mountain, beating in the dark on the other side of the stone.

And in the snow before the arch, half-buried, Quentin found the proof that needed no litany at all.

A sledge track. Days old, half-filled with fresh snow, but unmistakable—the wide runners of a heavy cart, the deep prints of draft oxen. Something had been brought *up* the mountain, through the pass, to this gate that the kingdom's maps did not mark. And leading away from the arch, toward the crack, the prints of men. Many men. Boots, good boots, military issue, marching in formation.

Quentin crouched and brushed the snow from a scrap of canvas caught on the stones. Military grey. A supply stamp. And beside the stamp, in the faded ink of the royal quartermaster's office, a destination code.

Quentin didn't know the code. Serah did.

She read it once and went the color of the snow.

"That's the Citadel deep-stores," she said. "That's *his* office. Provisions for a garrison." Her sword arm dropped to her side, the point dragging a line in the snow. "You don't provision a garrison at a sealed gate. You don't send oxen and tents and—Quentin, there are *supply lines*. This isn't an accident. This isn't a cult with a stolen chisel. This is a *deployment*."

The word hung in the cold air between them, and the mountain sang, and Quentin felt ten years of their life turn over like a stone and show what lived underneath.

For a decade, Quentin had guarded the gate against the war's return—against the old dark pressing from the far side, patient, hungry, waiting for the seals to fail. That was the story. That was the story that had made the exile bearable: *I am the wall. The dark wants through, and I say no.*

But the tracks went *in*. The provisions went *in*. The wards had been unmade by capital hands with capital tools, carefully, in order, and the door stood ajar like a mouth held open, and the singing was not the sound of something struggling to get out.

It was the sound of something being *welcomed*.

"The war isn't coming back," Quentin said slowly. Each word cost something, paid out of a decade of belief. "It never left. It was *waiting*—and someone on the inside of the walls has been carrying its messages."

Serah sat down abruptly on a ward-stone, the first ungraceful thing Quentin had ever seen her do. She put her face in her hands. When she spoke, her voice was muffled and young and stripped of every ounce of inquisitor.

"My orders said retrieve the map and execute the holder. I asked why the holder couldn't be questioned. I was told the holder was dangerous." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "I'm the dangerous one. I'm the *witness*. They sent me because I'd believe the file, because I'd never met you, because I'd do it and come home and never know what I'd—"

She stopped. Her hands came down from her face, and she looked at the gate, at the crack, at the dark beyond it where the pulse beat slow and vast.

"They're going to open all of them," she said. "Five gates, fresh ink on the map. This one's open. How long before the others—"

The singing cut off.

Not faded. *Cut*—like a throat gripped mid-note. The silence that followed was so total Quentin could hear their own blood. Then, from deep within the crack in the arch, from the far side of the stone that had stood sealed for three hundred years, came a new sound.

Footsteps.

Slow. Unhurried. The measured tread of boots on stone, marching in formation, coming *up* from the dark toward the light—and beneath them, fainter, a voice giving orders in a cadence Quentin recognized from a war that ended before they were born.

Serah was on her feet, sword up, backing toward the rocks. "Quentin. *Quentin.* Tell me you hear that."

Quentin had already turned, already reaching for the smugglers' marks on the southern ridge—the deep roads, the forbidden ways, the route to the sixth gate at the Spire Anew that the map showed in ink darker than all the rest. There was no time to decide. The deciding had been done for them, up this mountain, by men with royal chisels and the Lord Inquisitor's ring.

"Run," Quentin said. "And Serah—whatever you saw in that file about me, believe the opposite. Because in about an hour, you're going to need to trust a deserter with your life."

The footsteps in the dark grew louder. They ran.
