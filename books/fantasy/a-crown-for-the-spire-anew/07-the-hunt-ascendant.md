## 7. The Hunt Ascendant

Serah Vane had tracked enough fugitives to know when the hunt stopped being hers.

She heard them before she saw them—horses where no road ran, iron jingling against iron, and beneath it all a low, disciplined silence that only soldiers under strict orders could manage. She pulled Quentin down behind a shelf of granite as the first riders crossed the scree below, torches guttering in the wind that came howling off the Thornfell peaks.

"Six," she breathed. "Maybe eight."

Quentin's eyes were closed. They did this sometimes—listening to the mountain as though it were an old dog that still answered its name. "They've split," they said quietly. "Four taking the high line. The rest will cut us off at the Grey Stair."

"How can you possibly know that?"

"Because I've spent ten years learning every way through these mountains, and so has everyone who ever smuggled anything past my gate." A pause. "And because I can hear them. You'll have to trust me on that."

She didn't like trusting people whose file she'd memorized. *Deserter. Erased. Do not engage beyond necessity.* The words had seemed so clean in the capital, printed on good paper by clerks who had never smelled snow at altitude. Out here the file was a lie with frost on it, and Serah was beginning to hate the hand that wrote it.

The riders passed below. In the torchlight she caught their livery—not the grey of the border levies but the royal blue of the Crown's own hunters, the ones who never came back empty-handed. Her commission would mean nothing to them. Warrants were for people who still mattered; she and Quentin were now simply *loose ends*, and loose ends got cut.

"They know we're in this valley," Quentin said.

"They know more than that." Serah flexed her sword hand, working feeling back into frozen fingers. "They knew about the waystation. They knew about the ford. Someone's feeding them our route, and it isn't me."

Quentine looked at her then, and in the thin light their face was older than ten years of gatekeeping should have made it. "You're wondering if it's me."

"The thought occurred."

"It would. It shouldn't." They said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. Then their head tilted, that listening posture again, and Serah saw something cross their expression that she had never once seen there: fear.

"What is it?"

"Singing." Quentin stood slowly, drawing her up with them. "But not the gate. Not ours. Get your blade out, Vane. It's too late to run."

* * *

They came out of the rocks without horses, without torchlight, without any sound at all until the very last moment—and that was the horror of it, that men could move across snow like ink spreading on wet paper. Hooded, robed in undyed wool, and across their chests sigils stitched in thread the color of old bruises. Sigils Serah had seen exactly once before: on the ward-stones of the cracked second door, defaced and re-carved into something wrong.

The first bolt took her in the side.

There was no archery duel, no shouted challenge. One moment the night was empty; the next a crossbow spoke twice from the darkness and pain bloomed under her ribs like a struck bell. She went down behind the granite, and Quentin went down beside her, and the world narrowed to the geometry of cover and return fire.

"Left!" Quentin barked, and shoved her head aside as a quarrel split the air where her skull had been. They drew her own dagger from her belt—when had they taken it?—and threw it low, and somewhere in the dark a man cried out.

"Can you run?" Quentin's hands were on her wound already, pressing hard, and their voice had gone flat and calm in the way of people who had decided panic was a luxury.

"I can—" The blood came up hot between her fingers, mingled with theirs. "It's deep. But I can run."

"Then don't fight me tomorrow when I do something you won't like."

The hooded ones advanced, three abreast, unhurried. One called out in a voice like dry parchment: *"Keeper! The doors are singing and you keep pretending you are deaf! Come home. Come be remembered!"*

Serah felt Quentin flinch. Felt, in the same instant, the terrible steadiness of their answer as they lifted her arm across their shoulders and took her weight without complaint.

"I remember what I'm owed," Quentin said to the darkness. "That's why you'll never collect."

Then they ran.

Down the scree, through the pines, along a frozen streambed where the hunters' torches became smudges and then rumors behind them. Serah counted her breaths because counting her heartbeats was too frightening. Twice she stumbled and twice Quentin held her up as if she weighed nothing, as if they had carried wounded people out of mountains before—which, she realized, they probably had, back in the war nobody wanted to name, back when Ashgrove's name had meant something other than *traitor*.

At the tree line they stopped, in the black mouth of a ravine no map she owned had ever marked. Cold air rose out of it, mineral and old.

"There's a road down there," Quentin said.

"There's nothing down there. That's a fissure."

"It's a road. The smugglers call them the deep ways. Old mine workings, older pilgrim tunnels, some things that were here before either." Quentin looked at her, and the moon came out from cloud just enough to show their expression: resolved, and grieving, and free in a way that frightened her. "They're forbidden. My rules, actually—I wrote half of them. No keeper goes into the deep roads, because no keeper comes back out the same."

"Then why—"

"Because the surface roads are watched, and you're bleeding, and the sixth gate is three days away instead of nine." They crouched, tore a strip from their own cloak without ceremony, and bound her wound with hands that did not shake. "Because I spent ten years being invisible, Serah, and invisible almost let them win. I'm done with it."

Something in her chest turned over—something that had nothing to do with the quarrel lodged near her ribs. She had been trained her whole life to read lies, and here was a person telling her the plainest truth she'd ever heard, and she wanted, absurdly, to argue with it just to keep them talking.

"If we go down there," she managed, "and your smuggler friends find us on their forbidden road—"

"They won't find us." Quentin's teeth showed briefly, not quite a smile. "I'm going to tell them where we are. There's a knock for the deep doors. Only keepers and captains know it." They paused. "My captain taught me. Before I refused her order. Before she erased me."

*Her,* Serah noted. Filed it away. Another crack in the official record.

"And if the knock doesn't work?"

"Then we die in the dark, and honestly, Vane, given the alternatives topside—" They offered their hand. "Coming?"

She took it. Standing hurt; standing with Quentin's shoulder under her arm hurt less, which she decided was a fact about the world she would examine later, if later existed.

Together they stepped into the mountain.

* * *

The deep roads swallowed sound the way the sea swallows stones.

Serah learned the dark in layers: the dark of the entrance, which still remembered stars; the dark of the first galleries, where Quentin's shuttered lantern made a small gold room around them; and finally the true dark, when the oil ran low, the dark that pressed against the eyes like a question. Through it all, Quentin moved with terrible certainty, hand trailing the left wall, murmuring distances under their breath—*"three hundred paces to the junction, mind the drop, here the roof fell in '41"*—as though reciting scripture written in scars.

"You really did write the rules," she said at one point, because speaking kept her from thinking about how much blood she was losing.

"I enforced them. Old Marla wrote most of them, and the smugglers' grandmothers wrote the rest." A beat of silence. "Rule one: nobody's cargo matters more than anybody's life. Rule two: the gates stay shut. Rule three—" Their voice caught, resumed. "Rule three: nobody gets forgotten. Even deserters. Especially deserters."

"That's why they respect you."

"That's why they *respected* me. What I'm doing now breaks rule two straight down the middle." Quentin stopped. In the lantern's dying light Serah saw them press their palm flat against the tunnel wall, and saw—she would swear afterward—the stone seem to lean into the touch. "The gates stay shut. But five are opening anyway, and the people who opened them wear my kingdom's colors or carry my kingdom's coin. So the rule has to break from inside, by the one man who swore it. Otherwise it isn't kept at all. It's just obeyed until it isn't."

She thought about her orders. *Retrieve the map. Execute the holder.* Clean paper, clean clerk-hands. And underneath them, apparently, hooded things with bruise-colored sigils and capital authorization cracking doors open in the dark. Duty, she was beginning to understand, was a wall—and someone she had trusted had hollowed it.

"Quentin." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "If we reach the Spire. If we stop this. What happens to you? The hunters, the warrant—they won't evaporate."

"No." Quentin said it easily, terribly. "Probably not."

"So the plan is to save two kingdoms and then hang for it?"

"The plan," Quentin said, turning to look at her, and the last of the lantern light caught their eyes and held there, "is to make sure there's someone alive to write down what actually happened. To get the story home. That's always been the plan, Serah. From the night the courier died on my floor." A pause, softer: "I just hadn't met the someone yet."

She wanted to say something to that. She had a whole speech assembled somewhere behind her ribs, wedged in next to the quarrel-head, about files and names and how a record could be rewritten by whoever survived to hold the pen. But the lantern chose that moment to gutter out entirely, and the dark came in close and warm, and Quentin's hand found hers in it without either of them remarking on the fact.

"We're close," Quentin whispered. "Do you feel that?"

She did. A vibration in the stone under her boots, slow and rhythmic, patient as a sleeping pulse. And threaded through it—faint, rising, unmistakable—a sound like a great door humming to itself in the dark, tuning up for a song.

Ahead, far down the throat of the mountain, something knocked.

Three times. Deliberate. Waiting.

Quentin's grip tightened on her hand. "That's not the gate," they breathed. "Gates don't knock."
