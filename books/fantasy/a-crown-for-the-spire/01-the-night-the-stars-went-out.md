## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The Ledgerhouse of the Guild of Impossible Things stood on a street that only existed on Thursdays, and Oscar Oyelaran had counted every one of its floors twice: nine above ground, four below, and one that moved, which he had learned never to audit after dark.

He was in the sub-basement on the night the world ended, which was fitting, because the sub-basement was where the Guild kept everything it was ashamed of.

"Lot four hundred and eleven," he read aloud, in the flat voice he used for inventory. His quill moved in small, precise strokes. "One bottled Tuesday, contents cloudy. One jar of borrowed luck, seal broken, approximately half remaining. One grief, pressed, provenance unknown."

The grief had been sitting on the shelf for eleven years, and every audit it grew slightly heavier. Oscar had weighed it. That was the kind of man he was — the kind who weighed griefs and wrote down the number, because a thing unmeasured was a thing that could surprise you, and Oscar had been surprised enough for one lifetime.

Three years he had kept these accounts. Three years since he had run from Mother Ilse's cottage with smoke in his lungs and her true grimoire's first page burned into his memory, and had found, in the Guild, the only honest trade in a dishonest world. Numbers did not flatter. Numbers did not pretend to love you. A ledger balanced or it did not, and Oscar had made himself into a creature of balances.

He was halfway through lot four hundred and twelve — a caged echo, still faintly saying someone's name — when the first ward shattered.

He felt it in his teeth before he heard it: a high, thin note like a wineglass dying somewhere far above. Then the second ward went, and the third, and the sound came down through nine floors and four and the one that moved, a cascade of breaking that had no bottom.

Oscar did not run. That was the first thing to know about him, and the last thing anyone would learn about the man he had been. He capped his ink, squared his ledger on the shelf, and took the stairs two at a time with the counting-room's master key already in his fist, because if the wards were failing then the inventory was unsecured, and unsecured inventory was his responsibility.

The counting-room was chaos. Clerks he had known for years stood at the tall windows with their faces lit green by the ward-glass, and the ward-glass was wrong — every pane of it crawling with cracks that spread as he watched, hairline fractures blooming like frost across the whole eastern wall of the Guild.

"The border wards," said Mistress Vess Corvane from the head of the room, and her voice was calm in the way that made everyone else afraid, because Vess Corvane was only calm when the situation was worse than it looked. "The border wards are going down."

"All of them?" someone asked. "The northern line, or—"

"All of them."

Oscar went to the window. He looked up, because that was what everyone did, what anyone would do, when the sky began to fail.

The stars were going out.

Not dimming. Not hiding behind cloud. Going out, one by one and then in handfuls and then in whole constellations, like lamps being snuffed by a hand moving down a corridor. The Swan went. The Shepherd's Lantern went. The star that sailors called the Debt, because it always came back when it was owed, went and did not come back.

Within the space of a hundred heartbeats, the sky over the city was black and absolute, a ceiling instead of a heaven, and the cold began.

Oscar had grown up in the mountains. He knew cold the way he knew arithmetic. This was not that. This cold came up out of the ground and down out of the dead sky at once, patient as interest accruing, and it settled into the room like a creditor taking a chair.

"Seal the strongroom," Vess said. "Move the year-bottles first. Luck after. Souls last — they keep." Her eyes found Oscar across the room, and held him a moment longer than the order required. "Oyelaran. The deep ledgers. Bring me the vault inventory, the full one, and bring it unopened."

Unopened. In three years she had never once told him not to open a ledger. Auditing was his whole office; the Guild's motto was carved over his desk — *What Is Owed Shall Be Known* — and he had believed it, had built himself on it, the way a house is built on stone.

"Yes, Mistress," he said, and went down.

* * *

The vault was four floors below the counting-room, and the cold followed him down like something walking behind.

He had the master key. He had always had the master key; that was the point of him. The deep ledgers lived in iron cabinets sorted by decade, and the vault inventory — the full one, the true one — lived in the cabinet at the back, the one with no decade on its label, because it did not record what the Guild held.

It recorded what the Guild owed.

He carried it up unopened, as instructed, and set it on Vess Corvane's desk in a counting-room now lit entirely by lanterns, because the ward-glass had gone dark as the sky. The clerks were hauling strongboxes. The cold had reached the windows and was drawing fern-patterns of frost on the inside of the glass.

Vess looked at the ledger and did not touch it.

"Thank you, Oyelaran. That will be all."

"Mistress." He did not move. "The wards. I've been maintaining the schedules for three years and the border lattice was sound. I checked the resonance figures myself in autumn. They did not fail. They were *called* — something pulled the power out of them from the far side, and there is only one party on the far side of the border that buys power in that quantity, and we keep its account." He heard his own voice, flat and precise, and hated the tremor under it. "So before I go back to the sub-basement and start writing *cause of loss: unknown*, I would like to know what we owe them. And when it comes due."

For a long moment Vess Corvane looked at him, and something moved behind her eyes that he had never seen there before, and would spend a long time learning to name. It was not guilt. It was the thing guilt becomes after enough years — a kind of tired arithmetic of its own.

"Go back down, Oscar," she said quietly. "Please."

He went back down. He did not go to the sub-basement.

* * *

He opened the ledger.

He told himself, later, that it was duty — that a keeper who leaves a book sealed while the world ends is a keeper who deserves what's coming. But the truth was smaller and older than that. The truth was that Oscar Oyelaran had spent three years trusting numbers instead of people, and on the night the numbers were all he had left, he needed them to tell him something true.

The ledger was old. The paper had gone the color of tea, and the earliest entries were in a hand he did not recognize, iron-gall ink gone brown, the letters tall and severe. It was a debt book. Not the Guild's debts — the Guild's *collateral*. Every bargain the Guild had ever made that required a pledge of self, written here: names, years, breaths, shadows, souls.

He turned pages. The cold in the vault deepened. Above him, faintly, he could hear the Guild groaning on its foundations, and the sound of the clerks singing the inventory hymn, which they only sang when they thought the building might come down.

He reached the page dated fifty-one years before his birth.

His name was there.

*Oscar Oyelaran* — inked in a brown, severe hand, in a ledger older than his mother, older than Mother Ilse, older than the fever that had nearly killed him in his seventh winter. Not a namesake. Not a coincidence; he had audited too many "coincidences" in impossible inventory to believe in them. The entry was specific. It had his full name, the one no one used, the one he had never told anyone in the city. It had a date of maturity. It had a buyer.

The buyer's mark was a crown drawn over a mountain.

*Sold. Collateral held against Guild covenant. Payable upon demand of the court beneath.*

Oscar sat down on the vault floor with the ledger in his lap, and the cold came up through the stone and through him, and he did the thing he always did when the world broke, which was to check the arithmetic.

Fifty-one years. He was twenty-six. The entry predated him by a quarter century, and yet it named him — named him *wholly*, named him the way a creditor names a debtor, with the intimacy of ownership. Which meant the entry had been amended. Which meant someone had come back to this page, sometime in the last twenty-six years, and written his name into a debt that was already waiting for him. Which meant—

Which meant the numbers had known. The whole time. Every balanced column, every clean audit, every night he had gone to sleep believing that at least the ledgers were honest — the ledgers had been holding his name in their cold hands, and saying nothing.

He had trusted numbers because people lied. He understood, sitting in the dark of the vault with the stars dead overhead and winter pouring into the world like water into a hold, that he had simply chosen the liars with the better handwriting.

His own name. Sold before he existed. Payable upon demand.

And the border wards had just come down.

Oscar closed the ledger. His hands were steady — that was the horror of it, the hands were perfectly steady, three years of bookkeeping had trained the shaking out of them — and he stood, and he tucked the ledger inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel it like a second heartbeat.

He did not take it to Vess Corvane.

He climbed the four floors to the counting-room, and stood in the doorway, and watched her direct the salvage by lantern-light: the year-bottles crated, the luck jarred, the souls in their long lead boxes stacked and *kept*, exactly as she had ordered. The mistress of the Guild of Impossible Things, who had told him to bring her the debt book unopened, because she had known — she had always known — what was written in it.

Vess looked up. Their eyes met across the ruined green light of the dying ward-glass.

"Oyelaran," she said. "You opened it."

Outside, the first true snow of the long winter began to fall — upward, at first, as if even the cold was checking whether the sky would take it back.

"You signed my name," Oscar said, "before I was born."

And Mistress Vess Corvane, who had lied to kings and out-bargained devils and never once flinched in front of her clerks, closed her eyes as if the number had finally come due.

"Yes," she said. "And now we have until the snow reaches the door to decide what we do about it."
