## 2. An Inventory of Stolen Souls

The Ledgerhouse had a smell for every disaster. Fire smelled of scorched vellum. Flood smelled of ink blooming in wet paper. The end of the world, Oscar discovered in the small hours of the worst night in the guild's history, smelled of tallow candles burning down in a room where no one remembered to trim the wicks.

He found Vess Corvane in the Vault of Standing Debts, three floors below the counting hall, standing before the shelf where the guild kept its oldest and least forgivable contracts. She had not lit the lamps. She did not need to; the mistress of the Guild of Impossible Things had spent forty years learning to read in the dark, and besides, the shelf itself seemed to give off a faint cold light, the way deep water does, the way a held breath does.

"You're supposed to be upstairs," she said without turning. "Counting what we've lost."

"I counted." Oscar set the ledger down on the reading table between them with a care that was entirely false. He had carried it down three flights of stairs with hands that wanted to shake and had refused, because a keeper's hands did not shake, because if his hands shook then everything else was permitted to. "Eleven hundred and six bottles of luck, unaccounted. Forty years of borrowed time, called in by no one, sitting in the cellar like spoiled milk. The soul-cages in the east annex are all empty. Every star we held in escrow is gone. And the wards—" He stopped. Started again. "The wards are gone, and the sky is gone, and it is snowing in the courtyard, mistress, in the middle of harvest, and the snow does not melt."

"Then you've been thorough."

"I've been *terrified*," Oscar said, "and thoroughness is what terror looks like on me. You taught me that."

Now she turned. Vess Corvane was a small woman, narrow as a ledger's spine, with grey hair cropped short and hands that had signed away more futures than any priest had blessed. In three years, Oscar had never seen her look uncertain. He saw it now, and it was worse than any lie she might have told.

"You found it," she said. "Your name."

The words went through him like a cold nail driven into green wood.

"You knew I'd find it."

"I knew that on the night the wards fell, every seal on every contract would break open for one hour. I knew that you, of all people, would spend that hour auditing." She almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to see. "You were always going to find it, Oscar. That's why I kept it where I kept it."

He opened the ledger. He did not need to find the page; he had known it by heart since the stars went out, since he had stood in the counting hall with a lamp in one hand and his own life in the other, written in ink that had dried decades before he was born.

*Oscar Oyelaran. One soul, whole and unencumbered. Sold in perpetuity to the Court Beneath the Mountain. Collateral against the guild's covenant of immunity. Signed, Vess Corvane, Keeper.*

"Read me the date," she said quietly.

"I know the date."

"Read it anyway. I want to hear whether you understand it."

Oscar looked down. The date sat at the top of the entry in Vess's small, exact hand, and beneath it, in the same ink, the same hand, a second line he had read forty times and still could not make mean anything: *Contract executed on behalf of the signatory's future interests, per standing arrangement with the Court's procurement office.*

"The arrangement was made before I was born," he said. "You didn't sell my soul. You sold the *possibility* of me. You sold a child who didn't exist yet."

"I sold a debt that hadn't happened yet," Vess said. "It's not the same thing, whatever the priests tell you. Sit down, Oscar. You'll want to sit."

He did not sit. He stood with his palms flat on the reading table, on the ledger, on his own name, and made her tell it in order, because order was the only thing he had left.

* * *

The trade was older than the guild's charter, older than the city, older than the mountain it was cut into. When the first keepers had built the Ledgerhouse, they had built it in the one place in the world where winter could not follow — and winter could not follow because they had *paid* for that. The court beneath the mountain wanted souls. Not bodies; bodies were meat, and the court had no use for meat. Souls, whole and named and *specific*, because a soul was the only currency that appreciated. Every year the guild delivered its tithe — the souls of the dead who had no one to mourn them, the souls sold by desperate families, the souls of criminals whose names had been struck from the rolls — and in exchange, the winter stopped at the guild's threshold. The candles never guttered. The hearths never died. The snow, when it fell, melted.

"Forty years I've kept that covenant," Vess said. "My mother kept it before me, and her mother before her. Do you know what the world was like before the bargain? I've read the old counts. Whole provinces, frozen mid-harvest. Kingdoms where the sun rose for two hours a day and the people learned to eat the dark. The guild bought an island of summer, Oscar. We bought it with other people's souls, and I will not pretend that was clean, but it was *paid*, and paid debts are the only morality a keeper can afford."

"And me." His voice came out flat. "Where do I fit in your arithmetic?"

"You were the interest," she said. "The court doesn't only want souls. It wants *promised* souls — souls that don't exist yet, sold before they're born, because a promised soul ripens. It grows sweeter with waiting. Your mother's mother owed the guild a debt she couldn't pay, and the arrangement was made: her line's third generation, signed over in advance. I signed the paper when I was younger than you are now. I signed it the way you'd initial a delivery of candles. That's the truth, and I won't dress it."

"You signed a child."

"I signed a *number*," Vess said. "That's the sin of this whole house. We turn people into numbers so we can sleep at night, and then the numbers turn around and become people again, and there's never a good time to face it." She looked at him then, straight on, and her eyes were dry and old. "You became a person, Oscar. You walked into my counting hall three years ago with a stolen grimoire under your arm and lies in your mouth, and I took you in, and I told myself I was being kind. Maybe I was. But I also knew that the court's procurement office sends a collector every fifty years, and the fiftieth year is this year, and I wanted the account come due to have a face I loved."

The candles had burned to pools. Somewhere above them, the snow that did not melt was burying the courtyard.

"So the wards falling," Oscar said. "The stars going out. That's not the covenant breaking. That's the *collector*."

"The court has stopped honoring the old trade," Vess said. "It's hungry in a new way. It's called in everything — every star we held, every year we borrowed, every soul in escrow. And the account it wants settled most is the one it's waited fifty years for." She reached across the table and turned the ledger toward him, and her finger came to rest beneath his name. "It wants you, Oscar. It has always wanted you. And if I don't deliver you, it takes the island of summer, and the winter comes down on the world like a lid."

* * *

For a long time neither of them spoke. Oscar looked at his name in the ledger and felt the strangest sensation, as if he were reading about a stranger whose life had been spent without his knowledge — and then the stranger resolved into himself, and the feeling curdled into something harder.

Three years. Three years of hiding from Mother Ilse, of learning to keep accounts, of trusting numbers because numbers did not lie the way people lied — and the whole time, the first number in his life, the number he himself was, had been entered against him before he drew breath. His master had sold him once. His guild had sold him before he existed. He had been property so long that everyone involved had stopped noticing it was a strange thing to be.

"You could have told me," he said.

"When? The day you arrived? The day you passed your first audit?" Vess shook her head. "There was never a day. There's never a day for anything in this house. That's what the ledgers are for — to hold the truths we can't say out loud. And now the ledgers have opened, and here we are, saying it."

"Here we are," Oscar said.

He straightened. Something had settled in him, the way a column settles when the weight above it finally becomes too much and then, impossibly, holds. He was afraid — he was so afraid his teeth ached — but beneath the fear was a thought so clean it was almost a blade: *the court wants an account come due. Fine. Then let it meet an auditor.*

"I'm not going to be delivered," he said. "I'm not a tithe, mistress. I'm not interest. I'm not a number that ripened." He turned the ledger back toward himself and read his own name aloud, slowly, the way one reads a summons. "But I'll go down. Not as payment. As a claimant. If the court holds my soul, then the court holds stolen goods, and I am coming to conduct an inventory."

Vess went very still. "You don't know what you're saying. The court has eaten auditors. It has eaten kings."

"Then it will have indigestion," Oscar said, "because I've seen its books."

He hadn't — not yet. But he said it the way a man lays a cornerstone: not because the building exists, but because he has decided it will. And Vess Corvane, who had spent forty years reading people the way she read contracts, looked at him for a long moment and then did something he had never seen her do.

She bowed. Slightly. The way one keeper bows to another.

"Then you'll need to know the way down," she said. "And you'll need someone who knows what broke the wards — because it wasn't the court alone that broke them, Oscar. Someone on the surface opened the door." Her face, for the first time in three years, showed him something that was not control. It looked almost like fear. "There's a ward-smith in the lower city. Sable. She built the border wards, all of them, and three nights ago she came to the guild asking questions I couldn't answer. Find her before the winter does. And Oscar—"

"Mistress?"

"Whatever the court offers you — and it will offer; it always offers — remember that nothing beneath the mountain is free. Especially not the things that look like gifts."

He took the ledger off the table and tucked it under his arm, his name against his ribs, and went up out of the Vault of Standing Debts into the falling snow that did not melt — toward the lower city, toward the woman who had broken the world, toward the dark under the mountain where his soul was kept in a cage of starlight.

Behind him, in the cold vault, Vess Corvane took down a blank ledger and a pen, and began — in a small, exact hand — to write the contract that would one day cost her everything she had.
