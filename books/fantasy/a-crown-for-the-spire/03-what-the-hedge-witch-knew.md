## 3. What the Hedge-Witch Knew

The road north was a wound in the world, and Oscar walked along its edge counting the ways it could kill him.

Three days out from the Ledgerhouse, the frost had stopped behaving like weather. It lay in perfect parallel lines across the fields, as if combed by some enormous patient hand. The hedges wore their ice like jewelry. And in the village of Harrow's End — if it could still be called a village — the people stood in their doorways mid-gesture, mid-word, one woman frozen with a loaf half-lifted from her oven, her mouth open around a sentence she would not finish until spring, if spring ever came.

Oscar did what he always did when the world frightened him. He counted.

"Four hundred and six souls in Harrow's End," he murmured, walking the lane. "Give or take the ones already gone beneath." His breath came out in small white ghosts that refused to dissipate, hanging in the air like punctuation. "Four hundred and six debts I'm carrying now."

He had taken the ledgers. All of them. He'd told himself it was for the audit he would one day conduct, but in the dark of the first night on the road, wrapped in his cloak against a cold that bit through wool like teeth, he had opened the oldest volume by candlelight and read the entry again.

*Oscar Oyelaran. One soul, whole and unblemished. Sold by: Ilse Vane, hedge-witch. Purchased by: the Court Beneath. Price: the boy's life, continued.*

Dated nineteen years before his birth.

He had sat with that date for a long time, doing the arithmetic no ledger could make come out clean. A debt older than the debtor. A signature before the signer existed to sign. Somewhere in the guild's impossible inventory there was a word for this kind of transaction, and the word was *fraud*, and the fraudster was the only person who had ever loved him.

He closed the book. He did not cry. He was twenty-six years old and he had learned crying was just arithmetic done badly.

Ahead, the road bent toward the mountains, and the mountains were dark where stars should have been wheeling above them, and Oscar walked on into the cold with his dead name riding in his satchel like a stone.

* * *

Nineteen years earlier, Mother Ilse knelt in the fever-room and lied to a dying child with her whole heart.

"Breathe," she said. "That's it. In and out. You're doing beautifully."

The boy in the bed was seven years old and burning up like a lamp left too near the curtain. She had tried everything in her lesser grimoire — the willow bark, the cold compresses, the charms stitched into his nightshirt, the saint's medal buried under the floorboards for luck. The fever laughed at all of it. Fevers did that. Fevers were the world's oldest creditor, and they always came to collect.

She had bought him at three, Oscar Oyelaran, from a mother who couldn't feed him, for two silver coins and a jar of plum preserves, and she had never once thought of him as a purchase. He was the boy who talked to her bees. The boy who organized her herb shelf by an alphabetical system of his own invention and then defended it, red-faced, when she teased him. The boy who fell asleep in her chair every evening at the same hour as reliably as the sunset.

And the fever was going to take him because she had been slow, because she had been proud, because she had thought her own craft enough.

There was another book. There was always another book.

Ilse went down into the cellar where she kept the true grimoire under a flagstone she'd sworn never to lift again, and she lifted it, and the book exhaled a smell like winter and old coins. It was not a wicked book, whatever the priests said. It was only honest, which was worse. It told you exactly what things cost.

The page she needed was written in ink that moved when you weren't looking. *Soul, one (1), whole. Buyer negotiable. Terms: the life of the named party continues. The named party will be called upon.* No price listed, because the buyer set the price, and the buyers below the mountain were patient the way stones are patient.

"They'll call him down someday," she said aloud to the empty cellar. Her voice cracked on it. "They'll come with a ledger and a smile, and he'll hate me, and he'll be right to."

But he would be alive to hate her. That was the trade. That had always been the trade, since the first witch traded the first thing she couldn't afford.

Ilse Vane signed her name in the space provided, and her hand did not shake, because she was a professional, and professionals do their grieving later, in private, over many years, in small installments.

Upstairs, the fever broke.

* * *

Present. The pass road. Sable walked beside him in silence, which Oscar was beginning to understand was her native language.

The disgraced ward-smith carried her tools in a pack that clinked like a guilty conscience. Every few miles she stopped, crouched, and pressed her palm flat against the frozen ground, reading something in it that Oscar couldn't see.

"The wards didn't fail," she said, on the second day, apropos of nothing. "Everyone keeps saying they failed. They didn't. They were *opened*. From the inside of the working." She wiped her hand on her coat. "I keep trying to remember the commission. Who paid for the border wards. Who drew up the specifications." Her brow furrowed, and then smoothed oddly, like water closing over a dropped stone. "It's strange. I can remember building them. Every sigil. But the why slips away."

Oscar said nothing. He was thinking about toll-keepers and memories and the shape of a bargain he hadn't yet struck, and he was thinking that Sable's missing reasons sounded less like forgetting and more like *payment*.

"You're staring at me," Sable said.

"I'm auditing you," Oscar said. "It's different."

"Hm." Almost a smile. The first one. Then she looked up at the black sky and the smile died. "No stars. You know what the old ward-smiths used to say? The stars are holes. Holes punched in the ceiling of the world so the light underneath can get through. Someone's plugged the holes." She shivered. "Or someone's collecting the light."

That night they sheltered in a shepherd's hut whose owner stood frozen by the hearth, poker in hand, tending a fire that had gone out years ago and somehow still smelled of smoke. Oscar laid out his bedroll on the far side of the room and took out the oldest ledger, and Sable watched him read the same page for the hundredth time.

"That your name?" she asked quietly.

"It was sold before I was born."

"By who?"

He turned the page. He turned it back. The ink hadn't changed. It never changed. That was the thing about ink; it was more faithful than people.

"A hedge-witch," he said. "Who raised me. Who taught me my letters and my numbers and how to tell foxglove from comfrey. Who is the only reason I'm alive." He closed the book with exaggerated care, the way you close a door on a room you can't bear to look at. "And who apparently decided, decades before I existed, that my soul belonged to the court beneath the mountain."

Sable was quiet a long moment. The frozen shepherd tended his dead fire.

"What was her name?"

"Ilse Vane." Saying it aloud hurt in a way he hadn't budgeted for. "Mother Ilse."

"And do you hate her?"

"Yes," Oscar said. It came out fast and clean, the way a number comes out when the sum is simple. Then, because he was honest even when he wished he wasn't: "No. Both. I don't know how to carry both at once. There's no column for it."

* * *

Nineteen years earlier. The morning after the fever broke, Ilse burned breakfast, which she never did, and Oscar noticed, because he noticed everything, because he was that kind of child.

"You're being strange," he informed her, aged seven, with the authority of a magistrate.

"I'm always strange. Eat your eggs."

"You're being *stranger*." He ate the eggs anyway. Then he put down his fork and looked at her with those eyes that missed nothing, eyes she would one day see narrowed over a ledger, and said: "Something happened last night. Something you're not telling me."

Ilse Vane had faced down tax collectors, plague doctors, a demon of small debts, and the Bishop of the Marches. None of it prepared her for a convalescent seven-year-old looking at her over scrambled eggs.

"When you're older," she said.

"You always say that."

"And one day you'll be older, and I'll say it, and it'll be true, and you'll wish I'd kept saying it instead." She ruffled his hair. He ducked away, scowling, and she laughed, and the laugh was real, and that was the cruelest part — that everything after could be real too, the lessons and the summers and the bee-talk, all of it built on a cellar floor and a moving line of ink.

She should have hidden the true grimoire better. She knew that now, nineteen years downstream of that morning, standing in her cottage doorway watching the stars go out one by one like candles pinched by a passing hand.

Because he had found it. Of course he had found it. He was sixteen and brilliant and she had taught him to read the old scripts as a game, and while she was at market he had lifted the flagstone, because he was also thorough, and thoroughness in the clever ones always curdles into digging.

She came home to find him sitting on the cellar steps with the book open on his knees, and his face —

His face was the face of a boy doing sums and getting an answer that broke mathematics. She would remember that face until she forgot everything else.

"Before I was born," he said. His voice was very quiet and very level, the way it got when he was most frightened. "You signed it before I was born. How is that possible? Nothing comes before its cause. Even your rotten book says so. Page forty. *No debt precedes its debtor.*"

"Some debts do," Ilse said. "The important ones."

"Did you sell me?"

"I saved you."

"*Did you sell me?*"

"Yes." She made herself stand still under it. She owed him that much — to stand in the blast and not flinch. "When you were seven. When the fever had you. There was a price and I paid it and I would pay it again, and I know that makes me a monster, and I have decided to live with it."

"You decided." Sixteen years old, hands shaking around a book of moving ink. "You decided *for me*. My soul. You spent my — you didn't even ask. You didn't even—" His voice finally cracked, and she almost preferred the shouting. "You wrote me into a ledger like I was a jar of plums."

"You were dying."

"So are they!" He pointed at nothing, at everything, at the whole mortgaged world. "Everyone's dying, all the time, that's what being alive *is*, and you don't get to buy your way out of it with someone else's—"

He stopped. Breathed. And when he spoke again it was worse than the shouting, because it was calm, and it was hers, that calm, she had taught it to him herself.

"I'm leaving," he said. "Tonight. Don't follow me."

"Oscar—"

"If you follow me, I'll run faster. If you send anything after me, I'll know you think I'm property." He climbed the stairs past her without touching her, and at the top he paused, and for one moment — she lived on that moment for three years, rationed it like bread — his voice wavered back toward seven years old. "Did any of it mean anything? Any of it? Or was I just... interest accruing?"

"Every day," Ilse said. "Every single day."

He nodded once, the way a merchant acknowledges receipt, and walked out of her life with a satchel and her own good boots, and she let him go, because chasing him would only prove him right.

* * *

Present. The foothills. The mountain filled half the sky now, black against blacker, wearing the absence of stars like a crown worn wrong.

Oscar camped alone — Sable had taken the first watch — and built a fire from the last dry wood, and sat feeding it sticks and memories.

He remembered her hands. That was the thing that ambushed him, three years later, a thousand miles gone: not the betrayal, not the ledger, but her hands kneading bread, tying off skeins, pressing cool cloth to his forehead through the long bad nights. Hands that had held a pen and signed him away and then come home and made supper, and both things had been completely true, and he had no column for it, he had never had a column for it.

"Ilse," he said to the fire. "What did they give you? What was the price on the other side? Because the book lists what I cost, but it never says what you got, and I've been doing the math for nine years and it won't balance. Nothing you could have received balances against a soul. So either you were cheated, or..."

Or the payment wasn't for her at all. Or the terms ran deeper than the page showed. Or the court beneath the mountain had wanted him — specifically him, his particular soul, his inconvenient talent for seeing what numbers hid — since before he drew breath, and had simply needed a desperate woman with a dying child to hold the pen.

The fire popped. Above him, the starless dark pressed down like a lid.

"Sable," he called. "How far to the mountain's mouth?"

Her voice drifted back through the cold, flat and certain. "Two days. Maybe three, if the toll-bridge is still collecting."

Oscar pulled his cloak tighter and looked at the black peak, at the court that had owned him since before he existed, and felt something settle in his chest that was not fear, or not only fear — the cold bright thing that lives in the chest of a man who has finally finished the sum and knows the total.

"Good," he said, to no one. "I've been calling in a debt for nineteen years. Time somebody answered."
