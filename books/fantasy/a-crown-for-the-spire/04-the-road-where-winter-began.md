## 4. The Road Where Winter Began

The road north was a river of white that had forgotten how to move.

Oscar walked it with the guild's second-best ledger strapped to his back and the first-best's knowledge burning in his chest: *sold, decades ago, to the court beneath the mountain.* Every mile put another entry between him and Vess Corvane's face when she'd admitted it. Every mile also made the cold worse, and the cold was not weather. Weather had texture, warning, an order to its cruelty. This was different. This was arithmetic — the world subtracting itself, line by line.

He found the first village an hour past noon, or what he guessed was noon; the sky had been a lid of grey iron since the stars went out, without sun enough to cast a shadow worth trusting.

Hollowmere, the signpost said, though the letters had frosted over mid-stroke, the carver's chisel-marks still open in the wood as if he'd only just lifted his hand.

They were all only just lifting their hands. That was the thing about the frozen villages that no one had warned him about, because there had been no one left to warn anyone. A woman stood at a well with her bucket half-raised, and the water in the bucket was ice shaped like motion — a sculpture of pouring, caught forever between the rope and the fall. Two boys played at hoops in the lane, their sticks frozen a hand's width from the rolling iron, and the hoops themselves had stopped *in the air*, tilted, still turning in some sense Oscar's eyes refused to confirm. A dog slept curled on a doorstep, and Oscar could not tell — even after standing over it for a long, shameful minute — whether it slept or had simply been filed away.

Time had pooled here like water finding a cellar. He could feel it under his boots, thick and slow, a depth where there should have been none.

"Numbers," he said aloud, because saying things aloud kept him from screaming, and he was a bookkeeper before he was anything. "Count what you can count."

Fourteen villagers visible. One well. Zero birds, which was wrong; even in deep winter there were crows, there were always crows. Zero smoke from any chimney, and yet every chimney held warmth if he laid his palm near the brick — not fading warmth, but *suspended* warmth, held the way the guild held collateral: intact, inaccessible, waiting on terms.

He wrote it down. His hand shook and the entry came out crooked, and he hated the crookedness more than he feared the silence, because crooked entries were how lies got in.

That was when he heard the hammer.

Three strikes, then nothing. Three strikes, then nothing. Rhythmic as a pulse coming from somewhere it shouldn't. Oscar followed it down the lane, past the woman with her bucket, past a chapel whose bell hung frozen mid-swing at an angle that would have torn its mounting free if time ever resumed — and found, at the village's far edge, a person moving.

She was kneeling in the middle of the road with a smith's leather apron gone stiff with frost, setting ward-nails into the packed snow. Real ones — silver-cored, rune-struck, guild-grade. She'd hammer a nail three times, sit back, watch it, swear at it, pull it out, and start again. Around her lay dozens of pulled nails like extracted teeth.

"Don't bother," Oscar said. "There's nothing left here to ward."

She didn't look up. "I know what's here." Her voice was rough, unused. "I'm checking whether time comes back when I stop touching it. It doesn't. Which means it isn't my hands. Which means—" she pulled the fourth nail, "—it was never the nails."

Oscar knew her before she raised her face. Everyone in the Ledgerhouse had known Sable's name within a day of the wards collapsing; grief needs somewhere to go, and blame is the easiest ledger to keep. Sable Ferrance, journeyman ward-smith, ten years' service, the one who had signed off on the border lattice the night it failed. The one whose runes — the inquiry had found — were flawless right up until they weren't.

"You shouldn't be walking north," he said.

"Neither should you." Now she looked up. Her eyes were the colour of old ash, and there was frost in her eyebrows, and her hands were ruined with cold in the specific way of someone who had stopped feeling them hours ago and considered that a mercy. "North is where winter is coming *from*. Everyone sane is walking south."

"Everyone sane wasn't sold." He heard how it came out — flat, bitter, more confession than he'd intended to spend on a stranger. "I have business under the mountain."

The hammer stopped.

For a long moment the only sound in Hollowmere was the frozen bell groaning against its own stillness, a sound like a held breath. Then Sable stood, slowly, joints cracking, and studied him the way she'd studied the nails — looking for the flaw, the place where the making had gone wrong.

"You're guild," she said. "Ledger-side."

"I was. I'm not sure the guild will want me back after what I intend to audit."

"And you're going down there alone?"

"I was."

"Good," she said, and shouldered her satchel of nails. "Then you won't argue when I say I'm coming, because there's no one whose plans I'm ruining."

* * *

They walked, and did not talk, and the white road unspooled ahead of them like a column of figures that only went one direction.

Oscar had imagined, in the rare hours he allowed himself imagination, that travelling with the woman who broke the world would feel like travelling with a lit fuse. It didn't. Sable was quiet the way deep water is quiet, and twice as cold, and she checked her nails against the roadside milestone-wards every evening with the devotion of a penitent at a shrine. It took him two days to understand that this was guilt — not performed, not spoken, just *structural*, load-bearing guilt, holding up everything she did the way a keystone holds an arch.

It took him less than that to recognize it, because he kept his own books the same way.

On the third day they came to Bracken Ford, and Bracken Ford was worse.

Hollowmere had been frozen *around* its people. Bracken Ford had frozen *through* them. In the market square, a fruit-seller stood mid-haggle with her mouth open on an argument, and the argument itself hung in the air before her — visible now, a shimmer of heat-shaped sound turned to glass, words made of frost that spelled out *you call that a fair price for figs* in curves no alphabet claimed. A child chased a cat; both hung at full stretch, and around them the moment had pooled so deep that frost ferns grew in spirals, beautiful and wrong, time crystallizing into ornament because it had nowhere left to flow.

Sable stopped walking. Stopped entirely, mid-stride, and Oscar watched her face do what his had done in Hollowmere — the arithmetic, the counting, the horrible sum.

"How many?" she asked.

Oscar counted. It was the only kindness he had to offer anyone anymore, so he offered it. "Thirty-one in the square. Perhaps a hundred in the houses. They're not dead." He said it carefully, because he'd checked the ledgers' metaphysics against this and the numbers held: suspended, not spent. "Winter hasn't taken them. Winter is *waiting* for them. There's a difference, in the accounting."

"In the accounting," Sable repeated softly. "Is that supposed to comfort me? Because I built the lattice, keeper. Every anchor-point. I cut those runes for six years. And the night it failed, I remember—" her jaw worked, "—I remember thinking the design was wrong. Remember *seeing* it was wrong, clear as sunrise, and my hands signing it anyway. Like the thought was borrowed. Like it passed through me on its way somewhere else."

"That's not how guilt works. Guilt doesn't get to be a weather report."

"No," she agreed. "But coercion does. And I can't tell anymore which one I'm carrying, and I have lain awake every night since deciding whether it matters." She looked at him, ash eyes steady. "You keep honest books. Tell me: if a debt is engineered, is it still owed?"

Oscar opened his mouth to answer — and found, for the first time in his life, that he didn't trust his own reply. Because he knew what it was to have a contract signed in your name before you could speak. Because if he said *no*, then his own sale beneath the mountain was void, and hope was a currency he couldn't afford to inflate. And if he said *yes*, then Sable belonged in chains, and he did not want her in chains, and wanting had nothing to do with the truth and he knew that better than anyone alive.

"The books don't say," he said finally. "Books only record. People decide. That's the whole point of an audit — someone has to stand behind the numbers and take responsibility for what they mean." He started walking again, boots crunching the pooled frost. "So we'll decide together. Down there. Where the contracts live."

Behind him, after a moment, her footsteps followed.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen waystone, and Sable slept, exhausted past dreaming, her ruined hands curled near the coals. Oscar sat up with the ledger across his knees, because sleep had become a country he held no passport for.

By firelight, he did what he always did when the world went mad: he reconciled.

Entry: border wards, failed, author Sable Ferrance. Annotation, added in his own hand: *circumstances of signature unclear.*

Entry: long winter, cause unknown, effect measurable. Villages suspended: three confirmed, trend increasing with proximity to the mountain.

Entry: Oscar Oyelaran, soul, sold. Purchaser unnamed. Consideration received: survival of a fever, age four. Signed: *Mother Ilse*, hedge-witch, in a hand he'd know anywhere, because she'd taught him his letters with that same looping patience, guiding his chalk with her own warm fingers.

He had fled her for reading the true grimoire. He had spent three years telling the story as betrayal — *she lied to me, she trafficked in souls, she was exactly what I ran from.* The road north was dissolving that story entry by entry, because the numbers refused to lie even when he wanted them to: a fever that killed children across three parishes in the space of a season, and one child who lived. A woman who had gone to something hungry and paid it with the only coin she had that mattered, and then carried the secret so badly, so obviously, that a boy who read too much was bound to find it — as if some part of her had *wanted* to be caught. As if three years of his hatred were the interest on a payment plan she'd chosen herself.

"She should have asked me," Oscar told the fire, and the fire said nothing, and somewhere beneath the words he heard the child's voice underneath the man's: *she saved me, she saved me, why does saved hurt like this.*

Sable stirred. "Talking to the accounts again?"

"They're better listeners than most."

"Mm." She sat up halfway, watching him over the coals. "Ask them this, then. If the court bought your soul thirty years ago, and the court is what's sending the winter — why hasn't it collected you yet? Why let you walk straight to its door?"

Oscar looked up. The question sat in the air between them, and he felt it the way he felt a ledger that balanced *too* perfectly — every figure correct, and therefore something arranged.

"Because," he said slowly, turning the page, "an account come due isn't a loss. It's an asset maturing. You don't interrupt it. You *invite* it." His finger found the ledger's final written line, where his own name sat inked in a hand that was not his, dated before his birth, and beneath it — new tonight, fresh as a wound, in ink that gleamed faintly silver — a second notation that had not existed that morning:

*Expected arrival: the Tollbridge. Payment will be taken there.*

Down the road ahead, miles off through the dark, something vast and patient exhaled, and the frost on the waystone bloomed into shapes like teeth.

"There's a bridge," Oscar said, closing the book. "Between us and the mountain. And something on it already knows we're coming."
