## 5. A Bargain at the Tollbridge

The bridge appeared out of the white like a rib bone someone had buried and half dug up again.

Sable saw it first — a habit of ward-smiths, to see structure before scenery — a single span of black stone crossing a gorge that had no bottom she could find, only a slow grey breathing of mist. At its near end stood a hut built of stacked slate, and in the doorway of the hut stood a figure wrapped in so many layers of patched wool that it might have been one person or a committee.

"Someone's kept the road," Sable said. "In this."

Oscar came up beside her, ledger clutched to his chest like a breastplate. Three days they had walked since the frozen villages — three days of houses where soup cooled mid-stir and children hung mid-laugh in doorways, time pooling around them like ice around stones. He had not spoken much. When he did, it was numbers: miles, hours, the rate at which the frost crept south behind them. Numbers were how he prayed, she was learning.

"Tollbridge," he said now, reading the sign without needing to read it. "Of course. Of course there's a toll."

"You sound almost happy."

"I sound *oriented*." He tucked the ledger away. "Everything else has stopped making sense. A toll I can audit."

The figure in the doorway raised one arm — an arm ending not in a hand but in a small brass scoop, the kind used for weighing grain — and beckoned.

* * *

The keeper's face, when they got close enough, was unremarkable in the way of people who have been forgotten on purpose: middle-aged, weather-chapped, eyes the pale blue of skimmed milk. A lantern hung beside the door with no flame in it, yet it glowed, faintly, the color of candlelight remembered rather than seen.

"Travelers," the keeper said. Voice like gravel poured slowly. "Southbound. Toward the mountain." The brass scoop tilted toward them, expectant. "The bridge is open. The bridge is always open. But nothing crosses for free."

"We have coin," Oscar said, already digging in his coat.

The keeper looked at the coins the way a man looks at leaves offered as firewood. "Coin is what you keep when you've nothing real to spend. No. This road runs through country where winter eats everything soft. Coin freezes. Flesh freezes. What doesn't freeze—" the scoop turned, catching the false lantern-light, "—is what you carry in here." It tapped, gently, against the keeper's own temple. "Memories. Warm ones travel best. They burn slow."

Sable felt her stomach drop through the gorge mist. "You want us to pay with—"

"One memory each. Fair passage, both ways, no questions asked by me and none answerable by you." The keeper shrugged, a landslide in miniature. "I don't make the rates. I keep them. There's a difference, though fewer people notice it every year."

Oscar had gone very still. She knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a man reading a ledger line and finding the arithmetic wrong.

"Which memories?" he asked carefully. "Do we choose?"

"The toll chooses what it takes. You choose whether to pay." The keeper smiled, and it was the smile of someone who had watched ten thousand travelers do this arithmetic and knew exactly which way it always broke. "Or you can turn back. Winter's coming down the road behind you faster than it's coming from the sky. Your choice either way. That's the only honest thing left on this bridge — the choice."

Sable looked at Oscar. He was staring at the scoop, and his lips were moving slightly, counting something. Counting, she realized with a cold that had nothing to do with the air, *what he could afford to lose*. An accountant even here. Even now.

She stepped forward before he finished.

"I'll pay first."

"Sable—"

"You'll just stand there calculating until the frost takes your toes off. I'm quicker." She held out her arm to the keeper, palm up, like a woman offering to be bled. "Take it. Whatever it wants. Take it."

The keeper's milk-pale eyes rested on her a moment too long. Something moved behind them — not malice, she thought. Recognition. As if the keeper had been told to watch for her specifically, and was surprised at how easily she'd come.

"As you wish," the keeper said softly.

* * *

The scoop touched her temple and the world went white and warm, like stepping into bathwater, like the first swallow of brandy —

— and then she was standing in her own workshop, three years younger, lamplit, soldering iron in hand, and she knew — she *knew*, the way you know things in dreams — why she had built the wards wrong.

Not incompetence. Not haste, not drink, not the sloppy arrogance the Guild tribunal had named when they stripped her certification and burned her tools. She had been *visited*. A courteous voice in the dark of her forge, months before the collapse, patient as dripping water, explaining terms. Her sister's debt — Marla owed the court beneath the mountain, had owed it since the year she'd gambled away a winter of her own life and couldn't cover the note. The court had offered to forgive it. All Sable had to do was leave one seam unfinished. One hairline gap in the border wards, no bigger than a thought. And forget she'd done it — forgetting was included in the price, such a generous price, such a *courteous* arrangement—

She had said yes. She had soldered the last seam shut everywhere but there, and gone to bed, and woken clean.

And now the memory was being lifted out of her like a splinter drawn whole, and she felt it go, felt the shape of her own guilt pulled up by the roots, and she heard herself say — heard her mouth move around words she hadn't chosen:

"No — wait — I need to remember why—"

But the toll does not negotiate, and the memory left her, and she was kneeling on cold stone with snow in her eyelashes and a hollow behind her eyes where something important had been, and she could not remember what she had just paid, only that she had paid it, only that the keeper was looking at her with something horribly like pity.

"Paid in full," the keeper said. "Cross when ready."

"What did she pay?" Oscar's voice, sharp, close. "What did you take from her?"

"That's between the toll and the customer."

"It isn't." Oscar stepped between them, and Sable, swaying back to her feet, saw his face and thought: *he looks like the ledger found an error.* "I've read guild law. I've read *older* law than guild law. A toll taken in secret from a party under duress is voidable. And she was under duress — look at her. Look at the road behind us. Nobody pays a toll freely with winter eating the world."

The keeper regarded him. "You're offering me a legal argument, bookkeeper? On my bridge?"

"I'm offering you a choice," Oscar said. "Same as you offered us. Return what you took, and I'll pay double for both our crossings. From myself alone."

"And what would you pay, ledger-man? You've got coin I won't take and memories I can't reach—" the scoop gestured, almost apologetic, "—yours are locked up tight behind all those numbers. Wouldn't melt loose if I boiled you."

"Then I'll pay in kind. In *kind*." Oscar drew a breath, and Sable watched him do something she had never seen anyone do: invent a contract on the spot, out loud, with winter closing in. "I, Oscar Oyelaran, keeper's apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things, do pledge one true memory of equal warmth to this toll, payable on my return crossing, interest accruing at one memory per season unpaid. Witnessed by the road itself, which remembers everything, and by my companion, who will remember this offer even if I do not."

Silence. Mist breathed in the gorge. Somewhere far below, water moved under ice with a sound like slow applause.

The keeper's eyes flicked to Sable. "Does your companion confirm?"

And Sable understood — in the hollow where her memory had been, understanding bloomed like frost on glass — that this was the hinge. That whatever she had paid, it had mattered enough that Oscar was mortgaging his own mind to buy it back, and that the keeper needed her word to seal it, and that the truth — the true truth, the *she sold something vital, ask her what* — would crack the bargain open like an egg.

"He confirms," Sable said. "He's good for it. He's the best bookkeeper the guild ever had, and the guild has had centuries of them. Take his pledge and let us cross."

It was, she thought distantly, even true. Mostly true. True enough to pass over the part that wasn't.

The keeper studied them both a long moment. Then the scoop dipped into some pocket of the layered wool and came out holding — nothing visible, nothing at all, just a curl of warmth that steamed in the cold — and pressed it back to Sable's temple.

The world went white again, brief as a struck match.

When it cleared, she remembered.

She remembered the forge, and the voice, and Marla's debt, and the seam she'd left open, and she remembered that Oscar had just watched her remember, and she turned to him with the confession already climbing her throat like sick—

But Oscar was looking at the keeper, and saying, calmly, in the flat tone he used for inventory: "Confirmed. One memory returned intact. Now our passage, as agreed."

*He didn't hear it,* she thought wildly. *The return must have blurred — he was talking over it — he doesn't know—*

Except he had stepped aside then, and met her eyes, and in his face was no confusion at all. Only a careful, deliberate blankness, the blankness of a man who has seen the number and chosen to write a different one in the column.

*He knows,* she realized. *He knows, and he lied for me.*

To the keeper. To the road. With the road listening.

"Cross," the keeper said, and stepped aside from the span, and the black stone waited.

* * *

They were halfway across the bridge before Sable trusted her voice. Wind came up the gorge carrying the smell of deep stone and older cold, and ahead the mountain stood against the dead sky like a closed fist.

"You lied," she said finally. "Back there. You said I confirmed, and I hadn't — not honestly — and then you pretended not to hear what came back into my head. You lied twice in ten seconds."

"Yes." Oscar didn't look at her. His knuckles were white on the ledger. "I noticed."

"How did it feel?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Below them, under the ice, the water applauded on.

"Easy," he said at last, and his voice was quiet and horrified. "That's the problem, Sable. It felt *easy*. Like a number that finally came out even." He stopped walking. The mist curled past them toward the mountain, as if the gorge itself were headed somewhere and they were merely in its way. "My whole life I've believed the books because books don't lie unless someone makes them lie. And I just made one lie. Me. I'm the someone now."

"Would you take it back?"

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he looked at entries he intended to keep — and shook his head once. "No. But I'm going to hate myself efficiently about it. There's a method."

Despite everything — the hollow ache where her guilt had been reinstalled, the mountain growing ahead of them, the knowledge that somewhere beneath that stone a king sat counting souls including hers — Sable laughed. It came out cracked and steaming in the cold, and it felt like the first warm thing in days.

They walked on. And it was only at the far end of the bridge, where the road bent upward toward the mountain's root and the gates showed themselves — two doors of black iron, standing open, unguarded, lit from within by a light like caged stars — that Sable felt the returned memory shift inside her, and noticed what she had missed.

The courteous voice in her forge, all those years ago, had ended its offer with a phrase. A signing-off. A courtesy.

*"Until the account comes due,"* it had said.

And now, carved above the open gates in letters of old gold, the same words greeted them:

**THE ACCOUNT COMES DUE. WELCOME HOME.**

Oscar had gone pale as the snow.

"It's been expecting us," he whispered. "It's been expecting *me*."
