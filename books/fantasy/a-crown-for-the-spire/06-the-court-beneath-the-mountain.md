## 6. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not have a door. It had a debt.

Oscar understood this the way he understood most things — through arithmetic. The pass above them narrowed to a throat of black stone, and where a door should have been there was instead an absence, a shape cut out of the rock with such precision that his eyes kept sliding off it, insisting on filling in the missing stone. Sable stood beside him with her lantern raised and her jaw set, and even she, who had built wards for half her life, could not look at the opening straight on.

"It's not hidden," she said. "It's *paid for*. Someone bought the right to be unseen here, and the mountain is honoring the contract."

"Then we're expected."

"We've been expected since the stars went out." She glanced at him. "Since before that, if your ledger's honest."

His ledger was honest now whether he liked it or not. Three days on the road since the tollbridge, three days of walking through villages where winter pooled like spilled milk — a woman frozen mid-laugh at a well, a boy mid-throw with a snowball hanging in the air like a small white moon — and Oscar had found that his tongue had changed along with everything else. He had tried, once, to tell Sable the road ahead was short when it was long, just to soften the march, and the words had come out of him as the truth instead, ugly and unbidden: *the road is long and I am afraid of it.* She had looked at him strangely. He had hated how much better he felt.

Now he stood at the mouth of the court beneath the mountain, and the truth was all he had to carry in.

They descended.

* * *

The stair went down farther than stairs should. Oscar counted the steps because counting was his prayer — four hundred and eleven, four hundred and twelve — and somewhere past a thousand the walls began to change. The rough black stone gave way to worked masonry, then to masonry carved with figures, then to carvings so old their meaning had worn smooth, leaving only gestures: hands extended, mouths open, arms lifted toward something overhead.

Then they came around a final turning, and Oscar saw what the figures had been reaching for.

Stars.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They hung in the cavern dark in iron cages no bigger than his two fists, each one caged and chained and hung from hooks driven into the vaulted ceiling, and together they made a sky — a false, beautiful, imprisoned sky over a city carved into the living rock. Streets spiraled down into the depths. Bridges arched between towers of black basalt. And everywhere, from every window and doorway, light spilled out: starlight, stolen and domesticated, burning in lamps and sconces and lanterns shaped like moths.

"The missing stars," Sable whispered. Her voice cracked on it. "All of them. They didn't go out. They were *collected*."

"They were always collected," said a voice below them. "You only noticed tonight."

* * *

The king of the court beneath the mountain received them at the foot of the great stair, in a hall floored with polished obsidian that reflected the caged heavens so perfectly it seemed they walked upon the sky. He was tall and thin as a closing parenthesis, robed in grey that might once have been white, and his face was the face of a man who has been patient for so long that patience has become his features. His crown was a simple band of iron, unadorned, and Oscar's eye — the accountant's eye, the curse of his trade — fixed immediately on the fact that the crown was slightly too large, as though it had been made for a greater head, or a prouder one.

"Oscar Oyelaran," the king said. "Apprentice runaway. Keeper of accounts. Sold." He smiled, and the smile was genuinely warm, which was the worst thing about it. "Welcome home. You are the account come due."

"I'm not home." The words left him flat and true. "I've never been here."

"No," the king agreed pleasantly. "That is rather the tragedy of it. You were sold before you could form memories of the place, precisely so that returning would feel like exile. A kindness, in its way. My predecessor was sentimental about children." He gestured, and servants appeared — thin, courteous figures whose faces Oscar could not hold in memory, each glance sliding away like water off glass. "You must be hungry. You will dine. Both of you. Refusal is permitted, of course. Everything here is permitted. That is the entire point of the court."

Sable's hand had gone to the ward-chalk at her belt. The king regarded it without alarm.

"Ward-smith," he said. "The one whose wards failed. Do you know why they failed?"

"I know why," Sable said, and her voice was gravel.

"Do you?" The king tilted his head. "How interesting. We shall speak of it later, perhaps. Or perhaps you'd rather not remember. Memory is such a negotiable currency these days." His gaze moved back to Oscar, and it settled there with the weight of a hand on a scale. "Come. Walk with me. Your friend may rest. There are rooms prepared — rooms she will find very difficult to leave. Not by force. By comfort. It is more effective than force, and cheaper."

* * *

The king walked him through the city, and Oscar hated himself for how beautiful he found it.

There was no want here. That was the first thing he understood, and the understanding went through him like cold water. Every figure they passed was fed, clothed, unhurried. No beggars, because nothing was scarce. No thieves, because nothing was owned — or rather, everything was owned by the court, and the court was generous the way a larder is generous to the meat. Children ran laughing under the caged stars, and their laughter echoed up through streets that had never known rain or hunger or the particular dread of a bill come due.

"You feel it," the king said. They stood on a bridge over a canal of still water, and the reflected stars burned beneath them like a second sky. "Don't be ashamed. Everyone feels it. Above, every breath costs something. Food costs labor. Shelter costs money. Love costs risk. Even honesty — especially honesty — costs friends. Here, nothing is owed. That is our founding principle. Our whole architecture, physical and legal, is built upon it. Nothing owed, nothing owing, nothing due."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, they stay." The king spread his thin hands. "Freely. That is the part your guild never understood, with its ledgers and its audits and its little moralisms. We do not chain anyone. We simply make leaving unthinkable. What is freedom, Master Oyelaran, but the ability to choose among options? Remove enough options gently enough, and freedom remains — technically, legally, eternally — while becoming as meaningless as a word in a dead language."

"You're describing a trap."

"I'm describing *peace*. The trap is above. The trap is winter, and hunger, and the terror of insufficiency that drives hedge-witches to sell the souls of fevered children to save their bodies." The king turned to face him fully, and in the caged starlight his eyes were very old and very tired. "Yes. I know about Mother Ilse. I know about the fever. I know she came to us weeping, and I know what she traded, and I know she told herself it was mercy. It was. Mercy is frequently a transaction conducted between two parties who cannot afford it."

Oscar's hands had closed into fists without his permission. "My soul. My name in your ledgers. That's why I'm here."

"That is why you're here," the king agreed. "But it is not why you'll stay."

* * *

He dined alone with the king in a hall lit by a single uncaged star — the largest one, Oscar guessed, the pole star perhaps, hanging in a cage of black iron wide as a wagon wheel, burning with a patience that hurt to look at. The food was simple and perfect. The wine tasted like summers he half-remembered from childhood, and he suspected it was engineered to.

"You are wondering what I want," the king said.

"You want souls. You've been buying them for centuries. But your ledgers—" Oscar stopped. He had seen the archives' outer shelves on the walk; he had not been permitted inside yet, but the outer shelves had been enough. Volumes stacked to the ceiling, and the spines of the newest ones thinner than the oldest, and the ink in the newest ones fainter, as though the court were running out of something even ink needed. "Your ledgers are starving."

The king did not deny it. He poured more wine, unhurried.

"A court that owes nothing must still eat," he said. "We eat slowly. We eat what we buy. And we have bought so much, for so long, that the world above has begun to notice the missing weight — hence your winter, hence your collapsed wards, hence you, sitting across my table with your father's stubbornness and your mother's arithmetic, hating me and envying me in the same breath."

"I don't envy you."

"You envy my books." The king smiled again, that terrible warm smile. "Every keeper does. Complete records. Perfect continuity. No entry lost, no debt forgotten, no name mislaid. You have spent three years in a guild that keeps accounts of impossible things, and you know — you have always known — that their ledger is a sieve. Mine is not. Mine holds everything. Every soul. Every year. Every bottle of luck and jar of borrowed time, going back to the founding." He leaned forward. "And here is my offer, Oscar Oyelaran, and I make it to every account come due, and I have never once had to lie to make it:

"Stay. Keep my ledgers. Restore your soul to yourself — it is yours, I will sign it back this very night, freely, because a soul that wants to leave is worth less than one that chooses to remain. Live in a city where nothing is owed, doing the one work you love, for a court that will outlast the sun. Your mistress sold you. Your witch sold you. The world above froze mid-sentence and let you walk out into the cold. What exactly are you loyal to? What, precisely, do you imagine you'd be giving up?"

And the horror of it — the true horror, the one Oscar would carry longer than any scar — was that the answer took a long time coming.

He thought of the Ledgerhouse, cold and drafty, where Vess Corvane had signed his name like a line item. He thought of Mother Ilse, and the grimoire, and the fever, and the word *mercy* used as a receipt. He thought of the frozen woman at the well, laughing forever. He thought of Sable, asleep upstairs in a room designed to be impossible to leave, and of the tollbridge, and of the lie he had told for her, and how easily it had come, and how much he had hated that.

Nothing is owed here. It sounded like rest. It sounded like the end of a very long audit.

It sounded like a lie told so smoothly that even a man who could no longer lie wanted to believe it.

"My loyalty," Oscar said finally, "is to the numbers. And your numbers are wrong."

The king's smile faded by a single degree — the first true expression Oscar had seen on his face.

"Show me," the king said softly. "The archives are open to you, account come due. Read my ledgers. All of them. Find the error." He rose, and the caged pole-star dimmed as if in deference. "No one ever has. But you should know — the last auditor who tried is still down there. Reading. He has been reading for two hundred years, and he is very nearly happy."

* * *

The archive doors were older than the city, older than the crown, older — Oscar was almost certain — than the king himself. They opened at his touch without a sound.

Inside, the dark went back farther than lamplight could follow, and the shelves went back farther than the dark.

Somewhere in the deep stacks, a quill was scratching. Steady. Patient. Endless.

Oscar stepped inside, and the doors closed behind him like the cover of a book.
