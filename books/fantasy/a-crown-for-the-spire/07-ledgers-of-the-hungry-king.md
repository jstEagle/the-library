## 7. Ledgers of the Hungry King

The king kept his word.

That was the worst of it, Oscar thought, following the chamberlain down the long throat of the archive — that the king of the court beneath the mountain kept his word with the punctiliousness of a guild clerk. *You may read what we hold,* he had said, folding his long hands on a throne of black ice shot through with starlight, *for an honest reader is worth ten ambassadors.* And so Oscar was given a lamp that burned without oil and a key that fit every lock, and led into the deep shelves where the court stored what it had bought.

The archive was not a room. It was a city turned inward — galleries stacked on galleries, descending past the reach of any light Oscar had ever known, each shelf carved from the same glassy dark as the cavern walls. The books were not books. He had expected souls to be kept in bottles, or bells, or birds; instead they were kept in bindings. Every volume on every shelf was bound in something soft and pale that he refused, for three full galleries, to name aloud.

*Ledger-keeper,* the chamberlain had called him, with a small bow, as though the title were an honor rather than an accusation.

Oscar ran his thumb along a spine and felt it shiver. Warm. All of them were warm. Three years of auditing impossible inventory had taught him the difference between things that were alive and things that merely remembered being alive, and these bindings sat squarely between — held at the temperature of a sleeping body, breathing at the pace of a page turning.

He opened the nearest one.

Not a soul, this one. A record. The court's own hand, in ink the color of dried blood: *Received, one winter's patience, from the village of Harrow's End, in exchange for one good harvest per generation.* Below it, in smaller script: *Paid. Paid. Paid. Paid.* Four payments across four hundred years, and then nothing, because Harrow's End no longer existed, and contracts outlive their counterparties like debts outlive their debtors.

Oscar closed it gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and moved deeper in.

* * *

He worked the way he always worked — by column, by date, by cross-reference — because method was the only prayer he trusted. The court's accounting was beautiful. That was the horror of it. Every entry balanced. Every soul received was matched against something paid out: warmth, luck, borrowed years, immunity from winter, a child saved from fever. He found Mother Ilse's signature twice before he let himself look for it deliberately — her mark cramped and fierce, the same hand that had taught him his letters, selling him south for thirty-one years of his life and getting, by the ledger's cold arithmetic, exactly what she'd paid for.

*Debtor: O. Oyelaran. Term: indefinite. Collateral retained.*

His own name, in someone else's book, for longer than he had been alive.

He set that volume aside with hands that did not quite shake, because if they started shaking now they would not stop, and there was work to do. There was always work to do. Numbers did not care whether you wept over them, which was why he loved them, which was why they had betrayed him so completely.

It took him until the third bell of the court — a soundless bell, felt in the teeth rather than heard — to find the discrepancy.

Every great fraud lives in a discrepancy. He had learned that in the Ledgerhouse, auditing luck-traders who bottled other people's Tuesdays: find the number that doesn't move when everything else moves, and you've found the wound. Here, in a library of stolen souls, everything balanced except one figure, repeated at the bottom of every annual summary going back six hundred years, written in the king's own careful hand:

*Reserve: sufficient.*

Sufficient for what? No entry defined it. No contract referenced it. It simply recurred, year after year, century after century, a single unexamined line beneath columns of exquisite detail — the way a man might write *fine* when asked how he is, meaning anything but.

Oscar pulled the oldest summary off its shelf and carried it to his lamp, and began, line by line, to do the arithmetic the king had never done.

* * *

Here is what the numbers said:

The court beneath the mountain had been starving since before it had a king. It was a hungry place by nature — a country with no sun, no harvest, no outside — and it had survived the way starving things survive, by trade. It sold wonders. It bought warmth. For a thousand years the exchange had been manageable, a hearth fed log by log, each soul burned slowly, gratefully, over generations.

And then, some four centuries back, the entries changed character. The purchases grew larger. Faster. Souls taken whole instead of leased. Villages bought outright. Years stripped from people who hadn't agreed to sell them — acquired through intermediaries, through hedge-witches, through ward-smiths whose wards conveniently failed. The court stopped eating like a beggar and started eating like a famine.

Oscar traced the escalation to its source and found a single annotation in the king's hand, dated four hundred years ago, brief as a confession:

*Bought too much. Cannot give it back. They will not stop needing us, and we cannot stop taking. Find a way to keep it all.*

*Keep it all.* So the court had built cages for stars, to hoard their light against the lean centuries. So it had grown its holdings, soul upon soul upon soul, until the reserve — the *reserve*, that quiet little lie at the bottom of every page — meant the accumulated weight of everything the court owed and could never repay.

And a hoard that size needs somewhere to go.

Oscar stood very still among the warm books, and understood.

The winter wasn't weather. The winter wasn't punishment, or war, or the failure of Sable's wards — those had only been the hinge, not the hand. The winter was digestion. The court had swallowed more than any world could hold, and the world above was the stomach it was passing through. Every frozen village, every moment pooling like ice, every breath hanging unfinished in the air — that was the court processing its accounts. Taking the warmth it had purchased out of circulation. Converting the living into the liquid, the liquid into the stored.

The stars hadn't gone out. They'd been *collected*. Outstanding balances, called in all at once.

He thought of Vess Corvane in her tower of ledgers, signing names as collateral, buying the guild's immunity from winter — buying immunity from a thing she must have known was not a season. He thought of himself, three years behind a counting desk, recording trades of bottled luck with tidy precision, never once asking where the luck came from or who went without. He had believed the numbers made him innocent. The numbers had made him complicit. A ledger does not need your malice. It only needs your diligence.

Oscar put his palm flat against the shelf, against the warm pale binding of some farmer's grandmother, and felt it breathe under his hand.

"I'm sorry," he whispered, and hated the smallness of it. Sorry was not a currency anyone accepted here.

* * *

He did not remember deciding to keep reading, but he kept reading. That was the sickness of his kind — the keeper's curse — that even in the belly of the thing you audit it. Gallery after gallery, century after century, he built the true account in his head while the false one sat smugly balanced on every shelf: total souls held, total warmth extracted, total time remaining before the digestion finished and the world above went still forever.

The answer, when he finished it, was not a number. It was a date. Roughly speaking, it was spring — except that spring would no longer exist, because the last of the thaw would be inside these shelves, bound in pale leather, waiting to be spent by a king who had run out of ways to spend it.

"You've found it."

The voice came from the gallery above, unhurried, courteous, echoing down through the stacks like water finding its level. The king stood at the railing with his hands folded, caged starlight moving slowly in the dark of him, and he did not look angry. He looked relieved.

"Four hundred years I have waited," the king said, "for someone to finish the sum. My clerks can't. They stop at the discrepancy and write *sufficient* and go to lunch. My queens couldn't bear to. But you—" He descended the stair without seeming to touch it. "You read to the bottom of the page. That's rare. That's *precious*."

"It's just arithmetic," Oscar said.

"It is never just arithmetic." The king stopped an arm's length away, near enough that Oscar could feel the cold coming off him like the cold off a ledger left open in winter. "You know what I am now. Starving. Insatiable. A debtor wearing a crown. Tell me, keeper — knowing all of it — do you despise me?"

Oscar should have said yes. He opened his mouth to say yes, and what came out instead was the truth, because he had looked at six hundred years of entries and seen his own hand in every one of them:

"I understand you. That's worse."

Something moved behind the king's face — not a smile, but the place where a smile would be kept, the way the court kept stars. "Yes," he said softly. "That's why I let you in. Not to punish me. To *inherit*." He gestured, and the whole archive seemed to lean toward Oscar, every warm spine, every sleeping soul, the entire unbearable balance of it. "Someone must hold this account. Someone must decide what gets spent and what gets forgiven. A crown is only a ledger with better binding, and you, Oscar Oyelaran, are the finest reader I have ever owned."

"I'm not yours."

"Check the book," the king said gently, and turned, and left him alone with the shelves.

Oscar stood a long time in the breathing dark. Then he took out the stub of pencil he had carried since the Ledgerhouse, tore a blank leaf from the oldest summary, and wrote, in a hand that shook only a little:

*Audit commenced. All accounts to be examined. Nothing to be considered sufficient ever again.*

Above him, far above, through a mile of mountain, the frozen world hung waiting — and somewhere in it, Sable was doing something reckless with the last of her craft. He felt it the way you feel a change in pressure, the way a ledger feels a hand reaching for it.

He picked up his lamp and started for the stairs.
