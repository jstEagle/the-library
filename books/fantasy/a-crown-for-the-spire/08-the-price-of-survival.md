## 8. The Price of Survival

Sable had built a thousand wards in her life, and every one of them had been a promise: *nothing gets through this line that shouldn't.* She had believed, once, that promises were the strongest thing a smith could forge. Standing now in the antechamber of the hungry king's court, watching the caged stars pulse overhead like hearts in jars, she understood that promises were also the easiest thing to break. She had broken the biggest one ever made. The border wards had been her masterpiece, and she had built them wrong — or been made to build them wrong; the memory of that was gone now, spent at a tollbridge, and its absence was a hole in her she kept pressing her thumb against, the way you probe a missing tooth.

The king received them in a hall of black glass, and he was courteous the way deep water is courteous. It lets you in gently. It holds you. It does not care that you cannot swim.

"Two guests," he said, and his voice was the sound a ledger makes when it closes. "Two debts. And I am nothing if not fair."

He gestured, and the air between them unfolded like paper.

Sable saw it first as a door — a plain door, oak and iron, the kind she'd grown up behind in the ward-smith's quarter. Through it she could see the surface world, thawed. Green hills. Smoke rising from chimneys. Her old master's forge, the fire lit, her name chiseled back onto the lintel where the guild had struck it. *Reinstated. Forgiven. Home.*

"The winter ends for you," the king said. "The wards are rebuilt — rebuilt *right*, this time, by hands that remember how. Your disgrace unmade. All it costs is what you were already prepared to spend at the tollbridge: him." He inclined his head toward Oscar, who stood very still, the way men do when they've just realized the floor is a mouth. "The court will keep his account. He came here to be collected. Let him be."

Sable's mouth was dry. That was the horror of it — the offer wasn't grotesque. It was *reasonable*. It was the first bargain in this whole starving kingdom that made sense. Oscar had been sold decades ago; the debt existed before either of them drew breath. She would simply be letting an old transaction complete itself.

And she would go home.

She looked at the door. She looked at the forge-fire through it, at the light on the anvil, and something in her chest — the thing that had been freezing since the tollbridge, since the wards fell, since the stars went out — something in her leaned toward that warmth like a plant toward a window.

"Say yes," the king said softly. "You've already paid so much. Why not be paid *back*?"

*Why not.* She felt the yes rising in her like water filling a boot. She felt how easy it would be. And that easiness frightened her more than the winter did, because she recognized it — it was the same easiness she must have felt when she built the wards wrong, the same smooth, reasonable slope down into ruin. Whatever memory the toll-keeper had taken, she was certain now it hadn't been a mistake she'd made. It had been a *price* she'd accepted. The court had been buying her for years, in installments, and this was simply the final payment coming due.

Oscar hadn't moved. He was watching her with those ledger-keeper's eyes, and she realized he was doing the arithmetic for her — that he would let her take the offer, that he had already entered himself in the loss column, that he stood there not begging but *balancing*.

"No," Sable said.

The word came out small. She said it again, louder, and it became true by the saying. "No. You don't get him. You don't get any of them." She turned to Oscar. "You came here to steal everyone the ledgers ever bound. I'm not going to be the reason you leave with one."

"Sable." His voice cracked on her name. "You don't owe me—"

"I owe *everybody*." She was already moving, pulling the last of her kit from her coat — the ward-smith's kit, the one they'd let her keep because the court thought it was spent, because she'd traded its secret at the tollbridge. But a smith's craft was never in the tools. It was in the hands. And her hands still remembered everything the toll-keeper couldn't reach. "The wards I built were wrong in one specific way. I don't remember *why* anymore. But I remember *how*. And a ward built wrong can be un-built."

The king's courtesy flickered — the first hairline crack in that deep, patient water. "Child," he said, "you are in my court. Nothing here burns unless I allow it."

"Then allow it," Sable said, and struck the spark.

* * *

Her last ward-craft was not a thing of chalk and silver. It was the oldest kind, the kind apprentices were told was legend: a ward written in the smith's own warmth, in the body-heat of every cold hour she'd ever worked through, every night she'd stayed at the forge until her fingers blistered, every promise she'd ever kept. She poured it all out through her palms into the first cage.

The cage was a lattice of black glass, and inside it a star — small now, dim, but *hers*, she realized, hers and everyone's, a piece of the common sky the court had stolen and hoarded. The star pressed against the lattice as her ward bit into the glass, and it made a sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard. A sound like a held breath finally being let go.

"You cannot," the king said, and for the first time his voice was not courteous. It was *hungry*. "The winter is digestion. The court must feed. If you free them, everything here starves — including the souls we hold. Including *him*."

"Then you should have thought of that," Sable said, "before you bought more than any world can hold."

The second cage broke. The third. Each one cost her more — she could feel the ward-craft draining out of her like heat from an open door, could feel herself growing colder, thinner, a woman becoming an outline. The court screamed around her. Not the courtiers — the court itself, the black glass walls groaning as the architecture of theft came apart, as the hoarded starlight remembered it was sky.

Oscar was beside her now, breaking cages with his ledger-knife, slashing entries, and she heard him shouting the names as each star came free — reading them off the page, giving them back their names so they'd know the way home. *Alder. Maren. Sixteen souls of Harrow Fen. The child of the toll-keeper—*

The toll-keeper. Sable's hands stuttered on the fifth cage. The toll-keeper had taken her memory, had demanded it, and she had paid without question — because she'd assumed her guilt was real. But if the court had coerced her, if the toll-keeper was the king's creature, then the memory hadn't been payment. It had been *evidence*, removed. She had burned the world's wards wrong because she'd been made to, and then she'd paid to forget she'd been made to, and then she'd hated herself for three years for a crime that was done *to* her.

She almost stopped. Almost sat down in the cold and wept for the woman she'd been punishing.

Then she broke the fifth cage instead.

* * *

The court did not crack all at once. It cracked the way ice does on a thawing lake — one long report, then a shudder, then everything at once. The black glass walls split along lines no one had drawn, and through the cracks came not light but *darkness* — the honest dark of the world above, the real night, rushing in to reclaim what had been caged. Stars tore free of their lattices and streamed upward through the fissures, and where they passed, the hungry court simply *ended*, hall after hall going out like candles pinched between wet fingers.

The king stood in the center of his dissolving throne room, and Sable saw him truly for the first time: not a monarch but a *balance sheet*, a thing made entirely of what it had collected, and as the collection fled he grew thinner and thinner, more and more transparent, until he was mostly outline, mostly want.

"You will freeze," he told them, and there was almost pity in it. "The winter doesn't end because the stars go home. The winter ends when the debts are paid. And no one has paid."

"Someone's coming to pay," Sable said, pulling Oscar toward the rising dark, toward the crack of open sky above. "She's got a ledger, and she's got guild law, and she's got a co-signer. You're not starving anymore, your majesty. You're *in audit*."

The last thing she saw of the court was its king reaching after his fleeing stars the way a miser reaches after burning coins.

Then the dark took them, and the cold, and the long climb toward a surface world still locked in winter — but a world where, far above, faint as memory, the first returned star was finding its old place in the sky.

And Oscar, gasping beside her on the ice, said: "The audit has to happen *here*. Before the court finishes dying. If the king dissolves before the debts are settled—"

"—everyone he holds dissolves with him," Sable finished.

Above them, the mountain began to shake.
