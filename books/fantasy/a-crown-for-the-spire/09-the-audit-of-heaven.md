## 9. The Audit of Heaven

The court was running.

That was the only word for it. The cavern city beneath the mountain — with its bone-white arcades and its caged stars swinging in their thousands like lanterns in a storm — had begun to move, the way a body moves when it knows the blood is leaving it. Courtiers poured down the great stair in silks that had not creased in centuries. Servants carried away trays of bottled breath. Somewhere below us, doors I had never seen were being unbarred, and through them came a sound like the mountain swallowing.

Sable stood beside me at the edge of the archive terrace, her hands still blistered from the burning of her last ward-craft, and above us the freed stars strained against nothing now — they hung loose in the vaulted dark, untethered, humming like struck bells.

"They're going to run," she said. "The king's going to take the whole court deeper. There's always a deeper."

"Then we'd better open the books," I said, "before he closes them."

I had the ledger under my arm. Not the court's — mine. Three years of my own hand, every entry honest because I had made it honest, page after page of souls traded and years borrowed and luck bottled, and now one more thing written in ink that still smelled fresh: *Audit invoked. Guild law, first principle. All accounts subject to inspection. No contract stands unaudited.*

It was the oldest law the Ledgerhouse had, older than Vess Corvane, older than the guild itself maybe, back when the first keeper sat down with the first impossible thing and wrote down what it cost. And there was a reason no one had ever invoked it at this scale. Guild law did not bind by force. It bound by consent — the consent of every party who put their name to the audit, agreeing to be counted. All of them. Everything they were, weighed on the scales.

The king's hoard would have to be opened. His contracts read aloud. Every soul he held, named and returned. And every debt he was owed — collected from him, in the only coin he had left.

Himself.

* * *

We caught up with the procession on the Stair of Teeth, where the steps were actual teeth, each one taller than a man, molars of some creature that had dreamed this mountain into being. The king did not hurry. He stood halfway down, white hands folded, watching his court stream past him like water around a stone, and when he saw me coming he smiled the way winter smiles — patiently, without malice, certain of the season.

"The account come due," he said. "I wondered how long you'd make me wait, Oscar Oyelaran."

"Guild law," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, and I let it crack, because I had learned down here that a tremor honestly shown is worth more than any steadiness faked. "First principle. I invoke the full audit of this court. Every contract. Every soul. Every debt. Settled now, against your own hoard."

The stair went quiet. Courtiers stopped mid-flight, and I watched their faces — those beautiful starved faces — turn toward their king, and I understood that they were afraid of something other than the cracking of their world. They were afraid of arithmetic.

"You have no authority here," the king said mildly.

"I have a ledger." I held it up. "And guild law doesn't need your permission to begin. Only your name. Refuse to sign, and the audit proceeds as default — everything you hold, forfeit, unweighed, returned to the world that made it. You've read enough contracts to know what default means. You wrote the clause into half of yours."

Something moved behind the king's face. Not anger. Appetite meeting its limit.

"And if I sign?" he said. "If I let you open my books, boy — do you know what you're asking? This court has been buying for nine hundred years. Souls, years, names, first loves, last words. If every account is settled, there will be nothing left of this place but the mountain's bones. My people will scatter into a world that has forgotten them. They will starve in the light."

"Yes," I said. "That's what justice costs. I've been reading your ledgers all week. I know exactly what it costs. You taught me the arithmetic yourself — you just never thought anyone would dare do the sum out loud."

He looked at me for a long moment. And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I get: part of him wanted to sign. Part of him was tired the way only something immortal can be tired, hungry the way only something overfull can hunger. Nine hundred years of eating and still starving, and I think some sliver of him had been waiting for an auditor the way a debtor waits for the knock on the door — dreading it, and longing for it, because the knock means it finally ends.

"Then I'll need a co-signatory," he said softly. "Guild law requires two parties to bind an audit of this magnitude. You are one party, holding one ledger. Who stands with you, accountant?"

And that was the trap, and I knew it even as I stepped on it. Sable couldn't sign — she was party to the ward-collapse; she'd be audited into ash. I had no one. The guild was a mountain away and a traitor's bargain deep, and the king knew it, and his smile began to come back like frost across a window—

"I do."

The voice came from the top of the stair.

Vess Corvane descended the teeth the way she descended everything, as if the architecture owed her passage. She wore her traveling coat and her guild signet and an expression I had never seen on her in three years of working under her: she looked *light*. She looked like a woman who had set down something enormous.

"Mistress," I said. "You can't—"

"Don't tell me what I can't, apprentice. You've been doing it since the wards fell." She reached us and took my ledger from my hands and read the invocation page, all of it, unhurried, while the king watched her with something close to recognition. They had signed things together before. I could see it in the way they avoided looking directly at each other.

"You brought the guild's seal," the king said.

"I brought more than that." She set the ledger on a tooth, flat, and took out her pen — her good pen, the one with the nib of star-iron that had signed away my soul thirty years before I was born. "Guild law, first principle. A co-signatory binds herself to the audit entire. Everything she holds is weighed with the rest." She looked at me then, and her eyes were dry and very clear. "Everything I hold, Oscar. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

My mouth was very dry. "A name," I said. "You'd pay with your name."

"Audit requires payment from every party in proportion to what they hold. The king holds nine hundred years of theft; he pays himself. I hold one stolen soul and thirty years of interest." She uncapped the pen. "I hold your soul, boy. It comes back to you tonight. And the price of making me pay it is that no one will ever remember who paid it. That's how a name settles. It doesn't die. It's *unremembered*. Every record, every memory, every mark of my hand becomes anonymous. The guild will have ledgers no one can attribute. You—" her voice caught, once, the only crack she allowed herself— "you will keep an honest book, and you will never remember whose signature taught you how."

"Don't," I said. It came out small. "Vess. Don't."

"You think I'm sacrificing myself?" For a moment she was the mistress again, sharp as a paper cut. "I'm settling accounts. I sold you before you could walk. I spent thirty years keeping that entry balanced so no one else would collect it — do you know what that's been? Watching you count other people's souls for three years, waiting for you to find your own line. You were never my apprentice, Oscar. You were my penance. And penance isn't meant to be comfortable. It's meant to be *paid*."

She turned to the ledger. And the king, courteous to the last, inclined his head and extended one white hand, and I watched — I will always have watched — as Vess Corvane wrote the audit binding in her own hand and then, beneath it, signed not her name but the space where her name had been, and the space took the ink and closed over it like water, and she straightened, and she was still standing there, and she was already gone.

Not dead. Present. Solid. Looking at me with the faint courtesy of a stranger.

"I appear to be party to an audit," she said pleasantly. "Shall we proceed?"

* * *

We proceeded.

I will not pretend I conducted it well. My hands shook through the first hour. But the ledgers of the hungry king opened at Vess's anonymous word and the king's reluctant signature, and the audit ran through the cavern city like fire through a dry field — contracts read aloud in the star-light, each one weighed, each one broken or honored, and the souls came out of the hoard the way light comes out of a struck flint. They rose through the vaulted dark in their thousands, streaming past the caged stars, past the fleeing courtiers who stopped fleeing to watch, up the Stair of Teeth and out through the mountain's throat toward the sky they'd been bought from.

Some of them, as they passed, touched my shoulder. None of them knew why. Most hadn't known they'd been sold. That was the shape of the king's crime, and I wrote it all down.

By the time the last contract settled, the court was nearly empty. The king stood alone on his throne-step with his hoard dispersed into the world and his debts called in, and what remained of him was thin as paper, and I felt no triumph. I felt the arithmetic. Justice, it turns out, does not feel like victory. It feels like a column that finally balances, and the grief of everything the balancing cost.

"Accountant," the king said, and there was something almost like respect in it. "What becomes of me?"

I looked at the ledger. The final line was blank.

"You go in the book," I said. "Like everything else. Honestly."

Above us, the freed stars began to rise.

* * *

We came out of the mountain at dawn, Sable and I and the woman who had been Vess Corvane, who walked between us carrying nothing and belonging to no one and remembering, when I asked, only that she had always intended to see the sky. The snow was stopping. Above the tree line the first star of evening — or the first star of morning, I couldn't tell, and didn't care — punched through the clearing dark like a nail through a coffin lid, and then another, and another, until the whole sky was studded with returning light and somewhere far off I heard the long winter exhale, and begin, slowly, to break.

Sable put her blistered hand on my arm. "What now?"

I had the ledger under my arm. My hand, on it, had stopped shaking.

"Now," I said, "we start writing honestly. And the first entry — the first one, before anything else — needs a witness."

Down the valley, small in the morning light, a crooked cottage chimney was sending up smoke, and I could see, even at this distance, a figure standing in the garden, waiting as if she'd been told.

Mother Ilse raised one hand.

And I realized, with the ledger warm against my ribs, that I had no idea what I was going to say to her — and that for the first time in my life, whatever came out of my mouth would have to be true.
