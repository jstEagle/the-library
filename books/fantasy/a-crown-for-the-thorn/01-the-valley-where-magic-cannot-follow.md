## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The stones remembered the words even when Jonah Blackwood could not.

That was the whole trick of it, his master Aldric had told him, the last winter of the old man's life. You don't carry the ward. The ward carries you. You're just the hand that turns the key, and the key is older than your grandmother's grandmother, and it will outlast you too, boy, so stop looking so solemn about it.

Jonah looked solemn about it anyway. He had been looking solemn about it for nineteen years, since the night he buried Aldric under the rowan tree and walked back down to the village alone with a sword on his hip that no one else in the world knew how to wake.

Now it was the eve of the Founding Festival, and the light was going amber over the eastern ridgeline, and Jonah was climbing the ward-road with a lantern in one hand and a satchel of river-salt in the other, exactly as he had climbed it on the eve of every festival since he was twenty-two years old.

The Thorn Valley did not look like the last sanctuary of anything. It looked like what it was: a long green fold of country between two gray ridges, stitched with barley fields and orchards, threaded by a river that ran cold and quick from the high snows. Smoke rose from forty chimneys. Someone's dog was barking at someone's geese down near the mill. The evening smelled of woodsmoke and cut hay and the first apples rotting sweet under the trees where nobody had bothered to pick them.

It was, Jonah thought, the most ordinary place in the world, which was precisely the miracle. Everything else had gone strange. The free cities had grown fat and frightened behind their treaties. The usurper's banners — a black thorn on white — flew from more keeps every year. And magic, real magic, the kind that ate men whole and wore their voices afterward, crept back into the world like water finding cracks in a floor.

But not here. Here, magic came to the valley's edge and stopped, the way a wolf stops at a fire. A hundred years ago, the order of swordspeakers had spoken the Great Warding into twelve boundary stones set around the valley's rim, and the words had not faded since. Sorcery could not cross. Enchanted steel dulled at the line. A witch could stand on the northern ridge and look down at the barley fields and see nothing but barley fields, because the ward bent perception as easily as it bent power.

Inside the Thorn Valley, a man was only a man. Jonah had spent his whole life making sure it stayed that way.

He reached the first stone as the sun touched the ridge.

* * *

There were twelve stones, and the circuit took him from dusk until well past moonrise, and he walked it twice a year — once at the founding festival, once at the turning of winter — and he had never once skipped it, though there had been years he'd wanted to. The year his fever broke on the third stone and he'd finished the circuit shivering and half-blind. The year old Marta Hale died and he'd spoken the words at her stone with her funeral still fresh on him, ash still in his hair from the pyre.

The ritual itself was simple, the way a heart is simple. At each stone he set down the lantern. He poured a thin circle of river-salt around the base. He laid his bare palm flat against the stone and spoke the First Oath aloud, all nine lines of it, in the old tongue that no one alive used for anything else.

*I am the wall where the world breaks.*

*I speak, and the lie dies in my mouth.*

*What I guard, I guard without reward; what I love, I love without keeping.*

And so on. Nine lines. It took less than a minute. When he finished, the stone warmed under his palm — faintly, the way a hearthstone holds heat hours after the fire dies — and somewhere along the invisible line connecting this stone to the next, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep and settled again.

He'd felt it the first time at age nine, pressed between Aldric and the stone, and it had frightened him so badly he'd cried. He'd felt it ten thousand times since, and now it was like hearing his own heartbeat: proof of life, unremarked.

Tonight, at the first stone, it turned over in its sleep and settled again, same as always.

"Evening, old girl," Jonah said to the stone, because talking to it was another part of the ritual, the part Aldric had never written down. "Another year. Nobody missed us."

The stone said nothing. Stones were good listeners that way.

He shouldered the satchel and walked on toward the second.

* * *

The order of swordspeakers had numbered four hundred, once. Jonah knew this the way he knew the shape of his own hands, because he had read the rolls in the archive beneath his house — four hundred names in a ledger whose ink had gone brown as old blood. They had been the truth-keepers of an age that had needed them: men and women who could speak words so precisely, so honestly, that the world obeyed. Swordspeech, Aldric called it. Truth spoken into steel. A bound blade could not be raised against a witness. A bound army could not march on a lie.

And then the age had decided it preferred its lies comfortable, and the order had been hunted, and sued, and mocked, and finally simply forgotten, the way a well is forgotten after everyone moves to the river. Four hundred names. One left.

Jonah Blackwood, last oath-bound swordspeaker. Keeper of the Thorn Valley. Sworn to silence about the order's secrets, sworn to the ward, sworn never to speak the deep words except in defense of the innocent, sworn — this one had cost him the most — sworn to stay. To hold the valley until relieved or dead, and no relief was coming, because there was no one left to send.

He thought about the oaths a great deal on the ward-road, because there was nothing else to think about, and because the walking put him in mind of it. Nineteen years of circuits. Two a year. Thirty-eight times around the rim of the valley, speaking nine lines at each of twelve stones. Four thousand and some utterances of the First Oath, and in all that time, the words had never changed and the valley had never fallen and no one had ever come to relieve him.

Some nights he lay awake doing the arithmetic of his life and found it came out to almost nothing. A village of farmers who liked him well enough and understood him not at all. A house full of books no one else could read. A sword named Verity hanging above his hearth, which had not been drawn from its sheath in nineteen years, because drawing it would mean speaking the deep words, and speaking the deep words meant the valley had already failed.

He was forty-one years old. He had held the line his whole adult life. And if you asked him — and nobody ever asked him, but if you did — he could not have told you whether he was a guardian or just a man standing very still in an empty room, calling the stillness duty because the alternative was admitting he was afraid to leave it.

At the fourth stone, down where the ward-line crossed the old shepherd's track, he paused longer than usual. From here he could see the whole valley laid out below: the village with its festival lanterns already going up along the common, orange sparks strung between the eaves like a constellation someone had knocked loose. Children shrieking happily somewhere. Fiddles being tuned, badly, the way Tam Wicker tuned everything.

They don't know, he thought, the way he thought it every time. Not one soul down there knows what holds the roof up. They think the valley is safe because it has always been safe. They think the ward is a story we tell children, like the story of the founding, the twelve heroes and the burning night.

Well. Let them. A wall does its best work when nobody remembers it's there.

He spoke the Fourth Oath into the stone and moved on.

* * *

By the ninth stone the moon was up and full and the night had gone silver-blue, and Jonah's legs had settled into the long rhythm they knew, and his mind had done what it always did on the back half of the circuit: drifted to Aldric.

Nineteen years dead, and still the loudest voice in his head.

You'll want to leave, boy. Everyone wants to leave. The order wasn't made of monks; it was made of people who chose, every morning, to stay boring and useful instead of going off to be heroic. Heroism is a hunger. Duty is a meal. Learn the difference before it learns it for you.

"I learned it," Jonah said aloud to the empty hillside. His breath fogged in the cooling air. "I've eaten nothing but duty for nineteen years and I'm still hungry."

The hillside offered no comment. That was the trouble with a dead teacher: he got the last word forever.

Aldric had died badly, in the end — not in battle, nothing so clean. His heart had simply quit in the middle of a winter circuit, on the eleventh stone, mid-oath, and Jonah had caught him as he fell and finished the Ninth Oath for him with the old man's weight going cold against his shoulder, and that had been that. No enemy. No glory. Just a body giving out on schedule, and a valley that didn't notice, and a young man suddenly holding four hundred years of inheritance in two shaking hands.

Jonah had sometimes wondered, in the dark, whether Aldric had chosen that stone on purpose. Whether the old man had known his heart was failing and picked the eleventh stone so that his last act would be the work itself, so that his death would be indistinguishable from his duty. It would have been like him. It would have been exactly like him, and Jonah both admired it and resented it, and expected to spend the rest of his life doing both.

He reached the tenth stone as the moon cleared the ridge.

Set down the lantern. Poured the salt. Laid his palm on the stone, rough lichen under his skin, and opened his mouth to begin the Tenth Oath—

—and stopped.

Because the stone was cold.

Not cool. Cold. Winter-cold, grave-cold, the kind of cold that has a taste to it, iron and ash at the back of the throat. In thirty-eight circuits, through fevers and blizzards and the worst grief of his life, the stone had never once failed to warm at his touch. The ward answered the oath the way a struck bell answers the hammer. Always. It was the one fixed thing in a life that had otherwise come apart at every seam.

Jonah stood very still. Down in the valley, faintly, the fiddlers had started up, and the sound floated thin and bright across the dark fields.

He tried again. Palm flat. Breath steady. He spoke the first line of the Tenth Oath, and his voice came out low and true, the way it always did:

*I am the wall where the world breaks—*

The stone stayed cold.

Somewhere far off — north, he thought, or maybe northeast, hard to tell in the dark — a sound rolled across the ridges. Not thunder. Thunder didn't arrive in silence first. This was thunder with a hole in the middle of it, a boom with the center scooped out, and the silence inside it lasted a half-second too long, and every hair on Jonah's arms stood up beneath his sleeves.

Then, along the crest of the northern ridge, directly above the eleventh stone, the air flickered.

There was no better word for it. The darkness above the ridgeline shivered, the way heat shivers above summer road, and for one instant Jonah saw — or thought he saw, or prayed he didn't see — a seam of pale light open along the ward-line itself, a hairline crack running east to west along the top of the world, glowing like a fault of lightning frozen mid-strike.

Then it was gone. The ridge was just a ridge. The night was just a night.

Jonah's hand was still on the cold stone. His heart was slamming against his ribs like something trying to get out.

"Aldric," he said quietly, to no one, to the dark, to the dead. "Tell me I'm tired. Tell me I imagined it."

No answer. There never was.

He finished the circuit — he finished it, because the oath said finish it, because the oath was the only structure left holding him upright — and at each remaining stone he poured the salt and spoke the lines and waited for the warmth, and at the eleventh stone the warmth came late and thin, like a fire struggling to catch, and at the twelfth it came not at all.

Jonah stood at the twelfth stone with his palm on cold granite and looked down at the valley, at the lanterns, at the fiddle music drifting up like smoke, at the hundred small ordinary lives stacked inside a wall that had just, for the first time in a hundred years, flickered.

Something was coming through.

He didn't know what. He didn't know when. But the stones knew, and the stones were cold, and the last oath-bound swordspeaker of the Thorn Valley stood alone on the rim of the only safe place left in the world and felt it begin to be unsafe.

Down in the village, the fiddlers played on. The festival would start at dawn.

Jonah picked up his lantern and started down the ward-road, walking faster than he ever had in his life.
