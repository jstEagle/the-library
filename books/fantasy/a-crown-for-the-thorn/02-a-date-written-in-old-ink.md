## 2. A Date Written in Old Ink

The archive door had three locks, and Jonah Blackwood hated every one of them.

Not because they were hard to open. He'd been opening them since he was nine years old, when Master Corren first pressed the iron key into his palm and said, *You'll be the last to carry this, so carry it well.* The locks were easy. The weight was the hard part — the knowledge that behind the third lock sat everything his order had ever been, and that he was the only living soul who could walk in and touch it.

He lit the lamp on the archive's stone shelf, and the small flame threw long shadows across shelves of leather spines gone soft as old bread. Two hundred years of records. Oath-ledgers. Ward-maps. The correspondence of dead swordspeakers who had once numbered in the hundreds and now numbered in exactly one.

Him.

"Good evening," he said to the room, because his mother had raised him polite and because there was no one else to say it to.

The room, as always, did not answer.

Tonight he wasn't here for sentiment. Tomorrow was Founding Day — the hundredth anniversary of it, no less — and the whole valley would come down from its farms and terraces for the festival. There would be dancing on the green, and old Marta's honey-cakes, and children chasing each other through the wheat with stick-swords, pretending at a brotherhood none of them had ever seen. Someone had to make sure the ward-records were current before all that. Someone had to sign the ledger confirming the valley's protection held.

Someone. Always him.

Jonah pulled down the ward-ledger from its place and set it on the reading table, then paused. His hand hovered over the shelf where it always rested, and something made him look one row lower, at the books nobody touched. The sealed spines. The ones wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord gone brittle as straw.

Master Corren had told him once, near the end: *If the ward-line ever flickers, boy — if it so much as stutters — you go to the sealed row. Promise me.*

It hadn't flickered. Not tonight, not any night in the six years since Jonah had taken up the ritual alone. But promises have a way of itching, and the festival tomorrow felt like the kind of night a man should check his foundations.

He untied the first bundle. A treatise on oath-law, dry as dust. The second was a martyr's memoir, half of it water-damaged into illegibility. The third bundle was heavier than it should have been, and when he peeled back the oilcloth, the smell rose up out of it like something alive — ink and tallow and old smoke.

A book bound in grey calfskin, its corners capped in tarnished silver. No title on the spine. Just a single stamped device on the front cover: a sword driven point-down into a thornbush.

Jonah knew that mark. Every child in the valley knew it; it was carved over the well, stitched onto festival banners, pressed into the wax seals on the council's letters. The mark of the Swordspeakers' Oath. His mark. Except nobody had used it in earnest for a hundred years, because using it meant there were still swordspeakers to seal things with, and there weren't. There was just him, and a valley full of people who thought the order was a story they told children about why the wheat grew without fear.

He opened the cover.

*The Book of the Watch*, read the flyleaf, in a hand so fine it might have been printed. *Being a record of what is to come, set down by Sister Alenna of the Thorn, who saw it in the fire and wrote it true.*

Prophecy. Jonah snorted softly and almost put it back. The order had produced its share of cranks — every old brotherhood did — and prophecy was the particular vice of people who spent too many nights alone with lamps. He'd read enough of Sister Alenna's ordinary correspondence to know she'd been brilliant, disciplined, half-mad with devotion. Brilliant people made the most convincing lunatics.

But Master Corren's voice was still in his ear. *If it stutters, boy.*

And the ward-line hadn't stuttered, but Jonah had stood at the boundary stones last night doing the ritual, and for one moment — half a heartbeat, no more — the words had tasted strange in his mouth. Like speaking a password to a door that had already been opened from the other side.

Coincidence. He turned the pages anyway.

Sister Alenna's entries ran backward through the book, newest first, which struck him as backwards until he understood: she'd written it the way a person watches a road. What came toward you appeared first. Each entry had a date, and each date was written in the old reckoning, the one the free cities had abandoned after the war, the one only the order still kept because somebody had to remember how to count the years honestly.

Jonah did the conversion out of habit, lips moving, finger tracing the columns. Old reckoning to new. Add the intercalary days. Carry the—

His finger stopped.

The final entry — the first page, the one closest to the reader, the thing coming down the road — bore a date. And Jonah had done this arithmetic a thousand times in his life, for ledgers and anniversaries and the counting of his own age, and he had never once gotten it wrong.

The entry was dated tonight.

---

He laughed. He couldn't help it; it came out of him like a cough, sharp and ugly in the silent room. Of all the nights. Of all the coincidences. Some dead sister a century gone had picked a date out of the air — or picked the founding, maybe, the anniversary, some symbolic nonsense about cycles and returns — and here he was, a grown man with a lamp, getting chills off arithmetic.

He read the entry anyway. Because he was already holding the book, and because the alternative was going back to bed and lying awake wondering.

*Sister Alenna's hand changed here,* he noticed first. The earlier entries were neat, controlled, a scribe's script. This last one slanted hard, the letters crowded, as if she'd been writing fast or shaking or both.

*On this night the ward shall hold, and hold, and hold — and then it shall not. I have watched the thread of it a hundred times in the fire and it does not change. A woman comes through the tear. She comes through fire, walking out of burning, and she carries a small locked thing against her heart. The valley will not be able to keep her out, because the valley keeps out magic, and by the time she crosses the line she will not be carrying magic anymore. She will be carrying something older. Something the wards were never built to see.*

*The last swordspeaker will meet her at the stones with a drawn blade.*

*I am sorry for what we ask of you, whoever reads this. I have prayed to be wrong. I have burned the thread seven times and it comes back the same seven times. A woman through fire. A locked thing at her heart. And the last of us standing in the dark with steel in his hand, about to learn that everything he swore to protect was never the valley at all.*

Below that, in a different hand entirely — older, heavier, pressed deep enough to groove the paper — someone had added a single line:

*We sealed this book so that no one would live their life waiting for a date. If you are reading it, the waiting is over. God forgive us. — Corren.*

Jonah set the book down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be sleeping.

Corren. His master's handwriting, from before Jonah had ever met him, from before the old man's hands had gone to ruin. Which meant Corren had known. Had read this entry decades ago, had sealed it away, had carried the knowledge of it through his whole life and into his teaching — and had never once said a word beyond *if it stutters, boy, go to the sealed row.*

Because what else could he say? *One day a woman will fall out of a fire and your whole life will pivot around her?* You'd raise the boy mad. You'd raise him watching every stranger, flinching at every campfire, waiting for a date the way Alenna said not to wait.

No. Corren had done the kinder thing. He'd let Jonah grow up ordinary. Dutiful. Alone, but safe inside the routine, inside the oaths, inside the valley that could not be breached.

Jonah looked at the date again. Tonight.

"It's a coincidence," he told the empty archive. His voice sounded steadier than he felt. "She picked the founding. Symbolic nonsense. Cycles and returns."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. No draft reached this far underground, and yet for a moment the light bent, just slightly, the way light bends above hot ground in high summer.

Jonah closed the book.

* * *

He told himself he was going back to bed. He got as far as the stairs.

Then he turned around, climbed back down, and took the book with him — because if he left it locked in the dark it would sit there all night growing larger in his imagination, and if he was going to be a fool he preferred to be a fool with evidence in hand. He climbed the hill to the boundary stones under a sky salted with stars, the calfskin book tucked inside his coat like contraband.

The ward-stones stood in their ancient circle at the valley's throat, waist-high, worn smooth by a century of weather and ritual. Nine of them, spaced evenly, each carved with the sword-and-thorn. Between them ran the ward-line itself — invisible to anyone without an oath, but to Jonah it showed as a faint silver tension in the air, a harp-string strung across the whole width of the valley mouth, humming a note below hearing. It had hummed that note every night of his life. His master before him. His master's master before that.

Jonah walked the line, as he did every eve of Founding Day, and laid his palm against the first stone.

"Hold," he said.

The word was the smallest of his oaths, the housekeeping-oath, the one that asked nothing and cost nothing. The stone answered with its familiar warmth. The line hummed. All correct. All as it had been for a hundred years.

He moved to the second stone. "Hold."

Warmth. Hum. Correct.

At the third stone he stopped.

The warmth was there. But beneath it, faint as a wrong note in a familiar song, was something else — a tremor, a thinness, the feeling of ice on a pond that looks solid and isn't. Jonah pressed his palm flat and listened with the part of himself that wasn't flesh, the part the oaths lived in, and what he heard made the hair rise along his arms.

The line was afraid.

Stones don't get afraid. Wards don't get afraid. They're work, not creatures — mortar and word and will, nothing more. But there was no other word for it. The ward-line at the third stone felt the way a dog feels before an earthquake: pressed flat, ears back, watching a direction where there was nothing to see.

Jonah took out the book. Opened it to the final entry by starlight. Read it again, though he'd memorized it in one pass, the way he memorized everything — a curse of the oaths, total recall, every spoken truth lodged in him forever.

*A woman comes through the tear.*

"There is no tear," he said aloud. "There has never been a tear."

The ward-line shivered.

It was not much. A ripple, the way still water ripples when a fish turns somewhere far below. Any other man would have missed it. Jonah had been listening to this line since he was nine years old, and he did not miss it, and every rational explanation he owned — wind, fatigue, imagination, the fever of an old book working on a lonely mind — presented itself in turn and was rejected in turn, because he knew the difference between the line and everything that was not the line the way he knew the difference between his own pulse and the ground.

He drew his sword.

It was a habit older than thought, and he was slightly ashamed of it even as the steel cleared the scabbard — a lone man in the dark, pointing a blade at empty air because a dead woman's handwriting had frightened him. The moonlight ran down the flat of it. Nothing moved on the road. Nothing moved in the wheat. The valley lay quiet around him, ten thousand souls asleep, trusting him the way you trust a wall you've never tested because no one has ever thrown anything at it.

*The last swordspeaker will meet her at the stones with a drawn blade.*

"I'm not meeting anyone," Jonah said. "You're a book."

The ward-line tore.

There was no better word for it, and he would spend years afterward searching for one and never finding it. The silver tension between the third and fourth stones simply parted — not faded, not flickered, *parted*, the way cloth parts under a knife — and through the gap came a sound he had no framework for: a roar with heat in it, a rush of orange light where no fire had any right to be, and the smell of scorched air pouring through the breach like water through a split dam.

Fire. On the road. In the middle of the night, in a valley where fire obeyed, where everything obeyed, where magic had not crossed the boundary in a hundred years because the boundary was the one promise the world had ever kept to his order.

Out of the burning came a shape. Small. Running. Stumbling. A woman, silhouetted against her own catastrophe, hair whipping, one hand pressed flat against her chest as if holding something shut — or holding something *in*. Behind her the fire roared higher, impossibly, feeding on nothing, and Jonah understood with a lurch that the fire wasn't following her.

It was *delivering* her. Spitting her out across the line like something swallowed wrong.

She crossed the ward-line and fell.

The tear snapped shut above her, seamless, as if it had never been. The ward-note resumed its hum, steady, unbothered, a door closing politely behind a guest. The fire on the road guttered and died all at once, leaving darkness and drifting sparks and the woman face-down in the dirt three paces inside the boundary, breathing hard, one fist still clenched over her heart.

Jonah stood where he was. Sword up. Heart slamming. Every oath he owned crowding his tongue and none of them fitting.

The book had said a woman through fire. The book had said a locked thing at her heart. The book had said tonight, and the book had not missed by a minute.

He should speak. He knew the challenge by heart — he'd rehearsed it as a boy, playing at being the order, *who crosses the Thorn unbidden* — but his voice wouldn't come, because the rehearsal had never included this: the smell of burnt air, the dying sparks, the impossible arithmetic of a prophecy keeping its appointment to the hour.

The woman lifted her head. Even in the dark he could see the burns on her hands, the soot, the eyes that found him and his drawn sword and went wide — not with surrender, not with pleading.

With defiance. As if she'd been running all night precisely so she could arrive somewhere and refuse to beg.

Her lips moved. What came out was barely a breath, cracked and furious and aimed straight at him:

"Don't."

Jonah Blackwood, last of the Swordspeakers' Oath, keeper of the valley where magic cannot follow, stood in the dark with steel in his hand and a dead sister's words ringing in his skull, and understood that everything — the ledger, the festival, the hundred quiet years — had just ended.

The woman at his feet began, very quietly, to laugh.
