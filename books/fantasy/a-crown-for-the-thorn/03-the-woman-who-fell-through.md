## 3. The Woman Who Fell Through

The fire came first.

That was how Wren would remember it, in the years after — not the running, not the screaming, not the sound of Master Ellory's voice cut off mid-sentence, but the fire. It had come through the library window like something alive, like something that had been waiting for permission, and it had taken the shelves first. All those books. All those years of a careful man's careful life, going up in a breath.

Then the river. Then nothing.

She woke to cold.

Not the clean cold of a winter morning — the cold of a woman who has been lying in shallow water for hours, her clothes soaked through, her skin pruned and burning at the same time. She was on her side, cheek pressed to mud, and the water was moving past her in a thin, indifferent current, carrying leaves and a dead moth and, once, a long dark ribbon of ash that might have come from anywhere.

Might have. But didn't.

She opened her eyes. Grey sky, low and featureless, like a lid on a pot. Reeds around her, tall and brown, bending in a wind she couldn't feel. Somewhere a bird was making a sound like a hinge that needed oil.

Wren lay still and took inventory, because Master Ellory had taught her that too: *When you wake and don't know where you are, lie still. Count what you have. Count what you've lost. Then decide which list frightens you more.*

What she had: her body, mostly. A left arm that worked. A right arm that screamed when she tested it — burned from elbow to shoulder, the skin tight and shiny where it wasn't blistered. Boots, still laced. Trousers, ruined. The coat was gone; the river had taken the coat, and everything in its pockets, and she felt the loss of it like a second burn.

And at her throat, on its chain, the locket. Cold against her sternum. Still locked. Still hers.

*Count what you've lost.* Ellory. The library. The city of Vel Sarran, and every road out of it, and every name she'd trusted in it. Her whole life, in other words — the small, hidden, careful life of a woman who had spent twenty-six years pretending to be nobody, because being nobody was the only way to stay alive.

She'd been somebody's student for eleven years. She was nobody's anything now.

Wren pushed herself up out of the mud, and the world tilted, and she was sick into the reeds, and when she was done she knelt there shaking, and the bird went on making its hinge-noise, and the river went on going somewhere without her.

*Get up.* That was Ellory's voice, or her memory of it. *You can grieve when you're dead. Until then, you walk.*

She got up.

* * *

The river had carried her a long way. She knew that because the banks were wrong — not the low marsh country she'd fled through, but rising ground, and the water running fast and clear between stones instead of sluggish over silt. And there was something else, something she couldn't name at first, a wrongness in the air the way there's a wrongness in a room where a clock has stopped. A silence with a shape to it.

She walked upstream. It was as good a direction as any, and better than most, because upstream meant higher ground and higher ground meant you could see people coming. Ellory's third rule: *Never let them come at you from below.*

The burn on her arm had passed from screaming to a deep, grinding ache. She'd bound it with a strip of shirt in the first mile, clumsily, one-handed, and now the cloth was stuck to the blistered skin and every step tugged at it. She didn't stop to fix it. Stopping was for people who weren't being hunted.

Because she was. She had no doubt of that. Serah Vane did not lose things. Wren had watched her work for a single afternoon, three years ago, from a balcony in Vel Sarran — watched the inquisitor question a customs clerk about a missing shipment, watched the man's answers get shorter and shorter, watched him weep without ever being touched — and Wren had understood, in the way you understand a blade is sharp without needing to test it, that this woman did not stop. The clerk had hanged himself two days later. Vane had attended the funeral. Wren had never learned why.

So: hunted. By inquisitors with fire that came through windows, in a country where fire wasn't supposed to come through windows, in a country where —

Where the air had that stopped-clock feeling.

She stopped walking. Stood in the shallows, water pulling at her boots, and did the thing she was best trained to do, the thing that had gotten her mentor killed: she thought about it.

Magic did not work here. That was the first fact of her whole life, older than her own name, older than the locket. The free cities had been built on that fact the way houses are built on foundations. The Thorn countries, the warded valleys, the dead zones — the map she'd grown up studying had them inked in grey, *null zones*, places where the old power simply didn't reach, which was why the free cities had grown up huddled against them like sheep against a shepherd's back. You didn't flee *to* a null zone. You fled *along* one, the way you'd walk along a cliff edge for the view but never lean out.

And yet Serah Vane's soldiers had thrown fire through a library window in the heart of Vel Sarran, and the fire had *behaved* — had hunted, had curled around doorways, had found the stairs. And now Wren was standing in a place where the air felt scoured, scrubbed, silent as a swept floor, and the silence itself felt less like absence than like a held breath.

She looked up at the rising ground, at the grey sky, at the reeds, and she said, aloud, because Ellory's fifth rule was *say the frightening thing and it gets smaller*:

"This is a valley."

Her voice came out a rasp. She said it again anyway.

"And valleys have walls. And walls have makers. And makers have — "

She didn't finish, because that was when she saw the smoke.

Thin, domestic, ordinary smoke, rising from beyond the ridge to the north. Woodsmoke. Someone was baking bread, or boiling laundry, or doing any of the ten thousand small things people did with fire that didn't involve burning down libraries.

People. Which meant food, bandages, a horse if she was lucky, a road if she was luckier. And also meant questions, and eyes, and the risk that somewhere in that smoke was a man who wrote letters to the capital, or a woman whose brother was a customs clerk.

Ellory's seventh rule: *You can't do this alone. That's not a moral statement. It's arithmetic.*

She climbed the ridge.

* * *

The valley opened below her like a held secret.

Wren had seen the free cities — had lived in one, had learned its alleys the way you learn a face — and she had seen the imperial provinces on her one terrible journey south, the grain and the gallows and the gray-faced farmers who wouldn't meet your eye. This was neither. This was a bowl of green land cradled between two forested ridges, with a river stitching down its middle and fields laid out in long strips, and a town at the center with slate roofs and a stone bridge and a bell tower, and everywhere the small, unremarkable industry of people who expected to still be here tomorrow.

Farms on the near slopes. A mill downstream, its wheel turning. Children — actual children — driving geese along a lane without a single armed adult in sight.

Nobody did that. Nobody in the whole span of the map did that.

The wrongness in the air was stronger here, close to overwhelming, a silence so total it had texture. Wren stood on the ridge with the wind pulling at her soaked clothes and felt it press against her like deep water, and understood two things with the cold certainty of a woman reading her own death warrant.

The first: this was the Thorn Valley. Not *a* thorn valley — *the* Thorn Valley, the grey smudge at the top of every map she'd ever studied, the place so old and so warded that the free cities treated it as a fact of geography rather than a place where people lived. The last null zone that mattered. The place where magic could not follow.

The second thing she understood was worse.

She had come *through* something to get here. The river, the fire, the fall — she had no memory of the middle of it, only the beginning and the cold. And a null zone was not a place you arrived at by river. The Thorn Valley's whole existence was a locked door. Rivers didn't open it.

Unless the door was already open. Unless something had torn it.

Wren's hand went to the locket at her throat, and held it, and she stood on the ridge for a long moment, a burned and dripping woman with no coat and no plan, looking down at a town full of people who had no idea what was coming, and she thought about the prophecy — the one Ellory had made her memorize and then made her burn — and the line she had hated most, the line she had argued with him about over cheap wine in a hundred bad rooms:

*She will come through fire and water to the valley where magic cannot follow, and the door will not close behind her.*

"The door will not close behind her," Wren said to the grey sky. Her voice shook. "You absolute bastard, Ellory. You couldn't have written down the part about what I'm supposed to *do*?"

The sky didn't answer. Down in the valley, someone's dog started barking, and the sound carried up the slope with terrible clarity, and Wren Ashvale — nobody, archivist, orphan, last student of a dead man — started walking down toward the smoke.

* * *

She made it within a half-mile of the town before the swordspeaker found her.

She never saw him arrive. One moment the lane was empty ahead of her, hedgerows and geese and a distant cart; the next there was a man standing in it, as if the lane had grown him. Tall, dark-haired, somewhere past thirty, wearing a long coat the color of wet slate and carrying a sword that was, absurdly, still sheathed. He didn't draw it. He didn't need to. Everything about the way he stood — weight low, hands loose, eyes doing a fast, professional circuit of her body the way a butcher sizes a carcass — said that the sheath was a formality.

"Stop there," he said.

Wren stopped. Partly because he was between her and the town, and partly because her legs had been negotiating a surrender for the last mile and this gave them an excuse.

"You crossed the ward-line," the man said. His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man reading from a script he hated. "Nothing crosses the ward-line. The ward-line has held for a hundred years. So either you're a very clever ghost, or something very old just broke." He took one step closer. "Which is it?"

"Option three," Wren said. Her voice was wrecked — smoke and river and a night of screaming she hadn't done yet — but she made it work. "I'm the woman who fell through."

Something moved behind his eyes. She watched it happen: a flicker, fast as a fish under ice, there and gone, but she'd spent her life reading documents and she could read faces the same way, and what she'd seen wasn't confusion.

It was recognition.

"You're burned," he said. "And half-drowned. And you walked out of a null zone that nothing walks out of, and the first thing you do is quote prophecy at me." He stopped, ten feet away, and now his hand did come to rest — lightly, almost casually — on the hilt of the sheathed sword. "Who sent you?"

"Nobody sent me. Nobody *could* send me. That was rather the point." She lifted her chin. The motion pulled at the burn and she refused to flinch where he could see it. "My name is Wren Ashvale. I'm from Vel Sarran. Three nights ago the inquisitors burned down the library where I work and killed the man who raised me, and I ran, and I fell, and I don't remember the middle, and I woke up in your river. That's everything I know. You can kill me now, or you can get me something to eat. I'd prefer the second, but I've made peace with both."

The man stared at her.

Up close, she could see the cost of his composure — the sleeplessness ground into his face, the way his jaw worked like he was chewing on words and spitting most of them out. A man used to silence. A man who had practiced it.

"You're telling the truth," he said at last, and he sounded angry about it, as if her honesty were a personal inconvenience. "Every word. I'd know if you weren't."

"Would you?"

"It's what I do." He said it the way other men say *I'm a carpenter*. "Swordspeech. Truth spoken into steel. It's — " He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and Wren watched him swallow whatever came next like a man swallowing glass. "It doesn't matter. It's not supposed to matter. Nothing's supposed to *happen* here. That's the entire point of this valley. A hundred years of nothing happening, and then you fall out of the sky with a burned arm and a — "

He stopped again. But this time he'd stopped a half-second too late, and Wren, who had spent eleven years learning to hear the sentence behind the sentence, heard it anyway.

"A what?" she said.

"Nothing."

"A *what*?" She stepped forward, and he tensed, and she didn't care. Exhaustion had burned through fear and come out the other side into something harder. "Say it. You know who I am. You knew before I said my name — I watched your face. There's a book somewhere in this valley with tonight written in it, and my name in it, or close enough, and you've read it, and you're standing there deciding whether I'm a person or a *page*."

The silence stretched. Somewhere behind them the dog was still barking, and the geese had gathered at a respectful distance to watch, and the smoke rose from the town's chimneys into the grey lid of sky, and Wren stood in the lane with her burned arm bleeding through its filthy bandage and her whole life reduced to what she could carry at her throat.

The man looked at her for a long moment. And then, slowly, he did the last thing she expected.

He took his hand off the sword.

"Jonah Blackwood," he said. "Last oath-bound swordspeaker of the Thorn Valley. Which makes me the only man in a hundred miles with any business asking you this, so I'll ask it once." His eyes were steady and very tired and very afraid, and none of that showed in his voice. "That locket you're holding like it's the only thing you own. What's in it?"

Wren's hand closed over the locket, and eleven years of Ellory's rules came down over her like a shutter, and she heard herself say — smoothly, instantly, with the ease of long practice:

"Nothing that concerns you."

And she watched Jonah Blackwood's face as she lied to him, and watched him *know* it — saw the truth move through him like weather, saw his eyes narrow and his hand start to rise back toward the hilt — and understood, with a lurch, that she had just walked into the one valley in the world where lying was a thing a man could *taste*.

"That," Jonah said quietly, "is going to be a problem."
