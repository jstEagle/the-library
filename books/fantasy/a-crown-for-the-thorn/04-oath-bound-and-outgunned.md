## 4. Oath-Bound and Outgunned

The millstone room of the old order-house had no windows, which was the point. Jonah had spent half his life in rooms like this — stone, silence, and a table scarred by the knives of men who had once believed questions could be answered honestly.

He set two cups on the table anyway. One for her. One for show.

Wren Ashvale sat across from him with her wrists unbound, which had been Ilsa's doing and not his. The healer's apprentice had bandaged the burns on Wren's arms with the brisk tenderness of someone who patched what was put in front of her and asked nothing else. Doss stood by the door with his arms crossed, occupying it the way a boulder occupies a road. Tam hovered near the wall with a stub of charcoal and a scrap of paper he kept pretending not to write on.

Jonah sat down slowly, the way you sit when your knees have opinions about last night.

"Your name," he said.

"You know my name."

"I want to hear you say it."

Her jaw tightened. "Wren Ashvale."

"Where were you born?"

"Does it matter?"

"Everything matters. That's the whole trouble with everything." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the wood. "Where were you born?"

"Ashvale," she said, and something flickered across her face — surprise at herself, maybe, or at how easily the name came out when she stopped fighting it. "A town that doesn't exist anymore."

"Burned?"

"Worse. Renamed." She met his eyes. "You're not going to torture me, so why the questions?"

"Torture gets you lies that flatter the torturer. Questions get you truth if you're patient." He leaned back. "Who taught you to cross a ward-line through fire?"

"No one taught me anything about ward-lines. I didn't even know this valley existed until three days ago. A man in Corvess told me to walk north until the trees changed."

"What man?"

"A bookseller." Her mouth went dry-looking; she reached for the cup and found it empty. "He's dead now. They burned his shop with him in it because he sold me a map."

Jonah felt the words land somewhere under his ribs. *They burned his shop.* Inquisitors did not burn bookshops in Corvess. Inquisitors did not operate in the free cities at all — that was the entire architecture of the peace, the whole load-bearing wall of the last hundred years: magic stayed out of the cities, the crown's hunters stayed out of the free towns, and the Thorn Valley stayed forgotten by everyone, warded and silent and safe.

Three assumptions. He could feel one of them already cracking.

"The locket," he said. "What's in it?"

And there it was — the wall. Not hers. His.

Because he knew what was in it. He had read it in old ink not twelve hours ago, in a sealed archive no living person but him had entered: *a woman comes through fire, carrying the blood-claim in a case of iron and glass.* The prophecy hadn't said *locket*. It hadn't needed to. And the oaths of the Swordspeakers' Order — the nine oaths he had spoken over a dead man's sword when he was nineteen years old and certain he understood grief — the third oath forbade him from speaking a truth learned from the order's records to any soul outside the order.

The order was him. He was the order. Which meant, by the letter of the law he'd inherited, he couldn't tell himself either.

"The locket," Wren repeated, "is mine."

"It's the reason four men tried to kill you before you ever reached the tree line. It's the reason you crossed a hundred-year ward in a column of fire instead of walking around like a sensible person. So yes. It's mine too, now."

"You don't get to claim things just because they fell into your valley."

"This valley," Jonah said, "is the only place in the world where what's chasing you can't follow. That gives me a certain amount of claim."

"Does it?" Her voice dropped, and there was something underneath the defiance now — exhaustion, and fear worn down smooth like river stone. "Then explain it to me. Explain how they were behind me at the ford, close enough that I could hear their horses, and then I stepped into the fire and came out here — and they *stopped*. Why would they stop? If they wanted the locket so badly, why not follow me through?"

Jonah looked at her for a long moment.

"They can't," he said. "Magic cannot follow into the Thorn Valley. That is the founding fact of this place. The first swordspeakers bled it into the soil themselves. Nothing touched by the art crosses the line. Not a spell, not a scrying glass, not a hunter wearing a crown's writ. For a hundred years, that has been true."

"And now?"

"Now you're sitting in it," he said. "Burned, drowned, alive. Which means either the valley has failed—" and his voice caught on the size of that, the sheer architectural catastrophe of it, "—or you are carrying something so old and so heavy that the ward couldn't tell where it ended and you began."

Wren's hand went to her collarbone, to the small iron shape beneath her shirt, the way a person touches a wound they've stopped noticing. She didn't answer. She didn't have to. Jonah watched her decide, again, to carry it alone, and he felt the third oath sit in his chest like a swallowed stone, because he could have told her — he could have said *I know, the book named you, the ink was dry before your grandmother was born* — and instead he said:

"Get some sleep. There's a cot in the east room. Doss will walk you to it, mostly because he wants to stand between you and the door, and it makes him happy."

"I noticed he's been holding that axe since I woke up."

"He holds it during festivals too. It's how he prays."

She almost smiled. It died halfway up her face, and she stood, and paused at the door.

"The man who wrote your prophecy book," she said quietly. "Did it say whether I live?"

Jonah held her gaze and gave her the only honest thing his oaths allowed.

"I haven't finished reading it."

* * *

He hadn't lied. That was the trick of oath-bound speech — it didn't stop you from deceiving people, it just made every deception a small amputation. He had not finished reading it. He had also read the last page three times, and the last page did not mention her living at all.

He climbed to the archive an hour past midnight, taking the stairs two at a time out of a restlessness he refused to name, and lit the lamp beside the prophecy book where he'd left it open, weighted with a whetstone like a bird that might fly off.

The final entry again. Same date. Same woman through fire. He read it standing, lips moving without sound, and got exactly as far as he had every other time before the third oath closed around his tongue like a hand.

*Fine,* he thought. *Don't speak it. Think it.*

The oaths, Master Corren had told him once, bind speech, not mind. *A swordspeaker's weapon is the word, boy. Guard the word. Your thoughts are your own poor tenancy.* But Jonah had discovered, in twenty years of practice, that a truth you may think but never say grows teeth. It chews. Tonight it chewed on the shape of Wren Ashvale's face when she asked *did it say whether I live*, and he poured himself a finger of the order's terrible plum brandy and let it.

Below him, through the floor, he heard the house settle. Ilsa moving through the sickroom with her candle. Tam scratching away at something — the boy wrote the way other people breathed, compulsively, constantly, and Jonah had decided not to ask yet. Doss snoring like a landslide in slow motion.

Safe. The house was safe. The valley was safe. That was the covenant; that was the entire deal; a hundred years of dead swordspeakers had paid for it in advance.

He was still telling himself that when the bell began to ring.

Not the festival bell. The *ward-bell* — the bronze thing bolted above the order-house door that had rung twice in Jonah's lifetime, both times for deer blundering against the outer stones. This ringing did not stop. It did not stutter. It rang the way a heart rings when the body knows before the mind does.

Jonah took the stairs down four at a time.

Doss was already in the yard, axe in hand, half-dressed, looking up. Ilsa stood in the doorway with her candle guttering. Above the eastern ridge, where the ward-stones ran along the old farms — Hollowbrook, the Marshes, the Tanners' row — the night sky had gone the color of a bruise.

Not firelight. Firelight flickers. This light *pulsed*, slow and rhythmic, like breathing.

"That's the Marshes," Doss said. "That's the Hedgeman farm."

"The Hedgemans moved to the village for the festival," Jonah said. "Everyone's in the village for the festival. Nobody's out there."

"Something's out there."

They went. Of course they went. Jonah took the sword down from above the door — his master's sword, plain steel, the only relic the order had left worth stealing and worthless to anyone who couldn't speak to it — and ran the ridge path with Doss pounding behind him and Ilsa refusing, absolutely refusing, to stay behind with her satchel banging at her hip.

The Hedgeman farm sat in a fold of the eastern hills, and from the ridge they saw it whole: the barn burning with a flame that burned *wrong*, green-hearted and cold, casting shadows that moved against the wind. Figures stood in the dooryard in a ring, robed grey, hooded, and between them — floating, turning slowly, a foot off the ground — a man. Old Marten Hedgeman, who should have been at the festival, who must have come home early, who hung in the air with his arms pinned to his sides as though held by invisible hands, and he was screaming, and the sound carried up the hill thin and clear.

"Magic," Ilsa whispered. "That's — that's working magic. Here. Inside the line."

Jonah's mind performed the arithmetic with horrible speed. The ward held against magic. Magic was working here. Therefore either the ward had failed — and if the ward had failed, the whole valley was naked, the village, the festival, the thousand people singing in the square — or these men had found a way to bring their art across the line intact, which was worse, because wards could be rebuilt and doctrine could not.

Or — the coldest thought, arriving last and staying longest — or the ward had never failed at all, and something in what they hunted had *carried them through*, the way it had carried Wren. A door that opened wherever she went. A wake in the world.

"Stay here," Jonah said.

"Like hell," said Doss.

They went down.

The inquisitors heard them coming — of course they did; the gravel announced them like trumpets — and the nearest turned, and Jonah saw the grey mask-plate, the crown's thorn sigil stamped in black, and twenty years of discipline rose in him like floodwater. *Do not draw on living men inside the valley. Do not use the speech on living men inside the valley.* First oath, second oath, carved into him deeper than the scars.

"Inquisitors!" he called, voice pitched to carry the way Master Corren had taught him, the way that made words arrive in a listener's chest rather than their ears. "By the old compact, the crown's writ holds no force within the Thorn Valley. You are in violation. Withdraw beyond the stones."

The masked figure tilted its head, considering him the way a scholar considers a footnote.

"The compact," it said, and the voice was a woman's, level and almost gentle, "was written by men who believed this dirt meant something." It raised one gloved hand, and the floating farmer rose higher, higher, his screams climbing with him. "The dirt means what we say it means now."

Jonah drew.

Steel cleared leather with its old familiar song, and the sword woke against his palm the way it always did — a hum, a readiness, the sense of a vast attention turning toward him, waiting for words. Every blade he'd ever bound had felt like this: a door ajar. All he had to do was speak. Bind the steel to truth and the truth would act — he could disarm six inquisitors in six heartbeats, he could shatter their staves, he could drop the farmer gently as a leaf —

On living men. Inside the valley. The oaths stood in him like a wall built by better men, and he slammed against it at full run and stopped, shaking, sword bare and useless in his fist, because the oaths were all he had left of the order and breaking them felt like digging up his master's grave with his own hands.

The inquisitor watched him tremble and understood, somehow, exactly what she was seeing.

"Swordspeaker," she said softly. "So the stories are true. How wonderful. We were told you were extinct." She turned her hand, and the farmer dropped — not gently — crumpling into the mud of his own dooryard, alive, weeping, broken. "Keep your oaths, keeper. Keep them tight. We'll be curious, later, to see which one breaks first."

Green fire bloomed in the dooryard, and when it guttered out they were gone — the six of them, the burn marks, everything but the farmer and the wrongness hanging in the air like smoke after lightning.

Ilsa was already kneeling in the mud, hands moving over old Marten, murmuring. Doss stood with his axe lowered, staring at the space where the men had been, wearing the expression of a man whose god has just walked past him without stopping.

Jonah stood in the middle of the ruined farm with his sword still drawn and his arm still shaking, and felt the crack go through him — not loud, not dramatic, just a fine hairline fracture running through the foundation of everything he had believed since he was nineteen years old.

The valley did not make him safe. It had never made anyone safe. It had only made him *late*.

Behind him, down the dark hill toward the village, the festival bells were still ringing — the joyful ones, the founding-day ones, a thousand people dancing under lanterns who did not know yet that the world had come through their walls tonight and left bootprints.

Jonah sheathed the sword, picked old Marten up out of the mud with his own arms, and started carrying him home.

Halfway down the hill, Doss caught up, breath ragged.

"The girl," Doss said. "Whatever she's carrying. Whatever came through with her." He glanced back at the blackened farm. "It's not hiding her from them, is it. It's leading them."

Jonah didn't answer, because the honest answer was yes, and because at the bottom of the hill, standing in the lane with a blanket around her shoulders and her face turned toward the burned farm, Wren Ashvale had heard every word.
