## 5. What the Locket Keeps

The mill smelled of old grain and older dust, and Wren Ashvale had never loved a smell so much in her life.

She'd hidden in worse places. A wine cellar in Kettering with rats the size of cats. A coffin, once, for six hours while Vane's riders passed close enough that she could hear them complaining about their boots. But those had been places she chose out of desperation. This was different. This was a man handing her his hiding place and then standing guard at the door of it, arms crossed, sword loose at his hip like it grew there.

"You'll stay behind the flour bins," Jonah Blackwood said. "If anyone comes through the front, there's a ladder to the loft. If they come through the back—" He stopped.

"If they come through the back?"

"Then I've already failed you," he said, "and it won't matter where you are."

He said it without drama, the way he seemed to say everything, and went out into the gray afternoon light, pulling the door shut behind him until only a knife-slit remained. Through it Wren watched him take up a position against the mill wall where he could see both the road and the river path, and she thought: *he doesn't trust me either.*

Good. Trust was how people died. Trust was how Master Ellery died, standing in the doorway of his own study saying *run, child* to her while three men in gray walked up his stairs like they owned them.

She settled between the flour bins with her back to the cool wood and let herself breathe for what felt like the first time since the ward-line tore open around her two nights ago. Her hands still shook. She made fists until they mostly stopped.

The locket lay against her sternum under her shirt, small and hard as a knuckle of bone. She didn't touch it. Touching it was a habit she was trying to break, because touching it meant thinking about it, and thinking about it meant—

No. Not yet. First: count your exits. The ladder to the loft. The sluice gate at the base of the wheel, if it opened. The window over the grindstone, too narrow, probably. She got up twice to check all three anyway, and on the second pass she caught her reflection in the dark glass — a burned, half-drowned woman with river mud still in the seams of her collar — and made herself look.

"This is who they're hunting," she told the reflection quietly. "Know her face."

The reflection looked terrified. Wren decided not to hold that against her.

* * *

Jonah came back at dusk carrying bread, hard cheese, and a clay jug of water, and set them down within her reach without coming closer than an arm's length, which she noticed, and which she suspected he meant her to notice.

"There's a blanket in the loft," he said. "It smells like mice. It's warm."

"Thank you." She tore the bread and found her hands were steadier now. "You could have left me at the ward-line."

"Yes."

"But you didn't."

"No."

Wren almost smiled. Talking to him was like playing cards with someone who refused to look at his own hand. "Does the valley have a name for what I am? What they call me, when they talk about the stranger who fell through the fire?"

"They don't talk about you. I asked them not to."

"You *asked* them."

"People here still do what I ask," Jonah said. There was no pride in it. That was the strange part. Most men with that kind of authority wore it like a crown whether they wanted one or not. He wore his like a chore. "Mostly."

"And when they stop? When the inquisitors come through those farms like they did yesterday and magic works here now — what happens to the things people used to do because you asked?"

He was quiet for a long moment. The mill wheel turned outside, slow and groaning, counting time the way mills do.

"I don't know," he said finally, and Wren blinked, because it was the first completely honest sentence anyone had said to her in weeks. Everyone else lied beautifully. Vane lied like silk sliding off a table. The ferryman, Doss, lied by grunting. Even Tam, the scribe boy, lied constantly in small harmless ways about how much he'd eaten and whose fault it was.

But this man answered questions like every word cost him something, and therefore every word was true.

"The prophecy book," she said. "You read my arrival in it. You know more than you're saying."

"Yes."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

And there it was again. He wouldn't lie to her even to protect himself. She should have hated him for it. Instead something in her chest, something clenched tight since Ellery's study, loosened by a single degree.

"My teacher used to say," she heard herself begin, and then stopped.

Jonah waited. He was good at waiting. It was possibly the only thing he was good at besides killing, though she hadn't confirmed the second one yet.

"He used to say that truth is a stone you carry," Wren went on, before she could stop herself. "That everyone's carrying stones, most of them pebbles, little lies and little shames, and you can walk fine with pebbles. But some people get handed boulders. And the world keeps asking them to run." She looked down at her hands. "I'm tired of running with mine."

The wheel turned. Somewhere above them a pigeon shifted in the rafters.

"What is it you carry?" Jonah asked, and his voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of a man approaching something that might go off.

Wren's hand rose toward her collarbone before she could stop it. Fingers against the locket's shape beneath the cloth. Warm already, or she imagined it warm. It always felt faintly warm, ever since Ellery pressed it into her palm with his blood still on his lips.

*If they take it from you, Wren, they don't just kill you. They kill every name inside.*

She opened her mouth.

Here is what she would have said, if she had said it: *There was a family called Valemont, once, and they were real. Not legend — real, with ledgers and lands and a bloodline the usurper's grandfather bought, stole, and burned its way into. My mother was their last archivist. The locket holds the proof of descent, the seals, the names — proof that Aldric Vane sits a stolen throne, and that somewhere in the world, thin as a candle flame, the true line survives.* Here is what else she would have said: *and the reason the inquisitors can work magic inside your valley now is that the proof matters, the blood matters, the old houses are waking up, and everything is starting to move again because of what I carry.*

She got as far as a breath.

Then she saw the study again. Ellery's spectacles on the floor, one lens starred white. The smell — ink and blood and something scorched. Serah Vane's voice, gentle as a lullaby: *"Tell us where it is, and we will let the old man live."* And Ellery looking at Wren over their shoulders, calm as Sunday, shaking his head once, slow, so she would understand: *no matter what happens next, do not give it to them.*

They killed him anyway. Of course they did. Kindness from inquisitors was a coin they minted themselves and spent freely, because it cost nothing and bought everything.

And this man in front of her — kind, honest, oath-bound — was exactly the kind of man the Gray Order knew how to use. Every secret he kept for her was a secret they could torture out of him. Every kindness he showed her was a lever. She had watched them turn love into a crowbar often enough to know the shape of it.

"It's nothing," Wren said. "A trinket of my mother's. That's all they want. Sentiment."

The lie came out smooth and practiced and she hated every syllable of it, especially because Jonah just looked at her — that long, level, unhurried look — and nodded once.

"All right," he said.

"That's it? 'All right'?"

"You don't owe me your secrets." He gathered the empty jug, moved toward the door. "You owe them to yourself, to keep or spend as you choose. Anyone who tells you different wants something."

He paused at the door slit, gray light cutting across half his face.

"For what it's worth — whatever it is, however heavy — you haven't been carrying it alone since the night you fell through. You've been carrying it in front of witnesses. Me. Doss. The girl Ilsa, who stitched your burns and asked no questions, which for Ilsa is practically sainthood." A beat. "Witnesses are worth more than confessions. Remember that, if you remember nothing else from me."

Then he was gone, and Wren sat alone in the darkening mill with her heart going like a trapped bird, furious at him for making her want to tell the truth, and more furious at herself for wanting it.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She climbed to the loft, took the mousy blanket, wrapped it around her shoulders, and sat in the hay watching moonlight crawl across the floorboards, turning the whole thing over like a card sharp with one good trick.

Give it to him. Tell him everything. Let the last swordspeaker in Thorn Valley decide what to do with the proof of a stolen throne.

Or keep it. Keep it the way Ellery taught her: alone, silent, alive. A secret shared is a secret halved, and halves get found.

The trouble — the unbearable, grinding trouble — was that both choices felt like betrayal. Betray Ellery's grave, or betray the first honest man she'd met since the pyres started. There was no clean option. There were only stones, and running.

Near midnight she heard footsteps below — Jonah, changing watch with himself, because apparently the man slept maybe two hours a night and considered that generous. She heard him stop at the base of the loft ladder. Heard him stand there a long moment, not climbing, not calling up to her. Just present. Like a wall you could lean against if walls could be trusted.

"You should sleep," he said quietly, to the dark, knowing she was awake. "Tomorrow I ride to the outer farms. Something's wrong with the ward-stones. They're singing."

"Singing?" Her voice came out rough.

"In my grandfather's day, the stones sang when the ward held strong. A hundred years of silence. Last night they started again." He was quiet a moment. "Whatever woke them came in with you. Or came for you. I intend to find out which."

His boots crossed the floor. The door opened and closed.

Wren sat in the hay with her fist pressed flat against the locket, feeling its small stubborn heat through wool and linen, and for the first time since Ellery died she spoke to it aloud, barely a breath, the way you speak at a graveside.

"Not yet," she whispered. "But soon. I think — I think soon I'm going to need help carrying you."

Below the loft, out along the river road, faint as a memory, the ward-stones began to sing.
