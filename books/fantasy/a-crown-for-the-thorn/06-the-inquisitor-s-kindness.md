## 6. The Inquisitor's Kindness

The mill smelled of old grain and older dust, and Wren had learned in three days of hiding that dust could keep secrets better than stone. She sat in the loft above the grinding floor, knees drawn up, the locket warm against her sternum where it hung on its chain beneath her shirt. Warm, though the metal should have gone cold hours ago in this drafty dark. Sometimes she thought it beat.

Below her, Jonah moved through the mill's bones like a man who knew how to be quiet in his own grief. He checked the doors without checking them — a glance, a hand resting briefly on a latch — and then climbed the ladder with a bowl of barley soup he'd made himself, badly.

"You're burning it less," she said.

"I'm not burning it at all."

"Progress." She took the bowl. Their fingers didn't touch, but it was close.

He sat across from her, back against the wall where he could see both the window and the ladder, and she understood that he never sat anywhere else. A lifetime of guarding a valley would do that to a man. She wondered if he ever slept lying down, or if he'd simply trained himself to rest upright, sword across his knees, listening for the world to break again.

"The farms are empty east of here," he said. "Vane's patrol pulled back to the ridge."

"That's worse."

"Yes." He said it plainly, no comfort in it, and she was grateful. "Inquisitors don't retreat. They reposition. They want us to think the valley's ours again."

Wren ate her soup and watched light fail through the gap in the shutters. Three days since the mill had become her prison by choice, since Jonah had hidden her here among the flour sacks while the ward-line still smoldered along the hills. Three days of Ilsa bringing bandages, Tam bringing gossip, Doss bringing complaints about everyone. Three days of almost saying it.

*The usurper's blood is stolen. His line is a lie. The locket proves it.*

She had opened her mouth twice last night. Both times she'd seen Master Corvin instead — seen him standing very straight in the doorway of the archive while the inquisitors' lanterns came down the street, telling her to run before they arrived, telling her with his eyes what he would never say aloud: *they will make me tell, and I would rather die than give them you.*

So she swallowed the truth back down, and it sat in her chest beside the locket, two hot stones where a heart should be.

"There's something you're not telling me," Jonah said quietly.

"There's something *you're* not telling me. You look at that book like it bites."

"It might." He rose. "Sleep if you can. I'll take first watch."

He went down the ladder, and Wren lay back against the grain sacks and listened to the mill creak, and did not sleep.

* * *

They came at dawn, but not the way dawn raids come — no horns, no boots, no battering ram against the great door. Instead there was a smell: bread. Fresh bread, woodsmoke, and under it the faint sweet char of something burnt.

Then a voice, carrying up through the floorboards as easily as sunlight.

"Wren Ashvale. Might I come in? I've brought breakfast."

Wren was on her feet with a knife in her hand before she was fully awake, every muscle remembering Corvin's door, the lanterns, the running. Below, she heard Jonah's low voice, then the unmistakable sound of steel clearing a scabbard.

"Lower it, swordspeaker, or your friends in the village learn what question looks like when we stop asking politely." A pause, mild as milk. "There are forty of us on the ridge and six fires already lit in the far fields. This is not a threat. It is arithmetic."

Silence. Then Jonah, tight with fury he couldn't spend: "Come up, then."

Boots on the stairs — unhurried, even. The inquisitor who appeared at the top of the ladder was not what Wren had feared. She was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, wearing the plain charcoal coat of her order with no braid or ornament. Her face was lined around the mouth like someone who laughed more than she wanted to. She carried a covered basket and nothing else. No weapon that Wren could see, which meant weapons everywhere.

"Serah Vane," she said, setting the basket on the grinding floor below, well out of reach of anyone's knife. She lifted the cloth. Bread, cheese, apples. Steam curled off the loaf. "I know how this looks. I'd think less of you if you didn't hold that knife like you mean it."

"How did you find me?" Wren's voice came out steadier than she felt.

"You've been buying eggs from the Hollis farm through the boy. He overcharges you, by the way. Terribly." Vane folded her hands. "Three days, Miss Ashvale. I've had three days to take this mill apart with you in it. Do you understand that I haven't?"

"I understand you're playing a longer game."

"I understand," Vane said, "that your mother made better bread than this, and I've spent twenty years missing it."

The words landed like a dropped glass. Wren kept the knife up. Kept it steady, barely.

"You didn't know my mother."

"I set her broken arm when she was seventeen, after she fell out of a plum tree stealing fruit she could have asked for. That was before either of us wore our coats — hers white, mine gray." Something crossed Vane's face, quick and quickly buried. Grief, maybe. Or its excellent counterfeit. "Elin Ashvale. She called herself a historian afterward. She was the bravest liar I ever loved."

"My mother never spoke of you."

"No," Vane said softly. "She wouldn't. I'm the one who arrested her teacher, you see. The year you were born. She never forgave me, and she was right not to." Vane looked up at the loft, at Wren silhouetted there with her knife, and her voice stayed gentle, terribly gentle. "I let that arrest happen so that Elin could run. So that *you* could be born somewhere no one was looking. Whatever she told you about her choices — I made half of them for her."

"That's a lovely story."

"It's true."

"So was the Trojan horse full of soldiers. Truth isn't the same as gift."

Something flickered in Vane's eyes — surprise, and behind the surprise, respect. "Your mother's tongue exactly."

"Give me one reason I shouldn't scream for the swordspeaker to cut you down."

"Because he can't." Vane said it without heat. "His oaths bind his blade to lawful authority, and I carry lawful writs signed in wax he'd know blind. He knows it. That's why he hasn't moved yet. Ask him." She didn't wait for an answer; she turned slightly, addressing the shadows near the stairwell where Jonah stood. "Isn't that right, swordspeaker? You've been standing there working out whether you can touch me, and the answer is no. Not unless I raise a hand in violence first. And I have never once needed to."

Wren looked at Jonah. He didn't deny it. His jaw worked once, twice, and he said, "She's right. Which means everything she says next is a trap with legal corners."

"All kindness has corners, swordspeaker. Mine at least show the seams." Vane turned back to the loft. She reached into her coat — slowly, telegraphing every motion — and drew out a folded document, sealed in green wax, and laid it on the grinding floor beside the basket.

"Amnesty," she said. "Full pardon, signed under the usurper's own seal. Your name cleared. Free passage to any city in his domain. A stipend. A history lectureship, if you like — the University at Corvess has an open chair, and I have already recommended you for it. In exchange, the locket. Just the locket, Wren. Its contents go into an archive, sealed for a hundred years. No one reads them. No one is hurt by them. Your mother's secret dies of old age instead of getting people killed."

"And the people who already died for it?" Wren heard her own voice crack and hated it. "Master Corvin. They burned him alive in his own archive."

"I know." For the first time, something in Vane's composure shifted — a hairline fracture, real pain or perfect craft. "I read the report. I requested that duty specifically, to make sure the ones who did it were punished for doing it *badly.* That sounds monstrous to you. I know. But hear me, girl, because I will only say this once, and I will say it truly because I am bound by my own order to speak no lie inside an offer of amnesty: the men who burned Corvin acted on orders that did not come from the crown alone. There are others who want what's in that locket. Others worse than me. Worse than my master. If you run, they will find you, and they will not offer breakfast."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a weather report." Vane stepped back from the basket, palms open. "You have until midday. Think about what your mother would have wanted. Not what she told you she wanted — what she actually wanted, deep down, which I promise you was a daughter who grew old."

She descended the ladder. At the bottom she paused, one hand on the rail, and without turning around said:

"She used to sing to you. A valley song, about thorns. She sang it the day I helped her disappear. I've thought about that song every day for twenty years, and I still don't know if she forgave me. That's my punishment, and I accept it. But you — " her voice caught, just barely, artfully, Wren couldn't tell — "you get to choose differently than she did. Midday, Wren."

The door closed. The smell of bread stayed.

* * *

Jonah didn't speak for a long time after. He stood at the shuttered window watching the gray morning, and Wren sat on the top step of the ladder, amnesty papers unopened beside her, and the locket burned against her skin.

"You believe her?" she finally asked.

"I believe she believes it." He turned. "That's worse. Fanatics of kindness are harder to fight than fanatics of fire."

"She knew my mother."

"People who hunt us always knew someone we loved. It's how they get in." He came and sat on the step below her, close enough that she could feel the warmth off his shoulder. "What did she mean, about my oaths?"

"She said you can't touch her."

"She's right, mostly. Swordspeech binds my steel to lawful authority while she keeps the king's peace. The moment she draws on you, that changes." He looked at his hands. "There are words I could say. Words that would end this conversation permanently. But each oath I break tears loose something in me I don't get back, and I've been spending them like a dying man spends coin since the ward fell. I'm nearly poor, Wren."

For a moment, in the dusty light, she almost told him. Everything. The locket's weight, Corvin's last lesson, the name written in the stolen bloodline. It rose in her throat like a tide.

And then she heard Vane's voice again — *I made half of them for her* — and the tide went out.

Kindness with corners. Everyone had them. Even Jonah, with his book he wouldn't share and his oaths he counted like coins. She was so tired of other people deciding what she could carry.

"I need until midday," she said. "Alone."

"Wren—"

"*Alone*, Jonah. Please."

He left. She heard his horse going hard toward the village, and then the mill was hers, and the silence, and the bread going cold in its basket like an argument nobody had won.

* * *

Midday came gold through the shutter gaps, laying bars of light across the grinding floor like the bars of something. Wren sat in them. She had not packed. She had not decided. She had taken out the locket and held it in her palm for an hour, feeling it pulse faintly, and spoken to it as though her mother could hear.

"What did you actually want?"

The locket, being brass and bone-lock and blood-seal, said nothing.

At noon exactly — punctual as arithmetic — the door opened without a knock. Serah Vane came in alone, unhurried, and stopped a respectful ten paces away. Through the window behind her, Wren could see the ridge line. Empty. Truly empty, or made to look it.

"Well?" Vane said.

Wren stood. She put her hand flat against her chest, over the locket, the way her mother had taught her to stand when reciting the names of the fallen. "My mother sang me a valley song about thorns. Do you know the ending?"

"I do."

"Then you know it isn't a surrender song." Wren's voice found its footing, and then its fire. "You want me to believe you loved her. Maybe you did. But love that arrives with forty soldiers on a ridge and a warrant in its pocket isn't love. It's siegecraft. You built a trap out of breakfast, Serah Vane, and you made it comfortable, and you made it *warm*, and you expected a frightened girl to walk in because her legs were tired." She stepped down off the dais of light. "Corvin taught me one thing before he died. He said: when they offer you mercy, count what it costs the giver. Your master spends nothing on this pardon. Nothing but paper. While I'd pay with the only proof in the world that his throne is a theft."

Vane's face went very still. Stillness, Wren realized, was this woman's version of flinching.

"So you know what it proves." Not a question. "Then you know why I can't let you keep it. Not cruelty, Wren. Arithmetic. That proof in the wrong hands is a war. Four cities burning. Tens of thousands dead. Is a locket worth that?"

"You tell me. You work for the man who'd start the war to bury it."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — a flicker, fast, of a woman hearing a sentence she'd been refusing to finish for years. Then it closed over, smooth as pond ice.

"I'm sorry," Vane said, and meant it, and that was the most frightening thing yet. "Truly. I gave you the kind way."

"I know," Wren said. "That's how I knew to run."

She moved.

Not toward the door — toward the *mill*. Her shoulder hit the brake lever of the great grindstone, the one Doss had complained about for three days, rusted half-through, and the chain above her head — the hoist chain, loaded with a full hopper of grain for the morning's milling — shrieked and gave. Two hundredweight of stone and grain came down between her and the inquisitor in a white explosion of flour and dust, and under its cover Wren went through the loading door, out onto the sluice-gate walkway, and jumped.

The millrace caught her like a cold fist. It dragged her under the wheel, through black churning water, and spat her out downstream where the current slowed over the shallows. She surfaced gasping, one boot gone, the locket clenched so hard in her fist its edges had printed themselves into her palm.

Behind her the mill door boomed open. She didn't look. She hauled herself into the reeds and lay flat in the mud, breathing through her mouth.

Then the light changed.

Orange. Flickering. Heat rolling across her back like an opened oven. Wren pressed her face into the reeds as the mill went up — not slowly, not like fire behaves, but all at once, roof and walls and the beautiful old beams blooming flame from the inside out, unnaturally fast, magic-fast, and she understood with a sick lurch that Vane hadn't come alone after all. That the fire-starters had been waiting in the trees the whole gentle breakfast-time. That the kindness was real, and so were the soldiers behind it, and both had been aimed at her from the moment the bread came out of the basket.

*I offered you the kind way,* the burning building said, in Vane's mild gray voice. *This is the other one.*

Wren lay in the mud and let herself cry, briefly, silently — for the mill, for Corvin, for the mother who had trusted this woman with a broken arm and gotten a broken life. Then she wiped her face with a muddy hand and got up and ran, upstream toward the village, toward Jonah, toward the only ragged scrap of family the world had left her.

She was halfway there when she saw the smoke rising from the village too. Thin columns of it, five, six, seven — the exact number of fires Vane had promised on the ridge.

Arithmetic.

Wren ran faster, and the last thing she heard behind her was the mill wheel collapsing into the race with a sound like a giant's bone breaking — like an oath, tearing loose.
