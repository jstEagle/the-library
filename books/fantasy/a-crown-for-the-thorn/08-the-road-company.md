## 8. The Road Company

The rain caught them two miles past the valley's southern farms, and by then nobody had the heart to complain about it.

Wren walked at the rear because the rear was where you could watch everyone at once. Old habit. In the order — in the burned, murdered, finished order of Master Callen — the youngest acolytes always walked at the rear, and the lesson had been simple: *the one behind you is the one you trust, because they could kill you and don't.* She watched Jonah's back, broad and rigid under his soaked cloak, carrying his grief like a pack he'd forgotten how to set down. She watched the ferryman, Doss, who had not stopped grumbling since the valley fell and showed no signs of stopping. She watched the girl, Ilsa, who kept touching her healer's satchel every forty steps the way other people touched a wound. And she watched the boy, Tam, who had not spoken a full sentence since the farms, and whose eyes kept going to the smoke still rising behind them like he was trying to memorize its shape.

Five of them. That was the whole army.

"You're staring," Doss said without turning around.

"I'm counting," Wren said.

"Counting what?"

"Who's going to be a problem."

The ferryman snorted. Water ran off the brim of his hat in a steady trickle. "Girl, I've been a problem for sixty years. Ask anyone on the Thornwater. They'll tell you I'm the worst thing that ever happened to that river, and I'll tell you they're right, because I like being agreed with." He shifted the axe on his shoulder — the axe he'd pulled from the hands of a dead inquisitor, though nobody had said so out loud. "You counting me?"

"You're the easiest," Wren said. "You complain before you do anything wrong. It's the quiet ones you watch."

Jonah's head turned a fraction, and Wren felt the look like a hand on her shoulder. She didn't apologize. She was done apologizing for being right about people.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the hollow beneath a fallen oak, a place where the ground dipped and the rain ran past instead of through. Doss built the fire with the economy of a man who had cooked on a river for four decades — three stones, wet wood split to the dry heart, everything done in the order that wasted nothing. Ilsa laid out her supplies on a square of oilcloth with the same kind of reverence, checking and rechecking bandages that had already been checked. Tam sat apart, hugging his knees, a leather tube of documents pressed to his chest like a child's toy.

Jonah sat last, on the outside of the circle, facing the dark.

"So," Doss said, into the silence. "The free cities. Corvess, if we're mad, and I haven't heard an argument for sanity yet. Nine days on foot, six if the ferry's still moored at Millbrook, which it won't be, because I sank it." He said it flat, like a man reporting weather. "Sank it myself. Didn't want it carrying inquisitors across my river."

"You sank your own ferry," Ilsa said. It was the first thing she'd said in an hour.

"Best thing I ever built. Broke my heart proper." He poked the fire. "Point is, we walk."

"Nine days," Tam said, so quietly Wren almost missed it. "The roads will have patrols by the third day. Maybe the second. They'll want to seal the valley's exits before word spreads that—" He stopped.

"That the ward failed," Jonah said. His voice was gravel and ash.

"That magic works out here now," Tam said, and the words came out of him like something he'd been holding under water. "That's the real thing, isn't it? That's what everyone felt when the line tore. It wasn't just the valley. The valley was holding the door *shut*. Everywhere else, it's been creeping in for years, and now there's no door at all." He looked up, and his eyes were too old for his face. "I'm a scribe. I read things. I read the requisition ledgers out of the usurper's own chancery before I ran. They've been buying cold-iron at three times market for two years. You don't buy cold-iron for a war against farmers."

Nobody answered him. The fire cracked and spat.

Wren found herself looking at the boy with new eyes. He was maybe sixteen, all elbows and ink stains, and he had just said more true things in one breath than the entire council of Corvess would say in a year. She thought about telling him something. She thought about the locket, warm against her sternum even through the wet wool, and she thought about Master Callen's face in the last moment, and she kept her mouth shut.

But she moved her bedroll closer to the fire. Closer to the boy.

* * *

The rain stopped sometime in the night, and the clouds tore open, and the stars came out in that shameless abundance the free cities never got to see. Wren took the first watch because nobody argued with her, which she noted, and filed, and decided not to be offended by.

She was an hour into it when Ilsa crept out of the dark and sat down beside her, close enough to share warmth, far enough to bolt.

"You're hurt," the girl said. "Under the cloak. Left side. You favor it when you think no one's looking, and you've been favoring it since the mill."

"I'm fine."

"I'm an apprentice. I know what fine looks like. Fine doesn't bleed through a bandage I didn't put on." Ilsa opened her satchel without waiting for permission, and there was something in the matter-of-factness of it that disarmed Wren more than any gentleness could have. "Take the shirt off or lift it. I've seen worse. I saw a man last winter with his arm hanging by a tendon, and he was *fine* too, right up until he wasn't."

Wren lifted the shirt. The burn from her crossing — the crossing through fire that the old book had promised — had scabbed and cracked, and beneath it the skin was angry and weeping. Ilsa worked in silence, cleaning, salving, wrapping, her hands quick and sure and utterly without pity. Wren decided she liked her enormously.

"Your mentor," Ilsa said, tying off the bandage. "The one they killed. Was he good to you?"

The question came out of nowhere, and Wren's breath caught on it. "Yes."

"That's what I thought." Ilsa packed her satchel. "Jonah won't talk about the order. Doss won't talk about the ferry. Tam won't talk about the chancery. And you won't talk about him. So we're five people and four silences." She stood, brushing pine needles from her skirt. "For what it's worth, I talk. Constantly. Ask anyone. It's a flaw."

"It's not," Wren said, and heard something in her own voice she didn't recognize. "It's the rarest thing there is."

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold, and with it came breakfast, and with breakfast came the fight.

It started over nothing. It started over Doss's porridge.

"This is an abomination," Doss announced, stirring the pot with the gravity of a judge passing sentence. "I've eaten river mud with more character. I've eaten my own boot, and the boot had ambitions."

"You're welcome to cook," Ilsa said.

"I *am* cooking. That's the tragedy. This is my best work."

"It's porridge, Doss," Jonah said.

"It's a *crime* is what it is, and we're all accessories." He ladled out five bowls with the air of a man distributing punishment. "In sixty years on the water I never once served bad food. Cooked for smugglers, tax men, a duke's bastard once, and a heron that used to sit on my roof. Never once. And now I'm feeding refugees off a stone in the dirt, and the porridge is a disgrace, and I'd like it noted that this is the usurper's fault too. Add it to his list."

Tam, huddled over his bowl, made a sound. It took Wren a moment to identify it, because she'd never heard it from him before.

It was a laugh.

It was a small, cracked, rusty thing, like a hinge that hadn't turned in years, but it was a laugh, and it stopped the whole camp dead. Tam clapped a hand over his mouth like he could stuff it back in. His ears went red.

"Sorry," he mumbled. "Sorry. It's not funny. Nothing's — the valley burned, and the ferry's at the bottom of the river, and I laughed at porridge."

"Boy," Doss said, "if we wait for things to be funny, we'll be the grimmest company on this road. Laugh at the porridge. It's earned worse."

And that — that stupid, small, ridiculous moment — was the thing that did it. Wren felt it happen, felt the shape of the circle around the fire change, felt five separate silences become one shared one. Jonah was watching the boy with an expression Wren had never seen on him: not the grim duty, not the buried grief, but something startled and soft, like a man finding a door in a wall he'd lived against all his life. Ilsa was grinning. Doss was already complaining about the washing up.

*We're not a company,* Wren thought. *We're a wound that's learning to close.*

* * *

They walked. The country unrolled green and wet, hills folding into hills, the Thorn Valley shrinking behind them until it was only a smudge of smoke on the horizon that nobody looked at and everybody watched. Jonah set the pace and Wren matched it, and by the second afternoon they had fallen into step together at the front, and it had the feel of something that had been decided without anyone deciding it.

"You knew," Jonah said, without preamble. "In the archive. Before the ward tore. You knew the valley was going to break."

Wren kept her eyes on the road. "I knew I was coming. I didn't know where to."

"The book named the date. It named the fire." His voice was level, but she could hear the current underneath it, the way you hear a river under ice. "I've spent my whole life inside a hundred oaths, and every one of them is about silence. What not to say. What not to show. And the one time in my life the truth walked up to my door and knocked, it came wearing a burned cloak and I couldn't even ask it a straight question."

"Ask me one now."

He was quiet for three steps. Five. Ten.

"Is the locket the reason they're hunting you?"

"Yes."

"Is it the reason the prophecy wants you?"

"Yes."

"Will you show it to me?"

"No," Wren said, and stopped walking, and turned to face him. "Not yet. And I need you to not hate me for that. Because here's what I know about you, Jonah Blackwood: you've kept every oath you were ever given, even the ones that were killing you. So you of all people should understand that I've got one too. It's not written in any book. It's to a dead man who can't release me from it. And when I break it, I want it to be because I *chose* to, not because I was cornered."

Jonah looked at her for a long time. The wind moved through the wet grass. Somewhere behind them, Doss was telling Ilsa the heron story, and Ilsa was laughing at the wrong parts.

"I don't hate you," he said finally. "I'm the last of an order that died keeping secrets, and I watched it kill them. So I'll tell you what I told myself the night the ward tore, when I decided to walk out of the only home I've ever had: secrets kept out of fear are chains. Secrets kept out of choice are armor. I just need to know which kind yours is."

"Armor," Wren said. "I swear it."

He nodded once, and walked on, and she fell in beside him, and neither of them said anything else, but the silence had changed shape. It was no longer a wall between them. It was a wall around them, and she was inside it.

* * *

The third night, the fire was bigger, because Doss had found a deadfall and Tam had produced flint from somewhere in his infinite pockets, and because — though nobody said it — they all understood that a bigger fire was a statement. *We are here. We are five. Come and see what it gets you.*

They ate. The porridge was, by unanimous and vocal agreement, worse than the first night's, which Doss took as a personal affront and Ilsa took as evidence that he was doing it on purpose now.

"So," Doss said, settling back against his pack. "Stories. That's what a road company does. I've read about it. Bands of heroes, nights around the fire, everyone tells their tale." He pointed his spoon at each of them in turn. "Jonah. Go."

"I don't have a tale."

"Everyone has a tale. You've got the biggest one here. Last swordspeaker in the world, keeper of a hundred-year ward, walks out of paradise into the mud with four strangers. That's not a tale, that's a *ballad* waiting for a tune."

"The last swordspeaker," Jonah said, "spent thirty years talking to an empty valley. He learned the rituals from a book because there was no one left to teach him. He performed them alone every festival night, and the whole time he was doing it, he was talking to ghosts. And then the ward tore, and the ghosts went quiet, and he found out he'd been guarding a door that was already open." He looked into the fire. "That's the tale. It's not much of a ballad."

"It's a start," Doss said. "Ilsa."

"I'm an apprentice healer from Millbrook. My master stayed behind with the wounded who couldn't walk." Her voice was steady, but her hands weren't. "He made me go. He said someone had to carry the craft out, or it dies with the valley. I've got his kit, his notes, and everything he ever taught me, and I intend to use all of it." She looked up. "Your turn's coming, ferryman."

"I sank my own boat to spite a tyrant, and now I'm walking to a city I've never seen to complain at people who've never done a day's honest work. That's my tale. It's a tragedy in one act." He jabbed the spoon at Tam.

Tam went pale. The firelight made him look younger, which Wren hadn't thought possible.

"I copied documents," he said. "In the chancery. Requisitions, writs, ledgers. And then one day I copied a requisition I wasn't supposed to see, and I understood what it was *for*, and I ran, and I've been running for eleven months, and I copy documents now because it's the only thing I'm good at and because—" His voice cracked, steadied. "Because somebody has to write down what's true. Somebody has to. If nobody writes it down, then in fifty years it didn't happen. And I won't let it not have happened."

The fire popped. Nobody spoke. Wren looked at the boy — at the ink-stained, terrified, iron-spined boy — and thought: *the usurper has armies, and this is what we have.* And thought: *maybe it's enough.*

"And you?" Tam asked her, timid. "Wren. What's your tale?"

She felt every eye on her. She felt the locket against her sternum, warm as a coal.

"My tale," she said, "is that I'm not ready to tell it. But I'll tell you this much for free: it ends with a crown, and it doesn't end the way anyone wants it to."

"That's a terrible story," Doss said. "No ending? What kind of teller withholds the ending?"

"The kind who's still writing it," Wren said.

And Doss laughed — a real one, deep and rolling, the laugh of a man who had earned every year of his grumbling — and Ilsa laughed, and even Tam laughed, and Jonah, last of all, after a long moment, smiled. It transformed his whole face. It made him look, for one unguarded instant, like a man who had never kept an oath in his life.

* * *

They broke camp at first light, five figures on a wet green road, heading for the free cities and whatever waited there. Doss led the argument about the route. Ilsa argued about the pace. Tam argued about both, with citations. Jonah walked in the middle of them now, not the front, not the rear — the middle, where a family keeps its heart.

Wren took the rear again. Old habit. The one behind you is the one you trust.

She was watching the horizon when she saw it: a smudge on the eastern road, dust where no cart traveled, moving too fast and too straight for anything that wasn't ridden. She counted. Six horses. Maybe seven. Lances glinting.

"Jonah," she said quietly.

He turned. He saw it. She watched the softness go out of his face and the swordspeaker come back into it, and she heard him say, in the voice he must have used on the night the ward tore — the voice of a man opening a door he could never close again:

"Off the road. Now."
