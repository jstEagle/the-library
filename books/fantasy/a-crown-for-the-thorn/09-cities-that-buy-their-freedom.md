## 9. Cities That Buy Their Freedom

Tam had never seen a city before Corvess, and his first thought — the thought he'd be ashamed of later, when he understood what Corvess really was — was *it's like a coin the size of the world.*

The sun came up behind them as they crested the last hill on the river road, and the city caught the light and threw it back in a thousand pieces: copper roofs, brass weathervanes, the gilded dome of the Council Hall rising over the harbor like a fat gold blister. The walls were white limestone. The gates stood open. Banners snapped in the morning wind — not the usurper's black thorn, but the old free-city colors, a silver scale on green.

"They're not flying his flag," Ilsa said, and there was hope in her voice, the bright stupid hope Tam was already learning to distrust.

"They're not flying anyone's flag," Doss said. "That's the point. That's the whole point of a place like this." He shifted the ferry pole across his shoulders — he hadn't let go of it since Hollowmere, Tam had noticed, not once, like it was a limb he'd grown. "Corvess doesn't take sides. Corvess *sells* sides."

Wren said nothing. She'd said very little in the six days since the bridge, and when she did speak it was in a flat, careful voice, like someone walking on a frozen lake, testing every step. She stood at the crest of the hill with the wind pulling at her burned cloak, and Tam watched her look at the city the way a man looks at a door he knows is locked.

Jonah watched her watching it. He'd been doing that a lot. There was something he wasn't saying — Tam was a scribe's apprentice, or had been, before the mill and the fire and the running, and he knew what a man looked like when he was holding a line he didn't want to read aloud.

"Come on, then," Jonah said. "We need papers, food, and a roof. In that order."

"Forged papers," Tam said.

Jonah looked at him.

"You were going to say it eventually," Tam said. "I'm just faster."

* * *

The gate guards of Corvess didn't care who you were. They cared what you were worth.

Tam learned this in the first ten minutes, standing in the slow river of traffic flowing through the Gate of Scales, where a bored clerk in a green sash waved travelers through in order of apparent income. Farmers with carts went free. Peddlers paid a copper. Refugees — and there were refugees, a ragged trickle of them from the eastern valleys, from places with names that had been on no map Tam had ever copied — were directed to a side gate and a man with a ledger, who wrote down their names and their trades and their "sponsor," and Tam watched a woman with a baby try to explain that she had no sponsor, and watched the man with the ledger close his book on her like a lid.

"Three coppers," the clerk said to Jonah, "or a sponsor's mark, or you go to the registry and wait."

"How long's the wait?" Ilsa asked.

"Registry's backed up to midwinter," the clerk said. "War refugees. You understand. We're a compassionate city." He said it the way a man reads an inscription off a coin. "Three coppers each. Children half price."

Jonah paid. Tam counted what was left in the company purse — nine coppers, a bent silver bit, and Doss's lucky river-stone, which was worth nothing and which Doss would fight someone over — and did the arithmetic that said they could eat for two days or sleep for four, not both.

Corvess swallowed them. That was the only word for it. The streets inside the gate were wide and clean and full, and everyone in them was moving with purpose, and none of that purpose had anything to do with the war. Fishmongers shouted prices. A moneylender's runner wove through the crowd with a satchel of contracts. Two men argued cheerfully about shipping rates while a third, standing on a crate, read out the morning's news sheet in a booming voice: *grain futures up, harbor fees down, the Council announces a new aqueduct.*

"—and in the east," the news-crier read, and Tam stopped, "the situation remains, per the Council's assessors, *contained.* The usurper's forces have withdrawn from the Thorn Valley approach and are not expected to press westward before the spring thaw. The Council assures all citizens that trade will not be disrupted."

"Contained," Wren said.

Tam turned. She was staring at the news-crier with an expression he'd only seen once before — on the night at the campfire when she'd finally told them, in that flat careful voice, what the inquisitors had done to her mentor. Not rage. Something older and colder than rage. The look of a person reading a lie in beautiful handwriting.

"They burned the valley six weeks ago," she said. "I *saw* it. There's nothing contained about it."

"They don't know," Ilsa said. "Or they don't want to."

"Those aren't different things," Wren said, and walked on.

* * *

The forger Tam found was named Quill — of course he was, Tam thought, of course that's his name, men in this trade name themselves like characters in a bad play — and he kept a shop above a dye-house in the Tanners' Quarter, where the smell was so ferocious it made Doss's eyes water and made Tam feel briefly at home, because his master's scriptorium had smelled almost as bad, just of oak gall and old vellum instead of piss and lye.

Quill was a small man with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who had looked at a great many things through a great many keyholes. He looked at Jonah's sword, at Wren's burned cloak, at Doss's ferry pole, and at Tam, and Tam watched him do the sum.

"Refugees," Quill said. "From the east. You want papers that say you're not refugees."

"We want papers that say we're nobody in particular," Jonah said. "Journeyman licenses. A caravan pass. Something that lets us walk through this city without being a question."

"Papers that let you be boring," Tam said. "I can write the hands. I need the seals."

Quill smiled, and it was not a warm smile, but it was an honest one, which in Tam's experience was rarer. "The boy knows the trade. Sit. Talk seals to me."

They talked seals for an hour. Corvess had eleven guilds with the right to issue travel documents, four of them sloppy, two of them for sale, and one — the Lamplighters' Compact, of all things — whose counterseal had been copied so many times that half the genuine ones in the city were forgeries and nobody, including the Lamplighters, could tell the difference anymore. Tam listened, and memorized, and felt something in his chest that he hadn't felt since the mill burned: the small hot pleasure of being *good* at the thing his hands knew how to do.

His master had caught him forging his signature when Tam was eleven. He'd expected a beating. Instead the old man had sat down across from him, turned the forged signature around, and said, *the ascender's too proud, boy. A real signature is tired. It's been written ten thousand times. Forge the tiredness.* It was the kindest thing anyone had ever said to him, and it had gotten them both killed eventually, or the master at least, when the inquisitors came through the scriptorium's ledger and found three names too many. Tam hadn't thought about that in six days. He made himself think about it now, sitting in the dye-house smell with the company's last silver bit on the table, because forgetting it would be a kind of lie, and lies were what had gotten the whole world into this state.

"Three days," Quill said. "Come back at dusk on the third day and you'll be four boring people with a boring mule."

"We don't have a mule," Ilsa said.

"You will," Quill said. "The papers say you do."

* * *

They spent the three days being boring, or trying to. Doss found work hauling at the fish docks, and came back each evening with silver and a new stock of opinions about the Corvess harbor guild, all of them contemptuous. Ilsa took shifts at a charity house in the Weavers' Ward, and came back quieter each night, and on the second night she said, "There's a room with forty beds in it and forty families on a list for it, and the list is a year long, and the woman who keeps the list is kind, she's genuinely kind, and she told me the list can't get shorter because the Council won't fund a second room. She cried telling me. Then she went back to turning people away." Ilsa said it without anger, which was the worst way to say it.

Tam spent the days in the public scriptorium, a long bright hall where citizens could hire a scribe by the hour, and he wrote letters for people. Love letters, mostly, and complaints to the harbor guild, and one magnificent document in which a widow formally disputed a tax assessment on a boat that had sunk four years ago. He was good at it. He found the tiredness in every signature.

It was on the second day, in the scriptorium, that he saw the archive copy of the Council's assessors' report on the eastern situation, because the scriptorium kept a rack of public documents for anyone to consult, and because Tam had never in his life met a locked book he didn't want to open.

The report was beautiful. That was his first professional observation. Whoever had written it was better than he was — the margins were ruled in gold, the section headers were illuminated with little silver scales, and the prose was a masterwork of the tired hand. *The Council's assessors find no evidence of organized military movement west of the Thorn approach. Isolated incidents of banditry have been addressed. Trade flows remain within seasonal norms.*

Tam read it three times. He had stood in a burning valley six weeks ago. He had watched a woman fall through a torn ward with her clothes still smoking. He had buried a ferryman under a cairn of river stones two days ago. *Isolated incidents of banditry.*

It wasn't even a lie, he realized. That was the genius of it. Every sentence in the report was technically true — the assessors had found no evidence, because they hadn't looked; the incidents *had* been addressed, by burning; the trade flows were fine, because the trade was in grain and the usurper wanted the free cities fat and calm until he was ready to carve them. The document didn't lie to you. It arranged the truth so carefully that you lied to yourself, and then it charged you a copper an hour to read it.

He copied it out. All of it, in his own hand, fast, while the hall's attendant wasn't looking. He didn't know why yet. He just knew that documents were how this city thought, and that if you wanted to argue with a city you had to do it in its own handwriting.

* * *

Wren found the prophecy's second mention of Corvess before Tam could tell her about the report.

She came to the scriptorium at dusk on the third day, when the lamps were being lit, and she stood over him while he finished a widow's tax dispute, and she waited with a patience that Tam had learned to find frightening, because it was the patience of someone who had decided something.

"You've been reading the Council's documents," she said.

"Somebody has to."

"Jonah won't. He says the prophecy is a locked door and he's not the key." She sat down across from him, in the tired way people sit when they've been carrying something all day. "But you read everything. So read this." She put a scrap of paper on the desk — her own hand, cramped, the handwriting of someone who'd learned letters in a hurry and in fear. It was a line from the prophecy book, the one in Jonah's sealed archive, the one whose last entry had named the night she fell through fire. Tam had never seen the book. Jonah kept it wrapped in oilcloth and didn't speak of it, and Wren had only gotten fragments out of him, like teeth.

The line read: *When the coin-city counts its scales and calls the fire contained, the first flame is already lit beneath the counting-house.*

Tam read it twice. He thought about the news-crier. *Contained.* He thought about the assessors' report in its gold margins. He thought about the Council Hall dome, fat and gold, blistering over the harbor.

"It's not subtle," he said.

"Prophecy never is. That's what makes it unbearable." Wren looked out the scriptorium's tall windows, down toward the harbor, where the lamps of the counting-houses were coming on one by one, pretty as a festival. "Jonah thinks the prophecy is about me. The woman from the fire. Maybe it is. But this part isn't about me. This part is about *them*." She tapped the windowsill, the city beyond it. "They've already decided the fire is contained. It's written down. It's illuminated in silver. And a city that has written a thing down will defend the writing before it defends itself."

Tam thought of his master. *Forge the tiredness.* The Council's report had the tiredness. It had been written by someone very good, very tired, very well paid.

"I copied the assessors' report," he said, and pulled it out.

She read it the way she did everything — completely, once, without moving. Then she put it down and was quiet for a long moment.

"They know," she said finally. "Somebody in that hall knows. You don't write this well about something you haven't checked." Her hand went to the locket at her throat, the small unconscious gesture he'd seen a hundred times now, the fingers closing over it like a hand over a wound. "They've decided we're the price of the spring thaw. They've done the arithmetic. The city is worth more than we are."

"The city doesn't know it's arithmetic," Tam said. "That's the trick of it. Nobody in Corvess thinks they've been counted. They think they counted."

Wren looked at him then, really looked, and something in her face shifted — the flat careful look cracking, just a little, into something almost like companionship. "You're going to make a dangerous old man, Tam."

"I'm going to make a forger," he said. "Dangerous is Quill's department."

* * *

They collected the papers at dusk on the third day. Quill had outdone himself: four journeyman licenses in the Lamplighters' Compact hand, a caravan pass for a mule none of them owned, and a bill of lading for "textiles, assorted, 14 bales" that gave them a reason to be on any road in the free cities. Tam checked the seals against his memory of the genuine article and found the tiredness perfect. He told Quill so. Quill looked inordinately pleased.

Jonah paid with the last of the silver and the bent bit, and Quill bit the bent bit out of habit and then, unexpectedly, gave it back. "For luck," he said. "I don't take a man's last bent coin. Bad for the trade." Then, as they were leaving, quieter: "The Lamplighters' seal is good for six months. After that, the Compact rotates its counterseal. Whatever you're going to do with these —" his eyes went around the room, the burned cloak, the sword, the ferry pole, the girl with the locket — "do it fast."

They came out of the Tanners' Quarter into a city lit up for the evening. It was the Festival of the Scales, Tam realized — of course it was, the harvest-quarter festival, when Corvess celebrated the balance of its ledgers. Lanterns everywhere. Music from the Weavers' Ward. Children running with paper scales on strings. On the Council Hall steps, a herald was reading the year's accounts aloud to a cheering crowd: revenues, expenditures, the surplus, the *contained* east.

Doss stopped in the street and looked up at the lanterns, and Tam saw the old ferryman's jaw working.

"Forty families on a list," Ilsa said, to no one.

"Isolated incidents of banditry," Tam said.

"The first flame is already lit," Wren said.

Jonah said nothing at all. He stood in the lantern light with his hand on his sword — not on the hilt, Tam noticed, but flat against the scabbard, the way a man touches a thing he's sworn to and is afraid of — and he was looking at the Council Hall dome, and his face was the face of a man reading a document he wished he could unwrite.

"We could warn them," Ilsa said. "Go to the Council. Tell them what's coming. We have the prophecy, we have — we have *her.*" She gestured at Wren, apologetically, the way you'd gesture at a relic.

"And they'll write it down," Wren said. "In gold margins. *A woman arrived claiming the city's doom; assessors dispatched; situation contained.*" She shook her head. "I came here thinking the free cities were the good side. The side worth saving." She looked at the lanterns, the paper scales, the children. "They're not a side. They're a ledger. And ledgers don't bleed, so ledgers don't learn."

"So justice needs other hands," Tam said.

They all looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It had just come out, the way the true things did when he was tired.

"That's what my master used to say. Before the inquisitors. He said the law was a wall built by people who'd never been outside it, and justice was what you did in the road when the wall was between you and the person bleeding." He shrugged, embarrassed. "I thought he was being dramatic. He was a scribe. Scribes aren't dramatic. That was the whole point of him."

Jonah turned from the Council dome. When he spoke, it was in the voice he used for oaths, low and level, each word set down like a stone.

"Then we don't go to the Council," he said. "We go to the people the Council counts. The forty families. The dock hands. The registry line at the side gate." He looked around at them — the ferryman, the healer, the forger, the woman from the fire — and Tam saw, for the first time since Hollowmere, something in Jonah's face that wasn't grief. "The prophecy says the flame is lit beneath the counting-house. Fine. Let it come. But when it comes, there are going to be people in this city who know which door to run for, because somebody told them. Somebody *wrote it down.*" A ghost of something that was almost a smile. "Tam. How are you at broadsheets?"

It took Tam a moment to understand. Then he felt the hot small pleasure again, the one from Quill's shop, except this time it wasn't small at all.

"You want me to forge the news," he said.

"I want you to tell the truth," Jonah said, "in a format this city will actually read."

Behind them, up on the Council steps, the herald finished the year's accounts and the crowd cheered the surplus, and somewhere out in the dark east, beneath the contained silence, Tam could almost hear the flame working at the timbers.

"First problem," he said. "I need a press. Second problem —" he looked at the lanterns, the pretty paper scales, the whole fat gold blister of a city that had already decided it was safe — "I need it by tomorrow."

"Why tomorrow?" Ilsa asked.

"Because," Wren said, and her hand had gone still on the locket, and her voice had gone flat and certain, the voice of a woman reading a line she'd hoped never to reach, "the prophecy gives the coin-city nine nights after the festival. Tonight is the first."
