## 10. The Prophecy's Second Reader

The archive beneath the Hall of Records smelled of dust and candle smoke and something older — vellum, maybe, or the memory of vellum. Jonah had been in a hundred libraries since the valley fell, and they all had the same smell: the smell of things people were afraid to throw away.

Corvess's Hall of Records was built like a fortress because it was one. The free cities didn't trust their histories to chance. Three floors below street level, behind two locked doors and a gate that required a council writ to open, the archivists kept everything the city preferred not to think about.

Which was why Tam's forged writ had worked so well. Nobody checked a forgery against a document nobody wanted to read.

"You're sure he'll see us?" Wren whispered. Her hood was up, her hands buried in her sleeves. In the candlelight she looked like she was made of shadows and stubbornness.

"He asked for you by name," Jonah said. "That's what worries me."

* * *

The archivist's name was Maren Cole, and he was blind.

Not blind the way old men went blind, clouded and dim. His eyes were gone entirely — smooth lids over empty sockets, an old wound healed badly — and yet when Jonah entered the reading room, Maren turned his head toward him with the precision of a man looking straight at him.

"Swordspeaker," Maren said. "I can hear it in how you walk. You step like every floorboard owes you money."

"I don't carry a sword."

"No," Maren agreed. "You carry six oaths and a dead order. It weighs about the same." He gestured to a chair with a hand knotted by age. "Sit. Both of you. The girl stands where she is — I want to hear her breathe while we talk."

Wren stiffened. Jonah put a hand on her arm. *Easy.*

"You knew I'd come," Jonah said.

"I've known someone would come for eleven years." Maren settled back into his chair. He was small, ancient, wrapped in three layers of wool despite the archive's dry warmth. "Eleven years ago, a man came down those stairs with a writ signed by the Corvess Council of the day. He took a book from my keeping and cut four pages out of it with a razor. Then he paid me not to remember his face."

"Did you?"

"I'm blind, swordspeaker, not stupid. Of course I did." A dry smile. "But I also kept the pages' neighbors. Paper remembers where its brothers lived. When your scribe friend started asking questions about prophecy registries last week — asking cleverly, I'll grant, but asking — I sent word to the Broken Lantern inn that a man who owed me a debt could be repaid."

Jonah glanced at Wren. She hadn't moved. Her breathing was shallow and fast.

"The book," Jonah said. "The Thorn Valley registry. The prophecy of the woman through fire."

"*A woman comes through fire,*" Maren recited, without hesitation, without opening anything. His voice took on the flat cadence of text memorized so long ago it had worn grooves in him. "*And the valley that magic cannot follow shall follow her.* Yes. That's the page everyone knows. The page the order copied and sang and built a hundred years of vigilance on. There were other pages."

"The usurper's man took them."

"One man took them. Eleven years ago." Maren's head tilted. "You already know who. You just haven't said it aloud yet."

Jonah hadn't. But the arithmetic had been sitting in his chest since Hollowmere, cold and heavy as river stones. Serah Vane had known Wren's mother. Vane had offered amnesty for a locket before anyone should have known a locket mattered. And eleven years ago, a young inquisitor rising fast in the usurper's service would have been exactly the kind of person trusted to do quiet, ugly errands in free city archives.

"Serah Vane," Jonah said.

Maren nodded once, unsurprised. "She was younger then. Polite. She apologized to me before she cut the pages out. That's the part I never forgot. Most men who do damage enjoy it. She hated every minute, and she did it anyway, and then she thanked me for my patience." He spread his ruined hands. "I have thought about that apology for eleven years, swordspeaker. People who apologize while they burn things are either cowards or prisoners. I've decided she's neither. I've decided she's a woman waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone to give her permission to stop." Maren shrugged. "But that's not why you're here. You're here for the second page. So let's talk about the second page."

He reached beneath his chair and drew out a long wooden case, iron-banded, sealed with wax that had cracked years ago. He broke the rest of the seal with his thumb.

"I made a copy," he said. "Before Vane came. I'm an archivist. Copying is the whole of my religion." He held the case out toward Jonah — then stopped, and turned it instead toward the sound of Wren's breathing. "Girl. Before I give this to the swordspeaker, I want to ask you something. Have you read the whole prophecy? What your order sang, what the villages whisper?"

"I've heard it," Wren said. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't. "*A woman comes through fire, and the valley that magic cannot follow shall follow her.*"

"That's the first half. Do you know what the second half says?"

"No."

Maren was quiet a moment. The candles guttered. Somewhere above them, the city of Corvess went on being comfortable — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the ordinary music of people who believed walls would save them.

"Then hear it now," Maren said, "and understand that I am an old man who has kept this secret too long, and that whatever happens after tonight is on all three of us equally." He lifted the copy from the case — Jonah heard paper, real paper, old paper — and began to read.

* * *

"*And the woman through fire shall come to the crown that has no keeper,*" Maren read. His voice stayed flat, archival, which somehow made it worse. "*And the crown shall know her blood, and offer itself to her hand. And here is the fork that the wise must see: if she takes it up, she shall be queen of all that was lost, and the war shall begin again under a banner no one can refuse. And if she refuses it, the crown shall not forgive. What is refused turns to ruin. She shall be unmade by the thing she will not wear — burned from within, as she was burned without, until nothing remains but ash and the crown, waiting for the next hand, and the next, forever.*"

Silence.

The candle flame bent and straightened. Jonah became aware, distantly, that he had stopped breathing somewhere around *unmade*, and that his hands had closed into fists hard enough to ache.

"There's more," Maren said gently. "A final line. It's short."

"Read it," Jonah said. His voice came out wrong. Low and rough, like gravel dragged over steel.

Maren read it.

"*Only truth spoken over the crown can unmake it. Only a swordspeaker's voice can speak that truth. This is why the order was made, and this is why the order must end.*"

The words landed in the room and sat there like a body.

Jonah stared at the blind archivist. At the paper in his ruined hands. At Wren, who stood very still by the door, her hood shadowing her face, and whose breathing had gone so quiet she might have been trying to disappear.

*This is why the order must end.*

His order. His dead masters. They hadn't guarded the valley because the valley mattered. They had guarded it because they were waiting — a hundred years of ritual and oath and lonely duty, all of it a watch-post for one woman falling through fire. And the last line, the line that had gotten pages razored out of books across five kingdoms: the order existed to end. To be there, at the end, with a voice that could unmake a crown.

*Tell the truth even when it costs.* The first oath. He'd taught it to children once, in another life. He'd never understood it was a weapon specification.

"Why censored?" Jonah managed. "Why would the usurper hide this? If the woman can be destroyed by refusing the crown, that's good news for him. Let her refuse. Let her burn."

"Think again," Maren said. "Read it as a man who gives orders rather than a man who follows them."

Jonah thought. The firelight moved. And then it assembled itself, piece by piece, the way a battle assembles when you finally see the ground.

"If she claims the crown, there's a war — but a war needs a rightful heir to fight over, and a living heir is dangerous even to her own side. If she dies refusing it—" He stopped. Started again. "No. If she dies *anywhere but publicly*, the crown survives, waiting. The prophecy doesn't say the crown dies with her. It says it waits for the next hand. Forever. Which means the usurper can't just kill her in a ditch. He needs her dead in a way that ends the question — or he needs her alive, claiming the crown, so he can kill a queen in front of everyone and prove the bloodline broken."

"Yes," Maren said. "Either way, he controls the ending. That's why the pages were cut. Not because the prophecy threatened him. Because it told everyone else how he intends to win."

"And the garden," Jonah said quietly. "In the capital. The usurper keeps a garden prison where he puts people he wants to display."

He felt Wren move — a flinch, small, quickly mastered. He turned to look at her and wished he hadn't, because her face had gone the color of candle wax.

"You knew," she said. Not to Maren. To Jonah. "Your order knew. A hundred years ago, they wrote down that I exist. That I'd come. That I'd burn." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible part. It got quieter, and colder, and more precise. "Did they write down my name too? Did they have a page for what happens to me after?"

"Wren—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and it was shaking now, and she let it shake. "Don't say my name like you're sorry. You've known since the archive in the valley. Since the night I fell through. You've been walking beside me for weeks knowing there's a page that describes me like a — like a *weather event*. Woman through fire. Valley follows her. Unmade by refusal." She laughed, one note, no humor in it anywhere. "I thought the locket was my secret. Turns out I was yours."

"That's not fair," Jonah said, and heard immediately how useless the words were.

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't. Isn't that the whole point of prophecies?" She pulled her hood back. Her eyes were bright and furious and frightened underneath, all three at once, and Jonah thought: *she's twenty years old, maybe twenty-one, and every powerful person in the world has decided what she's for.* "Does it say how I get out? Does your precious second page say anything about the woman surviving? Or does it only describe the two ways I lose?"

Maren was silent. The silence was answer enough.

Wren nodded slowly. She looked at the paper in the old man's hands the way you'd look at a snake you suspected of being venomous. "Copy it," she said finally. "Tam will want it. Ilsa will want it. Everyone gets to read the page about me except, apparently, me — well. Now me too." She pushed off the wall. "I need air. Don't follow me, Jonah. I mean it. Ten minutes where you're not looking at me like I'm a prophecy with legs."

She left. Her footsteps went up the stairs, quick and hard, and the door banged, and the candles steadied in the sudden stillness.

* * *

"She'll come back," Maren said.

"She shouldn't have to." Jonah sat down heavily in the chair he'd refused earlier. He put his face in his hands. "Eleven years you've sat on this. Why tell us now? Why me?"

"Because you're the swordspeaker." Maren said it simply, folding the copy back into its case. "Because the last line names your voice, and I'm too old to hunt for a better candidate. And because—" he paused, and his empty sockets turned toward Jonah with unsettling accuracy, "—because I watched you lie to her just now. Or try to. You started to say something when she asked about the garden, and you swallowed it. What was it?"

Jonah lowered his hands. "The company talked about going to the capital. After Corvess. Rescuing people Vane's taken. I voted against it. I told them the capital was suicide." He exhaled. "What I didn't tell them is that I recognized the garden's name. From the order's records. The valley archive had a list of places the order watched. The garden was on it. The order always believed the crown would end up there eventually — that the usurper collects claimants the way other men collect coins."

"So you knew," Maren said, "that the place your friends want to break into is exactly the trap the prophecy predicted. And you said nothing."

"I said it was suicide."

"You said it was suicide. You didn't say it was *the* suicide. The one written down a century ago, with her name shaped into the space where the body goes." Maren set the case on the table between them with a soft knock. "Swordspeaker. I'm going to tell you something true, because telling true things is my trade and I'm out of practice at anything else. You lied to her just now, small and kind, to keep her from fear. And you lied to your company, small and kind, to keep them from hope. And you are lying to yourself right now, large and unkind, when you tell yourself you're protecting her."

Jonah said nothing.

"You're managing her," Maren went on. "The way the order managed everything — quietly, dutifully, alone. That's what killed them, in the end. Not the usurper. Solitude dressed up as duty." He tapped the case. "There's a third path in that prophecy. The page doesn't name it — pages never do; that's what makes them prophecy instead of instruction — but it exists. It always exists. Truth spoken over the crown can unmake it. Your voice. Hers. Together, maybe. But no one finds the third path by walking separately, and no one walks together on a road built out of omissions."

"She'll hate me," Jonah said. "When she finds out I hid the second page. When she finds out I've known about the garden since before Hollowmere."

"Yes," Maren said, with the serene certainty of a man reading a ledger. "She will. For a while. And then she'll decide whether you're a man who lies to protect himself or a man who lies because he loves something and hasn't learned a better way yet. That decision is hers. It was always going to be hers. The prophecy's whole argument, swordspeaker, underneath the crowns and the burning — the whole argument is that people get to choose." He smiled, dry as dust. "Even you."

Jonah took the case. It was lighter than it should have been, holding what it held. He stood.

"What do I owe you?"

"Nothing. I waited eleven years to hand this off. Consider us square." Maren turned his face back toward his candles. Then, as Jonah reached the stairs: "Swordspeaker. One more thing. The man Vane sent, eleven years ago — she took four pages. I copied two. The other two copies were in a sister archive in Corvess's upper vaults."

"And?"

"And three days ago, the upper vaults burned. Officially, a lamp fell." Maren's voice was very quiet. "Unofficially, someone is cleaning up. Someone who knows I exist, and knows what I keep, and is working down the list." He folded his hands. "Go home to your inn, swordspeaker. Walk fast. And whatever you decide to tell the girl — decide it soon. Lists get shorter."

* * *

The Broken Lantern was loud with the ordinary noise of a free city evening — dice, arguments, a fiddler murdering a ballad. Jonah stood in the doorway a moment, the case under his coat, and found Wren at the corner table with Ilsa and Tam, laughing at something Tam had drawn on a napkin. Doss's axe leaned against the bench where it had leaned since Hollowmere. Nobody had moved it. Nobody ever would.

Ilsa caught Jonah's eye and waved him over. Tam scooted down. Wren made room without looking at him, which was its own kind of message.

"We got it?" Tam whispered, eyes shining. "The second page? Is it real?"

"It's real." Jonah sat. He set the case on his knees, under the table, and looked across at Wren. She looked back, wary, arms crossed, still burning.

He had the words ready. All of them. *There's a third path. Maren believes it. I believe it. I should have told you about the garden. I should have told you everything, the night the ward tore open, and I didn't, and I'm done —*

"Tomorrow," Jonah said instead. "Tomorrow, when everyone's awake, I'll tell you all of it. Everything on the page. Everything I've been carrying. You deserve to hear it once, properly, instead of in pieces in a basement."

Wren studied him for a long moment. Something flickered behind her eyes — not belief, not yet, but a willingness to wait for it, which was nearly as precious.

"Everything," she said. Not a question. Almost not a threat.

"Everything," Jonah said.

And it was even mostly true — tomorrow he would tell her the page, every word, the fork and the fire and the line about his own voice. Tomorrow he would hand her the whole weight of it and stand there while she decided what he was.

But upstairs in his room, alone, with the case open on the bed and the candle burning low, Jonah read the final line again by himself, moving his lips without sound, practicing:

*Only truth spoken over the crown can unmake it.*

And beneath it, in the archivist's careful hand, a note Maren had added in fresher ink, small enough to miss:

*The vault fire was not the first. Ask the girl what happened to the mill in Thorn Valley, and who lit it, and whether she noticed the inquisitor hesitated before the flame caught. Lists get shorter, swordspeaker. Some names get crossed off. Some get moved to the top.*

Jonah sat very still.

Then he blew out the candle, because some things you understand better in the dark, and the thing he understood was this: Serah Vane wasn't hunting Wren anymore.

She was running out of time to save her.
