## 11. Loyalty Weighs Its Price

Wren found the second page by accident, which was the only way anyone ever found anything Jonah Blackwood tried to hide.

They had made camp in the shell of a shepherd's cot two days east of Corvess, close enough that the city's watch-fires still smudged the horizon at night like a bruise that wouldn't heal. Tam was asleep with his satchel for a pillow and ink stains on his cheek. Ilsa had gone down to the stream to fetch water. Doss was on watch, which meant he was sitting on a rock chewing something and glaring at the dark as if it owed him money.

And Jonah was asleep by the fire — actually asleep, for once, his sword across his knees the way he always kept it, his breathing slow and even.

Wren hadn't meant to go through his pack. She'd been looking for the flint, because hers had cracked on the road out of Corvess and she'd been too proud to say so. The flint wasn't in the side pocket. What was in the side pocket was a folded square of oilcloth, tied with the particular kind of knot archivists used — a knot Tam had shown her once, saying *this is how you seal something you don't want read twice*.

She should have put it back. She knew that even as her fingers worked the knot loose.

The page inside was old, older than the book it had been cut from, the vellum soft as cloth at the edges. The censor's mark sat in the corner — the black bar of the Usurper's Office of Records, stamped over text that someone had scraped away and someone else, long ago, had copied out beneath it in a hand so small it was almost an apology.

Wren read it once. Then she sat down on the cold hearthstone and read it again, because the first time her eyes had refused to do their work.

*And the woman who comes through fire shall stand before the crown of the old houses, and the crown shall know her blood. Two ends are written: she shall take it up and be remade by it, or refuse it and be unmade by it. There is no third road. The swordspeaker who walks beside her will learn this too late.*

There was more — dates, names of dead kings, a line about the valley that made her skin crawl — but those three sentences were the whole of it. The whole of everything. Either she took the crown or the crown took her. And Jonah had known. Jonah had known since Corvess, since the blind archivist, since the night he'd stood at the edge of the firelight and told her, calm as a man reporting the weather, that the prophecy named her arrival and nothing more.

*Nothing more.* He'd let her walk into every day since believing there was time. Believing they could outrun it, outthink it, that the locket could stay shut until some safer season. He'd watched her sleep by fires and taught her the grip of a blade and never once said: *the thing hunting you isn't Serah Vane. It's a choice with no good answer, and I decided you didn't need to hear it.*

"Wren."

She looked up. Jonah was awake. She didn't know how long he'd been awake — long enough, from the way his eyes went from her face to the page in her hands and stopped pretending otherwise.

"You were going to tell me," she said. It came out flat. "Eventually. Some night when I was tired enough not to argue."

Jonah sat up slowly, the way men do around wounded animals. "It wasn't—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "Don't say it wasn't mine to carry. It's my name on the page, Jonah. It's my blood the crown is supposed to *know*. You got to decide what I was allowed to know about my own death."

"It's not your death."

"Then read it again!" She was on her feet now, the page shaking in her fist. "*Refuse it and be unmade.* What does that mean? Unmade how? Burned? Erased? Turned into one of Vane's quiet little stories? You've had days to wonder about that and instead you wondered whether I'd sleep better not knowing."

"I wondered whether you'd *live* better," Jonah said, and his voice stayed low, which somehow made it worse. "You'd just come out of Corvess. You'd just learned the council wouldn't lift a finger. You were carrying a secret in a locked box around your neck and you hadn't slept a full night since the mill. And I was supposed to hand you a page that said *there is no third road*?"

"Yes." No hesitation. She surprised herself with how fast it came. "Yes, Jonah. That's exactly what you were supposed to do. Because that's the whole point of you, isn't it? Swordspeech. Truth spoken so precisely it becomes law. You told me in Hollowmere — you told me on the bridge while Doss was still—" Her throat closed. She pushed through it. "You said a truth half-spoken is worse than a lie, because a lie knows it's lying. So what does that make you?"

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, Doss shifted on his rock.

Jonah stood. He didn't come toward her; he picked up a stick and fed it to the flames, buying himself a moment, and she realized with a fresh wave of anger that this was what he did — he managed things. He managed wards and oaths and people, quietly, competently, deciding what they could bear.

"The archivist said the page was censored for a reason," he said finally. "That the old order pulled it because reading it changed how the women who read it chose. They started reaching for the crown just to end the waiting. One of them took it up at nineteen and the war ran another forty years with her name on it."

"So you censored me too. For my own good. Like the Office of Records."

"That's not fair."

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't. Not for either of us."

* * *

Ilsa came back with the water to find them still standing on opposite sides of the fire, and she read the room the way she read wounds — fast, without flinching. She set the bucket down. "What happened?"

"She found the page," Jonah said.

Ilsa looked at Wren. Wren held it out. Watched the healer-apprentice's face go through the same three stages hers had: confusion, comprehension, horror.

"*Unmade,*" Ilsa said softly. "By the crown. If she refuses it."

"There may be a way around it. The archivist thought—"

"You *asked* him?" Wren said. "You asked the blind man for more details and you didn't ask me whether I wanted any of it?"

"I was trying to find you a door before I showed you a wall!"

"And that was your choice to make." She heard her own voice rising and couldn't stop it, didn't especially want to. "You keep doing this. You decided the valley would keep me safe. You decided the locket could wait. You decided I couldn't handle one page of vellum. When do I get to be the one who decides something?"

Tam had woken at some point during all of it. He sat up in his bedroll with his hair flattened on one side, blinking at the fire like a man surfacing into a storm. "Is this about the charter? Because I can redraw the—"

"Go back to sleep, Tam," three voices said at once, and even that small accord felt like mockery.

Doss came in from the watch then, ducking under the lintel, taking in the scene with one long look. He'd been a ferryman for thirty years; he knew how to read water, and he read this. His eyes went to the page in Ilsa's hands, then to Jonah, and something in his heavy face settled, like a stone finding its place at the bottom of a river.

"You hid it," Doss said. Not a question.

"For the right reasons," Jonah said.

Doss grunted. He lowered himself onto the bench by the wall, joints cracking, and looked at the fire for a while. "Man I ferried across the Thorn, maybe fifteen years back. Tax collector. Nice enough fellow. Kept a ledger of everybody's debts in the valley villages, thought he was doing them a kindness keeping the figures himself so the Crown office couldn't inflate 'em. Called it protection." He spat into the fire. "It weren't protection. It were ownership. Same handwriting, though. Always looks the same from the outside."

"Doss," Jonah said, and there was something raw in it. Something almost pleading.

"I'm not saying you're wrong," Doss said. "I'm saying I've seen that ledger before." He folded his arms. "She should have had the page."

Wren felt it land somewhere under her ribs — not triumph, nothing so clean. Just the strange grief of watching an ally turn, and knowing that if the positions were reversed she might have done the same hiding, and hated herself for knowing it.

Ilsa set the page down on the hearthstone between them, carefully, like setting a bone. "I'm with Wren."

"You don't know what's coming," Jonah said. "None of you do. Vane has patrols on every road east. The usurper knows her face now. And the one piece of paper that says what he actually wants her for has been sitting in my pack while we walked straight through his country."

"Then we needed to know that," Ilsa said, "two days ago. In Corvess. Before we left. Before we planned anything." Her chin came up — she was the youngest of them and the steadiest, and it was a strange combination to watch. "You made the plan alone, Jonah. That's the part that doesn't wash off. Not the secret. The deciding."

The silence stretched until it hurt.

Jonah looked around the fire at each of them — at Doss with his arms folded, at Ilsa with her jaw set, at Tam pretending very badly to be asleep again — and Wren watched him understand it, really understand it, the way you understand a wound only when you try to stand on it. He had spent his whole life being the last of an order, sole keeper of its oaths, and he had built a family out here on the road without noticing that families don't take orders. They take truths. Or they leave.

"I was afraid," he said at last. It came out rough, unwilling. "Not of the page. Of your face when you read it. I've buried everyone I ever swore to protect, Wren. Every one. And I looked at you sleeping by the fire and I thought — not this one. Not this one too. Whatever it cost."

Wren's anger didn't vanish. That was the terrible part. She wanted it to — wanted to cross the fire and take his hands and say *I know, I know* — but the words on the vellum were still true, and the days he'd taken from her were still gone, and love that lies was still a kind of cage no matter how gilded the bars.

"I believe you," she said. "That's what makes it worse."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, earlier than they needed to, because nobody could sleep and everyone pretended they'd decided this together.

The company split at the fork in the road where the shepherd's track met the old king's highway — not a splitting of people, thank the stars, but of purpose, argued out in low voices while the sky went grey. Doss wanted north, into the hill country, away from roads and patrols, time to think. Wren wanted east, toward the Thorn Valley, because the last line of the hidden page — the one that had made her skin crawl — named the valley as the place where the crown must be claimed or refused, and she was done letting other people schedule her fate.

"You want to walk *toward* it," Doss said, disgusted. "Toward the thing that'll unmake you."

"I want to walk toward it *knowing*," Wren said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole difference."

Ilsa shouldered her pack and fell in beside Wren without a word. Tam hesitated on the fork like a compass needle, then scurried after the healer, mumbling something about needing witnesses for whatever chronicle he was going to write and *please don't make me choose between you, I'm begging*.

Which left Jonah standing at the fork with Doss, and Wren saw him look at the northern road, and look at her, and she understood that he was giving her the choice he'd taken away — loudly, publicly, with his feet.

"Well?" she said.

"The valley," Jonah said. "If the page is right, it ends where it began." He swung his pack up. "And if it's wrong, I owe you a funeral anyway. Might as well hold it somewhere with decent soil."

It wasn't an apology. It wasn't forgiveness either, not yet, not from either side. But it was the first honest sentence any of them had spoken since the fire, and Wren took it the way you take a rope thrown across a gap — gripping hard, not trusting it yet.

They had been walking an hour, the sun barely up, the frost still silvering the ditches, when Tam came pelting back down the road from his scouting turn ahead, waving both arms, his voice cracking across the cold morning:

"Smoke! Smoke on the east road — and riders, a lot of riders — and Wren — Wren, they're flying the usurper's black, but the lead banner's got a *key* on it, and I think — I think it's her. I think it's Vane."
