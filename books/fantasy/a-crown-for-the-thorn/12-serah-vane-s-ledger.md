## 12. Serah Vane's Ledger

The rain had followed Serah Vane for eleven days, and she was beginning to suspect it was not weather but a message.

She sat in the back room of a wayhouse outside Hollowmere with her boots off and her coat hung to dry, and she read her own orders for the fourth time. They were written in the High Hand of the Chancery — the looping, ornamental script reserved for documents meant to be admired rather than understood — and they were signed with the seal of the Lord Inquisitor himself, which was to say, with the usurper's wax and the usurper's will pressed into one red circle.

*Secure the Ashvale woman. Recover the artifact. Deliver both to the Garden by the first frost.*

It was a short order. Short orders were good orders; they left no room for interpretation, and interpretation was where inquisitors went to die. Serah had built nine years of service on that principle. You did not wonder about the man behind the seal. You did not ask what happened at the Garden after delivery. You caught the fox, you handed over the fox, you took your pension and your quiet house in Corvess and you never once looked inside the cage.

She turned the page.

There should not have been a second page. She knew this because she had written the first one — or rather, she had dictated it, three months ago, from a cold camp above the Thorn Valley, reporting the sighting of a woman who walked out of a fire that shouldn't have been possible. Her report had gone up the chain. The order had come back down. That was how the machine worked: one page up, one page down, clean as a blade being sharpened.

But someone had appended a codicil. Someone in the Chancery had taken her neat, obedient report and stitched a second instruction beneath it, in a different hand, in ink that was newer than the rest.

*In the event the artifact is recovered without the woman, proceed to Corvess on schedule. The city is to be chastened regardless. See attached disposition.*

Attached disposition. Four words that sounded like accounting.

Serah set the pages down on the table and looked at them the way you look at a dog that has just shown its teeth to you specifically, when it has never done so before.

The wayhouse was nearly empty. A carter snored by the hearth. Rain worked at the shutters like something patient trying to get in. On the table beside her orders lay her other papers: the route ledger, the expense book, the list of informants paid and unpaid, and — because she was a thorough woman and thoroughness was the only prayer she'd ever said regularly — her own private copy of everything, kept in a cipher of her own devising, because an inquisitor who trusted the Chancery to keep records straight was an inquisitor who would die of someone else's clerical error.

She had always assumed the danger would come from below. From the hunted. From some desperate witch or heretic with a knife in a stairwell. It had never once occurred to her that the paper itself might be the trap.

She picked the pages up again and read the codicil a fifth time, slower, the way she'd been taught to read confessions — looking not for what it said but for what it assumed she already knew.

*Proceed to Corvess on schedule.* Fine. Corvess was on her schedule anyway; the Ashvale woman had gone to ground there, or near enough, and the free cities were where loose threads collected.

*The city is to be chastened regardless.* Regardless. The word sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Regardless of whether she caught the woman. Regardless of whether the locket came out of this affair intact. Regardless of anything Serah Vane did or failed to do between here and the walls of the freest city on the coast.

Which meant the woman was never the point.

Which meant — and here Serah felt the first hairline crack run through nine years of certainty, thin as a flaw in glass, silent as one — which meant the locket might not be the point either.

* * *

She had joined the Inquisition at nineteen, and people always assumed it was for the cruelty, because people were simple. It hadn't been. It had been for the ledgers.

Her father had been a wool factor in a river town that changed hands three times during the succession wars, and each time it changed hands, someone burned the tax rolls, and each time the tax rolls burned, men with swords decided what her family owed, and their arithmetic was whatever they wanted it to be. She had watched a captain of horse levy a year's income off a widow because the widow couldn't prove she'd already paid, the proof having been ash. And Serah, fourteen years old, standing in the doorway with her mother's hand over her mouth, had thought: *the problem isn't the soldiers. The problem is nobody keeps honest books.*

The Inquisition kept honest books. That was the promise. Whatever else the order was — and it was plenty of things she preferred not to say aloud, even to herself — it was the one institution in the world that wrote things down and then stood behind what it wrote. Warrants before arrests. Confessions recorded verbatim. Sentences entered into the public roll so that any citizen could walk into any chancery house and read exactly why a neighbor had been taken, and know that the same rules had applied to him as to a duke. Magic answered to nothing. Kings answered to their appetites. But the ledger answered to everyone.

That was the catechism. That was the whole of it, really, stripped of the incense and the grey coats. *We are the memory of the state, and our memory does not lie.*

Serah Vane had believed it the way other women believed in saints.

And now she sat in a wet wayhouse holding two pages that did not match, and the crack in the glass widened by a degree she could feel in her teeth.

Because here was the thing about the codicil: it was not authorized. She didn't need the archives to tell her that. She had spent six years in the Chancery's record division before field work, and she knew the seals the way a musician knows keys. The Lord Inquisitor's signet was genuine — the wax had the right grain, the right depth of madder — but the countersignature beneath it belonged to Undersecretary Hallow, and Hallow had died in the spring. Fever, officially. Unofficially, fever was what the Chancery called it when a man asked too many questions about requisitions and then stopped coming to work. Serah had attended no funeral, because there had been no funeral announced, and the absence of a funeral for a man of Hallow's rank was itself a document, if you knew how to read it.

So: a dead undersecretary's name on a live order, appended to her own report without her knowledge, instructing her that a city of eighty thousand souls was to burn *regardless*.

Regardless of her.

She poured herself the last of the wayhouse's terrible wine and made herself do the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since dusk.

If the woman was the point, the codicil was redundant — you don't schedule a fire as a contingency for a capture that hasn't happened yet. If the locket was the point, same problem. So neither the woman nor the locket was the point. Which left only the third thing, the thing the whole hunt had been draped over like cloth over a frame: Corvess itself. The richest of the free cities. The one whose council had spent forty years perfecting the art of paying tribute while calling it diplomacy. The one city whose fall would teach all the others that freedom was a line item, negotiable.

They weren't hunting the fox. They were using the hunt to move the hounds into position.

And she — Serah Vane, keeper of honest books, servant of the memory that does not lie — was the courier carrying the torch and calling it a warrant.

* * *

She slept badly, which was unusual, and woke before dawn, which was not, and by grey light she had done the thing that could not be undone: she had copied the codicil into her private cipher, word for word, including the dead man's signature, and sealed the copy into the false spine of her expense ledger, where she kept the other things she was not supposed to keep.

Nine years, and she had never once hidden a document from the order. Informants' names, yes — every field officer padded their network against poaching, that was hygiene, not treason. But paper was different. Paper was the covenant. You surrendered the paper or you surrendered the oath, and there was no third door.

Except now there was, and she was standing in it, and the strange part — the part she would examine later, in the years when she had years — was how calm she felt. Not righteous. Calm. Like a woman who has finally put down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying, and discovered the weight was a lie she'd been told it was gold.

She dressed, strapped her knives, and went down to find her patrol mounted and miserable in the rain.

There were four of them. Brakka, her sergeant, a hillwoman with a face like a closed fist and the best eye for tracks Serah had ever seen. The twins, Pell and Odo, who finished each other's sentences and half her thoughts. And young Merrit, nineteen, who still crossed himself with the old signs when he passed a shrine, and whom Serah had personally pulled out of a gutter recruitment line in Corvess because he could read and the gutter couldn't afford him.

"Roads flooded south of the ford," Brakka reported. "Company won't make Hollowmere bridge before midday."

"We're not going to the bridge," Serah said.

Brakka's eyebrows climbed a fraction of an inch, which from her was a full expression of astonishment. "Orders say—"

"I've read the orders." Serah swung onto her horse and adjusted the stirrup with more attention than it needed. "We're going around. North bank, through the alder flats. We'll pick up their trail where it bends toward the high road."

"The Ashvale woman's trail," Pell said.

"That's right."

Odo glanced at his brother, and the twins had one of their wordless conversations, and Serah watched it happen and understood that her lieutenants had already noticed what she had noticed: that she was giving them a route that added a day, maybe two, to the pursuit. That the great hound of the Inquisition had suddenly developed a limp.

"Problem?" she asked pleasantly.

"No, Captain," they said together.

Merrit, who lacked the twins' discretion and possessed instead the fatal honesty of the young, frowned up at her from the saddle. "Captain — if the codicil says the city burns regardless, doesn't that mean catching her faster doesn't save anyone? I mean—" He heard himself, and went pale, and shut his mouth with an almost audible click.

The rain filled the silence. Four veterans looked anywhere except at their captain, waiting to learn which kind of officer they served.

Serah let the moment sit. Then she said, quietly, "Merrit, ride point."

His head came up. Point was the position of trust — first eyes on trouble, first to draw fire, first credit when the trail was found. It was also, in the grammar of a patrol, an answer to a question.

"Yes, Captain."

They rode north through the alder flats with the rain easing off and the light going silver behind the clouds, and Serah Vane catalogued herself with the dispassion she brought to everything: *You are now keeping secrets from the Chancery. You are now delaying a lawful pursuit. You are now, by any honest reading of your own warrant, in rebellion.* And underneath the catalogue, quieter, the sentence she could not enter into any ledger: *You do not yet know what you are going to do about it.*

That was the crack, she realized. Not doubt. Doubt was a luxury she'd never been able to afford and didn't want now. The crack was simpler and worse: for nine years she had known, absolutely, which side of the ledger she stood on. The side that wrote things down. The side that did not lie. And this morning, for the first time, she had read her own orders and found the lying happening on her side of the page — her seal, her chain of command, her catechism — and there was nowhere left to stand except on the truth itself, wherever it happened to be living these days.

Which, if the reports were accurate, was traveling with a burned woman, a disgraced swordspeaker, a ferryman, a healer, and a runaway scribe, somewhere on the high road toward a city scheduled to die.

* * *

They found the ambush site at midafternoon.

Or rather, Brakka found it, reining up so abruptly that Serah nearly rode into her, and pointed with her chin at the treeline above the road where the crows were working. What remained of the ambush party wore no colors, but they didn't need to: crossbows of Chancery pattern, grey cloaks gone dark with blood, and one man still alive enough to talk, propped against a stone with his hands pressed to his belly and his eyes already going somewhere else.

Serah knelt in the mud beside him. "Who gave the order?"

He tried to smile. It cost him too much. "Same as yours, Captain." His breath whistled. "Hollowmere bridge. Take the company alive if we can. The swordspeaker especially." A cough, red. "Alive, she said. *Especially* him."

"She?"

But he was past answering, and the crows were patient, and Serah rose slowly with the rain starting again on the back of her neck.

A separate force. Not hers, not under her warrant, operating on the same road, with orders to take Jonah Blackwood alive — *especially* him — which meant someone above her rank wanted the last swordspeaker delivered to the Garden alongside the Ashvale woman, and had not seen fit to mention it in her instructions. Two hunts running parallel, neither told of the other. That was not how you ran a manhunt. That was how you ran a purge, when you wanted witnesses to each other's crimes and no one left to compare notes.

"Captain," Brakka said behind her. Low. "This wasn't bandits. Bandits don't leave crossbows worth a year's wages in the mud."

"No," Serah said. "They don't."

She looked down the long wet ribbon of the high road, south, toward Hollowmere, toward Corvess beyond it, toward a fire that had been scheduled in a dead man's handwriting. Somewhere ahead of her, five ragged fugitives were riding toward the same ground, believing — because the world had taught them so — that the worst thing chasing them was her.

She almost pitied them. Then she corrected the entry.

"Mount up," she said. "North bank. We ride until dark."

And in the false spine of her expense ledger, in a cipher only she could read, the newest entry waited like a lit fuse: *The order lies. Find out who it lies for — and decide, before Corvess, which side of the book you write on.*
