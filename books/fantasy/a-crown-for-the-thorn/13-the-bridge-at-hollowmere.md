## 13. The Bridge at Hollowmere

Doss had ferried the Hollowmere crossing for thirty-one years, and in all that time he'd learned one thing worth teaching: a river doesn't care who you are. It takes the drunk and the pious with the same indifference. It was the only honest thing left in the world, as far as he could see, which is why he liked it better than people.

The bridge itself was older than the ferry trade — old grey stone, three arches, wide enough for two carts to pass if their drivers were friendly. It spanned the Merewater where it ran shallow and fast over white stones, the kind of water that sang all night whether anyone listened or not.

He stood on it now in the blue hour before dawn, axe across his shoulders, listening to that song, and thought: *this is a good place to make a stand, if a man were fool enough to make one.*

Which, apparently, he was.

"You're doing the thing again," Tam said behind him. The scribe sat on the bridge's near rail with his knees drawn up, ink-stained fingers laced around them. "The thing where you stare at the water like it owes you money."

"The water does owe me money." Doss didn't turn. "Thirty years of fares I never collected from fish."

"That's not how—"

"Boy, when you've been dead longer than you were alive, you can tell me how rivers work."

He heard Tam's grin more than saw it. The boy had a way of grinning like the world hadn't gotten to him yet, and Doss had decided somewhere on the road out of the valley that he'd fight anything that tried to change that. Inquisitors. Usurpers. Time itself, if it came down to it.

Behind them, up the eastern bank, the camp was stirring. He could smell Ilsa's fire — she cooked with some herb that made everything smell like it might survive the winter. Wren was awake too; she always woke before the birds, quiet as a debt coming due. And Jonah —

Jonah was already standing at the treeline, looking south, the way he'd been looking south for two days.

That was the thing about Jonah Blackwood. The man didn't sleep so much as keep watch against it. Doss had carried him across the Merewater once, years back, before any of this — a younger swordspeaker, hollow-eyed, asking questions about the valley's ward-line like a man asking after a sick relative. Doss had told him then what he'd tell him now: some things break no matter how well you guard them. All you get to choose is what you do the morning after.

* * *

They'd known since Corvess. Vane's patrols moved like weather — you couldn't stop them, only read the signs. Tam had read the signs: supply wagons rerouted through Hollowmere, a garrison courier riding hard toward the ford, birds going up off the reedbeds an hour before dusk like something had walked through them.

"They'll come at the bridge," Tam had said, spreading his map on the ground. "It's the only crossing for twenty miles. If they take it, they pen us against the river."

"Then we don't let them take it," Jonah said.

"And how does the last swordspeaker propose we hold a stone bridge against forty men?"

"We don't hold it. We cross it, and then we break the far arch."

"Break it with what? Prayers?"

"With me," Doss had said, and everyone had gone quiet in that particular way people go quiet around a man who's already decided.

Because here was the truth of it, the truth Doss had been carrying since the valley fell: he was sixty-one years old, his knees sounded like a door nobody oiled, and the life he'd had — the ferry, the little house above the boathouse, the wife buried under the willow twelve years gone — all of it had burned with the valley or been left behind in it. What remained was a river he knew better than his own hands, and four people he'd somehow, without noticing, started calling his own.

A man could spend sixty-one years learning what a river would bear. Or he could spend one morning learning what he would.

* * *

The plan was simple, because plans are like boats — the complicated ones sink.

Wren, Ilsa, and Tam would cross first with the horses and the packs, upriver at the shallows where the water ran knee-deep. Jonah would stay on the near bank until the last moment, because the binding in him answered to threats and there'd be no threat until the patrol showed its face. And Doss would hold the bridge just long enough for Jonah to bring down the far arch — long enough that the gap couldn't be jumped, couldn't be bridged with planks in under a day.

Long enough. That was the whole job. Long enough.

"You come back across before it falls," Ilsa had said, gripping his sleeve with those small fierce healer's hands. "You hear me, old man? You cross at the shallows. We wait for you."

"I'm not planning on swimming," Doss had told her, which was true. He wasn't planning on much of anything anymore. Planning was for men with futures.

Now, in the dark, he checked the bridge one last time. He knew every stone of it. Knew the third arch had a crack running through its keystone from the flood of his fortieth year. Knew the rail on the north side wobbled where a cart had clipped it. Knew that in fog — and there was fog on the Merewater this morning, thick as wool — you could hear boots on stone a full ten seconds before you saw the men wearing them.

Ten seconds. He smiled to himself. Ten seconds was a fortune, if you spent it right.

"Doss." Jonah came onto the bridge, quiet as ever. They stood together a while, two old men by the measure of the company, watching the fog eat the far bank.

"You don't have to say it," Doss said.

"I know."

"Then don't. I hate speeches. Speeches are for funerals and I'm not attending mine."

Something moved in Jonah's face — almost a smile, worn smooth by grief into something gentler. He put out his hand, and Doss took it, and they shook once, grip to grip, the way working men do it, no ceremony in it at all.

"For the record," Doss said, "I think your prophecy business is nonsense. I think the girl is trouble wrapped in trouble. I think we're all going to die in a war we didn't start."

"For the record," Jonah said, "I know."

"But?"

"But you're here anyway."

Doss looked out at the fog. Somewhere beyond it, the water sang its one song. "Well," he said. "Somebody's got to mind the crossing."

* * *

They heard the patrol before they saw it — the fog carrying sound the way it carried everything, faithfully and slightly distorted. Hooves first, then the creak of leather, then voices, low and disciplined. Not militia. Real soldiers. Vane's own, maybe, or close kin to them.

"Go," Doss said.

Jonah hesitated one breath — Doss felt it, the way you feel a rope go taut — and then he went, dropping off the bridge's end into the mist, and Doss was alone on the stone with his axe and his river.

He walked to the middle of the span and set his feet the way he'd set them on wet decking for thirty years, weight low, knees soft, axe head resting against his shoulder. The fog rolled past him in slow grey banks. Underneath, the water ran loud and white over the stones, singing.

The first soldiers came out of the fog like sketches of themselves — half-formed, then solid, then very solid indeed. A vanguard of six, swords drawn, moving quick because they'd expected no one.

"Hold the bridge!" someone shouted behind them. "Take the crossing alive!"

"Sorry," Doss said pleasantly. "Bridge is closed. Toll's gone up."

They rushed him. Of course they did. Six trained men against one old ferryman, and every one of them thinking about the reward for the woman, none of them thinking about the man in front of them.

But here's what thirty years on the water teaches you that a drill yard never will: footing is everything, and these men had never fought on wet stone over fast water. Doss gave ground the way a boat gives to a wave — not resisting, yielding exactly as much as needed, letting the first man's lunge carry him onto the slick curve of the arch where his boots skated and his balance died. The axe took him in the ribs, short and ugly, and shoved him over the rail into the singing water.

The second and third came together, and Doss stopped being pleasant.

He'd cut timber most of his life. An axe is an axe. You don't fence with it; you fell things. He felled the second man with a blow to the knee that would end a soldier's career even if he lived, and turned the third's blade aside with the haft and drove the butt-spike into his throat. The man folded like laundry.

Four left, and now they were afraid, and afraid men on a narrow bridge in fog are worse than brave ones. They came wide, trying to flank, and Doss backed up the span, giving them the width of the bridge and nothing more, breathing hard, shoulder already screaming where a sword-tip had opened it.

From the far bank, faint through the fog, he heard it begin — Jonah's voice, low and rising, speaking words that made the air feel heavier, the way it did when swordspeech worked on stone instead of men. The far arch groaned.

*Long enough,* Doss thought. *Just be long enough.*

The fifth man was young. Doss saw that even in the fog — young, scared, good boots, bad stance. The boy hesitated, and Doss, who had a son once, dead of fever at nine, found he couldn't take the opening. The sixth man had no such trouble; his sword opened Doss's thigh, deep, and the world went red and cold at the edges.

Doss laughed. It surprised them both.

"Fool," he said, and swung.

The sixth man went off the bridge into the dark water. The young one stood there, shaking, sword half-raised, and Doss leaned on his axe and looked at him across five feet of wet stone.

"Go home," Doss said. "Whatever they're paying you, it isn't enough. Go home."

And the boy — gods bless him — the boy dropped his sword and ran.

Which left the arch. Which left the crack in the keystone that Doss had known about for twenty years, the one the masons never fixed because the tolls never paid for it. Jonah's voice rose to a peak on the far bank, and the far arch shuddered — but the near arch held, and the near arch was the one that mattered, and there was no time, no time at all—

Doss knelt at the keystone of the near arch, pressed his palm flat against the cold stone the way he'd press it against the flank of a horse, and spoke to it. Not swordspeech. Nothing holy. Just a ferryman's words, the ones he'd said over every crossing for thirty-one years, the ones his father taught him and his grandfather before that:

"Bear me true, old friend. One more time."

Then he swung the axe into the crack, once, twice, three times, feeling the stone give beneath each blow like a joint coming loose, and the arch began to sing a different song than the water's — a deep groaning note, the note of something letting go.

Boots on the bridge behind him. More of them, out of the fog, shouting. A spear took him high in the back and he barely felt it, because the arch was falling, the whole beautiful grey span of it folding down into the white water with a roar like the world ending, and Doss rode it down.

The last thing he thought, as the cold closed over him and the current took him the way it takes everything, indifferent and honest at last: *the water doesn't care who you are.* But he cared about them. Four ragged souls on the far bank, waiting for an old man who wasn't coming, and that was the difference. That was the whole difference. The river takes everyone.

He had chosen whom to be taken *for.*

* * *

On the far bank, the company stood in the fog and watched the bridge die.

Ilsa was the first to understand, because healers understand loss fastest — they see it arrive daily, dressed in different clothes. She made a sound small and terrible and turned her face into Tam's shoulder.

Wren stood at the water's edge, soaked to the waist from the shallows, staring at the empty space where the crossing had been. "He was supposed to swim across," she said. "He said—" Her voice cracked clean through. "He said he wasn't planning on swimming."

"He lied," Tam said. His voice was flat and strange. "Old liar. Thirty years of fares he never collected." He sat down abruptly in the mud, still holding his map, as if the bridge might be in it somewhere, as if ink could argue with what they'd all seen.

Jonah said nothing. He stood looking at the broken arches, the stumps of stone, the water running on white and unbothered, and the others watched the swordspeaker's face and understood that whatever words he kept behind it, they were costing him.

At last he knelt at the bank, took a flat stone from the mud, and set it upright among the reeds. No marker. No prayer. No ceremony. Doss would have hated ceremony.

"He minded the crossing," Jonah said quietly. "All the way to the end of it."

The fog was lifting. Somewhere south, horns were sounding — the patrol regrouping, finding another way, taking the long road around. It bought them a day, maybe two. Doss had paid for it with everything he had, and it hadn't even slowed him down at the end, that old man; he'd gone into the water laughing.

Tam wiped his face with an ink-blackened hand, leaving a smear across it like a scar, and stood up. "We should move," he said, and his voice broke on *move*, and he said it again, steadier. "We should move. He bought us a day. We don't waste it."

They gathered the packs. They took the horses. And as they turned north toward the free cities, Wren looked back once at the little standing stone in the reeds, and made a promise to it she didn't say aloud — because some promises, like some crossings, are private between the maker and the water.

Behind them, far downstream, the Merewater ran on, singing its one song to no one.

But ahead — ahead, on the southern horizon, black against the lifting fog, the first smoke of the usurper's army was already rising. And with it, riding hard along the river road, a single rider in inquisitor's grey, watching the broken bridge, watching the four small figures vanish north.

Serah Vane lowered her spyglass slowly, and for the first time in her career, wrote nothing down.
