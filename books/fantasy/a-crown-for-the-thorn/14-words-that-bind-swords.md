## 14. Words That Bind Swords

They buried Doss at dawn, and Jonah spoke no words over the grave, because the only words he had were swordspeech, and you do not spend that on the dead. You spend it on the living, or you don't spend it at all.

Ilsa wept into Tam's shoulder. Wren stood apart, arms crossed against the cold, watching the river take the bridge apart piece by piece downstream — the same river Doss had ferried people across for thirty years, the same water he'd held with an axe and a bad knee while three strangers ran.

Jonah filled the grave himself. Nobody offered to help. Maybe they understood. Maybe they were just tired. He tamped the last of the earth down flat and set a stone on it, one of the flat gray ones from the bank, and thought: *this is what an oath costs.* Not blood. Blood was cheap. It cost this — a man who laughed at his own jokes, gone into the ground because Jonah had taught him to hold a line instead of run it.

He walked away from the grave before he could start hating himself in front of witnesses.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow of birches off the north road, close enough to Hollowmere to hear the water but far enough that the smoke from the burned bridge wouldn't find them. Ilsa slept first, curled against her pack like a child, her hands still smelling of the poultices she'd forced on all of them. Tam sat up late scratching numbers into his ledger — supplies, distances, the arithmetic of staying alive — then slept sitting up, pen still in hand.

Which left Wren, awake on the far side of the fire, and Jonah, who had not been able to sleep since the bridge.

"You're going to wear a groove in the ground," she said.

"I'm going to teach you something."

She looked up. In the firelight her face was still marked where the mill fire had scorched her, faint lines like old ink under new skin. "Now? At midnight?"

"Now. While I'm angry enough to say it right."

That got her attention. She came around the fire and sat cross-legged across from him, and he laid his sword between them on the bedroll, naked steel catching orange light.

"This is swordspeech," he said. "What I used on the inquisitors at the valley. What stopped forty men on a road."

"I remember." Her voice went careful. "It looked like it hurt."

"It did. It does. That's the part nobody writes down." He turned the blade so the flat faced up. "Everyone thinks it's magic. It isn't. Magic can't follow into the Thorn Valley — you know that better than anyone, you fell through a hole in the world to prove it. Swordspeech isn't magic. It's older. It's just truth, spoken so precisely that steel has to obey it."

"Steel obeys words."

"Steel obeys *true* words. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference." He tapped the flat of the blade. "A lie spoken over steel does nothing. A half-truth does nothing. A wish does nothing. But if you say a thing that is completely, exactly true — true all the way down, no corners cut, nothing hidden — the blade hears it. And once a blade has heard the truth, it can't act against it. That's the binding."

Wren stared at the sword. "You're telling me every blade in the valley is listening for honesty."

"I'm telling you every blade everywhere is listening. Most people just never say anything worth hearing."

She almost smiled. It was thin, but it was there. "So how does it work? Practically."

"You pick a truth. One truth. Small, at first. Something you know so completely that there's no doubt left in it anywhere." He took a breath. "Watch."

He put his hand flat on the steel and said: "*This blade has never struck a man who did not first raise a weapon against the innocent.*"

The air changed. Jonah felt it happen the way you feel a door close somewhere else in a house — a pressure shift, a settling. The sword grew heavy in a way that had nothing to do with weight, as if it had become aware of itself. Along the edge, the firelight bent slightly, the way light bends through heat.

Wren had gone very still. "What did you do?"

"I bound it. Now this sword cannot be used to murder. Not by me, not by anyone who picks it up. If someone tries, the arm simply won't finish the motion. The steel remembers what it heard." He let the breath out slowly. His hands were shaking. They always shook after. "That's the smallest binding I know. A single sentence, one blade, one rule."

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's not possible. Words don't work like that."

"They did once. Everywhere. Before the war, before the free cities decided truth was too dangerous to leave lying around, there were schools of us. Hundreds of swordspeakers. We were the law, back when law meant something you could say out loud." He picked the sword up and sheathed it, and felt the small relief of the binding going quiet. "The usurper killed the schools first. Before he marched anywhere, before he took a single city. He understood what I'm teaching you. A man who rules by lies cannot afford a single person alive who can bind steel to truth."

"And now there's you."

"And now there's me." He looked at her across the fire. "And maybe you."

"No." Flat, immediate. "No, Jonah. I'm not— I carry a locket. I don't carry oaths."

"The locket is a truth someone died to keep. Sounds like an oath to me."

Her jaw tightened, and for a moment he thought she'd get up and walk into the dark. Instead she said, quietly: "Teach me the first lesson, then. Just the first."

* * *

The first lesson took until nearly dawn, and it wasn't about speaking at all.

"Say something true," Jonah told her. "About yourself. Anything. Small."

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. "My name is Wren Ashvale."

The sword lay untouched between them. Nothing happened.

"Try again," Jonah said gently. "Your name is true, but it isn't *yours*. It was given to you. Say something you chose. Something you paid for."

She was silent a long time. The fire burned down to coals. Somewhere an owl called and got no answer.

"I ran," she finally said. "From Corvess. From the inquisitors. From—" Her voice caught. "I ran when Master Ellery told me to stay and fight. He pushed me out the back of the archive and locked the door behind me, and I ran, and they killed him for what I was carrying, and I have hated myself every day since, and I would run again, because if I hadn't, the locket would be ash and everything he died for would be ash, and I hate that most of all."

The sword shivered in its sheath. A sound like a low bell, felt more than heard. Wren flinched back as if the fire had spat at her.

"What was that?"

"That was the truth," Jonah said. His throat was tight. "All the way down. No corners cut. You didn't make yourself noble. You didn't make yourself a victim. You said the whole ugly thing, exactly as it is." He swallowed. "That's the first lesson. That's the entire first lesson. Everything after that is just learning to aim it."

Wren was staring at the sheathed sword like it might bite her. "It *heard* me."

"It always hears you. Everyone's does. Yours just finally said something worth hearing." He fed a stick to the coals. "Most people go their whole lives without saying one complete truth out loud. Think about that. All those armies marching around, all those kings making decrees, and not one complete sentence among them."

"Why does it cost so much?" she asked. "When you speak it. Why do your hands shake?"

"Because you can't bind a blade to a truth you haven't survived. The words come out of the same place the wound lives. Every time." He flexed his fingers, watching them tremble in the firelight. "My teacher called it paying the toll. You want steel to obey truth? Then the truth has to be true of *you*, first. You have to stand inside it while you say it."

"And the oath you broke. At the valley. When you used it on living men."

Jonah went quiet. The owl called again, closer now.

"The order had three oaths," he said. "First: never speak the binding against a living soul except to save a life. Second: never reveal what the archives keep. Third: never leave the valley." He laughed, short and joyless. "I've broken two. I'm working on the third."

"Jonah—"

"The first oath wasn't cowardice, whatever it sounds like. It was mercy. For us." He met her eyes. "Every swordspeaker who ever used the speech on a living man carried it afterward like a stone. My teacher carried four such bindings. I watched what it did to him. It's why I stayed in the valley my whole life, Wren. Not duty. Fear. I was afraid of what I'd have to say if I ever came out."

"But you came out."

"You fell through a hole in the sky and tore a hundred-year ward open, and a book in my cellar had already written down the date." He shook his head. "After that, staying felt less like duty and more like hiding."

* * *

The fire was embers now. Ilsa murmured in her sleep. Tam had slid sideways against his pack, ledger open on his chest. The birches stood pale and patient around them, and east, past the black line of hills, the sky had begun the slow gray work of becoming morning.

"Wren," Jonah said. "There's something I have to tell you. And I should have told you in Corvess, and I didn't, and I'm going to tell you now before I lose my nerve again."

She went still. He recognized the stillness — the hunted-animal stillness she'd had the first day, at sword-point at the valley's edge. He hated that he'd put it back in her.

"There's a second page," he said. "To the prophecy. The archivist in Corvess showed me. The page everyone knows says a woman comes through fire carrying a secret that could reignite the old war. The second page says what happens to her." He made himself keep talking, evenly, the way you walk on a broken leg. "It says she must claim the ancient crown — the crown of the old magical houses — or be destroyed by it. Those are the only two endings written. Claim it, or be unmade by it."

The silence stretched so long he could hear the coals ticking.

"You knew," Wren said at last. Her voice was very level. "In Corvess. You knew, and you let me walk around thinking I was just being chased."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because if you'd known, you'd have done something reckless. Given yourself up. Thrown the locket in a river. Anything to escape a fate with only two doors." He spread his hands. "I told myself I was protecting you. That was half true. The other half is that I couldn't stand the look I knew would come onto your face right now."

"And what look is that?"

"This one."

She looked away. For a moment he thought she would stand, take the locket, take Ilsa, take the road south, and vanish into the morning the way she'd appeared out of the fire. He deserved it. He'd known he deserved it since Corvess, and he'd lied anyway, because fear makes liars of careful men.

But she didn't stand. She reached up instead, and touched the locket at her throat, and when she spoke her voice had gone quiet and hard as whetted steel.

"My mother wore this. She died wearing it. Master Ellery died for it. An inquisitor burned a mill to smoke me out of it. And now a book tells me I get to choose between a crown and a pyre." She looked back at him, eyes bright and furious in the ember-light. "Do you know what I want, Jonah? Do you know what I actually want? I want to give this thing to someone else. Anyone. I want to wake up tomorrow and be a woman with no destiny, no prophecy, no locket. I want to be Ilsa, with her herbs. I want to be Tam, with his lists. I want the smallest possible life."

"I know."

"Do you?" She laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "You chose your silence. You built a whole life inside it. I never got to choose anything. This was handed to me in a burning archive by a dying man, and every step since, the world has been deciding for me."

"Yes," Jonah said. "I know that too."

And then, because the first lesson was still open between them, because the sword was still listening, because dawn was coming and he was tired of carrying things alone — he put his hand on the sheathed blade and said:

"*I hid the second page because I am afraid of losing you, and I have lost everything else, and I could not bear to watch you walk into either ending that book wrote for you.*"

The bell-sound rang through the camp, soft and enormous. Ilsa stirred. Tam's head came up, blinking. The sword under Jonah's palm grew heavy with hearing, and the words hung in the air like frost, undeniable, irreversible — a truth spoken all the way down, and now a law in steel, which meant he could never unsay it, never soften it, never pretend otherwise again.

Wren stared at him. The fury in her face had broken open into something rawer.

"You bound yourself to that," she whispered. "You made it permanent."

"That's the point of the lesson." His voice was rough. "You wanted to know why it costs. There's your answer. I can't take it back. Neither can you. Whatever happens next — the crown, the pyre, the road — you'll always know exactly why I lied to you, and it will always be true, and I'll always have said it out loud in front of witnesses." He nodded at the sleeping forms by the fire. "That's what trust is, I think. Not the absence of lies. Witnesses."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached across the dead fire, took his shaking hand, and pressed it flat against the locket at her throat.

"Then here's mine," she said. "Witnessed."

And she began to speak — the whole of it, the stolen bloodline, the murdered mentor, the mother, the war waiting underneath everything like water under ice — and the sword listened, and the sky went on turning gray toward morning, and somewhere far to the south, in a garden full of captured things, a man who ruled by lies was waking to the news that a swordspeaker had come down out of the mountains and was teaching people to tell the truth.

By noon, they would need to move. By evening, Vane's riders would find their cold ashes.

But that was noon. This was dawn, and for the first time since the valley fell, Jonah Blackwood was not carrying his truths alone.
