## 16. A Rescue Outside the Law

Ilsa had never broken a law in her life until three days ago, and now she was about to break a very large one.

She stood in the shadow of the garden prison's outer wall, her healer's satchel heavy on her hip, and tried to remember when exactly her life had come to this. The answer, she supposed, was the night the valley burned. Everything before that night belonged to a different woman — a woman who fetched bandages and boiled water and kept her head down. That woman had died somewhere on the road between the Thorn Valley and Corvess, and the one who replaced her carried forged papers, stolen livery, and a plan that would get them all hanged.

"You're doing the thing," Tam whispered beside her. "The face. The *I've-made-a-terrible-mistake* face."

"I'm doing the *we're-all-going-to-die* face."

"Same face. Different words." He adjusted the courier's cap that sat crooked on his head and patted the leather tube of documents at his belt like a talisman. "Relax. I've forged writs from six different magistrates this month. Nobody checks a plague quarantine seal. It's the one piece of paper everyone runs *away* from."

"That's reassuring," said Serah Vane.

Ilsa still hadn't decided how she felt about the inquisitor. Vane stood apart from them in the dark, out of her grey-and-crimson uniform, dressed instead in the plain leathers of a border warden — clothes Ilsa had helped dye herself, scrubbing the crimson out with lye water until her knuckles cracked. Without the uniform, Vane looked smaller. Older. Like a blade someone had finally taken off the wall.

"The tunnels are real," Vane said, as if answering a question no one had asked. "I inspected this facility two years ago. They were sealed after the grey fever, but sealing isn't demolition. The masons were paid by the day."

"And you remember which ones they didn't finish," Ilsa said.

"I remember everything." There was no pride in it. Just the flat exhaustion of a woman who had spent her life being useful to monsters and finally done the arithmetic. "That's the problem with me."

Tam snorted. "Welcome to the company. We're all problems here."

* * *

They went over the wall an hour before dawn, because Vane said the changing of the watch happened then and because Ilsa's hands stopped shaking if she kept moving.

The garden prison had earned its name honestly — it had been a lord's pleasure garden once, all terraced hedges and marble fountains, before the usurper converted the greenhouses into cells and the fountains into cisterns for the guards. Now the roses grew wild through the bars of the windows, and the prisoners inside could smell spring without ever touching it. Wren was in the east wing. Vane was certain. Third floor, fourth cell, the one with the view of the orchard — because the usurper wanted his prize prisoner comfortable enough to stay alive until the trial.

*Trial.* Ilsa spat quietly into the dark. She'd seen what passed for trials in the usurper's garden. Verdicts written before the accused arrived.

The tunnel mouth was where Vane remembered: behind the collapsed gardener's shed, under forty years of ivy, a door of rusted iron that groaned like a dying animal when Tam pried it open. Beyond it, stairs down into blackness that smelled of wet stone and old sickness.

"Lanterns low," Vane murmured. "There will be rats. Possibly worse."

"Worse than rats?" Tam asked.

"Grey fever killed four hundred people in these tunnels," Vane said. "Some of them were never carried out."

Ilsa lit her lantern anyway, cupping the flame small. "Then some of them have been waiting a long time for company," she said, and started down the stairs before her courage could reconsider.

* * *

The tunnels were worse than she'd imagined and better than she'd feared.

Worse, because the dark pressed close and the walls wept moisture and twice her lantern light found things she made herself not look at — shapes in alcoves, arranged with a care that meant someone had loved them once. Better, because Vane's memory held true. At every junction the inquisitor paused, touched the wall, and chose without hesitation: left, right, left again, following a map drawn in her own past.

"How many prisons have you inspected?" Tam asked quietly, somewhere around the third turning.

"All of them." Vane's voice echoed strange in the narrow dark. "It was my first posting. Finding the flaws. Where the walls were thin, where the guards drank, where the tunnels ran. I was good at it." A pause. "I told myself it was mercy work. That a well-run prison hurt people less than a badly-run one."

"And now?" Ilsa asked.

"Now I know the difference between running a cage well and breaking it open." Vane stepped over a fallen beam. "One of those is a job. The other is a debt."

They climbed a service ladder at the tunnel's end, up through a shaft that smelled of coal and lamp oil, and emerged through a hatch into a storeroom stacked with flour sacks and pickled vegetables. Above them, muffled by floors of stone, the prison breathed: boots on stairs, keys in locks, a guard coughing.

Vane checked a small clock she'd taken from her own confiscated office. "Quarter hour to the watch change. The east wing corridor will be empty for six minutes. No more." She looked at each of them in turn, and Ilsa saw something in her face she'd never seen there before — not command, not contempt. *Appeal.* "Once we take her, there is no going back for me. You understand that. Whatever happens tonight, I burn my old life completely."

"We know," Ilsa said.

"Good." Vane drew a knife — her only weapon; she'd surrendered her sword to Jonah weeks ago, and he'd thrown it in a river. "Then let's go collect our girl."

* * *

The six minutes went like this:

Minute one: Tam walked into the east wing checkpoint wearing his courier's cap and carrying a quarantine writ so official-looking that Ilsa's stomach clenched just watching him hold it. He announced, in a bored voice pitched perfectly between authority and tedium, that the eastern wing was to be sealed for fumigation by order of the Magistrate of Health, effective immediately, and that all prisoners in the affected block were to be transferred to the holding cells below for their protection. The guards — two of them, young, tired — squinted at the seal. One of them squinted at Tam. Tam squinted back, harder, and produced a second document, a complaint form, and asked the guard for his name and rank for the record of his obstruction.

Minute two: the guards decided they did not want their names on anything.

Minute three: while Tam herded the paperwork crisis, Vane took the stairwell in guard's stride — she knew the walk, Ilsa realized; she'd worn it for twenty years — and reached the fourth cell. The lock was nothing to her. She'd designed half the locks in the usurper's system.

Minute four: Ilsa stood at the wing's entrance with her satchel open and her heart hammering, ready to play her part — the plague nurse, come to examine the infected. She had charcoal dust on her face to suggest fever-shadow and a list of symptoms memorized in case anyone asked. Nobody asked. The sight of a medical satchel made people step *back*, always. Fear opened more doors than any writ.

Minute five: Wren Ashvale came down the corridor between Vane and the wall, limping, wrists raw, eyes blazing — alive, alive, *alive* — and Ilsa's whole body wanted to run to her and her training forced her to walk, calm, professional, a nurse escorting a patient.

Minute six: the bell for the watch change rang, and every guard in the garden turned toward the sound, and four fugitives slipped out of the east wing like smoke through a keyhole.

* * *

Wren cried in the tunnel. Not loudly. Ilsa heard it anyway — the small, ragged sounds of someone who had been holding herself together by main strength for too long — and she put her arm around the other woman's shoulders and let her lean.

"They were going to make me do it," Wren said, when she could speak. "In front of everyone. They built a stage, Ilsa. In the courtyard. With a throne on it." Her laugh came out broken. "He wants me to *claim* it. So he can kill me wearing it."

"He won't get the chance," Ilsa said, and was surprised by the steel in her own voice. "Jonah has a plan. Tam has documents. Doss—" she stopped. Swallowed. "Doss would have had opinions."

"Doss," Wren said softly. "Gods. Is it true? The bridge?"

"It's true."

Wren was quiet a long moment, walking in the dark with her hand on Ilsa's shoulder. Then: "Then we owe him a world worth the price."

"That's the idea," said Tam from behind them, voice thick. "Now move faster, both of you. Debt-paying is best done vertically."

* * *

They almost made it.

They were fifty feet from the tunnel door — fifty feet from the ivy and the dark and freedom — when the lantern found them. A single guard, older than the others, coming off shift early, rounding the corner of the greenhouse with a pipe in his teeth and thirty years of boredom in his eyes.

He looked at them. They looked at him. Ilsa watched him take it in: the prison dress on Wren, the knife in Vane's hand, the satchel, the courier's tube, the four of them frozen in the lamplight like actors who'd forgotten their lines.

And then the guard looked at Vane, and his face changed.

"Inquisitor Vane," he said slowly. "They said you'd gone traitor. Ran off with the swordspeaker's rabble." He sucked on his pipe. His hand did not move toward his whistle. "My daughter was in Corvess when they fired the granary districts. Last autumn. For crowd control." He took the pipe from his mouth. "Nobody was ever charged."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, the prison bells began to ring the alarm — late, always late, the machine grinding awake — and the guard heard it and made his choice in the space of one breath.

"Tunnel's behind the shed," he said. "Ivy's thick on the north side. Take the left fork when you're under the wall — right fork floods." He turned back toward the greenhouse, then paused. "Inquisitor. When you write your chronicle — and someone will — put my name in it. Aldous Merrin. Guard, third watch. I was here, and I saw nothing."

Then he walked away, unhurried, a man finishing a shift.

Ilsa stood with her heart slamming and understood, suddenly, what Jonah had been trying to teach them all along. It wasn't the swordspeech. It wasn't the magic or the oaths or the binding of blades. It was this: that ordinary people, given one clear moment, would choose. Most of them had just never been offered the moment before. The usurper's whole empire was built on making sure no one ever got asked.

Tonight, a tired old guard got asked. And answered.

* * *

They came up out of the tunnel into the grey hour before sunrise, behind the collapsed shed, under the ivy, and Vane pulled the rusted door shut behind them and broke the lock with the flat of her knife so it could never be used against them — or anyone else.

For a moment none of them moved. Four people standing in a dead garden, breathing, alive, free.

"Well," said Tam. "I believe that's the most illegal morning of my life."

"First of many," Wren said. She was looking east, where the sky was going pale over the hills, toward the Thorn Valley, toward Jonah, toward whatever came next. There was ash still in her hair and chains had marked her wrists, but her eyes had that look Ilsa had learned to recognize on the road — the look of someone who had seen the bottom of the thing and climbed back out with her fists full of truth. "Where is he?"

"The valley's edge," Ilsa said. "Where the ward-line used to stand. He's been gathering people. Witnesses. Hundreds of them already — farmers, clerks, soldiers who walked away. He says—" she hesitated, because saying it aloud made it real, made it enormous, "—he says he's going to speak. The last speech. Bind every blade on the field to the truth."

Wren closed her eyes. "He'll tear himself apart doing it."

"Probably," said Vane.

"Then we'd better hurry," Wren said, and started walking.

They fell in behind her — the healer, the forger, the defector, the rescued — four ordinary criminals in the grey light, moving fast toward the valley where the last oath-bound swordspeaker stood alone against an army.

Behind them, the garden prison's bells rang on and on, calling a hunt that had already failed.

Ahead, the sun was coming up on the field of spoken steel.
