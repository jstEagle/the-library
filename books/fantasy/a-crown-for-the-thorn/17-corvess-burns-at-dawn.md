## 17. Corvess Burns at Dawn

Tam had forged a hundred documents in his short, dishonest life — death certificates, guild licenses, a marriage contract for two lovers who couldn't afford the dowry — and he had never once thought of them as weapons. Paper was his shield. Paper kept him invisible. You didn't fight with paper; you hid behind it.

Then Corvess burned, and he learned what a page could do.

It started with the smell. He woke in the loft above Ilsa's cousin's dyehouse — the company had scattered across the Low Quarter after the bridge at Hollowmere, sleeping in shifts, moving every third night — and for a moment he thought someone was boiling dye wrong. Then the light through the shutters went orange.

He was down the ladder and out the door before his boots were fully on.

The harbor district was burning. Not one fire, not an accident: the whole waterfront, from the salt-warehouses to the customs house, lit like a festival gone wrong. And above it, riding the wind off the sea, came the sound Tam would hear in dreams for the rest of his life — the sound of a city waking up to its own death. Bells. Screaming. The low animal roar of ten thousand people all trying to run through the same streets at once.

"The usurper," Ilsa said. She'd come out barefoot, her healer's satchel already strapped across her chest, because of course she slept with the satchel. "He fired Corvess anyway."

"But Wren got away." Tam's voice cracked on it. "We got her out. There was no reason—"

"There was never a reason," Ilsa said. "There's only him." She grabbed his sleeve. "The tunnels. The plague-tunnels under the Temple Ward — they'll be choked with people by now. Someone has to organize them or they'll crush each other."

They ran.

* * *

The mob in the Temple Ward was everything Vane had feared and worse. The tunnel mouths — three of them, black arches behind the old leper chapel — were jammed with bodies, and the crowd behind was still coming, driven by the flames leaping roof to roof along Tanner's Row. People were being pressed against the walls. A woman had fainted and the crowd simply flowed around her like water around a stone.

Jonah Blackwood stood on the chapel steps with his sword drawn, and he looked — Tam would remember this exactly — like a man about to do something that frightened him more than any army ever had.

"Tam," Jonah said when they reached him. His voice was steady but his knuckles were white on the hilt. "How many writs can you write in five minutes?"

"Depends. Forged?"

"True ones."

"That's harder." But Tam was already digging in his coat. "What kind of true?"

"Swordspeech binds blades," Jonah said. "Steel to truth. It's why no soldier can lift a weapon against a witness once I've spoken over the field." He watched the crowd surge, and Tam saw him swallow. "But panic isn't steel. Panic doesn't have an edge I can bind. Unless—" He turned. "Unless the panic is holding something. Torches. Clubs. Kitchen knives. Everyone in this crowd is armed with fear, and fear is carried in the hands."

"You want me to write you a field," Tam said slowly. "Words so precise they become law. Like your oaths."

"I can't speak law into a thousand minds at once. But paper carries words too." Jonah's eyes were bright and terrible. "Write me an evacuation order. Signed by the city council, sealed with the city seal, ordering every soul in Corvess to move to the harbor stairs in columns, women and children first, no one runs, no one tramples, everyone carries someone weaker than themselves. Make it official. Make it *binding-looking*. Then I'll speak it true."

"It won't be true. I'm forging the council's signature."

"No." Jonah gripped his shoulder. "You're not forging anything tonight. Write the order as yourself. Tam of the Thorn Valley. Witness. Your name, your hand, your word. That's what swordspeech needs — not authority. Truth. Say what's happening and say what must be done, and mean both, and I'll make the words stick in the air like nails."

Tam stared at him. "I'm a runaway scribe. Nobody knows my name."

"They will," Jonah said, "by dawn."

* * *

He wrote on the chapel door, flat against the oak, because there was no time for anything else. Ink, brush, the back of his own hand as a blotter. Around him the crowd screamed and the sky rained embers, and Tam wrote faster than he had ever written in his life:

*By witness and by word: the city burns by a tyrant's act, not heaven's. All souls shall walk, not run, to the harbor stairs. The strong carry the weak. No blade raised in panic shall cut a neighbor. This order is spoken true and bound until the last person stands safe.*

His handwriting was atrocious. It didn't matter.

Jonah laid his bare palm flat on the page, drew his sword, and set the flat of the blade against the ink.

"*Spoken,*" he said, and his voice did the thing it always did — the thing that made the hair on Tam's arms stand up, the thing that made the night itself seem to lean in and listen. "*And witnessed. And bound.*"

The words left the paper.

Not metaphorically. Tam saw them go — a shiver of light running through the ink like current through water, then up off the page and out over the crowd, spreading through the square the way heat spreads through a room. And the crowd *changed*. Not instantly, not completely — panic doesn't die easy — but the front rank stopped pressing. A big tanner with a meat hook in his fist looked down at it as if he'd forgotten what it was for, and dropped it, and turned, and picked up the fainting woman instead.

"Columns!" Jonah bellowed, walking the steps like a general. "Walk! Walk, damn you — running kills more than fire! Strong backs to the edges — you, ferryman's build, take the children first!"

And the city obeyed. Not because Jonah commanded it — swordspeech couldn't compel, only clarify, that was the whole theology of the dead order — but because for the first time since the bells began, someone had told the crowd the *truth* in words solid enough to hold onto. Fear needs a shape. Jonah gave it one.

Tam stood in the doorway of the chapel with ink to his elbows and watched his own terrible handwriting save four hundred lives before sunrise.

Then Wren Ashvale shouldered through the crowd, soot-streaked, sword out, eyes wild, and ruined the moment beautifully.

"TAM." She seized his collar. "The customs house. The usurper's men are burning the charter archive — every record, every deed, every citizenship roll. If the rolls burn, half the city legally stops existing, and he gets to decide who's real." She shoved a folded paper at him — a guard's patrol schedule, stolen off a body. "Vane's defectors are holding the east door. They need documents. Now."

"What kind of documents?"

Wren grinned, and there was ash on her teeth, and Tam thought she looked like the prophecy come alive and then some. "New ones. Write us a city, Tam."

* * *

They worked through the fire hours in the customs house cellar while the building groaned overhead like a ship in a storm. Tam wrote. Serah Vane — defected, scorched, terrifyingly calm — dictated the legal forms from memory, because of course an inquisitor knew the usurper's bureaucratic machinery better than its own clerks. Ilsa triaged the wounded brought down the tunnel stairs. Doss was dead, and no one said his name, but everyone worked like he was watching.

Deeds reissued. Citizenship rolls recopied from memory and from refugees' own testimony — *"state your name and your years in Corvess, and it's true again, say it loud enough for the clerk"* — which meant, though nobody planned it quite this way, that hundreds of ordinary people spoke their own lives aloud into the record, witnessed, binding, true. Swordspeech by committee. The dead order would have wept.

By the time the eastern sky went grey, the fires were dying in the wet morning wind, and the customs house still stood, and in its cellar lay a stack of papers that was no longer a forgery of anything. It was just — documents. Real ones. Made by the only hands that had shown up.

Wren found Tam on the loading dock at dawn, sitting amid the wreckage of his ink supplies, staring at the smoke.

"They'll call it illegal," Tam said. "Everything we wrote. Every charter, every roll. Outlaw paper."

"Probably," Wren said. She sat down next to him, close enough that their shoulders touched. "But here's the thing about law, Tam. It's not what the tyrant says it is. It's what people agree to honor. And this morning, four hundred people walked out of a fire because a runaway scribe told them the truth on a piece of paper." She nudged his shoulder. "Let them call it outlaw paper. It held."

Below them, the city was stirring — dazed, smoking, alive. And moving toward the customs house. First dozens, then hundreds: people carrying salvaged records, ledgers, family papers, setting them down in the courtyard in neat, stubborn piles. Rebuilding the archive by hand, from memory, from grief.

A gray-bearded harbormaster climbed the dock steps, bowed stiffly to Wren, and unrolled a fresh sheet of parchment on the planks between them.

"Begging pardon," he said. "But the council's dead — fled or burned. And the word in the streets is that there's folk here who write things that *hold*." He dipped a quill and offered it, handle-first, to Tam. "Corvess needs a new charter. We'd take it kindly if the outlaws would draft one."

Tam took the quill. His hand shook. Then it didn't.

He bent over the parchment — and froze, because the first line wanted writing and every line after it would depend on it, and somewhere in his coat pocket, crumpled and singed, was the scrap of prophecy he'd copied weeks ago in the blind archivist's archive. The censored second page. The line he'd never shown anyone, because showing it felt like aiming an arrow at Wren's heart.

*When the crown is refused, the fire will ask for a reader.*

He'd thought it meant destruction. Standing here, quill dripping, with a whole city waiting for his first honest sentence, he understood it differently. The fire had asked. The city was asking. And the answer wasn't a crown at all.

It was a charter.

Tam wrote: *We, the witnesses of Corvess, who owe our lives to no throne...*

And from the courtyard below, a shout went up — a scout, sprinting in from the west road, waving his arms, screaming the word that stopped every quill in the yard:

*"The usurper's army — on the valley road — marching on the Thorn Valley!"*
