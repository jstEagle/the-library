## 22. The Company That Stayed

The bell over the schoolhouse door had been cast from a melted inquisitor's helm, and it rang like a promise kept.

Jonah Blackwood stood at the front of the long room with his hands behind his back and twenty-three children watching him from the benches — valley-born and refugee-born and one girl from Corvess who still flinched at loud noises but was learning not to. Morning light came through windows that Tam had insisted be made of real glass, because *a chronicle of honest things deserves honest light*, and the dust in the air turned gold as it drifted.

"Say it again," Jonah said.

The children said it together, raggedly, the way all first oaths are spoken:

*"Tell the truth even when it costs."*

"And when does it cost?"

A boy in the second row — Ferren, the ferryman's grandson, Doss's blood in his stubborn chin — raised his hand without waiting to be called on, because he was eight and patience hadn't found him yet. "Always," he said. "You said always."

"I did say always." Jonah let the silence sit for a moment, because silence was part of the lesson and always had been. "But here's what your grandparents' books won't tell you, and here's what I spent thirty years learning too late: the cost is never what you think it'll be. You imagine the cost is pain, or punishment, or losing something you wanted." He looked out at their faces, small and serious and sun-browned from a summer of helping rebuild the eastern terraces. "It's not. The cost is that once you've told one true thing, you can't ever fully go back to being someone who didn't. Truth is a door. It only opens one way."

The girl from Corvess, Maret, put her hand up. She was careful with her words the way some children are careful with broken glass. "Did it cost you?"

Jonah thought about the ward-line tearing open in the dark a year and a lifetime ago. He thought about a woman falling through fire into his valley with a secret burning a hole through her chest. He thought about Doss on the bridge at Hollowmere, axe high, laughing, gone.

"Yes," he said. "Everything. And I'd pay it again before you finished asking."

Maret nodded slowly, filing that away wherever serious children keep serious things. "Then why teach us to do it?"

And there it was. The question he'd been building toward since the first morning, the question he'd asked himself a thousand times on cold watches along the ward-line back when the valley was quiet and he was alone and the oaths were just words he inherited from dead men. He crouched down so he wasn't looming over them. Swordspeakers had a saying: *never speak law from above the listener's head.* It applied to more than swordspeech.

"Because a lie keeps you safe," he said, "and the truth keeps everyone else safe. And somewhere in your life — maybe soon, maybe years from now — you're going to have to choose which one you are. I want you to already know, when that moment comes. I don't want you standing there deciding. Deciding takes too long. The world moves fast when it's breaking." He straightened up. "So we practice. Every day. Small truths, cheap truths, until telling the truth is just what your mouth does without asking your fear's permission. Then when the expensive ones come — and they will come — you'll speak them like breathing."

Ferren's hand shot up again. "What if nobody believes you?"

"Then you're in good company," Jonah said. "Nobody believed me either. For most of a year, the only people who believed anything I said were a runaway scribe, a healer's apprentice, and an inquisitor who'd decided to stop believing her own orders." He smiled despite himself. "Belief isn't the point. The point is that the truth is now *out of your chest and in the world*, where it can do its work even while people argue about whether it's true. Lies need you alive and silent forever. Truth survives on its own. We buried my friend Doss under a bridge, and the story of what he did has crossed three borders since, and every time it's told, it doesn't need him to stand up and confirm it. It's true. That's enough."

He rang the small handbell on his desk — dismissal, not the door-bell; the door-bell only rang for arrivals — and the room dissolved into the beautiful chaos of twenty-three children who had been made to sit still for an entire hour. They poured past him into the yard, and he caught fragments: Ferren daring someone to race to the well, Maret explaining gravity to a smaller boy with the grave authority of someone who'd learned it yesterday, somebody's dog getting underfoot and loving it.

Jonah stood in the doorway a moment longer.

*No longer the last of anything.*

That was the sentence he kept returning to, on good days and strange days alike. For thirty years he had been the final entry in a dying ledger — the last oath-bound swordspeaker, keeper of a warded valley no one visited, inheritor of rituals performed alone at night with no witness but the thorn hedges and the moon. He had told himself it was duty. Some nights, in the honest dark, he'd admitted it was grief wearing duty's coat.

Now look at the place. The old archive hall — the one where he'd found the prophecy book with tonight's date written in century-old ink — had its shelves rebuilt and doubled, and Tam ran it with the tyrannical precision of a man who had once forged documents for a living and intended to spend the rest of his life making sure no one ever needed forgery again. The training yard behind the mill ruins — Wren's idea, though she'd deny authorship — held racks of wooden practice blades and a chalkboard where Jonah wrote out the forms of swordspeech each week, erasing them each Friday, because *the words belong in mouths, not on walls.*

And the ward-line — the great secret, the sacred burden, the thing he had renewed alone every year of his adult life — was now renewed by twelve hands working together, because Wren had argued, in front of everyone, that a secret kept by one person is a cage, and a secret shared by a community is a foundation. Jonah had resisted for exactly as long as it took him to realize she was right, which was approximately four seconds, though he'd made it look like three days out of professional dignity.

He walked out into the yard.

* * *

Ilsa had claimed the old healer's cottage and expanded it twice. The garden where she grew her medicines now sprawled where the goat pen used to be, and the goats had been relocated with much complaint to the hillside, where they were reportedly organizing a rebellion. She came out to meet him with her sleeves rolled and her hands stained green, and she was taller than Jonah remembered her being a year ago — not actually, but in the way that people become larger when they stop apologizing for taking up space.

"The Corvess delegation arrives tomorrow," she said, falling into step beside him. "Tam's been polishing the guest beds like they're going to inspect them for fingerprints."

"They will inspect everything. Councilmen inspect. It's how they breathe."

"Well, warn him, then. He hasn't slept properly in two days. He keeps rewriting the welcome address." She glanced sideways at him. "You could talk to him. He listens to you."

"He listens to Wren. He merely pretends to listen to me."

"Wren's at the border," Ilsa said, and something passed across her face — the quick shadow that still crossed all their faces, sometimes, when the border was mentioned. Not fear anymore. Just the memory of what borders were for, before Vane made hers mean something different.

"How is she?" Jonah asked. He asked every time, and Ilsa answered honestly every time, because lying to a swordspeaker in front of a swordspeaker's own student was a sport with no winners.

"Tired. Steady. She wrote to Tam last week — she writes to all of us, separately, did you know that? Same letter, mostly, but she adjusts it. She tells Tam the political news, she tells me who's injured, and she tells you—" Ilsa paused, enjoying herself. "Well. She tells you things she doesn't tell anyone else."

"She does not."

"She absolutely does. Last month she told you the exact number of steps from the guardhouse to the river ford."

"That was tactical information."

"It was forty-seven steps, Jonah. Nobody needs to know that except someone counting the distance between themselves and a conversation they intend to have." Ilsa grinned and peeled away toward a patient waiting on her porch — a farmer's son with a badly set arm — throwing back over her shoulder: "Delegation at midday tomorrow. Wear the good coat. And tell Tam about the beds!"

He watched her go, this girl who had arrived at his campfire a trembling apprentice with blood on her sleeves and a dead teacher's name she couldn't say aloud, and who now ran the closest thing the free territories had to a hospital, teaching anyone who'd listen that healing was not charity, it was justice practiced with clean hands.

* * *

The archive smelled of ink and cedar, and Tam was exactly where Ilsa had predicted: bent over a desk covered in fair copies, muttering.

"The beds," Jonah said.

Tam didn't look up. "I know about the beds. I'm aware of the beds. The beds are fine. The beds are excellent beds. What's not fine is that the Corvess council is sending Chancellor Merrick, and Merrick personally signed the edict that outlawed swordspeech in the city limits eleven months ago, and now he wants to come here and *learn from us*, and I am expected to compose a welcome speech that doesn't open with 'you absolute hypocrite.'"

"You could just say that."

"I could *not* just say that, because unlike certain people in this valley, I understand diplomacy." Tam finally looked up. His face had changed over the year — harder at the edges, softer at the eyes, the way faces do when someone stops performing a version of themselves. "Also, I finished it. The chronicle. Volume one."

Jonah went very still. "You finished it."

"The war year. Hollowmere to the field of spoken steel. All of it." Tam pushed back from the desk and looked, for the first time since Jonah had known him, genuinely afraid. "Including the parts you didn't want included."

"Which parts?"

"The part where I write down that you lied to Wren about the second page of the prophecy." Tam's chin came up, scribe-firm. "You told me to record everything truly or record nothing. Those were your terms. Your exact words, actually — I have them written down, because I write everything down, it's rather my whole—"

"I remember my own terms, Tam."

"Right. Well. So it's in there. Chapter fourteen." He swallowed. "Are you angry?"

Jonah picked up the manuscript — heavy, dense, six hundred pages of a year that had unmade and remade him — and turned to chapter fourteen. There it was, in Tam's meticulous hand: *Blackwood withheld the second page from Ashvale for nine days, fearing for her safety and, if he is honest, fearing her choice. Love that lies is still a kind of cage. He learned this late and paid for the lesson in trust, which is the most expensive currency there is.*

"No," Jonah said quietly. "This is why we built the school, Tam. This page right here. Someday a child reads this and understands that the man in the stories failed at exactly the thing the stories say he was best at. That's worth more than ten flattering chapters."

Tam exhaled. "Wren's read it already. She sent word through Vane's courier." He hesitated. "She said to tell you she cried at chapter fourteen. And then she said—" he squinted at a slip of paper, though he clearly had it memorized, "'Tell Jonah the cage had good intentions, and I broke out anyway, and next time just tell me. Forty-seven steps, Jonah. You know what the forty-seven steps mean.'"

Jonah laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and Tam looked deeply satisfied with himself.

"Go polish the beds," Jonah said.

* * *

Evening found him walking the western path toward the border station, because some habits deserved to become traditions. The thorn hedges had grown wild and glorious since the valley stopped being a prison and started being a home — magic still could not follow past the old lines, but everything else could: traders, teachers, runaways, letters, music. The road through the valley, once empty, now carried cart tracks deep enough to hold rainwater.

Serah Vane met him halfway, as she did most evenings, walking her border circuit with the unhurried stride of a woman who had traded hunting people for guarding them and had never once expressed regret, because expressing regret was inefficient and Vane did not do inefficient.

"Chancellor Merrick," she said, by way of greeting.

"What about him?"

"When he asks to see our defenses — and he will ask, councilmen always ask — I'm going to show him twelve farmers with pitchforks and a school full of children, and I'm going to enjoy his face enormously."

"They're not just farmers with pitchforks."

"No," Vane conceded. "They're farmers with pitchforks who can bind steel with a spoken truth. But I maintain the visual." She fell into step beside him. For a while they walked in the companionable silence of people who had once tried to kill each other and had since moved on to more productive arrangements. "Border report," she said finally. "Nothing from the east. The usurper's remnant lords are still squabbling over scraps. Two of them sent envoys asking whether the valley would recognize their claims."

"And you told them—"

"I told them the valley recognizes no crowns. Which is now official policy, thanks to your colleague planting one in the dirt." Vane's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile, the closest her face came to open weather. "She's well, by the way. The border post suits her better than either of us guessed. She's got the northern villages organized into a watch rotation, she's settled three feuds, and last month she talked a bandit captain into surrendering his entire company by asking him questions he couldn't answer honestly in front of his own men."

"Sounds familiar."

"It should. She learned from the worst possible teacher." Vane stopped at the boundary stone, where her circuit ended and his evening began. "One more thing. She asked me to give you this." From her coat she produced a folded paper, sealed with plain wax. "She said — and I quote, because I quote everything, it's a professional habit — 'Give this to Jonah at dusk, not before. He gets sentimental in daylight.'"

Vane left him there with it, boots crunching down the border path.

Jonah broke the seal and read by the last of the light.

*Jonah —*

*Tam says the chronicle is done and I know what's in chapter fourteen because I read it, and I'm not writing this letter to forgive you again, I already did that, in person, badly, with shouting, which we both enjoyed more than we admitted.*

*I'm writing this because the delegation comes tomorrow and after they leave, I'm coming home for the winter. First winter. I checked with Ilsa — the cottage holds two, if someone gives up his maps room, and I know exactly whose maps room I mean, and I know you've already cleared half of it out because Tam told me that too, he tells me everything, you should really stop confiding in a chronicler.*

*A year ago I fell through fire into your valley carrying a crown I never wanted and a truth I couldn't carry alone. You met me at sword-point and then, worse, you met me with honesty, which I had no defenses against whatsoever. Everything I have now — the work, the purpose, the ridiculous family you assembled out of strays and survivors — started the night you chose to break your oath rather than let me face it alone. I've decided that's the only oath worth keeping. The one you make to people instead of rules.*

*Forty-seven steps, Jonah. Count them yourself sometime.*

*— Wren*

Jonah stood a long time at the boundary stone, holding the letter, while the first stars came out over the Thorn Valley and the schoolhouse bell below stayed quiet in the way bells do when nothing needs announcing and everything is already known.

Behind him, the lights of the village burned steady — Ilsa's cottage, Tam's archive window, the long low shape of the school where tomorrow twenty-three children would say the first oath again, raggedly, beautifully. Ahead of him, down the dark border road, someone was counting her steps home.

He folded the letter, put it inside his coat over his heart where he kept such things, and started down the hill toward the lights — no longer the last of anything, and grateful for it, all the way home.
