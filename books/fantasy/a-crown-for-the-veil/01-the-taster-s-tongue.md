## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The banquet hall of Veymar Palace smelled of beeswax, roast pheasant, and the particular sweetness of lilies that had been cut too long ago—flowers kept past their prime because no one dared to remove them while the king still looked at them. Mara Joubert stood at her post behind the high table's third chair, hands folded at her waist, and catalogued the room the way she catalogued everything: by what could kill.

Forty-two guests tonight. The Duke of Callenmere to the king's right, his daughter Lady Sera beside him, and old Lord Ambrose Fennick three seats down, whose gout made him favor the left foot and whose heart, according to the physicians' gossip, was held together more by stubbornness than sinew. Three lives that would depend on her tongue before the night ended. She didn't know it yet, but her body knew how to count such things the way sailors know weather in their knees.

She was twenty-six years old and had been swallowing poison for six of them. Not enough to kill—never enough to kill—but enough that her blood had become a ledger of small deaths: foxglove on a Tuesday when she was twenty, distilled hemlock the winter she turned twenty-three, a dozen compounds with names in four languages that the palace apothecary mixed under seal and she drank without flinching, because flinching was a luxury for girls who hadn't been sold to the crown at nine years old with nothing but a name and a strong stomach.

"Taster." The head steward, Master Corvin, touched her elbow. "The fourth course approaches."

Mara nodded and moved to the service corridor behind the dais, where the dishes came up from the kitchens on silver trays carried by pages who never met her eyes. That was fine. She preferred not being seen. A taster who was seen was a taster who could be bribed, threatened, or poisoned herself—and then who would stand between the Veiled King and his death?

*His death.* She almost laughed at the thought. Six years serving Aldric of House Veymar, and she had never once seen him eat.

That wasn't strange, she told herself. Kings were careful. Kings had tasters precisely so they needn't risk their own throats. But there was careful, and there was the way King Aldric sat at every feast with his hands folded on the table like a man at a funeral, watching his guests eat with an attention that felt less like hospitality and more like hunger he'd learned to sit on.

She put the thought away. It was not her place. Her place was the spoon.

The fourth course arrived: pheasant in a sauce of blackcurrant and Thornfell pepper—the very pepper she'd traveled two weeks into the mountains to source, riding smuggler roads she knew better than the king's own highways. She took the tasting spoon, broke the bird's breast, and lifted a measure of sauce to her mouth.

Blackcurrant, sharp and dark. Butter, cream, a whisper of clove. Thornfell pepper blooming slow and warm at the back of the throat, that distinctive heat that tasted faintly of pine resin and cold stone—

And underneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, something *else.*

Mara did not move. This was the first discipline they'd taught her, before immunity, before anything: *when you find it, become furniture.* Her face stayed pleasant, her breathing even, her eyes soft with the mild boredom of a woman doing a job she'd done ten thousand times. Inside, her mind laid the flavor out like a corpse on a table and began the dissection.

Bitterness. Not the clean bitterness of the pepper's skin—that she knew intimately, had tasted raw off the vine in a mountain village where the growler's wife fed her stew. This bitterness was greener, sharper, with a sweetness curled inside it like a worm in fruit. It sat on the tongue a half-second longer than it should. It clung.

Foxglove. No—not quite. Foxglove was dusty, vegetal. This was brighter. She rolled the ghost of it against her palate, chasing it the way one chases a word just out of reach.

Then Lord Ambrose Fennick, three seats down, laughed at something the Duke of Callenmere said, and the sound of it—a big, wet, cheerful laugh from a man with a bad heart—clicked the last piece into place.

*Convallaria.* Lily of the valley. Or something wearing its scent. Cardiotoxic, fast-acting in the elderly, slower in the young and healthy—which meant whoever had dosed the sauce wanted the old lord dead and didn't much care who else fell. Three seats of contamination. The duke. Lady Sera. Lord Ambrose.

Mara set down the spoon with steady hands and crossed to Corvin, murmuring the words they had rehearsed a hundred times in drills: "Fourth course is refused. Send it back entire. Bring the fifth."

Corvin's face went carefully blank—the face of a man who would ask questions later, quietly, with the doors locked. He signaled; the trays were swept away; somewhere below, a kitchen full of people would be questioned and a cook would weep and someone would be taken to the cells, guilty or not, because the palace required a body for every failure and preferably a living one it could hurt.

But the dish had already been served to the high table's near end. Three portions. Three forks already moving.

"Lord Ambrose." Mara was moving before she'd decided to move, gliding along behind the chairs with the unhurried gait of a servant, though her pulse hammered in her ears. "Forgive me—the steward requires your plate. There is a question of the sauce."

"A question of—" The old lord frowned at his pheasant, at which he'd already taken two bites. "It's excellent. Best I've had all season."

"And it will be again," Mara said, lifting the plate from under his fork with a smile she'd practiced until it fooled mirrors. "A matter of accounting. The kitchens miscounted the birds."

Nearby, the Duke of Callenmere had cut into his portion but not yet raised it. Lady Sera, young and pretty and bored, was picking at hers between sips of wine. Mara relieved them of their plates one by one, murmuring apologies, playing the small comedy of palace ritual while inside she counted Lord Ambrose's bites. Two. Two bites of a sauce carrying enough cardiac glycoside to stop a strong heart in an hour and a failing one in half that.

"I feel rather odd," Lord Ambrose said suddenly, and his laugh from a moment ago had gone thin. "Warm. Is it warm in here?"

"It's the wine, my lord." Mara took his arm, all solicitous concern. "Let me fetch you water. Water cuts the richness."

What she actually fetched, from the taster's kit she carried sewn into her sleeve—because the apothecary had drilled her on this too, *the poison you can't prevent you must be ready to mend*—was a draught of activated charcoal and emetic, bitter as grave dirt. She got most of it down him in the shadow of a pillar while the orchestra played a galliard and forty guests danced, oblivious, above a man retching into a servant's basin.

By the time the physicians arrived—summoned by Corvin with discreet urgency—Lord Ambrose was pale, shaking, emptied, and alive. The duke and Lady Sera, having eaten nothing or nearly nothing, needed only observation and a foul tonic each. And Mara stood back in the shadows with charcoal dust on her fingertips, watching the physicians work, feeling the thing she always felt after: not triumph. Just the flat, gray relief of a wall holding.

Three lives. She counted them the way a miser counts coins. Three lives bought with a tongue ruined for pleasure, a stomach that rejected nothing anymore because it had learned to accept everything, a girl sold at nine who had made herself into a lock through which death could not pass.

*Well done,* she told herself, and almost believed it.

* * *

She felt the gaze before she found its source.

It came like a change in pressure, like the moment before a storm breaks—she'd spent six years learning to read rooms, and this reading arrived not through her eyes but through her skin, a prickle at the nape, a wrongness in the air. Mara turned, slowly, the way one turns toward a sound in a dark house, and found the king looking at her.

King Aldric of House Veymar sat at the center of the high table beneath the crown that had crowned seven generations of his line—a heavy thing of gold and dark iron, worked into shapes that might have been leaves or might have been fingers, set with stones the color of deep water. He was not a large man. He had never been imposing in the way of warrior kings; the portraits in the gallery showed him slender, scholarly, kind-eyed. The man in the flesh was thinner than his portraits, grayer, as if the paintings had been made of someone else and he'd merely agreed to resemble them.

He was looking at her the way no king looks at a taster.

Kings look through servants. Kings see the uniform, the posture, the function. In six years, Aldric had acknowledged her perhaps four times, each a nod, each forgettable. This was not that. This was attention—focused, deliberate, *interested*, the gaze of a scholar who has noticed an anomaly in his figures. His eyes were pale gray, almost colorless in the candlelight, and they did not blink.

She watched him for the space of five heartbeats. He did not blink once. She counted—it was a habit, counting, she counted everything—and at six, seven, eight seconds his eyelids finally descended, slow as portcullises, and rose again, and Mara understood that whatever had just happened behind those eyes had not been blinking. It had been *sampling.* The way she sampled sauces. A taste of her, taken and considered.

Her mouth went dry. Six years of discipline held her spine straight and her face smooth, but beneath the discipline something animal woke and pressed its face against the bars of her composure and screamed.

*He's just a tired old king,* she told herself. *You saved his guests. He's grateful. Gratitude looks strange on men who've forgotten how to wear it.*

But gratitude would have come with words. A word to Corvin, a pension, a public commendation at most—kings thanked servants the way they did everything, through channels. This was not thanks. This was the look of a thing that had been eating a banquet for years and had suddenly, in the middle of the fourth course, noticed a spice it did not recognize.

Across the hall, the music swelled into a pavane. Dancers turned. Candles guttered in their hundreds, and the light they threw was warm and golden and lay over the court like a blanket laid over something sleeping.

The king rose. The hall rose with him, a rustle of silk and a chorus of chair-legs, and he lifted one hand—not for silence, which would have been normal, but simply lifted, palm outward, fingers slightly spread, and held it there while the music played on and everyone waited to learn what the gesture meant. No one asked. That was the strangest part, Mara thought. Forty nobles and a hundred servants, and not one of them seemed to find it odd that their king stood holding his hand up to a room like a man testing rain.

His eyes found hers across the distance, over the heads of the dancers, through the candle-smoke. And the corner of his mouth moved. Not a smile. The *memory* of a smile, worn thin, the way a coin wears thin from passing through too many hands.

*Taster,* his mouth shaped, without sound. Or perhaps it shaped nothing at all, and the word arrived in her head by some road she had no name for.

Then he lowered his hand, and the hall exhaled as one, and the king of Veymar turned and processed from the banquet hall with his crown catching the light, and the court bowed and curtsied in waves behind him like grass bending under wind, and no one—no one—looked at him as he passed except Mara, who saw what she saw:

That the torches nearest the door dimmed as he went by. All of them. In sequence. Like breath drawn in.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when the hall was stripped and the dancers gone and the servants moved through the wreckage of the feast with brooms and buckets—Mara climbed the narrow stair to her cell in the Taster's Wing and lit her single candle and sat on her narrow bed and shook.

Not visibly. She had learned to shake invisibly too, the way she'd learned to do everything invisibly; it was a small tremor held in the chest, a hummingbird frantic behind the ribs, invisible to anyone but herself. She sat with her hands flat on her knees and breathed the way the apothecary had taught her for the days after a poisoning—slow in, hold, slow out—and she took inventory, because inventory calmed her the way prayer calmed other women.

One: the sauce had been poisoned with a cardiac agent mimicking lily of the valley. Someone in the kitchens, or someone above them, had tried to kill Lord Ambrose Fennick tonight, and had used a method clumsy enough to catch three targets instead of one. Clumsy meant frightened, or new, or desperate. She would mention none of this to anyone but Corvin, and Corvin would carry it to Spymaster Vess, and Vess would do what Vess always did: make it disappear into the great quiet machinery of the crown's security, leaving Mara with a nod and perhaps, if the spymaster was feeling maternal, a hand briefly on her hair. *Good girl. You saw nothing. You are nothing. That is your armor.*

Two: the king had looked at her. Truly looked. And his eyes had been wrong in a way she could not file, could not categorize, could not reduce to fatigue or age or the vanity of powerful men. Eyes that sampled. Eyes that dimmed torches.

Three—and here her inventory faltered, because three was the thought she'd been refusing since the banquet hall, the thought that crouched under all the others like a killer under a feast table:

*In six years, I have never seen the king eat.*

Not a morsel. Not a sip. At every state occasion, every private dinner she'd attended in her official capacity, every ceremony where bread and salt were exchanged—he lifted the cup, he broke the bread, and the food reached his lips and departed them untouched, and she had watched him do it a hundred times and her mind had folded the observation away each time because it was impossible, because kings eat, because the alternative was a shape her mind refused to take.

Tonight, with the taste of foxglove-ghost still coating her tongue and the memory of unblinking gray eyes fixed on her, the shape would not stay folded away.

She rose, crossed to the water basin, and splashed her face. In the little mirror above it—a cracked thing, a luxury Vess had allowed her—her own face looked back: sharp-boned, dark-eyed, ordinary. A tool's face. A good tool's face.

"You're being foolish," she told her reflection quietly. "You swallowed death for breakfast for six years and it's finally gotten into your head."

The reflection didn't answer. Reflections in cracked mirrors never did. But as she blew out the candle and lay down in the dark, Mara became aware of a sound below her window—the Taster's Wing stood apart from the main palace, connected by one covered walkway, and below her window lay the herb garden where she was permitted to walk.

Footsteps in the garden. Slow. Unhurried. One set, pausing here and there, as if their owner were strolling, admiring the night-blooming stock, taking the air.

At midnight. In the cold. Alone.

Mara did not go to the window. She lay still and listened, and the footsteps wandered the garden below for the better part of an hour, and they never crunched on gravel, never brushed the rosemary hedge, never made any sound at all beyond the footfalls themselves—soft, patient, circling.

And once, directly beneath her window, they stopped, and stayed stopped, long enough that she counted two hundred of her own heartbeats before they resumed and faded toward the covered walkway that led, alone among all the paths of Veymar Palace, to the king's apartments.

Mara lay in the dark with her hand pressed flat over her hammering heart and made herself a promise, the first promise she had ever made that was entirely her own and not the crown's:

*Tomorrow I'll request the Thornfell run early. The pepper harvest, the rare resins—I'm due. I'll ride out before the week turns, and I won't think about gray eyes or dimming torches, because thinking about them is how a taster becomes visible, and visible is how a taster dies.*

She closed her eyes. Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it brought her no dreams—only a vast, patient darkness that watched her with something that was almost fondness, and did not blink.

She was on the road to Thornfell within three days. What she found there would burn away every comfortable lie she had left.

But that, as the smugglers say, is further up the mountain.
