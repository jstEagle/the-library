## 2. Blood on the Thornfell Road

The Thornfell mountains did not forgive carelessness, and Mara Joubert had never once asked them to.

She rode the smuggler's path the way other people said their prayers—by rote, by feel, with a private certainty that something was listening. The trail wound up out of the river valley in switchbacks so steep her mare, Brindle, had to be led on foot through the worst of it, and the pines pressed close enough on either side that their branches combed through her hood like fingers checking for contraband. Which, in fairness, they would have found. Saffron wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs. Dried ghost-pepper from the southern coast, worth more by weight than silver. A vial of crystallized honey-venom that no customs officer in three kingdoms could name and every poisoner in two would kill for.

None of it was for herself. That was the joke of her life, if she let herself laugh at it: the woman who tasted death daily, who carried enough toxin in her blood to stop a grown man's heart, who had spent six years turning her own body into a fortress of swallowed poisons—all of it so that other people could eat safely at the king's table.

The morning was cold enough to see her breath. Three days out of the capital, four days' hard ride to the spice market at Vell's Crossing, and she had not seen another living soul since the charcoal-burner's hut on the first night. She preferred it that way. The palace taught you to read faces; the mountains taught you to read ground. And the ground this morning was telling her something.

Crows. Too many of them, and too quiet. They sat along a dead pine like mourners at a funeral where nobody wanted to speak first, heads turned toward a bend in the trail ahead, and not one of them cawed when she passed beneath them. Birds scattered for wolves, for bears, for men with crossbows. Birds did not go silent for anything.

Mara stopped Brindle and listened.

Wind in the pines. Water somewhere below, running fast over stone—the melt-swollen creek that marked the halfway point of her route. Her own pulse, steady, because six years of holding a calm face while nobles died around banquet tables had taught her pulse better manners than that. But underneath all of it, faint as a smell of smoke on clothes after you'd left the fire behind, there was something else. Something wrong. She couldn't have named it. She only knew that the air ahead tasted the way the air in the palace kitchens had tasted the night old Cook Aldous collapsed at his bench, gray-faced and gasping, three minutes after sampling a soup he'd made ten thousand times before.

She tied Brindle to a birch and walked forward alone, one hand resting on the knife at her hip—not drawing it, just knowing it was there.

* * *

She found him around the bend, crumpled against the rocks beside the creek, and her first thought—her shameful, human first thought—was *robbery*, because the man wore courier's leathers, mud-spattered and travel-worn, and couriers carried things worth killing for.

Her second thought came when she got close enough to see him properly, and it drove every other thought out of her head like a slammed door.

The wounds were smoking.

Not bleeding—or bleeding too, there was blood, dark and ordinary, pooled beneath his side where he'd been run through or torn—but beneath the blood, across his chest and down his left arm, his flesh glowed. Faintly. Like embers raked over with ash and refusing to die. The skin around the glow had gone gray and cracked in fine lines, the way clay cracks in a kiln, and thin ribbons of white smoke curled off him and rose into the cold air and simply *stopped*, hanging there, unmoved by the wind that bent the grass at the creek's edge.

Mara had seen men burned, flayed, poisoned, gutted, and once—in the early years, a memory she kept locked in the deepest room of herself—a man whose own body had betrayed him cell by cell until he begged the guards to finish it. She had catalogued every way a person could come apart. Nothing in her catalogue looked like this.

"Hey." She knelt beside him, hands hovering, afraid to touch. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

His eyes opened. They were brown and human and terrified, and for one merciful moment he was just a man dying badly on a mountain road, and Mara knew what to do with that. She pulled her water skin, propped his head, wet his cracked lips.

"Poison?" he rasped. It took him two tries. His voice sounded scorched, as if the words were passing through fire on their way out. "Is it—did I swallow something?"

"No. You're not poisoned." She almost laughed at the absurdity of it, the taster diagnosing health. "You're hurt. Who did this? Was there anyone else? Another rider?"

His hand came up and caught her wrist with a grip that should not have been possible in a man so far gone, and she felt it then—heat, wrong heat, coming off his skin like he'd been standing in a forge. Not fever-heat. Something drier. Hungrier.

"The road," he whispered. "They were on the road. In the—" He coughed, and the smoke that curled from his lips was tinged gray-gold, ember-colored, and Mara's stomach turned over. "In the shadows. They walk in the shadows."

"There's no one here," she said gently. Delirium, then. Blood loss and shock and whatever burn had done this to him. "You're safe. I'm going to bind your side and get you to Vell's Crossing, there's a healer—"

"No healer." His grip tightened. His eyes fixed on hers with a desperate clarity that frightened her more than the smoke had. "No healer. No priest. Listen to me. *Listen.*"

"I'm listening."

"You're the taster." He said it like a verdict. "Mara Joubert. The king's tongue. I know you—I was told—" A shudder went through him, and where it passed, the ember-light brightened along his arm, and he gritted his teeth against a sound that was half scream and half something else, something that didn't belong in a human throat. "Told to find you. If it came to it. If I couldn't make the crossing."

"Told by whom?"

But he wasn't answering questions anymore. His free hand was clawing at the satchel strap across his chest, fingers clumsy, and Mara—acting on some instinct older than thought—reached down and unbuckled it for him. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth the same as her saffron, lay a leather tube, the kind mapmakers used, sealed at both ends with wax stamped by a signet she did not recognize.

He pressed it into her hands and folded her fingers closed over it with both of his, and his burning palms left marks on her skin—not burns exactly, more like the memory of heat, a prickling that faded even as she watched.

"Take it," he breathed. "South. Don't open it in the open. Don't sleep under the sky. Thirteen turns." His chest hitched; the ember-glow pulsed brighter, and the smoke rising from him thickened, and somewhere in the trees above them the crows finally found their voices—one raw cry, then another, ragged as torn cloth. "Thirteen turns of the moon. The shrine. You understand me? The shrine."

"What shrine? What turns? Who gave you this?"

"Unbind him." The words came out broken, each one costing him visibly, his lips cracking as they moved. "Unbind *him*. Before the thirteenth turn or it's done. Or we're all—" His eyes rolled back, caught, focused on her again with a final surge of will that made tears spring to Mara's eyes without her permission. "You taste everything. So taste this. Taste what's coming. And don't let them take the map back."

"Them," she repeated. "Who's *them*?"

He tried to answer. His mouth opened. And instead of words, light came out—a thread of gold-white flame, thin as a needle, rising from between his lips and unraveling into the air like smoke written by a careful hand. For one impossible instant Mara saw it form shapes: letters, maybe, or a figure, a tall shape wearing something round and bright upon its head—

Then the wind moved again, the light scattered, and the courier slumped back against the rock with his eyes still open and fixed on a point somewhere beyond the mountains, beyond the sky, and he did not breathe again.

* * *

Mara sat beside the dead man for a long time.

The crows had gone quiet again. The creek ran on, indifferent. The smoke had stopped rising from his flesh, though the ember-glow lingered in the cracks of his skin, dimming slowly, like a hearth going out overnight in an empty house. Up close, now that terror had loosened its grip on her spine enough to let her think, she could see the details her first glance had missed. His boots were good leather but worn through at the heel—long roads, hard roads, the roads that didn't appear on any king's map. His cloak pin was plain pewter, unmarked. No livery. No house colors. A courier for someone who didn't want to be known, carrying a map sealed in wax by a signet she didn't recognize, dying of wounds that smoked.

And he had known her name. Known what she was. *The king's tongue.*

Six years, and she had been careful. Careful the way only invisible people are careful. The court knew there was a taster, of course—they knew her the way they knew the number of stairs to the throne room, without ever looking at them. But her name? Her face attached to her name? Someone out in the world beyond the palace walls, someone desperate enough to die for it, had sent a man over the Thornfells to find *Mara Joubert*.

She checked his body with the detached thoroughness Vess had drilled into her—Vess, who had found her starving in a gutter at nine years old and said, *you have a good mouth for this work, girl, meaning both senses of it—and found nothing else. No papers. No coin purse, which meant robbery had indeed happened, or else whoever killed him had taken payment in something other than coin. His sword was still at his hip. Drawn, but unblooded. Whatever had torn through his side and set him smoldering had not needed to parry anything.

She stood, wiped her hands on her trousers, and made herself look at the sky. Mid-morning. Vell's Crossing was four days south if she pushed. The capital was five days east. The map tube weighed nothing and felt like it weighed a hundred pounds.

Every sensible instinct she owned said the same thing: ride east, deliver the spices, tell Vess everything, let the spymaster's web handle whatever strange thing had crawled onto her mountain. Vess had raised her. Vess had taught her which lords lied with their left hands and which poisons smelled of almonds and how to be invisible in a room full of people trained to see. Six years of trust sat on one side of the scale.

On the other side sat a dead man's grip on her wrists and a voice scorched down to embers: *don't let them take the map back.*

And underneath that, quieter, in the place where she kept the things she knew but hadn't admitted yet: Vess always knew. Vess always knew everything. If someone was smuggling maps over the Thornfells, Vess would already have heard the whisper of it. If someone was dying of wounds no blade made, Vess would already have a file opened, a network activated, a tidy explanation prepared.

So why did the courier come to *her*?

Mara looked down at the dead man one last time. There was nothing to bury him with and no time to dig anyway, so she dragged him among the rocks where the crows would do their work and the mountain would keep its secrets, and she stood over him and said the small words her mother had taught her before the fever took her mother, the ones for strangers and animals and anyone who died without family near. Then she took the map tube, and she did not open it—*don't open it in the open*, and she intended to obey a dead man's last instructions to the letter—and she untied Brindle and swung up into the saddle.

South, then. Away from the capital. Four days to Vell's Crossing, where there was a smuggler's den beneath the Broken Antler tavern with a locked room and a bolted door and no windows facing the sunrise, and where a woman named Ketty owed Mara enough favors to buy silence for a week.

Brindle picked her way down toward the creek crossing, and Mara let her, mind churning faster than the meltwater.

*Thirteen turns. The shrine. Unbind him.*

Unbind whom? The king? The thought surfaced unbidden and she shoved it down hard, angry at herself for even forming it. The king was the king. The king ate nothing she hadn't tasted. The king had watched her across a hundred banquets with those pale, patient eyes and never once blinked when she expected him to, and she had told herself for six years that it meant nothing, that great men had still habits, that a taster's imagination was the occupational hazard of her trade—

The mare's hoof slipped on a wet stone and Mara grabbed the saddle horn, heart slamming, and the motion shook the thought loose like a dog shaking off water.

Below the crossing, half-buried in the mud of the far bank, something glinted. She reined in and stared at it for a long moment before dismounting, because the morning had taught her that glints in the mud were not always horseshoes.

It was a second seal. A broken wax disc, stamped with the same unknown signet, torn from a tube like hers—no, *the twin of hers*, the fracture pattern matched perfectly, as if two tubes had been sealed together and then wrenched apart. Whoever had attacked the courier had gotten hold of something and lost it, or split the cargo deliberately and failed to retrieve both halves.

Or there were two maps. And somewhere in the mountains, someone else was carrying the other one.

Mara stood in the freezing spray of the creek with the broken seal in her palm and the intact tube heavy inside her coat, and she realized that her hands were shaking—not from cold, though it was cold, but from the specific shaking that comes when the ground shifts under everything you believed you were standing on.

She had spent her whole life learning to detect poison in food. She had never once considered that a kingdom itself might be poisoned—that you could taste it in the air of a banquet hall, in the too-long stillness of a king's gaze, in the silence of crows over a smoking corpse on a smuggler's road.

Four days to Vell's Crossing. Five days back to a palace full of dishes she suddenly did not trust herself to taste.

She mounted up, turned Brindle south, and rode. Behind her, on the dead pine, the crows lifted as one and wheeled east—toward the capital—and Mara did not let herself watch them go.
