## 3. What the Map Shows

The smuggler's den called the Hollow Kettle sat where the Thornfell road bent around a shoulder of black granite, half-buried in the mountain's shadow like a stone someone had dropped and forgotten. From the outside it was nothing—a lean-to against the cliff face, a door of warped pine that had not closed properly since before Mara was born. Inside, it was warm and dim and smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular sourness of men who drank to forget what they carried over the passes.

Old Sefa kept the den now. She had kept it for thirty years, back when she'd run spice herself, back when her hands were steady enough to pick a lock or pick a pocket with equal grace. Now her hands shook, but her memory didn't, and her discretion had never once wavered. She took one look at Mara's face as she came in out of the dark—mud to the knees, courier's blood dried brown on her sleeve—and asked no questions. She poured plum brandy without being asked and put a bowl of barley stew in front of her, and only when Mara had eaten half of it did Sefa say anything at all.

"You look like a woman carrying something heavier than a map tube."

Mara set down her spoon. "Is there a room?"

"The back one. The one with the good bolt." Sefa's eyes, pale as river ice, held hers for a moment longer than comfort allowed. "You didn't come through here alone, did you?"

"No."

"Then whatever's following you will take until morning to find the road. Sleep first. Read after." The old woman gathered the bowl. "Dead men's errands keep. Living ones don't."

Mara wanted to argue. She wanted to spread the map across the table right now, by lamplight, and tear its secrets open while the courier's last words still rang fresh in her ears—*Thirteen turns. The shrine. Unbind him.* But Sefa had survived three decades in the Thornfells by knowing when caution was the better part of courage, and Mara had learned everything she knew about staying alive from women like her. So she drank the brandy, all of it, let the warmth loosen the knot between her shoulders, and went to the back room.

She did not sleep. She lay on a straw pallet under a blanket that smelled of cedar and listened to the mountain settle around her, and she turned the leather map tube over and over in her hands until her fingers knew every crack in it.

*Unbind him.* Unbind who? A king could not be bound. A king was the binding—the crown itself was the oldest symbol of law made flesh in the whole history of Velesse. Every schoolchild learned it: the crown binds the kingdom together, the kingdom binds the crown.

She fell asleep at some grey hour before dawn with the words still circling, and dreamed of the banquet hall, of the king's face turning toward her across a sea of candles, of eyes that blinked too seldom, and woke with her heart hammering and the light already failing outside—which meant she had slept the whole day through, which meant whoever followed her was a full day closer.

She bolted the door, wedged a chair beneath the handle, lit the single lamp, and unrolled the map.

It was vellum, good vellum, the kind that cost more than a smuggler earned in a season, and it had been drawn by a careful hand. At first glance it looked like exactly what she'd expected: a map of the Thornfell mountains, rendered in fine brown ink, the ridgelines picked out with delicate hatching, the passes named in a small, precise script. She recognized the territory immediately. This was her country. Six years of running spices through these mountains had etched them into her memory more deeply than any childhood home ever had. There was the Widow's Notch. There was the frozen waterfall they called the White Stair. There was the river gorge where she'd nearly drowned at seventeen, and the shelf of rock above Kettle Falls where she'd hidden from a tax patrol for two days eating snow and dried plums.

But the roads were wrong.

Not misdrawn—she would have caught that instantly, the way a musician catches a false note. They were *labeled* wrong. Each route bore a name in that same precise hand, and none of the names were places. The pass above Kettle Falls was marked *First Turn*. The high road along the Serpent's Back ridge read *Second Turn*. A route she'd never traveled, up into the glacier fields where no smuggler had reason to go, was labeled *Third Turn*, and beside it, in faded ink, someone had written a date.

Mara counted backward from the date and felt the floor tilt gently beneath her.

It was a month. One month per road. Thirteen roads, thirteen names, thirteen dates stretching forward from seven years ago into a future that ended—her finger traced the final line, the fourteenth route, the one that climbed past the maps' edge into the unnamed white heart of the range—*Thirteenth Turn*. And below it, in ink so fresh it might have been written yesterday:

*The shrine.*

She sat back on her heels. Her breath fogged in the cold room. Outside, wind worried at the loose shutter, a thin rhythmic creaking like a rocking chair, like a lullaby, like something patient.

These weren't roads. Or rather—they were roads, real roads, physical paths through real mountains—but they had been chosen, sequenced, annotated according to something else entirely. She looked again at the dates and saw the pattern she'd been too startled to see before. Each date fell on a new moon. No—not quite. She checked her memory of the almanac, the one every taster kept because certain poisons lost potency with the lunar phase, an old wives' tale that happened to be true for belladonna derivatives. The dates fell on the nights when the moon rose latest and thinnest. Dark-moon nights. Nights when the sky held almost nothing.

Thirteen dark moons. Seven years ago to—she calculated, lips moving—to this coming winter. Which meant twelve had already passed. Which meant there was *one left*.

One turn remaining, and a route that led into the high glaciers, to a shrine she had never heard of in mountains she thought she knew like her own heartbeat.

Her mouth had gone dry. She reached for the brandy bottle Sefa had left and found her hand shaking too badly to pour. She laughed at herself—a short, ugly sound—and drank straight from the neck.

*Unbind him.* The courier's blood had smoked. His flesh had burned from the inside with no mark of any blade she knew, and he had died whispering about unbinding, and now she held a map that treated the mountains of Velesse like the mechanism of a lock, each pass a tumbler, each dark moon a turn of the key.

A key turned to do what?

She forced herself to be systematic, the way Vess had trained her. *When you're afraid, make lists. Fear is just information you haven't organized yet.* So she made lists. Facts, in order:

One: the map was old—seven years old, if the first date marked its making—and maintained. Newer annotations overlaid older ones; the ink changed twice across the vellum's face, suggesting at least three different hands, or one hand over many years.

Two: the final annotations were the freshest, and among them, near the shrine notation, someone had pressed a seal into warm wax that had then been glued flat to the corner of the vellum, half-hidden beneath a folded-over flap of the material itself. Deliberately concealed. She pried at the flap with her thumbnail, working it loose the way she'd been taught to work seals off documents she wasn't supposed to have read, patient, gentle, letting heat from her palm soften the glue.

The flap lifted.

The wax was deep blue—the royal blue, the shade reserved for the bloodline and no one else—and stamped in it was a sigil she had seen perhaps five times in her life and never once in the palace, because the palace did not speak his name.

A stag rampant, crowned with a broken coronet, standing on a crescent moon.

Prince Caelan's seal. The exile's seal. The prince who had been banished seven years ago in disgrace—she'd been newly arrived at court then, sixteen years old and learning which servants' corridors led where, and she remembered the scandal the way you remember weather from your childhood: vaguely, in impressions. Shouting behind doors. The queen weeping in public, which queens did not do. Then the prince gone, his name struck from the toast at formal dinners, his portraits removed so thoroughly that within a year Mara had half-convinced herself the man had never existed at all. Banished for what? Even now she couldn't recall the charge. Treason, someone said. Theft of state secrets, said others. The charges had mattered less than the silence that followed, the way the whole court had simply agreed, wordlessly and completely, to amputate him from its memory.

Seven years. The map was seven years old. The first annotation dated from the very month of his exile.

Mara stared at the seal and felt the shape of her entire understanding begin, slowly, to rotate—like those carved puzzle boxes Vess kept in her study, the ones that looked like solid spheres until you found the seam and twisted, and the whole thing opened into something else entirely.

Caelan had made this map. Or commissioned it. In the month of his banishment, the exiled prince had begun charting a course through the Thornfells keyed to the dark moons, a course that ended at a shrine in the high ice, and for seven years someone—several someones, by the ink—had continued the work, refining it, annotating it, hiding it inside a courier's map tube where no loyal servant of the crown would ever think to look.

And the courier had died to carry it. Had crossed the border, wounded in ways no blade accounted for, burning from within, and had spent his last breath pressing it into the hands of a stranger because a stranger was what he had.

*Unbind him.*

Unbind *who*? The question circled back, and this time it landed differently. Because there was one person in Velesse whom a prince might want unbound. One person whose freedom might require a map of turns and moons and a shrine beyond the maps' edge. One person sealed away so completely that even the idea of his imprisonment had been amputated from the court's collective mind—

No. Mara pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. That was madness. That was the brandy and the fear talking. The king sat his throne. She had seen him. She had knelt before him. She had watched him watch her across a banquet hall with those terrible patient eyes, and whatever he was, he was not bound—he was the opposite of bound, he was the binder, the center, the fixed point around which the whole smiling court turned like moths around a lamp.

Wasn't he?

She thought of the banquet again, and this time she made herself remember it properly, not the way she'd filed it away but in full detail, taster-fashion, every observation logged and cross-referenced. The king had tasted nothing. She had assumed—she had *assumed*—that the cupbearers handled his plate separately, that his food was prepared in the private kitchens under guard, that a king of Velesse would never eat from the common service. It was protocol. It was always protocol.

Except she had served the palace for six years, and protocol was her scripture, and she suddenly could not remember a single meal—*a single meal*—at which she had seen Aldric eat. Not one. Not in six years of banquets, audiences, feasts of state. He raised his goblet; the wine stayed. He gestured at his plate; the food grew cold and was cleared away untouched. And everyone bowed their heads and smiled, and no one noticed, because noticing would have been unthinkable, and the court of Velesse was very, very good at not thinking the unthinkable.

Her skin crawled. She wrapped her arms around herself and rocked slightly, once, twice, and made herself breathe.

*The crown is the cage.*

The words were there on the vellum—she had seen them earlier and read past them, dismissed them as some smuggler's superstition, some cryptic fragment—but now she found them again, written small along the bottom margin in that same precise hand, and this time she read them as they were meant to be read. Not a metaphor. An engineering note. The way a locksmith labels a diagram: *here is the mechanism; here is the ward; here is the cage.*

The crown was the cage. Something was in the crown—or the crown was around something—and the map was the sequence of turns required to open it, and Prince Caelan, exiled and erased, had spent seven years planning to open it, and the thing wearing Aldric's face on the throne either did not know or did not care, and the courier had known enough to die for it, and Vess—

Vess had questioned her too gently. Too carefully. Had asked, with that soft mother's voice she used like a knife, exactly where Mara had gone and exactly whom she had met and whether she had brought anything back. Had searched her pack herself, laughing, calling it habit. Had found nothing, because Mara had slept with the map tube strapped to her body like a second spine.

*If I tell Vess,* Mara thought, *and Vess already knows—then I've handed her the last piece. And if Vess doesn't know—then I've painted a target on whatever remains of us both.*

The lamp guttered. Wind screamed briefly down the chimney and died. Somewhere far off, down the valley, a dog began barking and would not stop.

Mara looked at the map a long time, at the thin line climbing into the white unnamed country, at the stag rampant standing on its crescent moon. An exiled prince. A dying courier. Thirteen dark moons, twelve already fallen into the past like coins down a well.

One turn left.

And she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, why the king's gaze had lingered on her at the banquet, why those under-blinking eyes had tracked her across the hall. It wasn't her skill he'd noticed. It was the map. It was the errand. Somewhere in the machinery of the court, a thread had gone taut, and the thing on the throne had felt it pull, and it was looking—for the courier, for the map, for the hands that carried it.

For her.

She rolled the vellum with hands that had steadied themselves by sheer professional discipline, slid it back into the tube, and strapped it against her ribs. Then she sat in the dark with the dead lamp and listened to the wind and the distant frantic barking of the dog, and she made the only decision the night allowed.

At dawn she would go to Caelan. To the border towns, to the taverns where exiles drank, to the last place anyone loyal to the crown would think to search. She would find a disgraced prince who had every reason to hate her and the crown she served, and she would show him a dead man's map and pray that the message scrawled in his own hidden seal meant what she was beginning to fear it meant.

Because the alternative—that the King of Velesse had been dead for seven years, and something else had been sitting in his chair, wearing his crown, eating his court one hollow-eyed soul at a time—was a thought too large to hold alone.

Outside the shuttered window, far down the dark valley, the dog stopped barking all at once, mid-cry, as if a hand had closed around its throat.

Mara did not sleep again.
