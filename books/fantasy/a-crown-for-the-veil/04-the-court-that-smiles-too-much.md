## 4. The Court That Smiles Too Much

The palace smelled the same when Mara returned—beeswax and lilies and, beneath it all, the faint mineral tang of the mountain's breath seeping up through the foundations. Six years of coming and going had worn the smell into something like a skin she wore. She had never once thought of it as strange.

Now it was the first thing that felt wrong.

She stood in the servants' corridor below the east wing, travel-stained, her pack still heavy with spices and a leather map tube hidden in its false bottom, and she breathed in, and something in her chest flinched. The air was stale. Not dirty—the palace was scrubbed daily by an army of hands—but *stale*, like a room where someone had died and no one had opened a window since. Like breath that had been breathed too many times by too few lungs.

*You're tired,* she told herself. *You rode three days with a dead man's map against your spine. You're seeing ghosts because you buried one on the Thornfell road.*

But her body had been trained for this. Six years of tasting, of reading the smallest wrongness in a spoonful of honeyed wine—a bitterness under sweetness, an oil-slick shimmer on broth, the way a spice sat heavy where it should have bloomed. Her tongue knew poison. And lately, her eyes were beginning to learn it too.

She walked toward the kitchens, and she made herself look.

---

The first thing she noticed was the smiles.

Palace servants smiled; that was nothing new. A servant who didn't smile didn't last. But there was a difference between a smile that lived in the eyes and one that lived only in the mouth, and the corridor was full of mouths.

A laundry girl passed her with a basket of linens, smiling, and Mara watched the smile hold perfectly still as the girl turned a corner—as if it had been painted on and the face behind it had simply rotated away. A footman polishing the same brass sconce he'd been polishing, she realized, when she'd left. The cloth moved in long, even strokes. His eyes never tracked the work. They looked at nothing, at the middle distance, at some point six inches behind the world.

"Hallo, Tam," she said, because she'd known him since she was fourteen, when he'd smuggled her extra bread during her first brutal winter of training.

Tam smiled wider. "Good morning, Mistress Joubert. Lovely weather we're having."

It was raining. It had been raining for two days; her cloak was soaked through.

"Tam," she said carefully. "How's your mother? Is she still keeping the stall at Millmarket?"

"Good morning, Mistress Joubert." The same words. The same cadence, note for note, like a musician replaying a phrase from memory rather than feeling. "Lovely weather we're having."

Mara walked on. Her heart was doing something quiet and terrible in her chest, a slow cold knock like a fist against a door no one would open.

At the next landing, a chambermaid smiled at her and said, "Lovely weather we're having." Two minutes later, halfway down the gallery, a scullery boy said it again—same words, same flat warmth, as though the whole palace had been given a single sentence to wear this season. She stopped counting after the fifth repetition. Counting made it worse. Counting made it real.

*They've always been like this,* whispered the old habit of her mind, the trained part, the part Vess had built brick by brick: *Servants are dull. Dullness is safety. You learned not to see them years ago.*

And that was the horror of it, she thought, standing in the gallery with rain-gray light falling through the windows. She *had* learned not to see them. Vess had taught her that invisibility was armor—that the taster who noticed everything about the court would eventually be noticed back. So Mara had spent six years perfecting a blindness, cataloguing only what mattered: dishes, doses, faces at high table, the king's moods. The servants had become furniture. The nobles had become furniture. The palace itself had become a machine she moved through without looking at, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

She was looking now. And the furniture was smiling at her with borrowed mouths.

---

The great hall was being prepared for evening court, and Mara drifted along its edge with a taster's tray in her hands—an excuse, a costume, a reason to be anywhere. The nobles had begun to gather early, drawn out of their wings by habit or summons, and she watched them the way she'd once watched a suspect dish: not staring, but letting her peripheral vision do the work.

Lord Aldous Renner, Master of Coin, stood by the hearth speaking to Lady Sarelle Vane. Their conversation had the rhythm of any court conversation—bows, murmurs, laughter pitched just loud enough to carry. Mara edged closer with her tray, pretending to check the wine.

"—and I told him, the harvest levy cannot be doubled twice in one season," Lord Aldous said, smiling.

"The harvest levy cannot be doubled twice in one season," Lady Sarelle agreed warmly.

"I told him myself, last spring. The levy cannot be doubled twice."

"Cannot be doubled twice," she said, and laughed, and the laugh was lovely and hollow as a struck bell.

Mara's blood went thin and cold. She had heard echoes before—courtiers parroting rumor, repeating a clever phrase until it wore out. This was different. This was two people having a conversation made entirely of each other's sentences, passing the same handful of words back and forth like children playing catch with one ball, and both of them smiling, both of them nodding, both of them looking at each other with eyes that registered nothing at all.

She moved down the hall. At the far end, a cluster of younger lords argued cheerfully about the spring hunt—and then, as she passed, one of them said, "The passes will open soon enough," and another said, "The passes will open soon enough," and a third, "Soon enough, soon enough," and they all laughed together, in unison, a single laugh with three throats.

Her tray rattled faintly against her fingers. She steadied it.

*When did this start?* she asked herself, and could not answer, and that frightened her more than anything else. Had it been gradual? Had the court been dying by inches while she tasted and spat and memorized and slept, so slowly that no single day showed the change, the way a man doesn't see his own hair going gray? Or—

Or had she simply never looked?

She remembered the courier on the Thornfell road, his flesh smoking like banked embers, wounds no blade had made. *Thirteen turns. The shrine. Unbind him.* She remembered the map tube pressed into her hands with the last strength of a dying man, warm as a living thing. She had spent three days telling herself the courier had been mad, or cursed by something local, some mountain thing that had nothing to do with the palace and its beeswax smell.

Standing in the great hall, listening to a hundred people say forty words between them, she stopped telling herself that.

---

She found her way, almost without deciding to, to the royal dais. The king's table stood ready for evening court: plates of gold, goblets racked and gleaming, and laid out in state, the evening meal she would be expected to taste within the hour.

And beside it, the king's own place setting.

Mara stared at it. Then she did something she had never done in six years of service: she called over the under-steward and asked him a question she already knew the shape of.

"His Majesty dines at the seventh bell?"

"At the seventh bell, Mistress Joubert, as ever." He bowed. "Lovely weather we're having."

"Yes," she said. "Lovely."

She waited until he'd gone, then she leaned close to the king's plate and did what she was made for. She examined the meal the way a jeweler examines a stone. Roast capon glazed in quince. A soup of white beans and smoked ham hock. Bread, butter, a compote of winter pears, wine dark as garnets. Every dish correct. Every dish prepared exactly as the king liked, seasoned exactly to the preferences she had catalogued across six years and a thousand meals.

Every dish untouched-looking in a way that no fresh dish should look.

That wasn't possible, she told herself. It had been cooked that afternoon; the kitchen clocks confirmed it; the steam still rose off the soup. And yet the capon's glaze had a stillness to it, a lacquered perfection, like fruit painted on porcelain. The wine caught the light and held it without moving.

She dipped the silver tasting spoon—it was her right, her office, no one questioned the taster at the king's table—and touched it to her tongue.

Nothing.

Not *no poison*. Nothing. No flavor at all. The soup sat on her tongue like warm water shaped like soup, and when she swallowed, it went down without taste, without weight, without anything, and a moment later she felt it dissolve somewhere behind her sternum into a sensation she had no name for except *wrongness*, a faint cold hum like a struck tuning fork buried in her chest.

Mara set the spoon down very carefully, the way you set down a sleeping snake.

In six years she had tasted everything. Bitter, sweet, metallic, oily, alkaline, the ghost of arsenic under almond, the telltale numbing of hemlock at the back of the throat. Her tongue had a vocabulary of a hundred poisons. What was in the king's food—or what passed for the king's food—was none of them. It was absence wearing a meal's clothing.

And the king never ate. She knew that now, suddenly, completely, the knowledge arriving whole the way a fever breaks. She ran backward through the years and found the truth waiting there, patient, enormous: she could not remember a single time she had seen King Aldric swallow. He lifted his fork. He gestured with his goblet. He dismissed her with a wave after her tastings, and the plates went away full, and she had never once wondered where the food went, because wondering was not her office. Tasting was her office. Tasting, and silence, and survival.

*What have I been protecting?* she thought. *All these years. What have I been keeping safe?*

---

"You're back early."

The voice came from behind her, soft as wool, and Mara's whole body flinched before she could stop it—which was its own alarm, because in six years she had never flinched at Vess. Spymaster Vess moved through the palace like a draft through a keyhole; everyone knew that. Everyone accepted being startled by her. But Mara had been raised in the woman's shadow, trained to hear the particular creak of the third stair, the particular pause at a doorway, and she had never once failed to know when Vess was near.

Until now. Until her senses had started screaming about everything else, and the one danger she trusted had slipped past them entirely.

Vess stood in the shadow of the great hall's western arch, small and gray and neat, her iron-colored hair pinned without a strand astray, her hands folded at her waist. She looked exactly as she always looked. That was the problem, Mara realized. Vess was sixty-one years old. Mara had known her for twelve of those years, and in twelve years the woman had not aged a single day—not a new line, not a thickened waist, not a gray hair gained. She had filed that fact away long ago in the drawer marked *unimportant*, alongside the servants' sameness and the nobles' echoes, in the great cellar of things a survivor must not look at.

"Mistress Joubert." Vess's voice was gentle. Too gentle. Vess interrogated traitors with gentleness; she saved the sharpness for those she loved. "Your trip went well?"

"Well enough. The spice roads are quiet this season."

"Quiet." Vess nodded slowly, as if tasting the word. "You were gone nine days. Your usual circuit is seven."

"A landslide closed the high pass above Kettering. I went around by the low road."

"Mm." Vess's eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, and they rested on Mara with a weight like a hand on the back of the neck. "The low road. Through Thornfell proper. You met no one?"

"No one worth mentioning."

"There is no one on that road *not* worth mentioning, child. That's why I send you on it." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "There was a courier. Royal courier, carrying dispatches to the garrison at Vael's Crossing. He never arrived. The garrison is asking questions I don't enjoy answering." Another pause, longer. "You met no one?"

The map tube seemed to burn against Mara's spine, through leather and linen and the false bottom of her pack, which she had left, thank every god there was, locked in her own room. She kept her face still. She had been trained for this too—by the woman now looking at her—and she hated how well the training held.

"Dead men don't talk to me, Vess. Living ones barely do."

Something crossed Vess's face then—something quick and strange, there and gone, like a fish turning under ice. Not suspicion. Mara knew suspicion; she'd grown up inside it. It was older than suspicion, and sadder. It looked almost like grief.

"No," Vess said softly. "I suppose they don't." She unfolded her hands and refolded them. "You look tired, child. Eat something. Sleep. Court sits at the seventh bell, and His Majesty—" and here the gentle voice did the thing Mara would remember for the rest of her life, the thing that finally snapped the last thread holding her old life together—His Majesty has been *asking after you.* He likes to know where his taster is. He notices, when you're gone."

"He notices," Mara repeated.

"Oh, yes." Vess smiled, and it was the warmest smile Mara had ever seen her wear, and it reached all the way to her mouth and not one inch further. "He notices everything now."

She turned and walked away down the gallery, small and neat and gray, and the lamplight bent oddly around her, or perhaps Mara's eyes were failing, or perhaps the light simply preferred not to touch her.

---

Mara did not eat. She did not sleep.

She climbed to her narrow room above the taster's quarters, barred the door, and sat on the floor with her back against the bed frame and the map tube across her knees, and she let herself think the thoughts she had been drowning for six years, one by one, like prisoners released from a cell.

The servants repeated one another. The nobles spoke in circles, forty words among a hundred mouths. The king's food had no taste and no purpose, because the king did not eat, because the king had not eaten in—how long? How long does a court go on smiling around an empty chair? The courier had died with his flesh smoking, saying *unbind him*, and the map bore the seal of a prince exiled seven years ago, and Vess had not aged in twelve, and Vess had asked about the courier with grief in her face, and the king had been *asking after her*, and *he notices everything now*—

*The crown is the cage.*

The words from the map rose in her memory in the dead cartographer's cramped hand, and Mara pressed her palms against her eyes and understood, with the terrible clarity of a woman who has finally looked at the room she's lived in all her life, that the palace was not a house.

It was a body. And something inside it was eating.

She could feel it now that she knew to feel it—the hum she'd caught on her tongue from the king's soup, fainter here, diffuse, everywhere, rising out of the stones themselves like damp. The whole palace hummed with it, a note below hearing, and every smiling mouth and hollow eye and repeated phrase was a cell in a vast slow feeding, and she had lived in the belly of it for six years, tasting its meals, keeping its secrets, calling it home.

Somewhere below, the seventh bell began to ring.

Court would sit. The king would take his chair beneath the crown, and the court would smile its forty words at him, and Mara Joubert would stand at his shoulder with her silver spoon and taste nothing, and smile, and say nothing, because that was her office.

Her hand closed around the map tube until the leather creaked.

*Unbind him,* the courier had said. *Thirteen turns.*

The bell finished ringing. Down in the hall, faintly, through floors and stone, she heard the sound of a hundred people beginning to applaud—and it was perfectly, precisely, uniformly timed, one single pair of hands multiplied, and it did not stop when applause should have stopped, and it did not change, and it went on and on and on like the beating of a heart that belonged to no one at all.

Mara picked up her pack, checked the false bottom, and blew out her lamp.

She had until the thirteenth turn. And she had a prince to find.
