## 5. An Audience with the Crown

The summons came at the hour when the palace should have been asleep.

Mara had been awake anyway—she was always awake now, since Thornfell, since the courier's flesh smoking like banked coals beneath her hands, since the map tube's weight in her satchel like a second heart. She sat by her narrow window in the tasters' wing, watching the thirteenth moon—no, the twelfth, she corrected herself, counting backward from the map's final mark—hang fat and yellow over the eastern towers, when the knock came.

Three soft raps. Then a pause. Then one more.

She knew every servant's knock in the palace the way a musician knows scales. This one belonged to no one.

She opened the door to find a page she half-recognized—a boy called Tam, or had been called Tam, though his face had gone smooth and vague as a thumbprint pressed into wax. His eyes were the color of weak tea. He did not blink when he should have.

"His Majesty requests the taster's presence," he said. "In the Amber Gallery. Alone."

"Alone," Mara repeated.

"The king prefers it so."

Every instinct Vess had drilled into her screamed at once. Private audiences with the king happened for two reasons: reward or burial. And kings who never ate, whose eyes didn't blink enough, whose court smiled too much—they did not give rewards.

"I'll need to dress," she said.

"You are dressed adequately." The boy turned and walked away without waiting, his footsteps making no sound on stone that had always creaked before.

Mara looked at her satchel where it lay beneath a loose floorboard. The map stayed. If she carried it to the Amber Gallery, she might as well carry her own head. She took instead the small knife she kept in her boot—the one for opening spice parcels—and, after a moment's thought, the vial of concentrated nightlock she'd distilled years ago and never had cause to use. A taster's last resort. Not for others. For herself.

Then she followed the silent boy down through the sleeping palace, past corridors where the torches burned without flickering, past guards who stood so still their chests barely moved, and felt—as she had felt since Thornfell—that she was walking through the body of something vast and patient, and that its blood was beginning to notice her moving inside it.

* * *

The Amber Gallery ran along the palace's eastern face, a corridor of honey-colored glass and gilded frames where the royal portraits hung: Aldric's father, his grandfather, the long procession of Joubert-crowned kings going back four hundred years. By day it was bright and mercantile, all gold light and gilt edges. By night, with only the moon pouring through the glass, it became a tunnel of drowned amber, and the portraits floated in it like things preserved.

King Aldric stood at the far end, before his own portrait.

He had his back to her. That was wrong too—kings did not turn their backs; it was the first law of their bodies. He stood very straight, straighter than she remembered him standing at the banquet, and his shadow on the polished floor lay at an angle that did not match the moonlight. It stretched toward her instead of away, long and thin as a crack in ice.

"Taster," he said.

His voice was his voice. That was somehow the worst of it. She had spent six years learning the timbre of that voice across banquet halls—*the roast pleases me, bring the taster forward*—and here it was again, perfect, while everything around it had gone subtly, catastrophically wrong, the way a dish goes wrong. One note off. She had built her whole life on catching that one note.

"Your Majesty." She sank into a curtsy and kept her eyes down, watching his shadow on the floor. It did not move when he did. "You honor me."

"Do I?" He turned.

Up close, by moonlight, the wrongness resolved into details. His skin was too even, like porcelain glazed over something. His eyes were Aldric's grey eyes, but they moved with small mechanical precision, left-right-left, cataloguing her the way she catalogued dishes. And beneath it all—she would not have seen it a month ago—there was a flicker. Something behind the glaze, pressing outward, like a face against silk.

"You saved three lives at the autumn banquet," the king said. "The Lady Ondrey's table. The spiced quail."

"The seasoning was off, Majesty. Bitterness where there should have been none. I judged it safer to—"

"Yes." He tilted his head, an angle no comfortable neck permitted. "You have a remarkable tongue, Taster Joubert. Remarkable. We have been watching you for some time."

*We.* The word dropped through her stomach like a stone through water.

"Your king is grateful," he continued, and began to walk—slowly, along the line of portraits, his footsteps soundless. "Grateful servants are rewarded. Tell me, what does a taster want? Gold? Land? A husband of good family?"

"Only to serve, Majesty."

"To serve." He stopped before a portrait of Aldric at thirty, painted in the full flush of his early reign—broad, laughing, a hawk on his gauntlet. The living king looked at the painted one for a long moment. "Yes. We know about service."

And then the voice changed.

It was still his voice—same throat, same instrument—but the music inside it shifted so violently that Mara's skin crawled. The new voice was low and ragged and *hurried*, a man speaking from underwater, from behind a wall, from the bottom of a well:

"—girl, listen, listen, there isn't time, it lets me up sometimes, little cracks, and this is one—"

Mara went cold from the scalp down.

The king's hand shot out—not toward her, but pressed flat against his own portrait, fingers spread, as if holding himself upright. His face contorted. The porcelain cracked, metaphorically; underneath was exhaustion, terror, a humanity so starved it burned.

"The map," the human voice rasped. "You have the map. I felt it come into the palace—it burns me, girl, it *burns*, which means it's true. Corren carried it. My courier. Did he—"

"He was dying," Mara heard herself say. Her voice came out steady. Six years of training. "He gave it to me. He said—thirteen turns. The shrine. Unbind him."

"*Unbind me,*" the king whispered. "Yes. Yes, that's it, that's the whole of it." He laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle like cheap crockery. "Seven years I have been in here. In the walls of my own skull. It wears my face at supper and I watch from underneath. Do you know what it eats, Taster? Not flesh. Flesh is nothing. It eats *agreement*. Everyone agrees, everyone smiles, everyone repeats what it puts in their mouths, and every agreement is a mouthful. My court is a banquet that has lasted seven years." His grey eyes found hers, and they were wet, and they were human, and they were the most frightening thing in the room because they were real. "You don't agree. You taste everything before you swallow it. That's why it noticed you. That's why I'm begging you."

"Begging me—" Mara's mouth had gone dry. "Your Majesty, I'm a taster. I test dishes. I don't—"

"You memorized every smuggler road through Thornfell." The human voice sharpened. "You think I don't know? I am the king. What remains of me is still the king. Vess trained you for roads and poisons both, and you learned them better than she dreamed, and now you hold a map to the one place in this kingdom that can end it." His grip on the frame whitened. "Find Caelan."

The name landed like a blade laid flat against her sternum.

Caelan. The exiled prince. The son banished seven years ago—seven years, she thought, *seven years, the same span*—in a fury so public and so cruel that the court had whispered about it for a season before the whispering itself had gone strangely quiet. She had seen Caelan once, at fourteen, from the gallery above the throne room: a boy with his father's dark hair and his mother's stubborn jaw, standing rigid while the king disowned him. She remembered thinking the boy hadn't cried. She remembered thinking she would not have managed that.

"The seal on the map," she said slowly. "It was his."

"My seal. Given to him when he was nine, so he could ride messages between me and his mother's kin in the east." The king's breath shuddered. "Corren was taking it to him. To the border towns, the river country—he drinks, they say, they say my son drinks himself blind, and God help me, I let that boy believe I hated him, because it was the only way to make him run far enough, fast enough, before—" The voice strangled itself. When it resumed, it was barely audible. "Before I was no longer able to protect anyone. Even by hatred."

Mara stood very still. Every part of her wanted to bolt—to run, to burn the map, to crawl back into the small safe life of tasting other people's food and calling it duty. But she had watched a man die on the Thornfell road with wounds no blade made, and he had spent his last breath pressing this burden into her hands, and she had carried it this far.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why tell me? Why let me leave Thornfell with it? Your—*it*—must have known."

"It knows." The king's face spasmed; for a moment the porcelain look slid back over it, smooth and blank, and then the human broke through again, weaker. "It's curious about you. Curiosity is its one weakness—it was bound once by people it underestimated. It wants to see what you'll do. It thinks curiosity is safe." A ghost of a smile, terrible and tender. "It doesn't understand poison-tasters. We're the ones who assume everything is unsafe."

"Your Majesty—Aldric—" She had never used the king's given name aloud in her life. It fell out of her like something stolen. "I'm one woman. I have a knife and a vial and a map I can barely read. What do you imagine I can do against something that's eaten your whole court?"

"Nothing." He said it simply. "Alone, nothing. That is why you must not be alone. Find my son. His blood opened the cage; his blood can end it. The binders wrote it into the mountain itself—royal blood freely given, at the thirteenth turning of the moon. There is one more turn, Taster. One moonrise left. After that the cage fails on its own, and everything it has eaten becomes a doorway, and this kingdom becomes a mouth."

Somewhere below them, deep in the bones of the palace, something groaned. Not settling stone. Not wind. A groan with appetite in it.

The king's spine straightened. She watched it happen—watched the human get shoved down like a drowning man pushed under, watched the porcelain rise, watched the eyes go grey-mechanical again. But this time she saw the struggle, and seeing it changed everything. All those years of serving this man's food, guarding this man's life, and the whole time the man had already been dead—or worse than dead, buried alive in his own crown—and no one had tasted it. No one had caught the one wrong note.

Because the wrong note was the whole song.

"There is one thing more," the king said, and the silken voice was back, layered now, both voices braided together so that she couldn't tell which spoke next. "You will speak of this audience to no one. Not to your mistress. Not to the servants. Especially not to Vess."

Mara's blood chilled. "Majesty?"

"We know what Vess is," the two voices said in unison, softly, almost kindly. "We have known for years. She feeds us, Taster. Little treasons, like sugar to a tame bear. Ask her sometime what she fed us the winter your parents' village burned." The king smiled, and the smile was pure Veil, wide and white and empty. "Or don't ask. Sentiment is such a heavy thing to carry on a long road."

She couldn't breathe. *Vess.* Vess who had pulled her out of the orphan pens at eleven, who had taught her to read poison in the curl of steam off a broth, who had held her hair back the night of her first near-fatal dose and told her, gruffly, that weakness got girls killed. Vess, feeding the thing in the crown. Keeping it fed. Keeping the court *stable.*

"You may go," the king said.

She didn't move. "You're letting me leave."

The mechanical eyes fixed on her, and something ancient looked out through them, unhurried, amused, a spider regarding a fly that had just done something interesting in the corner of its web.

"We are letting you leave," it said, "because we wish to see. Will you run and burn the map like a sensible animal? Will you go to your mistress and be folded back into the web? Or will you do the mad thing—the human thing—and go looking for a drunkard prince in the river country with one knife and one vial?" The smile widened a fraction. "Whatever you choose, we learn. And we have waited seven years, little taster. We can wait one moon more. Run along."

Mara curtsied—her knees nearly failing her on the way down—and walked the length of the Amber Gallery with her spine held straight by sheer professional discipline, past portraits of dead kings floating in drowned amber, past her own reflection ghosted faintly in the glass, small and pale and carrying a secret that weighed more than the crown itself.

At the door she paused. She didn't mean to. Her body simply stopped, and she turned, and looked back at the figure standing before his own painted youth.

"Aldric," she said quietly. "When this is done—when you're free, if you're free—I'll taste for you again. Properly. One honest meal."

For a heartbeat, the porcelain cracked all the way through, and the man inside looked at her with an expression she would carry for the rest of her life—gratitude and grief and a love so starved it hurt to witness.

"Hurry, girl," King Aldric whispered. "The moon is already thinning."

* * *

She made it back to the tasters' wing without meeting a soul, though twice she heard footsteps keeping pace just beyond the reach of the torchlight, stopping when she stopped, resuming when she moved. Escort or surveillance. With the Veil, she suspected, there was no difference.

Inside her room she barred the door, dragged the satchel from under the floorboard, and sat on her cot with the map tube across her knees, shaking so hard the leather rattled.

One moon. Thirteen turns, and twelve already fallen behind them like doors closing. Somewhere in the river country a drunkard prince was drinking himself blind under a false memory of his father's hatred, and she had perhaps four weeks to find him, convince him, and climb a mountain guarded by trials and a spymaster's web and a god wearing a crown.

Vess. She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. *Ask her what she fed us the winter your parents' village burned.* She couldn't. She wasn't sure she'd survive the answer either way.

But she knew one thing with total clarity, the way she knew a poisoned dish from a clean one: she could not stay in this palace one more day. The Veil was watching her with the patience of a thing that had eaten a kingdom, waiting to see what the interesting little fly would do—and the only move it hadn't predicted, the only move that was hers alone, was to stop being predictable.

Mara dressed in her road clothes. Dark wool, soft boots, the knife, the vial. She left the gold Vess had paid her stacked on the pillow—an old smuggler's courtesy, payment refused, debt declared. From the maps drawer she took her private charts of the river country, inked in her own hand over six years of "spice runs."

At the window she paused once, looking east, where the twelfth moon was setting thin and white over the mountains, and beyond the mountains, somewhere in the dark, a shrine was waiting with its cage nearly broken.

"Hold on," she said—to a dying courier, to a buried king, to a prince she'd met once across the height of a throne room. "I'm coming."

She went out through the kitchen gardens, where she knew which flagstones covered the old coal tunnels, and by the time the palace bell rang the hour before dawn, Mara Joubert was already three miles down the river road, riding hard toward the exile she'd been sent—or chosen, she no longer knew the difference—to find.
