## 6. The Exile's Price

The rain in Harrowgate came down like a judgment nobody had bothered to read aloud.

Caelan Aldricson—no, he'd stopped signing himself that years ago; just Caelan now, or *you there* if the barkeep wanted his coin—sat at the corner table of the Drowned Meridian and watched the water crawl down the window like it was trying to escape the town faster than he could drink his way out of it. Seven years in this border sinkhole and he still hadn't managed either.

"Another," he said, and the barkeep—Bramm, a man built like a dock piling with the conversational range of one—refilled the cup without comment. That was why Caelan drank here. Bramm didn't ask questions. Bramm didn't ask for anything, really, except coin on the first of the month and an occasional hand hauling casks from the wagon bed. Caelan paid both debts gladly. Work gave him a reason to be sore in the morning that wasn't memory.

The cup before him was his fifth. Or sixth. The counting had gone soft somewhere after the third, which was the point of the exercise. Drink enough and the past blurred at its edges, softened into something almost bearable—a painting of a life rather than the thing itself. His father's face. His father's voice. *You are no son of mine.* The courtyard, the mud, the two guards gripping his arms while the king—he had still been the king then, still warm, still human, whatever he'd become since—turned his back with the finality of a door closing on a tomb.

Seven years, and Caelan had never once wondered whether the words had sounded strange even as they left his father's mouth. He hadn't let himself wonder. Wondering was a wound you kept open on purpose.

He reached for the cup and found his hand unsteady—not from drink, though there was plenty of that, but because the door of the Meridian had opened, letting in a knife of gray light and cold air, and a woman walked through it.

She was small, dark-haired, dressed like a spice merchant's clerk with road dust to her knees, and she moved wrong for a clerk. She moved the way cats move through rooms full of dogs: aware of every body in the space, cataloguing exits without appearing to look at them. Her eyes swept the tavern once, dismissed it, and landed on him with the weight of something thrown.

Caelan looked away first. Old instinct. Princes who stared got recognized, and princes who got recognized got reported, and reports traveled east to a father who did not want to hear from them.

Except his father was seven years and half a kingdom away, and dead men didn't read reports.

He didn't know that yet. All he knew was that the woman crossed the room like she owned the deed to it and sat down across from him without asking, which no stranger had done since the second year of his exile, when a bounty hunter had tried exactly that trick and left with three broken fingers for his trouble.

"You're drinking alone," she said.

"It's the only company I can afford."

"I've watched you for two days." She said it flatly, no coyness in it, no apology. "You buy six cups a night and four of them go warm. You haul casks for Bramm at dawn so he'll keep serving you past what your purse earns. You walk the river wall every evening at dusk like a man pacing a cell he's forgotten the walls of."

Caelan turned the cup slowly on the scarred wood. "And you're either a very dedicated creditor or a very bored spy. Which is it?"

"My name is Mara Joubert." She leaned forward, and up close her eyes were the brown of strong tea, and they held something he hadn't seen directed at him in seven years: not recognition, not contempt, not fear of what he represented. Just urgency, worn raw. "I need you to listen to me, and I need you to do it sober, and I know—" she glanced at the row of cups, the puddled rings on the table, "—that may take some arranging."

"Then you've wasted a trip from wherever people waste trips from."

"The palace."

His hand stopped moving. The cup sat still, and the rain filled the silence with its patient static.

"Nobody comes *from* the palace," Caelan said. "Not here. Not to me. There's a standing order—"

"I know what the standing order is. I know what your father said in the courtyard. I know about the mud and the guards and the way the whole court watched and nobody spoke." Something flickered across her face when she said it—something that suggested she knew more about courtyards and silence than her spice-clerk clothes admitted. "I'm not here on his behalf."

"Then on whose?"

"On yours." She pulled something from the satchel at her hip and laid it on the table between them: a leather map tube, scuffed, water-stained, tied with a cord that had been knotted and reknotted until the leather beneath was worn soft. "Though I suspect whoever wrote this would say the same."

"I don't open packages from strangers. It's how you end up in a river."

"Then don't open it. Look at the seal." She loosened the cord and drew out just the edge of a rolled parchment, enough to show the wax stamped at its foot. And Caelan felt the floor of the world tilt, gently, the way a boat tilts when you shift your weight wrong.

He knew the seal. Of course he knew it. A stag courant beneath a crescent moon—the sigil he'd pressed into letters he'd sent home in the first year of exile, begging, pleading, receiving nothing. The sigil he'd carved into a beam over his bed in the garret above Bramm's stable because a boy of nineteen, bleeding out his belonging into foreign mud, needs something to hold onto or he drowns.

"That's mine," he said quietly.

"It's dated eleven months ago," Mara said. "Which is impossible, unless someone carried it a very long way. Or unless the man who sealed it isn't where everyone thinks he is."

* * *

They took the room upstairs because Bramm's tavern had one, because the rain showed no sign of stopping, and because Caelan refused—with the last shred of dignity available to a drunk in a border town—to have this conversation where dockhands could hear the word *king.*

He sat on the room's single chair. She took the bed's edge, map unrolled across the mattress between them, and he drank nothing, which made the shaking worse and the world sharper, and both felt like cruelties.

It wasn't a map of roads. He'd seen a hundred maps of Thornfell—his tutors had beaten geography into him before they'd beaten anything else—and this was not that. Roads appeared only faintly, ghosted under lines that meant nothing until Mara traced them with her fingertip and explained: the moon. The moon's path, month by month, thirteen stations marked in a fine, obsessive hand, each annotated with dates and tide-tables and observations of star-rise, converging toward a point deep in the mountains drawn with unusual care. A shrine. And at the foot of the parchment, in ink darker than the rest:

*the crown is the cage.*

"Cage," Caelan repeated. The word tasted like copper. "Whose cage? What's in it?"

"That depends on who you ask," Mara said. "Ask the court, and there's nothing in it at all. Ask the man who sealed this—" she tapped the stag-and-crescent, "—and I think you already know his answer."

"I don't know anything. That's rather the theme of my life." He stood, because sitting felt like drowning, and went to the window where the rain ran down the glass and Harrowgate lay gray and drowned below. "My father banished me. Whatever game this is—whatever the spymaster's web, whatever faction wants the exiled prince as a flag to rally around—you've come to the wrong ruin. I owe that crown nothing. Less than nothing. He looked at me in front of the entire court and unmade me with one sentence, and I spent the first year out here waiting for a letter recalling me, and the second year hating myself for waiting, and every year since learning to stop wanting things. You will not sell my own blood back to me at interest. Find another banner."

Silence behind him. Then, softly: "When did you leave the capital?"

"You know when. Seven years ago. Everyone knows when. It was quite the spectacle."

"Do you remember how he looked?" Her voice stayed level, gentle in a way that was somehow worse than cruelty. "In the courtyard. Not angry. Did he look angry?"

Caelan opened his mouth to say *yes,* because the answer was yes, had always been yes, was the load-bearing wall of everything he'd built out here in the wet gray ruins of himself—

And found he couldn't.

Because he remembered. He remembered with terrible, sudden clarity the thing he'd spent seven years not looking at directly: his father's face in that courtyard had not been angry. It had been *afraid.* Wide-eyed, sweat-sheened, hands trembling on the armrests of the ceremonial seat, staring at his son the way a drowning man stares at the thing dragging him under—as if Caelan's presence hurt him, as if the sentence *you are no son of mine* had been torn out of him rather than spoken. As if something else had been steering his mouth.

"He was ill," Caelan said. His voice came out strange. Distant. "Everyone said. A wasting sickness. He was not himself for the last year of—that was the talk of the court."

"And the year after?"

He turned. "The year after, I was here. I don't know what happened the year after. That is what exile *is.*"

"No," Mara said. "Exile is being alive somewhere else while everything happens without you. Three years ago the court stopped receiving foreign ambassadors' grievances. Five years ago the queen mother's household was dissolved and her servants reassigned—quietly, all at once. Six years ago the king stopped eating in public, and the tasters' hall doubled in size, and nobody thought that was strange." She paused. "I work in the tasters' hall. I've worked there since I was ten years old. I am the reason three nobles are alive last month instead of dead of hemlock in the wine, and the king watched me save them, and the way he watched me—" She stopped herself. Drew breath. Started again, quieter. "Something is wrong in that palace, Caelan. Wrong in the bones of it. Your father sealed this map eleven months ago, which means part of him is still in there, still fighting, and it chose to fight by sending word to the one person it could trust to hate the crown too much to want it."

"That's mad."

"Yes."

"That's the reasoning of a madwoman. Or a traitor."

"Also yes," she said, without flinching. "Both, probably. I've been a traitor for three days now and it suits me better than service ever did."

He laughed—it escaped before he could stop it, short and cracked, the first laugh in longer than he cared to count. She didn't smile. That was the thing about her, he was beginning to understand: she didn't perform. Every word cost exactly what it appeared to cost.

"There's more," she said. "There's a message. Not written. Spoken." She looked down at the map, and for the first time since she'd walked through the Meridian's door, her composure showed hairline fractures. "Your father—what's wearing your father—I was brought before him privately, two nights ago. He spoke to me in two voices. One belonged to the Veil, the thing in the crown. The other—" her hands closed slowly in her lap, "—the other begged. It said: *find my son. Tell him it was never his name I renounced. Tell him the last thing I said to him was a lie told to save him, and the truest thing I never got to say was that I loved him.*"

The room went very quiet. The rain kept on, indifferent.

Caelan found he had gripped the windowsill hard enough to whiten his knuckles, and made himself let go, finger by finger. "You could know that," he said hoarsely. "Anyone could know it. Servants talk. The courtyard was full of—"

"Full of ears, yes. But those words weren't said in the courtyard, were they? They were said to you after. In his study. The night before the banishment became official. When you went to beg him privately, one last time, and he wept, and you told no one, because princes who report their fathers weeping get their fathers' enemies as well as their fathers' thrones." Her eyes lifted to his. "I shouldn't know that happened. Neither should anyone. But he does. He remembers it. It's one of the only things he remembers, and it's keeping him human inside that cage, and he is spending what's left of himself to send it to you."

Caelan sat down. Not gracefully; his legs simply stopped participating in the project of standing. The chair scraped. Somewhere below, Bramm shouted at a delivery boy, and the ordinary world went on with its ordinary noise while Caelan Aldricson's whole architecture of grievance—seven years of it, mortared with cheap wine and self-pity and the fierce clean warmth of justified anger—shivered, groaned, and began to come down.

*A lie told to save him.*

Save him from what? From the same rot that had eaten the court's smiles hollow? Had his father looked at his son across that study, seen the thing in the crown turning its attention toward the boy, and done the cruelest kindest thing a dying man could do—driven him out with hatred so convincing that the crown itself would believe the son worthless, unreachable, not worth consuming?

*You are no son of mine.* Spoken like a blade. Meant like a shield.

"Oh," Caelan said. It was not a word so much as a structural failure.

Mara waited. She was good at waiting, he could tell—she had the patience of someone whose profession required swallowing poison and holding still, though he didn't know yet that this was literally true.

"If I say no," he said finally, staring at the map, at the thirteen moons marching toward their shrine. "If I ride east tonight and forget you ever found me. What happens?"

"Thirteen turns of the moon," she said. "Twelve are nearly spent. On the thirteenth, the binding fails entirely, and whatever has been drinking the court drinks the rest—maybe the city, maybe the country. Your father dies twice: once as a prisoner, once as a memory. And you live out your exile in Harrowgate, buying six cups a night and drinking four, pacing a cell you've forgotten the walls of, until the walls come to find you anyway."

He should have bristled at that. Instead it landed like an honest blow between old friends—clean, deserved. She had watched him two days and read him like weather, and there was mercy in that, the merciless kind.

"I hated him," Caelan said. His voice was wrecked and he let it be. "Every day. It was the only thing I owned."

"I know."

"He was a good king. Before. He used to carry me on his shoulders through the autumn gardens and tell me the names of all the trees in the old tongue, and none of them were right, and I loved him so much it was embarrassing." The words came out of him like water from a cracked dam, seven years backed up. "And then he faded. Slowly, that last year. Like a fire going down. And I couldn't—I *ran*, do you understand? Banishment was just running with witnesses."

"I understand," Mara said. "Believe me. I've been running my whole life; I've simply been doing it inside the palace walls."

He looked at her then—really looked, past the road dust and the urgency and the terrifying steadiness—and saw someone else carrying an impossible weight because no one else would pick it up. Saw, absurdly, an ally. The kind you didn't choose. The kind that chose you.

"How long," he asked, "until the thirteenth turn?"

"Nine days. Perhaps eight. The moon is unreliable near the mountains."

"Nine days to cross Thornfell in winter, pursued by—whoever it is pursuing us—reach a shrine that may not exist, and break a binding older than the kingdom using my blood." He rubbed a hand over his face. "This is the worst plan I've ever heard, and I once heard my uncle propose invading the sea."

"So you'll come."

He was silent a long moment. Outside, the rain eased, and a knife-blade of late sun cut through the cloud and lit the map on the bed, the thirteen stations gleaming like a rosary, the stag-and-moon seal burning gold.

"I'll come," Caelan said, and heard in his own voice something he hadn't heard in seven years—not the drunk, not the exile, not the ruin. His father's son. "But I'm bringing the rest of this bottle, because if I'm going to die in the mountains for a crown I don't want, I intend to be extremely unpleasant about it."

For the first time, Mara Joubert smiled. It transformed her face entirely, and Caelan understood, dimly, that he was in a great deal more trouble than the Veil had put him in.

Then she stood, rolled the map with brisk hands, and said: "We leave before dawn. And Caelan—pack nothing you'd grieve losing. We're riding the smuggler roads, and I know exactly which ones, because I've been sourcing poison on them for six years."

The bottle froze halfway to his mouth.

"The *what* roads?"
