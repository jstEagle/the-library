## 7. Vess's Web

The summons came the way Vess always sent them: a plain gray card slipped beneath Mara's door, no seal, no signature, only a single line in her mentor's cramped hand.

*Come to the rookery before the second bell. Come alone.*

Mara held the card for a long time after reading it, watching candlelight gutter across the paper as though the words might rearrange themselves into something kinder. They did not. The rookery was where Vess kept her pigeons and her secrets in equal measure, a round stone room at the top of the west tower where the wind never stopped talking. It was where Mara had learned to pick locks, to read a man's gait for the weight of hidden steel, to swallow poison with a smile. It was, in every way that mattered, the closest thing she had ever had to a childhood home.

And she could not stop thinking about what she'd seen on the Thornfell road. The courier's flesh smoking like banked coals. The map tube warm in her hands. *Thirteen turns. The shrine. Unbind him.*

She had spent three days telling herself the dying man had been mad. Three days of tasting dishes at the king's table and finding nothing wrong with any of them—nothing wrong with the food, at least—and pretending that the hollow-eyed servants were simply tired, that the nobles who repeated one another's sentences word for word were merely dull, that the king's habit of sitting through entire banquets without touching his plate was some new fast, some penance, anything but what the map's annotations whispered in her memory late at night: *the crown is the cage.*

Vess would know what to do. Vess always knew what to do.

That was the thought Mara clung to as she climbed the tower stairs, and it was the thought that made what came afterward hurt so much worse.

* * *

The rookery smelled of straw and droppings and old rain. Pigeons murmured in their wicker cages along the curved walls, hundreds of soft throats cooing in the dark, and beyond the open archers' slits the city of Caelmere spread out below like spilled jewelry, all gold lamplight and silver river. Vess stood at the central table with her back to the door, sorting dispatches by lantern light, and for one suspended moment Mara saw her exactly as she had seen her at nine years old: a tall, spare woman with iron-gray hair bound back so tightly it seemed to stretch the worry from her face, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hands stained with ink.

"Close the door," Vess said without turning. "And bar it."

Mara barred it. The sound of the bolt sliding home was very loud among the pigeons.

"You went to Thornfell." Vess still hadn't turned around. Her voice was level, unhurried, the voice she used when she already knew every answer and was only measuring how much of the truth the other person knew. "You told me you were sourcing saffron from the lowland brokers."

"I was sourcing saffron." Mara kept her own voice steady. Six years of training; her face was a locked box even now. "I sourced saffron. I also found a courier dying on the switchback above Miller's Ford."

Now Vess turned. In the lantern light her face looked older than Mara remembered it looking even a week ago—the deep lines around her mouth carved sharper, the eyes set deeper, as if something inside her had been slowly withdrawing, packing its belongings, preparing rooms for departure. "Describe him."

"Wounds no blade makes. His skin was hot enough to blister my fingers. He burned from the inside." Mara took a breath. "He gave me a map tube and said four words before he died. *Thirteen turns. Unbind him.*"

The silence that followed was long enough that Mara heard individual pigeons shifting in their cages, heard the wind worrying at the slits in the stone, heard her own heart doing something slow and heavy against her ribs.

Vess set down the dispatch she was holding. She smoothed it flat with both palms, an old nervous habit Mara had never once seen her admit to. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—not softer, exactly, but stripped of its armor, and somehow that was worse.

"So it's begun early," Vess said. "Or perhaps it never stopped, and we've all just been very good at not counting."

"What's begun?" Mara asked, though some cold, clear part of her already knew. Some part of her had known since the banquet, since the Veiled King had turned those under-blinking eyes on her across a hundred candles and smiled with a mouth that moved a half-second behind the smile itself. "Tell me what's begun, Vess. Tell me what I'm carrying in my pocket right now."

"The map." Vess extended one hand, palm up. An order dressed as a request, the way all her orders were. "Give it to me, child."

"I want to know what it is first."

"It is the reason you are still alive," Vess said quietly, "and quite possibly the reason everyone else in this palace dies. Give it to me."

But Mara didn't move. She stood with her hand resting over the pocket where the leather tube lay against her hip, and she looked at the woman who had pulled her out of a foundling cart at eight years old, who had taught her letters with a riding crop's handle pointing at chalked words, who had held her hair back the night of her first poisoning and said, *this is how we save lives, little bird, this is how you matter*—and she made herself ask the question properly.

"How long have you known?"

Vess's hand lowered slowly. For a moment Mara thought she would lie. She watched the lie assemble itself behind her mentor's eyes—watched the practiced calm reach for it like a tool from a rack—and then, visibly, Vess let it go.

"Seven years," she said. "Since the winter the king changed."

* * *

What followed was not a conversation so much as an excavation. Vess talked, and Mara stood by the barred door with her back against cold iron, and the pigeons murmured their mindless comfort around them while everything Mara believed about her life was dug up and turned over like grave soil.

"He died in the twelfth month of that year," Vess said. "Not assassinated—that's the official story, and it's even half true, because what killed him wanted killing. He rode up the mountain alone with six of his most trusted men and came back down alone, wearing the crown, and he was not alone inside anymore." She laughed, a dry sound with nothing in it. "You know what the worst part is? For months I congratulated myself. The king seemed *better*, I told myself. Sharper. More decisive. A man recovered. It took me until spring to understand that decisiveness isn't the same thing as a soul."

"And you told no one."

"Who?" Vess spread her hands, and the lantern threw her shadow huge and broken across the pigeon cages. "The council? Half of them were already gone by then—I didn't have the word for it yet, but I could see it happening. Lords who'd argued with Aldric for thirty years suddenly agreeing with everything. Ladies who wept at nothing and then couldn't remember why they'd wept. Servants standing in corridors at odd hours, faces tipped up toward the throne like sunflowers finding the sun." Her voice dropped. "You learn to see it or you learn to be it, Mara. Those are the choices this palace offers. I chose to see."

"And the treasons." Mara's mouth was dry. "The small ones. The ones you've been feeding me to carry to the king's informants for years—the misrouted shipments, the bribed customs men, the little betrayals you always called *pressure valves*. That wasn't spycraft, was it. That was feeding."

Something flickered across Vess's face—shame, maybe, or its fossil. "It keeps it calm," she said. "It wants transgression. Treason is its bread, did you know that? Not blood—blood is trivial to it. What it feeds on is the breaking of oaths, the corruption of loyalty. Every small treason I let slip through these walls is a treason it consumes instead of going looking for larger ones. I have spent seven years keeping it fed and fat and *bored*, and in exchange it has left the court functional. Children still get born here. Harvests still come in. The kingdom runs." She stepped closer, and her voice gentled into the tone she'd used on Mara's fevers, on her nightmares, on every wound of Mara's childhood. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am not its servant. I am its jailer. And the jail does not fall because one dying madman pressed a piece of leather into your hand on a mountain road."

"The map shows the moon," Mara said. "Thirteen turns of it, ending at a shrine in the high Thornfells. It bears Caelan's seal."

"Caelan is dead to us. Caelan has been dead to us for seven years, and if he were wise he'd stay dead." Vess reached out and took Mara's wrist—not cruelly, never cruelly, but with the absolute authority of the woman who owned every lockpick callus on Mara's fingers. "Give me the map. Burn it, if you prefer; I'll stand beside you while it burns. But it doesn't leave this tower, and you don't speak of it again, and in the morning you go back to tasting soup and being invisible, because invisibility, little bird, is the only safety this place has ever offered anyone."

Mara looked down at the hand on her wrist. At the ink stains. At the woman who had been mother, master, and country all in one.

"You knew," she said. Her voice came out very small and very cold. "All those years. You watched me swallow poison for him—six years, Vess, you watched me build a graveyard in my own blood—for a thing wearing a dead man's face. You let me kneel to it. You let me love it."

"I let you survive," Vess said sharply. "That is what mothers do. We choose which truths our children can carry, and we carry the rest ourselves until our backs break." Her grip tightened, just slightly. "Which is why I am asking you—one last time, as the person who taught you everything—to give me that map."

And Mara, who had been raised to read the space between words the way other people read words themselves, finally heard what lived underneath Vess's voice. Not love. Not even fear.

*Urgency.* A jailer's urgency. The urgency of someone whose prison had just developed a crack and who needed the crack sealed before the prisoner noticed.

"No," Mara said.

* * *

She saw the change come over Vess's face and understood, in the same terrible instant, that she had miscalculated everything. She had expected grief, or anger, or the wounded dignity of a betrayed teacher. What she saw instead was calculation—swift, cold, professional—and beneath it something that might have been sorrow but was moving far too fast to be felt properly.

"Mara—"

Mara was already moving. Six years of tasting had taught her that the body knows danger before the mind consents to name it, and her body had been screaming since the moment the bolt slid home. She went sideways off the wall, knocking the lantern from the table—it burst on the flagstones, oil and flame skidding—and in the sudden chaos of shrieking pigeons and leaping shadow she had the door unbarred and half open before Vess's voice cracked across the tower like a whip.

"Hold her!"

Boots on the stair below. Two of them, maybe three—Vess had posted them before the summons, of course she had, the summons itself had been the trap's soft jaws—and Mara did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat: too many bodies between her and the bottom, so she went up. Through the rookery's upper hatch, into the belfry loft where the great bronze bell hung silent and moth-eaten ropes swayed, and out onto the lead roof of the west tower with the night wind shoving at her like a drunk friend.

Below, the palace glittered. Below, somewhere in that glitter, the Veiled King sat in a room full of food he never ate, and waited, patient as rot, to see what she would do.

*Mara!* Vess's voice, raw now, torn open at last. *Mara, please—you don't know what you're carrying!*

She did, though. That was the unbearable thing. She was beginning to.

She went down the outside of the tower the way she'd gone down a hundred times in training—the ivy course on the north face, the ledge above the armory, the drainpipe that Vess herself had once joked was the palace's most loyal spy. Her palms tore on the ivy stems. She barely felt it. By the time she dropped into the kitchen court, her wrist was bleeding where Vess had gripped it, and the map tube was still against her hip, warm as a living thing.

* * *

The keys were almost laughably easy.

That was the horror of it, she thought later, crouched in the dark of the steward's office with a stolen ring of iron biting cold against her thigh: it was easy because Vess had built the system, and Vess had built it to be survivable. There was a key for every corridor, a route for every emergency, a bolt-hole behind every wall—and Vess had drilled them into Mara across ten years of games disguised as lessons, *show me you can cross the palace unseen, little bird, show me you can vanish,* never saying why, never admitting there might come a day the lesson would save her life from Vess herself.

The steward's office smelled of tallow and ledgers. Mara lifted the key ring from its hook behind the wine cabinet—the third hook, the real one, not the decoy—and paused there a moment in the dark, listening to her own breathing.

Betrayal, she discovered, was not the sharp thing she'd imagined. It was slow. It worked like poison, in fact—she recognized the progression perfectly. First the numbness at the edges, the refusal to believe. Then the heat rising, the body's furious denial. Then the long cold settling in, the understanding that the damage was done and irreversible and that the only question left was whether you'd live long enough to build an immunity to it.

She thought of Vess's face in the lantern light. *I am not its servant. I am its jailer.* And underneath the words, older and quieter, the truth Vess had not said: *I have been in this cage so long I've forgotten which side of the bars I stand on.* Something had been eating Spymaster Vess for years, bite by patient bite, and it had never needed the Veil to do it. Duty was enough. Duty, swallowed daily in doses too small to kill, until the woman who took it could no longer tell the difference between protecting the kingdom and protecting the cage.

*I won't become that,* Mara promised the dark. *Whatever else I do. Whatever it costs.*

Somewhere above, bells began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the strange arrhythmic pattern the watch used when the palace was being sealed. Doors locking. Gates closing. Somewhere in the dark, Vess's people were fanning out with descriptions of a girl with bleeding palms and a leather tube, and somewhere above them all, in a high room full of untouched food, something ancient and patient turned its attention toward the west tower and found it empty.

Mara slid the steward's key into the office door's inner lock—the one that opened onto the servants' passage behind the kitchens, the one that led, eventually, down and out, through the bones of the palace to the postern gate and the river road and the long gray miles toward the border town where a disgraced prince was drinking himself to death.

She had a map of moons, a stolen ring of keys, and no plan worth the name.

But she knew every smuggler's road through the Thornfells better than anyone alive, and she knew, now, exactly whom she had to find.

The passage swallowed her. Behind her, faint through the stones, she heard Vess's voice one last time—calling her name into the empty rookery, over and over, in a tone Mara had never heard from her before and would spend many nights afterward trying not to hear again.

It sounded like a mother calling a child home to a burning house.
