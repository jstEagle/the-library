## 8. Smuggler's Roads

The rain came down the mountain in sheets, and Mara loved it.

Not the cold that soaked through her cloak to the skin, not the mud that sucked at her boots with every step—but what the rain did to pursuit. Scent died in it. Tracks filled in. And the enforcers, whatever they were, moved by something other than scent, but even things that hunted by wrongness needed *something* to follow, and a storm gave them nothing but noise.

"Tell me again," Caelan said behind her, breath ragged, "why we're climbing *up*."

"Because down is where they'll look." She didn't turn. Turning cost time, and time was the only coin she had left to spend. "Every road out of Thornfell funnels into three passes. The king's men hold all three. But smugglers don't use roads."

"And smugglers use—"

"The places roads can't go." She found the handhold by memory, a root thick as her wrist jutting from the cliffside, and hauled herself up onto the ledge above. "Sheep tracks. Old quarry cuts. A drainage channel the miners dug two hundred years ago and abandoned when the silver ran out. There's a way through the mountains that fits inside a shadow, if you know where the shadow falls."

Caelan pulled himself up beside her, and for a moment they both stood gasping under the drumming rain, the valley below them drowned in gray. He looked terrible. Three days of flight had stripped the last softness from him—the drunkard's bloat gone from his face, leaving something harder underneath, cheekbones she recognized suddenly from a coin minted before his exile. She'd carried one as a child. Every child in the capital had. Prince Caelan, smiling on the silver penny, back when the palace still smiled honestly.

"You could have taken the passes," he said. "Faster. If you'd left me."

"If I'd left you, they'd have caught you by now, and then everything I've run for is worth nothing." She started along the ledge, testing each foothold. "Keep moving. We talk when we're walking or not at all."

He obeyed, which surprised her. The prince she'd heard stories of—the one who'd brawled with border lords and insulted ambassadors—should have bristled at being ordered about by a taster. But exile had done something to him, worn grooves into him the way water wears stone, and what remained seemed willing, at least, to be led.

They walked an hour before the first of them appeared.

Mara saw it from the corner of her eye and froze so abruptly that Caelan walked into her back. On the slope below—a hundred feet down, maybe more, through rain thick enough to blur anything—a figure stood among the pines. Black coat. Stillness like a held breath.

"There," Caelan whispered. "How did—"

"Watch its shadow."

The rain came from the west. Every tree on that slope leaned its shadow east, every rock, every broken branch. The figure's shadow fell *north*. It lay across the ground at an angle no sun had ever made, long and jointed wrong, and as Mara watched, the shadow shifted—slid across the wet stone like oil across water—while the body above it never moved.

"What in the old gods' names—" Caelan's voice cracked.

"It's been there since the ridge." Her mouth had gone dry despite the rain. "It doesn't walk. It *arrives*. Wherever the ground looks like ground it's already seen." She grabbed his sleeve. "Down. Off the skyline. Now."

They scrambled, slid, half-fell into a gully choked with fern, and Mara pressed them both against wet rock while her heart hammered out a rhythm she hadn't felt since her first year in the kitchens, when she'd learned what the king's food could hide. Below and behind them, she heard nothing—which was worse than hearing something. Things that arrived without walking made no sound arriving.

"They're Vess's people?" Caelan breathed.

"They were men once. Some of them." She thought of the spymaster's face in the lamplight, too gentle, too knowing. *Surrender the map, little wren.* "The Veil wears them the way it wears the king. Hollowed out and pointed at whatever threatens it. Which means—" she laughed, and it came out ugly, "—which means we're the most important thing in the kingdom right now. Congratulations, Your Highness."

"Don't call me that."

"Why not? It's what you are."

"It's what I *was*." His voice had an edge now, the first real edge she'd heard from him. "Seven years ago my father looked me in the eye and told me I was no son of his. That my mother's blood had rotted something in me. He put me on a boat with twenty crowns and told the harbormaster I was dead if I returned." His hands had closed into fists. "So don't ask me to feel anything about what's wearing his face. Whatever sits on that throne, it isn't suffering. It isn't *him*. It's just the thing that threw me away, wearing a better mask."

Mara said nothing for a long moment. The rain filled the silence.

"You saw its shadow," she said finally. "I've seen its eyes. Not enough blinking. And I've heard—" She stopped. Told herself not to say it. Said it anyway. "Two days before I ran, the king summoned me alone. And for about ten heartbeats, the man talking to me wasn't the thing in the crown. It was a tired old voice, and it begged. It said *find my son before the thirteenth moon*. It said *I am still in here, and it is eating me from the edges inward*." She watched Caelan's face as the words landed. "Then the other voice came back, silk and threat, and let me walk out because it was curious what I'd do. So I'm not asking you to love him. I'm asking you to consider that the father who banished you may have been already half-devoured when he said it—that those might not have been his words at all, but the cage rattling."

"No." Flat. Final. He turned away up the gully. "If I let myself believe that, then I have to forgive him, and I'm not—"

"I'm not asking you to forgive anyone. I'm asking you to climb."

*

They climbed.

Through the afternoon and into the failing light, Mara took them up routes that weren't routes: a stag trail barely wide enough for her boots; a shelf of black slate where she made him cross on his belly like a lizard; the drainage cut, finally, a slot in the mountain's flank just wide enough for their shoulders, running cold and dark and smelling of old iron. Twice she heard—or felt—something arrive behind them, a pressure in the air like a door opening in an empty room, and twice she took them sideways off the obvious path into tangles of deadfall where the wrong shadows couldn't follow. Whatever the enforcers tracked them by, it failed in chaos. They needed pattern. Smugglers dealt in the absence of pattern.

Dark caught them in a hollow beneath an overhang, a dry pocket in the wet stone with room for a fire the size of two cupped hands. Mara built it small and screened it with her cloak, and they ate hard bread and dried venison and drank snowmelt, and for a while neither spoke.

It was Caelan who broke first.

"In the gully. What you said. About the voice begging." He was staring into the flames. "Say it again. Exactly."

"*Find my son before the thirteenth moon.*"

"And after that?"

"After that it said—I am still in here. It is eating me from the edges inward." She turned the words over carefully, handing them to him whole. "And then, quieter, almost like it was ashamed: *tell him the banishment was the map. Tell him I sent him away because the thing wanted him close.*"

Caelan went very still.

"That part you left out before."

"Because you weren't ready to hear it. You'd have called me a liar, or worse, called it a trick—and maybe it is a trick. Maybe the Veil wants you found and is using your father's ghost as bait." She hugged her knees, watching him across the tiny fire. "I don't know which is true, Caelan. I only know what I heard, and I've spent six years learning to tell poison from food. That voice was poisoned. It was also *in pain*."

"My father never sent me anywhere in his life." But the flatness had gone out of his voice. Something had cracked in it, hairline-fine. "He kept me close. Kept me *leashed*. Every post I wanted, he blocked. Every alliance I tried to build, he unraveled. I thought he hated me, and I ran because—" His jaw worked. "Because watching him fade was unbearable. He started dying years before he stopped breathing. Everyone saw it and no one named it. And I couldn't stand in that palace one more winter watching a great man become a hollow one, so I picked a fight I knew would get me exiled, and I let him do it, and I told myself it was his cruelty when it was—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "When it was both of us being cowards together."

Mara let the silence sit. In the kitchens, she'd learned that grief was like a trapped bird—you didn't grab at it. You waited, and kept the room quiet, and eventually it settled on its own.

"The map shows thirteen moons," she said at last, gently. "Twelve turns of the path, and the thirteenth ends at a shrine in the high Thornfell. The seal on it is yours. Whoever carried it died getting it to me, burned from the inside out by wounds no blade makes. Someone believes you can unbind this thing. Someone who knew your father, maybe. Or was your father." She fed a twig to the fire. "You don't have to decide tonight what's true. You just have to decide whether you want to know. Those are different decisions."

"And which are you making?"

"Both. Every day since the courier died in my arms." She looked up at him. "I swallowed poison for six years so that strangers wouldn't die at their own tables. Don't ask me why I'm doing this. It's the same reason wearing a different coat."

Something shifted in his face then—not belief, not yet, but the ground belief grows in. He looked at her the way she imagined he'd once looked at maps of territories he intended to win: reassessing. Recalculating where she stood.

"You knew the courier route," he said. "The smuggler roads. All of it. How does a palace taster come to know the mountains better than the army does?"

"Because someone has to source the poisons." She smiled without humor. "Rare toxins grow in rare places, and the crown can't send soldiers to buy them. So they send the girl whose tongue can tell nightshade from hellebore at forty paces, and they assume she's too frightened to run. Vess trained me on these roads herself. Every cache, every safehouse, every farmer who'll trade silence for salt." The smile faded. "Vess taught me everything except how to see her clearly. That I had to learn the hard way, same as you."

"She raised you?"

"Found me in a debtors' gutter when I was nine and sold me to the palace kitchen myself. Fed me, clothed me, taught me knives and herbs and which nobles lie with their left hand." Mara turned her hands over, palms up, scarred faintly with a hundred small burns and nicks. "Six years ago she sat me down over a cup of wine and explained my new duty. One grain of arsenic in the morning. A leaf of this, a drop of that, building up slow, until my blood became a lock no toxin could pick. She held my head when I vomited the first month. She told me I was saving lives." Her voice hardened. "Maybe she believed it. Maybe that's the cruelest thing about her."

The fire crackled. Somewhere far below, too far to be real, too real to be dismissed, something howled—not wolf, not wind, a sound with structure in it, like a word stretched until it snapped.

"We should sleep in watches," Caelan said.

"You take first. You'll hear them coming; I won't. My ears are full of the palace still." She wrapped herself in her cloak and lay back against the stone, and just before sleep took her, she heard him say, quietly, to the fire or to her:

"Mara. The voice. When it begged—it used my name?"

"It said *my son*. It said *find my son*." She was already half under. "Why?"

"Because my father never once, in my whole life, called me *his son* to my face." His voice was very low. "Only in letters he never sent. I found one once, in his desk, when I was sixteen. *To my son, whom I cannot keep.*" A long pause. "I burned it. I was so angry I burned it."

Mara slept, and dreamed of a crown turning slowly in dark water, and woke once in the deep of night to find Caelan sitting watch with his sword across his knees, awake and silent, looking east toward the capital as if he could see through the rain and the miles and the walls to the thing on the throne. As if he were counting moons.

*

Dawn came gray and thin, and with it, the second hunter.

Mara spotted it while packing the fire's ashes—a figure on the ridge above their hollow, black-coated, motionless. But this one was different. This one's shadow lay correctly beneath it, and it simply *stood*, and after a moment it raised one arm, and the arm was wrong, too long, joints bending in directions arms don't bend, and it pointed—not at them, but past them, deeper into the mountains. Toward the shrine.

Then it stepped backward off the ridge and was gone, the way a candle goes out.

"It's herding us," Caelan said. His hand hadn't left his sword hilt. "Or warning us. Can you tell the difference anymore?"

"No. And that's the point." Mara shouldered her pack and looked at the ridge a long moment. "It wants us to go forward. Which means forward is either salvation or a trap, and the Veil knows which and we don't." She started climbing. "But here's what it doesn't understand about smugglers, Your Highness. We never take the road we're pointed down."

"Stop calling me that."

"Make me."

Behind them, far below, the storm had cleared from the valleys, and in the clean morning light Mara could see, strung out along the distant pass roads, small dark figures walking in perfect unison. Dozens of them. All their shadows falling north.

Caelan saw them too. He stood a moment, watching, and when he spoke his voice had changed overnight—something burned away, something else settling into the ash.

"How many days to the shrine?"

"Four, if the weather holds. Five if it doesn't." She glanced back at him, and found him already looking at her, and neither of them smiled, but something passed between them anyway—an agreement older than words, the kind two people make when they've decided, without deciding, to survive the same direction. "Walk fast, Caelan. The twelfth moon rises tomorrow night."

And far behind them, at the edge of hearing, the mountain began—very softly—to groan.
