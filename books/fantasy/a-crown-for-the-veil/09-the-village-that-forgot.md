## 9. The Village That Forgot

The village appeared out of the morning mist the way all Thornfell villages did—suddenly, and as if it had been waiting to be discovered. Grey stone houses with slate roofs furred in moss, a crooked bell tower, smoke rising from a handful of chimneys in thin, obedient columns. A stream ran beneath the road on its way down from the high passes, loud and cold and utterly indifferent to the two riders who had slept badly in the trees above it.

Caelan reined in at the crest of the lane and looked down at it, and something in his chest tightened with an ache he couldn't immediately name.

"I know this place," he said.

Mara pulled her horse up beside his. She looked tired—she had been tired for three days now, ever since the shadows on the ridge behind them had moved wrong—but her eyes were sharp as always, cataloguing the village below with a smuggler's instinct: exits, sightlines, the number of able-bodied men visible. "That's not possible. You said yourself you've never come this far east."

"I haven't." He heard the strangeness in his own voice and couldn't smooth it out. "But there's a well under that leaning elm, third house past the shrine-post. There's a carving on its rim—a stag, with antlers like branches. My mother's sigil." He swallowed. "I've never been here, Mara. But I know where the water is."

She studied him for a long moment. Six days on the road together had taught him to read her silences: this one meant she was deciding whether to say something true or something useful. She usually chose true when it was worse.

"Then maybe it isn't you remembering," she said quietly. "Maybe it's him. Your father's blood in your veins knows roads you've never walked." She nudged her horse forward. "Or maybe every village in these mountains looks the same. Either way, we need bread, feed for the horses, and a night indoors before the rain comes. Keep your hood up."

He kept his hood up.

The village was called Brindlemere—he learned that from the sign nailed above the first crossroads, though the paint had faded so thoroughly that the name seemed more suggestion than declaration. And from the moment they passed beneath it, Caelan began to notice what he would later think of as the wrongnesses: small things, each forgivable alone, but accumulating like drops of water filling a cup.

There were no old people working. He saw a woman hanging laundry who might have been fifty; he saw a man splitting wood with the easy strength of middle age. But no one bent with great age. No white hair, canes, shawls. In a mountain village, where the winters thinned families like frost thins windows, that absence should have been impossible.

There were no graves older than seven years. The churchyard beside the bell tower was small and neat, and he counted the headstones from the saddle without meaning to—eleven stones, all of them weathered identically, all of them softened by the same number of seasons. As if time itself had begun recording here only recently, a fresh page torn from a very old book.

And there was the shrine-post at the crossroads. Every Thornfell village had one—an upright stone carved with the old symbols of hearth and harvest and the turning year. Brindlemere's post had been defaced. Not broken, not toppled. *Scraped*. The carvings had been worn smooth deliberately, sanded down by patient hands until the stone was blank as an egg. Someone—or everyone—had decided, together, that the old signs should go.

"Nobody's looking at us," Mara murmured. "You'd expect it. Strangers in a village this size. Dogs barking. Children running to gawk." She was right. The villagers went about their work with their heads down, moving through their morning like dancers following steps rehearsed so long ago the music had been forgotten. A woman drew water. A man led goats. Another man—the same build, nearly the same face—led goats. Caelan blinked, and the second goatherd was gone, or had never been, and he told himself the mist had doubled the man for a moment.

"The inn," Mara said. "If there is one."

There was. The Drowned Bell, according to a sign whose painted bell sat oddly in a painted lake, ringing silently forever. Inside, the common room was clean and warm and almost empty: two farmers drinking small beer over a silent game of dice, an innkeeper polishing a mug that was already polished. A fire burned in the hearth. Above it hung a portrait.

Caelan stopped just inside the door.

It was a portrait of a king.

Not Aldric—not exactly. It was older than that, cruder, the kind of likeness painted by someone working from memory or from another painting. A crowned man with a stern jaw and dark hair, and across the bottom, in letters that had once been gilt: *Long Live the King.*

Seven years of dust lay on the frame. Seven years, and not one hour more.

"Sit," Mara breathed at him, low, and steered him toward a table with her hand at his back. "Innkeeper. Two of whatever's hot, bread if you have it, and oats for two horses. We're spice-buyers, up from Callow's Ford, headed over the pass."

The innkeeper smiled. That was the thing about the smile—it arrived fully formed, warm and correct, without passing through any expression that preceded it. "Of course. Travelers are always welcome in Brindlemere. You'll be wanting to rest before you climb. The mountain's been groaning at night. Old bones settling, most likely." He set down mugs of cider neither of them had ordered. "On the house. For travelers."

Caelan kept his eyes on the table. Under his hood, his heart was hammering in a way he despised, because he could not tell whether it was fear or grief.

*Old bones settling.* The mountain had indeed been groaning—he and Mara had felt it through their bedrolls two nights past, a deep tectonic complaint from the peaks around the shrine. But a goatherd in Brindlemere should not know that. Should not mention it with that same pleasant, pre-formed smile.

*They're all connected*, he thought. *The palace and the post-road and this kitchen with its dusty king. One body. One appetite.*

Mara talked easily with the innkeeper—she could talk easily with anyone, it was one of the thousand things about her that unsettled him—and extracted, between pleasantries, the shape of the village's recent history. Yes, Brindlemere had known hard times. No, she couldn't recall precisely when the wool trade had failed. No, her grandmother hadn't left any stories; nobody's grandmother had, really; the old ones had all passed, and it seemed such a shame, didn't it, that so many had gone in the same few years? A fever, perhaps. Or the winter. It was hard to remember winters now. All the winters ran together after the seventh year.

"After the seventh year," the innkeeper repeated, to no one, smiling at the fire. "It's strange how everything before gets foggy. Best not to dwell. Dwelling gives you headaches."

Caelan looked at the dice players. One of them threw, and both of them laughed—at exactly the same instant, with exactly the same sound, a laugh like an echo arriving before its voice. Then they gathered the dice and threw again and laughed again, identical, and Caelan understood that they were not playing a game. They were performing the memory of one.

*This is what the court looks like,* he thought. *This is what Mara tried to describe. I thought she was speaking of a palace. The palace is just where it eats best.*

His hands had gone cold around the mug. He was six years exiled and seven years grieving and he had taught himself to feel nothing at the sight of suffering too large to fix—that was what exile was *for*, he had told himself, it was a mercy granted to a prince too weak to watch. But this was not suffering. Suffering would have been easier. This was subtraction. An entire village with its past scooped out like the flesh of a fruit, and no one left inside who knew enough to mourn.

Except—

The old woman came in from the rain.

She was the first truly old person Caelan had seen since they entered the village: bent nearly double, wrapped in a shawl of undyed wool, her face a map of lines so deep they seemed carved rather than grown. The innkeeper's smile flickered—the first crack in anything Caelan had seen here—and he said, gently, too gently, "Mother Sorrel, you shouldn't be out. You'll catch your death."

"And where would I catch it?" she asked, in a voice like dry leaves. "I've nowhere to put it. Nothing stays."

She shuffled toward the fire, and Caelan made himself look away, made himself study his cider, because staring was dangerous and they were strangers and strangers who stared got remembered—

She stopped in front of him.

He felt it before he saw it: the stillness of her, the sudden gravity. When he raised his eyes her face was tilted up at him, rain-wet and ancient, and her pale eyes were searching his shadowed features with an intensity that had nothing vacant in it at all.

"Oh," she said softly.

"Grandmother," Mara said smoothly, rising, "our friend here is shy of company—"

"You have his chin." The old woman's voice cracked on the word. Her hand rose, trembling, and hovered near Caelan's jaw without touching, the way one hovers before a flame or a relic. "You have his chin, and the way you sit—shoulders back, like the chair might be taken from under you—oh, my lord, oh, *my lord*—" And then she was weeping. Not politely. Terribly, wrenchingly, the way people weep at gravesides, the whole small cage of her ribs shaking with it, tears cutting channels through the dust-dry lines of her face. "I don't know you," she sobbed. "Forgive me, I don't know you, I don't know why—seven years and everything's fog and I can't find the *beginning* of me—but I know your face, I know it, I know it like the well knows water—"

The innkeeper was already moving. "Mother Sorrel." His voice was still gentle. That was the horror of it—how gentle it stayed. "You've wandered again. Come, sit by me. You're confused. There's no lord here. Just travelers."

"There *is*—" she wept, even as he took her shoulders, even as he turned her, feather-light, impossibly strong, toward the far bench. "There is, there is, why do they keep saying there isn't—"

"He's confused her," the innkeeper told the room apologetically, seating her, tucking the shawl around her with terrible tenderness. "She does this. Sees faces in strangers. It's the age, poor soul. She has no family left to speak of." He patted her hand. "No one left at all, really."

And Mother Sorrel looked up at him, mid-tears, and agreed. "No one left at all," she echoed, wondering, and the weeping simply stopped, shut off like a tap, and she smiled the village smile and asked the innkeeper whether it would rain all day.

Caelan could not move. Could not breathe. Mara's boot pressed down on his under the table—once, twice, an anchor—but the pain reached some distant country where he was not living.

*I know your face like the well knows water.*

Seven years ago, a boy-prince had ridden east through these mountains in disgrace, and somewhere along this road—perhaps this very road—the villagers had lined up to watch him pass. He remembered none of it; exile blurred into a single grey wound. But they would have watched. A prince riding to banishment was the greatest spectacle Brindlemere had seen in a generation. Children would have run alongside. Mothers would have lifted them up.

And then something had come out of the palace and eaten every memory of him, and left only the shape of the feeling behind, like a footprint fossilized in stone. They had forgotten his name, his crime, his father's decree. But an old woman's heart had kept hold of his *face* the way a drowning man keeps hold of driftwood, because hearts are deeper than heads and harder to hollow, and for seven years some splinter of her had been waiting by the fire for a boy with a familiar chin to walk in out of the rain.

*It reaches beyond the walls,* Mara had said, the night they'd fled the palace, when he'd still believed the Veil was a court disease, a poison confined to the throne room and the banquet hall. *How far beyond, we won't know until we see it.*

Now he saw it. The crown was the cage, the map had said. But the cage was leaking, had always been leaking, and everything within reach of the leak forgot. How many villages? How many valleys? Was the whole kingdom slowly becoming Brindlemere—a nation of smiling people performing memories they no longer possessed, keeping portraits of kings they could not name, while the thing in the crown grew fat on everything subtracted?

*My father is in there,* he thought, and the thought was not abstract anymore, not a cause or a quest or a duty. *My father is screaming inside that thing, and it is eating his people's lives to keep him quiet, and I am sitting here drinking cider.*

He stood. Mara caught his sleeve; he let her, barely.

"Mother Sorrel," he said.

The old woman turned. For a heartbeat her eyes were hers again—flooded, frightened, ancient—and Caelan knelt on the common-room floor of the Drowned Bell, hood falling back, letting her see all of his face in the firelight, because she had carried his face for seven years through the fog and she deserved one clear minute of it.

"I don't remember you either," he said quietly. "But I think—I think I'm sorry for that. I'm sorry for all of it. Whatever I was to you, whoever I was, I hope I earned the tears. I'll try to be worth them."

Her mouth trembled. She raised her hand and touched his cheek, feather-light, and whispered: "Come home before the last moon turns, lad. Whoever left you out there will want you back. Mothers always do."

*I know,* he almost said. *I know he wants me back. He's been trying to tell me through dying couriers and stolen maps.* Instead he covered her hand with his own and held it for a breath, and then Mara was drawing him up, murmuring something about rooms and rest, and the innkeeper watched them climb the stairs with that warm, finished smile, and called after them pleasantly:

"Sleep well! The mountain's been groaning, but old bones settle. Everything here settles. Nothing ever leaves Brindlemere."

The words followed Caelan up the stairs like a hand on the back of his neck.

The room Mara had bargained for was small, with one window overlooking the churchyard. She barred the door, checked the shutter, and only then turned to him, arms crossed, waiting. She had learned, in six days, when to wait.

"It's everywhere," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "It's not a court, Mara. It's not a king gone wrong. It's in the *roads*. In the wells. That woman—" He stopped, pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "She wept for me and didn't know why, and I stood there and let her, because I needed her to. What kind of—"

"She'd have wept anyway," Mara said. "Grief doesn't ask permission. Believe me." She crossed to the window and looked down at the churchyard, at those eleven evenly-weathered stones, and her voice dropped to the flat, fast register she used when she was thinking aloud and didn't want pity for it. "Eleven graves, all the same age. No elders but her, and her half-hollowed. That portrait dusted with exactly seven years. Caelan—this village didn't get sick. It got *harvested*. Regularly. Like a taster's diet." She turned. "Small doses. Steady doses. The whole kingdom is being fed to it a little at a time, and the palace is just the mouth."

Through the floor, faintly, the common room hummed with conversation—warm, pleasant, and underneath it, if you listened the way Mara had taught him to listen, perfectly rhythmic. Dozens of voices, rising and falling together, like breathing.

"We leave tonight," Caelan said. "Before the moon's up."

"Agreed. We cut north through the birch—"

The bells began.

Not the cracked bell of the Drowned Bell's sign—the real bell in the crooked tower, which had hung silent all morning. It tolled once, deep and slow, and the conversation below stopped mid-syllable, every voice ceasing at once the way a candle goes out. Then it tolled again. And Caelan, looking down through the window slats, saw them come out of the houses into the street—all of them, the whole village, unhurried, unafraid, faces tilted up toward the mountains. Toward the northeast.

Toward the shrine.

"They've never done that," Mara whispered beside him. Her face had gone the color of the mist outside. "The bell. I asked the innkeeper if they still ring for festivals. He said the rope rotted years ago."

The bell tolled a third time, and far off in the high passes, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, the mountain groaned—and this time, unmistakably, the groaning sounded less like settling stone and more like something answering.

Mother Sorrel stood alone in the middle of the street, the only villager not looking at the mountain. She was looking at their window. And though they were three floors up behind a shutter's gap, though no human voice could carry it, Caelan heard her clearly—heard her the way he had heard the courier's dying message, the way he was beginning to understand his blood heard things.

*"Run, my lord,"* she said, smiling gently up into the rain. *"It knows your name now."*
