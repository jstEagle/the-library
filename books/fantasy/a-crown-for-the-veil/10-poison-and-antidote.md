## 10. Poison and Antidote

The river came out of the mountains the way all things in Thornfell did—sideways, stubbornly, and with no respect for the roads men tried to impose on it. Mara heard it before she saw it, a low constant murmur beneath the pines, and felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't realized was wound tight. Water meant a boundary. Water meant the enforcers' wrong-shadowed shapes would have to cross it slowly, and slow was the only advantage she and Caelan had left.

"We camp on the far bank," she said, reining in at the tree line. The crossing below was a sprawl of grey stones and white water, maybe forty feet of it, the kind of ford smugglers called *the Washerwoman* because it scrubbed tracks clean off any stone you stepped on. "There's a hollow up the ridge—old charcoal burner's camp. No one's used it in years."

Caelan slid down from his horse beside her, moving like a man whose body had become an inconvenience he tolerated. Three days of hard riding since they'd fled the palace, three nights of cold camps and cold silences broken by conversations neither of them knew how to have. He looked older than his twenty-five years, the exile's stubble darkening his jaw, his eyes carrying that particular hollowness she was coming to hate—the same hollowness she'd spent her whole life training herself not to see in the servants at court.

"You're sure it will hold them?" he asked. Not *if*. He'd stopped asking *if* somewhere around the second night, after the thing wearing a huntsman's body had paced them along a ridgeline for an hour, its shadow bending toward them across a valley no shadow should have been able to cross.

"No." She dismounted, knees protesting. Six years of swallowing poison built a strange kind of endurance—not strength, but a grim capacity for feeling awful and continuing anyway. "Nothing holds them. But water slows them, and slow is enough if we're gone before dawn."

They led the horses down to the ford. The light was failing, autumn sun bleeding out behind the peaks so that everything went the colour of old pewter. Mara chose her stones the way she'd been taught at eight years old, Vess's rough voice still living in her head: *wet stone lies, dark stone dies, trust the one that rings.* She hopped the first few, listening to the ring of each landing, and let the rhythm of it carry her thoughts downstream.

She was halfway across when she understood that the river had gone quiet.

Not stilled—rivers didn't still—but quieted, the white noise of it dropping as though something enormous had pressed a hand over its mouth. Mist gathered along the banks where no mist should be, low and deliberate, curling against the current.

"Mara." Caelan's voice from behind her, very flat. "Don't turn around yet."

She turned around anyway. She'd never been good at doing what she was told; it was practically the founding principle of her life.

Three figures stood on the south bank. They wore the shapes of palace guardsmen—she recognized one vaguely, a man named Dorran who had once held a door for her and complimented her mother's memory, back when anyone at court could still be said to have a mother. But their shadows lay wrong on the ground, pooled beneath them like spilled ink refusing to dry, stretching toward the water in thin grasping filaments. Their faces were smooth in a way faces shouldn't be, the features present but somehow unoccupied, like rooms swept for guests who were always about to arrive.

The mist thickened. And then the foremost figure simply *wasn't* on the bank anymore—it was on the first stone of the ford, and the river hadn't splashed, hadn't noticed, and every instinct Mara owned screamed at once.

"Run," Caelan said, already wading, graceless and fast. "Mara, *run*, don't fight it—"

But the third figure had flanked them. It came out of the pines upstream where she'd sworn there was nothing, and it moved with that terrible economy, closing the bank between her and the far side, cutting her off from Caelan as neatly as a knife divides an apple. She heard him shout her name and heard the water swallow it.

The thing before her raised Dorran's hand. The fingers were too long now, jointed like a heron's leg, and the shadow beneath it rose off the ground and reached for her the way smoke reaches for a chimney draft.

She had a knife. It was, she understood immediately, about as useful as a spoon. She drew it anyway because dying with a spoon in your hand seemed worse somehow, and she braced on the wet stone with the white water roaring around her calves—

—and the shadow touched her.

It happened fast, and it happened strangely, and afterward she would spend a long time turning it over in her mind like a stone with a fossil inside. The darkness closed over her wrist—and *recoiled*. Snapped back the way a hand snaps from a stove, the whole shadow-shape shuddering up into the air like a startled bird. The thing wearing Dorran's face made a sound. It was not a scream, exactly. It was the sound of a scream being remembered by something that had forgotten how.

Then it came apart. That was the only word. The shape of the guard unraveled from the feet upward into ribbons of dark that streamed away on the wind over the water, and what hit the stones where it had stood was just a man—an ordinary dead guardsman, eyes open, face slack and human and terribly pitiful, as though whatever had ridden him had dropped him mid-stride and fled.

The mist tore. The river roared back to full voice like a held breath released.

Caelan was beside her. She didn't remember him crossing; she only registered his hands gripping her arms, hauling her back from the waterline, his voice saying her name over and over with increasing urgency until she understood he'd been saying it for some time.

"I'm fine," she said. Her own voice sounded distant. "I'm—look at my arm."

He looked. So did she. Where the shadow had touched her, above the cuff of her sleeve, her skin showed a pale mark—a flare of white, fading even as they watched, like frost melting off glass. It didn't burn. It didn't crawl. It was already nothing.

"It touched you," Caelan said. His hands were shaking. "I watched it touch you. Nothing touches it. I've seen it take men through armor, through walls—"

"I know what I've seen it do." She was looking at her own wrist as though it belonged to someone more interesting. "It took the courier on Thornfell Road without ever laying flesh on him. His blood smoked right through his skin. But this—" She flexed her fingers. The mark was gone entirely. "It pulled away from me. Like a horse that scents a cliff."

They stood in the shallows while the light died around them, and the enormity of what had almost happened and what hadn't settled over them both like the cold coming off the water.

"The bank," Caelan said finally. "We need the far bank. Now. Before anything else remembers how to walk."

They crossed fast and badly, hands linked at the worst of it, and drove the horses up the switchback trail to the burner's hollow—a shallow overhang walled with dry-stone, floored with decades of pine needles, invisible from below unless you knew to look. Mara built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. Caelan saw to the horses with mechanical care. And then he sat down across from her, elbows on knees, and looked at her with an expression she couldn't read, and said nothing for a long time.

"Say it," she said eventually.

"I've been trying to find the words." He turned his hands over, studying his palms as if the words might be written there. "Six years, Mara. You told me. Six years they fed you poison, drop by drop, dish by dish, because a taster who can be killed is no use to anyone. You told me what the arsenic nights were like. What the mercury did to your hands those first years. You told me all of it on the road like it was just—biography. Like telling me which roads flood in spring."

"It is biography."

"It's *arsenal*." He leaned forward, and the firelight caught his eyes, and she saw that the hollow look was gone from them, burned out and replaced by something fierce and rapid, a man thinking at speed. "Don't you see what happened at the river? The Veil can't wear you. Can't touch you. Can't consume you. Everything we've seen it do—every horror on this road, every hollow-eyed servant, everything it did to my father—it does by *touching* people. Getting inside them. Wearing them. And it put one finger on you and it *flinched*."

"That's very flattering to my wrist," Mara said, "but one lucky flinch isn't a weapon."

"Isn't it?" He stood, pacing the length of the hollow, restless as the river. "Think about what you are. Every dose it couldn't kill you with, it taught your blood instead. Your body learned poison the way a scholar learns languages. You are six years of accumulated toxicity walking around in a dress, and the oldest thing in this kingdom took one look at you and recoiled." He stopped. Turned. "You're not just immune, Mara. To that thing, you're venomous."

She wanted to laugh. She opened her mouth to do exactly that, and found that instead her throat had closed, and her eyes were burning, and she had to look away at the fire because Caelan was watching her with that terrible open kindness and she could not—could not—be seen to come apart over a sentence.

Because here was the thing she had never said aloud, not to Vess, not to the mirror, not to anyone: for six years she had believed her suffering was waste. Poisoned, tested, survived, poisoned again—a girl consumed drop by drop to keep fat nobles alive through banquets, and for *what*? For a king who never ate. For a court that smiled too much. She had told herself it was service, and then told herself it was survival, and underneath both stories ran the cold true one: that it meant nothing. That her ruined childhood bought nothing, proved nothing, was worth nothing except another morning in which the palace continued to eat itself and she continued to be the spoon that fed it.

And now an exiled prince was standing in front of her saying: *your ruin is the one blade in the world that cuts it.*

"Don't," she managed.

"Don't what?"

"Don't make it noble." Her voice came out lower than she intended, scraped raw. "I didn't choose it so it could turn out useful. I was nine. Vess gave me the choice between the kitchens and the taster's bench and told me girls in the taster's bench got beds and girls in the kitchens got other things, and I picked the bed, and then I spent fifteen years learning to smile while my tea tasted like copper. If my blood kills that thing, good. Wonderful. But don't tell me the suffering was for something, because it wasn't. Nobody chose it *for* me and nobody chose it *with* me. It just happened, the way weather happens."

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, the river kept talking to itself.

"No," Caelan said quietly. "You're right. Nobody chose it for you. Which means—" and he crouched down in front of her, deliberately, bringing himself to her level, and she hated how much she noticed that, "—you get to choose what it becomes. They took six years from you, Mara. They don't get to decide what those years mean. That's yours. It's the only thing they never managed to take."

She looked at him for a long moment. Firelight, wet wool, the smell of pine needles and horses. An exiled prince who had run from watching his father fade, offering her ownership of her own pain like it was something he could actually give.

"The trials at the shrine," she said at last, because her feelings were rising toward some surface she wasn't ready for, and work had always been her rope ladder out. "The courier's map. 'Unbind him.' Blood opens that door—royal blood, the seal was royal. But binding, unbinding—that's exchange. Something given for something taken." She turned her wrist over in the firelight, where the mark had been. "If my blood is poison to the Veil, and the crown is its cage..."

"Then the cage might be opened with the one key it can't swallow." Caelan sat back on his heels. A breath left him that was half laugh, half something heavier. "A taster and a traitor. My father would be appalled."

"Your father," Mara said gently, "is a cage."

"I know." He looked into the fire. "I know. It's just—today, at the river, when that thing fell apart—I saw the man underneath. Dorran. He held a door for me once, when I was twelve, and told me my mother's roses were the best in the western provinces. He was under there the whole time. All of them are under there." He was quiet a moment. "That's what it wants us to forget. That everyone it wears is still in there, screaming. Well." His mouth twisted. "Most everyone. It seems it made an exception for you."

"It didn't make an exception." Mara pulled her blanket around her shoulders. "It made a mistake. There's a difference, and I intend to survive long enough to rub its face in it."

That earned her a real laugh, short and surprised, the first in three days. It did something warm to the air between them, and she let it, and didn't examine why.

They took watches. She woke once in the black hours to find Caelan asleep sitting up against the dry-stone wall, head tipped back, fire down to embers, and the ember-light moving on his face, and she permitted herself ten full seconds—counted, disciplined, taster's precision—to look at him without guard. Then she banked the fire and took her watch and let the thought go, the way she'd learned to let every dangerous thing pass her lips without swallowing.

Dawn came grey and clean. They were saddling the horses when Mara paused, one foot in the stirrup, and looked east toward the mountains where the shrine waited, days away still, up roads that appeared on no honest map.

"Caelan. The twelfth moon rises tomorrow night."

He followed her gaze. The peaks stood sharp against the paling sky, white-capped, indifferent, hiding their shrine the way a fist hides a nail.

"Then we ride," he said. "Hard as they'll bear it."

"And when we get there—" she swung up, gathering the reins, "—we're going to need rather a lot of my blood. So try not to spill any of it on the way."

They rode north into the mountains, two small figures on a road no king had ever mapped, and behind them the river ran loud and free, scrubbing their tracks from every stone.

But far to the south, in a palace where a thousand hollow-eyed courtiers stood facing a golden crown, something ancient turned its attention from a spymaster's report and considered, for the first time in seven years, the sensation of *pain*—and began, with infinite patience, to plan around the girl it could not touch.
