## 12. Turn Twelve

The twelfth moon hung over Thornfell like a coin worn thin by handling, and Mara could not stop watching it wane.

She stood at the mouth of the cave Caelan had found—a smuggler's cache, old Sable's Rest, its walls still marked with the charcoal tallies of men who had counted winters here the way she counted dishes—and she watched the light drain out of that pale disc night after night, one sliver at a time, as if the sky itself were being eaten from the edges inward.

*One turn left,* she thought. *Thirteen months, thirteen moons, and we have spent twelve of them running.*

Behind her, in the cave's cold heart, Caelan slept badly. She could hear it—the shift of his boots against stone, the half-voiced mutter of a man dreaming of his father's throne and a hundred versions of her death. He would not speak of the dreams. He had told her once, honestly, around a fire with his hands shaking, and since then he simply endured them, jaw set, eyes shadowed, as though sleep were another trial the shrine demanded of him before he'd even reached its doors.

Mara envied him nothing, but she understood endurance. It was the only language she'd ever been fluent in.

The mountain had begun to groan three nights ago. Not wind—she knew Thornfell's winds the way she knew the taste of arsenic, faintly sweet, deceptively gentle. This was deeper. A tectonic complaint, stone grinding against stone somewhere far beneath them, as if the mountain itself were a joint being forced to bend. The shrine was waking. Or the thing inside the crown was clawing at the last bars of its cage, and the cage was the mountain, and the mountain felt every blow.

*We are too slow,* she thought. *Or it is too fast.*

Either way, the thirteenth moon was coming whether they were ready or not.

She heard the horses before she saw anything else—too many horses, moving without the ragged rhythm of a patrol. This was a column. Disciplined. Silent except for hoofbeats, because hollow men did not talk to one another; they had nothing left to say.

Mara went down through the scree on silent feet, the way Vess had taught her when she was nine years old and still new enough to cry at night. *Move like water finding its level,* Vess had said, crouched beside her in the palace gardens, her gray eyes kind and sharp at once. *Nothing notices water until it's already everywhere.* The memory came with its usual small knife-twist, and Mara let it pass through her and out, the way she let everything pass through her. Six years of swallowing poison taught you that too: you did not refuse the pain. You metabolized it.

From behind a shelf of granite she looked down into the valley and saw them.

Two hundred, perhaps more. They moved in the starlight like figures in a dream someone else was having—too smooth, too synchronized, their shadows falling wrong in the moonlight, leaning east when the moon stood west. She recognized some of them. That was the worst part. There was Master Corvin, the palace steward, who had once slipped her honey-cakes on her name-day and told her she had her mother's stubborn chin. There was a kitchen girl whose name Mara had never learned but whose face she'd seen a thousand times across the steam of the serving line. Their faces were all the same now—not identical, but *emptied*, like rooms swept clean for guests who never arrived. Their eyes caught the moonlight and gave none of it back.

And at the head of the column, on a white mare that walked with its head bowed as if in mourning, rode Spymaster Vess.

* * *

Mara did not wake Caelan immediately.

She crouched in the dark and made herself think, because panic was a flavor she refused to swallow. Two hundred against two. But they were not soldiers, not truly—the Veil wore bodies, it did not wield them well. Hollow men did not flank. Did not feint. They walked forward, always forward, like water rising, and water could be channeled if you knew the ground. And she knew this ground. Every goat track, every collapsed mine shaft, every smuggler's cut through the Thornfell highlands was inked into her memory alongside the taste of every poison the crown's enemies had ever tried.

There was a way out. There was always a way out. She had built her whole life on that sentence.

But Vess was down there, and something in Mara's chest—the part of her that was still, despite everything, a nine-year-old girl waiting in the spymaster's study for a cup of tea and a lesson in cipher—wanted to understand before she ran. Wanted to know why. Wanted, God help her, to see if there was anything left behind those gray eyes worth saving.

So instead of retreating, she did the mad thing. She lit no torch, took no weapon but the thin blade at her hip and the vials sewn into her coat—her old arsenal, six years of immunity distilled into weapons for other people—and she walked down into the valley to meet her mother.

Vess reined in twenty paces away. Behind her, the column stopped as one body stops, all joints at once, and the silence that followed had weight, had texture, pressed against Mara's ears like deep water.

"You've grown thin," Vess said.

Of all the words in all the world, Mara had not prepared for those. Her throat tightened around something that was not quite laughter. "You've grown hollow."

"I eat." Vess dismounted, unhurried, her cloak settling around her the way it always had, gray wool smelling faintly of the cedar chest where she kept her maps. For one vertiginous moment Mara was back in the study, fourteen years old, learning which couriers could be bought and which only borrowed. "It doesn't matter anymore, but I eat. Habit is the last thing to go." She tilted her head. "You look well, little wren. The mountains agree with you."

"Don't call me that."

"I named you."

"You *branded* me." Mara's voice came out low and steady, and she was grateful for it. "Like everything else you own. What do you want, Vess?"

"The obvious." Vess gestured, a small economical motion, at the mountain looming black above them. "You will give me the prince, or you will walk away from him, and either way you will come home. The king has extended a pardon. Full pardon. Your name cleared, your service honored, a house in the capital with a garden—you always wanted a garden. You used to steal seed catalogs from my desk."

"I stole them because you never bought me any."

"And yet you read them cover to cover." Something moved at the corner of Vess's mouth—not a smile, not quite. An echo of a smile, the way an empty hall echoes a voice that has already stopped speaking. "A pardon, Mara. No one else alive has been offered one. Do you understand what that means? It means *it* wants you. It finds you interesting. In thirty years of feeding it treasons, I have never once been able to say that about anyone and mean it as a mercy."

"Feeding it." Mara took a step forward, and the hollow column behind Vess rippled, two hundred heads turning in unison, and she forced herself not to flinch. "You said stable. You said you kept the court stable. You didn't tell me you were the caterer."

"I told you what you needed to survive the telling." Vess's voice remained level, reasonable, the voice of a thousand debriefings. "Would the truth have changed what you did? Would you have run any faster knowing the full shape of it? No. You'd have frozen. You freeze when the horror is too large—I trained that out of you for assassination work, but grief undoes training. So I gave you a horror you could carry." She paused. "I am giving you one now. Hear it. The Veil does not want the prince dead. It wants him *willing*. It wants him to kneel at that altar of his own choice, and it wants you gone first, because your blood burns it and your presence makes him stubborn. You are the obstacle, little wren. Not the prize. Walk away, and he may yet live out his life as a comfortable puppet. Stay, and you both burn on that mountain for nothing."

"That's a lie."

"It's arithmetic."

"It's a lie with numbers in it." Mara's hand found the hilt of her knife, not to draw—just to feel the honest weight of steel. "I've spent six years tasting lies for a living, Vess. I know how they're seasoned. You always salt yours with truth. The garden. The pardon. The part about it wanting me willing—that part might even be real. But *comfortable puppet*?" She shook her head slowly. "I've seen the court. I watched three hundred people stand in the great hall at midnight with their faces turned up like flowers after rain. There is nothing comfortable in that house. There is nothing left in it at all. If I walk away, Caelan doesn't live. He just takes longer to be emptied."

For a long moment Vess said nothing. The wind moved through the valley, and the hollow column swayed very slightly with it, like wheat, like weeds, like things that had forgotten they were supposed to resist the wind.

Then Vess said, quietly: "When did you start calling yourself a person?"

"What?"

"You heard me." Those gray eyes fixed on Mara, and in them—for the first time—Mara saw something flicker. Not light. Not life, exactly. More like heat behind a shuttered window, a coal remembered rather than felt. "Six years old, sold to the palace with a number instead of a name. Nine years old, drinking poison so nobles could eat safely. Fifteen, killing a man in Vellmark because I told you to and you never asked why. I built you the way masons build arches, little wren—every stone placed to hold the others up, nothing placed for beauty. And you stood there tonight and called me a liar to my face, and grieved for me, and chose a broken prince over your own survival, and—" Her voice caught. Actually caught, a hitch like a thread snagging, and the whole column behind her shuddered as if the snag had passed through all of them at once. "And I cannot remember," Vess finished, barely above a whisper, "when I stopped doing that. When I stopped choosing. I have been trying to remember for seven years, and there is nothing there. There is a hole where it used to be, and the hole is shaped exactly like me."

Mara's breath left her slowly.

This was the moment, she understood dimly, that she should draw her blade. Vess was off guard. The Veil's grip had slipped—she could see it straining to close again, the gray eyes flattening by degrees, the humanity draining back out of that face like color from a fading cloth. One throw of the poisoned needle hidden in her sleeve, and the woman who had raised her, used her, betrayed her, and loved her in whatever broken currency the Veil had left her to love with, would fall. The column would scatter like a severed hand.

Instead Mara said: "What was your daughter's name?"

Vess went absolutely still.

"You never spoke of her. But there was a locket you wore under your collar, and once, when you thought I was asleep in the coach outside Bellharrow, you opened it and I heard you say a name. I was eleven. I pretended to be asleep because asking would have made it real, and real things got taken away." Mara stepped closer, close enough to see the fine tremor in Vess's hands, hands that had never trembled, hands that had taught her to hold a blade steadier than her own heartbeat. "The Veil eats memories older than seven years. That's what the village showed us. Everyone in it forgot everything before the binding. But you remember me at nine years old. You remember seed catalogs. You remember teaching me to move like water." Her voice cracked and she let it. "It can't take what you gave away. Love isn't stored in the mind, Vess. It's stored in what you *did*. And you did it for years. Whatever's left of you—it's made of me. Of us. Of every kindness you buried under tradecraft and told yourself was strategy."

"Stop." The word came out raw.

"Her name, Vess. Say her name. Prove there's someone home."

"*Stop.*"

But Vess's hand had risen to her collar—to the place where the locket had once lain, though the collar was bare now, the locket long since confiscated or consumed—and her fingers pressed against the fabric as if they could feel through it the small weight of a face she could no longer picture. Her mouth worked. Her eyes, those terrible flat gray eyes, filled with tears that did not fall, that seemed unable to fall, as if the machinery of weeping had been removed along with everything else and only the pressure remained.

And then the shutter closed.

Mara watched it happen—watched the flicker gutter and die, watched the gray eyes go smooth as river stones, watched Vess's spine straighten and her voice return to its level, reasonable register, saying: "You have until moonset. After that, we come up the mountain, and I am not permitted to offer twice."

The white mare turned. The column turned with her, two hundred bodies pivoting as one, and began to withdraw down the valley, unhurried, inevitable, water flowing backward toward its source.

At the edge of the starlight Vess paused. She did not look back. But her voice carried, thin and strange across the cold air, in a tone Mara had never heard from her—not the spymaster, not the hollow thing, but something older and smaller, something that had been locked in a cellar for seven years with its knuckles bleeding from knocking:

"The garden was real, little wren. I bought the seeds the winter before. I never found the courage to give them to you."

Then she was gone into the dark, and the mountain groaned beneath Mara's feet like a man turning over in a fever.

* * *

Caelan was awake when she climbed back to the cave, standing at its mouth with his sword drawn and his hair wild, and the relief on his face when he saw her was so naked and so young that it hurt to look at.

"They're leaving," he said. "I heard them. Why aren't we running?"

"Because running buys us hours, and we need days, and there's no road in Thornfell that sells them." Mara sank down onto a stone at the cave's edge and looked up at the twelfth moon, a fingernail paring of light now, almost gone. "They'll climb at moonset. Two hundred of them. We can't outrun that and we can't fight it." She pulled the map tube from her coat, unrolled the leather across her knees, and studied the final ascent inked there in Prince Caelan's careful hand, seven years younger and seven years more hopeful: the switchback trail, the outer door, the trials beyond it, and at the summit, drawn in a circle like a wound that would not close, the inner shrine.

"So we don't run," Caelan said slowly. He sat beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him, the only warm thing on the entire mountain. "We climb."

"We climb." She traced the last stretch of trail with one finger. "Tonight. Now, while they're regrouping. The outer door opens to royal blood—that's a gate they can't follow us through unless the Veil can wear *him*, and it can't, not yet, not while he's unwilling. That's why it wants him willing. The door knows the difference." She looked up at him, and found him already looking at her, and whatever was in her face made him reach over and close his hand around hers, rough palm against rough palm, two people who had both been built as tools and had decided, against all sense, to be more.

"Mara," he said. "Whatever happened down there—"

"She's still in there." Mara's voice was quiet. "That's the horror of it, Caelan. It's not a monster wearing her. It's a monster *inside* her, and she's in the cellar of her own body, listening to everything it says with her mouth. Seven years. Maybe longer." She closed her eyes briefly. "She bought seeds for me. She never gave them to her. She kept them in a drawer for a year and then she planted them herself in the palace garden and never told me why the marigolds came up."

Caelan said nothing. There was nothing to say, and he was learning, slowly, the rarest skill she knew: when silence was the only honest answer.

Above them the last sliver of the twelfth moon slipped behind the western ridge, and the darkness that followed was total, and in that darkness the mountain groaned again—longer this time, louder, a sound like a great door being forced—and far below, faint as breath, they heard the hollow column begin to move.

Mara rolled up the map, stood, and held out her hand.

"One turn left," she said. "Let's not waste it standing still."
