## 13. What the Veil Wants

The dream began, as it always did, with the smell of beeswax and cedar—the scent of his father's study, the room Caelan had loved best in all the world.

He was twelve again. He knew it even as he sat in the too-large chair across the desk from King Aldric, knew it was a lie the way a man knows he is dreaming: the candlelight fell wrong, pooling where it should have scattered, and the rain against the window kept no rhythm at all. But knowing did not loosen the dream's grip. Knowing only meant he had to watch himself believe it.

"You've grown," his father said, and smiled, and it was his father's smile—crooked on the left side first, then the right, the smile that had preceded every joke and every forgiveness of Caelan's childhood. "Too fast. Kings never get to be boys long enough."

"I don't want to be king," Caelan heard himself say, in his own young voice, high and unbroken.

His father laughed. "Good. Neither did I. That's how you know you'd be decent at it." He leaned forward, elbows on the desk scattered with maps and petitions, and for one terrible, sweet moment his eyes were clear—brown, warm, human, nothing veiled behind them. "Caelan. Listen to me. Whatever they tell you after I'm gone—"

The rain stopped. Mid-fall, mid-word, the sound simply ceased, as if the world outside the window had been struck dumb.

"—whatever they tell you," Aldric finished, and now the voice came out layered, doubled, a man speaking in unison with something that wore his throat like a glove, "the crown was never meant for your head. It was always meant for mine. Come and take it back."

Caelan woke with his hand on his knife and Mara's name half-formed in his mouth.

* * *

They were camped in the hollow beneath an overhang of grey stone, three days' climb below the shrine's outer door, where the pines grew twisted and the wind came down off the peaks smelling of iron and old snow. The fire was small; Mara insisted on small fires now, though the enforcers had not been seen since Vess's riders lost their trail at the river crossing. Small fires, she said, were harder to find than no fires, because no fires made a man look like he was hiding.

She slept the way she did everything—efficiently, lightly, her bedroll close enough to the rock wall that no one could come at her from behind, her knife loose in its sheath. Even asleep she looked like a woman doing arithmetic. Caelan had watched her for weeks now, across smuggler roads and cold camps, and he still could not decide whether that constant calculation was armor or simply what she was.

The moon hung above the notch in the ridge, two nights from full. The twelfth moon. He could feel it the way he imagined sailors felt weather in old scars—a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the quiet.

He did not sleep again that night. He told himself it was the cold.

* * *

The second dream came the next night, and this time the Veil did not bother with childhood.

It gave him the throne room.

He stood on the dais of the great hall at Caer Duloc, and the hall was full—not hollow-eyed and silent as he remembered it from his last visit seven years ago, but alive, roaring, banners of the stag-and-mountain hanging from every pillar. And on the throne sat his father. Whole. Hale. His beard trimmed, his shoulders square under the mantle, his hands—those broad, scarred hands that had taught Caelan to hold a sword—resting easy on the arms of the seat.

"Caelan." Aldric rose, and the hall went quiet, and every face turned toward the prince standing frozen by the doors. "Come here, boy. I've waited long enough."

"You're dead," Caelan said. His voice sounded very small in all that grandeur. "I burned the letters. I read the reports. You died seven years ago, or something wearing you died, and everything since has been—"

"Has been a kingdom at peace." His father descended the dais steps slowly, and the court parted before him like water, and there was such love in his face that Caelan's chest physically hurt. "Seven years without war. Seven years without famine. Seven years of harvests and marriages and children born who would otherwise have starved. Is that so monstrous a price? One old man's comfort, traded for a nation's stability?" He stopped an arm's length away, and up close Caelan could see the flaw in the illusion at last—the light in the hall came from nowhere, the shadows all pointed toward the throne instead of away from it. "I am not asking you to fight me, son. I am asking you to stop fighting. There is a difference, and it is the whole of politics."

"The court stands at midnight staring at the ceiling," Caelan said. "The servants don't blink. Villages forget their own dead. That isn't peace. That's a meal being eaten slowly."

Something flickered across his father's face—hurt, genuine and brief—and Caelan hated himself for how much it moved him.

"You always did see too much," Aldric said softly. "Your mother used to say you'd worry yourself into an early grave, seeing things that weren't there and things that were." He put out his hand. "Take my hand, Caelan. Take the crown when the time comes. Rule beside me. Nothing needs to break. Nothing needs to burn. You can have him back—" and here the voice split, just for a heartbeat, into the doubled register Caelan had learned to dread, the human voice and the other speaking together, pleading and silk at once, "—you can have your father back, whole, forever, if you simply stop. Stop climbing. Stop running. Stop helping her."

"There it is," Caelan whispered.

"There it is," his father agreed gently. "Every bargain has its shape. Mine is simple: her life for your surrender. Walk away from the shrine. Let the thirteenth moon rise over an unclaimed mountain. And in exchange—" the hall brightened, impossibly, warmly, "—everything else stays exactly as it is. Or better. I can make it better. I can make it *right*, Caelan. I can give you the father you mourned. All I need is for you to want it."

And that was the poison in it, Caelan thought, standing in the beautiful lying light. Not the threat. The wanting. It didn't need to force him. It only needed him to be a man who missed his father—which he was, which he had been since he was seventeen years old and rode out of Caer Duloc with banishment ringing in his ears, believing until the map and the courier and Mara that his father's last words to him had been contempt.

"That's not him," he said. "You're not him. He's in there somewhere—Mara heard him, she told me, a voice begging for help—and you're the thing sitting on top of him."

"A thing," the king said, and sighed, and the sigh was so perfectly, heartbreakingly paternal that Caelan's eyes stung. "Son. When you're older, you'll learn that every crown is a thing sitting on top of a man. Most men just die before anyone notices."

The hall began to dim at the edges, like a page curling in fire.

"Think about it," his father said, receding, smiling, growing smaller and more beloved with every foot of distance. "You have until the moon turns. Everyone does."

* * *

The third night, the Veil stopped offering him anything at all.

Instead it showed him Mara dying.

A hundred futures, strung together like beads on a wire, and the wire ran through his sleep without mercy. Mara falling from the shrine's heights, her body turning over and over against the snow. Mara white-lipped on an altar, blood pouring from her opened arm faster than any taster's craft could explain, her eyes finding his with the last of her strength and holding not fear but apology—as if dying were a failure of professional courtesy. Mara cut down by Vess's soldiers on the mountain road, and this time the dream let him arrive too late, let him kneel in the slush and hold her while the light went out of her face with the same efficiency she brought to everything, even this. Mara old, Mara young, Mara drowned, burned, poisoned—poisoned most of all, poisoned in ways that made no sense given six years of immunity, as if the dreams knew that particular death would wound him worst.

In every future, the cause was the same: him. His plan, his blood, his claim, his stubborn hope. She died because she carried the map. She died because she believed in the unbinding. She died because a disgraced prince needed saving from his own grief, and she was the kind of woman who saved people even when it cost her everything, and the Veil wanted him to understand—with the clarity that only cruelty achieves—that her death was not a risk of the mission.

It was the price. And the Veil was willing to pay it, in futures, over and over, until he flinched.

He woke before dawn with his heart slamming and the taste of ash in his mouth, and found Mara already awake, feeding the fire, watching him with those level dark eyes that missed nothing.

"You're sleeping worse than I am," she said. "That's an achievement. I'm usually the champion."

"Mara."

"Yes?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Three days of dreams sat in his throat like swallowed stones, and he understood suddenly why the Veil had chosen this weapon—not because Caelan was weak, but because honesty was the one currency he had never learned to counterfeit. He could lie to courts, to councils, to himself. He could not lie to her. He had tried, on the road, about small things, and she had caught every falsehood within a sentence, the way she caught poison.

"If I tell you something," he said finally, "will you do me the courtesy of not deciding what to do with it until morning?"

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "That depends on what it is."

"It's what the mountain wants me to know."

That got her full attention. She set down the fire-tongs and turned to face him fully, cross-legged, firelight carving her face into planes and shadow. "Tell me."

So he told her. All of it. The study and the boyhood and the doubled voice. The throne room and the offered hand and the bargain dressed as mercy. And last—the hardest, and he made himself say it plainly, looking at her, because she deserved plainness—the hundred futures, and the single thread that ran through every one.

"In every version," he said, "you die. Not sometimes. Every time. It wasn't threatening me, Mara. It was teaching me. It wants me to understand that the only way to keep you safe is to stop. Walk away. Let the thirteenth moon rise. And it knows—I know—that some part of me would trade the whole kingdom for that. That's what it's counting on. Not my ambition. My—" He stopped. The word sat between them.

"Say it," Mara said quietly.

"My love for you." The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the pines with a sound like distant applause. "There. It's said. Against all sense, against every lesson exile taught me, I love you, and the thing on the mountain has done the arithmetic, and it knows that a man who loves you can be bought with your life. So now you know what I am going into those trials carrying. If you want to send me down the mountain tonight, I'll go. If you want to finish this alone, I'll draw the enforcers off and buy you the days. Just—decide quickly. Because every hour I spend loving you is an hour it can use."

Silence. Long enough that the fire settled twice.

Then Mara reached out and took a stick from the woodpile, and drew in the ash between them: a circle, and inside it, a smaller mark like a drop.

"Do you know what this is?" she asked.

"No."

"It's the mark the palace kitchen master chalked on my door when I was nine years old. Sold to the palace, orphaned, useless except for one thing—they'd discovered I could taste hemlock in wine the way other people taste honey. So they fed me poison. Every day, a little more, for six years. Do you know what that does to a child?" Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "It teaches you that survival is a thing you build yourself, dose by dose, out of the very thing meant to kill you. It teaches you that fear is information, not instruction." She looked up. "The Veil showed you a hundred futures where I die. Fine. I've tasted a hundred poisons. I'm still here. Its futures are just another vintage, Caelan. Bitter, but survivable—if you refuse to swallow whole."

"And if the futures are true?"

"They're true the way maps are true," she said. "They show roads. They don't show the walker." She brushed the ash-mark away with her palm, deliberately, erasing it. "I chose this road. I choose it again every morning. You don't get to protect me from my own choices—that's not love, that's just another cage, and we're rather short on cages right now."

He laughed—cracked, unwilling, real. "You're terrifying."

"I'm a taster," she said. "Terrifying is the job description." Then, softer, in the voice she almost never used, the one with no calculation in it at all: "And for the record—it's not against all sense. Sense would be walking away. I've never once managed sense where you're concerned either. So we're both fools, and fools climb mountains together."

Above the notch in the ridge, the moon was rising—swollen, a night from full, the twelfth turn nearly spent. Its light came down the slope and touched the snowfields above them, and far up in the dark, where the shrine waited behind its sealed door, something answered the moonlight with a faint, sourceless glow, like a lamp being uncovered in a window of a house everyone believed empty.

Mara saw it too. She went still, head tilted, listening to a silence that had acquired texture.

"It knows we're coming," she said. "It's known since the river. Maybe since the banquet." She looked at him, and the firelight and the moonlight met in her eyes, and she was smiling—not the reassuring smile she wore like armor, but the other one, rare and crooked and entirely hers. "Two nights, Caelan. Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we climb."

He lay back down and did not sleep. But for the first time since the dreams began, the darkness held no throne room, no offered hand, no father's borrowed voice. Only the wind, and her breathing, steady as a metronome across the cold camp—and the mountain above them, groaning once, deep in its roots, like a door being tested by something impatient to walk through it.
