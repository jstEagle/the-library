## 14. Confessions at the Fire

The fire was small, because small fires were harder to see.

Mara had built it in the lee of a granite shelf two-thirds of the way up Thornfell's shoulder, where the pines thinned to wind-bent scrub and the smuggler road gave up pretending to be a road at all. Above them the mountain went on climbing into darkness, and somewhere past that darkness—past the scree fields and the frozen falls and the shrine's first door, which still seemed to hum faintly behind them like a struck bell that would not finish ringing—the inner shrine waited. The twelfth moon hung low and swollen over the eastern ridge, one night from full, one night from its final turn. It lit the snowfields the color of old bone.

Caelan sat across the flames with his knees drawn up and his sword across his thighs, though they both knew by now that the sword was useless against what followed them. He kept it anyway. She understood. There was comfort in holding something with an edge, even when the thing you feared had no body to cut.

They had not spoken for an hour. The silence between them had changed over the weeks on the road—it had started as the silence of strangers forced together, then the silence of allies too tired for talk, and now it was this: a silence with weight and warmth to it, a silence that did not need filling but wanted to be.

The enforcers were a day behind, maybe less. Vess was with them. Mara could feel it the way she could feel weather coming in her teeth—an old ache, an old certainty. Tomorrow or the day after, they would climb the last stretch with the hollow-eyed host at their backs and whatever remained of the Veil's patience tearing loose ahead of them. The mountain had been groaning all evening, deep tectonic complaints that rose through the soles of her boots like the muttering of something turning over in its sleep.

One night left. Perhaps only this one.

"Caelan," she said.

He looked up. Firelight moved across his face and made him look younger than he was, and older than he'd ever been—both at once, the exile and the heir occupying the same tired eyes. "If you're going to tell me we should turn back," he said, "I'll remind you that I already refused twice, and you threatened me with the river crossing."

"I was going to say that if we die tomorrow, I don't want to have spent tonight not saying things."

He went very still. In the weeks she had known him she had learned his stillnesses—the soldier's stillness when danger came, the boy's stillness when his father's name was spoken, and this third one now, wary and hopeful, the stillness of a man who has learned not to reach for things he wants.

"Say things," he repeated carefully.

"You first." She pulled her cloak tighter and drew a breath that tasted of pine smoke and cold stone. "You told me once, on the river barge, that you ran from the palace. You said it like a confession and then you never finished it. I've carried you across half of Thornfell since, and I still don't know what you were running *from*. Not the court. Not the politics. You're not afraid of those—I've watched you walk into a room full of armed men like it was a dinner party."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, far down the dark slope, something howled—not wolf, not wind. They both ignored it. They had gotten good at ignoring things.

Caelan turned his hands toward the flames, palms open, as if warming them, though Mara suspected he simply needed somewhere to put his eyes. "My father began fading when I was fourteen," he said. "Not dying. Fading. That's the only word I've ever found for it. He'd be talking to you—really talking, asking about your studies, laughing at his own jokes—and then he'd go somewhere. Mid-sentence. His eyes would fix on nothing, just for a breath, and when he came back he'd be... thinner. Not his body. Him. Like a candle flame guttering and catching itself again, except each time it caught, there was less candle."

Mara said nothing. She knew that flicker. She had served that flicker wine for six years.

"Everyone else saw a king who was tired," Caelan went on. "A king who aged. Kings age; that's what kings do. But I sat with him. I was the one who read to him in the evenings when his hands shook too badly to hold the pages, and I could feel him going out by degrees, like heat leaving a room. And no one—" His jaw tightened. "No one would say it. The physicians called it melancholy. The priests called it the weight of the crown. Vess called it nothing at all, which should have told me everything, but I was fifteen and stupid with grief before I even knew what I was grieving."

"And so you ran."

"And so I ran." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only the raw edge where humor would have gone if there had been any left. "Seven years ago. I told myself it was the banishment—that Father cast me out unjustly, that I owed him nothing, that my pride wouldn't let me stay and be a ghost's son in a haunted house. And some of that was true. The banishment was cruel. He said things—" Caelan stopped. Closed his eyes. When he opened them again they were wet, and he let them be, which was something else she had learned about him: he did not hide his grief, he only rationed it. "He said I was a disappointment he should never have crowned in his hopes. He said it in front of the whole court. And I went, gladly, because going meant I didn't have to watch."

"Watch what?"

"Watch him disappear." The words came out flat and fast, the way a man pulls a splinter. "I couldn't bear it, Mara. Do you understand? Everyone thinks exile broke me, and exile was a mercy. Exile was me choosing to remember my father alive. Every year I stayed away was another year he stayed alive in my memory. If I had stayed—if I had sat by that bed and watched the thing wearing him eat him bite by bite—I would have nothing left of him at all. So I drank. I drank in border towns for seven years and told myself I was free, and really I was just a coward with a better view."

The fire cracked a knot of pine and threw sparks up toward the indifferent moon. Mara watched them rise and die.

"He wasn't casting you out," she said quietly. "That's what the trials showed you. At the first door. His last words weren't rejection."

"No." Caelan's voice frayed. "They were love. I know. I've known since the shrine. And knowing it doesn't undo seven years of believing otherwise. It just means the cage had two prisoners, and I built mine myself." He looked at her across the fire, and his gaze was steady and terrible in its honesty. "Your turn. You said I first. I've paid. What were you running from?"

Mara pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them. This was the story she never told. She had told pieces of it—to herself, in the dark, in the years when sleep wouldn't come—but never aloud, never to another living person, because the telling made it real in a way memory alone did not.

"I wasn't running from anything," she said. "I was sold."

* * *

"The Jouberts were farmers, up near the northern marches. Bad land, worse luck. I was six when the blight took the second harvest in a row, and my father walked me to the city and sold me to a man who collected children for the palace. I didn't understand what for. I thought—I don't know what I thought. That palaces were where orphans went to become princesses. I was six. I'd heard stories."

Caelan had stopped breathing, she noticed. She kept her eyes on the fire.

"There were four of us, that intake. Two boys, two girls. They housed us in a wing of the palace I never found again, and a woman came—kind-faced, soft-voiced, the way people are kind to livestock—and she explained our work. We were to be tasters. Not the kind you're thinking of, not the ceremonial sippers at feasts. Real ones. The palace had reason to believe someone was poisoning the king—this was before your father's time, or early in it, I don't know—and poisoners are clever, and antidotes are slow, and the only reliable defense ever invented is a body that already knows the poison intimately."

She heard Caelan inhale slowly through his nose.

"They fed us poison, Caelan. Measured doses, every day, increasing. Arsenic in the morning porridge. Belladonna in the evening wine. Compounds I still can't name, brought up the smuggler roads from places whose names I learned later, when I started walking those roads myself. Two of the four died in the first year. A boy named Tammo who cried in his sleep, and a girl named Bette who didn't cry at all, which I understood even then was the worse sign. I survived because I was angry. Nobody tells you anger will keep you alive. Nobody puts it in the songs."

"Mara." His voice was rough. "You were a child."

"I was a child who got very good at swallowing." She smiled, and knew it was the wrong smile, the one that made people uneasy, and let it stay. "Do you know what microdosing does, over years? It doesn't make you strong. It makes you *other*. My blood is a pharmacopoeia. My body flinches at nothing anymore—nothing that comes in a bottle or a dish. I can drink things that would drop a horse. For six years I ate the king's meals before the king ate them, every dish, every course, waiting each time to find out if this was the one that had finally been dosed past what I could survive. And here is the part I have never said to anyone, not even to myself in the dark."

She looked up at him. The moonlight and firelight met on his face, and she made herself hold his eyes, because this part deserved to be seen being said.

"I didn't do it for the king. I want that understood, before tomorrow, before whatever happens up there. I didn't love Aldric. I barely knew him. I did it because every plate I emptied was a plate no one else had to test. Every poison in my blood was a poison kept out of someone else's. Four children came into that wing. Two died. And I swore on Bette's grave—which was no grave at all, just an unmarked plot behind the kitchens—that the other deaths would stop being wasted. That I would swallow everything they tried to feed this kingdom, and I would keep swallowing until either they ran out of poisons or I ran out of throat."

The silence after was long. Down the mountain, the howling came again, closer, and this time neither of them pretended not to hear it. Caelan reached across the fire—not dramatically, not the way men reached in stories, but the way a person reaches when they cannot stand the distance one moment longer—and took her hand. His palm was warm and calloused and slightly trembling.

"You think you're poisoned," he said. "You talk about yourself like a vessel. Like a thing that was filled up with harm and is only useful because of the harm."

"It's not untrue."

"It's entirely untrue." His grip tightened. "I've watched you for weeks. On the roads, at the river, in the shrine when the trial asked you for truth and you gave it without blinking. You are the least poisoned person I have ever met. Everything they put in you, you *transformed*. They meant it to kill you and it made you instead. That's not a vessel. That's a forge."

Mara felt something crack in her chest—a hairline fracture, the kind you don't notice until warmth starts leaking through it. She had spent six years being careful with everyone, guarding every word, every reaction, because a taster who showed what she felt was a taster who could be read, and a taster who could be read was a taster who could be managed. She had not known how heavy that vigilance was until this moment, sitting in the cold with a disgraced prince holding her hand, wanting—for the first time since she was six years old—to set it down.

"I was going to die at the river," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "When the enforcers ambushed us. I felt their touch start to take me, and some part of me thought, *good, finally, the last dose.* And then my own blood fought back, and I lived, and I've been trying to decide ever since whether that was survival or just one more thing I swallowed."

"It was you," Caelan said. "It was always you. The poison never made you. You made the poison kneel."

She laughed—really laughed, a startled, undignified sound that scattered up the mountainside—and had to press her free hand over her mouth. "Against all sense," she managed. "Everything about us. An exiled prince and a taster who smells like arsenic and bad decisions, climbing a haunted mountain to fight a god with a vial of my blood and your royal veins. If I said it aloud in any tavern in the kingdom, they'd laugh me into the street."

"So don't say it aloud." He shifted around the fire, close enough now that she could feel his warmth separate from the flames', and his voice dropped to the register he used only when he meant a thing completely. "I'm saying it here, to you, where it counts. Mara Joubert. I have loved exactly three things in my life without apology: my father, before he faded; the idea of a kingdom worth serving; and you. The first is gone. The second may yet be saved. But you—" His thumb traced a line across her knuckles, deliberate, memorizing. "You I choose. Not because of prophecy, not because of blood, not because the map says we're useful to each other. Because you swallow the world's worst and come up standing. Because you're angry and kind in the same breath and don't see that they're the same thing. Because when you look at me, you don't look at a prince or a weapon or a key. You look at *me*, and somehow that's the most frightening and the safest thing that's ever happened to me."

The fire burned low between them. Above, the twelfth moon leaned against the ridge like a held breath.

"I chose you weeks ago," Mara said. "On the smuggler road, when you complained about the food. I decided then that if the world ended, it could end while I was annoyed at you about barley stew, and that seemed like a good way to go." She turned her hand over and laced her fingers through his. "I love you, Caelan. Against all sense. Against every lesson they ever beat into me about attachment being a lever and love being a hook. I know exactly what I am risking tomorrow, and I'm risking it anyway, because a life spent not loving you isn't a life I survived all that poison to keep."

He kissed her then, and it tasted of woodsmoke and cold air and the strange mineral tang that clung to them both now, the taste of people walking toward something enormous. It was not a desperate kiss. It was a promise made in the only language that had never lied to either of them.

When they parted, the fire had burned down to embers, and the mountain groaned again—longer this time, deeper, a sound like a door straining on ancient hinges. Mara felt it in her teeth, in the old scars along her jaw, in the poison-savvy marrow of her.

And beneath the groaning, faint but unmistakable, rising from the heights above them where the inner shrine waited in the dark:

Singing.

A thin, sweet, many-voiced singing, like a choir heard through water. The hollow-eyed host had begun to sing, a day's march below, and the mountain answered note for note.

Caelan stood, drawing his sword out of pure reflex, staring up into the black. "What is that?"

Mara rose beside him, her hand finding his, her blood suddenly loud in her ears. The twelfth moon slid free of the ridge, huge and white and nearly done.

"It's starting," she said. "It knows we're coming. It's *welcoming* us."
