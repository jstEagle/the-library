## 15. The Hollow Court

They did not bind her hands.

That was the first thing Mara understood, and the understanding was worse than any rope. They had taken her at the river ford—Vess's soldiers, or what wore Vess's colors—and carried her back through Thornfell in a litter like something precious, like an offering being returned to a temple. No chains. No gag. Her wrists had been free the entire journey, and she had not once tried to use them, because the soldiers rode on either side of her with their faces turned forward and their eyes fixed on nothing, and she knew with a certainty that sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone that it would not have mattered if she'd fought. There were forty of them. There was one of her. And somewhere behind them all, riding a black horse without a saddle blanket, Vess kept pace with the patience of a woman who had already won.

"You could have taken the pardon," Vess had said, only once, on the second night. The firelight had caught the grey in her hair—the same grey it had always been, though Mara now understood that Vess had not aged a day since the Veil took her, that the grey itself might have been part of the costume, a detail stitched into the mask to make it convincing. "You could have walked away whole. I offered you that."

"You offered me a cage with a prettier door."

"I offered you survival." Vess's voice had been gentle, and that gentleness had cut deeper than cruelty ever could, because it was *her* voice, the voice that had taught Mara to read poison residue in candle smoke, the voice that had sung to her through fevers when she was nine years old and dying by inches for a king who no longer existed. "I have kept this court alive for seven years, child. Do you know what that costs? Do you know what I have fed it, so it would leave us enough of ourselves to go on breathing? You are looking at the price. You are looking at me."

Mara had turned her face to the dark and said nothing, and Vess had let her keep her silence, which was its own kind of mercy, or its own kind of contempt. She still hadn't decided which.

Now the palace rose out of the evening mist, gold-lit and terrible, every window burning as though for a festival. Mara stood in the courtyard while grooms led the horses away and servants bowed to her—not to Vess, to *her*—and the wrongness of it crawled up her spine. Servants did not bow to tasters. Servants did not look at tasters at all; she had spent six years training herself into invisibility, into being furniture that happened to swallow things. But they bowed, all of them, in perfect unison, and when they straightened their faces held expressions of such serene welcome that Mara's skin prickled.

"Come," Vess said. "You'll want to wash. The king receives you at midnight."

"The king doesn't eat," Mara said. "Why would he receive anyone?"

Vess paused on the steps. For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind her eyes, something old and exhausted and drowning, like a face pressed against the underside of ice. Then it was gone, smoothed over, and the spymaster who had raised her looked down with cool amusement.

"He doesn't eat," Vess agreed. "He *receives*. There is a difference, and you are about to learn it."

* * *

They gave her the room she had lived in before, the narrow cell above the kitchens with its iron bed and its single window facing the herb garden. Her own cloak still hung on its peg. Her cup still sat on the shelf where she'd left it, and someone had filled it with water, and there was bread on the table, and honey, and a sprig of thyme arranged beside the plate with a care that made her throat close. They wanted her comfortable. They wanted her to understand that comfort was available, that comfort was *conditional*, that everything she saw in this small room was a sentence in an argument the Veil was making and she had not yet heard the conclusion.

She ate nothing. She drank nothing. Six years of discipline held: you never consumed what an enemy provided, even—or especially—when it was kind.

Through the window she watched the sun go down over the capital, and she watched the palace light up from within, hall after hall after hall, until the whole building glowed like a lantern with something alive inside it. And she watched the people arrive. Carriages, riders, courtiers on foot, streaming in through the gates as the last light failed—more of them than there should have been, far more, nobles whose estates lay weeks distant, minor functionaries from provinces she had mapped on smuggler roads a thousand miles away. All of them coming. All of them summoned, or called, or drawn, the way moths are drawn, the way blood is drawn to a wound.

By full dark, the courtyards were full. And nobody was talking.

She heard it from her window: the absence. A palace at festival should roar. This one hummed—a low, layered sound, hundreds of footsteps moving in near-perfect rhythm, hundreds of breaths drawn and released together, and beneath it all a silence where conversation should be. Mara pressed her forehead against the cold glass and thought of Caelan, wherever he was, whether he had run, whether he had fought, whether he hated her for being taken, whether he was even alive. She had left him sleeping at the shrine's outer wall with a promise to return by dawn. She had been gone perhaps two hours when the enforcers found her—found her too easily, as though her hiding places had been described to them in advance, as though someone who knew every smuggler road in Thornfell had drawn them a map.

The thought arrived and she refused it. Refused it again. It circled back anyway, patient as hunger: *someone told them where you would be.*

Not Vess. Vess had simply collected her afterward. Someone else. Someone who had listened to her plans across a dying fire, who had kissed her forehead and said *sleep, I'll take first watch*, and who had then—

No. She would not believe it. Not yet. Not until she heard him say it himself, or heard the truth that unmade the accusation. Hope and suspicion fought in her chest like two poisons in one bloodstream, and neither would neutralize the other.

At midnight, the door opened without a knock.

* * *

They came for her in silence and walked her down through the palace, and Mara counted doors out of habit, mapped turns out of habit, marked the guards who did not guard and the corridors that had changed since she'd last walked them—walls moved, she was almost certain, passages that should have bent instead running straight, the architecture subtly rearranged around some purpose she couldn't name. The throne room doors stood open at the end of the great gallery, and light poured out of them, golden and sourceless, and the sound of breathing grew louder with every step. Hundreds of lungs. One rhythm.

The throne room was full.

Every noble house in the kingdom stood between the pillars, ranked in order of precedence, dressed in their finest, and every one of them stood perfectly still. Not asleep—awake, she could see it, eyes open and gleaming in the golden light—but motionless in a way living bodies are never motionless. No shifting of weight. No swallowing. No flicker of gaze following her as the guards walked her down the center aisle. Their faces were all turned upward, tilted at the same angle, toward the dais, toward the throne, toward the crown.

Duke Aldous of Merren stood three rows deep, and Mara had saved his life at the autumn banquet—had spat his wine into a basin and shouted *poison* while the hall panicked, and he had grasped her hand afterward and wept with gratitude. Now his eyes were open and empty and wet, tears tracking slowly down his unmoving face, and his lips moved without sound, repeating something, repeating it, repeating it, and she caught the shape of the words on the third cycle: *we are honored, we are honored, we are honored.*

Her legs nearly gave. She kept walking because stopping meant falling and falling meant lying among them, and she would die standing before she lay down in this field of harvested people.

On the dais, the throne. On the throne, the king. And above the king's brow, catching the sourceless light and giving back none of it, the crown.

She had seen it ten thousand times. She had knelt before it. But she had never *looked*, and now she could not stop looking: the metal was wrong, the way flesh gone numb is wrong, and it did not sit on the king's head so much as grow there, filaments fine as spider silk running down into his temples, into his jaw, vanishing beneath the skin. His eyes were closed. His chest did not move. And the crown breathed—it expanded and contracted, slowly, with the rhythm of the hundreds of chests below it, drawing them all into time with itself, a conductor keeping a orchestra of stolen bodies in perfect measure.

"There she is."

The voice came from everywhere—from the pillars, from the floor, from inside her own skull, resonant and warm and horribly reasonable, a voice like a father's, like a lover's, like a king addressing a beloved subject.

"The taster. The little vessel of poisons. Do you know how long I have wanted to speak with you properly? Your master watches you, your prince trusts you, but no one has ever simply *talked* with you. That seems a cruel oversight. You have such an interesting interior. So much swallowed and never once digested."

Mara stopped at the foot of the dais. Her knees shook. She locked them.

"What do you want?" Her voice came out smaller than she intended. She made herself lift her chin. "If you wanted me dead, I'd be dead. If you wanted the map, Vess would have taken it. So what am I for?"

"Ah." The warmth deepened, and the whole court sighed—one vast, soft exhalation, hundreds of mouths releasing a single breath, and the sound of it nearly broke her. "Directness. Yes. I remember directness. I wore a man who was direct, once." The king's eyes opened. Both voices were in them now, the pleading human fragment drowned deep beneath the shining thing, but present, present, watching her from the bottom of a well. "You are for *bargaining*, Mara Joubert. It is what you are for. Everything in this room is for bargaining. That is what power is—a pile of things you can threaten to break."

It descended the dais. The body wearing Aldric's shape moved stiffly, joints working like hinges unused for years, and the crown's filaments stretched and flexed with each step, silver threads glinting.

"Here is my offer, and I will make it only once, because unlike the men who built cages, I do not enjoy repetition. The boy. Caelan. He carries the blood that can unmake me, and he is climbing my mountain with my name in his mouth. Bring him to me—willingly, walking, believing he comes to negotiate—and you walk out of this palace free. Not pardoned. *Free.* No duty, no leash, no taster's tongue, no Vess. A horse, gold enough to drown in, roads going south where I have no interest and no reach. You will live fifty years and die in your own bed under your own name, and no one will ever use you again."

The words landed in her like hooks finding flesh. Because it was true—that was the horror of it, every word of it was true, it *could* give her exactly that, and some starved animal part of her, the orphan sold to the palace at eight years old, the girl who had poisoned herself so others wouldn't die, that part rose up howling *yes, yes, take it, take the road, take the name, take the life—*

And then she looked at the court.

At Duke Aldous weeping his gratitude into silence. At the young countess from the northern marches whom Mara had seen laugh just last spring, genuinely laugh, at some fool's joke in a corridor—standing now with her head tipped back and her throat bared like something waiting for the knife. At the servants along the walls, the chambermaids who had slipped her extra bread when she was a child, the bootboy who whistled off-key, all of them hollowed and humming, all of them *furniture* now, the fate she had spent six years choosing for herself made total and eternal and inflicted on everyone she had ever shared a kitchen with.

This was the machinery. This was what power looked like with the skin peeled off: a pile of things that could be threatened with breaking, and every person in this room was a thing, and the thing on the throne had been making this bargain for seven years with seven hundred people, and every one of them had said yes to some smaller version of it—*keep your post, keep your pension, keep your family safe, just look away, just taste the dish and say nothing, just hold the border, just smile*—until the saying-yes had eaten them down to this. Standing. Smiling. Honored.

"No," Mara said.

The word came out steady. She was almost surprised.

The court exhaled again. The crown pulsed, slow, considering.

"No?" The Veil's voice lost none of its warmth, but something moved underneath it now, something with teeth. "You refuse freedom? You, of all creatures in this kingdom—you who have been owned since childhood, used since childhood, poisoned since childhood? I offer you the one thing no one else ever has, and you say *no*?"

"I've been offered freedom before." Her heart was slamming so hard she could hear it in her ears, but her voice held, and holding it felt like the truest act of her life. "Everyone in this room was offered freedom once. A little piece of it. Enough to make them stop looking. That's how you got them—that's the whole trick, isn't it? Nobody sold themselves all at once. They sold themselves in dishes. One course at a time. And I—" she stepped forward, one step, up onto the first stair of the dais, close enough to see the human fragment flinch deep in the king's eyes, "*—I have been tasting dishes my entire life. I know poison when it's served to me warm and wrapped in kindness. And yours, majesty, is the worst vintage yet.*"

Silence. True silence—the breathing stopped, hundreds of chests frozen mid-rise, the whole room suspended like a held note.

Then the Veil laughed, and the laughter shook dust from the rafters, and the court resumed breathing all at once with a sound like wind through a forest of dead trees.

"Oh," it said, softly, delighted, dreadful. "*Oh.* You will be so much more interesting broken than the others were. Very well, little taster. Keep your defiance; defiance is just another flavor, and I have time—I have *such* time. But understand what your refusal purchases." The king's ruined face leaned close, and both voices spoke together now, the silk and the scream braided into one: "When the boy arrives—if he arrives—he will find you here, among my honored guests. And everything that happens to him will happen because you chose it. Every turn of the wheel, every trial, every drop of his blood on my mountain—you are the lever now, Mara Joubert. I did not need to hunt him. I needed only to *hold you*."

Guards took her arms—gently, courteously, which was worse than force—and turned her toward the doors. As they drew her back down the aisle between the ranks of the harvested, Mara lifted her head and met the eyes of the thing on the throne one last time, and she did not look at the crown, and she did not look away.

*You're wrong,* she thought, pouring everything into it, every swallowed poison, every year, every name of every person she had ever failed to save. *You think I'm the lever. But levers don't choose where they press. And I have six years of practice surviving exactly one thing: being used.*

They put her in a tower room with a view of the eastern mountains, where the thirteenth moon would rise. Through the barred window, far off, a peak stood black against the stars, and on its shoulder, faint as breath on glass, a light burned that had not burned there in seven hundred years.

Someone was lighting fires on the mountain.

Caelan was climbing.
