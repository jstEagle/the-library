## 16. Escape from the Gilded Cage

They came for Caelan at dawn, or what passed for dawn beneath the Veil's sky—a grey light that never quite warmed, filtered through windows that had been bricked over and replaced with painted glass. He heard them before he saw them: the particular silence of the hollow-eyed moving in formation, boots striking stone without echo, as though the palace itself had agreed to swallow their footsteps.

He was already dressed. He had been dressed for three days.

*Let them come,* he thought, and found to his surprise that his hands were steady. *Let them carry me to whatever altar they've built. Let them think they've won.*

The doors of his gilded prison—because that was what it was, however softly they called it a guest chamber—opened without being touched. Vess stood in the frame, flanked by six of the court's smiling dead. Her face was composed in its usual mask of weary competence, but Caelan had spent seven years learning to read people who didn't want to be read, and he could see the seams now. The way her left hand curled too tightly around her own wrist. The way her eyes, when they touched his, flickered with something that might once have been guilt.

"Prince Caelan." She said it like a sentence being passed. "His Majesty requests your presence."

"Does he." Caelan rose. "Or does the thing wearing him?"

Something moved behind Vess's eyes—quick, pained, gone. "You'll come quietly. That is not a request I enjoy making."

"No," Caelan said. "I imagine you enjoyed nothing for a very long time."

She didn't answer. The hollow ones closed around him like water closing over a stone, and he let himself be carried down through the palace he had grown up in and no longer recognized. Corridors he had raced through as a boy, chasing his father's hounds, now lined with servants who stood motionless as furniture, faces tilted toward the ceiling, toward the crown room, toward the thing that fed at the heart of everything.

*Hold on, Mara,* he thought, and hated himself for thinking it, because thinking it meant hoping, and hoping meant they had leverage. *Whatever they're doing to you—hold on.*

They took him down. Down past the throne room, past the crown chamber where he had watched his father's face go strange and smooth seven years ago, down stairs that hadn't existed when he was young—stairs cut into living rock, breathing cold air that smelled of iron and old honey. The walls here were veined with something dark that pulsed faintly, slowly, like the heartbeat of an enormous sleeping animal.

At the bottom was a circular chamber, and in the center of it, a basin of black glass filled with liquid that reflected no light. And beside the basin, bound to a chair of silver wire, sat King Aldric.

What remained of him.

Caelan stopped walking. The hollow ones pressed against his back, patient as tide.

His father had been a big man once—broad-shouldered, loud, a king who filled rooms by accident. What sat in the chair was a husk wearing Aldric's skin, cheeks hollowed, lips cracked, hands so thin the bones showed through like branches under ice. But the eyes—the eyes were still fighting. Caelan could see it, the way you can see a candle guttering behind smoked glass: something human, trapped, burning itself down to keep from going out entirely.

"Caelan." The voice came out ruined, two voices braided into one—the silk-smooth tone he'd heard in dreams, and beneath it, ragged and desperate, his father's actual voice, worn thin as parchment. "My son. My—*run*, boy, don't look at me, don't—"

"Hush," said the other voice, gently, horribly. "We're having a family moment."

"What is this?" Caelan kept his voice flat. His heart was hammering against his ribs like a prisoner at a door. "What are you doing to him?"

"A transfer." The Veil smiled through the king's ruined mouth. "Seven years in one vessel wears thin. Your father has been such a gracious host, but even hospitality has limits. A younger body. Royal blood. Yours." The head turned, slow as a drawbridge. "And your taster friend tells me you'd trade anything for her life. So we made an arrangement. You sit in the basin willingly, and she walks out of the palace untouched. Everyone keeps their promises. Everyone is happy. Isn't that what kings are for? Keeping everyone happy?"

"You're lying."

"About which part?" The smile widened past any human limit. "That she bargained? Or that I keep my word? Ask her yourself—oh, wait. You can't."

Caelan looked at his father. At the wreck of him. At the small, fierce light still guttering behind those eyes, and the mouth that kept trying to move against the thing controlling it, shaping words without sound: *run, run, run.*

"I'll do it," Caelan said.

The chamber went very quiet. Even the pulsing veins in the walls seemed to pause.

"Willingly?" the Veil asked, savoring it.

"Willingly. On one condition. I see Mara walk free first. Out the gates. I watch her go."

"Done." The Veil said it too fast, too easily, and some cold instinct in Caelan's gut whispered: *it doesn't care whether you see it, because it doesn't intend for there to be anything left of you to feel cheated.* But there was nothing to do about that now. Nothing except the plan he had made in the dark three nights ago, alone, with no one to tell and no one to save him if it failed.

*Buy time. That's all. Buy time and trust her.*

It went against every instinct he owned. Princes were not raised to trust; they were raised to verify, to hedge, to hold power close and suspicion closer. But Mara Joubert had walked into a palace full of monsters to bargain for his life, and if she had a plan, it would be built out of the same material she was built from: poison, patience, and paths nobody else knew existed.

He let them lead him to the basin.

* * *

The liquid was warm. That was the worst part—he had expected cold, expected pain, and instead the black glass held something like bathwater, something almost kind, and the kindness was the trap. As he sank into it, the Veil's presence settled over him like a blanket drawn up by careful hands.

*There you are,* it murmured, everywhere, inside his skull. *I have wanted to meet you properly for so long. You look so much like him. Before.*

"Before what?" Caelan said aloud, because speaking kept him anchored, kept the edges of himself sharp.

*Before I wore him smooth.* The presence rippled with something like nostalgia. *Do you know what it's like, little prince, to be a crown? To sit on the heads of the mighty and listen to their thoughts for centuries? They put me on to be a symbol. Symbols get hungry. Hunger gets clever. And cleverness, given enough time, becomes ambition, and ambition becomes need, and need—*

"—becomes you," Caelan finished.

*Becomes us,* the Veil corrected fondly. *You'll understand soon. You'll be on both sides of the crown. Won't that be novel? Father and son, sharing a kingdom forever. He always wanted more time with you. I'm giving him eternity.*

"He wanted—" Caelan's throat closed. Through the surface of the liquid, distorted, he could see the king's chair, the husk slumped forward, the cracked lips still moving. *Run. Run. Run.* "He wanted me to stay. Didn't he. When I ran. He wasn't banishing me. He was trying to get me away from you."

*Oh, he tried so many things, that man.* The voice was almost tender. *Letters he never sent. A horse saddled in secret. He told you he was disappointed because disappointment was the only weapon I let him keep. Every cruel word he ever spoke to you—I chose them. Aren't I generous? Giving a father exactly the right cruelty to drive his son to safety?*

Caelan went still in the warm dark. Seven years of bitterness shifted inside him like a landslide beginning—not falling yet, but trembling, ready.

"You're lying again," he managed.

*Am I?* The presence laughed, low and vast. *Ask him yourself. Oh—wait. You can't.*

But he could. Because at that moment, across the chamber, the husk in the silver-wire chair convulsed—and the Veil's attention, stretched between holding the transfer and gloating, thinned—and King Aldric's true voice tore out of that broken throat, raw and enormous:

"*CAELAN!*"

Caelan surged upright in the basin, water streaming off him.

"Every word," his father gasped, eyes blazing, human, *his*. "Every cruel word was mine. Chosen. To make you hate me enough to leave. You stubborn, brave, *foolish* boy—you did it, you got out, and now you've come *back*—"

"Dad—" The word broke him open. He hadn't said it in seven years. It came out sounding twelve years old.

"Don't waste it on grief." Aldric's voice dropped, urgent, scraping. "The ritual needs a full turn of the moon-clock. The basin must hold you until the thirteenth hour. If the vessel leaves the water before—" The body seized; the silk voice slid back in like oil over water. "*Enough.*"

The pressure clamped down on Caelan like a fist. He sank, gasping, and the warm liquid began to pull at him—not his body, but the shape of him, his memories loosening like stitching, his name starting to feel like someone else's word—

And then, from somewhere above, the palace screamed.

Not a person. The building itself. Stone groaning, iron shrieking, and through the floor came a sound he would remember for the rest of his life however long that turned out to be: the roar of water where no water should be, rushing, rising, *flooding*—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, a woman's voice bellowing in triumph:

"**CAELAN! THE KITCHENS! FOLLOW THE WATER!**"

* * *

Mara had escaped an hour before he surrendered.

He learned this later, in pieces, shouted over chaos. Here is how it happened, as the palace told it afterward in whispers: the taster had played her part perfectly. She had begged, bargained, offered herself as collateral, let them lock her in a tower room with a barred window and a guard who no longer truly watched anything. And then she had pulled up the third flagstone from the hearth—because Mara Joubert had spent six years memorizing this palace the way scholars memorize scripture, and every smuggler road through Thornfell had taught her the same lesson: *every fortress is built by people who also build kitchens, and kitchens need drains.*

The drain beneath the old scullery ran forty feet to a cistern, the cistern to a service tunnel from the reign of a king two hundred years dead who had feared assassination more than damp. The tunnel ran beneath the throne room, beneath the crown chamber, and ended—per the maps she'd stolen from Vess's own study, keys lifted from Vess's own belt—in the rock-cut levels below, where the Veil had carved its ritual hall and never imagined anyone remembered the older ways through the mountain.

She came up out of the floor like vengeance given practical form.

The first hollow guard she didn't fight; she simply opened the sluice-valve she'd found in the tunnels and let two hundred years of cistern water do the work, a black torrent down the stairwell that swept the smiling dead off their feet and shorted whatever passed for their thoughts. The second and third she fought properly, and lost, and won anyway, because her blood—six years of swallowed poison, the curse she'd carried like a sentence—burned them on contact, sent them staggering back with flesh smoking like the dying courier on the Thornfell road. The Veil's creatures could not touch her. Had never been able to touch her. She had known since the river crossing, and she had built her whole mad plan on knowing it.

By the time she reached the ritual chamber, Caelan was drowning in something worse than water.

* * *

He felt her before he heard her—a wrongness in the Veil's grip, a hesitation, the vast attention flinching sideways like a man whose hand has brushed a hot stove.

*What—* the presence began, and then Mara Joubert hit the black-glass basin's rim and drove her knife—coated, he would learn, in her own blood, the only weapon in the world the Veil could not abide—straight into the seam of the ritual circle.

The basin shattered.

Warm liquid exploded across the chamber, and with it the pulling, dissolving pressure vanished, and Caelan fell forward onto wet stone, coughing, alive, *himself*. Above him the Veil rose out of the king's body like smoke forced from a burning house, a shape of darkness with too many angles, screaming in a voice that shook dust from the ceiling.

"**YOU.**" It found Mara. Of course it did. "**LITTLE POISONER. I OFFERED YOU EVERYTHING.**"

"You offered me freedom," Mara said, hauling Caelan up by the arm, her grip fierce and real and shaking only slightly. "From a cage you built. That's not freedom. That's a smaller cell with a view."

The darkness lunged. She stepped into it—*into* it, deliberately, arms spread—and the Veil recoiled as if she were made of fire, its edges hissing and fraying where they neared her, and in that moment Caelan understood what she had understood at the river: six years of poison had made her the one thing in the world it could not swallow.

"The king!" she shouted at him. "Get your father! We have maybe minutes before it stops being afraid of me!"

He ran to the chair. The silver wires had fused; he tore them apart with bare hands, skinning his knuckles, not caring. His father weighed nothing. That was the detail that would haunt him—that a king who had once lifted him onto his shoulders like he weighed nothing now weighed nothing himself.

"Caelan." Aldric's eyes found his, clear for one more moment. "The shrine. Thirteenth moon. Don't bury me here, boy. Finish it. And Caelan—" the ruined mouth shaped a smile, terrible and beautiful, "—your mother would have liked her. The taster. She'd have liked her *very much*."

"Dad—"

"*Run,*" said the king, and this time both voices said it together, human and monster united in a single furious command, and the wall of the chamber burst inward in a flood of cistern water and collapsing stone.

Mara grabbed his collar. "Tunnel's flooding—we go up or drown. *Move.*"

They ran. Behind them the Veil howled and thrashed in its wounded fury, and ahead of them the smuggler's passage climbed steep and narrow toward the night air of the Thornfell foothills, and above them, through cracks in the mountain, Caelan could see the sky—and the moon, fat and nearly full, hanging like a wound about to open.

Twelve turns done. One remained.

"We have days," Mara panted, hauling him around a collapsed junction she clearly knew by heart, her lantern throwing wild shadows. "Maybe less. It'll come after us with everything now—it's hurt, it's *furious*—"

"Then we climb faster." He caught her hand as they scrambled up a ladder of iron rungs ancient with rust. For one second, in the lantern light, they looked at each other—mud and blood and water, both half-drowned, both grinning like lunatics.

"To the shrine," she said.

"To my father," he said.

"To the end of crowns," she said, and pulled him up into the dark.
