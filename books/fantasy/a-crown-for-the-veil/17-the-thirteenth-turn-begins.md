## 17. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The mountain began to scream an hour before dawn.

Not a sound, exactly—Mara felt it more than heard it, a vibration rising through her boots and into her teeth, as though the stone itself were being pulled apart at some seam too deep for human eyes. Snow shivered loose from the ledges above them in long white breaths. Somewhere far below, a whole shelf of the mountain let go with a groan that rolled through the pass like the voice of something waking.

Caelan caught her arm as the path tilted beneath them. "That's not an avalanche."

"No," Mara said. "Avalanches end."

They climbed.

The smuggler road to the inner shrine was not a road at all anymore—or rather, it had stopped pretending to be one. For six years Mara had carried every switchback of Thornfell in her head the way other people carried prayers: the goat trails that looked like nothing and held like iron, the scree fields where you walked heel-first and silent, the frozen waterfall at Kestrel's Tooth where the ice made a stair if you knew which three feet of it would bear weight. She had learned these paths from men who used them to move contraband and fugitives, men who trusted no crown and no god, and she had never once imagined she'd climb them toward a crown instead of away from one.

Now the mountain was unlearning itself around her. Trails she knew ended in fresh falls of rock. A ravine she'd crossed a dozen times yawned wider than her memory claimed, its edges crumbling even as they watched. The thirteenth moon was still hours from rising, hidden behind the western peaks, but its pull had already begun—the map had said as much, in that cramped, dying hand: *the last turn begins before the moon crests. The cage thins. Run.*

She had run. They both had. And it hadn't been enough.

"They're behind us." Caelan spoke without turning, his eyes on the switchbacks below, where the darkness had begun to organize itself into shapes. "I can hear them."

Mara listened. Beneath the mountain's groaning she heard it too: footsteps. Hundreds of them. Perfectly synchronized, perfectly unhurried, climbing a mountain in the dark with no torches and no fear. The hollow court had left their gilded halls and come up into the cold in their banquet silks and their ceremonial cloaks, nobles who had never walked farther than the garden maze now ascending Thornfell like pilgrims, faces empty, eyes turned always upward toward the peak where their master strained against its cage.

And behind them, somewhere in that silent tide, Vess.

Mara's chest did the thing it always did when she thought of Vess—a tightening, half grief and half fury, the two feelings so knotted together after twenty years that she could no longer tell which was holding which. Her mentor. Her mother in every way that mattered and none that counted. The woman who had taught her that love was a liability and loyalty a currency, and who had been feeding both to the thing in the crown for longer than Mara had been alive.

"She'll catch us," Caelan said.

"I know."

"You don't have to sound calm about it."

"I'm not calm." Mara found the next handhold and pulled herself up onto a ledge of black stone furred with ice. "I'm a taster, Caelan. Calm is just what my face does while everything underneath is screaming. You should know that by now—you've spent two weeks watching me do it."

He hauled himself up beside her, breathing hard, and for a moment they stood together on the ledge with the wind tearing at them, looking down at the river of pale faces flowing up the mountainside. In the pre-dawn grey they almost looked human. Almost looked like people out walking. It was the wrongness of the movement that undid it—the way not one of them stumbled, the way their arms swung in identical rhythm, the way the column bent around obstacles without a single word or glance passing between them.

"They were people," Caelan said quietly. It wasn't the first time he'd said it. He kept saying it, and Mara understood why: each time, he needed to remind himself that this was real, that the court he'd grown up in, the chamberlains and ladies and old tutors whose names he still remembered, were in there somewhere, drowned seven years deep. "Lady Merren used to sneak me honey cakes when I was eight. She's down there. Third row, the green cloak."

"Then we give her back her own face," Mara said. "That's the whole plan. That's the only plan we've ever had."

"And if the plan fails?"

Mara looked at him. Wind-whipped, frost-burned, exhausted past the point where exhaustion meant anything, the exiled prince who had chosen to be here—who had run from the palace once because he couldn't bear to watch his father fade, and had spent every mile since learning that running was the one thing he could no longer afford. Two weeks ago he'd been a drunk in a border town. Now he was climbing toward a shrine to cut open whatever remained of his father's prison, and he hadn't hesitated once since the river crossing, since the night he'd watched poison pour out of Mara's blood and understood what six years of swallowed death had made her.

"Then we fail together," she said, "and I'd rather do that than succeed alone. Which is new for me, so don't make me say it twice."

Something moved through his face—not quite a smile, too desperate for one, but the shape of one trying to be born. Then the mountain screamed again, louder, and the ledge beneath their feet shuddered, and there was no more time for anything but climbing.

* * *

The inner shrine revealed itself all at once, the way secrets do.

One moment they were scrambling up a shoulder of bare granite with the sky going strange above them—the stars smearing, the dark between them pulsing faintly, as though the night itself were a membrane stretched too thin—and the next, the rock simply opened, and there it was.

A doorway. Not carved; grown, if stone could grow. An arch of black basalt threaded with veins of dull silver, set into the mountainside like a wound that had healed over centuries into something almost beautiful. Beyond it, a passage sloped down into the mountain's heart, and from that depth came a light that was not firelight and not moonlight but something older, a grey-gold glow like the last moment of a sunset remembered by someone who had forgotten sunsets entirely.

"The trials are inside," Caelan said. His voice had gone rough. "Memory. Sacrifice. Truth. The binders built them so no one could reach the cage without paying."

"I know what's inside."

"I know you know. I just—" He stopped. Pressed his palm flat against the basalt, and Mara saw the old scar there, the one he'd cut at the first door, royal blood given freely to open the way. "I keep thinking about what the trials took from me last time. My father's last words. Do you understand what that means? The binders didn't guard the door with weapons. They guarded it with grief. Whatever's waiting in there will ask for worse this time. It'll ask for everything."

"Good," Mara said.

He stared at her.

"It should cost everything," she said. "Anything less and the Veil would have bought its freedom centuries ago. Some king with a bad conscience, some queen with a grudge—they'd have paid a little grief and opened the cage like a door. But if the price is everything—" she touched her own sternum, where six years of poison lived coiled in her blood like a second heart, "—then only someone with nothing left to lose can pay it. Or someone willing to become nothing. That's not a flaw in the design, Caelan. That's the design."

The mountain groaned again, deeper this time, and high above them the smear of stars pulsed brighter. Mara felt it against her skin like heat from a forge: the cage thinning. The thirteenth moon wouldn't crest the peaks for hours yet, but its turning had already begun, and the binding that had held for a thousand years was coming apart ahead of schedule, thread by thread, the way a rope parts—one strand at a time, invisibly, until the final strand goes and the whole load comes down at once.

From below, faint but unmistakable, came the footsteps. Closer now. Unhurried. Certain.

"We go in," Mara said. "We pay what it asks. And we come out before the moon does."

"And if the trials take longer than the moon?"

"Then we're already dead," she said, "and it won't matter. Come on."

They stepped through the arch together, and the mountain closed its wound behind them.

* * *

Inside, the world went quiet.

Not silent—the mountain's groaning still reached them, muffled now, felt in the walls more than heard—but quiet in the way a sickroom is quiet, a held-breath quiet, the quiet of something enduring. The passage descended in a slow spiral, its basalt walls veined with silver that brightened as they went down, and the grey-gold light grew until Mara could see every detail of the stone around her: the marks of ancient tools, yes, but also things that might have been carvings or might have been scars—long parallel gouges, like claw-marks, like something had once been dragged down this passage fighting every foot of the way.

"The binders brought it here alive," Caelan murmured. He was walking close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers; neither of them acknowledged it, and neither of them broke the contact either. "They didn't kill it. They couldn't. So they dragged it down and caged it and built a crown to hold the lock, and then they put the crown on a man's head and told him it was honor."

"And every king since has been the lock," Mara said. "Turned and turned until the mechanism wore out."

"Aldric lasted seven years." His voice cracked on the number. "Seven years, and the thing wearing him ate a whole court."

"Not ate." Mara had thought about this through every mile of the climb, turning it over the way she turned a suspect dish on her tongue, testing for the wrongness beneath the flavor. "It doesn't eat them. It empties them. There's a difference. What it takes, it keeps—somewhere. All those people down there on the mountain, all those hollow-eyed servants repeating their phrases verbatim—something of them is still in there. In it. The fragment of your father that begged me for help—that was real, Caelan. That was Aldric, drowning inside his own body, reaching out through the thing that holds him. If we break the cage right—if we break it *open*, not apart—everything it swallowed comes back out."

"And if we break it wrong?"

"Then everything it swallowed stays swallowed," Mara said, "forever. Including your father. Including Lady Merren and her honey cakes. Including, probably, us."

The spiral ended.

They came out into the heart of the mountain, and Mara stopped breathing.

It was a cavern vast beyond sense, a cathedral hollowed out of the world's bones, and every surface of it was silver-veined basalt that caught the grey-gold light and gave it back doubled. At the center rose the altar: a single block of black stone, waist-high, its surface worn smooth by a thousand years of ritual, and upon it—

The crown.

It lay where the last binder had placed it, or perhaps where it had crawled to rest: a circlet of gold and black metal, twisted like briarwork, beautiful the way a spiderweb is beautiful, and around it the air shimmered and tore in slow, silent ripples, like heat-haze in winter. The cage. She could see the cage now—could see how the crown was not the cage but the *lock*, and the cage itself was woven of something finer, threads of binding laid through the very fabric of the cavern, through the stone and the air and the space between moments, and those threads were fraying before her eyes. Even as she watched, one snapped with a sound like a plucked harp string, and the shimmer around the crown flared, and somewhere far above them a shelf of mountain the size of a village fell away into the dark.

"It's ahead of schedule," Caelan whispered. "The moon hasn't even—"

"It knows we're coming." Mara's mouth had gone dry. Six years of tasting had trained her tongue to detect poison at concentrations that would kill a horse, and what she tasted on the air of this cavern was the same wrongness she'd found in the banquet dish that started all of this, refined a thousandfold, concentrated until it was less a flavor than a presence. "It's been ahead of schedule since the twelfth turn. Since Vess. It's rushing because it's afraid of us."

"Good," Caelan said, and there was something fierce and broken in his voice that she recognized because she'd heard it in her own. "Let it be afraid."

They crossed the cavern floor together. The altar waited. And as they came within ten paces of it, the trials woke.

* * *

The first trial took the light.

It didn't dim—it *left*, sucked out of the cavern in a single heartbeat, and Mara stood blind in absolute black with the mountain groaning overhead and the footsteps of the hollow court audible now even through miles of stone, closer, always closer, and then—

Her mother's kitchen.

Not a memory she visited. A memory she had buried so deep she'd convinced herself it was gone: the smell of woodsmoke and onions, the chipped blue bowl on the table, her mother's hands—flour to the wrists—working dough while she sang off-key. Mara was four years old again, standing in a doorway, and her mother turned and smiled at her and said, "There's my girl. Wash your hands, love. Tonight you help."

"I sold you," Mara said to the vision, and her voice came out steady, which was its own kind of horror. "You sold me, actually. To the palace taster's guild, for bread money, when I was nine. I spent six years learning to swallow poison so no one else would die, and I told myself I'd forgotten your face. I didn't forget. I just decided remembering was a luxury I couldn't afford."

The kitchen held. The smile held. The trial waited, patient as stone, for her to look away first.

So she didn't. She walked into the memory—took her dead mother's flour-white hands in her own, knowing they were smoke and suggestion, and said, "This is what I am. I am the girl who was sold and the woman who survived being sold, and I did it by putting death in my own mouth six times a day for six years, and I am not ashamed of any of it. You don't get to be the worst thing that happened to me. You weren't even the fifth-worst. Now show me the door, because the moon is coming and I have a cage to break."

The kitchen dissolved like sugar in water.

Beside her, Caelan stood rigid, tears freezing on his cheeks—he had been in his own trial, she realized, his own buried room—and when the blackness lifted and the grey-gold light returned, he looked at her with an expression she'd carry the rest of her life, whatever length that turned out to be.

"What did it show you?" she asked.

"My father. Alive. Telling me the exile was mercy—that he sent me away to save me." His jaw worked. "And the worst part is I think it might be true, and I hate that it might be true, because if it's true then I wasted seven years hating a man who loved me, and I can't—" He stopped. Breathed. "Later. We can't afford later yet. What's next?"

"Sacrifice," Mara said, because the second trial was already forming, rising out of the altar's shadow like frost forming on glass.

* * *

The second trial asked for blood, but not the way she'd expected.

It built a scale in the air before them—an actual scale, iron and chain, hanging from nothing—and into one pan it poured a shimmering weight that Mara recognized with a lurch: her immunity. Six years of microdoses, six years of a body remade into a weapon against the very thing before them. The pan sank under it like it was lead.

Into the other pan, the trial placed a question: *What will you give?*

And Mara understood, finally, completely, what the binders had designed. The crown could only be shattered by blood that the Veil could not touch—poisoned blood, immune blood, freely offered. But freely offered meant the offering might kill her. The immunity was a tolerance, not a cure; six years of poison had made her blood a blade, and blades cut both ways. To strike the crown with it, she would have to open her own veins and pour out the thing that had kept her alive, and no one—no binder, no god, no trial—could tell her whether what remained afterward would be enough to live on.

"That's the sacrifice," Caelan said hoarsely. "It's not gold. It's not a throne. It's you."

"It's both of us." She nodded at the scale, where a second weight had appeared beside hers: his claim. Not his blood—his *claim*. The royal line, the lawful succession, the birthright he'd spent seven years drinking himself numb to forget. The trial wanted him to surrender it entirely—not renounce it in words, but unmake it, give it up so completely that no cage could ever be forged from it again, no crown ever bound to his bloodline's back. "You give the throne. I give the blood. That's the price. That was always the price."

"Mara—"

"Don't." She turned to face him fully, and above them the mountain shrieked, and through the stone they could both hear it now: the footsteps, hundreds strong, entering the outer shrine. Vess. The hollow court. The Veil's last desperate play to stop them before the moon crested. Minutes, maybe. "Don't tell me it's too much. Don't tell me we'll find another way. There is no other way. There's you, and there's me, and there's a knife, and there's maybe a quarter hour of moonlight left, and I have spent six years swallowing death for a king who was already dead. I will not spend my life's last coin on anything less than ending it. Do you hear me? This is the only trade I've ever wanted to make."

His hands came up and framed her face, cold fingers, trembling slightly, and his forehead dropped to hers, and for one breath—one single stolen breath in the heart of a screaming mountain—they simply existed together, two people who had been used their whole lives, choosing each other in front of the oldest witness in the world.

"Together," he said.

"Together," she said.

Above them, the mountain's roof split with a crack like the world's spine breaking, and grey daylight poured down through the fissure—and in it, rising over the western peaks, pale and swollen and utterly wrong, the thirteenth moon crested the horizon early.

And with it, from the tunnel mouth behind them, unhurried and certain, came the sound of footsteps—and a voice Mara had known since childhood, dry and familiar and no longer entirely her own:

"Mara. Give me the map, child, and I'll let you both walk away."

Vess had arrived.

The trial's scale hung waiting in the air, pans empty, patient as the grave—and the moonlight through the fissure began, slowly, to turn black.
