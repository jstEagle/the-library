## 21. A Reluctant Coronation

The morning of the coronation, Mara Joubert woke before dawn out of six years of habit and lay staring at the canopy of a bed that was far too fine for her, listening to the palace breathe.

It breathed differently now. She had not realized, until the Veil was gone, how silent the court had been—how the servants had moved like clockwork figures, how laughter in the corridors had always arrived half a beat too late, as though summoned. Now there were sounds she'd forgotten a palace could make: a scullery maid swearing at a dropped pot, two guardsmen arguing about dice, somewhere above a window being opened and the ordinary racket of gulls arguing over the harbor. The hollow-eyed had woken three days ago with seven years missing and no way to fill them, and the grief of that filled every corridor—but it was human grief, at least. It stumbled. It wept at wrong hours. It was real.

Mara dressed herself. There had been an offer of a lady's maid; she'd refused it so quickly that the young woman doing the offering had looked wounded, and Mara had spent an awkward minute explaining that it was nothing personal, that she simply had never in her life let another person fasten her buttons, and could not start now with her heart hammering the way it was.

Because today Caelan would be crowned. And she had heard, secondhand through a flurry of clerks and heralds, what he intended to be crowned *with*.

* * *

The throne room had been scrubbed within an inch of its life, but no amount of scrubbing could take the memory out of the stones, and Mara felt it as she walked the length of the hall toward the dais: the pressure of all those years of standing audiences, the court arranged in rows with their faces upturned, motionless at midnight, drinking whatever the Veil poured into them through the crown's dark gold. Her spine prickled. Old habits. She catalogued the exits without meaning to—three doors, one servant's passage behind the arras, the high windows that would not bear a climber—and then made herself stop. She was not fleeing anything today. It took more discipline than facing Vess had.

Vess.

Her mentor stood near the dais in plain grey, no rings, no chain of office, her silver hair braided simply for the first time since Mara had known her. They had spoken twice since the mountain—since Aldric's spirit had unfolded from the ruin of the Veil like a letter finally opened, thanked his son, and gone wherever dead kings go, taking his seven stolen years with him. Vess had woken on the shrine steps old and emptied and *herself*, and the first thing she had done was look at Mara and say, in a voice scraped raw, "How much did I give it?"

"Everything," Mara had said. Because lying to Vess had never once worked, and it seemed a poor day to start.

Vess had nodded slowly, and wept, and Mara had stood beside her and not touched her, because some betrayals you cannot comfort your way across; you can only wait on the far side of them until the other person crosses.

Now Vess watched the hall filling with nobles and said, quietly, without turning, "You're standing like a taster."

"I am a taster."

"You were. You aren't anymore. Stand like whoever you've decided to be instead." A pause. "You'll want to decide soon. Everyone in this room is watching to see what you are. You saved their lives at a banquet and then vanished for a season and came back down off Thornfell with the heir and a shattered god at your back. That is the raw material of ten legends, girl, and legends get shaped by whoever reaches the wax first."

"That's cynical even for you."

"I'm taking quiet penance in a records office for the rest of my natural life. Let me be cynical; it's all I'm keeping." But there was something underneath the dryness—not warmth exactly, but the ghost of where warmth had lived, like a hearth's outline on a wall beneath new paint. "For what it's worth. I would have chosen differently, at every step. And I was wrong to teach you that survival is the only loyalty. Watch the king today. He knows something I didn't learn in forty years of spycraft."

"What's that?"

"How to hold power like it's going to burn him." Vess's mouth curved, faint and sad. "Because he watched it do exactly that."

Trumpets then, and the doors, and the hall rising in a rustle of silk and wool, and Mara turned to watch Caelan come.

* * *

He had argued with the heralds for two days, she knew. She had been there for part of it, arms crossed, offering unhelpful commentary while a procession of officials tried to explain to the King of Aldenmere that a coronation required certain things—a crown foremost among them, followed by an anointing oil blessed by the Temple, followed by an oath sworn upon the Crown Sceptre, all of which Caelan received with the flat, patient expression of a man being lectured on the proper care of wolves by the last surviving shepherd of a devoured flock.

"There will be no crown," he had said, for perhaps the ninth time. "There is no crown. I melted the shards myself. You were all there when I gave the order."

"The ceremonial crown, then, Your Majesty—the replica crafted for pageants—"

"A replica of the thing that ate my father's soul." He had smiled, which somehow made it worse for the heralds. "Master Corvel, I am going to say a word and I want you to think about it carefully before you object again. The word is *iron*."

So: iron. A plain circlet of blacksmith's iron, unadorned, hammered by the palace smith in a single evening from a bar of horseshoe stock, because—as Caelan had observed with terrible mildness—nothing in the treasury was as trustworthy as the metal they put on horses' feet. The Temple had protested that iron was a commoner's metal, fit for fetters and farm tools. Caelan had agreed. *Fit for work,* he'd said. *That's rather the point.*

Now he walked the length of his own hall in a doublet of dark blue with no gold on it anywhere, and the whispering started before he was halfway to the dais, and Mara watched the court look at their new king and try to reconcile him with every king they had ever flattered.

He stopped in front of the throne—his father's throne, which he had also wanted to burn, and had been talked down from with great difficulty—and did not sit. He stood beside it, hand resting on the worn arm, and let the silence stretch until the whispers died of embarrassment.

"Seven years ago," he said, and his voice carried in the hush without any effort at all, "my father banished me from this city. I believed, for seven years, that he did it because I was weak, and disappointing, and because he could not bear the sight of me. I have since learned that he did it because the thing wearing his face told him to, and because some small stubborn piece of the man my father actually was spent his last living strength getting me out of the cage before it closed." His hand tightened on the armrest. "I am not going to stand here and tell you the story. You'll hear it a hundred times, embroidered past recognition, within the month. What I am going to tell you is what I learned on a mountain, and it is short."

He lifted the iron circlet from a cushion borne by old Tam the under-chamberlain, who had served three kings and cried openly now, and who held it out with both hands like something consecrated.

"No crown will ever again be made in Aldenmere that can be bound to anything but the head that wears it. No regalia will be enchanted, no oath sealed in blood, no symbol raised above the law—it doesn't matter whether the thing bound into it calls itself a god or a tradition. I watched an ancient hunger wear my father for seven years and call itself the stability of the realm, and I watched good people—" his eyes found Vess, briefly, and stayed steady— "*good* people feed it small treasons and tell themselves it was mercy. So this is the whole of my coronation oath, and I will have it carved over the doors of this hall, and if any king after me orders it chiseled away, I want you to remember today and ask him why."

He raised the iron circlet.

"I am Caelan, son of Aldric, and I will rule you badly sometimes, because I am a man and not a symbol. I will be wrong, and you will be able to say so without losing your tongues. I will not ask you to be hollow, or smooth, or smiling. I ask one thing only: that when you see power beginning to eat—beginning anywhere, wearing anyone—you say it out loud, loudly, in this hall, and that you do not stop saying it until someone answers." He set the iron on his own head, unassisted, before the Temple patriarch could reach him. "That's the ceremony. Somebody bring wine. I know for a fact it isn't poisoned."

The hall did not cheer. For one long heartbeat it only breathed—and then old Tam laughed first, wet and cracked and human, and the sound broke loose and rolled through the court like the ice going off the river in spring, and Mara stood amid the noise of three hundred people laughing and crying in the same breath and thought: *he means it.* Six years of tasting other people's intentions had trained her palate finer than any poison-sense, and she tasted nothing in Caelan but a terrified, stubborn honesty, and it was the rarest flavor she had ever pulled from a room.

* * *

The feast afterward was the first meal in six years that Mara did not taste professionally.

She sat at the high table with a plate in front of her and caught herself, twice, running the old ritual—scent, color, texture, the faint bitter alarm at the edge of each bite—before making herself eat like an ordinary woman. It was absurd. Every cook in the palace had been questioned twice, every ingredient traced, and besides which the Veil was ash on a mountain and its poisons had been the old-fashioned mortal kind anyway. But the tongue does not unlearn its vigilance in three days. Perhaps it never does. She suspected she would spend years catching her own hand hovering over a wine cup, and decided she could live with that. Vigilance was only a curse when it was all you were.

Caelan leaned toward her, iron still on his head, looking faintly stunned by his own feast. "You're glaring at the pears."

"I'm assessing the pears."

"They're pears."

"And the last time someone brought pears to a state table in this palace, three people nearly died and I got a commendation and a rash of nightmares. Let me assess the pears."

"As you like." He was quiet a moment, turning his goblet. Around them the hall roared and clattered—real noise, messy noise—and he watched it like a man watching weather break after a long drought. Then, without preamble: "Stay."

Mara set down her fork.

"Not—" He winced at himself, pushed on. "That came out like a command. I've been rehearsing this for two days and it's already ruined. Not as taster. Not as spymaster's creature, not as the woman who broke the Veil so the ballads have somewhere to hang their verses, not as any post I could invent for you, because everything I could offer you is a cage with better upholstery and you've earned the right to tell me so to my face." He breathed. "As an equal. Whatever that looks like. If it looks like rooms in this palace, they're yours. If it looks like a horse and the road south, the horse is yours and I'll saddle it myself. Only—stay, or go, or stay and go and come back, but do it because you chose it. I watched a kingdom run on people who weren't allowed choices. I'm not building another one."

The strange thing—Mara thought about it later, turning it over like a coin she couldn't quite read—was that she had known it was coming. Since the fire on the mountainside, since *love admitted quietly against all sense*, some part of her had been waiting for exactly this moment with her answer already made, the way she had once waited for poison to bloom on her tongue: not hoping, just knowing.

"You're asking me to choose a kingdom that fed me to its fear for six years," she said.

"I'm asking you to help me make it into one that wouldn't."

"And if it fails? If in thirty years some clever chancellor finds a smoother cage and kinder words, and everyone shrugs and climbs in?"

"Then you'll be there saying it out loud, loudly, in the hall." He smiled—tired, lopsided, entirely without royal gravity. "You're better at refusing kings than anyone I've ever met. I'd like that particular skill kept close at hand."

Mara looked at him: the iron on his brow, the pear juice on his thumb, the eyes that had watched his father die twice and had come out of it gentler instead of harder, which she still did not fully understand and suspected she never would. Six years ago an orphan had been sold to a palace to swallow death in small doses so strangers could dine safely, and had told herself that was enough—that survival was the only loyalty, that wanting anything more was how they caught you.

Wanting, it turned out, was not how they caught you. Wanting was the only thing the Veil had never managed to consume.

"All right," she said. "But I want it written down. Not a title. A line in the law: *the king shall keep about him one person with no duty but to tell him the truth*, appointed for life, answerable to no office." She held his gaze. "And I want the first appointment to go to someone annoying."

Caelan laughed—the real laugh, the one from the smuggler roads—and around them the court glanced over at their new king laughing with the woman nobody could place, and began, in the way of courts, to build the legend.

* * *

She found Vess at dusk on the west terrace, where the old woman stood with her hands folded, watching the sun go down over Thornfell's far blue teeth.

"You heard, then," Mara said, coming to stand beside her.

"Heard that the king has appointed an official annoyance? Half the palace heard. The other half is drafting petitions to become the deputy annoyance." Vess did not smile, quite. "It's good law, Mara. Better than anything I built in forty years, and I built it knowing what I was feeding. That's my penance, you understand—not that I didn't know. That I *did*."

"I know."

"The records office. Seven years' worth of ledgers to reconstruct, names to restore, families to find and tell what happened to their mothers and fathers. Small work. Slow work. Work I can't talk my way out of." Vess's gaze stayed on the mountains. "I taught you that every road through Thornfell has two maps—the honest one and the useful one—and that a spy survives by carrying both. I was wrong about which one keeps you human. You carried the honest one. You always did, even when I beat it out of you, and I want you to know that I noticed, and that noticing was the last thing left in me that was mine."

Mara reached over and took her hand. Vess gripped back hard, once, and then let go, and neither of them said anything else, because there wasn't anything else that words were the right shape for.

Below the terrace, lamps were coming on across the city—one, then five, then dozens, ordinary lights in ordinary windows. Somewhere a fiddler had struck up, badly. The palace smelled of bread and beeswax and horse, and none of it hummed, none of it whispered in two voices, none of it was watching.

"Caelan wants to reopen the Thornfell passes," Vess said at last, conversationally. "Trade roads. Honest maps. Says the kingdom should know its own paths."

"He mentioned."

"He'll need someone who knows every goat track from here to the shrine." Vess turned, and in the last light her eyes were tired and clear and entirely her own. "Go with him, when he rides the borders. Come back when it suits you. A court that lets its conscience roam free is a healthier beast than one that chains it in the kitchens." She paused. "That's advice, not an order. I've retired from orders."

Mara looked east, where the mountains were going dark, where a shattered crown lay buried under cairn-stones and a shrine stood open to the sky with nothing bound inside it. In the morning there would be councils, and petitions, and a hundred small battles over the shape of a kingdom learning to be ruled by a man instead of a myth. Tonight there was a fiddler playing badly, and lamplight, and a door she could walk through whenever she wished—which, she understood now, was the entire difference between a home and a cage.

She heard Caelan's boots on the terrace stairs, unhurried, coming to find her.

She stayed to meet him.
