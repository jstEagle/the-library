## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The fence post had rotted through at the base, and Quentin Coldharbour knelt in the wet grass with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, working the old post loose the way he'd worked a hundred others loose before it. No magic in it. Just his hands, a digging bar, and the slow arithmetic of a man who had learned to measure his life in chores.

The Hollow did not care who he had been. That was its great mercy, and its great trick. The valley sat folded between two ridgelines like a coin dropped into a purse, and the road that entered it forgot itself halfway down the slope, so that travelers arriving at the ford could rarely say afterward which way they'd come. Maps showed a blank. Songs skipped the verse. Even the crows, Quentin suspected, only stayed because they'd forgotten there was anywhere else to go.

Magic could not follow it down. He'd tested that truth for thirty years, quietly, the way a man tests an old scar to see if it still aches. It did not ache. Nothing ached here. The valley held its people the way cupped hands hold water—loosely, patiently, asking nothing of them but that they stay.

He set the new post, tamped the earth around it with the flat of the bar, and paused, as he always did, to roll his left sleeve down before he straightened. The scars ran from wrist to elbow in interlocking bands, fine as woodgrain, the ritual marks that had been cut into him when he was nineteen and certain he would die young and gloriously. In the Hollow, a man with strange scars was just a man who'd been careless around a threshing machine, or so the story went, and no one had ever asked twice. He'd learned to move so the marks never caught the light. Thirty years of practice. He could do it in his sleep.

He usually did.

"Quentin! Quentin, the widow Tam's goat's in the barley again!"

He looked up. Merren stood at the fence line, ten years old and all elbows, breathless from running the length of the lower field. The boy had dirt on his chin and that particular urgency children carried like a torch—every small catastrophe a matter of state.

"Did you chase it out?"

"I talked to it," Merren said, with enormous dignity.

"And did the goat listen?"

"It listened to the rock I threw near it." Merren grinned, gap-toothed, and Quentin felt the old familiar ache—not the scar, but the other one, the one no valley could numb. The boy had a way with stone. Nothing anyone would call magic. Just a knack for finding the loose rock on a slope, for knowing where the well water ran, for throwing a stone that always seemed to land where it would do the most good. The Hollow's forgetting kept such small gifts safe, smothering them before they could catch the crown's attention.

Quentin intended to keep it that way until the day he died, which he had arranged, in his own mind, to be a long time from now, and uneventful.

"Go tell the widow her goat owes me a fence post," he said. "This one's coming out of her barley profits."

Merren ran off, whooping, and Quentin shouldered the digging bar and let himself, for the length of the walk back to his cottage, pretend that this was the whole of his life. A fence. A valley. A boy who talked to rocks and would never know why that mattered, why it made an old man's chest go tight as a fist.

* * *

The grave sat on the hill above the village, under a rowan tree that had been a sapling when he'd planted it. He climbed up in the late afternoon with a bundle of rowan sprigs and a rag, the way he came every seventh day, and he knelt in the grass and cleaned the lichen from the stone with the same care he'd once used to clean a sword.

The stone read: *ALDERIC, TEACHER. HE KEPT THE DOOR.*

No dates. No house sigil. A stranger passing would think it a shepherd's marker, or a midwife's. Only Quentin knew that the door Alderic had kept was the door of an entire tradition—the swordspeakers, the oath-bound, the men and women who had once carried the king's justice in spoken steel. Only Quentin knew that Alderic had carried that door on his back for forty years while the Purge burned it off the face of the world, and had died in this valley, in Quentin's arms, whispering that one survivor was one too many to waste.

"He kept the door," Quentin said to the stone, as he always said. "I'm only keeping the grass."

The wind moved through the rowan. He cut the lichen, pulled the weeds, and let himself talk a little, the way he did when the silence grew too heavy. He told the grave about the fence post. About Merren and the goat. About the dream he'd had twice this month, the one where he stood in a burning hall and someone called his name in a voice like a struck bell, and he woke reaching for a sword that had been ash for thirty years.

"You'd tell me that dreams mean nothing," he said. "You'd tell me a swordspeaker who dreams of bells is a man who misses the noise, nothing more." He sat back on his heels. "But you'd be lying, old man. You were always lying to me, at the end. Telling me I'd be safe. Telling me the valley would hold."

The rowan leaves shivered. He knew it was wind. He knew it was wind the way he knew the valley forgot, the way he knew his scars were only scars: by deciding to.

He was halfway down the hill when he saw the stranger.

* * *

The man came up the valley road at dusk, and he came wrong.

Quentin had spent thirty years watching the road, the way a man watches a scar. Most travelers came down the switchbacks in daylight, moving at the pace of the valley, already forgetting why they'd come by the time they reached the ford. This man came in the last red light, stumbling, one hand pressed to his side, and he did not forget. Quentin could see it from the fence line—the way the man's eyes fixed on the village, on the smoke of the chimneys, with terrible purpose. The road forgot. The man refused to.

Quentin dropped the bar and ran.

He reached him at the ford. The stranger was perhaps forty, dressed as a tinker, but the clothes were wrong—a tinker's coat doesn't hide a soldier's shoulders, and a tinker doesn't bleed through his side in a long dark sheet that says *sword wound* to any man who's ever given or taken one. Quentin caught him as his knees went, and the man's weight came against him, and up close Quentin could see the fever-bright eyes, the lips already gray.

"Water," Quentin said. "There's a house—"

"No house." The man's hand shot out and gripped Quentin's wrist—gripped the left wrist, the scarred one, and the grip had a soldier's accuracy, closing exactly where the marks began. His eyes went wide. "You're him."

"You're bleeding out. Walk with me."

"You're *him*." The stranger fumbled at his coat with his free hand, dragging something from the inner lining—a leather tube, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. Royal wax. Quentin's stomach turned over slowly, like a stone in a well. "I carried it through three checkpoints. Through the Ashford burn. They took the others, they took—" A cough took him, and it came up red. "The writ. For the last swordspeaker. It has to reach—"

"Stop." Quentin said it quietly, and the word came out with an edge he hadn't let himself use in thirty years, and the man's eyes fixed on him with a kind of terrible joy.

"There you are," the stranger whispered. "There you are. *Lord Swordspeaker.*"

The title went through Quentin like a blade finding the gap in armor. No one living knew it. Alderic was dead. The others were ash. The title had been struck from every record, burned from every tongue, and here it was in a dying tinker's mouth, in the valley that forgot, at the ford where nothing was supposed to be able to follow.

"Who sent you?" Quentin lowered the man to the grass. "Who gave you that writ? Who cut you?"

"The hunt," the man said. "The witch-hunters. They're not hunting, swordspeaker. That's the lie. That's the—" His back arched. Blood at his teeth. "The king. Look at the king. It's not the king anymore. It eats the gifted, it eats them all, it's *almost done*, it needs—" His hand found Quentin's collar and pulled, with the last of a dying man's strength, until their faces were a hand's width apart. "It needs *you*. You're the last. You're the only door left. The writ—take the writ—swear you'll—"

Quentin took the tube. The man's eyes were going glassy, fixed on something far past Quentin's shoulder, past the rowan hill, past the ridgelines.

"Alderic's boy," the stranger said, and smiled, and there was blood in the smile. "He said you'd be mending something. He said I'd know you by the fences."

Then he was gone, between one breath and the next, with the valley's first star coming out above him, and the writ heavy as a stone in Quentin's hand.

Quentin knelt there a long time in the dark, the ford talking quietly around his knees, the dead man's blood soaking cold through his shirt. He did not open the tube. He knew what was in it. He knew it the way he knew the dreams of bells, the way he knew the itch under his scars, the way he had known, for three nights running, that the valley's forgetting had begun to thin.

Somewhere up the switchbacks, in the last light, something was coming down the road that the road could not make forget.

Quentin closed the dead man's eyes, took up the writ, and turned toward the village—toward Merren, toward the widow's goat, toward thirty years of mended fences—walking faster than he had walked in three decades, because the door Alderic kept had just been broken open, and he could hear, faint and far and getting closer, the sound of hooves at the ford.
