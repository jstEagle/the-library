## 2. Hunters at the Ford

The rain came up the valley at dawn, and Quentin felt it in his scars before he heard it on the roof.

That was the cruelty of the Hollow: it took the magic out of a man but left him remembering. Thirty years of mending fences and hauling water, and still his body woke some mornings humming with the ghost of words he'd sworn never to speak again. He lay in his narrow bed listening to the rain drum the shingles and pressed his thumb against the ritual scarring at his wrist—the old binding-marks, smooth as river stone now, cold as they had always been since Brother Aldous cut them—and told himself what he told himself every morning. *You are a farmer. You are a widower of nothing. You are no one.*

The dead stranger from three days past said otherwise.

They had buried him behind the meetinghouse, under the rowan that marked the valley's only consecrated ground, because whatever else he was—a courier, a spy, a fool who'd bled out on Quentin's doorstep—he had died saying *Swordspeaker* like a prayer, and the village deserved to know what had come looking for them. Old Marta had stitched him shut. The children had been kept away. And Quentin had spent three nights not sleeping, listening for hooves.

On the fourth morning he stopped waiting and went down to the ford.

* * *

The Aldenwater ran shallow there, brown and quick over its stones, and the crossing was the only way into the valley wide enough for horses. Beyond it the road climbed through alder and hazel until it vanished into the pass. Quentin stood among the reeds with a scythe in his hands—not a sword; he hadn't touched a sword in thirty years, wouldn't let himself, though his grip found the scythe's balance as if it had never forgotten anything—and watched four riders come down through the trees.

They wore grey. Not the king's scarlet, not the black of the old Inquisition. Grey cloaks, grey hoods, and across their breasts a badge worked in silver thread: a circle broken by a single line. Quentin knew it. Everyone of his generation knew it. The Purge had worn that mark like a brand wears hide.

Their own magic failed them at the treeline. He saw it happen—one of them raised a hand and spoke a word of finding, the soft grey whisper hunters used to sniff out gifted blood, and the word simply *died*, swallowed by the valley the way the Hollow swallowed everything. The rider flinched as if slapped. His companions laughed, short and ugly.

But steel came over the ford just fine.

Quentin counted them the way Aldous had taught him—count everything twice, trust nothing once—and did not like the arithmetic. Four riders, one of them big through the shoulders, moving like a man who'd spent his life in a saddle. Two more carried crossbows slung easy at their hips. The last rode slightly apart, and when the hood turned toward the reeds where Quentin stood, he felt the look go through him like a fishhook.

"Old man," the leader called. Her voice was a woman's, dry as chalk. "Come out where I can see you."

He stepped from the reeds. No point pretending otherwise; they'd marked him already, or thought they had—an old farmer with a scythe was worth a glance, nothing more. Let them spend their suspicion on nothing. That had always been his talent, these thirty years: being nothing so well that even he believed it sometimes.

"We're looking for a man," the woman said. She dismounted, boots splashing, and held up a folded paper. The royal seal hung off it in red wax. "Messenger came through here four days back. Never arrived at the far villages."

"He died," Quentin said. "Fever. We buried him by the meetinghouse."

"Fever." She smiled without warmth. "And what did he carry?"

"I don't read other men's letters, mistress."

"No," she agreed. "But you *buried* him. Odd duty for a farmer." She looked past him, up the valley, at the smoke of chimneys, the wet green fields. Something in her face hungered. "This place stinks of old wards. Dead ones. Someone paid dearly to make this hole in the world, and someone paid again when they died. Who was he? The warden?"

"There's no one like that here."

"There is always someone like that everywhere," she said. "We have burned forty-one valleys like this one since spring."

The number landed in his chest like a dropped anvil. Forty-one. He had told himself the Hollow was forgotten, that the world beyond the pass had better things to hunt than one old man growing cabbages. Forty-one valleys said otherwise. The Vessel—or whoever wore the crown now—was sweeping the map clean.

"You'll want to be going, then," Quentin said. "Lots more valleys."

One of the crossbowmen laughed. The big rider didn't. He had been watching Quentin's hands the whole time, and now he said, quietly, "Marta, look at his wrists."

Quentin's sleeves were wet from the reeds and had ridden up an inch. An inch was enough. The binding-scars caught the grey morning light, smooth and pale, unmistakable to anyone who knew what binding-marks looked like.

The woman called Marta drew a long knife instead of a sword. "Bind-cut," she said, almost tenderly. "Oh, you poor old hound. They cut your voice out and left you alive. Do you know how rare that makes you?" She stepped closer through the shallows. "A swordspeaker who can't speak. You're not prey, grandfather. You're a relic."

Everything in Quentin screamed to run—but running confirmed everything, and Merren was upstream.

Merren. The thought arrived all at once, whole and terrible. The boy fished this stretch every morning at first light, rain or shine, and it was raining, and he would be under the willow at the deep pool not two hundred paces above the ford, and he had been talking to the stones there for years, and Quentin had told himself it was a child's fancy, the way children talk to anything, because the alternative was unthinkable—

Because if the boy was what Quentin feared he was, then the writ the dead courier carried wasn't about Quentin at all. Or not only. *Every gifted soul.* The harvest didn't skip children.

"Last chance to be a farmer," Marta said.

Quentin swung the scythe.

It was not a sword stroke. It was a haymaker's arc, graceless and desperate, and she was fast—she caught it on her knife's spine and the shock nearly tore the tool from his hands—but gracelessness has its own surprise, and for one heartbeat she had expected artistry and gotten a farmhand's fury. Then the crossbowman was cranking his piece up and the big rider was drawing steel, and Quentin did the thing he had sworn for thirty years he would never do again.

He killed a man.

Not with magic—with a scythe's hooked blade opened up along the crossbowman's thigh, deep, arterial, the man folding into the water with a sound of pure astonishment. Quentin barely registered it. His body remembered everything his mind had tried to bury: distance, angle, the half-second where a man decides whether to commit. The big rider committed. Steel met steel—not scythe against sword, because Marta's knife had opened Quentin's forearm and he'd abandoned the tool for the rider's own short sword, torn from a dying hand—and the fight lasted six seconds and felt like a year.

When it ended, Quentin stood knee-deep in the Aldenwater with blood thinning off his blade, and two of the four hunters floated or sprawled, and Marta and the big one had fallen back to the far bank, and the rain came down on all of it equally, indifferent as the valley itself.

His hands shook. Not from fear. From the *rightness* of it—that was the horror. Thirty years of peace and his body had taken to killing again like water to a wheel-race.

"Marta!" the big rider shouted across the water. "The writ says bring them breathing!"

"The writ," Marta called back, and there was something almost merry in it, something that made Quentin's skin crawl worse than any threat, "says bring the *gifted*. This one's gift is dead. Kill the relic."

She came back across the ford.

* * *

Later, Quentin could never say how long the second fight lasted, only that it ended at the willow tree, because that was where he had been retreating—drawing them away from the village, away from the boy, buying distance with his own failing strength. Marta was better than him. Younger, faster, trained while he'd been planting turnips. She had him against the willow's trunk with her knife at his throat when the ground spoke.

Everyone heard it. A groan under the world, deep as a mountain clearing its throat, and the roots of the willow heaved from the earth like the fingers of a drowning man, and Marta stumbled back into a pit of mud and stone that hadn't existed a heartbeat before, screaming, and the big rider's horse went down shrieking.

At the edge of the pool, soaked to the waist, white-faced, stood a twelve-year-old boy with a fishing pole dangling forgotten from one hand and tears cutting tracks through the dirt on his cheeks.

"*Stop!*" Merren screamed—at the rocks, at the world, at himself. "*Stop, stop, stop!*"

And the valley stopped. The roots settled. The stones rolled back down like guilty animals.

In the ringing silence, Marta pulled herself from the pit, bleeding, mud-caked, and looked at the boy with an expression Quentin would see in his dreams for the rest of his life: the rapture of a hunter who has just flushed exactly the quarry she was sent for.

"There you are," she breathed.

Quentin moved before thinking. The short sword was still in his hand. What happened next happened in the space between two raindrops—Marta turning, Merren frozen, the big rider struggling free of his dead horse—and Quentin drove the blade through the gap beneath her jaw, quick and merciful as he knew how to be, because some part of him understood that whatever mercy meant anymore, it did not mean letting her take the boy.

She died looking at him with those hungry eyes, and the last thing she did was smile.

The big rider was gone—fled upstream, horseless, into the hazel scrub, and Quentin let him go because Merren was on his knees in the shallows sobbing and shaking and saying, over and over, "I didn't mean to, Uncle Quentin, I only asked the stones for help, I didn't know they'd listen, I didn't *know*—"

Quentin gathered the boy against his chest, blood and rain and river water between them, and held on.

"It's not your fault," he said, and his voice broke on it, because the lie was enormous and ancient and his. It had never been the gift's fault. It had never been the boy's fault. It was the world's fault, the crown's fault, the hunger wearing the throne's fault—and it would come for Merren now, the way it had come for all of them, the way it had once come for Quentin, and no valley deep enough would hide a child whose voice moved mountains.

"They heard me," Merren whispered into his coat. "The stones. They've always heard me, and now *they* heard too, didn't they? The bad ones. They heard."

"Yes," Quentin said, because the boy deserved truth after this, even this truth. "They heard."

He looked downstream, at the bodies drifting gently against the reeds, at the grey cloak snagged on a willow root with its silver badge of the broken circle, and understood that the surviving rider was already carrying word over the pass. Whatever came next would not be four. It would be forty. It would be everything.

Thirty years of hiding, ended in six seconds of remembering.

He wiped the sword on the grass, took Merren's small cold hand in his scarred one, and turned up the valley toward home—to fetch what mattered, to warn the village, to decide in the next hour whether to run again or, for the first time since the Purge, stand.

Halfway there, he stopped. Because standing in the path ahead, mud-streaked and breathing hard like a woman who had run a very long way very fast, with a hunting bow strung across her back and the broken-circle badge torn from her own grey cloak, was a stranger with a huntress's eyes and a prisoner's chains hanging broken from her wrists.

"Don't," she said, raising empty hands. "Whatever you're about to do—don't. I need to tell you what's really on that writ. And you need to hear it before their reinforcements reach the ford."
