## 3. What the Prisoner Knew

The rope bit into Sera Vane's wrists, and she was almost grateful for it.

Pain kept her honest. Three days in the root cellar beneath Quentin Coldharbour's cottage—three days of cold, dark, and the smell of turned earth—and the pain in her shoulders was the only thing that still felt like the Order. Everything else had come apart.

She heard him coming down the ladder before she saw him: the careful weight of a man who had spent thirty years learning to move quietly in his own home. A lantern's glow preceded him, gilding the dirt walls, the hanging bulbs of dried garlic, the shelf of clay jars sealed with wax. He carried a bowl of barley stew and set it on an upturned crate a full arm's length from her, as he always did, as if proximity were a courtesy they both understood.

"You haven't eaten," he said.

"I have eaten."

"Half of it. You're testing whether I'll poison you."

"And are you?"

"No." He crouched, forearms on his knees, and studied her with those gray eyes that gave away nothing. "I'm testing whether you'll lie to me again."

Sera leaned her head back against the stone. The cellar was old, older than the cottage above it; the walls were fitted without mortar, by hands that had known what they were doing. She'd noticed that the first night, cataloguing escape routes out of habit. There were none. The ladder was the door, and Coldharbour stood at the top of it.

"My name is true," she said. "Sera Vane. Third Circle, Order of the Quiet Flame. Witch-hunter eleven years. That much I told you straight."

"The rest you told me crooked."

"The rest you didn't ask for."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, quickly gone, but she caught it—the way a hunter catches movement in tall grass. *There. He can still do that.*

"Ask me, then," she said.

* * *

He asked her everything, and this time she answered, because there was nothing left to buy with lies and no one left to sell them to.

She told him about the writ the dying courier had carried into the valley—a royal summons bearing the king's seal, ordering the swordspeaker Quentin Coldharbour brought to the capital alive. She told him how the Order had laughed when the order came down, quietly, the way soldiers laugh at things that frighten them. Swordspeakers had been ash for thirty years. The Purge had seen to that. And now the crown wanted one found, named, and delivered like a lost hound.

"We thought it was sentiment," she said. "An old man's obsession. The king has been strange these two years—withdrawn, silent at council, his edicts coming down in a hand none of the clerks recognized. We told ourselves it was grief. The queen died, and kings go strange."

"And it wasn't grief."

"It was hunger." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. She made herself keep going. "Commander Dross took me aside before we crossed the ford. Not to brief me—to warn me. He said, *Vane, whatever you see in that valley, count the gifted, not the guilty.* I didn't understand him. So I checked the ledgers."

Quentin had gone very still. In the lantern light his face looked carved from something patient and old.

"The Order keeps records of every arrest," Sera continued. "Every witch, every hedge-mage, every child who talked to wells or charmed a fever out of a goat. Eleven years I've written my share of them. And for two years, the disposition column has changed. Used to be *executed*, *imprisoned*, *released*. Now it says one word, over and over: *delivered*. Delivered to the palace. Delivered to the king's household. No trials. No executions. No bodies returned to families for burial." She swallowed. "Four hundred and six souls in twenty-two months, Coldharbour. The gifted of three provinces, loaded into wagons under seal of night and driven east. And none of them ever seen again."

The lantern flame bent in some draft she couldn't feel. Or maybe it bent because the room itself seemed to lean toward him, toward the silence he was keeping.

"You think it eats them," he said at last. Not a question. His voice was rough, like a blade drawn across stone.

"I know what I saw." She shifted forward despite the ropes, and the movement cost her, and she let him see it cost her. Honesty was all she had left to trade. "Last autumn, in Corren's Ford, we took a woman who could quiet storms. Old, half-mad, harmless. Standard transport to the capital. But the escort commander met us on the road himself—Dross, in person, which never happens for a storm-quieter—and he looked at her through the wagon bars the way a man looks at a purse of gold he's about to spend. And when the wagon pulled away, I swear on my mother's grave the horses were afraid of it. Not of us. Of *her*. Like something inside her was already not her anymore."

She stopped. The cellar held the words the way the earth holds water.

"They're not prisoners," she said quietly. "They're provisions."

* * *

He untied her after that.

Not all the way—one wrist, so she could eat—but it was more than he'd given her in three days, and they both knew what it meant. Trust, in the Order, was measured exactly this way: in inches of rope.

They sat on the crate together, passing the bowl back and forth like two soldiers on a wall, and Sera found herself talking about things she hadn't planned to talk about. About joining the Order at fourteen because it was that or the tanneries. About the first arrest she'd made, a boy of nine who glowed faintly in the dark, and how she'd told herself the cells were warm and the food was adequate. About the morning, half a year ago, when she'd finally read the ledgers properly and understood what *adequate* had been feeding.

"Why defect?" Quentin asked. "Why not stay blind? Blind hunters live long."

She looked at him. Up close, in the lantern light, the famous swordspeaker was just a man with sunburned hands and a fence-mender's stoop, and yet something came off him like heat off a forge—the sense of a held breath, thirty years long.

"Because my brother was gifted," she said. "Small gift. He could make bread rise perfect, every loaf, just by humming at it. When the new dispositions started, he ran. I helped him run. I gave him coin and a road name and I told him to go north past the salt marshes where the Order doesn't ride." Her jaw tightened. "Six weeks ago his name came through the intake rolls. *Delivered.* They took him off a ferry at Marlow's Crossing. Someone sold him, or someone recognized the humming, and they put him in a wagon heading east, and somewhere between there and the palace my brother stopped being a person and became—" She couldn't finish it. She set the bowl down with exaggerated care, the way you do when your hands want to shake and you won't let them.

Quentin said nothing. But he did something she hadn't expected: he reached over and closed her fingers back around the bowl, gently, and pushed it toward her.

"Eat," he said. "Grief keeps better on a full stomach. My mentor taught me that."

* * *

It was near dawn when she gave him the rest of it—the part she'd been holding back, saving, the way you save your last arrow.

"There's a route," she said. "Into the capital. The Order moves supply wagons twice a month from the garrison at Thornwick—grain, salt, lamp oil. The wagons pass the East Gate at dusk, and the gate guards don't search crown property. I know the rotation. I know the passwords. I know which sergeant takes bribes and which one takes offense." She paused. "I also know that if this is a trap, if any part of me is still loyal enough to be used, then walking you into that city is exactly what the Order wants. You should consider that."

"I have considered it."

"Have you? Because I've considered it about myself, every hour since the ford. I don't know what's left in me that isn't theirs. Loyalty rots slow, Coldharbour. You don't feel it going."

"That," he said, "is the first honest thing anyone from the Order has ever said to me." He rose, joints cracking, and stood over her in the lantern light. "Here is what I know. You could have lied about the ledgers. You could have let me walk into the capital blind. Instead you gave me four hundred and six names' worth of reason to burn their whole grey house down. If that's rot, it's rot pointed the right direction."

From above, faint through the floorboards, came a sound that made them both freeze: footsteps. Small ones. Then a knock at the cellar door, and a voice—Merren's voice, thin with sleep and fear.

"Mister Quentin? Mister Quentin, the stones are talking again. They say riders on the north ridge. Lots of them. And they're saying—" the boy's breath hitched— "they're saying one of them smells like the cellar lady."

Sera was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, rope falling from her wrist, heart slamming. Quentin was already reaching for the sword above the lintel, and his eyes when they met hers were not the eyes of a farmer.

"Dross," she breathed. "He tracked me. He tracked *us*."

Above them, on the north ridge, the first light of dawn showed a line of riders against the burning sky—and Sera Vane understood, with terrible clarity, that the family she hadn't meant to start was about to be tested before it finished being born.
