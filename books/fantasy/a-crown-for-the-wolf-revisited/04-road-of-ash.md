## 4. Road of Ash

The road out of the Hollow ran north through country Quentin had not seen in thirty years, and it had changed the way a face changes after grief—same bones, same shape, but emptied of something essential.

The first village was called Brackenmere. He knew because the signpost still stood at the crossroads, though the paint had blistered and run so that the letters looked like they were weeping. Beyond it, where he remembered whitewashed cottages and a mill wheel turning slow and green with moss, there was only black. The fire had come through months ago—the ash had settled into the ruts of the cart track, gray as old snow—and yet the smell of burning still hung in the air, faint and stubborn, the way a dead man's name hangs over his house.

Quentin reined the mule to a stop and sat looking at it for a long moment.

"Was it like this when you came here?" Merren asked from behind him. The boy's voice was carefully flat, the way children's voices go flat when they are trying very hard not to feel something.

"No," Quentin said. "When I came here, everything was alive."

Sera Vane said nothing. She rode with her hood up and her wounded arm bound tight against her chest, and she looked at the ruin of Brackenmere the way a soldier looks at a battlefield she once fought on—not with horror but with recognition. That frightened Quentin more than the ashes did.

They walked the mule down the village's one street. Charred doorframes stood open to the sky like missing teeth. A well remained in the square, its stones scorched black on one side, and beside it someone had chalked a mark on the ground—a circle with a slash through it, the crown's sign for *cleared*. Beneath it, smaller, in a different hand: *taken, 14 souls*.

"Fourteen," Sera read aloud. Her voice did not waver, but her good hand tightened on the reins until the leather creaked. "That's a harvest crew, not a hunt. Hunts take one or two. This—" She gestured at the whole gray silence of the place. "This is what happens when the lists come back full."

"The lists?"

"Every witch-hunter keeps a ledger. Names, villages, signs of the gift." She turned her hooded head toward him. "You'd be in mine, Coldharbour. Page one. I wrote your entry myself, eleven years ago. *Q.C., Hollow, swordspeaker presumed dormant.* I argued against sending anyone. I lost that argument the day the king started eating."

Merren made a small sound. Quentin glanced back and saw the boy staring at the chalk circle, at the number beneath it, his lips moving as he counted to fourteen and stopped, because fourteen was more than a boy could hold in his head all at once.

"We won't find anyone here," Quentin said gently. "We'll find water, if the well's not fouled. We rest, we move on."

But Merren wasn't listening anymore. He had slid down off the mule and was crouched by the well, his palm flat against the scorched stone, and Quentin saw the boy's shoulders change—the particular stillness that came over him when the stone spoke.

"It remembers," Merren whispered. "The fire. It says the fire was cold."

Quentin went cold himself, all the way through. He crossed the square in four strides and pulled the boy's hand off the well. "Don't do that here."

"But it wanted to tell me—"

"I know what it wanted. And I know what happens to boys who listen too loudly in places like this." He kept his voice low, kept his grip gentle, but he did not let go. "There are things that ride on gift-given sound, Merren. Things that follow it home. You speak to stone in a burned village, and somewhere a ledger gets a new line written in it."

The boy looked up at him with those grave, slate-colored eyes, and Quentin saw that he hadn't scared him. He had taught him something the boy already half-knew, and the knowing had settled into him like a stone settling into mud.

"All right," Merren said. "But it wasn't the fire that burned them. Stone doesn't lie about fire. The people were already gone before it burned. Taken first. Then the fire, to hide the taking."

Behind them, Sera dismounted slowly. "That matches the reports I was never supposed to read," she said quietly.

*

They made camp that night in the shell of a tithe-barn two miles past Brackenmere, because the barn had walls on three sides and a roof over one corner, and because none of them could bear to sleep among the houses. Quentin built no fire. Sera built no fire. But Merren, sitting with his back against a beam that still smelled faintly of grain and horses, cupped his hands around nothing and whispered to a fist-sized piece of fieldstone he'd picked up on the road, and the stone glowed.

Not much. A dull ember-orange, like a coal remembering heat. But in the dark of the ruined barn it was a beacon, and Quentin crossed the space between them in three steps and closed his hand over the boy's.

"What did I tell you?"

"Not to talk loud." Merren's chin came up, stubborn. "I wasn't talking loud. I was talking *small*. There's a difference."

"There is," Quentin admitted. "And you found it faster than most. But small isn't silent. Look." He nodded at the light leaking between their fingers. "That glow? Every gifted soul within a mile can feel that. Every hunter's charm can taste it. You're ringing a bell, boy, just quietly."

Merren stared at their joined hands. The glow died. In the sudden dark his face was a pale smudge. "Then how do I learn without ringing bells?"

It was a fair question, and it deserved better than the answer Quentin had been carrying for thirty years, which was *you don't; you bury it; you let it starve in the dark until it stops asking*. He had buried his own gift so deep that some mornings he woke and could not remember the weight of a sword singing in his hand, and other mornings—the bad ones—he woke with the oath-words rising in his throat like bile, *I renounce, I refuse, I bind*, and had to press his face into the pillow until they receded.

He thought of the chalk circle in Brackenmere. Fourteen souls. He thought of the way Merren's whole face opened when the stone answered him, like a window thrown wide in a shuttered house.

"You learn by learning what the bell is," Quentin said at last. "Come here. Both of you. This is going to sound like nonsense, and then it's going to sound like sense."

They gathered close in the dark. Quentin took the fieldstone and held it flat on his palm.

"A gift isn't a shout," he said. "That's what the hunters understand, and it's why they win. They listen for shouting. But every gift has a whisper-shape too. Swordspeakers—we don't have to cry the oath aloud. There's a way to say it inside the bone, where the sound never leaves your body. Stone-speakers—" he nodded at Merren— "can do the same. Don't ask the stone to hear you. Ask it to *feel* you. Put the words in your skin instead of your breath."

"That's not how the peddler described it," Merren said. "Back before. He said magic was wanting something so hard the world gives it."

"The peddler's dead," Sera said softly, "or harvested. Wanting loudly is how they find you."

Merren was quiet a moment. Then he took the stone back and held it against his chest, both hands, the way a child holds something broken that he means to mend. His eyes squeezed shut. His lips moved—but no breath came, no sound, and Quentin watched closely, watching not the boy's mouth but the stone.

For a long time, nothing. Then—faintly, so faintly Quentin might have imagined it—the stone warmed. Not glowing. Just warming, the way a hearthstone holds the memory of yesterday's fire.

Merren opened his eyes. They were wet. "It heard me," he whispered. "Inside. It heard me *inside*."

"Yes," Quentin said, and found that his own voice had gone rough. "Yes, it did."

He sat back against the beam and looked up through the hole where the roof had burned away, at the stars, sharp and indifferent. And beneath the small warmth of the lesson, beneath the boy's quiet wonder, the old oath stirred in him like a snake under floorboards. *I renounce. I refuse. I bind.* He had spoken those words over his mentor's grave with blood still on his hands, and the binding had taken his gift and left him the ache of it, the phantom limb, the song he could no longer sing.

And now he was teaching a child to sing quieter. Not to stop. To hide. To survive. Was that mercy or was it training another soldier for a war that ate children? He didn't know. He suspected he wouldn't know until the war arrived at whatever door they were standing behind.

"Quentin?" Merren's voice, small in the dark. "What does it feel like? Yours. When you use it."

"It feels like being right," Quentin said, before he could stop himself. "Like the world finally agreeing with you."

"And now?"

"Now it feels like remembering a language I used to dream in." He reached over and ruffled the boy's hair, rougher than affection, gentler than apology. "Sleep. Tomorrow we teach your feet to be quiet too."

Three days north, the villages stopped being burned and started being empty.

That was worse. Wickerdown stood intact—roofs on, doors shut, washing still strung between windows, gone gray with weather. A cradle rocked in an upstairs room where no draft should reach it. Meals had been left on tables. The chalk marks were everywhere: circles with slashes, on doors, on lintels, on the church steps, dozens of them, and beneath each one numbers. Quentin counted as they walked the street because someone had to, and the total, when he finished, was sixty-one.

"No fires here," Sera murmured. "No resistance. They came at night, marked the houses, and the gifted walked out on their own feet." Her jaw worked. "Charm-work. Compulsion laid over the whole village. I've seen the crates they pack the sleepers in. Straw and wool, like glassware."

"They took a whole town's worth," Quentin said. "Sixty-one. From one village."

"From one village on one night." Sera pulled her hood back—she'd stopped bothering with it since Brackenmere, as if the ash had made hiding pointless—and Quentin saw that her face had gone the color of the road. "Coldharbour, I told you the Vessel was harvesting. I didn't tell you the scale, because I didn't know it. I was a huntress. I caught poachers and hedge-witches and one poor madwoman who talked to rivers. This isn't my war anymore than it's yours. This is an ending. It's gathering everything, everywhere, all at once."

Merren had stopped walking. He stood in the middle of the empty street with his fists clenched, and the stones under his feet—Quentin felt it before he understood it, a tremor coming up through his boot-soles, fine as a plucked string.

"Merren," he said. "Boy. Breathe."

"They left the cradle rocking," Merren said. His voice cracked down the middle. "Who leaves a cradle rocking? They didn't even wake the babies up. They just—took them, and the cradle kept—" The tremor deepened. A shutter banged somewhere. A chimney stack shed a thread of dust.

Quentin knelt in front of him, right there in the ash and the silence, and took the boy's face in his scarred hands.

"Listen to me. Anger is fuel. I know that better than anyone breathing. But fuel burns the house down if you don't build it a hearth." He pressed his forehead briefly to the boy's. "So we'll give it one. You want to fight them? Then every ounce of this goes into control. Into getting us to the capital alive. Into being the stone that breaks their teeth instead of the pebble they kick aside. Can you give me that?"

Merren's breath shuddered out of him. Under their feet, the trembling eased, slowed, stilled. "I can try."

"Trying is the whole craft," Quentin said. "Everything else is noise."

He rose, and turned to say as much to Sera—and stopped.

She was standing rigid at the far end of the street, staring at the last house, the one with the chalked circle on its door. Nailed above the lintel, where a horseshoe might hang on any farm in the realm, was a strip of parchment bearing the crown's seal, and beneath the seal, in a clerk's neat hand, a date.

Nine days hence. The solstice.

And below the date, a line that made Sera's breath catch audibly across the whole empty length of Wickerdown:

*All vessels to the capital. The King requires them complete.*
