## 5. The Daughter of Cinders

Isolde had learned long ago that the capital could be read like a face. Ashvale's walls had a way of telling the truth to anyone patient enough to watch: the merchants' smiles that didn't reach their eyes, the guards who counted coins instead of watching gates, the beggars who vanished in the night and reappeared as rumors. For three years she had watched, catalogued, survived—wearing her grief like a second skin beneath whatever gown the occasion demanded.

Tonight the city wore mourning it wouldn't name.

She stood on the balcony of the derelict customs house above Ropemaker's Stair, watching the river traffic below. The Solstice was nine days off, and the eclipse would swallow the sun whole—the astrologers said so with the nervous certainty of men who had checked their figures four times. The palace had already begun hanging black-and-gold banners for the *Vigil of Renewal*, a ceremony Isolde had never heard of before this year. Her father had never been a ceremonial man. Her father had loved loud feasts, bad hunting songs, and her mother more than sense. The thing wearing his crown now loved silence, and order, and the slow disappearance of anyone whose eyes caught light strangely.

That was why she was here, in a borrowed cloak, in a building no one used, waiting for smugglers.

The report had come through one of her few remaining contacts—a dockhand who owed her mother's memory a debt. Three travelers on the ash road from the west. A woman moving like a soldier though dressed as a peddler. A boy of perhaps ten who flinched from every crowd. And a man in his fifties, gray at the temples, walking with the particular economy of someone who had once been very dangerous and had spent decades pretending otherwise.

And the woman had asked questions at the ferry crossing. Questions about the king. About whether His Majesty still received petitioners. About whether anyone had seen him eat.

*Anyone who asks those questions is either mad or mine,* Isolde thought. *Either way, I want to meet them.*

Below, in the shadow of the customs house archway, lanterns moved. Three of them. Right on schedule.

She descended the stairs without hurry, pulling her hood low, and let herself become the person she'd needed to invent two years ago: Cinder, the information broker with no face and no name, who traded in secrets the court whispered but never spoke aloud. Even her own household believed Cinder was a man. It was easier that way. Everything was easier when nobody looked too closely at the king's disgraced daughter, the princess who had publicly accused her father's new counselors of murder and been confined to her apartments for a season for her trouble. Confinement taught a person many useful things. Chief among them: how to leave.

The archway was dark except for the single lamp she'd permitted—one flame, guttering, casting more shadow than light. She stepped into it deliberately, letting them see her before they saw anything else.

"You're late," she said, in the flat rasp she used for Cinder. "The tide doesn't wait, and neither do I."

Three shapes resolved out of the gloom. The woman first—Sera Vane, if the dockhand's description held true, and Isolde knew it did because she'd memorized the rosters of the Royal Hunt before burning them. A huntress gone missing eight months past, presumed dead or worse. Her hand rested on a knife with the ease of long habit, and her eyes did the arithmetic of exits before they did anything else. A soldier's eyes. A hunted soldier's.

Then the boy, pressed close to the woman's side, staring up at the vaulted stone overhead as though listening to it. There was something in the way he stood—weight slightly forward, head tilted—that made the hair rise on Isolde's neck. She had seen that posture once before, in a courtyard, on a girl the counselors had called "afflicted" right up until the girl was taken away and never spoken of again.

And last, the man.

He stopped at the edge of the lamplight, just outside it, and studied her the way she imagined he might study a drawn bow—not afraid, exactly, but measuring the distance. Gray stubble. A swordsman's calluses visible even from here. And around his left wrist, half-hidden by his sleeve, the edge of an old scar that ran in a pattern too deliberate to be accidental.

*Oh,* Isolde thought. *Oh, you're real.*

"You're Cinder," the woman said. Not a question. Sera Vane had the voice of someone who'd stopped asking permission years ago.

"I'm the one holding your lives in my hands tonight," Isolde rasped. "Close enough." She let the silence stretch, let them feel it. "You came down the ash road through Vellin Hollow and Marrow's Ford. You passed through villages where every gifted soul between here and the border has vanished inside a year. And you're asking about the king's health. So either you're the bravest fools in the realm, or you know something worth killing for. Which is it?"

The man shifted his weight. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet and rough, like a blade being drawn slowly. "We know what sits on the throne isn't the king."

The words dropped into the dark like stones into deep water.

Isolde kept her face still. She had practiced stillness for three years; it cost her nothing now. But inside, something turned over—something that had been waiting, starving, for another human voice to say it aloud.

"Bold claim," she said. "Treason, technically. I could earn a great deal selling you to the Vigil." She stepped closer, into full lamplight, letting the hood fall back just enough. "Tell me why I shouldn't."

"We don't have coin," the man said.

"I don't want coin."

"Then what?"

"The truth. All of it. And then—" her voice cracked, just barely, on the last word, and she hated herself for it, "—then I need you to prove me right. Because if I'm wrong about my father, I've committed treason against the only parent I have left, and I will spend the rest of my life in a tower cell for nothing."

The boy made a small sound. The woman—Sera—went rigid. Isolde watched the recognition move across her face like weather: the broad cheekbones, the dark hair, the stubborn jaw that everyone at court said came from the queen.

"You're—" Sera started.

"Cinder," Isolde cut in, hard. "I'm Cinder. That's all I am tonight." But it was too late; she saw it land in all their faces. She sighed, and let three years of pretense fall away like a burned page curling to ash. "Yes. Isolde. Fourth of her name, heir to a throne I'll likely die before touching, daughter of a man who hasn't looked at me in two years. Not since I stopped being useful and started being inconvenient." She pulled the hood fully back. "There. Now we're all traitors together. Shall we proceed?"

* * *

She took them through the servants' warrens beneath the customs quarter—tunnels built for tax evasion centuries ago and never entirely forgotten by the people who paid the taxes. The boy walked with one palm trailing the wall, and twice Isolde saw him pause where the stone was old, his lips moving silently, and once—only once—a loose brick shifted itself half an inch out of his path before he touched it.

He looked up and found her watching. His face went white.

"It's all right," Isolde said quietly.

"They took a girl," the boy said. "In the courtyard. When I was little. They said she was sick."

"They did," Isolde said. "I tried to stop it. I failed. I've been trying to stop it ever since." She crouched to his height. "What's your name?"

"Merren."

"Well, Merren. In nine days, there's going to be a ceremony at the palace. Something bad happens at it—I don't know what yet, only that the preparations frighten people who are never frightened. I've spent two years gathering proof that my father isn't my father anymore, and everything I have is whispers and inference. Whispers get you a tower cell. I need witnesses. I need—" she glanced up at the gray-templed man, "—whatever he is."

"And what am I?" the man asked dryly.

"That depends on whether the stories about swordspeakers are true," Isolde said, "or whether my mother told me bedtime tales about extinct monsters to keep me hopeful."

Something moved behind his eyes—old pain, banked like coals. "Your mother told you about swordspeakers?"

"She told me everything. Before they erased it all." Isolde straightened. "My name is the only proof I have left that any of it happened."

They emerged near the palace's eastern service stair, into cold air smelling of tallow and wet stone. Isolde produced three sets of livery from a cache she'd prepared days ago. "Laundry workers. You'll be invisible. That's the point of laundry workers."

"And you?" Sera asked, shrugging into a smock with visible distaste.

"I'm expected at my father's table tonight. He dines alone these days. He always dines alone." Her jaw tightened. "Which means, conveniently, no one sees what passes between us. No one but me."

Quentin—she'd gotten his name from Sera during the walk, given grudgingly, like currency counted twice—studied her for a long moment. "You're going to walk in there and look at it? At the thing wearing him?"

"I'm going to walk in there and try once more to find my father," Isolde said. "And if he isn't there, then I'll know for certain, and so will you. Come to the gallery above the hall at moonrise. The grate in the east wall looks down on the king's table. Watch us. Then tell me what you see."

* * *

The dining hall had been her favorite room as a child—thirty yards of candlelit stone where her father had once balanced a honeycake on his nose to make her laugh while her mother scolded them both. Now it was lit by a single candelabrum, and the table had been shortened to its first third, and the king sat at its head like a puppet hung neatly on its hook.

Isolde entered and curtsied. "Father."

The thing raised its head.

Her father's face was still there—still hers, still the face from the honeycake nights—but the eyes were wrong. Not cruel. Not empty either, which might have been a mercy. *Busy*. That was the word she'd never found until now. The eyes were busy, working at something behind them, the way ants work, endlessly, purposefully, at purposes no sane creature would choose.

"Daughter." The voice was her father's voice pitched slightly wrong, like a bell recast with base metal in it. "You honor the vigil."

"I wished to dine with you before the solstice."

"There will be no dining after the solstice," it said pleasantly. "Only renewal."

A cold finger walked down Isolde's spine. She sat, folded her hands, smiled the smile she'd practiced. "Tell me about the renewal, Father. You never speak of it."

"It is a harvesting," the king said, with her father's smile, in that almost-right voice. "A gathering-in. The realm is a field, little one, and the field must be cut before winter. You understand fields. Your grandfather understood fields."

"Where do the taken ones go?"

The busy eyes fixed on her, and for one vertiginous moment Isolde felt *seen*—truly examined, the way a butcher examines a carcass for the best cuts—and then the gaze slid off her as if she were glass.

"They go home," it said. "As all things go home. As I went home. As you shall—" and here the smile widened by a degree that broke her heart, because it was *his* smile, her father's exact smile, worn by something else the way a coat is worn, "—in time, when you ripen."

Isolde rose. Curtsied. Walked out with her spine straight and her hands steady and her soul screaming.

* * *

The gallery above the hall was unheated and smelled of pigeon droppings and old rope. Quentin was already there when she climbed up, a shadow against the grate, and he did not turn when she arrived.

"You heard," she said.

"Some." His voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Not all. Enough."

"Then say it. Say what I've known for two years and couldn't prove. Say it so it's real."

He turned then. In the thin moonlight through the arrow slit his face was lined and tired and kind, and Isolde thought absurdly of her grandfather, dead ten years, who had also carried kindness like a weight.

"That is not your father," Quentin said quietly. "Whatever wears him has worn others. It's older than the crown. Older than the kingdom. And it's hungry in a way I hoped never to think about again." He paused. "It recognized what you are, Isolde. Did you feel it? It looked at you like something ripe."

"Yes." Her voice came out small. She steadied it. "It's been looking at me like that since the purges began. Every counselor it appoints finds a reason to keep me close. Every guard rotation puts more men on my door. I thought it was suspicion. I know now it was patience."

Silence stretched between them. Below, faintly, they could hear the king's chair scrape as the thing left the table, and footsteps receding down the long hall, unhurried, certain.

"I spent thirty years hiding from that thing's kind," Quentin said at last. "Thirty years mending fences and burying friends and telling myself the world could burn politely elsewhere. And then hunters crossed my valley, and a boy nearly died, and I remembered that hiding is just dying slowly with better manners." He looked at her sideways. "You've been fighting alone in the middle of the enemy's house for three years. That's longer than most soldiers last in open war."

"Alone is a strong word for it," Isolde managed. "Mostly it's just... quiet. You learn to be your own army. You learn that the loneliest room in a palace is the one where everyone knows your name and no one knows you're right."

"I know that room," Quentin said. "I lived in it for thirty years. It's bigger than palaces."

And there it was—the moment Isolde would remember afterward, in the days when everything burned: two exiles standing in the dark above an eclipsed throne room-to-be, recognizing each other across the gulf of their different prisons. Not friendship yet. Friendship required trust, and trust required time, and they had neither. But something adjacent to it. Something that felt, dangerously, like relief.

"So," she said, blinking hard and blaming the pigeon dust. "Nine days. We have a plan, presumably? Please say we have a plan. I've been improvising for three years and I'm so tired."

"Sera knows the palace. Merren can hear things in the walls that no spy could find. And there's a masque on the fifth night—your father's thing will attend, and so will every counselor and commander it trusts." Quentin's jaw tightened. "If we're going to learn how this ends, we learn it there."

"A masque." Isolde laughed, short and disbelieving. "Of course. Treason at a party. My mother always said I'd come to a bad end at a ball."

Down in the hall, the candles had burned to stubs. Above them, through the arrow slit, the moon was rising toward the ninth night, and somewhere below the flagstones, in rooms she had loved as a child, something ancient was counting down with her father's stolen mouth.

"Come," Isolde said, turning for the stair. "We have a masque to ruin."
