## 6. Court of Whispers

The palace of Vael Amaranth had been built to be looked at. Every arch and balustrade, every pane of stained glass in its great rotunda, was an argument the crown made to itself about its own permanence—and Sera Vane had spent eleven years learning how arguments like that could be turned against the people who made them.

She stood now at the edge of the servants' corridor beneath the rotunda, dressed in the dove-grey livery of a minor house's chaperone, and felt the old knowledge settle over her like a coat she'd never truly taken off. The rhythm of the palace hadn't changed since her disgrace: the same measured thunder of feet above as guests arrived, the same smell of tallow and lilies warring with roast quail drifting down from the kitchens, the same hush that fell whenever a guard's boots rang too close.

What had changed was what lived on the throne.

*You are not one of them anymore,* she reminded herself, pressing her palm flat against the cool stone wall. *They hunted you out. They broke you.* And yet her heart still answered the drums of the place like a dog hearing its master's whistle, and she hated it for that.

Behind her, in the shadow of a linen alcove, Quentin Coldharbour stood in borrowed finery that fit him like an apology—a wine-dark doublet a size too generous across the shoulders, his grey-shot hair combed back from the ritual scars at his throat by some mercy of candlelight. He looked, Sera thought, like a wolf persuaded into a dinner jacket, and no more comfortable than such a creature would be. Merren was nowhere in sight; the boy had been smuggled in through the coal cellars with the kitchen drudges, tucked into the undercrofts where the stone would tell him everything it heard. He'd gone willingly, clutching a lump of cellar granite like a talisman, whispering to it the way other children whispered to dogs.

"Remember," Sera murmured without turning her head. "You are Aldous Crane, a land-surveyor from the Marches, bored half to death and twice as rich as you look. You do not know me. You have never seen me."

"I remember," Quentin said. His voice was low and even, but she heard the tension strung through it, a bow drawn and held. "And if I'm asked to dance?"

"You dance badly and complain about your boots. No one will press a man who complains about his boots." She allowed herself half a smile. "It's the oldest armor there is. Tedium."

Above them, a fanfare shivered through the floors. The masque had begun.

* * *

The Rotunda of Mirrors had been Isolde's idea of hell long before it became anyone else's. Tonight it was a sea of lacquered faces—herons, foxes, moths, wolves—all masked in gilt and feather, all turning slowly beneath the great dome where painted clouds gave way to painted heaven. The orchestra played something in three-four time that sounded like water running uphill.

Sera moved through it the way water moves through reeds, never hurrying, always angling. Her mask was a plain silver half-moon, forgettable, and forgettable was the point. Eleven years ago she had walked this floor in huntress black with the king's writ on her shoulder and thirty names in her book. Now those same instincts that had once served the harvest were turned against it, and she catalogued the room the way she used to catalogue a village before a seizure: exits, guards, irregularities.

There were more guards than a masque warranted. They stood in pairs at every archway, and they were not watching the dancers. They were watching the *guests*—or rather, watching for something in the guests. Sera had seen that particular vacant attentiveness before, on the night the first seizures began. The Vessel's servants carried a stillness in them, a patience that didn't belong to living men.

She found the Chancellor's table near the eastern colonnade and did what she had come to do: she listened. Chaperones were furniture, and furniture heard everything.

"—nine days, no more," a fat merchant was insisting, his moth-mask askew. "The solstice eclipse falls on the ninth, and after that the granary tariffs double. Everyone knows it. Everyone *whispers* it."

"The eclipse," said his companion, a thin woman in a crane's mask, "is why the court is summoned at all. His Majesty will hold the Rite of Unbroken Light. A renewal of the realm's fortune." She lowered her voice, and Sera bent closer behind a serving tray, heart ticking. "My cousin serves in the Undercroft. She says the star-chambers have been sealed since Duskmark. That the priests have not slept. That the King has not eaten—but that he is *always hungry*."

A cold thread slid down Sera's spine. Nine days. The solstice eclipse. She thought of the burned villages on the road of ash, of the empty cradles and the chalk circles scoured off doorstones, of the wagons that rolled toward the capital loaded with people who never arrived anywhere. The harvest had a deadline. Of course it did. A rebirth needed a due date.

She was still holding that thought when she saw Commander Dain Hale across the floor.

He had aged well, damn him. Broad as a barn door even in formal huntress grey shot with silver, his mask a snarling boar of black iron—the only iron mask in the room, because Dain Hale had never cared what anyone thought of him. He stood at the head of the western stairs with two of his hunters flanking him, and he wasn't watching the dancers either.

He was reading faces.

Sera turned, unhurried, toward the refreshment tables, and took stock with the calm of a woman who had already died once. Thirty paces between them. Two hundred guests. If she kept her back to him and drifted east, she might pass the whole night unremarked. But Dain Hale had taught her herself, years ago, in a training yard slick with rain: *a face is a story, Vane. Your job is to catch the ones that lie.*

And hers was lying with every breath.

She was three steps from the colonnade when his voice cut across the music—not loud, but carrying, the way a blade carries when it's laid flat on a table.

"There's a face I buried once."

The dancers nearest him laughed uncertainly, assuming theater. Sera kept walking. One step. Two. Behind her she heard his boots leave the stair, unhurried, closing like weather.

"Chaperone." His hand closed on her shoulder—not hard, but absolute—and turned her.

Through the eye-slits of the boar mask, his eyes went to her throat first, then her jaw, then—worst—to her left hand, where eleven years ago a hunter's ring had sat and now only a pale band of skin remained. She watched the recognition assemble itself in his face piece by piece, patient as masonry.

"Well," he said softly. "Sera Vane. Come back from the dead to see the Rite?"

Around them, the nearest dancers had begun to notice. Music faltered at the edges. Sera calculated distances, angles, the knife at her hip beneath the chaperone skirts, the odds—and found them all wanting. Dain Hale alone she might manage. Dain Hale plus forty guards plus two hundred witnesses meant one ending: the undercroft cells, and then the throne room, and then whatever waited there wearing the king's hunger like a crown.

"Commander," she said, pitching her voice light, bored. "I think you've mistaken me. I'm here attending Lady Merrow's daughters."

"Lady Merrow's daughters are in the west wing," he said, and squeezed. "I wrote their invitations myself. Looking for you, Sera. For nine days I've been looking." He leaned closer, and through the mask's slit his breath was warm and terrible. "His Majesty dreams of you. Did you know? Dreams of the huntress who slipped the leash. He says you'll come home before the Light is renewed. He says everyone comes home before the Light."

Her blood went to ice water. *He knows the date. He's told his commander. Which means—*

Which means the masque wasn't a celebration. It was a net. The whole glittering floor of it, two hundred masked guests, and somewhere among them a land-surveyor named Aldous Crane who complained about his boots, and somewhere below him a boy in the coal dark talking to stones.

"Dain," she said quietly, using the old name, the training-yard name, because some weapons work both directions. "You taught me that a hunter's first duty is truth. So look at the King. Truly look. When did you last see him eat? When did you last hear him say his own daughter's name?"

Something flickered behind the iron boar—there and gone, a crack in masonry. She saw it, and knew it for what it was: doubt, planted long ago and denied ever since. Dain Hale was not a stupid man. He had simply chosen, year after year, not to look.

"Take her," he said to his hunters, and his voice was rough. "Gently. She's to be presented to His Majesty after the Rite."

* * *

Isolde Amaranth had learned long ago that the best place to hide in a palace is in plain sight, wearing your father's colors and a smile you practice in the mirror until it stops costing anything.

So when she saw the hunters close around a grey-clad chaperone near the western colonnade—when she saw the chaperone's chin lift in that particular stubborn angle she had memorized over three weeks of shared roads and shared silences—she did not run to intervene. Running drew eyes. Instead she set down her glass, adjusted the circlet of cinders-and-gilt at her brow, and walked directly into the middle of it.

"Commander Hale." Her voice carried the specific boredom of royalty interrupted. "Is there a problem at my father's masque?"

Dain Hale released Sera's shoulder instantly and dropped to one knee, hunters folding down around him like wheat. "Highness. This woman is a traitor to the crown. A former huntress, disgraced. She is to be held for presentation after the Rite of Unbroken Light."

"How tiresome." Isolde let her gaze drift over Sera with exquisite indifference, the look of a princess assessing a stain on a curtain. Then, sweetly: "Presented to His Majesty, you said? After the Rite?"

"Yes, Highness."

"That won't do at all." She sighed, as if burdened by the endless pettiness of protocol. "The King has commanded that all prisoners taken during the festival season be remanded to the Cinder Guard first—for purification of records. My guard. Surely you received the order, Commander. It went out under the privy seal."

It had not gone out under any seal. Isolde had written it herself that afternoon, in her father's own hand, which she had been forging since she was twelve years old, and it sat in Dain Hale's dispatch tray at that very moment, waiting to ruin his evening.

She watched him weigh it. She watched him *almost* weigh the other thing—her words on the colonnade steps three nights past, *look at him, Dain, truly look*—and she watched discipline win, the way it always won with good soldiers, right up until it killed them.

"...The order will be verified," he said at last, rising.

"Verify it," Isolde agreed pleasantly. "In the morning. Meanwhile, my guards will take custody." She snapped her fingers, and four men in the black-and-cinder livery she'd raised herself from soldiers the hunt had discarded—men who owed her their lives and knew precisely what they owed—stepped forward and took Sera's arms.

As they turned her away, Sera passed close enough to speak through her smile, lips barely moving.

"He'll check by dawn."

"Then we're gone by dawn," Isolde murmured back, lifting her chin to survey her father's glittering, doomed court. Above the rotunda, painted clouds wheeled toward a painted heaven, and somewhere beneath them, on a throne beneath a dome of real stars, something ancient and starving was counting down nine days—and smiling with her father's mouth.

Across the ballroom, a bored land-surveyor from the Marches set down his untouched wine, caught her eye for half a heartbeat, and inclined his head almost imperceptibly toward the servant's stair.

Time, Sera thought as the Cinder Guards marched her into the dark, had just gotten very short indeed.
