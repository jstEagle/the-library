## 7. The Oath Unspoken

The crypts beneath the Almonry of Saint Vessa had not been opened in a hundred years, and they smelled like it—stone dust and candle tallow gone to wax-ghosts, the sweet rot of old linen, and underneath it all something older, like the breath of the mountain itself. Quentin held the lantern high and let its light crawl along the walls, where names had been cut into the rock in ranks upon ranks, floor to vaulted ceiling.

He could not read most of them. The script was pre-Purge, the old flowing hand that had been chiseled out of every public monument in the realm within a decade of the burning. But he knew what the walls said without reading them. He had grown up in a house where such names were whispered like prayers: *here lies a speaker of rivers; here lies a speaker of birdsong; here lies a speaker of the sword.*

His own name was not here. It would never be here. That had been the whole point of thirty years in the Hollow.

"Quentin?" Merren's voice echoed down the stair, too loud, the way a boy's voice always is in the dark. "Sera says there's rats down here. Big ones. She says one of them nearly took her boot."

"Tell Sera the rats are more frightened of her than she is of them," Quentin called back, "and that both of you should stay above ground until I call."

A pause. Then, grudgingly, the shuffle of feet retreating up the stairs. The boy did not like being sent away. None of them liked being sent away. Isolde least of all—she had argued for an hour last night that whatever the crypts held, they held it for *her*, because it was her father's city and her father's dead and her father's war they were all stumbling through. Quentin had won the argument only by refusing to have it. Some things a man had to read alone first, so that he could decide how much of the truth the others could carry.

The lantern found the alcove Sera had described. A sword cut into the stone above the lintel—not carved, *cut*, the way a blade parts silk—and beneath it, a shelf of lead-lined boxes, seven of them, each sealed with a sigil he recognized from his childhood nightmares: the crossed swords and broken crown of the Order of the Oathbound.

His hands were steady. He noticed that, and was grateful for it, and hated himself a little for being grateful. Thirty years of practice at keeping his hands still while everything inside him screamed.

The sixth box bore his mentor's mark. Aldous Wren, swordspeaker, last of the line before Quentin, dead eleven years now in a grave Quentin had dug with his own hands in the Hollow's soft black soil. Dead, Quentin had always believed, of age and quiet, the way good men die when they have finally outrun their wars.

He broke the seal.

Inside lay letters. Dozens of them, bundled in oilcloth, tied with cord gone brittle as autumn grass. And on top of the bundle, a single sheet addressed in a hand Quentin would have known anywhere, even blindfolded, even across the distance of death:

*To my student, if he ever stops running long enough to look.*

Quentin sat down on the cold floor. It seemed the thing to do.

* * *

The early letters were ordinary. Reports, really—Aldous had written them the way he'd done everything, methodically, a farmer's patience applied to the work of survival. Where the remaining gifted had fled. Which villages still remembered the old tongue. How to recognize the signs of the Vessel's hunger in a countryside: wells that went bitter, children born silent, birds abandoning whole valleys at once. Quentin read these with the dull comfort of a man rereading scripture he already knew by heart. He had lived this knowledge. He had built his life inside it.

Then, near the bottom of the bundle, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Hurried. The ink blotched where the pen had been pressed too hard or the hand had shaken—he could not tell which, and the not-knowing hurt worse than either.

*I have done the arithmetic,* Aldous wrote. *You will forgive an old man his sums. The Vessel cannot be killed. Every speaker who ever tried fed it instead—their gift, their years, their names. It drinks power the way fire drinks air. This is why we ran. Not from the hunters. From the truth of it.*

*But there is a door, and I am a coward for not walking through it myself.*

Quentin turned the page. The lantern flame bent in some draft from the deep stone, and his shadow swayed enormous against the wall of names, as if all those dead speakers had gathered behind him to read over his shoulder.

*The unbinding oath. You will find fragments of it in the third box, but I will set out the shape of it plainly, because plainness is the last kindness left to me. The Vessel is bound to the crown by an oath spoken at its first crowning—an oath of feeding, of perpetual hunger given royal license. To unbind it, a swordspeaker must speak a counter-oath in the old tongue, in the presence of the crown, and a counter-oath is only as strong as what stands behind it. Words are wind. Sacrifice is iron.*

*It requires the gift itself. Willingly given. Not spent, not lost, not stolen—given, with full knowledge and a whole heart, the way a man gives his life for another on a field he could have walked away from. The magic must be surrendered at the moment of speaking, every thread of it, and the speaking must be true. If the speaker holds anything back—if one corner of him whispers maybe, if one small door in him stays open hoping to take it back afterward—the oath fails, and the Vessel takes the gift anyway, and the speaker besides. There is no partial version of this. There is no surviving it halfway.*

*And whether the man survives the giving—*

The letter stopped there. Not ended. Stopped, mid-line, the way a sentence dies when the hand writing it can no longer pretend to know how it finishes. Below the break, in a different ink, darker, added later:

*I went to the ford at Carrowmere to do it, twenty years ago. I stood on the near bank for a day and a night. I could not make my heart willing. I loved my life too much—or I feared my death too much; the two are hard to tell apart at a riverbank. I came home and buried the letters and told myself the realm could endure. Forgive me. Forgive me. I chose myself, and every gifted soul taken since is a debt I signed my name to.*

*If you are reading this, then the choice has come to you, and I am sorry, and I am proud, and I do not know which of those to say louder. Know only this: the oath does not ask you to die. It asks you to stop holding on. Whether the world gives the gift back after—that is not yours to decide, and it never was. We only ever borrowed it.*

*A.C.W.*

Quentin read the letter three times. The third time, he realized he was not reading it so much as bracing himself against it, the way a man braces against a door with something terrible on the other side.

*Willingly given.* He looked at his hands again, those steady traitor's hands, and thought about the oath he had sworn in the Hollow thirty years ago—the private one, kneeling at Aldous's grave with frost in his beard: *never again*. Never speak the magic. Never draw its attention. Never be the thing the hunters came for. He had kept that oath through blood and boredom and loneliness, through Merren's arrival and Sera's defection and Isolde's hollow-eyed king, and he had believed, right up until this moment, that keeping it had made him good.

But there was a difference, wasn't there, between setting a thing down and giving it away. Between *never again* clenched like a fist, and open hands. Aldous had died holding on. Quentin could feel himself doing the same—could feel the fist forming even now, even here, even knowing what the fist cost. Because the oath wanted a whole heart, and his heart was full of holes where the gift used to live, and he did not know if a man could fill those holes with willingness alone.

Somewhere above him, muffled through a hundred years of stone, Merren laughed at something Sera said.

That was the worst of it. Not the dying—though his body flinched from that the way a hand flinches from a stove, honest and animal and beyond shame. The worst was the arithmetic of love. If he told them, Sera would argue strategy until dawn and Isolde would forbid it in a voice that expected obedience, and Merren—Merren would look at him with those stone-grey eyes and understand instantly, completely, the way children understand the things adults spend lifetimes avoiding, and Quentin would see that understanding and lose his nerve entirely. They would talk him out of it. Or worse, they would fail to talk him out of it, and then they would spend nine days watching him, grieving him in advance, carrying his death around with them like a stone in the shoe, and he could not—*would not*—make the people he had finally found into mourners before their time.

So he folded the letters along their old creases. He wrapped them in the oilcloth. He tucked the bundle inside his coat, against his chest, where it sat like a second heartbeat.

There was a practical matter, too, and he clung to it gratefully: the third box held the oath fragments themselves, pages of the old tongue in Aldous's careful copyist hand, and Quentin needed time alone with them. Nine days, if Sera's intelligence held. Time enough to memorize words that had not been spoken aloud since before the Purge. Time enough to practice the cadence in the dark, mouthing them silently like a boy rehearsing a confession. Time enough to become willing—or to discover, at the final hour, standing in an eclipsed throne room, that he was his teacher's student after all.

He climbed the stairs slowly. At the top, light fell through the Almonry's broken roof in dusty shafts, and the others were waiting exactly where he'd left them: Sera sharpening a knife with the bored precision of a woman who trusted nothing, Isolde pacing, Merren crouched over a loose flagstone, whispering to it, coaxing it flat with a tenderness that made Quentin's chest ache.

"Well?" Isolde said. "What did the dead have to say?"

"Ritual details," Quentin said. "Old liturgy. Nothing useful yet." The lie came out smooth and level, worn smooth by thirty years of practice, and he despised how easily it came, and was thankful for how easily it came, and understood finally that this was what love looked like for men like him: not the grand declaration but the concealed letter, the swallowed truth, the door held shut with your own body so the people behind you can keep laughing a little longer.

Merren glanced up. Just for a moment. Those grey eyes passed over Quentin's face the way water passes over stone—finding every crack, every seam—and the boy frowned faintly, as if filing something away.

Then he shrugged and went back to his flagstone, and Quentin smiled, and carried the letters against his heart like a knife he had not yet decided to fall on.

Above the Almonry, far off toward the palace, the first of the solstice bells began to ring. Eight days now. Somewhere in the dark of his coat, Aldous's last words pressed cool against his skin: *the oath asks you to stop holding on.*

Quentin held on.
