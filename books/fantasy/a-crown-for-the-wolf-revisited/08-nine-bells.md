## 8. Nine Bells

Merren counted the bells because counting kept the fear small.

The safehouse sat above a disused chandlery in the Tanners' Quarter, where the streets smelled of tallow and old smoke and nobody asked questions about strangers who came and went at odd hours. From the attic window he could see three of the city's bell towers, and every hour they rang out of step with one another, like three old men arguing about the time. Nine bells meant nine o'clock. Nine bells also meant nine days had become zero days, because tomorrow the eclipse came, and with it the thing wearing the king's face would finish its long slow meal of the world.

He pressed his palm flat against the attic floorboards. Beneath the dust and the mouse-smell, the stone underneath was awake the way stone always was—slow, patient, half-dreaming. *Hold*, he told it, without words, the way Quentin had taught him. *Just hold. You don't have to do anything else tonight.*

The stone murmured back something like sleep. It was the only conversation that didn't scare him anymore.

Behind him, Sera was cleaning her crossbow for the fourth time that evening. She did it the way other people prayed: quickly, mechanically, with her lips moving around things she'd never say aloud. The bandage on her forearm was fresh—Isolde had changed it at dusk—but Merren could still smell the old blood underneath, from the alley fight two nights back, from the rooftop, from all the small violent arithmetic of getting nine people's worth of hope into one borrowed room.

"Stop staring at me, sprout," Sera said, not looking up.

"I'm not staring."

"You're staring with your whole face. It's very loud."

Isolde laughed softly from the corner where she sat sharpening a knife that probably didn't need it. She'd been doing that a lot lately—the laughing, the knife-sharpening—two different ways of being afraid that she'd gotten very good at disguising as calm. Tomorrow she would walk into the palace through the servants' gate under a false name, and Merren knew—and she knew he knew—that the plan called for her to get close to the throne room and stay there, close to her father, close to the Vessel, when Quentin spoke the unbinding oath. Close to the thing that ate gifted souls for breakfast.

"Come here," Isolde said, patting the floor beside her. "If you're going to be loud, be loud over here."

He went. He was eleven years old and everyone in this room would die for him, which was a strange weight for a boy who still sometimes wet the bed after bad dreams, though he'd never told them that and never would. Isolde let him hold the whetstone while she worked the blade against it, the rhythm of it steady as a heartbeat, and for a little while the fear got small again.

Footsteps on the stairs. Two sets, one heavy, one light. Quentin and the chandler's widow, Marta, coming up from the cellar where they'd been moving stores.

Quentin looked wrong tonight. Not hurt-wrong. *Decided*-wrong. He'd been reading old letters for three days—letters he'd found somewhere in the crypts beneath the city, letters he carried now in a leather folio he never set down—and whatever they said had gone into him like water into chalk. He smiled at Merren the way he always did, but the smile sat on top of his face instead of inside it.

"Nine bells," Quentin said. "Everyone should eat. And then sleep. Tomorrow will want us rested."

"You first," Sera said. "You look like a man planning a funeral."

Quentin's hand moved, just slightly, toward the folio. Then away. "All the more reason," he said, "to eat."

* * *

The dream came anyway, the way dreams did.

In it, Merren stood in the burned village outside Harrow Fen, where they'd buried eleven people in a common grave because there wasn't time or hands enough for anything kinder. In the dream the grave opened, and the stones themselves began to speak—not to him, but *about* him, in voices like millwheels turning, saying *the small one, the small one hears us, take the small one*—and he woke with his heart slamming and the attic dark and the bells ringing.

Not nine. Not any number he knew. All the bells at once, every tower in the city, ringing wild and arrhythmic, and underneath the ringing, boots. Many boots. On the street below, and in the alley behind, and on the roofs across the lane, where lanterns bloomed one after another like terrible flowers.

He was moving before he finished waking. That was the part he'd remember afterward with a kind of stunned pride—that his body knew what to do even when his mind was still tangled in the dream. He rolled off his pallet, crossed the attic in four steps, and hit the floor beside Quentin's pallet hard enough with both palms to wake a dead man.

"They're here," he whispered. "The bells are wrong."

Quentin was already up. There was no fumbling, no confusion; thirty years of hiding lived in his muscles, and they remembered everything. He put one finger to his lips, then pointed—Sera, already at the window with her crossbow; Isolde dragging Marta up from below; the widow's two granddaughters huddled in the corner with their grandmother's hand over both their mouths.

Then the front door of the chandlery exploded inward, and thinking quietly stopped being an option.

They came up the stairs fast—witch-hunters in grey and black, the ash-grey cloaks with the white circle stitched at the shoulder, the mark of the Vessel's harvest. Merren heard Sera's crossbow snap twice, heard two men fall, heard the others keep coming anyway because witch-hunters were not brave so much as they were *full*, filled up with something that made dying seem like a fair trade. Quentin met them at the top of the stairs with his sword drawn, and the sound of steel in the narrow space was like the sky tearing.

"We're boxed," Sera shouted over the din. "Back stair's blocked—they fired it. Roof?"

"Roof's watched—I saw the lanterns." Isolde had a knife in each hand now, and her voice was the court voice, cold and clear, the voice of a woman who had grown up watching people die politely. "Marta, the girls—"

"The cellar," Marta said. "There's the old cistern channel. It runs under Tanner's Lane—"

"It's stone," Merren said.

Everyone stopped. Even the fighting seemed to pause around the word, the way weather pauses before lightning.

"It's stone," he said again, and his voice cracked in the middle, and he hated that, and he kept talking anyway. "I can hear it. I can—I can ask it to move. But if I bring the channel down, I bring the street down. Part of it. Maybe more than part of it."

Quentin looked at him across the chaos—at the boy, not the weapon. That was the thing about Quentin that Merren would carry for the rest of his life, longer than he'd carry the scars: even now, with hunters pouring up the stairs and the roof burning, Quentin looked at him like a child to be protected and not a tool to be spent.

"You don't have to," Quentin said.

"If we don't, everybody dies." Merren swallowed. His hands were shaking. He put them both flat against the attic floor, against the old boards, and reached *through* them, down past the joists and the cellar and the clay, down to the bones of the city. "And you have to go do the thing tomorrow. So everybody doesn't get to die tonight."

Down in the deep, the stone heard him. It was old, older than the city, older than the crown, and it was sleepy and heavy and full of the weight of everything ever built upon it. It did not want to move. But it listened, the way it always listened, because he was the only one left in the world who asked nicely.

"Get everyone to the channel mouth," he said. "Now. Please."

* * *

Later—much later, in the years after, when people asked him about the night the street fell, Merren would never be able to describe what it felt like to speak to that much stone at once. The nearest he ever came was this: imagine holding a sleeping giant's head in your lap, and asking it to turn over.

The cistern channel ran beneath Tanner's Lane like a buried river. When the last of them—Marta, the girls, Isolde hauling a limping Sera—had dropped through the trapdoor into the dark, Merren knelt alone in the burning attic with the sounds of hunters breaking through the floor below, and he stopped asking nicely.

"*Move*," he said, with his whole chest, with every scrap of the gift that had marked him since birth as food for the thing on the throne.

The city groaned. Somewhere below, mortar turned to sand and sand turned to memory. The wall of the chandlery leaned, sighed, and came apart into itself, and the street above the cistern channel folded down into the dark like a kneeler collapsing in prayer, and the hunters who had been crossing it went down with it, their shouts cut off mid-word, buried under forty tons of patient, obedient stone.

Dust filled the world. Merren couldn't breathe, couldn't see, couldn't hear anything but the enormous ringing silence of stone settling back into sleep. He thought, distantly, that he might have brought the whole quarter down. He thought, more distantly still, that he might be crying.

Hands found him in the dust. Quentin's hands—soot-black, bleeding at the knuckles, shaking worse than Merren's own.

"You did it," Quentin said into his hair. "You did it, lad. Breathe. Breathe."

Then Sera screamed.

It wasn't a scream of pain, though she had plenty of cause. It came from the mouth of the cistern channel, where the survivors huddled in the dark, and it was short and furious and ended in a sound like a body hitting stone. A lantern flared. Figures in the tunnel mouth—more hunters, a second wave, come from the far end of the channel, from the direction nobody had watched because nobody had known there was a far end.

What happened next happened fast, and Merren saw all of it and could stop none of it. Isolde killed one man with a knife and a second with the first man's sword. Quentin waded in and the tunnel filled with the terrible efficient violence of a swordspeaker, even a rusted one, even a hidden one. But there were too many, and they hadn't come to kill.

They came to *take*.

Merren heard the commander's voice—Sera's former commander, the one from the masque, the one whose name made Sera's face go grey—ringing flat and certain off the tunnel walls: "The anchor. Take the anchor. Kill the rest if they resist."

They put a hooded sack over Isolde's head. She fought like someone who had been preparing for exactly this her entire life, and it nearly was enough, and it wasn't. They dragged her backward into the dark, toward the far end of the channel, toward the palace hill, toward the throne room and the eclipse and the thing that wore her father's face, and the last thing she said, muffled through the sack, clear as a bell:

"Quentin! *Tomorrow!* Don't you dare be late!"

Then the tunnel was empty except for dust and blood and the ones left standing.

Sera was on the ground with a hunter's blade opened along her side, and Marta was pressing cloth into the wound and saying words that weren't prayers but sounded like them, and Sera's eyes found Merren's across the dark and held them, fierce and furious and alive.

"Nine bells," she rasped. "We've still got till the eclipse. We go get her back."

Above them, through the wound in the fallen street, the sky was beginning to pale. One day left. Merren put his hand against the broken stone, and the broken stone, gentle as an apology, hummed back.
