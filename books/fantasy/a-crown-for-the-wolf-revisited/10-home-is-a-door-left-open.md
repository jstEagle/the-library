## 10. Home Is a Door Left Open

The king's abdication took less time than his possession had.

Quentin stood in the back of the great hall on a grey spring morning, hat in his hands like any farmer come to petition, and watched Aldric of House Veyne set the crown of the realm on a cushion of black velvet and step away from it. The crown did not gleam. It had never gleamed, Quentin thought, even before the Vessel wore it. It was an ugly thing, old iron and river-gold, made to look heavier than it was. He supposed that had always been the point.

The king's voice was thin. Whatever Quentin had severed on the night of the eclipse, it had taken more than the hunger with it. Aldric spoke the words of renunciation clearly enough, but his hands shook, and twice he lost his place on the page his daughter held for him. Isolde stood beside the throne she did not yet claim, dressed not in white and gold but in travel-stained grey, as if she had come to the ceremony on her way to somewhere else and simply stopped. Perhaps she had. Perhaps she always would be on her way somewhere else; that seemed to be the family inheritance.

When it was done, the hall erupted into the careful noise of courtiers deciding what was safe to feel. Aldric did not look at any of them. He looked at the back of the room, at Quentin, and his hollow eyes were not hollow anymore—only tired, and terribly, humanly ashamed. He inclined his head, a small motion, a man bowing to a farmer over a fence line. Quentin inclined his own in return, and that was the whole of the conversation. Some debts were too large to speak aloud. They only grew mold if you handled them.

Isolde found him afterward in the colonnade, where the light came down in dusty bars and the smell of cut stone reminded him, absurdly, of mending fences.

"You could stay," she said. No preamble. She had never learned preambles, or had deliberately unlearned them. "The council will need—"

"The council will need someone who can sit in chairs for long hours," Quentin said. "I've spent thirty years learning to stand up and walk away. I'd hate to waste the education."

"You're not walking away from anything anymore. That's the point of it."

He looked at her. She had her father's jaw and her mother's eyes, and beneath both, something that belonged to her alone: a stubbornness that had carried her through nine days of hunting proof in a palace full of things that ate proof, and out the other side. She was twenty-three years old and she had already buried more of herself than most people buried in a lifetime. She would dig the rest of it up. She would rebuild the realm on the bones of what it had been, and it would cost her everything soft, and she would do it anyway.

"I'm not walking away," Quentin said. "I'm walking toward. There's a difference. It took me thirty years to learn that too."

"Where?"

"Home."

* * *

Sera Vane slept through most of the journey north, propped in the bed of a farm wagon among sacks of barley seed, and woke in fury whenever the wagon hit a rut, which in the border country was often.

"I can ride," she said, the fourth time.

"You can bleed," Quentin said. "The wound weeps when you sit a saddle. Merren counted the bandages."

"Merren counts everything. He counted the buttons on your coat. He says you're missing two."

"He's not wrong."

She subsided, glaring at the sky, which was doing its best spring impression—high pale blue, smeared with cloud like breath on glass. The scar across her throat had begun to fade from angry red to the pale silver of old work, and she hated it, Quentin could tell, because it was proof she had been saved rather than proof she had fought. He understood that. He had spent thirty years hating his own scars for the same reason. The ritual marks on his forearms, the ones he had kept wrapped and hidden in the Hollow, he now wore bare. They were only scars now. The magic was gone out of them, burned away in the throne room along with everything else, and what remained was just a history written on skin. A man could live with his history, if he stopped trying to hide it.

"The valley," Sera said, after a long silence. "The Hollow. You really mean to go back. To *stay*."

"I never meant to stay the first time. I meant to hide. It's different."

"Quentin." She turned her head, and her eyes were the flat grey of a hunter's, the eyes that had tracked him across three provinces and a burning ford. "There's nothing in the Hollow. There's a fence you mended, a grave you tended, and forty farmers who will take one look at me and remember every witch-hunter who ever knocked on their door."

"There's a valley where magic cannot enter," Quentin said. "And there is a door, and I am going to leave it open. Those are not the same as nothing."

She stared at him. Then, slowly, she laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate that hadn't swung in years—and turned her face back to the sky. "A refuge for the gifted, in the one place their gifts don't work."

"Gifts don't keep you alive. People do. The valley can teach them that. It taught me."

"And after?"

"After, when they're ready, they walk out the far side of the ford and the magic comes back to them like a bird to a window. Or it doesn't, and they stay, and they're still worth something." He shrugged. "I'm not building a fortress, Sera. I'm building a place where nobody has to be the last of anything."

She was quiet for a long time. The wagon creaked. Somewhere ahead, Merren was walking with the ox, one small hand on its warm grey shoulder, talking to it in the low murmuring way he talked to everything—though the stone-speech was gone quiet in him here, this far from any bedrock he knew, and what he said to the ox was just a boy's talk, and the ox flicked its ears and listened the way oxen do.

"You know it'll take years," Sera said at last. "Word travels slow. The gifted are scattered, and frightened, and half of them won't believe the Vessel's dead."

"Then we'd better start."

* * *

The Hollow had not forgotten them. That was the thing Quentin had never understood about the valley, all his years of hiding in it: everyone said the Hollow forgot, that magic could not enter and memory slid off it like rain off slate. But the valley had remembered him, in its way. The fence he had mended was still mended. His garden had gone to seed but the seed had held. And the grave of Master Ellery Voss, under the rowan at the top of the rise, had been tended.

He stood before it a long time, the others waiting respectfully back by the gate, and read the words on the stone again as if they might have changed.

*Here lies a teacher, who taught one student too well and too late. May the valley keep what the world could not.*

"I brought you something," Quentin said aloud. He set the old sword on the grass—his sword, the one that had drunk the oath and the backlash both, its blade gone grey and dead as lead. It looked smaller lying there than it ever had in his hand. "I'm done needing it. And I'm done being the last of anything. There's going to be more of us. You'd have hated the noise."

The rowan moved in the wind. That was all. That was enough.

* * *

They came, in the end. Not all at once, and not quickly, but they came.

First it was a girl of twelve whose hands set fires she never meant, carried north by an aunt who had heard the rumor from a grain merchant. Then a pair of brothers who heard voices in wells. Then a hedge-witch, old and half-blind, who arrived at the ford with a knapsack and a look of a woman walking into her own funeral and walked out the far side weeping, because her magic had come back to her in the shallows like warmth returning to a frozen hand.

Sera drilled them. That was the surprise of the first summer—Sera Vane, who had spent her life hunting the gifted, standing in the dooryard with a wooden staff, teaching a fire-handed girl how to breathe, how to count, how to hold the power like a bird rather than a blade. She never smiled about it. She never explained it either. But Quentin noticed that she always gave the hardest drills to the ones who hated themselves the most, and that she was always, always gentler with them after.

Merren grew. He grew the way spring things grow, all at once and overnight, and his gift grew with him, and by the second year he could sit on the valley's rim and feel the bones of the mountains the way other boys felt the wind. He still counted things. He counted the new roofs going up on the south slope—seven, then eleven, then nineteen. He counted the letters Isolde sent, and read them aloud at the supper table in his careful, stone-deep voice: reports of the reforms, the new law repealing the Purge edicts, the trials of the hunters who had known what the harvest was. And once, folded small at the bottom of a letter, a line in her own hand that was not a report at all: *Tell your stubborn farmer that the door he left open works both ways, and that I intend to use it. When the realm can spare me. It cannot spare me yet.*

Quentin read that line four times, then put it in the box under his bed with the other things he could not throw away.

* * *

On an evening in the third autumn, Quentin sat on his own doorstep with his hands wrapped around a mug of cider, watching the valley go gold and then blue in the failing light. His forearms were bare. The scars had gone white as birch bark. Somewhere behind him, Sera was arguing with the hedge-witch about warding the well, and Merren was explaining something to the stones of the new schoolhouse foundation, and the stones, being stones, were keeping their own counsel.

His gift was gone. He had checked, in the early days, quietly, the way a man checks a wound. Spoken the old words to the empty air and felt nothing answer. It was a loss like a loss of sight. And it was also, he had come to understand, the least of what he had paid, and the least of what he had been given in return.

The door stood open behind him. It always stood open now; that was the whole point, and he had made Sera promise to keep it so even in winter, even when the snow drifted across the threshold, because a refuge with a shut door was only a prettier prison.

He heard footsteps on the road below—the long, deliberate stride of someone who had walked a very long way and was almost afraid to arrive. He didn't turn. He listened to the footsteps come up through the gate, past the fence he had mended thirty years ago and mended again last spring, and stop at the bottom of the steps.

"Quentin." Isolde's voice, roughened by road dust and by everything else. "The realm can spare me now. I find I can't spare the realm, not entirely—I'll be going back and forth, there's work—but I thought I'd see the valley first. I thought I'd see what you built."

He stood, and turned, and looked down at the king's daughter, the queen who would not be crowned for years yet, standing in his doorway with her travel-grey cloak and her stubborn jaw and her eyes shining in the last of the light.

"We built a home," Quentin said, and stepped aside, and left the door open wide.
