## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The king's supper arrived on silver trays the color of moonlight on a frozen pond, and Beatrix Eriksen greeted each dish like an old acquaintance arriving slightly drunk at her door.

"Roast capon with plum glaze," announced the steward, lifting the first lid.

Beatrix leaned in and breathed. Plum, yes. Fat rendered golden. And beneath it all — faint as a rumor — bitter almond.

"Hello, you," she murmured.

She took a sliver of breast meat, a spoonful of glaze, and let them sit on her tongue while she counted. One. Two. Three. The almond note was real but shy; someone in the palace kitchens had gotten careless with apricot kernels again, which meant either sabotage or Old Marta's stubborn refusal to throw out last summer's preserves. Given that Old Marta had once tried to poison her own husband with a badger stew and missed, Beatrix put her money on carelessness.

She swallowed. Somewhere behind her sternum, a small cold flower opened its petals — the seventh poison she'd catalogued this month, if you counted the wine from Tuesday, which had been laced with something the Guild's apothecaries called widow's patience and Beatrix privately called *why is my tongue blue*.

"Acceptable," she said to the steward, and watched his shoulders drop half an inch.

That was the whole performance, really: one word, delivered flatly enough to seem bored, warmly enough to seem honest. Acceptable. The court had learned to read her mouth like scripture. When Beatrix Eriksen said *acceptable*, the king ate. When she said nothing at all, the tray went out the window into the moat, and somewhere below, the carp grew fat and strange.

The second dish was a soup of white beans and smoked ham hock. She tasted it twice, found only honesty and too much salt, and gave the steward a look over the rim of the bowl that said, *tell Marta the king's physician will lecture us all again about sodium.* The third was honeyed pears, which were perfect, and she said so with genuine feeling, because even a taster deserved one clean pleasure per shift.

By the time the cheese course came, she'd swallowed four potential deaths and one actual mild toxin, and her body had begun its familiar evening conversation.

There was the almond, humming in her blood like a neighbor's distant fiddle — annoying, survivable, known. There was the trace of henbane in the gravy that she suspected was deliberate, because Lord Vessik's man had been in the kitchens that afternoon and Lord Vessik had never met a scheme he didn't season. Her fingertips were tingling in the way they did when something wanted her nerves more than her stomach. She made a note of it. Not on paper — paper left the room, and things written down could be read — but in the ledger she kept behind her eyes, where every entry was written in a hand only she could see.

*Henbane, trace. Vessik, probably. Watch for numbness in the feet by midnight.*

"You're making that face," said Tam, the youngest of the kitchen boys who ferried trays and gossip in equal measure. He was fourteen, freckled, and under the impression that being a food-taster was glamorous.

"What face?"

"The face like you're doing sums."

"I am doing sums."

"Of what? How much you hate your life?"

Beatrix considered the question with more seriousness than it deserved. "Of how many people want the king dead this week, versus how many are competent. This week it's four against none, so we eat well." She speared a pear slice off the taster's plate — hers alone, the one indulgence of the office — and handed him the rest. "Tell Marta her plums have opinions."

Tam grinned and fled with the tray, and Beatrix stood alone in the antechamber while the great hall beyond filled with the sounds of a court eating a supper she'd certified. Laughter, the clink of gold-rimmed plates, some lordling's harp played badly enough to constitute a fifth poison.

This was the part of the evening she disliked most: the quiet after the work, when there was nothing left between her and her own company.

She had been royal food-taster for six years now. She was good at it — better than good; the Guild had trained her until her tongue could tell the difference between two vintages of arsenic by the way they sat on the palate, until her pulse was a clock and her stomach a fortress and her memory a vault no one else held the key to. She was, by any reasonable measure, one of the most valuable people in the palace.

And she was also, by every measure that mattered at dinner parties, furniture. Tasters were not introduced. They were not invited. They stood near walls and were spoken about in the tones reserved for weather and disease. Once, at a midsummer feast, a duchess had spent twenty minutes complaining to Beatrix about how impossible it was to find decent servants these days, having entirely failed to notice that the woman holding her wine glass hostage was the reason the entire court hadn't died in the spring.

Beatrix hadn't corrected her. Correcting people required wanting something from them, and she had long ago decided to want very little.

Her rule — the rule she'd carved into herself the way other people carved initials into trees — was simple:

*Record everything. Feel nothing.*

It worked. Mostly. On evenings like this one, when the antechamber candles guttered and the laughter through the wall sounded like a party happening in someone else's life, it worked about ninety percent, and she'd learned to live in the remaining ten the way sailors lived in storms: braced, busy, and counting the hours until landfall.

Landfall, for Beatrix, happened at midnight, three districts away, down a flight of stairs that officially did not exist.

* * *

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its offices beneath a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, which everyone agreed was the sort of joke the Guild would make. To reach it, Beatrix walked out of the palace through the servant's gate with her hood up, crossed the fish market where the night-sellers were just setting out their ice-crusted wares, and knocked on the chandler's door in a rhythm of five beats, a pause, and two more.

The door opened onto darkness. The darkness said, "Password."

"Barnaby, I've known you nine years. You cried at my sister's wedding."

"The password, Beatrix, or I start screaming about thieves."

"The password is 'the candle burns both ends.'"

"That was last month's password."

"Then I'll wait here while you fetch this month's, shall I? In the cold. With my hands full." She held up the wrapped parcel of honeyed pears she'd liberated from the kitchens. Barnaby, a large soft man built like a friendly landslide, peered at the parcel, weighed his principles against pears, and stood aside.

"They're changing it next week anyway," he muttered. "Oren says nobody guesses 'the candle burns both ends' because it's too obvious."

"Everything's obvious once you've heard it."

"That's what I keep telling him." He squinted at her in the gloom. "You look tired."

"You look like a man who's about to receive pears. We're both suffering."

The stairs went down farther than the building above justified — forty-one steps, then a landing, then thirty more — and at the bottom, the Guild of Impossible Things unfolded in warm candlelight like a secret deciding to be generous.

Officially, the crown pretended the Guild didn't exist. Unofficially, the crown used it constantly. It was, depending on whom you asked, a bank, a brokerage, a sanctuary, a blackmailer's clearinghouse, and occasionally — when the mood struck — a matchmaking service for debts. People came to sell things that couldn't legally be sold: a grudge, a birthright, a year of sleep, the memory of a face. They came to buy things that couldn't be bought: forgiveness, silence, one impossible favor. And everything, *everything*, was written down.

Which was why the Guild needed Beatrix at night, and why Beatrix needed the Guild at night, though neither of them ever said so aloud. The arrangement suited them both: the Guild got the finest auditor in the city, a woman whose mind held numbers the way a riverbed holds water, and Beatrix got a cellar full of order in a life otherwise governed by poison and pretending.

The ledger room was long and low, its ceiling ribbed with old beams, its shelves stretching back into shadow where the oldest books slept. Dozens of lamps burned behind green glass shades, turning everything the color of deep water. At the central table sat Guildmaster Oren, a narrow man of sixty-odd years with spectacles he polished when he was thinking and removed when he was worried, currently polishing them with the intensity of a man scrubbing a stain out of his conscience.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm early. You moved the hour."

"I moved it because the crown's men were sniffing around Candlewick Row this afternoon, and I thought you might prefer arriving after dark."

"The crown's men are always sniffing somewhere. Last week it was the tallow stores. The week before, they arrested a goose."

"A goose?"

"For treason. It bit a tax collector. They made quite a show of it." She unwrapped the pears, set them on the table as tribute, and hung her cloak on the peg by the door, which was hers, which everyone knew was hers, because Beatrix Eriksen had occupied the same peg for six years and the Guild respected territory the way cats did. "What's tonight's work?"

Oren set down his spectacles. That was her first warning. Guildmasters did not remove their spectacles for routine audits.

"We received a request today," he said. "From inside the palace."

"The palace sends us requests constantly. Half of Lord Vessik's correspondence comes through our boxes."

"Not a request for services." He slid a folded slip of paper across the table. "A payment. An old one, come due. And with it, instructions to open the Founding Ledger."

Beatrix went still.

Every guild had its sacred texts. The bakers had the first recipe book; the surgeons had their charter signed in blood, allegedly. The Guild of Impossible Things had the Founding Ledger — a book so old that no one living had entered anything in it, so heavy it took two people to lift, chained to its lectern in the inner vault since before the current dynasty, possibly since before the concept of dynasties. It recorded the original debts. The ones the Guild was founded to hold. The ones nobody talked about because nobody alive remembered what they'd bought.

Nobody touched the Founding Ledger. It wasn't forbidden, exactly. It was simply understood, the way it was understood you didn't dig up graveyards for fun.

"Why tonight?" she asked.

"Because the payment came due tonight, and the instruction says the audit must begin tonight, and—" Oren paused, and Beatrix watched him choose, deliberately, to say the next sentence plainly rather than soften it. "—because the instruction names the auditor. By name. Yours. Written a hundred years ago."

The lamp flames leaned, all together, as though the cellar had drawn breath.

"That's a joke," Beatrix said. "A very old, very expensive joke. Someone paid us a century ago to mess with me."

"Read the slip."

She picked it up. The paper was thick, cottony, absurdly well-preserved, and the ink had gone the brown of dried blood. The handwriting was small and precise and slanted slightly left, and it said:

*Let the keeper of accounts open the book on the appointed night. Let it be the taster. She will know the hand.*

"She will know the hand," Beatrix repeated. "Whose hand?"

"That," said Oren, "is the interesting part. Because I've seen that handwriting before, Beatrix. Every day for thirty years." He tapped the audit ledger lying open before him — her ledger, the one she balanced nightly in her neat, precise, slightly left-slanting script. "It matches yours."

The ten percent of her that lived in storms — the part she kept braced and busy — went very quiet indeed.

"Don't be ridiculous," she said, and her voice came out steady, which she counted as the evening's single professional triumph. "I'm twenty-nine. That page predates my grandmother's grandmother."

"I'm aware of the arithmetic." Oren rose, took a ring of keys from around his neck, and nodded toward the inner vault, where the Founding Ledger waited behind iron and oak. "Which is why I'm not asking you to explain it. I'm asking you to do your job. Open the book. Read the entries dated tonight. Record everything."

He paused at the vault door, key in the lock, and looked back at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before — something old and sorry and careful.

"And Beatrix. Whatever you find in there. My advice, for what it's worth, is the same advice I give all our clients." The lock turned with a sound like a bone breaking politely. "Feel nothing. Until it's finished."

The vault exhaled cold air scented with dust and lamp oil and, underneath it, faintly, something sweeter. Bitter almond.

"Hello, you," Beatrix murmured to the dark, and stepped inside.
