## 2. A Debt Written in Ash

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts in a cellar beneath a chandler's shop that had never once sold a candle.

This was deliberate. Beatrix had asked about it her first week, when she was still new enough to ask questions and old enough to know better. Oren had looked at her over the top of his spectacles — spectacles he did not need, worn purely for effect, since his eyes were the color of milk glass and saw, as far as anyone could tell, everything except the present moment — and said, "Would you buy a candle from a man who smelled like us?"

"Like what?" Beatrix had asked.

"Exactly," said Oren, and that was the end of the conversation.

So she descended now, as she did every night after the king's supper, down the stairs behind the chandler's false wall, past the smell of tallow and old paper and something faintly metallic that Beatrix had long ago decided not to identify, into the cellar where the Guild kept its ledgers.

There were forty-one of them. She knew because she had counted, twice, on two separate nights, and both times arrived at forty-one, which was itself slightly impossible, because on the second night one of them had clearly been missing from the shelf where it had sat the first time, and yet the count held. The Guild's books were like cats. They came when they pleased.

Tonight she wanted the oldest one. The First Ledger, the others called it, though no one called anything anything in front of her, so really she'd decided that herself. It lived at the far end of the shelf, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, and it weighed roughly as much as a sleeping dog, which was appropriate, because opening it felt like waking something.

Beatrix set it on the counting table, lit three lamps — the cellar ate light the way the king ate oysters, greedily and without gratitude — and pulled her stool close.

"Audit," she announced to the empty room, because she had found that saying things aloud made them true, or at least made them feel truer, which in accounting amounted to nearly the same thing.

Her rule for the night, as every night: *record everything, feel nothing.*

It was a good rule. She recommended it highly.

* * *

The audit itself was routine, if routine could be said to exist anywhere in the Guild's holdings, which was debatable. She worked through the middle ledgers first, the soft ones — debts of favors, debts of secrets, one truly remarkable entry recording that a miller in Lower Harrow owed the Guild "one apology, sincere, delivered to a person of the Guild's choosing," an obligation now ninety years outstanding and accruing interest at a rate Beatrix had calculated, out of idle spite, would require the apology to eventually be delivered to someone not yet born.

She noted it. She felt nothing. Mostly.

Then there was the entry for Lord Vessin, who owed a memory of his wedding day, pawned during a bad year at cards and never redeemed, and who now sent flowers to the Guild every anniversary without knowing why. Beatrix had checked, once, whether the flowers were part of the debt. They were not. They were just a man's hands remembering what his mind had sold. She had recorded the flowers under *miscellaneous income* and then sat very still for a while.

*Record everything,* she reminded herself. *Feel nothing.*

By midnight she had cleared the middle shelves and there was nothing left between her and the First Ledger but her own reluctance, which she identified, catalogued, and set aside the way she catalogued the almond note in the king's wine — present, accounted for, harmless in small doses.

She unwrapped the oilcloth.

The book exhaled. That was the only word for it. A hundred years of closed pages pushed out a breath of dust and something older than dust — cold air, mountain air, the kind of air that had never met a chimney. Beatrix sneezed with tremendous dignity.

"All right," she told it. "Let's see what your predecessors got up to."

The early entries were written in iron-gall ink gone brown as dried blood, in a hand that slanted hard to the right, as if the writer had been perpetually leaning into a wind nobody else could feel. Beatrix read the way she tasted — methodically, in layers. Surface first: names, dates, sums. Then texture: the pressure of the nib, the hesitations, the places where the pen had been lifted mid-word and set back down changed.

The first keeper of the Guild, whoever she had been, wrote like someone arguing with the page.

And the entries — Beatrix turned pages slowly now — were not like the modern ones. No pawns of memories, no apologies owed. These were bigger. Colder. Entries marked not with the Guild's usual wax seal but with a pressed print of something that might have been a thumbprint, if thumbs had ridges like frost ferns.

*A debt of nine winters, forgiven by the court beneath the mountain, against future consideration.*

*A toll paid in names, seven, unclaimed.*

And then, near the back, in a section Beatrix had never audited because no one had ever asked her to and because the pages stuck together with a residue she chose to think of as candle smoke:

*Received this night, by agreement of crown and court: one soul, freely surrendered, to keep the winter falling until the debt is satisfied. The crown's hand is signed below. The Guild stands witness.*

Below it, two signatures. One was a royal hand, grand and looping, the kind of signature that existed to be admired rather than read.

The other was smaller. Careful. Slanted hard to the right, like someone leaning into wind.

Beatrix stared at it for a long moment, and the cold feeling in her stomach was not dread, she told herself firmly. It was curiosity. Cold dread disguised as curiosity was unprofessional, and she was a professional. Curiosity, on the other hand, was simply thoroughness wearing a nicer coat.

"The soul," she murmured, running one finger above — not touching, never touching — the line where a name should have been. Because that was the strange thing. Every entry in every Guild ledger named its parties. Debts required names the way wounds required skin. But here, where the surrendered soul should have been written, there was only a blank space, a rectangle of untouched paper, as if the ink had refused.

Except it hadn't refused. Looking closer, tilting the lamp, Beatrix saw there *had* been writing there once. Faint ghosts of it, pressed deep and then scraped away — the paper worn thin, as though someone had erased the name with such force they'd nearly gone through to the next page.

Someone had sold a soul and then couldn't bear to write down whose.

"No," Beatrix said quietly. "You wrote it down. You always write it down. You just—" She stopped.

Because she had turned the page.

* * *

The last entry in the First Ledger occupied a single leaf, and it was dated — she checked three times, holding the lamp so close she scorched the edge of her sleeve and had to pat it out with the calm of a woman who regularly set small parts of herself on fire professionally — dated tonight.

Not this year. Not this season. Tonight. The same date, a hundred years distant, in a calendar whose feast days she knew by heart because she planned the king's menus around avoiding them. The Feast of Deepwinter. The longest night. Tomorrow, as it happened, was also the date of the king's great winter feast, the one where Aldric —

No. She wasn't thinking about the prince. Princes were not entries in ledgers. Princes were hazards, like arsenic: notable, dangerous, to be handled with gloves.

She read the entry.

*Owed to the court beneath the mountain, upon this night, one hundred years from signing:*

*One taster's death, freely walked into.*

*Payment to be collected in person by the creditor or his agent.*

*Collateral: the winter.*

*Signed,*

And there it was. The signature.

Her own.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. Not the eerie coincidence of two people trained in the same school of penmanship. Beatrix knew her own hand the way she knew her own pulse — the slight hitch before the capital B, the way the x in Eriksen trailed off like a sigh, the little hook she added to the final n because a schoolmistress had beaten it out of everyone else in her class and somehow missed her.

All of it. There. In ink a century old.

She did the sensible thing, which was to stand up, walk four steps away, drink half a cup of watered wine she kept for exactly these occasions, and return.

The signature remained hers. The watered wine remained insufficient.

"Right," she said. Her voice sounded fine. Steady. Slightly too bright, like a coin polished to hide the clipping. "Options."

She held up fingers, an old habit from balancing books.

"One: forgery." She lowered the finger. Unlikely. Forgers imitated; this didn't imitate, it *was*, down to the pressure of the pen, and pressure couldn't be faked a hundred years ahead by anyone, even the Guild, even — she glanced around the dark — whatever else the Guild was besides a lending house for broken people.

"Two: coincidence of handwriting." Lowered. She didn't believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what the crown called evidence it didn't want to audit.

"Three." She paused on the third finger, because the third option was the one the page itself suggested, the one that lay under the words like the almond note under the honey: that the entry was accurate, that it had always been accurate, that some version of her — or some claim *on* her — had existed in ink since before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath, and that tonight, the hundredth anniversary of the signing, the debt had come due.

*One taster's death, freely walked into.*

Freely. That word sat wrong with her, and Beatrix Dunmore-Eriksen — no, just Eriksen, she'd dropped the double name when she dropped the family that gave it — Beatrix Eriksen knew wrong words the way her tongue knew wrong flavors. A debt freely walked into was not a debt at all. It was a trap with good manners.

And the blank space on the previous page, where a sold soul's name had been scraped away until the paper wore thin?

She looked at the two pages side by side. The ghost-scraped name. Her own name, fresh as tomorrow.

"Well," she said to the ledger, pleasantly, the way she spoke to dishes she suspected. "Aren't you interesting."

The ledger, being a book, said nothing, which she appreciated. Books were the only honest company she kept.

She copied the entry. All of it, every word, into her working book, in her own hand, which now felt like quoting scripture in a voice she'd stolen. Then she wrapped the First Ledger back in its oilcloth, returned it to the shelf — it settled with a sound almost like relief, or hunger — and climbed the stairs into the ordinary world, where candles were sold, or at least plausibly pretended to be, and the snow fell down instead of sideways, and her death was still a private matter scheduled for approximately now.

*Record everything,* she reminded herself, blowing out the lamps one by one. *Feel nothing.*

She was aware, distantly, that the rule was beginning to cost more than it paid. But rules were rules, and she was, whatever else, a keeper of accounts.

* * *

She was halfway through copying the entry a second time — because the first copy had developed, she noticed with professional horror, a faint tremor in the downstrokes — when the chandler's bell rang.

At half past two in the morning, the bell did not ring for customers. Customers came at hours sanctioned by daylight and self-deception. At half past two, the bell rang for people who needed the cellar, not the shop, and those people were either Guild business or trouble, and in Beatrix's experience the categories overlapped considerably.

She slid the First Ledger's copy into her apron, blew out all but one lamp, and stood in the shadow of the stairs with her hand on the counter where she kept, among other implements, a letter opener she had personally tested against eleven common poisons and found wanting in none.

Footsteps on the stairs. Two sets. One heavy and familiar — Oren, who moved like furniture being politely relocated. And one lighter, quicker, with the particular controlled rhythm of someone who had been taught to walk quietly and resented it.

Oren appeared first, milk-white eyes finding her shadow without any difficulty whatsoever.

"Taster," he said.

"Guildmaster." She stepped into the lamplight. "We're closed."

"We are never closed," Oren said mildly. "We are merely selective. And tonight we've been selected." He stepped aside.

Behind him, descending the last stair, was a young man in a plain grey cloak that fooled absolutely no one, because it was cut by a tailor who charged by the stitch, and beneath its hood was a face Beatrix knew from a hundred state dinners — knew from across tables, from behind the rim of the king's wine cup, from the careful corner of her eye where she kept everyone whose death would inconvenience her.

Prince Aldric. Sleepless, by the look of him — shadows under his eyes like spilled ink — and hunted, by the set of his shoulders, the way a man holds himself when the whole court is measuring him for a coffin or a crown and hasn't decided which fits worse.

He looked at her. He looked at the single lamp. He looked at the counting table, where her working book lay open, and where — Beatrix's stomach performed a slow, elegant drop — the edge of her copied page protruded, the ink not yet dry, her own name facing the ceiling like a confession.

"I need to buy something," the prince said. His voice was quiet and rough at the edges, a voice used to being listened to and tired of it. "Something impossible."

"We sell those," Beatrix said, and heard her own voice do the thing it did when she was afraid: go pleasant. Go smooth. Go exactly, precisely level. "What is it?"

"One honest answer," said Prince Aldric. His eyes had not left the open book. "About a debt."

Outside, far above, the snow went on falling — down, for now, and for now only — and somewhere beneath their feet, beneath the city, beneath the frozen river and the roots of the mountain, something old took an interest in the date.

Beatrix put her hand flat on the page and smiled the smile she saved for dishes she intended to survive.

"Now," she said, "that depends entirely on which ledger you mean."
