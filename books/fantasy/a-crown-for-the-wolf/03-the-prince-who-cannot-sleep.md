## 3. The Prince Who Cannot Sleep

There were one hundred and forty-seven steps between the prince's chambers and the kitchens, and Prince Aldric had counted them so many times that the number had stopped meaning anything. It was simply the distance he walked at night, back and forth, like a fox pacing a cage it had long since memorized.

He was not permitted to walk them during the day. During the day he had lessons in statecraft from Lord Vessery, who smiled too much; fencing with Master Corvin, who reported every stumble to the king's council; and audiences with petitioners who looked at him the way farmers look at a horse they're deciding whether to send to the knacker — kindly enough, but with arithmetic behind their eyes.

The arithmetic went something like this: the crown prince could not sleep. The crown prince woke screaming some nights, though no one dared say so to his face. The crown prince had fainted at his grandmother's funeral, wept at nothing twice in public, and been found once at dawn standing in the fountain courtyard in bare feet, watching snow fall on water that refused to freeze. Therefore the crown prince was unfit, and there were four men on the council — Vessery chief among them — who would be very happy to gather the physicians' notes into a tidy bundle and present it to His Majesty along with a proposed regency.

His father, to his credit, resisted. But his father was tired, and tired kings sign things.

So Aldric walked his hundred and forty-seven steps and thought about what a man might do when everyone around him had already decided what was wrong with him.

The answer, he had decided three weeks ago, was that a man might buy something impossible.

* * *

Everyone knew the Guild of Impossible Things existed. That was the joke of it. A trade in souls, debts, and favors, operating out of cellars beneath a candlemaker's shop on Tallow Row, and the crown pretended not to see because the crown, on occasion, needed things that could not be seen either. There was a story that the old king — Aldric's grandfather — had once bought six months of good weather for the harvest, and that the price had been paid by someone whose name nobody remembered anymore, which Aldric had always assumed was just how stories worked.

Tonight the snow fell straight down, thick and silent, muffling the city until it felt like walking inside a held breath. Aldric wore a servant's grey coat over his own shoulders — he'd stolen it off a laundry hook himself, badly, and the cook's boy Marten had watched him do it and said nothing, which was the closest thing to loyalty the palace offered.

Tallow Row smelled of wax and tallow and, beneath that, something older — cold stone, cold iron, the smell of a cellar that had been cold longer than the street above it had existed. The candlemaker's shop was dark. The door beside it, unremarkable and slightly warped, opened before he knocked.

"You're expected," said the doorkeeper, who was approximately as wide as the door and considerably less warm.

"I'm— I wasn't aware I'd sent word."

"Everyone who comes is expected." The doorkeeper stepped aside. "That's how the ledgers work. Down the stairs, third door, don't touch anything with dust on it. The dust is doing a job."

Aldric descended into a corridor lit by candles that burned without melting — he noticed this only later, and it bothered him for days — past doors marked with symbols instead of names: a key, an eye, a closed hand. Through one door he heard the murmur of a negotiation. Through another, someone weeping with what sounded like relief. The third door stood open onto a small room lined floor to ceiling with ledgers, thousands of them, spines cracked and leather gone soft with handling, and a single lamp burning on a table where a woman sat writing.

She didn't look up. "We're closed to new debts after midnight," she said. "If you've come to sell something, come back tomorrow. If you've come to buy something, you should know the prices go up at midnight out of spite."

"It's quarter past," Aldric said.

"I know," she said, and turned the page.

He had time, standing there, to take her in: a young woman, plainly dressed, ink to the second knuckle of her right hand, hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils and a fork. She wrote the way Master Corvin fenced — economically, without a single motion wasted, as though the page were an opponent she respected but did not fear.

"I'm told," Aldric said, "that one can buy an honest answer here."

Now she looked up. Her eyes were the grey of the snow outside, and they went over him with the same economy — face, hands, posture — and he saw her arrive at the correct answer and decide, visibly, not to care.

"Can you," she said. "That's an expensive purchase. Honesty doesn't keep. You have to use it immediately or it spoils."

"That suits me. I've nowhere else to be."

"No," she agreed, looking at the circles under his eyes, "I imagine you haven't." She set down her pen. "State your question. One question. And understand the terms before you ask it, because the Guild sells answers, not comfort, and I am contractually obligated to give you the truth even if it makes you cry, and I say that with respect."

Something in Aldric's chest loosened — a knot he hadn't known was tied. He couldn't remember the last time anyone had spoken to him like a customer rather than a diagnosis.

"The council means to declare me unfit," he said. "They're gathering evidence. My sleeplessness, my — episodes. I want to know: are they right? Is something wrong with me?"

The woman studied him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she pulled a ledger toward herself — not from the shelf nearest her but from a stack beneath the table, an old one, its cover scarred — and began flipping through it with one finger while keeping her eyes on him, which should have been impossible and did not appear to be difficult for her.

"That's not a yes or no question," she said, still turning pages. "Honest answer, properly given, has three parts. Whether something is wrong with you. Whether it makes you unfit. And whether those are the same thing." Her finger stopped on a page. "Part one first. Yes. Something is wrong with you."

"Oh," said Aldric.

"But — hold on." She bent closer to the ledger, frowning. "Hold on, that's not—" She turned the book slightly toward the light, and Aldric saw, upside down, columns of entries in a cramped, beautiful hand, and one entry near the bottom that seemed to interest her very much indeed.

"Is something the matter?" he asked.

"Yes," she said absently. "No. Possibly. This isn't your entry." She put her palm flat on the page — Aldric had the distinct impression she was hiding it — and looked up at him again, all business. "Part two. Does it make you unfit? No. Sleeplessness makes a man tired. It doesn't make him unfit. Half the kings in history couldn't sleep; they conquered things instead. Part three — are those the same thing? Also no, and whoever taught you they were has a reason for wanting you to believe it, and you should find out what that reason costs them."

Aldric stood very still. "That's it? That's the honest answer?"

"That's the honest answer. The crying is optional."

"I wasn't going to cry."

"People say that." She was already looking back down at the ledger, and her frown had deepened. Her finger traced a line of text, stopped, traced it again. "That's strange."

"What's strange?"

"Nothing. Guild business." But she didn't close the book, and she didn't look away from the page, and after a moment she said, quietly, mostly to herself: "This entry is dated tonight."

"Tonight?"

"Dated tonight. Written a century ago." She turned the ledger fully toward him then — perhaps because she'd forgotten he was a stranger, perhaps because she wanted a witness — and pointed at a column of dates rendered in an old calendar system. "Look. Same date. Transposed through the reform. Someone entered this debt a hundred years before it came due, and the due date is—" She checked the wall clock. "Forty minutes ago."

Aldric leaned in despite himself. The entry was brief. A name — a common-sounding name, Eriksen, though the letters were smudged as if the ink had been written wet and handled dry. A debt owed *to the court beneath the mountain*. And a date.

And below the name, a line that made the hair rise on his arms:

*Paid in full upon collection.*

"What does 'the court beneath the mountain' mean?" he asked. "Is that a metaphor?"

"No," she said. "It's an address."

* * *

She should have closed the ledger. Later, lying awake — though she never lay awake, she slept like the dead, a professional courtesy — Beatrix would identify the exact moment she made the mistake, and it was the moment she let a stranger read over her shoulder.

But there was something about him. He had the look she'd learned to recognize among the Guild's clients, the ones who came in at odd hours wearing other people's coats: a person kept in a cage so long they'd started arranging the cage nicely. He stood like someone who had practiced standing casually and gotten quite good at it, and his hands were wrong for a servant's coat — too soft at the fingertips, one faint sword callus, and a signet-shaped indentation on the ring finger where a ring lived by day and traveled separately by night.

She knew who he was within four seconds of him entering the room. She'd said nothing because it wasn't her business, and because the Guild's first rule was record everything and feel nothing, and princes were records waiting to happen.

"You're staring at my hands," he said.

"I'm staring at your coat. It doesn't fit you. Servants' coats fit servants." She closed the ledger — finally, belatedly, a century too late — and laced her fingers on top of it. "Your Highness."

The flinch was small but real. "Does everyone know?"

"At the Guild? We know everything. It's the whole business model." She stood, because protocol apparently lived somewhere in her body even though she'd evicted most of it, and gave a bow shallow enough to be deniable. "Beatrix Eriksen. Night keeper. Your honest answer stands as sold; the crown's account will be billed."

"Don't bill the crown," he said quickly. "Bill me. I'll pay."

"With what? You have no money. Princes never have money; it's how you can tell them from merchants."

"I'll owe you."

The words hung in the lamplight. Beatrix felt them land somewhere below her ribs, which annoyed her. Owing was her native language, and she knew better than anyone that a debt freely accepted weighed differently than one imposed — lighter in the taking, heavier over time.

"One favor," she said. "Redeemable at my discretion. Not tonight, not anything criminal by Guild standards, and nothing involving the crown's official attention."

"Agreed." He said it fast, the way people agree to things they'll regret slowly, and then, because he was apparently constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone: "What was in that ledger? The entry dated tonight?"

"Guild business."

"You said the Guild knows everything. Surely it can survive one prince knowing something."

"It can. Can you?" She cocked her head at him. "Because here's what I know about entries dated a century ahead, Your Highness, and I know it from the filing system: prophecies are just debts with better handwriting. Somebody promised something to somebody, a long time ago, and tonight somebody's account came due." She tapped the ledger's scarred cover. "And if the name in that entry is what I think it is, then the somebody is me."

The lamp flickered. Neither of them moved.

"Your name?" he said.

"Eriksen," she said. "My name. In a book older than my grandfather's grandfather, in handwriting I'd swear on any altar you like is mine." She heard her own voice and was displeased with it — too steady, too much like recitation, the voice of a woman reading numbers off a page because feeling something would require stopping. "So you see why I'm closing the shop now. Good night, Your Highness. Mind the stairs; the fourth step lies."

"The fourth step *lies*?"

"It presents itself as being the last one when it isn't. People have hurt themselves." She blew out the lamp, plunging them both into darkness except for the candle-glow leaking under the door, and in that darkness she heard him laugh — short, startled, genuine — the laugh of a man who had not been surprised by anything in years.

"An honest stair," he said. "Of course. Why not."

"Everything down here is honest," Beatrix said. "It's the only place in the city that can afford to be." She found the door by touch. "Go home, Your Highness. Sleep if you can. And if the council comes for you — remember part three. Find out what their certainty costs them. Everything in this building has a price, including being sure of yourself."

"And what does yours cost?" he asked from the dark. "The honesty. What did you pay for it?"

She paused, hand on the door.

"Ask me in forty years," she said, "if I'm still here."

She heard him smile in the dark, which shouldn't be possible, and then the door opened onto the candlelit corridor, and the prince of the realm climbed the liar's stair and went up into the falling snow, and Beatrix Eriksen sat back down at the table, relit the lamp, and opened the century-old ledger to her own name one more time.

*Paid in full upon collection*, it said.

Below it, in fresher ink — ink that had not been there an hour ago — a second line was drying.

*Witnessed: A.A., quarter past midnight.*

Her pen was exactly where she'd left it. The cap was off.

She had not written that line.

She stared at it for a long time, and then, because recording everything was the one law she had never broken, Beatrix dipped the pen and added a third line in her own careful hand:

*Witnessed: B.E. Someone is writing in my books.*

Somewhere beneath the floor, in the deep cold under Tallow Row, something turned over in its sleep — and far above, in the palace, Prince Aldric slept that night for the first time in three weeks, and dreamed of a ledger opening, and a page turning toward a date circled in red.
