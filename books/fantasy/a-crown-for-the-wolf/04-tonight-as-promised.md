## 4. Tonight, As Promised

The morning of my death began, as most inconvenient days do, with a summons from the Lord Chamberlain.

"Her Majesty requests the taster's presence at tonight's feast," he said, in the tone of a man announcing a change in the weather. "Full service. All courses."

"Of course," I said. "Wouldn't miss it."

He gave me a look — the look courtiers give servants who fail to grovel properly — and swept off down the corridor in a rustle of grey silk. I watched him go and thought, not for the first time, that if the crown ever fell, it would fall because someone tripped him on those stairs.

The date had been sitting in my ledger for three weeks now, copied out in my own careful hand: *Beatrix Eriksen — the longest night of winter.* A century-old prophecy, inked by someone whose handwriting matched mine so exactly it could have been me at my own desk, tired and working late. I had checked it against samples of my writing going back eleven years. Same slant. Same cramped little loops on the E's. Same habit of pressing too hard on the downstrokes, as though the pen owed me money.

Tonight was the longest night of winter.

I spent the day doing what any sensible woman would do when her death is scheduled: I worked. The Guild's cellar books wanted balancing — a debt of one shadow, held against a cooper's son until midsummer; two favors owed by the Widow Marsh, redeemable only on Tuesdays; an entry I still didn't fully understand involving a lute, a duke's daughter, and a promise made under a bridge. Numbers are honest company. They don't prophesy. They just sit there, adding up.

At four o'clock, Oren found me hunched over the hard ledger and set a cup of tea beside my elbow without being asked.

"You've been here since dawn," he said.

"I'm being thorough."

"You're being frightened." He lowered himself onto the stool across from mine, which creaked its objection; Oren has the build of a man who once wrestled a bear and won on points. "Beatrix. A hundred years ago, some taster wrote your name in a book. That's not a sentence. That's a record. Records can be amended."

"That's what frightens me," I said. "I keep the ledgers, Oren. I know how entries get amended."

He had no answer to that, which was answer enough. He stayed while I finished the page, and when I finally closed the book he said, quietly: "Wear something warm tonight. And eat nothing you haven't tasted yourself."

It was the closest thing to affection either of us knew how to say, and I took it with me like a coin in my shoe.

* * *

The great hall at midwinter was a study in gilded desperation.

The long winter had entered its fourth month, and everyone in the capital pretended this was normal. Snow to the second-story windows. The river frozen solid enough that carts crossed it, and the ice groaned beneath them like something waking up. Inside the palace, the fires burned twice as high as they needed to, and the courtiers wore their brightest colors and laughed a half-beat too quickly at everything, the way people do in a house where the floorboards have started talking.

I stood at my station behind the king's right hand, tasting spoon by spoon, and catalogued the evening's poisons out of habit.

Not real ones — the kitchen was clean; I'd walked it myself at noon. The other kind. Chancellor Vess's smile had arsenic in it, all sweetness with a metallic finish, aimed across the table at the Master of Coin. Lady Isolde drank nothing she hadn't poured herself, which told me she expected trouble from someone. And Prince Aldric —

Prince Aldric wasn't looking at anyone. He sat at his father's left, pale as the snow outside, and stared at his plate with the fixed attention of a man performing the act of eating rather than actually doing it. He'd been at the feast forty minutes and hadn't swallowed a bite. I'd noticed because noticing things was my trade, but also because I couldn't seem to stop noticing him lately. Since the cellar. Since we'd both reached for the same ledger page and pulled our hands back like children caught at the jam.

*One honest answer,* he'd asked the Guild for. He never got to hear it before the doors were sealed and the cellars seized. I wondered, sometimes, whether he thought about it. Whether he lay awake in his tower composing questions.

The courses came. I tasted. Venison broth — clean. Honey-glazed roots — clean, and cloying enough to count as a mild assault. The fish course arrived under a sauce of white wine and herbs, and I took my spoonful, held it on my tongue the way I'd been trained since I was nine years old, and felt nothing but butter and tarragon and the particular smugness of a well-made beurre blanc.

Then the swan came out.

Roasted whole, re-dressed in its own feathers, gilded at the beak — the centerpiece dish, the one the king would carve himself, the one every eye in the hall was already on. The kitchen boy who carried it was sweating in a room cold enough to freeze breath, and I noted that too, filed it away, and stepped forward to take my portion from the carving board.

The smell hit me first.

Under the roast and the glaze there was something else — faint, greenish, wrong. A scent like crushed ferns and old coins. My stomach knew it before my mind did. My mind caught up a heartbeat later, dredging it up from the deepest shelf of my memory, where I kept the tastes I'd survived:

*Hollowroot.* Tasted once, aged fourteen, hidden in a honey cake meant for my predecessor. It had put me in the infirmary for nineteen days and taught my body, slowly and cruelly, what poison was. There is no immunity to hollowroot — the herb-mistresses say so, and the herb-mistresses are usually right. But mine had faded to something almost gentle, the way a burn scars over, and I had spent ten years sipping small doses of everything the Guild's enemies might send, building tolerances the way a miser builds a fortune, coin by terrible coin.

There was no time to think. There was barely time to move.

Aldric's serving man was already lifting the carved portion toward the prince's plate. I did the only thing I could do, the thing that would end my quiet life forever: I knocked the fork from his hand.

It rang off the flagstones. Forty minutes of careful court noise stopped dead, the way a clock stops, and then every face in the hall turned toward me — the food-taster, standing over a ruined swan with her heart hammering and her face arranged into what I hoped read as professional alarm and not terror.

"The prince's portion," I said, and my voice came out steady, thank every patient saint, "is spoiled."

"Spoiled." Chancellor Vess rose from his seat like a blade leaving a sheath. "You'll forgive us, girl, if we require more than your word."

"Then require this." I picked up the fallen piece of swan meat, and before anyone — including me — could stop me, I ate it.

The hall went very quiet.

Hollowroot, even faded, is not nothing. It came on slow, a cold spreading through my chest, my fingertips going numb, my pulse stumbling like a drunk on stairs. I stood there in front of the assembled court of a frozen kingdom and let it happen, counting my breaths the way I'd learned at fourteen, riding it down and down until it bottomed out and began, grudgingly, to climb again. It took perhaps two minutes. It felt like a season.

When I could speak, I said: "Hollowroot. Ground fine, folded into the glaze. Whoever prepared the prince's portion used more than mine had — a full dose would have stopped his heart inside the hour." I turned to the king, and bowed, and hated myself for how smoothly my body performed the gesture. "Your Majesty. Your son lives because your taster is difficult to kill."

Silence. Then the king said, in a voice like a door closing: "Arrest the cook."

And Aldric said, rising, pale and furious in a way I'd never seen on him: "No."

Every head turned again. The prince looked at his father, and then at me, and something passed across his face — recognition, horror, and underneath both, a fierce and desperate calculation. He knew. Of course he knew. He'd seen the ledger page; he knew what night this was; he knew the prophecy had promised me to this exact moment. And now I'd gone and thrown myself between him and a poisoned dinner in front of three hundred witnesses.

"Father," he said carefully, "if there is a poisoner at this court, arresting a cook solves nothing. The taster identified the substance. Let her trace it. She's the only one here qualified."

Vess sneered. "Qualified? The woman just ate poison for sport, Your Highness. What manner of person—"

"What manner indeed," Aldric said, and there was steel under the softness now, and I understood suddenly that this sleepless, hunted young man had been sharpening himself in the dark for years. "Perhaps, Chancellor, you'd like to explain why you're so eager to dismiss her. Or perhaps—" and here his voice dropped, carrying in the dead-silent hall like a knife laid flat on a table, "—you'd simply prefer no one looked too closely at the kitchens tonight."

I have seen many things in the Guild's ledgers. I have never seen a room change temperature the way that one did.

The king ended it, as kings do, with a verdict nobody liked. The feast was concluded. The kitchens would be sealed and searched. The taster — me — would be confined to the palace for questioning come morning, "for her own protection," which everyone present correctly translated as *so we can decide what to do with her.* And the prince was escorted from the hall by guards who had not been guarding him an hour ago.

As they led me out through the servant's corridor, past the steam and shouting of a kitchen about to be torn apart, a hand caught my sleeve and drew me into the shadow of the buttery. Aldric. Close enough that I could see the sleeplessness printed under his eyes like a watermark.

"You knew," he whispered. "This was the date. The page. You knew and you came anyway."

"I taste the king's supper every night. This was simply the night my supper tried to fight back."

"Don't." His grip tightened. "Don't make a joke of it. Whatever happens tomorrow — whatever they ask you, whatever they threaten — you tell them nothing about the Guild. Nothing about the ledger. Swear it."

"Why?" I asked, though I suspected I already knew the shape of the answer. "What are you going to do?"

He glanced down the corridor — at the guards waiting, at the sealed kitchens, at the whole glittering trap closing around us both — and when he looked back at me, he wore the expression of a man stepping off a cliff and finding, to his surprise, that he'd always intended to fly.

"Tomorrow," he said, "I'm going to commit treason. Try to stay out of prison long enough to watch."

And then he was gone, swallowed by torchlight and escort, and I stood alone in the dark of the buttery with hollowroot still humming faintly in my blood, and realized that somewhere between the swan and the shadows, the trap had sprung — not on me.

On both of us.
