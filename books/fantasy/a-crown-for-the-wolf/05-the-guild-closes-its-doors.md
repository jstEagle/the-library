## 5. The Guild Closes Its Doors

Oren Vask had been Guildmaster for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one lesson above all others: when the crown comes knocking, it never knocks politely.

He heard the boots on the stairs at half past the ninth bell — too many boots, and the wrong kind of rhythm. Soldiers march in time. The Guild's people shuffle, drift, and slink. These were soldiers.

"Upstairs," he said to Marta, who was mending a candle stub with a needle and thread because the Guild wasted nothing, not even wax. "Take the soft ledgers down to the counting room. You know which ones."

Marta's needle stopped. "All of them?"

"All of them."

She looked at him the way an old dog looks at its master when the master picks up the coat he only wears to funerals. Then she set down her mending, gathered the armful of slim volumes bound in calfskin and dyed linen, and went.

The soft ledgers. Oren had spent forty years in this trade and he still hated that word, *soft*, as though the debts inside were somehow gentle. They were not. They were the small cruelties: the midwife who'd traded three years of her life for her daughter's fever to break; the soldier who'd sold his memory of home to forget what he'd done there; the widow who'd bought back her husband's last words and regretted it every day since. Small debts, human debts, debts with faces attached. If the crown seized those pages, every soul in them became crown property by law of contract, and the crown had never once honored a contract it hadn't written itself.

The hard ledger was different. The hard ledger stayed where it was: chained under the floor of his office, older than the city above it, older than the dynasty that thought it owned everything down to the air in people's lungs.

The boots arrived at his door before Marta reached the bottom stair. Of course they did. Timing was half of tyranny.

"Guildmaster Oren Vask." The man in the doorway wore the silver stag of the Lord Chancellor's office on a chain heavy enough to hang a man with. He had the particular pallor of someone who did paperwork about other people's suffering and had grown quite good at not thinking about it. "By order of His Majesty, these premises are hereby—"

"I know what they're hereby," Oren said. "Come in, then. Mind the third step. It's been loose since the reign of Aldric's grandfather, and I've no intention of fixing it just so the crown can fall through my floor in comfort."

The chancellor's man blinked. Behind him, four soldiers filed in with the grim efficiency of men who'd been told they were raiding a den of criminals and had found instead a fussy old bookkeeper in a cardigan the color of dishwater.

"You were warned," the man said. "After the incident at the winter feast—"

"The incident," Oren said, "in which a taster saved your prince's life, and your response is to arrest everyone involved. Yes. I read the proclamation. It was very well spelled."

"The taster is a suspect. The prince is a suspect. And this establishment—" he gestured at the cellar, at the shelves of ledger-spines, at the brass scales that weighed things no honest scale should weigh, "—is known to traffic in matters the crown has long tolerated out of... pragmatism."

"Tolerance." Oren smiled with all the warmth of a January window. "That's a lovely word for it. Sit down, Master Corwin. No? Standing, then. Let me tell you how this will go, so we can both be home before the snow gets serious."

* * *

They took the cellars first. Of course they did. Soldiers always go down, never up — crowns are afraid of heights they haven't built themselves.

Oren stood at the top of the stairs and watched them carry out the scales, the vials, the cabinets of bottled favors, each one labeled in his own careful hand. A soldier held up a jar containing a single gray feather and asked Corwin what it was. Corwin said he didn't know and didn't want to. That, at least, showed sense.

Marta appeared at Oren's elbow, breathless, ash already smudged along one forearm. "Counting room's clear," she murmured. "Chimney's drawing well tonight. Cold snap helps."

"Good girl."

"They'll smell it."

"They'll smell woodsmoke in a city of ten thousand chimneys and think of supper." He paused. "Did you burn the index first?"

A flicker of guilt. "I started with the middle."

"Marta."

"I panicked!"

"You don't panic. You mend candle stubs. Panic is for people with nothing to lose." But he said it gently, and she knew it, and the guilt turned into something almost like a smile. "Middle's fine. Middle's where the embarrassing ones live anyway. Let the crown dig through what's left and find the Duke of Harrow's IOU for a dancing partner. Serves him right."

Below, a crash. A soldier had knocked over the shelf of hourglasses — the ones filled with sand that ran backward, or sideways, or not at all. Oren watched the sand scatter across the flagstones and felt something in his chest do the same.

Thirty-one years. He'd inherited this place from a woman named Serah Koln, who'd inherited it from a man whose name wasn't written anywhere anymore, and now it would end with soldiers bagging up miracles like confiscated dice.

"Guildmaster." Corwin again, at the foot of the stairs, holding a ledger open. "What is this entry? Dated tonight. A name entered a hundred years ago."

Oren's blood went cold in a way the cold snap had nothing to do with.

"Give it here."

Corwin hesitated — the hesitation of a bureaucrat who has just realized the document in his hands might be important, and important documents are dangerous documents — but handed it over. Oren read the page he already knew by heart. Beatrix Eriksen. Debt owed to the court beneath the mountain. Entered in a hand that matched the girl's own, a century before she was born.

"A clerical error," Oren said. "Old ink, misdated. Happens more than you'd think when you've been keeping books as long as we have."

"It's dated *tonight*."

"Then it's a remarkably punctual error." He closed the ledger. "This one stays with me. Chain of custody. You understand — if the crown takes a ledger with an unresolved debt in it, the debt transfers. To the crown. With interest." He let that sit. "You've seen what the interest on *this* one looks like. It's falling on your head right now. All that white stuff."

Corwin paled. Good. Fear of weather was the most reliable fear in the kingdom.

* * *

They left at midnight, carting away two decades of the Guild's soft history, leaving behind the hard ledger, the empty shelves, and Oren, who sat down heavily at his desk and poured himself the last of the plum brandy he'd been saving for exactly this caliber of disaster.

Marta found him there an hour later, staring at the floorboard under which the hard ledger slept.

"They'll come back," she said.

"They'll come back. Crowns always come back. It's the one thing they're consistent about." He drained the glass. "Which is why the hard ledger doesn't stay here either. Not tonight."

"Where?"

"With the person the prophecy seems to be about." He rubbed his eyes. "Fetch me the page, Marta. The original. From the oldest book."

She brought it wrapped in oilcloth, and Oren unwrapped it with hands that shook slightly, though he'd have denied it under torture. A century-old page, brittle as frost, covered in that impossible handwriting — Beatrix Eriksen's hand, down to the little hook she made on the descender of every *k*, a habit Oren had teased her about for six years.

"Do you believe it?" Marta asked quietly. "That it's a prophecy?"

Oren was quiet a long moment.

"When I was young," he said finally, "Serah Koln told me something I didn't understand until recently. She said: *the Guild does not predict the future, child. We keep receipts.* Every debt, every bargain, every terrible thing anyone ever traded away — it's all already been paid for. Already signed. Prophecy isn't the future arriving. It's the past collecting."

He folded the page carefully. "Beatrix thinks she's found her death date. She hasn't. She's found an invoice. Someone, a hundred years ago, bought something with her name on it, and the mountain has come to collect. The question isn't *what will happen to her*. The question is *what did they buy, and can we return it*."

"And if we can't?"

"Then we renegotiate." He stood, joints cracking like the ice on the river. "We're the Guild of Impossible Things, Marta. Renegotiation is our entire business model."

* * *

He found Beatrix where he knew he would: not at her lodgings, not at the palace, but in the Guild's back garden, standing in the falling snow beside the grave-marker of a cat that had died before she was born, because even grief needed a place to be filed.

She heard him coming — of course she did, she was the best taster the crown had ever employed, she could taste intent in a cup of water — but she didn't turn around.

"They took everything," she said.

"Not everything."

"They took Mrs. Halloway's debt. She traded five years for her son's cough. What happens to those five years now? Does the crown collect them? Does the boy cough forever?"

Oren had no answer that wouldn't be a lie, and lying to Beatrix had never once worked. "Probably the crown loses them in a filing error," he said. "Bureaucracy devours its own eventually. It's the one mercy in the system."

She turned then. Her face in the snowlight was the face of someone who had catalogued every poison in creation and filed her own terror somewhere between arsenic and heartbreak.

"You knew," she said. "About the page. About my name. You've known."

"I suspected. Knowing came tonight, same as yours." He held out the oilcloth bundle. "But I know something else now, and it's the only thing worth knowing, so listen close, girl, because I'm old and the cold is getting into my knees and I won't say it twice."

She took the bundle without opening it.

"The prophecy is not a prediction. It's a receipt. Someone paid for something a century ago, and your name was the currency, and the mountain is simply delivering the bill. Which means it can be disputed. Amended. Returned." He gripped her shoulder, hard, the way he'd gripped the arms of every apprentice he'd ever loved and lost to this trade. "You are not fated, Beatrix Eriksen. You are *owed*. And nobody — no crown, no court, no king under any mountain — gets to collect from one of mine without going through me first."

Her eyes were wet, but her voice, when it came, was steady as a scale at rest. "Where do I start?"

"With the prince. He'll come looking for you tonight — he's already been asking after you, and Aldric never asks after anything unless he means to walk straight into it." Oren pressed the bundle closer against her chest. "And take this. The hard ledger follows you now. Whatever's in the mountain that wants a debt settled — make sure it settles against *them*, and not against you."

Behind them, up the lane, bells began to ring — wrong bells, alarm bells, the pattern that meant fire in the palace quarter. Somewhere in that direction, a feast hall full of nobles was learning what happened when a century-old debt came due.

Beatrix was already moving toward the sound.

Oren watched her go, a small figure swallowed by the snow, carrying a hundred years of somebody else's crime in her arms.

"Well," he said to the empty garden, to the cat's grave, to thirty-one years of a life's work walking out the door in the shape of one stubborn girl. "Let's see what the Guild of Impossible Things can still manage."

He turned back toward the cellar to salvage what he could — and stopped.

There, on his desk, where no one had been, lay a single sheet of fresh paper. New ink, still glistening. One line, written in a hand he recognized as well as his own:

**Debt received. Collection begins at the year's turning.**

And beneath it, a signature that made the cold go all the way through him:

*The Court Beneath the Mountain.*
