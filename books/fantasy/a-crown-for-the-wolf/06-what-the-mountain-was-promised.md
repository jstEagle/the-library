## 6. What the Mountain Was Promised

The city gates closed behind them at the hour when the watch changed, and Beatrix did not breathe properly again until the road bent north and the walls of Vareth became a smudge of torchlight against the snow.

She had stolen a horse. Not a fine one — a piebald mare with a limp and an opinion — but the Guildmaster's coin purse had bought them two hours' head start, and Oren's parting words had bought her something else entirely: a folded page of century-old paper, pressed into her palm like a coal she was expected to carry without burning herself.

*It's a receipt,* he'd said. *Not a prophecy. Receipts tell you who paid, and what for.*

Behind her, Aldric rode hunched in a borrowed fishmonger's coat that fooled no one, least of all the mare, who kept turning her head to look at him as though he owed her money too.

"You're sitting like a sack of turnips," Beatrix said.

"I'm sitting like a man who has never ridden anything but parade horses," Aldric said. "Parade horses are flattered to carry you. This animal despises me."

"She despises everyone. It's why I chose her." Beatrix checked the sky out of habit — habit being the only thing she trusted anymore — and found the clouds low and the wrong color, the gray of old dishwater rather than new snow. "We stop at the shrine of Saint Hedda at the crossroads. There's a well. We'll need water."

"And then?"

"Then we read this." She patted the pocket over her heart where the old ledger page sat. "And I suspect neither of us is going to enjoy it."

They rode on through the falling dark, two fugitives from a feast where a prince had nearly died and a taster had publicly refused to, and somewhere behind them the crown was waking up to the fact that both its problems had left town together.

* * *

Aldric had learned to read his father's moods the way sailors read weather, which was why he'd known for three years that the king intended to have him declared unfit. You didn't survive court by being clever; you survived by knowing exactly how much cleverness you could afford to show.

But he had not known about the ledger. That was the humiliation of it. He had walked into the Guild of Impossible Things prepared to buy one honest answer with a fortune in unmarked silver, and instead he had collided with a food-taster over a page written before either of their grandparents drew breath — a page with *her* name on it, in handwriting that was *hers*, dated to tonight.

Now they sat in the lee of Saint Hedda's crumbling shrine, sharing watered wine from a skin while Beatrix smoothed the ancient paper flat across her knee. The mare dozed. The wind worried at the shrine's broken bell.

"Read it aloud," Aldric said. "I want to hear how it sounds."

"It sounds like a debt," Beatrix said. But she read it anyway, her voice level in the way he was beginning to understand meant the opposite.

*"Received of the Crown of Vareth, in the year of the Long Hunger, one soul entire, freely surrendered by royal warrant, in exchange for the ending of the famine and the turning of the winter. Interest accrues. Payment falls due upon the hundredth year, to be collected by the hand of the taster, called home by the blood of the crown."*

Silence, except for the wind.

"One soul," Aldric said slowly. "Freely surrendered."

"That's the part I keep snagging on." Beatrix traced the line with one finger, careful, the way she touched everything — as if the world might be poisoned. "Nobody surrenders a soul freely, Your Highness. Not even to end a famine. Especially not to end a famine."

"Aldric," he said. "If we're going to be hanged together, you may as well use my name."

"Aldric, then." She didn't smile, but something around her eyes loosened by a degree. "Whose soul, do you think? A criminal? A hostage?"

"My grandfather's journals mention the famine," Aldric said. "The Year of Ash, the tutors called it. Three harvests failed. They don't mention how it ended. History books rarely mention how things end — only that they did." He stared into the dark toward the mountains, white and vast and patient. "But there's a story my nurse told me. She was from the mountain villages. She said the winter up there never fully leaves. That every year it comes a little earlier and stays a little longer, and the old people say someone under the mountain is *keeping* it. Keeping it like — " he hesitated — "like a candle is kept lit."

Beatrix went very still. He had seen her still before, at the feast, in the half-second before she'd knocked the poisoned cup from his hand — the stillness of a woman doing arithmetic very fast.

"The wick," she said. "Oren used that word. When he gave me the page. He said the prophecy was a receipt, and receipts tell you what was bought." Her jaw tightened. "A soul doesn't just sit in a vault, Aldric. A soul has to *do* something. And if the under-court took a soul in exchange for ending one winter — "

"They'd put it to work making winters," Aldric finished. "Forever. As interest." He laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "Gods. My father sits on that throne every solstice and prays for a mild spring, and the whole time — the whole time the crown has been *paying rent* on our own weather."

"Not paying." Beatrix rolled the page closed with the precision of a woman closing an account book. "Accruing. There's a difference. Paying ends a debt. Accruing feeds one." She looked up at him, and in the light of the small fire she'd allowed herself, her face was grim and clear. "The famine ended a hundred years ago. The winter never did. It's been coming down off that mountain a little more each year, and everyone calls it weather, and no one calls it a bill."

* * *

They rode at dawn because Beatrix insisted dawn was for riding and dusk was for thinking, and because standing still felt too much like waiting for the date on the ledger page to catch up with them.

It already had. That was the thing she couldn't say aloud yet. The entry was dated *tonight* — the night of the feast, the night of the poison, the night she'd grabbed a prince's wrist in front of four hundred witnesses and tasted winter-iron and ash on his lips and known, with the certainty of her own tongue, that the poison was one she'd survived once before.

One she'd survived because she'd spent eleven years feeding it to herself in drops.

She hadn't told Aldric that. There were things a person got to keep, even from princes, even from allies. Even from whoever Aldric was becoming to her — some category she hadn't named yet, filed carefully between *burden* and *something she would deny under torture*.

At midday they reached the village of Thornmere, or what the maps called Thornmere. The maps were forty years old. What stood in the valley now was a village buried to its second stories in snow, doors dug out like burrows, smoke rising from holes in the drifts. An old man digging a path to his woodpile straightened when they passed and looked at them with eyes gone pale as frost glass.

"You're headed up?" he asked.

"Toward the mountain," Beatrix admitted.

"Then you know, do you?" He leaned on his spade. "Everyone asks about the weather. Nobody asks about the *why* of it. My grandmother said there was a bargain. Said the king's men came through in the hunger year and bought something from a family here. Paid in gold enough to choke a horse." His mouth twisted. "Family ate well that winter. Every winter after, a little less. Now look at us."

"Did your grandmother say what was bought?" Aldric asked quietly.

The old man spat into the snow. "A daughter. Third one they had. Girl named Ylva." He said the name the way people say names of the dead — carefully, so as not to wake anything. "Went up the mountain with the king's men singing, my gran said. Singing, because she thought she was going to be a lady at court." He shook his head and returned to his digging, done with them.

Beatrix rode out of Thornmere with her hands shaking on the reins and made herself catalog the shaking the way she catalogued everything: not fear, exactly. Recognition. She kept ledgers of debts for a living. She knew what it meant when the numbers finally resolved into faces.

Ylva. A girl with a name, a family, a song in her throat. Sold by her own kin for grain and gold, purchased by a crown that wrote it down as *freely surrendered* because paper forgives whatever you ask it to.

And the crown had renewed the bargain. She was sure of it now, sure the way she was sure of arsenic in wine. Debts like this didn't lapse; they compounded. Someone had signed again. Perhaps several someones, down a century of kings, each one inheriting the arrangement along with the throne and the seal and the comfortable lie that the long winter was nobody's fault.

"We should turn back," Aldric said that evening, at their fire. "No. Listen. If my father signed the renewal — if any of this traces to the crown — then going to the mountain isn't justice, it's treason with extra steps. We could expose it here. Petition. There are laws — "

"There are laws the Guild keeps in cellars precisely because the crown's laws don't reach," Beatrix said. "You want a petition? To whom? The man who renewed the debt?" She fed a stick to the fire. "Besides. Read the clause again."

He did, by firelight, lips moving slightly — a habit she found absurdly human and refused to find charming. *"To be collected by the hand of the taster, called home by the blood of the crown."*

"Called home," he repeated. "That means you. The taster goes to the mountain whether we will it or not."

"Whether *I* will it or not. You could still ride back and pretend none of this touched you." She said it evenly, offering it the way she offered bitter medicine — straight, no honey. "In fact, that's the sensible course. Go back. Deny me. Say the mad taster drugged you and dragged you out of the city. It might even be believed."

"No," Aldric said.

"It wasn't a real offer. I was testing your character."

"I know. I decided to pass anyway." He turned the page over in his hands, frowning. "There's something else. 'The blood of the crown.' Beatrix — the entry was written a hundred years ago, but the ink matched yours. Oren said the first taster wrote it. Your ancestor. Which means the prophecy — the receipt — has been waiting a century for a taster with your name." He looked up. "What if it isn't waiting for you by accident?"

The fire popped. Somewhere far off, a wolf sang, long and low, a sound like the country itself clearing its throat.

"Everything about me is an accident," Beatrix said. "An orphan with a cast-iron stomach, taken in by a Guild that trades in impossibilities. That's not destiny. That's inventory."

"Maybe." He said it gently, which was worse than arguing. "Or maybe the Guild has been keeping you the way the under-court keeps Ylva. For a purpose. Against a due date."

She wanted to argue. She was, professionally, excellent at argument. But the words wouldn't come, because underneath her ribs, in the place where she kept the things she recorded and refused to feel, something had begun — very quietly, very patiently — to feel.

* * *

On the fourth day the snow began to fall upward.

Aldric saw it first and said nothing for a full hour, convinced his sleepless years had finally presented him the bill. Flakes rising from the drifts, drifting past them toward the peaks, unhurried, as though the mountain were inhaling. When he finally spoke, Beatrix only nodded, as if upward snow were a minor administrative matter.

"The land remembers what happened here," she said. "Oren told me once that the Guild's oldest ledgers weren't written on paper. Paper burns. The first entries were cut into the mountain roads themselves — tolls and treaties, carved into stone so the land itself would hold the memory." She glanced at him. "I thought it was a metaphor. Guildmasters love metaphors. It's cheaper than magic."

"And now?"

"Now I think we're riding up a ledger." She reined the mare aside to let a rising flurry pass between them like a curtain. "And ledgers, Aldric, always balance. Something was taken from this mountain. The mountain intends to collect."

That night, sheltering in a shepherd's hut with a roof of turf and a floor of packed earth, Aldric sat awake — of course awake; sleep and he had long ago agreed to see other people — and watched Beatrix by the banked coals. She was writing. Of course writing. A small notebook, entries in her quick precise hand, and he realized with a strange lurch that she was recording *him*: what he'd eaten, what he'd said, the exact hour the upward snow began. Documenting their flight the way she documented everything.

"Why?" he asked softly.

She didn't pretend not to understand. "Because things that get written down can't be rewritten. Names, dates, debts, promises. Ink is the only honest witness I've ever had." A pause. The coals shifted. "My mother sold me to a taster's household when I was six. No papers. No record. One morning I simply belonged to someone else, and there was nothing anywhere in the world that said I had ever belonged to anyone else at all. So I started keeping records. First of my own life. Then everyone's." She closed the notebook. "Record everything. Feel nothing. It's a good rule."

"Is it holding?"

"No," Beatrix said, with the honesty of a woman balancing accounts at last. "It's failing completely. I'm beginning to feel quite a lot, and I resent every minute of it."

Aldric laughed — really laughed, for the first time since the feast — and outside, the snow went on falling upward, and the mountain waited above them with a girl's name carved somewhere in its memory, and a hundred-year-old bill coming due at the year's turning.

Neither of them slept. Neither of them mentioned the third figure the firelight threw on the wall — tall, thin, standing just behind Beatrix's shoulder, gone whenever either of them turned to look.

But both of them saw it. And both of them, in the manner of people raised on secrets, wrote it down.
