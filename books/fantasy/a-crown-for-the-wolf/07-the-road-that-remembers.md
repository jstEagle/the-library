## 7. The Road That Remembers

The snow began falling upward on the third day.

Beatrix noticed it the way she noticed everything — sideways, in the corner of her attention, the way a taster notices the wrong sweetness in wine. They were climbing the old shepherd's switchbacks above Thornmere, Aldric two paces ahead with his hood up and his royal posture abandoned somewhere around the second switchback, when she stopped and held out her bare hand.

A flake landed on her glove. Then another. Then, absurdly, one rose past her wrist, past her elbow, drifting up into the grey sky as if it had forgotten something at home.

"Aldric."

"I'm resting," he said, which was not what she'd said.

"Look at the snow."

He looked. He watched one flake rise past his nose, tilt slightly, as if considering, and continue upward into the clouds. His mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"Is that supposed to happen?"

"Nothing on this road is supposed to happen. That appears to be the point of it." She turned her hand over and let another flake rise off her palm. It was warm. That was the strangest part — not the direction, but the warmth, like a cat deciding to sit on you. "The Guild's records mention the mountain road exactly four times. Three of those entries are complaints. The fourth is a bill of sale for a mule that came back down the mountain without its rider and refused to be loaded again."

"The mule had the right idea."

"The mule," Beatrix said, "was not sworn to protect a prince."

"No," Aldric said, and there was something careful in the way he said it. "That was you."

She let that sit between them the way she let a suspicious silence sit between a cook and a casserole. They had been two days out of the city, two days since Oren pressed the century-old ledger page into her hands and told her the prophecy was a receipt, and they had not yet had the conversation that page demanded. Beatrix was an expert at not having conversations. She could not-have a conversation for years if the occasion required it.

But the snow was falling upward, and the road was watching them — she was fairly sure the road was watching them — and some conversations, like poisons, only got worse with delay.

"Keep walking," she said. "We'll reach the treeline by dark if the treeline hasn't moved."

"The treeline moves?"

"The treeline," said Beatrix, "is the least of our problems."

* * *

It was Aldric who spotted the village first, which surprised her, because she'd assumed a lifetime of palace corridors had dulled his eyes. But he stopped at the ridge and pointed down into the valley, where a scatter of stone cottages huddled around a frozen well, smoke rising from chimneys in thin, obedient columns.

"People," he said, with the naked relief of a man who had spent two days eating hard bread and pretending it was fine. "Real people. We can ask about the pass."

"We can ask about the pass," Beatrix agreed, "and they will tell us something, and it will be useless, and we'll be grateful anyway. That's what villages are for."

She regretted the joke before it finished leaving her mouth, because the village was not fine.

It was the well that told her. A village of twenty-odd homes should have had twenty-odd trails worn in the snow, a mess of comings and goings, laundry lines, woodpiles in varying states of neglect. This village had one trail. A single path, wide and worn, that ran from every door to the well and back, as if the whole settlement were one creature with many windows.

The woman who answered the first door had flour on her hands and a face like a winter apple — round, wind-chapped, kind around the eyes.

"Travelers," she said warmly. "Come in, come in. You'll want soup. Everyone wants soup, up here."

The second cottage belonged to a man mending a net, though the nearest water was the well and the well was frozen. He had flour on his hands. He had a face like a winter apple. He said, "Travelers. Come in, come in. You'll want soup."

Beatrix stood in the lane between the cottages and felt the ground tilt gently under her certainty.

The third door opened before she knocked. The woman behind it — winter apple, flour, warmth — said, "You'll want soup," and then paused, and looked at Beatrix with those kind eyes, and added, "But you're not hungry, are you? Isn't that strange. Everyone's hungry, up here. Everyone but you."

"Aldric," Beatrix said quietly, "do not eat the soup."

"I wasn't going to eat the soup."

"You were absolutely going to eat the soup. You have the face of a man about to eat the soup."

He did. He had the face of a starving prince confronted with hospitality, and she watched him wrestle it down with visible effort, which she would have found funny under almost any other circumstances. The villagers gathered around them in the lane — eight of them now, nine, all the same face wearing different coats — and they did not threaten. That was the horror of it. They simply stood there, smiling with the same smile, waiting, patient as snowfall.

"You're not from the mountain," the flour-handed man said. It wasn't a question.

"No," Beatrix said.

"But you were made for it."

The lane went very quiet. Even the upward snow seemed to hang in the air, listening.

"That's a strange thing to say," Beatrix managed.

"It's not strange at all," said the woman, and now they were all speaking together, or perhaps it had always been one voice wearing many coats, gentle and vast and faintly sorry. "Nothing up here is strange. It's only the lowlands that are strange, with all your separate faces and your separate hungers. Down there, everyone gets to be only one person. Up here we remember better." The smile didn't waver. "You were made for the mountain, taster. Ask yourself who did the making. Ask yourself what you were made *from*."

And then, all at once, they lost interest. The villagers turned back to their cottages, their nets, their nonexistent fishing, and the single trail filled softly with upward-drifting snow behind them, and the lane was empty, and the soup smell was gone, and Aldric was gripping her arm hard enough to bruise.

"Beatrix."

"I heard it."

"What did it mean, *made for the mountain*?"

"I don't know." Her voice came out level, which cost her something. "I keep ledgers. I don't keep answers."

* * *

They did not stay in the village. They climbed past it as the light failed, and made a cold camp in the rocks above the treeline — or above where the treeline had been an hour before, and was not now, the trees having quietly relocated downslope with the sheepish air of furniture being rearranged.

Aldric built a fire with the competence of a man who had, apparently, actually been paying attention during those wilderness tutorials the crown forced on its heirs. Beatrix sat with the ledger page on her knees and read it again by firelight, for the fortieth time, hoping it would say something new.

It never did. It said what it always said:

*Item: one soul, sold in good faith, to the court beneath the mountain. Payment rendered: the end of the famine, and the winter that follows as interest. Witnessed and entered by the hand of the first taster, who swore the debt would come due in her successor's time, on this date, by this road, in the company of the prince.*

And beneath that, in ink that was somehow both a century old and fresh as this morning: *Beatrix Eriksen. She will consent.*

"She will consent," Aldric read over her shoulder, for the fortieth time. "You know, most prophecies at least have the decency to threaten you. Yours just assumes."

"That's what frightens me about it." She folded the page away. "A threat you can fight. An assumption just sits there, being correct at you."

He sat down beside her, close enough that the fire's warmth had to negotiate between them, and was quiet for a while. Aldric was better at quiet than he'd been a week ago. A week of running from the crown he was born to inherit had burned a lot of ceremony off him, the way a long illness burns off vanity.

"The villagers," he said at last. "What they said. About you being made."

"I've been turning it over."

"Your immunity. The poison you survived — the one that was meant for me. The tasters' guild trains immunity, I know that, they dose you all from childhood, it's in the histories—"

"It is. And I was dosed. Everyone is." Beatrix held her hands toward the fire. They were steady. They were always steady; it was one of the things she liked least about them. "But there's dosed, and there's what I am. I've taken things that should have killed a trained taster three times over. I tasted the ash-wine at the winter feast and my tongue didn't even water. Master Oren used to watch me work with a look I never understood. I thought it was pride." She paused. "It wasn't pride, was it. It was the way you watch a lock you built."

"You think the Guild made you."

"I think someone made me. The Guild is the likeliest someone." The words came out of her with a strange, terrible ease, like a tooth that had been loose for years finally letting go. "Beatrix Eriksen. Orphan of the Ashwinter, raised in the Guild's cellars, apprenticed to the ledgers at six, dosed with poisons no child should survive — and survived them all, every one, without a scar, without a fever, without a single bad night. I always thought I was lucky." She laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and glassy. "I catalogued poisons like old friends. I told myself it was a skill. It was never a skill, Aldric. It was a *feature*. Someone sat down with a ledger and designed a taster who could walk this road, and I've been balancing their columns my whole life without knowing I was one of the entries."

The fire cracked. Above them, snow rose off the rocks in slow, warm handfuls and went home to the sky.

Aldric didn't offer comfort, which she was grateful for. He was learning. Instead he said, "My mother used to say that being made for something is only terrible if you let someone else keep the recipe."

"Your mother," Beatrix said, "sold a memory of her own death to buy your life. With respect, your mother's aphorisms come from a complicated place."

"Yes," he said. "That's rather the point of her."

And despite everything — the face-village, the rising snow, the loose tooth of her whole identity rattling in her skull — Beatrix felt her mouth twitch. That was the trouble with Aldric. He was growing on her the way a poison grows on you: slowly, invisibly, until one day you realize you can no longer imagine your blood without him in it.

"Here's what I know," she said, because saying things in order helped, it was the one ritual she had left that still felt like hers. "One: I was built for this road, by someone, for some purpose. Two: the purpose involves the court beneath the mountain, and a soul, and a consent the prophecy is very confident I'll give. Three: everything I believed about myself — the luck, the talent, the iron stomach, the *rule*—" her rule, the old one, *record everything, feel nothing* — "was scaffolding. Someone put it there. And scaffolding, Aldric, is only ever built around something they intend to use."

"Then we take the scaffolding down," he said simply. "You've read enough ledgers to know what a forged entry looks like. So read yourself. Find the forgery. And whatever's underneath it — that's the real you, and the real you gets to decide."

It was a foolish, princely, beautiful thing to say, and it helped, and she hated how much it helped.

* * *

She woke in the black hour before dawn to find the fire dead and the snow falling upward in earnest now — a whole blizzard of it, rising in silence, and in the rising snow, light. Not firelight. A line of it, faint and blue-white, running along the road ahead of them like a seam of silver stitching the mountain's hem.

Beatrix sat up. Her body, her impossible body, was *humming*. That was the only word for it — a low, tuning-fork hum in her bones, in her blood, in the tongue that had never once failed her. The road was calling something home, and whatever it was calling, it was calling with her voice.

"Aldric," she whispered.

He was already awake. Of course he was. The prince who cannot sleep. He was staring up the road at the silver seam with an expression she was beginning to know well — the expression of a man reading a ledger entry with his own name in it.

"It knows we're coming," he said.

"Yes."

"Beatrix." He hesitated. "The villagers said to ask who made you. But there's a worse question, isn't there. I keep circling it."

She knew. She had been circling it all night, all her life, and it had finally landed and folded its wings.

"Not who made me," she said. "*When.* Because if someone built a taster for this road, a century ago, and wrote my name in a ledger a century ago—" her breath came out white and rose, like the snow, like everything on this mountain, in the wrong direction, "—then they didn't build me *for* this journey, Aldric."

The silver seam brightened. Somewhere far above, in the dark under the peak, something ancient and patient turned a page.

"They built me *from* it. Come on. The road's open, and it isn't going to stay that way."
