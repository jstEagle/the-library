## 8. An Inn with No Keeper

The inn appeared the way a lie appears — all at once, and only when you needed it.

One moment there was nothing on the road but snow and the skeletons of pines, and the next moment there was a building squatting in a clearing like it had always been there and had simply been waiting for them to stop being rude about it. It was two stories of dark timber with a stone chimney smoking cheerfully, windows glowing amber, a sign swinging over the door on hinges that did not creak.

The sign read *The Wayfarer's Rest* in letters that rearranged themselves slightly every time Aldric blinked.

"That wasn't there," Beatrix said.

"No," Aldric agreed.

"Roads don't do that."

"This one does. Everything past the tollgate does." He urged his horse forward before he could think better of it, which was, he was learning, how most of his decisions got made these days. "And we're past the tollgate."

Behind him, Beatrix made a sound that meant she was cataloguing objections and would present them later, itemized. He'd known her for three weeks and he already had a private taxonomy of her silences. This one was the *I have counted seventeen ways this can kill us and you've chosen the stupidest one* silence.

The stable, when they found it around the side, was warm and full of hay and contained no horses other than their own, though three stalls stood open with fresh straw as if someone had expected exactly two travelers on exactly this night. There was no groom. There was no anyone. The whole establishment had the feeling of a stage set between performances — everything arranged, nothing inhabited.

"I hate this," Beatrix announced, handing him her reins.

"You said that about the village with the shared face."

"I stand by both statements. Consistency is a virtue."

"It's also going to freeze to death out here. Come inside."

---

Inside was worse, or better, depending on how you felt about fireplaces that burned without wood. A great hearth dominated the common room, flames leaping from what appeared to be absolutely nothing, casting more heat than any fire Aldric had ever sat beside. A long table held bread that steamed, cheese that smelled of summer meadows, and a jug of something dark that Beatrix examined for a full minute before pronouncing it "probably wine, probably safe, probably not a trap."

"Three probablies," Aldric said. "That's practically a guarantee, by your standards."

"My standards are the reason you're alive."

This was true. She'd tasted everything he'd eaten since they fled the capital — snatched each morsel from his hand with the reflexes of a woman who had spent years being paid to die on schedule. Old habits. He suspected she'd taste his food at his coronation if she were still speaking to him by then.

Which brought him, unpleasantly, to the thing he needed to say tonight.

He'd been circling it for two days. He'd rehearsed it against the rhythm of his horse's hooves, against the upward-falling snow, against the memory of her face when she'd caught him lying about why he flinched at lullabies. He had drafted eleven versions. Nine were cowardly. Two were honest. He could not tell, sitting here in an inn with no keeper, watching her butter bread with surgical suspicion, whether he had the courage for either.

She caught him staring. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The princely thing. Where you arrange your face like a state dinner and pretend nothing is wrong." She set down the knife. "You've been doing it since the mountain road. I keep ledgers, Aldric. I know what concealment looks like. It looks exactly like you."

The fire popped, though there was nothing to pop.

He should tell her. That was the whole point of the last two days of rehearsal. And yet his mouth, traitorous organ, said instead: "There's no keeper here. Doesn't that strike you as strange? An inn built to be kept, with nobody keeping it."

"Don't change the subject."

"I'm not changing the subject. I'm delaying it. There's a difference, and the difference is cowardice."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, quickly filed away behind the taster's professional blankness. But not fast enough. He saw it. And seeing it shamed him enough that the truth finally broke loose, the way a cart wheel breaks loose on a steep road: all at once, downhill, crushing whatever stood beneath it.

"I've been to the Guild before," he said.

Beatrix went very still. Stillness, in her, was louder than shouting.

"Before the ledger page. Before the prophecy. Before any of this." He turned his cup in circles on the table, watching the wine catch the impossible firelight. "Three years ago. I came at night, through the cellar door, the way everyone does. I bought something."

"Bought what?"

"A deletion."

The word hung in the air between them. Beatrix's hands, folded on the table, tightened until the knuckles went white.

"The crown pays the Guild a tithe every year," Aldric went on, because now that the dam had broken he couldn't seem to stop, words spilling out in the order they'd been dammed up rather than any sensible sequence. "Everyone knows the crown pretends not to see the Guild. What nobody says aloud is *why*. It's not tolerance. It's payment. My father has been buying things from Oren's predecessor since before I was born. Silence. Forgetting. Small mercies with large prices. And three years ago, when the court faction that wanted me declared unfit started gathering evidence—" He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Do you know what my mother died of? Officially?"

"Fever," Beatrix said quietly. "Winter fever. It's in the court records. I've read them."

"There is no record because there was no fever. She was poisoned. Slowly, over months, by her own physician, on my father's instruction, because she opposed the renewal of the mountain debt and she was beginning to say so where people could hear." His voice came out flat, which surprised him; he'd expected it to shake. Perhaps he'd spent all his shaking years ago. "I found out. I was nineteen and furious and stupid, and I went to the Guild to buy proof. Evidence I could use against my father. And Oren's predecessor looked at me with those terrible patient eyes and told me the truth about purchases: that the Guild doesn't sell weapons, Highness, it sells relief. And that what I actually wanted — what I'd walked through half the city in the rain wanting, without knowing it — was to stop remembering."

The fire burned its silent nothing-fire. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled, though no one walked upon it.

"So I sold it," Aldric said. "The night she died. Not the fact of it — I kept the fact, the way you keep a scar. I sold the memory of it. Her voice. Her hands. The way the room smelled. Three hours of the worst night of my life, gone, and in exchange the Guild marked my debt to the crown as *transferred*, which meant the faction hunting me lost their leverage, which meant I lived." He finally looked up. "That's the price the crown paid to spare me, Beatrix. It didn't spare me. It let me pay my own ransom with the only coin I had that they wanted. And then I walked out of that cellar lighter than I'd walked in, and I hated how light I felt, and I have hated myself for it every day since."

Silence.

Beatrix had not moved. She sat with her hands flat on the table like a woman steadying herself against weather, and her face had done something he'd never seen it do: it had stopped performing altogether. No taster's blankness. No ledger-keeper's composure. Just a woman looking at a man who had just handed her the worst thing about himself and was waiting, with his heart in his throat, to find out what she'd charge him for it.

"You knew her," she said at last. "Your mother. You knew her your whole life."

"Yes."

"And you sold the knowing."

"I sold the worst night of the knowing. Which turned out to be most of it. Memory is a greedy creditor — take one piece and the rest comes due." He swallowed. "I remember that she existed. I remember that I loved her. I remember that I grieved. I remember everything *around* it, the way you remember the shape of a hole. But the middle is gone. Someone else owns my mother's death, Beatrix. Some stranger in the Guild's cellar keeps the last hours of Queen Isolde in a jar somewhere, and I paid to lose them, and some nights I would burn the whole kingdom for one minute of them back."

Beatrix exhaled slowly. When she spoke, her voice was careful in the way of someone walking on ice they've decided to trust.

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because you asked me on the road why I flinch at lullabies, and I lied to you, and you knew I lied, and you let it go, and letting it go was worse than catching me." He spread his hands. "Because we're walking into the under-court together, and the under-court trades in truths, and I will not walk into that place carrying a lie about you. Because—" and here his rehearsal failed him entirely, and the twelfth version, the unwritten one, came out raw: "because you've been deciding for three weeks whether loyalty to me is worth anything, and you deserve to make that decision with the full ledger. All the entries. Even the shameful ones. *Especially* the shameful ones. You taught me that. Record everything. Feel nothing. Well — I've recorded everything. The feeling part I'm still working on."

For a long moment Beatrix said nothing. Then she reached across the table, took his cup, poured a measure of the probably-wine into it, tasted it herself first — old habit, and this time the gesture landed on him like a blow, gentle and devastating — and pushed it back toward him.

"Not poisoned," she said. "Drink. You look like you need it."

He drank. It was, in fact, excellent wine. It tasted faintly of summer, which in this winter-strangled country felt like a small miracle or a small mockery, and he couldn't decide which.

"I'm angry with you," Beatrix said.

"I know."

"Not for the sale. That was a boy in a rainstorm being offered the only mercy anyone had ever offered him. I'm angry that you let me build three weeks of trust on a foundation with a crack in it and never said *mind the crack*." She turned her own cup slowly. "I have a rule. Record everything, feel nothing. You just watched me break the second half of it twice today — once when I cried at the village with the shared faces, once just now when you said *some stranger keeps my mother's death in a jar*. So understand what it costs me to say the next part."

"Which is?"

"Loyalty isn't a clean ledger. I've known that longer than I've admitted, even to myself. Every entry in the Guild's books is somebody's worst night, written down in a fair hand by someone who pretended not to care. Including mine." She met his eyes. "You lied by omission. I've done worse — I've lied by profession. We're neither of us clean, Aldric. The question was never whether you were spotless. The question is whether the crack runs all the way through." She paused. "It doesn't. I checked. I'm very good at checking."

The laugh that escaped him was wet and unplanned and he didn't bother to hide it. "Is that — was that a joke? Did the taster just make a joke?"

"Don't get used to it. Humor is a controlled substance in my company."

"Then I'll ration it."

But she wasn't smiling, quite. She was looking at him with an expression he was starting to recognize as the one she wore when she was balancing accounts — weighing what a thing cost against what it was worth, and finding, reluctantly, that the columns nearly matched.

"One condition," she said.

"Name it."

"When this is over — when Ylva is free and the winter breaks and we're either dead or heroes or both — you buy it back. The memory. Whatever it costs. Whatever the Guild charges, whatever the under-court demands, whatever price gets attached to three hours of one terrible night." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands did, very slightly, on the table. "No one should have to live as a stranger to their own grief. I've seen what that does. I *am* what that does, a little. Promise me."

"I promise," Aldric said, and found that he meant it in the marrow-deep way, the way he'd sworn almost nothing else in his carefully preserved life.

She nodded once, businesslike, and returned to her bread, and the crisis was — not over, nothing with Beatrix was ever over, merely entered into the ledger and carried forward. But the air between them had changed. Lighter. As if a splinter long lodged had come out, and the wound could finally close around its own shape.

They ate. The fire burned its nothing. Outside, the upward snow drifted past the windows like the sky reconsidering itself.

It was Beatrix who noticed, first, that the bread never diminished no matter how much they ate. Then that the candles hadn't shortened. Then that the jug refilled itself whenever neither of them was looking directly at it.

"This inn," she said slowly, "is feeding us."

"Inns do that."

"These things don't *do* things, Aldric. They're arrangements. Transactions. Somebody built this place to serve a purpose, and hospitality is the bait." She stood, chair scraping, and looked around the empty common room with narrowed eyes. "So what's the hook inside the bait?"

As if summoned — and Aldric would wonder, later, whether it had been — a sound came from the stairs. Footsteps. The first footsteps the inn had produced all night, slow and deliberate, descending.

At the bottom of the staircase stood a figure in a gray traveling cloak with the hood drawn, and where a face should have been there was only a smooth oval of shadow, and in one gloved hand it held a small brass bell, which it rang, once.

The sound of the bell did not so much ring as *subtract* — one clear note that seemed to take something away from the room rather than add to it. The fire dimmed. The warmth thinned. And Aldric felt, with cold certainty, that something had just been weighed.

"Hospitality," said the figure, in a voice like pages turning, "is never free on this road. The keeper is absent, but the account remains. In the morning, at the tollgate of small mercies, payment will be collected." The hood tilted toward Beatrix. "Truths are the accepted currency. Truths freely given. The gate knows the difference."

"And if we refuse to pay?" Beatrix asked, hand drifting to the knife at her belt.

The hood turned to her, and though it had no eyes, Aldric felt it look *through* her — felt it read her the way she read ledgers, line by line, entry by entry.

"Then the road takes its toll anyway," the figure said. "The road always takes its toll. The only choice offered travelers is *who chooses what is taken*." It raised the bell again. "Sleep well. The beds are honest, at least. One of the few honest things left on this mountain."

It climbed the stairs backward, which no body should be able to do, and was gone, and the fire came back up, and the inn was warm and bright and empty again, and somehow that was worse.

Aldric looked at Beatrix. Beatrix looked at Aldric.

"Truths," she said.

"Truths," he agreed grimly. "Freely given."

She sat back down and stared into the fireless fire for a long time, and when she finally spoke, her voice was very quiet.

"Aldric. There's a truth I've been carrying that I haven't given you. And I think the mountain already knows I have it."
