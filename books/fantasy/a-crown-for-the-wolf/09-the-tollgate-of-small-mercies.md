## 9. The Tollgate of Small Mercies

The road to the mountain did not so much climb as *accumulate*, gathering altitude the way a miser gathers coins — grudgingly, and all at once when you weren't looking. By the third day out of the city, the pines had given up pretending to be trees and become mere suggestions of trees, black spines against a sky the color of old pewter. The snow here fell in a manner I can only describe as *sulky*: not down, not quite sideways, but drifting upward at the edges, as if it were trying to return to the cloud from which it came and losing its nerve halfway.

I had catalogued this. In my head, in the ledger-voice, the one that turned everything into entries: *Upward snow. Note: unnatural. Note: do not comment on it aloud, as His Highness will only want to discuss it.*

Aldric commented on it anyway.

"It's falling up," he said, for the fourth time since dawn, holding out his bare hand like a man greeting an old friend he'd half expected to be dead. "Beatrix. It's falling *up*."

"I heard you the first three times."

"Yes, but it keeps doing it." He caught a flake on his palm and watched it lift away again, and there was something in his face that made me soften despite myself — the same look he'd worn over the shared one-faced village yesterday, the wonder of a boy who had been kept indoors his whole life and was seeing weather for the first time. Which, in a sense, he was. Princes are kept indoors from far more than weather.

"The villages back there," I said. "Everyone with the miller's face. You didn't find that worth four repetitions."

"I found it terrifying," Aldric said cheerfully. "Terrifying things I don't repeat. Only marvelous ones." He tucked his hand back into his cloak. "You should try marveling sometime. I think you'd be good at it. You have the face for it — very stern, very repressed. All that wonder backed up like water behind a dam."

"I am a professional, Your Highness."

"You are a *witness*, is what you are. You walk through the world like a woman writing testimony." He said it lightly, but Aldric had a way of saying true things lightly; it was, I'd begun to understand, how he survived. "One day the testimony is going to be full and the woman will be empty."

I did not answer that, because the road answered for me.

It happened between one step and the next. The pines simply stopped — not thinned, not dwindled, *stopped*, as if drawn by a hand that grew bored — and before us stood a gate across the whole width of the mountain pass, which was absurd, because the pass was a quarter mile wide and gates are built for doors. This one didn't care. It was made of gray wood gone silver, and it stood alone in the open snow with nothing to connect to, two posts and a lintel and a barrier of... I want to say mist, but mist implies softness. It was more like the memory of a wall. You understood, looking at it, that passing through it would be like passing through your own worst winter.

And sitting on the lintel, swinging her boots like a girl on a fence, was the tollkeeper.

She was small. That's the first thing. Everyone expects border spirits to be vast — antlered, storm-crowned, the whole theatrical inventory — and she was perhaps five feet nothing, wrapped in seven or eight shawls of clashing patterns, with a face like a dried apple and eyes like two coins dropped in deep water. A tin cup hung at her belt. A ledger — an actual ledger, leather-bound, swollen with inserts and loose pages — lay open beside her on the lintel.

"Oh good," she said, in a voice like a kettle just coming to the boil. "Travelers. And only two of you. Do you know what I usually get? Armies. Armies pay in *atrocities*, and atrocities take *forever* to process. But two travelers." She rubbed her hands together with a sound like paper. "Two travelers pay in truths. Lovely. Come along, come along, mind the snow, it bites."

Aldric, to his credit, did not reach for a sword he wasn't carrying. He bowed instead — the automatic court bow, shallow and graceful — and I saw the tollkeeper's coin-eyes gleam with delight.

"Oh, *nobility*," she breathed. "This is going to be expensive."

"We're traveling to the court beneath the mountain," Aldric said. "We request passage."

"Request granted, pending payment. One truth each." She hopped down off the lintel with surprising lightness and opened her ledger on the air itself, which held it, because apparently physics was another thing that stopped at the tree line. "House rules. Not a lie dressed up as truth. Not a truth you've already given away so often it's worn smooth. Not someone else's secret — your own, or nothing. And it has to *cost* you." She looked up, and her apple-face went suddenly ancient, suddenly vast, and I understood why armies paid in atrocities. "The tollgate of small mercies takes only what hurts to give. That's the whole point of the name."

"And how do you verify?" I asked. Ledger to ledger. Professional courtesy.

The smile she gave me had too many teeth in it for the size of her mouth. "Oh, I've been doing this a long time, taster."

I hadn't told her what I was. Neither had Aldric, insofar as I could recall our entire acquaintance involving anyone else.

"Who goes first?" she asked, quill appearing in her fingers from somewhere among the shawls.

"I will," Aldric said, and I looked at him sharply, because princes do not volunteer for things that hurt unless they're performing, and I'd have sworn on every ledger in the Guild that he was past performing with me. Three days on the road does something. It strips the ceremony off a person like bark off a birch.

"Go on then, princeling."

He stood straight. He looked at her, and then — this was the part I didn't expect — he looked at me first, briefly, as if asking permission, or granting warning.

"When I was nine years old," he said, "my mother died, and I don't remember it. Not the death. Anything of her. I sold the memory to buy my own life, and I have spent twenty years being told I should grieve a woman I cannot even picture, and the worst part —" his voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse — "the worst part is that I don't even feel bad about not feeling bad. I feel *nothing*, and I hate the nothing more than I could ever have hated the grief."

The snow around us went very still. Even the sulky upward drift paused, flakes hanging in the air like held breath.

The tollkeeper wrote. The scratch of her quill sounded like something being cut.

"Paid in full," she said softly, and for a moment her voice was kind, ancient-kind, grandmother-kind. "That's a hard one, princeling. You'll feel lighter and worse, both. That's normal." She turned the coin-eyes on me. "Yours, taster."

There is a technique to giving up information, and I know it better than anyone alive. I have been interrogated by Guild auditors, crown inquisitors, and once, memorably, a dowager countess armed only with a raised eyebrow. You give them something adjacent. Something true but cheap. You spend the counterfeit coin and let them believe it's gold.

"One truth each," the tollkeeper said again, patiently, and the hanging snow began, very slowly, to fall upward again, and the memory-wall behind her shimmered, and somewhere under my ribs a clock I had been ignoring for eleven years ticked one degree louder.

Because here is the thing about a toll that must cost: I had already calculated, days ago, what I would pay if forced. It's what I do. I calculate. I keep accounts. And the account I have kept longest, most faithfully, most privately, is the one no ledger holds.

"Aldric," I said, "you may want to walk ahead."

"Why would I—"

"*Ahead.*"

He took exactly two steps, which put him approximately eight feet away, facing deliberately toward a nonexistent view, and I realized with distant annoyance that he had done it instantly, without argument, which meant he already knew there was something to not-hear, which meant the conversation ahead of us was going to be insufferable.

I turned to the tollkeeper.

"My name is Beatrix Eriksen," I said. "I am the royal food-taster, and I am very good at my job, and the reason I am very good at my job is that I began practicing on myself eleven years ago." The words came out in the ledger-voice, flat and itemized, because that was the only voice I had for this. "Not immunity-training. Or — not *only*. Every taster builds tolerance; it's the trade. Small doses, escalating, documented. That's the craft." My hands were steady. I noted that with detached approval. "But I never stopped escalating. Past safety. Past sense. Eleven years, compounds no taster needs, doses no tolerance requires. I built a body that can survive almost anything, and I built it on purpose, because—" 

Here is where I had planned to stop. Here is where any sensible witness stops, gives the fact and withholds the meaning. But the tollkeeper was watching me with those coin-eyes, and the quill was poised, and the gate did not shimmer, and I understood that the ledger-woman in me had miscalculated after all: the truth I'd budgeted was not the truth being asked for.

"Because when I started, I wanted a way out that wouldn't be anyone's fault," I said. "A slow door. If I ever took enough by accident, if the king's enemies ever got cleverer than me, if the weight of it all simply — compounded past what interest I could pay — then it would look like duty. Like the taster's death, honorable and expected. Nobody would ever have to know I'd been walking toward it on purpose, one measured dose at a time, for eleven years." I swallowed. My mouth tasted of iron, which was appropriate. "I keep poison in my own body the way other people keep savings. Against a future I assumed I'd never want. And I still take it. Every night. Even now. Even on this road. Even—" my voice did something I did not authorize — "even since the mountain, since him, since I started wanting, for the first time in eleven years, to see what's on the other side of the winter. I still take it, because stopping means admitting what I was doing, and I would rather die of habit than live of hope. There. Is that *expensive enough*?"

The silence afterward had texture. The tollkeeper wrote for a long time, the quill scratching like frost forming on glass.

"Paid," she said quietly, "in full," and then she did something tollkeepers presumably do not do: she reached up — she barely reached my shoulder — and pressed her shawled hand flat against my sternum, right over the ticking clock. "Child," she said, "the mountain will taste that in you the moment you cross. The under-court loves a body full of poison. It reads like an *invitation*. Whatever bargain you're walking toward — settle this account first. Hear me?"

"I hear you," I said, which was not the same as agreeing, and she knew it, and let it go, because spirits are wise about which debts to press.

She stepped aside. The memory-wall parted like breath on cold glass. Behind it, the pass climbed toward a notch of sky that glowed faintly, wrongly, the color of a candle seen through a closed fist — the mountain's mouth, still two days off, already lit.

"Go on, then," the tollkeeper said, flapping her shawls. "Solstice won't wait and neither will the interest."

We walked through. The wall passed over us like a page turning, and I felt it read me — felt a brief, impersonal attention move down my spine like a finger down a column of figures.

Then we were through, and the gate was behind us, and Aldric was beside me, and he had not gone eight feet. He had gone perhaps three. Close enough.

We walked in silence for a while. The upward snow resumed. Somewhere behind us, faintly, the tollkeeper began humming, off-key and content.

"You take it every night," Aldric said at last, not looking at me.

"That was the content of the statement, yes."

"How much is left? Before the slow door opens on its own."

I considered lying. Then I remembered where we were going, and what the tollkeeper had said, and how the mountain reads bodies like books. "*Years*," I said. "If I stop now. Fewer if I don't. The last compound accumulates in the liver; it's patient. Fitting company for me."

"Stop tonight."

"It isn't that simple. The regimen has its own—"

"Beatrix." He stopped walking. He had that look, the one from the feast, the one from the cellar where we collided over the same impossible page — wary recognition, prisoner meeting prisoner, except that on this road it had warmed into something with no wariness left in it at all, and that terrified me considerably more than the gate had. "Eleven years is long enough to pay for a crime you didn't commit. Whichever crime it was — being born into the wrong ledger, being useful to the crown, whatever taught you that dying tidily was the kindest thing you could do for people." His jaw tightened. "I bought my way out of grief. You're drinking your way toward death. We are *not* the same, and I'll thank you to stop treating us as if matching wounds makes us equal. Yours can be treated."

"And yours?"

"Mine," he said, "is already paid. You heard me tell the gate." He started walking again, and then, without turning, in a lighter voice that fooled neither of us: "Besides. If we survive the under-court, someone has to remember you. I've decided it's going to be me, and I refuse to do it from across a sickbed. So." He shrugged elaborately. "Stop taking the poison. For purely selfish reasons. Mine."

I did not answer. But that night, in the shelter of an overturned waystone while he slept — badly, as princes do — I sat with the little lacquered case in my palm, eleven years of nightly ritual arranged in graduated vials, and performed the single most difficult act of accounting of my life.

I did not throw them away. A keeper wastes nothing.

I simply closed the case, and put it at the bottom of my pack, and told the ledger in my head to enter a new line:

*Debt to self: deferred. Terms: negotiable.*

And far up the pass, where the sky glowed like a fist around a candle, the mountain's mouth opened a little wider, as if it had smelled the change in me —

and did not like it at all.
