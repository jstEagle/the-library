## 10. Descent at the Year's Turning

*Beatrix*

The mountain had been watching them for two days.

Beatrix felt it the way she felt poison in a cup — not as a taste exactly, but as a pressure behind the tongue, a wrongness that knew it was known. The road had narrowed to a shelf of black stone above a valley of unbroken snow, and the snow was falling upward now, gently, patiently, flakes rising from the drifts like moths returning to a lamp no one could see.

"Stop looking at it," she said.

Aldric looked at it anyway. He'd been looking at it for an hour, the way he did everything — openly, stubbornly, like a man who had never learned that some things were safer unexamined. "It's falling *up*, Beatrix."

"I noticed."

"You notice everything. It's your whole personality." He said it without malice; there was even something fond in it. "I'm just saying, of all the things you've catalogued in my presence — seventeen kinds of arsenic, the exact number of times Lord Vessin blinks per minute — this is the first one you've pretended not to see."

She pulled her cloak tighter and kept walking. "Because seeing it won't help."

"And knowing it will?"

"Knowing is always better than not knowing," she said. "Even when it's worse."

He was quiet for a few steps after that, which for Aldric constituted a philosophical crisis. Then: "That's very Guild of you."

"That's very taster of me. The Guild only ever taught me to write things down. My mother's milk taught me that ignorance is a flavor, and it's the worst one."

Ahead of them, through the thinning pines, the mountain's mouth came into view — a cleft in the rock like a wound that had never been stitched shut, breathing out air that was warm where every other breath on this road had been cold. Warm air from under a mountain. Beatrix had tasted enough of the world by now to know that nothing warm came out of the dark unless something down there wanted company.

She stopped. So did he.

"The solstice is tomorrow night," she said.

"I know."

"When the sun goes down tomorrow, it stays down longer than any other night of the year. And according to a certain page I've been carrying against my ribs for three hundred miles—" she tapped the oilskin packet inside her coat, where Oren's century-old ledger page rode like a second heartbeat "—that's when the last clause wakes up."

Aldric recited it from memory. She hated that he could; she hated more that she'd made him learn it. "*Only the taster may enter, and only the prince may call her back.*"

"Yes."

"So we stand here until sundown tomorrow, and then—"

"Then we find out which of us the prophecy was lying about."

They made camp in the lee of the rocks, because there was nothing else to do, and because Beatrix had long ago learned that terror was best endured with hot tea in hand. She built the fire small — the upward-falling snow didn't seem to mind smoke, but she wasn't taking chances — and dug out the last of the travel bread and the honey she'd been hoarding since the inn with no keeper.

Aldric took his share and ate it like a man who still found food interesting, which she envied. Three days from the capital, hunted by his own father's men, walking toward a court that ate souls, and he still chewed thoughtfully and said, "Is that juniper in this?"

"It's pine. We're out of juniper. You ate it all."

"I did not eat it all, I *rationed* it all, which is different and nobler."

"It's the same thing with extra steps."

He smiled around the bread, and Beatrix looked away first, which was becoming a habit she'd need to audit later. There was a ledger entry forming somewhere in her chest that she refused to write down. Record everything, feel nothing. She was failing at half of it, and the wrong half.

"Aldric," she said, before she could stop herself. "If the clause means what it says — if only I can go in — what will you do?"

He didn't answer right away. He fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"I'll wait," he said finally. "At the mouth. And I'll call you back."

"You don't know that calling someone back works. No one's ever tested it. The first taster wrote the clause and then went in alone, and we both know how that ended."

"She failed," Aldric said. "But she also wrote it down. Every step, every dish, every rule of the under-court. Whoever finds her notes inherits her mistakes instead of making new ones." He glanced at her sideways. "You told me once that knowing is always better than not knowing. Even when it's worse."

"I say a lot of things. Half of them are coping mechanisms."

"All of them are coping mechanisms," he said gently. "It's why I trust them."

* * *

*Aldric*

Aldric could not sleep. This was not remarkable — he hadn't slept properly in years, not since the crown took his mother's death out of his head and left him a hole shaped like her — but tonight the sleeplessness had a different texture. Tonight it felt less like insomnia and more like standing in a queue.

Tomorrow, or the day after, Beatrix would walk into the mountain, and he would remain outside, and the entire architecture of the prophecy rested on whether he could do the one thing required of him: call her back.

He turned the word over. *Call.* Not fetch, not rescue, not drag. Call. As if she were a name being spoken into a dark room, and the room would decide whether to answer.

"Beatrix," he said quietly, testing it.

Across the fire, her eyes opened instantly. Of course they did. She slept the way she did everything else — lightly, armed, ready to be betrayed by the world at a moment's notice.

"What."

"Nothing. Go back to sleep."

"You said my name at midnight while staring into a fire. That's either a confession, a curse, or a rehearsal. Which is it?"

"...Rehearsal."

She sat up. The firelight did something unfair to her face — softened it, made her look like a person rather than a function. "Of what?"

"The clause. Only the prince may call her back. I was practicing."

Something moved across her expression too fast for him to catalogue. He wished, briefly and uselessly, that he had her gift — that he could read people the way she read cups, naming each ingredient of a feeling before it reached the heart.

"You think it's going to be that simple," she said. "Say my name and I come walking out."

"I think nothing about this is going to be simple. I think the under-court has spent a hundred years arranging this, and I think we're walking into a trap that was set before either of us was born." He paused. "And I think I'm going to say your name anyway, as many times as it takes, because it's the only part of the prophecy I get to choose."

Beatrix stared at him for a long moment. Then she lay back down and pulled her cloak over her shoulder, and he thought the conversation was over, until her voice came out muffled and low:

"If it doesn't work — if I don't come back — don't let them put me in a ledger. Don't let them write me down as a debt paid. I'd rather be nothing than be balanced."

"I'll burn the page myself," he said.

"No, you won't. You'll read it aloud in front of the whole court and commit treason over it." A pause. "But thank you for offering."

He laughed despite himself, and heard her breath even out, and did not sleep, and did not mind.

* * *

*Beatrix*

Sundown on the solstice came early and slow, the way debts do.

They stood at the mouth of the mountain as the light died — a cleft twice the height of a man, its edges worn smooth as if by centuries of hands gripping them on the way in. The warm air breathed out steadily, carrying a smell Beatrix catalogued despite herself: iron, candlewax, old honey, and underneath it something faintly sweet and rotten, like fruit kept past its season in a cellar.

Regret, she thought. It smells like regret brewed slowly.

The ledger page in her coat had grown warm. Not metaphorically — actually, physically warm, the ink beginning to shimmer faintly gold as the last light left the sky. She drew it out and watched the final clause darken, sharpen, assert itself like a signature pressed deeper into the paper.

"Only the taster may enter," she read aloud. Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "And only the prince may call her back."

"Then it's time," Aldric said.

"Not yet." She held up a hand, and he — miracle of miracles — waited.

She took stock, the way she did before every tasting. One knife. One flask of water. One hardtack biscuit she'd been saving for a day that clearly qualified. The century-old page. And herself: a body pickled in twenty years of poisons, immune to nearly everything the world could brew, made for this journey by hands she was only beginning to suspect.

"Well?" Aldric said. "What does the taster's tongue say?"

She almost laughed. Almost. "It says the air tastes like an invitation. And I have never once accepted an invitation without reading the fine print."

"There isn't time to read the fine print."

"There's always time. That's the difference between me and everyone who's ever died of anything." She folded the page carefully and handed it to him. His eyebrows went up.

"You're giving me the receipt?"

"I'm giving you the proof. If I don't come back, you take it to the throne room and you read every line of it out loud — the famine, the sale, Ylva's name, my name, all of it. Promise me."

"I promise." He took the page, and then, because he was Aldric and restraint had never once survived contact with him, he added: "But you are coming back. That's not sentiment, that's logistics. I'm the only one allowed to call you, and I am extremely reliable."

"You fell off a horse in chapter four of our acquaintance."

"The horse was treacherous."

"The horse was standing still."

Their eyes met, and for a moment neither of them performed anything — no wit, no armor, no ledger-keeping. Just two prisoners standing at the door of the prison that had been built for them, choosing to walk in anyway, together, in the only way the prophecy permitted: apart.

Beatrix stepped toward the cleft. The warm air wrapped around her like a hand.

"Beatrix," Aldric said.

She stopped. Turned. "We're doing it *now*? I haven't gone in yet."

"Practice," he said. "I want the first one to count."

And because she was who she was — a woman who recorded everything, even the things that undid her — she stood at the threshold of the court beneath the mountain, on the longest night of the year, and let him say her name into the dark like a debt he intended to collect.

Then she turned, and walked down into the warm, and the mountain closed its mouth behind her like a ledger snapping shut.

The last thing she heard was not his voice at all, but a sound from far below — silver on porcelain, a hundred settings laid, and a voice like cold honey saying:

"*The taster has arrived. We've been keeping her seat warm for a hundred years.*"
