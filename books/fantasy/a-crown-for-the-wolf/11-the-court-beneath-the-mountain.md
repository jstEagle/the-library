## 11. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The first thing I noticed about the court beneath the mountain was that it had excellent lighting.

I want to be honest about this, because everything else about the place was designed to make you forget your own name, and yet someone down there — or something — had decided that what the underworld of a frozen kingdom really needed was chandeliers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. They hung from a ceiling so high it might have been sky, and every one of them burned with a cold white flame that gave no heat and cast no shadow.

"No shadows," Aldric said beside me, in the hushed voice he used when he thought he was being profound. "Why are there no shadows?"

"Because," I said, "shadows would tell us where things actually are."

He considered this. "That's worse than anything else I've imagined since we started walking."

"Good. You're learning."

We had descended for three days through passages that the mountain seemed to rearrange behind us, and I will not pretend I hadn't been afraid. But fear, like poison, is something you can catalogue. Fear of dying: familiar, an old friend, we'd met at least four times on the road. Fear of being wrong about Ylva: newer, sharper, sat higher in the chest. Fear of mirrors: that one I'd been carrying since the tollgate, where I'd paid a truth I never meant to say aloud, and where Aldric had heard every word of it and then — this was the part I kept turning over like a coin I suspected was counterfeit — said nothing cruel, nothing kind, only *noted it*, the way I noted arsenic in wine. Filed away. Not forgotten.

The passage opened, and there it was.

A palace made entirely of mirror.

Not mirrored *walls* — I want to be clear, because the distinction matters and the distinction is the trap. The walls were not stone faced with glass. They were reflections all the way through, as if someone had taken the idea of a wall and polished it until only the shine remained. Our footsteps rang on floors that showed us our own faces looking back up, startled, from below. Pillars reflected pillars reflecting pillars, going back and back until the palace seemed to contain infinite palaces, infinite versions of the two of us walking through them, and I caught myself counting the copies before I remembered my rule.

Record everything. Feel nothing.

There were perhaps forty thousand entries in the Guild's hard ledger, and I had read every one of them twice. I could recite the debt schedule of a kingdom's worth of impossible bargains from memory. So when I tell you that the under-court felt like a ledger — that it had the same quality of *everything accounted for, nothing alive* — you should understand that I am speaking as an expert.

Courtiers stood along the walls. Or rather, shapes stood along the walls, wearing courtiers the way a hand wears a glove. They were beautiful in the way that arithmetic is beautiful: correct, symmetrical, and utterly without mercy. As we passed, each one turned its head in perfect unison, and each reflection showed me a slightly different version of myself — older, younger, dead, crowned, drowned — and I fixed my eyes on the middle distance and did the accounts of my own breathing to stay calm.

Inhale, four counts. Hold, seven. Exhale, eight. A taster's trick. You learn to slow your heart after the third course, because panic makes you swallow, and swallowing is how they get you.

"They're staring," Aldric murmured.

"They're *accounting*," I said. "Staring has warmth in it somewhere. This is inventory."

At the far end of the great hall rose a dais — black mirror, naturally — and on it a throne, and in the throne a figure I will describe as accurately as I can, because accuracy is the only weapon I've ever owned.

The under-king wore a crown of frost ferns, the kind that grow on window glass, delicate and branching and doomed to melt at the first touch of breath. His face was the face of a man who had been handsome so long ago that even he had forgotten why anyone had cared. He did not move. He did not need to. When he spoke, his voice came from everywhere at once, including, I noticed with professional interest, from inside my own skull, which is a trick I would very much have liked to add to the Guild's repertoire.

"The taster," he said. "And the prince who cannot sleep. The mountain keeps its appointments."

"We've come for Ylva," Aldric said.

I could have kissed him for skipping the diplomacy. Princes are trained to open negotiations with three pleasantries and a compliment about the host's architecture, and every one of those words would have been a hook here. Aldric just planted his boots on the mirror floor and named the thing we wanted, which was either courage or exhaustion, and by now I knew him well enough to know it was both.

The under-king's gaze moved to me. It had weight. Actual weight — I felt it settle on my shoulders like a second cloak.

"And the Guild sends its little bookkeeper," he said. "Do you know what you are standing in, Beatrix Eriksen?"

"A receipt," I said.

Something flickered across the frost-fern crown. Interest, maybe. Or the memory of interest, preserved like a fly in amber.

"Go on."

"You bought a soul a hundred years ago. The crown paid; your predecessor collected. But a purchase isn't finished until it's recorded, and whoever holds the record holds the truth of the transaction." I lifted my chin toward the mirrored walls. "This whole palace is a filing system. You didn't build a court under the mountain. You built a vault, and you put a girl in it, and you called the vault a kingdom so no one would come looking for the lock."

The hall went very quiet. Even the courtiers stopped their synchronized breathing, if they had been breathing, which I genuinely could not confirm.

Then the under-king laughed. It was not a warm sound. It was the sound ice makes when a lake decides, all at once, to let a body go.

"Oh, Oren trained you well," he said. "Or badly. We'll see which."

* * *

They took us to her through corridors that corrected themselves as we walked, so that no matter how many turns we made, we were always going exactly where the under-court wished. I counted the turns anyway. Forty-one lefts, thirty-eight rights, one flight of stairs that went down eleven steps and up fourteen, which should be impossible, and I recorded the impossibility in the ledger of my own head with a small note: *stairs cheat. Verify all vertical distances.*

Ylva was in a room with no doors.

It was round and small and lit by one candle — a real candle, wax and wick and honest little flame, the first warm light I had seen since we entered the mountain, and I understood immediately that the candle was the point. That it was allowed. That it was, in fact, the whole mechanism.

She stood in the center of it, a girl of perhaps nineteen, dressed in clothes a century out of fashion, and she was burning.

Not screaming. Not consumed. Burning the way a wick burns — steadily, quietly, from somewhere inside, and the cold white light of every chandelier in the palace above came from her, I understood that now, all of it, the whole endless winter's worth of illumination drawn up out of one girl in one round room who had been on fire for a hundred years.

She looked at me and she smiled, and it was the smile of someone profoundly, catastrophically tired.

"You're the taster," she said. Her voice was hoarse, the way voices get when they haven't been used, though I suspected hers had been used plenty — just never for conversation. "They told me someone would come. Eventually. They've been telling me that for about eighty years, so forgive me if I don't throw a feast."

"I'm sorry," I said, because there was nothing clever to say, and I have found that when there is nothing clever to say, saying the true stupid thing is usually the best available option.

"Don't be. Sorry doesn't burn." She tilted her head at Aldric. "And you're the prince. You look like your grandfather. He visited too, you know. Before he was king. Came down here to negotiate the renewal and couldn't meet my eye the whole time. Sat over there—" she pointed at a stretch of blank mirror "—and talked to the floor about interest rates while I stood here being the interest."

Aldric's face did something complicated. I watched him decide not to apologize on behalf of a dead man, and then decide to do it anyway.

"He was wrong," Aldric said. "All of them. Every king who signed."

"Yes," Ylva said simply. "Now. Which of you is going to ask me the question?"

"What question?" I asked.

"The one you climbed all this way not to ask." She held up her hands, palms out, and the firelight moved under her skin like fish under ice. "You've read the ledger page. You know the terms. Only the taster may enter, and only the prince may call her back. So ask me what happens when the taster enters."

I looked at Aldric. He looked at me. And I realized, with the particular horror of a woman who has spent her whole life reading other people's poisons, that neither of us had asked because some part of us already knew, and knowing would make it real, and real things require decisions.

"The trade," I said instead, because I am a coward in exactly one direction and it is always toward paperwork. "Your under-king offered me a trade. Tell me what it is, so I can refuse it properly informed."

Ylva laughed — a short, cracked, genuine laugh, and I liked her enormously right then, the way you can only like someone who has suffered long enough to develop a sense of humor about it.

"He'll offer it himself. He wants to watch your face." She sobered. "But I'll tell you this first, taster, free of charge, because nobody down here ever gives anything free and I miss the taste of it. Whatever he offers you, it will be fair. That's the worst part. He never cheats. He just makes sure that whatever you choose, you chose it."

* * *

The under-king received us again in a smaller chamber, a dining room, because of course it was. A table of black mirror set for three, though only two chairs were occupied when we arrived — his, and mine, placed opposite, and Aldric's conspicuously absent, which I noted and resented and filed.

Between us, on the table, sat a stack of books.

I knew them before I touched them. I would have known them blindfolded, by smell alone — vellum and iron-gall ink and the faint ghost of the beeswax Oren used on the covers. The soft ledgers. The ones Oren burned in the cellar while the crown's men hammered at the doors.

Except these were not ash. These were whole. Every volume, every year, every debt the Guild of Impossible Things had ever recorded, stacked on a table beneath a mountain, in the court of the thing the debts were owed *to*.

"How," I said.

"You burn paper," the under-king said, "in a kingdom where everything is a copy of something else, and the copies go where copies go." He gestured, and the topmost ledger opened itself to a page I knew intimately: entry one, the founding debt, the famine year, the sale of a soul. "Oren Vane burned the *records*. He could not burn the *debts*. Debts are not paper, taster. Debts are weight. And all this weight belongs to me."

"You're asking me to give you the Guild," I said.

"I'm offering you a trade, and I'll state it plainly, because unlike your kings I honor plain speech." He folded his hands. "The girl walks free. Tonight. The winter ends — not gently, winters don't, but it ends, and your capital thaws, and no more of your countrymen freeze in their beds to feed my chandeliers. In exchange, you bring me the hard ledger. The true one. Every debt the Guild holds, every bargain, every name, every impossible thing ever sold or bought or promised. The Guild's whole power is in that book. Give me the book, and the Guild is finished — and so, incidentally, is every poor soul who ever ran to it hoping for a miracle, because their debts come due to *me*, and I collect differently than Oren does."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above us, ten thousand chandeliers burned on Ylva's hundred years of stolen life.

"And if I refuse?" I asked.

"Then the girl burns another century, the winter falls until your kingdom is a story told in warmer places, and you climb back up carrying nothing but the knowledge that you had one honest choice and you spent it on an institution." He leaned forward, and the frost ferns of his crown glittered. "Understand me, Beatrix Eriksen. I am not tempting you. Temptation is for saints. I am offering *justice*. The Guild has traded in souls and sorrows for three hundred years while the crown looked elsewhere and the poor signed what they were told to sign. Burn it down. Hand me the matches. Free the girl. Everyone wins except the people who deserve to lose."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I eventually get: it *was* justice. That was the poison of it, and I recognized good poison when it was poured for me — smooth, sweet, no bitterness on the finish, every ingredient exactly what it claimed to be. The Guild had ruined lives. I had balanced the ledgers of ruined lives with my own hand, recording everything, feeling nothing, telling myself the record was a kindness. Oren had handed me a century-old page and called it a receipt and sent me down the mountain like a coin being spent.

Give the under-king the ledger, and every creditor in the kingdom loses their leverage overnight. Every debt of sorrow the Guild ever brokered becomes uncollectible, because the collector is a monster, and even the desperate would rather owe a man than a mountain.

Everyone wins except the people who deserve to lose.

"That's a very good offer," I said slowly.

"It is," he agreed pleasantly. "That's what makes it a trap. If it were a bad offer, you'd have refused already."

Across the table, in the mirror surface between the candlesticks, I saw Aldric's reflection — standing outside the room, because the room had no door for him, watching me with an expression I had learned to read on the road: not fear for himself, fear for *me*, which was infinitely more annoying and considerably harder to survive.

I thought about my rule. Record everything, feel nothing.

I thought about how rules are also cages, and how I had built mine deliberately, brick by brick, after the first time tasting killed someone I failed to save, and how a cage you build yourself is still a cage.

I thought about Ylva, standing in a round room with no doors, burning for a hundred years so that a kingdom could pretend its crime had been paid.

And I reached for the topmost ledger — and stopped, because my fingers were shaking, and a taster's hands do not shake, and if they are shaking then something in the air or the wine or the *words* is working on me, and I needed to know what, before I agreed to anything.

"One condition," I said.

The under-king's smile widened by a degree I measured carefully. "Name it."

"I want to hear it from her. Not you. Whatever this trade costs, whatever it frees, I want Ylva to stand in this room and tell me, in her own voice, whether she wants me to take it." I pulled my hand back from the ledger. "Because everyone in this mountain keeps deciding things *for* her. A hundred years of kings and guildmasters and under-kings signing papers about her soul. I'm a bookkeeper. I don't move money without the signature of the person it belongs to."

For the first time since we had entered his court, the under-king went still in a way that suggested I had done something unplanned.

And behind me, from the doorway that had not existed a moment ago, came the sound of bare feet on mirror, and a hoarse voice that had not spoken in a hundred years of anyone asking:

"Well," said Ylva. "Finally. Someone who checks the signatures."

She walked to the table, trailing her small honest flame, and the under-king watched her come with an expression I could not read — and I have never in my life been more frightened by a sentence I couldn't parse than I was by the next words out of her mouth.

"There's something you should know about the ledger before you sign anything, taster," Ylva said. "Because I was there when it was written. And the hand that wrote it wasn't the Guildmaster's."
