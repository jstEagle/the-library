## 12. Ledger of the Under-King

They took Beatrix's hands first.

Aldric had expected chains. He had braced himself for iron and screaming and some dramatic tearing of her from his side — but the under-court did not work that way. Two figures of smoked glass simply stepped forward, bowed with terrible courtesy, and said, "The collateral will be kept comfortable," and then they were holding her wrists, and she was looking at him over their shoulders with an expression he would spend the rest of his life trying to describe.

It was not fear. It was arithmetic.

"Don't sign anything," she said.

"I never sign anything."

"You signed a Guild receipt last month."

"That was a forgery of my signature. Someone else signed it badly." He was aware his voice had gone up half an octave. "I'll get you back."

"Obviously," she said, as if he'd promised to return a borrowed book. "Try not to be interesting while I'm gone. Interesting is expensive down here."

And then the glass figures walked her backward through a doorway that hadn't been there a moment before, and the doorway folded shut like a page turning, and Aldric stood alone in the antechamber of the court beneath the mountain with nothing but his title, his terror, and the hard ledger strapped to his chest like a breastplate that had never once stopped an arrow.

* * *

The throne room — if it could be called a room, being less a space than a suggestion of one, all mirrors and candlelight that came from no candles — smelled of cold stone and old honey. The court assembled itself around him as he walked: courtiers of smoke and salt, ladies whose faces were polished obsidian, a chamberlain who appeared to be made entirely of ledger-paper, its skin covered in columns of faded ink.

Aldric counted his steps. It was something his mother had taught him, before — count your steps when you're frightened, darling, and by forty you'll have forgotten why. He got to thirty-eight before the under-king noticed him, which he chose to consider a personal victory.

The under-king sat upon a throne of black ice that was not ice, because it did not melt and did not chill and reflected Aldric's face back at him slightly older than he actually was. In every mirror, in fact, he looked older. In one he looked dead. He decided not to look at that one again.

"Prince Aldric," the under-king said. His voice was the sound a fire makes when it goes out. "The crown sends its heir at last. We had begun to think the debt embarrassed you."

"It does," Aldric said. "Embarrassment is why I've come to settle it."

Something rippled through the court — amusement, or hunger, or both. The paper chamberlain's skin rustled.

"Settle," the under-king repeated, tasting the word. "How very merchantly. Your ancestors preferred other words. Deny. Delay. Forget." He leaned forward on his throne of not-ice. "But you have brought the ledger. The true one. So perhaps this generation of Dunmores has finally learned to read."

Aldric unstrapped the book and held it out. A servant of grey mist drifted forward to take it, and he found his arms wouldn't open — not from enchantment, he realized, but from his own stubbornness, which was somehow worse.

"I want to see the terms first," he said. "All of them. Not the summary the crown keeps. The original contract."

"The original contract." The under-king smiled, and his teeth were small and white and numerous, like rice. "Do you know, in a hundred years, no one from above has ever asked to read it? They ask to end it. To escape it. To pretend it was never written. You are the first to ask for the document itself." He gestured, languid, to the paper chamberlain. "Fetch the founding instrument. And fetch the renewals. If the boy wants honesty, let him drown in it."

* * *

They brought the documents on a tray of slate, and Aldric read standing up, because he trusted his legs more than any chair in that place.

The founding contract was written on vellum gone soft as cloth, in ink that had shifted color over the century — brown to red to something that might have been rust or might have been blood, and he refused to speculate further. The terms were laid out in a clerk's beautiful hand, precise and terrible:

*One soul, freely named, in exchange for the ending of the famine. The soul to serve as wick for the winter owed the mountain. Interest to accrue seasonally. The debt to be discharged only by payment in kind: a taster's consent, freely given, before the year's turning.*

He read it three times. The third time, he noticed what he'd been refusing to notice.

*A taster's consent.*

Not a death. Not a sacrifice. Consent. The prophecy — the one that had haunted him since Oren pressed that century-old page into Beatrix's hands, the one that named her death date like an appointment — the prophecy had never been about dying at all. It was about agreeing.

*She walks willingly into the wolf's mouth,* the old entry read, *and the crown's debt is paid.*

Someone had translated *consent* into *death* somewhere along the hundred years, and everyone — the Guild, the crown, Beatrix herself — had swallowed the mistranslation whole.

"There's an error," he said aloud.

"There is never an error," said the under-king, mildly. "There are only interpretations."

"The contract says consent. The prophecy says death."

"And which do you suppose came first?" The under-king rose from his throne, and Aldric discovered the king was much taller than the throne had suggested, unfolding upward like something that had been waiting a long time to stand. "Come. Walk with me, prince. If you intend to bargain, you should know what you're bargaining with. Most men bargain blind and call it wisdom."

* * *

They walked through galleries of mirrors, and in each mirror Aldric saw a different piece of the past, which he understood was the under-court's idea of hospitality.

In the first mirror: a famine. Fields of grey wheat lying flat as beaten dogs. His great-great-grandfather's kingdom, a hundred years ago, starving in a way the histories had softened into a single dignified paragraph.

In the second: the negotiation. A delegation of the crown descending this same road, carrying the same kind of slate tray. And among them —

Aldric stopped walking.

Among them stood a woman in a taster's plain grey dress, and though the mirror was old and the image swam like a fish beneath ice, he knew the set of her shoulders. He had watched those shoulders carry poison and ledgers and insults for months without once bending.

"This isn't Beatrix," he said. "This is a hundred years old."

"No," agreed the under-king. "It is Maren Eriksen. First of her line to hold the tongue. First keeper of the hard ledger." He paused before the mirror with something almost like tenderness. "And the author of your prophecy."

The floor did not drop away. Aldric rather wished it would have; it would have been simpler than what actually happened, which was that he simply stood there while a hundred years rearranged themselves around him.

"Maren wrote the entry," he said slowly. "Dated tonight. Her descendant's name. She wrote Beatrix's death date a century before Beatrix was born."

"She wrote what she saw," the under-king said. "We showed her the terms. We showed her the mechanism. A taster's consent, freely given, and the debt dissolves. And Maren Eriksen — clever woman, honest woman, the only honest person in that entire starving delegation — understood immediately what the crown intended. That they would not ask anyone's consent. That they would dress coercion up as fate and call it prophecy, so that when the girl walked to her death, she would believe she was fulfilling destiny rather than obeying a lie."

"So she wrote it down. The truth. As a warning."

"She wrote it down as a *receipt*," the under-king corrected. "Your Guildmaster understands receipts. Maren recorded the debt exactly as it stood, in the exact hand the contract required, and hid it where only a keeper would find it — because she believed, poor optimist, that a keeper would come. Someone who read everything. Someone who felt nothing, until the day feeling became necessary." He glanced sideways at Aldric, and in the mirror beside them, Aldric saw himself look away first. "She believed someone would come and *read carefully*. Instead, a century passed, and everyone read carelessly, and the careful reader nearly died of a mistranslation."

"But she failed," Aldric said. "Maren. The first taster. She didn't stop it. Ylva is still here. The winter still falls."

The under-king was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its performance, and what remained underneath was older and wearier and far more frightening.

"Come and see the last mirror," he said.

* * *

The last gallery held a single mirror, tall as a door, and its surface was not silvered but smoked, like glass breathed on by winter. In it, Aldric saw the famine's end: the delegation ascending back toward the sun, lighter by one soul. And at the rear of the column, apart from the others, Maren Eriksen walked with her head down and her hands fisted in her skirts, and she was weeping, and no one waited for her.

"She argued," the under-king said. "For eleven days she argued. She offered herself — did you know that? A taster's consent, freely given, hers, on the spot. But the contract required a debt of equal weight, and Ylva's soul had already been weighed and entered. A substitution would have required the crown to admit the sale. And the crown—" he spread his hands, "—does not admit. The crown renews."

"Renews," Aldric said. The word landed wrong in his mouth, heavy, and then he understood why, and wished he could take it back off his tongue.

"Every twenty-five years, prince. The famine ended, but the interest accrued, and interest must be serviced. Your great-grandfather renewed it. Your grandfather renewed it. And your father—" the under-king turned fully to face him, and in the smoked mirror behind them both, Aldric saw a smaller version of himself signing a document with a hand that shook, "—renewed it six years ago. The winter before your mother died. He came down this road in secret, with four guards, and he traded another quarter-century of the crown's comfort for the continued service of the girl you call Ylva. He did not ask what she wanted. He did not ask if the debt might instead be *paid*. He asked only how much it would cost to keep things as they were."

"He told me the winter was natural," Aldric heard himself say. "He told me the long winters were weather. He stood in front of the court and blamed the mountain gods and the northern winds and—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, and he kept going anyway, because Beatrix had taught him that too: record everything, feel nothing, until feeling becomes necessary. "Six years ago. I was twelve. That was the winter he started locking his study. That was the winter he stopped looking at me."

"Guilt is a poor companion at dinner," the under-king said, not unkindly. "Kings mostly choose flattery instead."

Aldric laughed — one short, ugly sound that echoed strangely in the mirrored hall, coming back to him from a dozen reflections, all of them looking sicker than he felt. "You're telling me all this very calmly. For a creditor. Aren't creditors supposed to want me ignorant and desperate?"

"Oh, I want you desperate," the under-king said pleasantly. "Desperation improves offers. But ignorance improves nothing. An ignorant debtor bargains with rumors and wastes everyone's evening. Whereas a debtor who knows the full shape of his inheritance—" the smile again, rice-white, patient, "—bargains with something real. So. You have seen the founding contract. You have seen the renewals, including your father's signature, which I will happily provide a copy of, sealed, for use at whatever trial I imagine is coming. Now tell me, prince of the thawing kingdom: what do you offer?"

* * *

What he offered, in the end, was everything he had, and it wasn't enough, and he knew it while he was saying it, and he said it anyway.

Ylva's freedom, unconditional. The crown's public confession, witnessed and sealed. Abolition of the taster's office and every debt structure built upon it. His own coronation oath rewritten to include the under-court's terms — a treaty, not a trickling renewal. In exchange: the winter ends, the debt dissolves, and no soul serves as a wick ever again.

The under-court listened with the patience of people who had heard a hundred years of offers, and when he finished, the silence had a texture to it, like wool.

"It is a fine speech," the under-king said. "Truly. Your mother's blood, I think; your father negotiated like a man reading a menu." He descended from the dais, unhurried, and stopped close enough that Aldric could see his own terrified reflection in the king's obsidian eyes. "But contracts are not speeches. The debt stands at a specific weight, and weight is weight. One soul, served a century. Interest, compounded. It cannot be dissolved by good intentions any more than a famine can be dissolved by prayers. There is exactly one discharge written into the founding terms, and you have read it."

"A taster's consent. Freely given."

"Precisely. Which brings us," and here the under-king's voice went soft, and softness from him was worse than any thunder, "to the matter of your companion. Who is, at this moment, being shown our hospitality. Who is, I will note, asking remarkably few questions for a prisoner, and requesting writing materials, which concerns my staff considerably." He tilted his head. "You love her. That is obvious; everything about you is obvious; mirrors are honest places. So understand what I offer next, and understand that I offer it because Maren Eriksen once wept in my gallery, and I have wondered for a century whether her line reads more carefully than her crown did."

"Say it."

"The debt requires a taster's consent. It does not specify *which* taster. Maren offered herself once, and was refused on grounds of accounting, not cruelty — the weights did not match. But now the weights have shifted. A century of interest has accrued. And the taster currently in our keeping is the last of Maren's line, keeper of the hard ledger, immune to every poison we brew, and worth — how shall I put this — considerably more than Ylva." The under-king stepped back, folding his hands. "Her consent would clear the debt entire. Tonight. No substitutions, no renewals, no further generations of Dunmores walking this road in shame. One word from her, freely given, and the winter ends and the books balance forever."

"And if she doesn't give it?"

"Then we return to the original arrangement, and wait for the prophecy to fulfill itself as the crown always intended — by force dressed as fate. I am patient, prince. Patience is the one luxury of the deep places." He began to turn away, then paused, and added, almost as an afterthought, in the tone of a man mentioning the weather:

"Oh — and you should know. The feast is being prepared. Our guest will be invited to taste it. It is our custom, when we wish to measure a taster's worth. Each dish is brewed from a regret. Each taste takes a memory she has chosen to keep. If she refuses the invitation, she forfeits her standing to negotiate at all." The under-king smiled his rice smile. "I mention this only so that you may decide, before the courses arrive, whether the woman you love is bargaining with us — or whether we are bargaining with her. Do give it some thought. The first course is soup."

He swept away into the mirrors, and the court folded itself after him like a fan closing, and Aldric stood alone in the gallery with his father's forged-by-shame signature burning in his pocket and the words *each dish takes a memory* ringing in his skull like a struck bell.

Somewhere beyond the walls, faintly, he could hear the kitchens of the under-court beginning to boil.

He started running before he'd finished deciding to.
