## 13. A Taste of Ash and Iron

The under-court set its table the way an executioner sets his tools: everything gleaming, nothing accidental.

Beatrix counted eleven chairs at the obsidian banquet table, which she found telling, since there were only three guests — herself, Aldric, and the under-king, who occupied a seat carved from what looked suspiciously like a single tooth. Eleven chairs meant ten empty places, and ten empty places meant the court beneath the mountain liked its feasts crowded even when they weren't. Ghosts, maybe. Or memories. Or debts, sitting patiently in the dark, waiting to be fed.

"You'll forgive us our numbers," said the under-king. His voice was the sound of a cellar door closing somewhere far below and never opening again. "We have always been generous with seating. Hospitality is the first law down here."

"And the second?" Aldric asked. He stood beside her, close enough that his sleeve brushed hers. He had refused a chair on principle, which was either princely courage or princely stubbornness; after nine days on the mountain road with him, Beatrix had learned these were frequently the same thing.

"Payment," said the under-king, "is the second."

The dishes arrived without servants. They simply appeared, one after another, borne up through the black stone as though the table itself were serving them, each covered with a dome of smoked glass that fogged and cleared like breathing.

"To prove your worth," the under-king said, and his smile was the worst thing in the room, which was saying something, because the room contained a chandelier made entirely of icicles that had never once melted. "You claim to be a taster. Anyone can taste. Few can *swallow*. The court beneath the mountain does not deal in trifles, taster. We deal in what a thing costs."

He gestured, and the first dome lifted.

The dish beneath was simple: a grey broth in a bowl of bone, steam curling off it in slow spirals. It smelled of rain on a schoolroom window. Of chalk dust and cedar pencils and the particular warmth of standing too close to a stove while someone reads aloud.

Beatrix's stomach dropped.

"You know what this is," the under-king said.

"Regret," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "You brew your feasts from regrets."

"Our vintage. Our harvest. Every soul that passes beneath our mountains leaves its sorrows in the soil, and the soil is generous." He slid the bowl toward her with one long finger. "But you have it backward, taster. We do not serve you regret. We serve you the *memory the regret is attached to*. Drink, and remember. And then — " the finger tapped the table, once, gently, like a debt collector knocking — "the memory is ours. That is the price of each course. A taste for a memory. Eat the whole feast, prove you can pay, and we will negotiate honestly about the girl called Ylva."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you are a guest who will not leave, and Prince Aldric remains our collateral, and the winter continues falling, and everyone goes home very slowly and very coldly." The under-king folded his hands. "I should note, for fairness, that the memory does not vanish all at once. You will feel it go. Like a word on the tip of the tongue. And then not even that."

Beatrix looked at the broth. Rain on a schoolroom window. Cedar pencils. She was seven years old, and her mother was teaching her letters at the kitchen table, and every wrong stroke earned a gentle tap of the pencil against her knuckles, and every right one earned a smile, and —

*Record everything, feel nothing,* she told herself. But her hands were already shaking.

"The first course is always childhood," the under-king said, almost kindly. "It is the least expensive. Most people give it up without a fight. The later courses are harder."

Aldric shifted beside her. "There has to be another way to prove — "

"There isn't," Beatrix said quietly. "He's telling the truth. I'd know. Lying has a taste, and he isn't wearing any."

She picked up the spoon.

She thought, absurdly, of the king's supper back at the palace. Of the way she catalogued poisons like old friends: nightshade was a flirt, arsenic a quiet accountant, foxglove a liar who smiled too much. She had spent eleven years putting terrible things in her mouth on purpose. Surely she could manage one more.

The broth tasted exactly like being seven years old, and loved, and safe.

She swallowed.

It left her the way a tide leaves a beach — gradually, then all at once. She felt her mother's kitchen table slide out from under her mind. She felt the pencil taps fade to nothing. She reached for the memory the way you reach for a name you've just been introduced to, and found her hand closing on smooth, blank water.

"Oh," she said softly.

"Yes," said the under-king.

"I can't remember my mother's face." She wasn't crying. That seemed important to note. Her eyes were wet, but she wasn't crying, because crying would be feeling something, and she had a rule. "I know I had one. I know she taught me letters. But it's — it's a report now. Not a memory. Someone told me about her. That's all that's left."

"That is what we sell," the under-king agreed. "And what we buy." He gestured, and the second dome rose. "Shall we continue? You are doing beautifully."

* * *

The second course was a small pie, golden-crusted, smelling of browned butter and thyme and the inside of the Guild's cellar on a winter evening.

She ate it because stopping was worse than continuing. That was the trap of the feast, she realized somewhere around the third bite — the only way out was through, and every course made her less able to remember why she'd wanted out. It was clever. It was monstrous. It was exactly how the Guild's own contracts worked, dressed up in pastry, and some cold, distant part of her admired the craftsmanship.

This one took the face of Guildmaster Oren — no. No, that wasn't right. She checked, carefully, the way she audited a ledger. Oren was still there. Grey beard, ink-stained fingers, the smell of pipe smoke and burnt vellum. Still there.

What was gone was the day she'd come to him. The day she'd knocked on the Guild's hidden door at fourteen years old with a stolen ledger under her arm — no, not stolen, *found* — and he'd looked at her over the ledger's spine and said words she could no longer hear. Something about her handwriting. Something about hiring her. The words were gone; the fact remained, bald and contextless: *Oren hired me.* A line item without an invoice.

"Why did he hire me?" she whispered.

"You'll want to save your questions," the under-king said. "You're running low on answers."

* * *

By the fifth course she had lost the smell of the taster's chamber and the sound of the king's laugh (she still knew he laughed; she had written it down once in her head, *the king laughs like a man paying interest*, and now the sentence floated unanchored, pointing at nothing). By the sixth she had lost the name of her first poison, which frightened her more than anything so far, because that knowledge had lived in her body, in her tongue, not her mind, and apparently the feast did not respect jurisdiction.

The seventh course was soup again. Iron-grey, smelling of snow.

"This one," said the under-king, "we saved for near the end. This one is special."

She looked into the bowl and understood before she tasted it. Because the smell was the cellar. Not the Guild's cellar generally — the specific cellar, the one with the hard ledger chained to its desk, the one where she went every night to balance impossible accounts. The smell of candlewax and cold stone and old ink.

"No," she said.

"It is the finest dish we serve," the under-king said. "The reason you began. The first entry in your private ledger, so to speak. Do you know what you'll find if you reach for it now?"

She reached.

She found the shape of a habit. *I keep ledgers because —* and then nothing, a cliff-edge where a reason used to be. She knew that she had a reason. She could feel the socket where it had sat, the way your tongue finds a missing tooth. Somewhere behind that blankness was the thing itself: the moment, the person, the crime or kindness or grief that had turned a food-taster into a keeper of debts. The load-bearing wall of her whole double life. And the under-king had put it in a bowl and lit a candle under it.

"If I eat this," she said, "I won't know why any of it mattered. The ledgers. The records. Any of it. I'll be keeping books out of — out of muscle memory. A machine."

"Yes," said the under-king, with genuine appreciation. "Most people don't see it so clearly. They think we take *happy* memories. Sentimental ones. We don't, particularly. We take the ones that hold up the roof."

Aldric's hand found hers under the table. His grip was crushing. He had eaten nothing; princes, apparently, were exempt from proving their worth, which was its own familiar injustice. "Beatrix. Don't. Tell him the negotiation is off. Tell him — "

"Quiet," she said, not unkindly. "I'm auditing."

She stared into the soup. Snow-grey. Iron-smelling. In its surface she caught her reflection and was distantly surprised by it — a thin woman, sharp-faced, eyes red-rimmed, looking like someone who had been eating her own life one course at a time.

*Record everything, feel nothing.*

That was the rule. But rules had reasons, and reasons had beginnings, and hers was in this bowl.

Unless.

Her heart was going like a rabbit's, but her hands were steady — eleven years of holding steady while kings dined had trained them well — and she thought, slowly, the way she thought through a poisoned meal: *he wants me to swallow. Swallowing is the payment. The taste is the theft. Every course so far, I drank it down, and every course, it took something.*

But she was a taster. And tasting, properly done, is not swallowing.

"Hypothetically," she said to the under-king, "if a guest merely held the wine in her mouth and spat it into her napkin, what would your court call that?"

For the first time, something moved behind the under-king's eyes. It might have been annoyance. It might have been delight. With him, she suspected, those lived in the same house.

"We would call it," he said, "a cheat."

"You'd call it *good manners*," said Beatrix. "In the capital, we have an entire profession built on it."

And she lifted the spoon — and stopped, because the truth arrived a half-second late, the way truth always did with her, and the truth was this: she didn't want to cheat. Not this one. There were things worth losing. There were prices worth paying, and Ylva — a girl who had burned alive in awareness for a hundred years as the wick of a kingdom's winter — was worth every memory in her skull, including this one. Especially this one.

*Why did I start keeping ledgers?*

She reached into the blank space one more time, hunting the void the way she'd hunted the shape of her mother's kitchen, and this time, at the very bottom of the nothing, her hand closed on something small and hard and certain. Not the memory itself — that was already half-eaten by distance, blurred, more warmth than picture. But the *shape* of the memory. Its weight. Its moral gravity, the way you can know a coin is gold by heft alone.

She couldn't remember why she'd started.

But she could remember that the reason had been *just*. That whatever she had witnessed, whoever she had failed, the ledger had begun as an act of justice — a promise made by a girl to someone who could no longer collect. She didn't know the someone's face. She didn't know the promise's words. But the direction of it, the *true north* of it, she could still feel, like heat through a closed door.

That would have to be enough. That would be her anchor.

"Beatrix," Aldric said again, urgent now. "Whatever you're deciding, decide faster. The whole court is watching."

He was right. The empty chairs were full — she saw it clearly for the first time, the way you see a room clearly at the end of a fever. Shapes sat in every seat. Thin ones. Patient ones. The debts of a century, attending dinner.

She raised the spoon.

"For Ylva," she said — a name she could still say, a fact she could still hold, and she wrapped both hands around it like the last coal of a fire. "For the girl in the walls. Watch closely, Your Majesty. I'm going to show you what a taster is *for*."

She ate the seventh course in one swallow, eyes open, and felt the last of the reason leave her — felt the true north flicker, gutter —

— and held.

Not the memory. Just the certainty. A single entry, unwitnessed, unprovable, written nowhere but in the marrow of her: *it mattered, and it was just, and it was mine.* The feast could strip the page. It could not strip her refusal to let the book be blank.

The under-king regarded her for a long moment. Around the table, the ghost-debts stirred, restless, hungry, unsatisfied.

"Well," he said at last, softly, in the tone of a merchant discovering a customer smarter than advertised. "One course remains, taster. The eighth. We do not offer it to everyone." A dome lifted from a plate she hadn't seen arrive — a single dark fig, glistening, smelling impossibly of sunlight. "This one does not take a memory. This one *gives* one. Yours, I believe. Sold to us ninety-nine years ago, by a man named Oren's predecessor. Paid in full, never delivered. Would you like to know what your Guild bought with it — and whose it was?"

Beatrix looked at the fig. At the sun it remembered.

Behind her, she heard the great doors of the mirrored hall begin, very quietly, to unlock.
