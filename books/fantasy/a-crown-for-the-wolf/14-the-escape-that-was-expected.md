## 14. The Escape That Was Expected

*Beatrix*

The trouble with a perfect plan, Beatrix thought as she pressed her palm against the mirrored wall of the under-court's eastern corridor, is that it never survives contact with anything. And this one hadn't even had the decency to fail.

She had expected guards. The under-court was a palace of mirrors and candleless light, a place where the walls showed you versions of yourself you'd rather not meet — she'd passed three reflections on the way in that were wearing expressions she had never once made in her life, and one that was crying. She'd expected the under-king to post sentries at Ylva's chamber. She'd expected at minimum a locked door.

Instead the door had been open. Unlocked. Standing ajar like an invitation to tea.

"Ylva," she'd whispered into the dark of the girl's chamber, where a hundred years of awareness had been spent as fuel for winter, "we're getting you out."

And Ylva — small, silver-eyed, wrapped in a shroud of frost that melted only near her heart — had looked up at her and said, quite calmly, "I know. I've known for six days."

Six days. They'd been in the mountain six days. Which meant Ylva had known before they'd finished bargaining, before Aldric had traded half his dignity and all his father's secrets for her freedom, before Beatrix had eaten four courses of regret at the under-court's feast and lost, among other things, the memory of her grandmother's voice.

"You could have mentioned it," Beatrix said now, hurrying along the corridor with Ylva's cold hand tucked in hers.

"You didn't ask," said Ylva. There was something in her voice that Beatrix was learning to read after a century of silence — not bitterness exactly, but the dry patience of someone who has watched a great many people make the same mistakes and has stopped being surprised by any of them. "Also, if I had mentioned it, you would have suspected a trap, and then you would have hesitated, and hesitation down here is expensive. The mirrors charge interest."

"That's absurd."

"So is everything else about this palace." Ylva nodded ahead. "Door's there. It'll be open too."

It was. Of course it was.

Beatrix stopped in front of it and did what she always did when something was too easy: she counted. It was the taster's discipline. When the soup arrived smelling perfect and tasting perfect and looking perfect, you counted the ways it could kill you. She counted now. One: no guards. Two: no locks. Three: the corridor behind them, which had been a maze of identical mirrored turns on the way in, now ran straight as a ledger line directly toward the mountain's mouth. Four: the under-king himself, who had watched them over dinner with eyes like coins left in a dead man's pocket, and who had smiled — *smiled* — when Beatrix told him they intended to leave.

Five. Five was the worst one, and it took her until they were actually walking up the long throat of the passage, Ylva's frost trailing behind her like the hem of a gown, before she let herself say it out loud.

"He wanted us to go."

Ylva didn't break stride. "Yes."

"The debt. The prophecy. All of it." Beatrix's breath came out white even here, in the warm dark of the ascent. "He wasn't careless. He was *cooperative*. Oren told me the prophecy was a receipt, not a prediction. Receipts don't predict, Ylva. They record terms. So what are the terms?"

"You already know," Ylva said gently. "You've known since the feast. You just haven't let yourself say it, because saying it makes it real, and you're very good at keeping things unreal by refusing to name them. I've read your ledgers. Well — I've *been* your ledgers, in a manner of speaking. A hundred years gives one time to eavesdrop on the Guild's cellars through the roots of things."

"Ylva."

"The prophecy doesn't need your death, Beatrix." The girl stopped walking. In the dim glow of her own frost, her face looked carved, ancient, and terribly young all at once. "It needs your consent. A taster must return willingly. Those were the terms written a century ago, when my soul was sold. The crown couldn't sell me outright — souls can't be taken, only given. So the contract says: the debt comes due when a taster of the old line walks back down this mountain of her own free will and agrees to stay. Your death date isn't a death date. It's a *due date*."

The words settled over Beatrix like snowfall — soft, silent, and burying.

She thought of the ledger entry in her own handwriting, dated tonight, entered a hundred years ago. She thought of Oren pressing the page into her hands and saying *it's a receipt, girl, receipts get paid.* She thought of every step of this journey, every door that opened, every guard that wasn't there, every bargain that went suspiciously smoothly — and underneath it all, the quiet, patient architecture of a trap built to catch not her body but her *yes*.

"I would never consent," she said. "Obviously. I came here to steal you back, not replace you."

"I know," said Ylva. "That's what makes it elegant. You won't choose to stay. But consent is a wide door, and the under-court is very good at widening doors. You might say yes to save him." She tilted her head toward the darkness above, where somewhere a prince was climbing. "You might say yes to save me, if staying meant I truly went free. You might say yes out of guilt, or duty, or exhaustion, or love, or some clever bargain you convince yourself isn't really consent. The under-king doesn't need you to want it. He just needs you to agree. Once. On any pretext. For any reason."

"And if I never agree?"

"Then the winter falls forever, and he waits. He's very good at waiting. He's had a hundred years of practice, and before that—" Ylva shrugged, frost sliding off her shoulders. "Before that he had longer."

Beatrix leaned against the tunnel wall and laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. "So we stole you successfully. We're escaping successfully. And none of it matters, because the trap was never the cell. The trap is me."

"Now you're beginning to think like a ledger-keeper," said Ylva, and took her hand again, and pulled her upward toward the thin grey line of daylight at the top of the world.

*Aldric*

Aldric had fought in two skirmishes, survived eleven assassination attempts (twelve, if you counted the incident with the honeyed pears, which he absolutely did), and grown up in a court where every kindness came with an invoice attached. He liked to believe this had made him difficult to surprise.

Then he reached the surface, saw the sky, and forgot how to breathe.

The winter solstice had come while they were beneath the mountain, and the long winter had finished falling. The capital valley below was a sea of white — not the pretty white of festival snows but a drowning white, roofs swallowed, roads erased, the whole kingdom muffled under a silence so complete he could hear his own heartbeat stumble. Snow hung in the air without falling, suspended, as though the sky itself had been told to hold its breath and had obeyed.

"We did it," he said stupidly. "We got her out. The winter should be breaking."

"It isn't," said Beatrix behind him.

He turned. She stood at the mouth of the cave with Ylva's hand in hers, and her face was doing the thing he'd learned to dread over these weeks of flight — the thing where all expression drained out of it and left only the ledger-keeper, the woman who recorded everything and felt nothing, standing guard over whatever she refused to feel.

"What happened?" he asked. "Down there. After the feast. What did he say to you?"

"Nothing I didn't work out myself." She stepped past him into the snow, which rose to her knees and did not melt around her feet, though Ylva's presence should have warmed the air for a mile in every direction. Instead the frost simply parted around the girl like water around a stone, respectful, deferential, *recognizing its own*. "Aldric. Before anything else — before the city, before your father, before any of it — I need you to answer a question honestly. Can you do that?"

"I bought an honest answer from the Guild once," he said. "Cost me a fortune. I've been trying to get the rest of them at a discount ever since."

"Aldric."

"Yes. Honestly. Ask."

She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her decide, sentence by sentence, what to tell him — he knew that look, he'd sat across from it at a hundred state dinners while ministers decided how much truth the heir could afford. But then something in her face shifted, and she said, plainly:

"The escape worked because he let it work. The debt requires a taster to return willingly. My death date was never a death date. It's a due date, and the payment is my consent, and he will wait as long as it takes for me to say yes — to anything, for anyone, by any means. That's the trap inside the trap. We haven't stolen Ylva. We've carried the invoice up the mountain ourselves."

The silence afterward was very long. Somewhere in the held-breath sky, a single snowflake finally remembered how to fall and landed on Aldric's nose.

"All right," he said.

Beatrix blinked. "*All right?*"

"Well, what did you expect? Screaming? Throwing myself off the mountainside?" He brushed the flake away. "Beatrix, I have spent my entire life being managed by people who arrange outcomes and let me walk into them believing I chose. My father sold my memory of my mother's death and called it mercy. The court spent years building a case to declare me unfit and called it concern. This is the first time anyone has handed me the shape of the trap *before* I stepped in it, and I find I'm almost grateful." He grimaced. "Almost. Ask me again after we've solved it."

"There may be nothing to solve. If the only exit is my consent—"

"No." He said it flatly, the way he'd learned to say things in council chambers when he meant *this discussion is over*. "There's always another clause. Contracts are written by people, and people make mistakes, and you—" he pointed at her, "—are the best auditor in a guild full of auditors. If there's a loophole in a century-old soul-debt, you'll find it. That's not faith. That's arithmetic."

Something moved behind her eyes — not a smile, exactly, but the place where smiles were kept. "You've gotten disturbingly good at flattering me."

"I've gotten disturbingly good at meaning it. Different skill entirely."

Ylva cleared her throat. Both of them turned. The girl stood ankle-deep in snow that bowed away from her in rings, like courtiers making room, and her silver eyes were fixed on the valley below with an expression Aldric couldn't read — hunger and horror in equal measure.

"There's a third thing you should both know," she said quietly. "Before you go down there. Before you start looking for loopholes in a contract you still don't fully understand." She lifted her chin toward the buried capital, where the royal palace's tallest tower jutted from the white like a broken tooth. "I remember the day I was sold. Everyone assumes a century underground blurs such things. It doesn't. It sharpens them. I have had one hundred years and nothing to do but remember." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands, Aldric noticed, had closed into fists inside her sleeves. "And I remember *who* signed the bill of sale. Not the king. Not the crown's men."

Beatrix went very still. "Oren's predecessor," she said. "You told us on the way up. The Guild sold you."

"The Guild wrote the contract," Ylva corrected. "But that's not the third thing." She looked at Beatrix then, and there was something in her face that made Aldric's stomach drop before she'd said a word — the look of someone holding bad news the way you hold a hot pan, wanting to put it down and knowing you can't. "The third thing is about the signature. I've seen it twice in my life. Once on my own bill of sale, a hundred years ago. And once last week, on the oldest page of the hard ledger, in the entry dated tonight."

The wind moved through the held snow, and nothing fell.

"The hand that sold me," Ylva said, "and the hand that recorded your debt — they match, Beatrix. Down to the flourish on the E. Whatever you believe about who you are, and why you're immune, and why the prophecy chose you—" she paused, and gentled her voice, and somehow that was worse than cruelty, "—the woman who wrote your doom in that ledger a century ago signs her name exactly the way you sign yours."

Above them, the frozen sky creaked like a ship's hull.

And far below, in the drowned white silence of the capital, a bell began to ring — once, twice, three times — the old signal, the one every child in the city learned before they learned their letters, the one that meant the crown was summoning all debts to be paid.

Beatrix looked at the bell-tower. Then at her own right hand, flexing slowly, as if seeing it for the first time.

"Well," she said at last, in the voice of a woman opening a ledger she already knows is going to ruin her, "I suppose we'd better go find out whose name I've been signing."
