## 15. What Ylva Remembers of the Sun

The mountain did not want to let them go.

Ylva felt it in the stone beneath her bare feet — a reluctance, a slow dragging grief, like a child being pulled from sleep. For a hundred years she had been the mountain's wick, its candle, its warm little heart of winter. Now three of them climbed away from the under-court with the mirrored halls collapsing into silence behind them, and every step upward was a step the mountain took personally.

"You're limping," Beatrix said.

"I'm walking," Ylva corrected. "There is a difference, and it matters to me."

Aldric, ahead of them with a lantern he had no business still carrying — the flame had gone strange hours ago, burning blue and cold as a dead star — glanced back. "We could rest."

"We could also be buried," Ylva said. "I've spent a century underground, Your Highness. I have opinions about ceilings now. All of them are *no*."

That got the smallest huff out of him, which Ylva counted as a victory. She was counting victories carefully these days. She had very few in stock.

They climbed through a gallery of frozen waterfalls, each one a sheet of glass-green ice with something moving inside it — fish, perhaps, or memories, or simply light that had gotten trapped and never found its way out. Ylva kept her hand on the ice as they passed. It was warmer than the air. Everything down here was warmer than the air; the mountain hoarded warmth the way misers hoarded coins, and she had been its vault.

*You could stay,* the stone whispered, not unkindly. *You were good at it.*

"I was *used* at it," Ylva whispered back, and kept climbing.

* * *

They made camp in a hollow where the rock widened into something almost like a room, and Aldric built a fire from wood that shouldn't have existed this far down — driftwood, pale and salt-smelling, though the nearest sea was a week behind them. The mountain provided. The mountain had always provided. That was rather the whole problem.

Beatrix sat across the fire with the hard ledger on her knees, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, and did not read it. Ylva noticed that. She noticed everything now; a century of watching teaches you to notice, because noticing is the only entertainment available and eventually you get very good at your one hobby.

"You haven't opened it since we left," Ylva said.

"No."

"Is it heavier than it looks?"

"Yes," Beatrix said, and then, because Beatrix could no more leave an honest answer hanging than she could swallow poison without cataloguing it: "It weighs what it says. Every page. I can feel the debts pressing through the cover."

"Mm." Ylva drew her knees up. The firelight did things to her skin she was still relearning — shadows moved across it, real ones, cast by real flames, instead of the mirror-light of the under-court where every shadow was just yourself, arriving slightly late. "Then you should know what's in it. Some of it. The part about me."

Beatrix looked up.

"I was there," Ylva said. "At the beginning. I remember the famine."

* * *

Here is what Ylva remembered of the sun:

She had been nine years old, and the sun had been a thing you could lie in, like a bed. That was the part nobody believed when she told it — that sunlight had once been *soft*. In the capital now, in the long winter, children knew the sun as a pale coin behind gauze, a rumor, a thing their grandmothers swore at. But Ylva had lain in grass up to her chin and felt the sun press down on her like a large warm hand, patient, asking nothing.

The famine came the year she turned ten.

Everyone always asked what the famine looked like, and Ylva had stopped trying to explain that famines don't look like anything. They look like ordinary days. They look like your mother measuring flour twice because the first time might have been a dream. They look like your father walking further each morning to find work and coming home later each evening with less of himself. The famine was not a monster. It was arithmetic, done slowly, in every kitchen in the kingdom, and the answer was always the same.

"The crown tried everything," Ylva said, watching the fire. "I know they tell you otherwise now. I know the story has been... tidied. But King Magnus — old Magnus, the grandfather-king, the one they call wise in the songs — he emptied the granaries. He sold his ships. He sent men east past the salt marshes and west over the passes, and the passes ate them, and the ships came back empty, and the east was already starving too. It wasn't cruelty. It was a man doing sums that wouldn't come out right, and then doing them again, and again, hoping the numbers would take pity on him."

Aldric had gone very still. He had his father's face, Ylva thought, but not his father's way of listening. His father listened the way a trap listens. This boy listened the way a wound does.

"And then," Beatrix said quietly, "someone offered him a different sum."

"Someone offered him a different sum," Ylva agreed. "And here is the part you will not like, taster. Here is the part I have carried for a hundred years, turning it over like a stone, looking for the soft side."

She let the fire crackle. Let them lean in. A century underground had taught her patience, and besides — this was the only story she owned outright, and she intended to tell it properly.

"It wasn't the king who went to the mountain," she said. "It wasn't any lord or ambassador or hooded minister. When the under-court's door opened — the door in the deep cellar, the one that opens inward, the one that should never open inward — the people standing before it wore gray aprons and carried ledgers."

Beatrix's hands stopped moving.

"They called themselves factors," Ylva said. "Brokers. The Guild of Impossible Things did not begin as a smuggler's den, whatever Oren told you. It began as the crown's *other* treasury — the one that dealt in what granaries couldn't hold. And when the kingdom ran out of grain, gold, and prayers, the Guild did what guilds do. It found a price."

"No," Beatrix said. Not loudly. Just firmly, the way you'd correct a ledger entry. "No. The Guild keeps accounts. It doesn't make them. Oren said—"

"Oren," Ylva said gently, "is the fourth keeper of those books, and every keeper believes the books began clean in his own hands. It's how the rot survives. Each generation inherits the debt and the story together, and the story always says *it was someone else who signed.*"

She watched Beatrix's face do several things in quick succession — denial, arithmetic, memory, and then the terrible quiet of a woman auditing her own life and finding a column that didn't balance.

"The master of the Guild in those days," Ylva continued, "was named Corvus Hale. He wore gray like Oren wears gray. He spoke softly like Oren speaks softly. And when the under-court demanded a soul to seal the bargain — not a life, mind you, a *soul*, something that would burn slow and keep the winter gentle while the land recovered — it was Corvus Hale who said, 'The crown cannot spare a soul. The crown must remain blameless. But the Guild deals in impossible things, and this is merely the most impossible.' It was Corvus Hale who walked the villages with his ledger and his kind voice, looking for a child whose family owed too much."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, ice groaned and settled, the mountain shifting in its sleep.

"My mother owed eleven crowns," Ylva said. "Eleven. For seed grain, and a doctor, and a funeral — my father's, though the doctor hadn't helped. Eleven crowns. Corvus Hale sat at our table and drank our nettle tea and told my mother that the debt could be settled entire, that her daughter would live in comfort below, that the alternative was the debt collectors, and everyone in that village knew what the debt collectors took when coin ran short. My mother signed. She wept while she signed, and Corvus Hale held the pen steady for her, kindly, the way you'd help someone carry something heavy."

Ylva looked up and found both of them staring at her.

"I don't hate him anymore," she said. "That took ninety years, so appreciate the effort. Hate needs somewhere to go, and there was nowhere to put it, down there. But understand what I'm telling you. The crown sold me — yes. Old Magnus took the bargain and let it happen and spent the rest of his reign planting memorial orchards he couldn't look at. But the *hand* on the pen, the hand that found me, priced me, and delivered me — that was the Guild's hand. Your Guild, taster. The institution that taught you *record everything, feel nothing* learned it from the man who wrote me into a book as *one soul, aged ten, quality: resilient, terms: perpetual.*"

"*Quality: resilient,*" Beatrix repeated. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice Ylva imagined she used for poisons.

"He was thorough," Ylva said. "He was, I am sorry to say, excellent at his work. That's the horror of it. Not a monster. A professional."

* * *

Aldric spoke for the first time in a long while. "My father renewed the debt."

"Yes."

"He stood in the under-court — I saw the record — and he renewed it rather than let the winter end, because ending it would mean admitting what the crown had done."

"He did," Ylva said. "And before you drown in that, boy-king-in-waiting, hear the shape of the whole thing, because the shape matters. Magnus sold me to save the kingdom. Your father renewed me to save *himself*. And the Guild — the Guild sold me to save the kingdom too, and then spent a hundred years keeping the books that made sure no one could ever undo it, because an undone debt means an institution that admits it was wrong. Everyone in this story did the monstrous thing for a defensible reason. That's not an excuse. It's worse than an excuse. It's a *pattern*."

She unfolded herself and stood, restless, and paced to the edge of the firelight where the dark began.

"You want justice," she said, without turning around. "Both of you. I can smell it on you like smoke. You want to climb up out of this mountain, march into the capital, throw open the ledgers, and let truth fall on everyone like clean snow. And I am telling you, as the only person here who has read every page of what happens next: the ledgers will damn the crown, yes. And they will damn the Guild. And the Guild is the thing you, taster—" she turned now, and pointed at Beatrix, and her finger did not shake, which she was proud of— "*you* have given your nights to. The justice you're carrying is written in ink that includes your own hands' work. There is no clean page in that book. There is no institution in this kingdom that comes out of it white as snow. The snow itself comes out of it dirty."

Beatrix looked down at the hard ledger on her knees. She touched its cover the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you suspected of biting.

"Oren gave me this," she said. "He burned the soft ledgers to protect people. He told me the prophecy was a receipt. He—" She stopped. Started again, slower. "He handed me the burden and called it an inheritance. And all along, the first hand on that inheritance was the hand that bought a ten-year-old girl for eleven crowns."

"For eleven crowns and a kind voice," Ylva said. "Don't forget the kind voice. It's always the kind voice. Monsters roar; institutions murmur."

* * *

They slept in shifts after that, though Ylva suspected none of them slept deeply. She took the middle watch, sitting by the dying fire while the prince dreamed fitfully and the taster lay awake with her eyes open, which Ylva recognized, having done it for a hundred years: the posture of a person doing arithmetic in the dark.

Near dawn — if there was such a thing as dawn down here; the blue lantern said maybe — Beatrix sat up abruptly.

"The entry," she said. "The original entry. The one dated tonight, in my handwriting, a century old. If Corvus Hale wrote me into the ledger as a sale..." She was talking fast now, the way she talked when a poison finally had a name. "Then the prophecy isn't a prophecy. It's an *entry*. A scheduled payment. And entries have authors, and authors have reasons, and—" Her eyes came up, sharp and bright and frightened. "Ylva. You were in the under-court when the first taster came. You said once the mirrors showed you things. Did you see her? Did you see who wrote my name?"

Ylva fed a stick of driftwood to the embers and considered how much truth a person could carry before it became a second kind of debt.

"I saw her," she said. "The first taster. She came down the year after me, following the receipt, trying to undo what her Guild had done. She bargained. She failed. And before the under-court took her voice for her failure, she wrote one last entry in the hard ledger — a promise, a warning, a trap, depending on how you read it. She wrote it in the hand her granddaughter's granddaughter's granddaughter would one day inherit."

Beatrix had gone white. "That's why the page matched my writing."

"That's why," Ylva said, "the page has been waiting a hundred years for you to finish the sentence she started. And taster — there's something else she left, at the bottom of that entry, in letters so small the Guild's four keepers all missed them. I've had a century to memorize it."

The fire guttered. Above them, far above, the ice sang the thin high note it always sang when the mountain dreamed of spring.

"What does it say?" Beatrix whispered.

Ylva smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile — a century underground earns you that much.

"It says: *The debt is paid by the one who owes it, not the one who carries it. Look to whose name is on the first line.*"

Beatrix reached for the ledger.

And high above them, faint even through a mile of stone, the capital's bells began to ring — all of them at once, wild and wrong, the alarm-pattern that meant the winter had broken its banks and the snow was coming down for good.
