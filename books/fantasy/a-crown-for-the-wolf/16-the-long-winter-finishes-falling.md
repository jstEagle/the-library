## 16. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The last hundred steps of the mountain were the worst, because they were the easiest.

For eleven days the road had fought them — snow that fell upward, bridges that remembered being rivers, a village where every child had worn the same freckled face and waved with the same hand. Beatrix had catalogued each impossibility the way she once catalogued poisons: by name, by weight, by how much of it her body could carry before it stopped carrying anything at all.

But the final stretch was only stone and cold and Ylva's small hand in hers, and somehow that was harder. There was nothing to fight. There was only the walking, and the waiting to see what the world would do when it got its soul back.

Ylva had not spoken since the under-king's hall. She walked like someone relearning that ground could be trusted, one careful step at a time, her bare feet leaving prints that steamed faintly, as though the mountain itself were reluctant to let go of her warmth. A century beneath the earth, serving as the living wick of an endless winter — and still she climbed without complaint. Still she paused, twice, to look at a frozen lichen on the rock wall as if it were a miracle.

"It is," she said, catching Beatrix looking. "Lichen. I'd forgotten things grew."

Aldric, ahead of them, pretending not to listen and failing completely, cleared his throat. "When we reach the top, there will be soldiers."

"There are always soldiers," Beatrix said.

"Yes, but these ones will be *mine*." He said it lightly, but his jaw was tight. "My father's, rather. Which is worse. His men follow orders; mine would at least have the decency to be bribable."

Beatrix almost smiled. Eleven days of flight had taught her the shape of him: the prince who could not sleep, who bought honest answers from cellars because he could find none in palaces, who confessed his sins in inns that shouldn't exist and then waited, braced, for the people he loved to stop loving him. She was not sure she loved him. She was not sure what love looked like when both parties had been built, brick by brick, out of other people's debts. But she knew she trusted him, which in her ledger was worth considerably more.

They came over the shoulder of the mountain as the sun set, and the world below took her breath and did not give it back.

The capital lay buried.

Not snowed upon — *buried*. The white went to the second stories of the merchant quarter. The great cathedral's spire stood alone above the drifts like a candle drowned in milk. Smoke rose from perhaps one chimney in twenty, thin and desperate. And the winter did not fall so much as *press*, a slow gray weight descending without hurry, without end, patient as interest accruing on a debt no one wanted to name.

"The prophecy," Aldric said quietly. "The long winter finishes falling."

"Finishes," Beatrix repeated. "Not ends. It finishes falling tonight, and then it simply... stays." She had read the clause a hundred times in the century-old page Oren had pressed into her hands before the Guild burned. *On the night the sold soul walks free, the sky shall give up all it owes.* She had thought it meant the winter would break.

She should have known better. She was a ledger-keeper. Nothing is ever given up freely; everything given up must first be paid for.

Ylva stood at the edge of the overlook, her face lifted to the falling gray, and her expression was the worst thing Beatrix had seen in eleven days — worse than the tollgate spirit's hunger, worse than the feast of regrets. It was recognition.

"This is my doing," Ylva said.

"No," Beatrix said, too fast.

"I was the wick. A hundred years, I burned down there so this—" she gestured at the buried city, the dying chimneys, "—would keep falling instead of finishing. They told me it was mercy. That as long as I served, the winter would be slow." Her voice did not break. It had gone past breaking somewhere in the last century and come out the other side flat and clear, like ice over deep water. "I was the mercy. And now I've walked away, and the sky is collecting."

*The horror of inherited crimes,* Beatrix thought, *is that eventually you inherit the bill.*

"We're going to fix it," Aldric said. He said it the way he said most true things — too loudly, as if volume could substitute for certainty.

"How?" Ylva asked, and it was not cruel. It was genuinely curious, the way a child asks, and that made it worse.

Beatrix opened her mouth to answer and found, to her considerable professional embarrassment, that she had three answers and none of them were good.

---

They came down through the outskirts after dark, past farms where the roofs were white mounds and the only light came from the windows of root cellars dug deep enough to survive. In one farmhouse, through a gap in the shutters, Beatrix saw a family at supper by tallow-light: grandmother, parents, four children, all of them eating slowly, deliberately, the way people eat when they are counting what remains.

"They'll last until spring," Aldric murmured. "If spring comes."

"And if it doesn't?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to. She had done the arithmetic herself, on the mountain, in the inn with no keeper, at the tollgate where she had traded away her last secret. A kingdom can survive one famine year. Two, if the granaries were full. Three—

It had been three years already. The long winter had been falling since before she was born, growing a little heavier each season, and everyone in the capital had learned to call it weather. The crown called it weather. The Guild's ledgers called it something else entirely: *interest, accruing, payable on demand.*

The checkpoint at the river road was exactly where Aldric predicted. Six soldiers around a brazier, halberds rimed with frost, a sergeant with a list. When the prince stepped into the firelight, pulling back his hood, the sergeant's face went through a remarkable sequence of expressions — disbelief, terror, relief, and then the particular blankness of a man realizing that whatever happens next, it will be reported upward, and he will be blamed.

"Your Highness. We were told — that is, there are orders —"

"I know what the orders are," Aldric said. "I wrote half of them myself before I left. Where is my father?"

"The palace, sir. He's — there's a council sitting. They've been sitting for two days." The sergeant hesitated, then lowered his voice. "Sir, they say the winter's going to finish. The priests are saying it openly now. There's panic in the low quarters. And there's a warrant out."

"For me," Aldric said.

"For the taster, sir." The sergeant's eyes flicked to Beatrix, and away again, fast. "Treason. Abduction of crown property." He swallowed. "The property being... the girl."

*Crown property.* Beatrix felt the words settle into her chest like a swallowed stone. A hundred years ago, some clerk — some perfectly ordinary clerk, doing his job, recording everything and feeling nothing, a man exactly like her — had written *one soul* into an inventory, and the ink had never dried. Ylva had been a line item longer than she had been a person, as far as the law was concerned.

"Thank you, Sergeant," Aldric said. "You saw us pass through toward the eastern road. You attempted pursuit. Your horse threw you."

"My horse hasn't thrown me since I was nineteen, sir."

"He's due," Aldric said, and walked on.

---

The palace rose out of the snow like a whale surfacing — vast, pale, half-drowned. Its windows glowed against the dark, and the glow was wrong: too many lights, too frantic, the whole building lit like a house where someone has knocked over a lamp. Beatrix counted guards at the gates and did not like the number. Counted more along the walls and liked it less.

"They'll take Ylva the moment we're inside," she said. "Whatever my father's council decides, the bargain holds only if she goes back. He'll trade her before he lets the capital starve. It's what the bargain is *for*."

"Then we don't go in the front," Aldric started, and Beatrix shook her head.

"No. Listen to me." She turned to face him, and Ylva, and the falling snow, and made herself say the thing she had been turning over for eleven days like a coin she couldn't decide whether to spend. "We have three choices, and I want to say them out loud, because I've watched people ruin themselves by keeping their options in the dark where they could pretend all of them were still alive."

Aldric blinked. "You're making a ledger of us."

"I make ledgers of everything. It's either that or feel things, and I'm mid-crisis." She held up one finger. "The law. We return Ylva to the under-court. The winter slows. The capital survives another generation, and a girl who has already given a hundred years gives the rest, and the crown's crime stays buried under a hundred years of respectable bookkeeping. That is what the king will call mercy."

Second finger. "The Guild. We take Ylva and run — north, over the sea, somewhere the under-king's reach thins. Oren always said the hard ledger was the only truth the Guild ever kept, and the truth says the debt was *ours* — the Guild's, the first keeper's, not the crown's alone. We could spend our lives running from a court that doesn't age, or we could spend them trying to buy the debt back. Either way, the winter finishes falling tonight, and the capital buries while we're gone."

Third finger. She found her hand was shaking and made it stop, because a taster's hands do not shake, because she had trained them not to across ten years of lifting cups to her own lips.

"The truth. We walk into that palace, and we tell everyone what was sold, and who sold it, and why the sky is owed. All of it. The famine. The bargain. My grandfather's Guild. Your father's renewal of the debt. The prophecy, which isn't a prophecy at all — it's a receipt, Oren said so, and receipts can be disputed but only in front of witnesses." She exhaled. "And then we let the kingdom decide what it's willing to pay. Which might be everything."

Silence, except the snow.

"You know it has to be the third one," Aldric said finally.

"I know the third one is the only one where I don't have to live with myself afterward. Those aren't the same thing." She looked at Ylva. "It's not only our choice. You've been inventory for a century. You don't get handed back to the shelf because two strangers decided your life for you on a mountainside. What do you want?"

Ylva looked at the buried city for a long time. Snow gathered on her lashes and did not melt, and Beatrix understood suddenly, horribly, that the girl was still partly winter herself — that a hundred years as the wick leaves residue, the way poison leaves residue in a taster's blood.

"When they took me," Ylva said, "my mother ran behind the cart for two miles. I could hear her. Then I couldn't. Everyone who made that bargain is dead now, and everyone who benefited from it is comfortable, and the only person left who remembers the price is standing right here." She turned. Her eyes were the gray of the sky. "I want it written down. All of it. In a book that can't burn. And then I want to choose — *me* — whether I go back down that mountain. Not the king. Not the Guild. Not even you two."

Beatrix felt something in her chest crack open — not painfully. More the way ice cracks on a river in thaw: a sound like release.

"Then we go in the front door," she said. "And I'll bring the ledger."

---

The throne room doors opened onto heat and noise and the smell of too many candles burning too fast, and Beatrix understood in one glance that the council had been sitting for two days because no one dared be the first to leave.

Her father the king sat slumped on the throne like a man holding up a roof. Around him, clustered on the dais steps, the council: the Lord Chancellor with his ink-stained fingers, the High Priest with his ash-marked brow, the Marshal of the North with maps spread across his knees showing a country going white. And standing apart from all of them, near the great hearth, a figure in travel-worn gray with a burned smell clinging to his coat.

Oren.

Guildmaster Oren, who had burned the soft ledgers and kept the hard one, who had put the century-old page in her hands and told her the prophecy was a receipt — here, in the palace, on the night the winter finished falling. He met her eyes across the room, and his expression gave her nothing, which from Oren was itself a message: *I have been selling something. Decide quickly whether it was you.*

"Your Majesty," the chamberlain announced, in the strangled voice of a man reading words he does not believe, "Prince Aldric. And — the taster, Beatrix Eriksen, under warrant for treason. And..." He faltered, looking at Ylva, and Beatrix watched the whole room understand, in one rippling motion, what stood before them. The girl from under the mountain. Crown property, returned — or escaped, depending on who told the story.

The king rose.

"You brought her back," he said. Not to Aldric. To Beatrix. As if she were the servant here, the instrument, the cup-bearer. "Good. Good. The bargain can be preserved. Chancellor, prepare the escort — she goes back tonight, before the sky finishes—"

"She goes nowhere," Beatrix said, "until this room hears a reading."

Every head turned. Somewhere in the galleries above, a courtier whispered, and the whisper traveled like wind through wheat.

"A reading," the king repeated.

Beatrix reached into her coat and drew out the hard ledger — the one Oren had saved from the fire, the one that held a century of the Guild's impossible accounts, including the entry dated the night of her birth, in a hand that matched her own. She had carried it eleven days. It weighed what it had always weighed. It weighed everything.

"The crown sold a soul to end a famine," she said, and her voice did not shake, because she had trained it not to, across ten years of swallowing poisons and calling them breakfast. "The Guild brokered the sale. The winter is the interest. And I am holding the account book."

At the hearth, Oren closed his eyes — briefly, like a man hearing a debt called in that he had always known would come due.

The king's face went gray as the sky. "Seize her," he said quietly. "Seize the book."

And the Marshal of the North, who had spent three winters burying farmers, did not move.

* * *

The guards did not move either. That was the thing Beatrix noticed first, standing in the middle of the throne room with a stolen ledger under her arm and a warrant on her head: nobody moved. The chamberlain looked at the Marshal. The Marshal looked at the maps. The High Priest looked at the windows, where the snow pressed against the glass like a crowd.

"Your Majesty," the Marshal said at last, heavily, "before anyone seizes anyone. I'd like to hear the reading."

"So would I," said the Lord Chancellor, whose ink-stained fingers had, Beatrix now noticed, signed the original renewal of the debt thirty years ago, and whose face was performing the particular calm of a man calculating how much of the book mentioned him.

The king looked around his throne room and discovered, as kings sometimes do at the worst possible hour, that authority is a loan and the lenders are in the room.

"Read, then," he said to Beatrix, and his voice had something in it she recognized from mirrors she'd caught of herself late at night: the sound of a person who has been keeping a ledger of their own, alone, for a very long time.

She opened the book to the first page. The fire cracked. The snow ticked against the high windows. Ylva stood at her shoulder, and Aldric at hers, and above them in the galleries the courtiers leaned forward like a congregation.

"On the third night of the famine year," Beatrix read, "the undersigned brokers the following exchange..."

She got through four entries before the doors at the far end of the throne room boomed — once, twice, a third time — and the candles all leaned sideways at once, though there was no wind.

Everyone turned. The doors strained against their bars. From beyond them came a cold that Beatrix felt in her teeth, in the old scars of her poisons, in the place where her immunity lived — and beneath the cold, a voice like a mountain clearing its throat.

"The account holder," said the under-king, through a hundred feet of palace stone, "has arrived to review his holdings."

Beside her, Ylva went very still. Aldric's hand found Beatrix's sleeve. And Oren, the old traitor, the last keeper, stepped away from the hearth at last and said, in the mild voice of a man balancing books:

"Well. I did tell you the third option would cost everything."
