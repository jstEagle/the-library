## 17. The Trial of the Taster

The throne room had frozen from the inside out.

Aldric noticed it first in the way sound behaved — how his boots on the ice-slicked flagstones rang too long, how the whispers of the court skated across the hall and came back to him doubled, like the room itself was eavesdropping and couldn't keep its opinions to itself. Frost climbed the pillars in ferns and feathers. The great windows had gone white as milk glass. Somewhere above, a chandelier hung sheathed in ice, its hundred candles burning behind a skin of frost like drowned stars.

His father sat upon the throne wrapped in three fur cloaks and a fury that had kept him warmer than any of them.

"Kneel," the king said.

Beatrix Eriksen did not kneel.

She stood in the center of the hall where the guards had placed her, hands bound before her with silk cord — silk, Aldric noted, because even now his father's household could not bring itself to put iron on a servant of the crown, only ribbon — and she looked up at the throne with the particular patience of a woman who had spent years cataloguing poisons and found treason merely another entry in the ledger. Her hair was still crusted with mountain snow. There was a bruise along her jaw from the tollgate that he knew about because he'd been there, and a smaller one on her knuckle that he didn't know about, which bothered him more than it should have.

Beside her, wrapped in a blanket that someone — Aldric suspected Ylva, who had developed strong opinions about human comfort after a century without it — had draped over her shoulders, the girl from under the mountain watched everything with wide gray eyes. The court did not know what to make of Ylva. The court did not know what to make of anything anymore. The winter had finished falling two nights ago, burying the capital to the second stories, and the king had responded by summoning everyone he could still reach and demanding an explanation, and the explanation he had chosen was *her*.

"The taster Beatrix Eriksen," Lord Chancellor Vess intoned from the steps of the dais, unrolling a scroll with fingers so cold they shook, "stands accused of high treason: of stealing the crown's property from the court beneath the mountain; of conspiring with persons unknown to break the winter compact; of—" Vess squinted at the scroll, which was clearly longer than anyone had expected it to be, "—of *general sedition*, the particulars of which are... extensive."

"She stole nothing," Aldric said.

He hadn't meant to speak. He'd sworn to himself, walking down the frozen corridor that morning, that he would hold his tongue, watch, wait, find the moment. But his mouth had apparently decided that waiting was for people with better self-control, and here they were.

His father turned to look at him, and the temperature of the hall seemed to drop by several degrees, which was genuinely impressive given the circumstances.

"You will speak when testimony is required of you, Aldric."

"I am required to testify." He stepped forward before the sensible part of him could file a formal objection. "I'm heir apparent. I was there. On the mountain. In the court beneath it. If you want the truth of what happened, Father, no one in this room has it except me and her."

"And the witch-child," someone said from the back, and Ylva turned her head toward the voice with such slow, deliberate gravity that the speaker visibly reconsidered his entire life.

"The girl," the king said, "is a matter of state. She will be returned."

"She'll be returned over my body," Aldric said, "which I understand is an option you're considering, so let me save you the deliberation: it won't work either. You've read the prophecy, Father. Or the receipt, rather, since that's what it turns out to be. Only the taster may enter the mountain. Only the prince may call her back. Your bargain doesn't function without both of us alive and willing. Killing us isn't a solution. It's a *warranty void*."

Murmurs ran through the court like wind through wheat. His father's jaw tightened. Aldric had learned to read that jaw over twenty-three years: this was the setting that preceded either a decree or a dismissal, and lately it had been difficult to predict which.

"You stand there," the king said quietly, "and lecture me on bargains. Do you know what I inherited? A kingdom that eats. A famine that ended a century ago and never explained itself. A winter that comes earlier every year and stays later. I renewed the payment because the alternative was watching children starve in the streets while the mountain grew fat. You know nothing of weighing a crown."

"No," Aldric agreed. "But I've been reading the ledgers."

Something flickered across his father's face — not guilt, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a debt collector at the door and realizing, with horror, that he'd known this day would come.

Beatrix shifted beside him. Just slightly. He'd spent enough weeks on the road with her to translate her movements the way other men translated weather: that half-step was her saying *don't*, and the tilt of her chin was her saying *do it anyway, but be smart about it*, and the almost-invisible exhale was her saying *you're being an idiot, and I've decided to allow it*.

He had missed her terribly in the four days since the guards had taken her, and he was not going to examine that feeling right now, because there was a trial on.

"Then testify," the king said. "Tell the court what happened on the mountain. Tell them how the taster betrayed her oath."

And there it was. The trap, laid out flat and gleaming. Testify against her, and he bought his father's forgiveness and lost whatever fragile thing had grown between him and the woman who had once saved his life with her own tongue and a stolen cup. Refuse, and he confirmed every whisper that he was unfit, compromised, a prince led by his — well. The courtiers had never been subtle.

Aldric looked at Beatrix.

She looked back at him, and to his lasting astonishment, she *smiled*. It was a small smile, crooked, tired, the smile of a woman watching someone open a door she'd already picked the lock of. And she gave him the smallest nod.

*Do it anyway,* said the tilt of her chin. *But be smart about it.*

So he reached into his coat and drew out the hard ledger.

* * *

Guildmaster Oren had pressed it into his hands the night the Guild's doors closed, ash still on his sleeves, his eyes holding the specific grief of a man who has just burned forty years of careful work and kept only the part that would get everyone killed.

"The soft ledgers were mercy," Oren had said. "Favors, kindnesses, the debts people pay gladly. The hard ledger is the truth, and the truth is that the crown has been in arrears for a hundred years, and the mountain keeps excellent books. When the time comes — and it will come, boy, receipts always come due — someone will need to read it aloud. Reading aloud is the oldest magic there is. A debt spoken in front of witnesses becomes real in a way paper never manages. That's why they burn records. Not to erase the past. To keep the present comfortable."

Aldric had carried it against his ribs ever since, through the snow and the tollgates and the terrible inn with no keeper, and it had weighed less than a loaf of bread and more than a cathedral.

Now he opened it on the frozen floor of the throne room, kneeling — not to his father, though the court would remember it otherwise, but to the book itself, which felt correct — and began at the beginning.

"In the year of the Long Hunger," he read, "the crown of this realm entered into compact with the court beneath the mountain. Payment rendered: one soul, freely named. The soul of Ylva Sorensdatter, aged eleven years, given in exchange for the ending of the famine. Interest accruing: one winter per annum, lengthening. Signed for the crown: King Magnus the Second. Witnessed for the Guild: Master Corvin Hale, keeper of ledgers."

He paused. Let it sit. Watched the words travel through the hall the way frost traveled through the windows — slowly, then all at once.

"Father," he said, "you didn't start this debt. I know that. Grandfather didn't either. It goes back five kings. But you *renewed* it. Here." He turned the page. "In your hand. 'Payment deferred. The girl remains. The winter continues. So ordered.'"

"Aldric." His father's voice had gone very soft, which was worse than shouting. "Close the book."

"There's more."

"*Close the book.*"

"There's a century more," Aldric said. "There's the year the crown sold the memory of Queen Lisbet's death to spare me — itemized, Father, with a price. There's the famine relief diverted to buy silence from three border lords. There's the tasters. Do you know what the mountain pays for tasters? Every royal food-taster for six generations has been bred and trained for one purpose, and it isn't protecting kings. It's preparing a body that can survive the under-court's feast. Beatrix wasn't hired. She was *harvested*. Slowly. By us. By the crown, by the Guild, by everyone in this room who ever ate a meal she tasted and slept soundly after."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature. Somewhere in the gallery, a woman began to cry, quietly, the way people cry when something makes sense at last and they wish it didn't.

Beatrix spoke for the first time.

"For the record," she said, in the mild tone she used for announcing arsenic in the soup, "the training did work. I survived the feast. Mostly. I'd avoid the regret-broth if any of you are ever invited, which, given current events, seems unlikely."

Someone near the back made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Aldric loved her so much in that moment that it was frankly inconvenient.

"Treason," the king said. "This is treason, Aldric. You read crown secrets to a packed court. You side with a thief and a witch against your own blood."

"I side with the truth," Aldric said, standing, closing the ledger, holding it against his chest like a shield or a heart. "You taught me the crown stands above all. You were wrong. The crown stands on top of all — on top of a dead girl's name, on top of a taster's ruined body, on top of a hundred winters we purchased with someone else's life and called governance. I am committing treason, Father. I do it knowingly, publicly, and with witnesses." He gestured at the frozen hall. "All of whom are currently trapped in here with us until spring anyway, so they may as well hear the rest."

"That's enough testimony," Lord Vess said faintly.

"It really isn't," said Ylva.

Everyone turned. The girl from under the mountain rose from her place beside Beatrix, letting the blanket slide from her shoulders, and walked — small, barefoot, unhurried — to the base of the dais. When she spoke again, her voice had changed. It was still a child's voice, but it carried the way a bell carries, filling the whole frozen hall, ringing off the ice on the pillars.

"A hundred years," she said, "I kept the wick burning. A hundred years, I held the winter so your harvests would grow and your children would eat, and no one told me why, and no one asked if I minded. I minded." She looked up at the king, and her eyes were old and gray and patient as stone. "I don't want your throne. I don't want your heads. I want the debt *read*. All of it. Out loud. Because the mountain sent me back with a message, and the message is that the interest has compounded, and the collector is coming, and he does not accept apologies."

The candles guttered, all hundred of them, all at once, though there was no wind.

"What message," the king said. "What collector."

Ylva looked at Beatrix. Beatrix looked at the floor, and Aldric saw her do the thing she did — the cataloguing, the tallying, the quiet arithmetic of a woman counting exits and finding none — and then she lifted her head and said, in a voice perfectly steady:

"The under-king released us because the debt requires a taster to return willingly. I consented to go back down. That was the final clause. What nobody wrote down — what even the ledger doesn't say — is that consent, once given, can't be withdrawn. The contract is live. The mountain has my yes." She exhaled. "And according to Ylva, it's coming to collect tonight."

The frost on the great windows bloomed outward all at once, ferns becoming forests, and beneath their feet — deep, deep below the frozen flagstones, below the cellars and the buried streets and the roots of the city itself — something vast shifted its weight, the way a sleeper shifts before waking, and the throne room floor rang like a struck bell.

Aldric moved without deciding to, closing the distance between himself and Beatrix, putting the hard ledger between them and the dais, one hand finding hers — silk-bound, cold, steady.

"Well," he said. "I suppose we should discuss terms."
