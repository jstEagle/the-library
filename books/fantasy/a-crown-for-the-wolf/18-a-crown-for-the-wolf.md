## 18. A Crown for the Wolf

The floor of the throne room split like a loaf of good bread — cleanly, along a seam no one had known was there.

Beatrix felt it before she heard it: a tremor in her boot soles, the particular shiver of stone remembering it was once part of a mountain. The courtiers nearest the dais stumbled back from the crack as if it were fire. The king did not stumble. The king went very still, the way men do when they realize the debt collector has come to the door and there is nowhere left to hide the silver.

"Everyone out," the king said. It was meant to be a roar. It came out as a request.

Nobody moved. Nobody ever moved when told to run; that was the first thing Beatrix had learned in twenty years of tasting other people's food. Panic made people stupid, and stupid people stayed at the table.

The second thing she had learned was to count what was in the room.

She counted now, quickly, the way she'd once catalogued poisons by their aftertaste. Aldric, three steps to her left, snowmelt still dripping off his cloak, his hand hovering near a sword he was far too decent to use well. Ylva, standing just inside the great doors with the hard ledger clutched to her chest like a child, one hundred years of winter steaming gently off her shoulders. The king on his frozen throne. Forty courtiers who would swear tomorrow they had never been here tonight.

And rising up through the split in the floor, unhurried as smoke poured backward into a bottle, the under-king.

He was not enormous. That was the horror of him. He was exactly the size of a man, exactly the shape of a man, dressed in gray so fine it seemed to drink the torchlight, and his face was the face of someone you had met once at a funeral and never quite placed since. He stepped onto the marble of the throne room as though testing whether it would hold him, and where his foot fell, frost bloomed outward in the pattern of a ledger's ruled lines.

"Taxes," said the under-king mildly, to no one in particular. "A hundred years of them."

* * *

The king found his voice somewhere around the third step of the dais. "The bargain was honored," he said. "The soul was delivered. The winter was the agreed interest, and the interest has been paid every season since—"

"The interest," the under-king said, "was the *grace period*." He turned his head, slowly, until his eyes — gray, patient, terribly bookkeeperly eyes — found Ylva. Something crossed his face that might have been regret, if regret were a currency anyone down below actually traded in. "The principal remains outstanding. One soul, freely given, held in trust against the crown's debts. I have come to collect the principal."

"You can't have her," Aldric said.

It was not a kingly statement. It was barely even an intelligent one — you do not tell the under-king what he can and cannot have; the mountain has heard better arguments from avalanches. But he said it anyway, stepping forward, and Beatrix felt something in her chest do a complicated maneuver that she filed, as always, under *later*.

The under-king regarded him with mild curiosity. "Princes," he said, "are not parties to this contract. Neither are tasters." His gaze slid to Beatrix then, and settled, and she understood with a lurch that he knew precisely what she was, had perhaps always known, the way a ledger knows its own entries. "Though the prophecy does mention a taster. Freely given. You've read your clause, I assume?"

"I've read everything," Beatrix said. "It's rather my whole personality."

Someone among the courtiers made a small hysterical sound. Even now. Especially now.

* * *

Here is what Beatrix Eriksen knew about debts, and she knew more than any living person except possibly the creature climbing out of her floor:

A debt is not the money. A debt is the *record*. Burn the coin, steal the coin, sink the coin in the sea — the debt survives, because the debt lives in ink. In agreement. In two parties saying *this is owed*, and a page between them holding its shape. She had learned it in the Guild's cellars at fourteen years old, balancing ledgers by candlelight while Oren muttered over the soft books, and she had learned it again in the under-court, watching dishes brewed from regrets strip her memories away one by one while she gripped the one memory that mattered: why she kept ledgers at all.

Because someone had to write down what was true. Because unrecorded suffering is the only crime that commits itself twice.

And because — this was the part she'd fought hardest to keep, the taste she'd refused to swallow — a record is also the only thing that can make a lie real. The prophecy was a receipt, Oren had said. Receipts cut both ways. Somewhere in the under-court's mirrored halls, in the under-king's own hand, there was a page that said Beatrix Eriksen owed herself, freely given, on the longest night of the year. And pages like that do not expire. They wait. They are very, very good at waiting.

So when the under-king looked at her and said, "Well, taster? The hour is yours. The prophecy requires your consent, and I find I am... curious... whether you'll give it," Beatrix did not look at the prince. She did not look at the girl with the ledger or the king on his melting throne.

She held out her hand.

"Before we discuss my consent," she said, "let's discuss collateral."

* * *

The under-king's eyebrows rose a fraction. For a creature of the deep places, she suspected, this was roughly equivalent to falling out of his chair.

"Collateral," he repeated.

"You want a soul. Freely given. Mine, specifically, per the receipt — which, incidentally, is beautifully drafted, whoever your scrivener was, though the marginalia is showing off." She kept her voice level, warm almost, the voice she used for telling a cook his soup was fine, truly, nothing wrong with it at all, please stop looking at me like that. "But here's your problem. If I give myself freely, the debt closes. Paid in full. And when a debt closes—" she watched his face carefully, the way she'd once watched a king's face for the flicker of hemlock— "*the record dies with it.*"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"Every entry in your ledgers," Beatrix continued, "every contract, every receipt, every century of careful accounting between your court and ours — all of it exists because the debts exist. A closed debt is a dead page. And I don't think you want dead pages. I think—" and here she smiled, the small genuine smile she saved for balanced columns— "I think you are, at bottom, a keeper of records yourself. I think you'd rather have the library than the payment."

"You presume," said the under-king, "a great deal."

"I'm a food taster. Presuming is how I stay alive." She let the smile fade. "Name your price honestly, then. What would you take instead of me? Not a substitute soul — we're done trading girls, all of us, everyone in this room, done forever. What else does a debt-collector value?"

The under-king was quiet for a long moment. Frost crept along the ruled lines at his feet, patient as arithmetic.

"The ledger," he said at last.

Not Ylva's arms-tight copy. He tilted his head toward the doors, toward the Guild, toward the cellars where Oren had burned the soft books and kept the hard one — but Beatrix shook her head before he finished the sentence.

"The hard ledger is already spoken for. It testifies Thursday." She said it flatly, and watched the king flinch on his throne, because Thursday was the trial, and the trial was coming for him like winter. "No. You don't want Oren's book. Oren's book records what the Guild *did*. You want the thing underneath all of it. The master record. The one that makes every debt real."

"And where," said the under-king softly, "would I find such a thing?"

Beatrix reached into her coat.

* * *

She had carried it since the cellars. Since Oren pressed the century-old page into her hands with his old man's fingers shaking, saying *it's a receipt, girl, not a prediction — receipts can be torn up, but only by the party who holds the account.* She had carried it through the snow that fell upward and the village of one face and the inn with no keeper. Through the tollgate where she'd paid in truths. Down into the mirrored dark, where she'd read it aloud to Ylva by the light of the winter's living wick and watched a hundred years of stolen seasons flicker in a girl's eyes.

The page was soft as cloth now, worn at the folds. Her name in her own handwriting, entered a hundred years before her birth. *Beatrix Eriksen. Owed to the court beneath the mountain. Freely given.*

"It was written by the first taster," Beatrix said. "My ancestor. She failed — she couldn't give her consent, because the crown coerced it, and a coerced gift isn't free, and your own law rejected it. So the debt rolled forward. Generation to generation. Waiting for a taster who could say yes with nobody forcing her." She looked at the under-king steadily. "You didn't send the prophecy to kill me. You sent it to *find* me. To find the one heir to that failed contract who might actually pay."

"Perceptive," said the under-king. "Yes."

"But here's what your scrivener missed, and mine wouldn't have." She unfolded the page fully, held it up between them. "This isn't just a receipt. Look at the hand. My hand. Century-old ink, my exact script — because the first taster wrote it in the Guild's master style, the binding style, the one that makes a record *true*. This page doesn't describe the debt. This page **is** the debt. Tear it, burn it, drown it in the sea, and the debt doesn't transfer. It doesn't roll forward. It simply... was never properly recorded. And an improperly recorded debt—"

"—is no debt at all," the under-king finished.

His voice had gone very quiet. Around his feet, the frost-lines flickered, and for one heartbeat Beatrix saw the truth of him: not a monster, not a god, but a bookkeeper of the deep places, ancient and meticulous and *bound by his own columns*, a creature who could no more break a rule of accounting than the mountain could apologize.

"So," she said. "Two offers. One: I give my consent, freely given, as prophesied. You get your soul, the debt closes, the record dies, and a hundred years of contracts start unraveling — including, I'd wager, several your own court would rather kept standing. Or two:" she turned the page in her fingers, "you take the record itself. The original instrument. The one thing in either kingdom that makes this debt real. Take it into your keeping, under your seal, and the account transfers — crown to under-court, permanently, irrevocably, with the Guild and the throne both released as parties. You don't get a soul. You get something better. You get *the proof*. Forever. And no one — not kings, not guilds, not time — can ever again pretend it didn't happen."

The throne room was silent enough to hear snow settling on the broken roof.

"That is not mercy," the under-king said. "That is blackmail with excellent manners."

"Thank you," said Beatrix. "I've been practicing."

* * *

"You'd erase yourself," Aldric said.

He had come closer without her noticing — of course he had, he was a prince, sneaking was the one court skill he'd mastered. His face was doing something terrible and open, the way it did when he forgot to be royal. "Beatrix. The page holds your name. Your *name*. If he takes it into the deep places—"

"Then the deep places remember me," she said. "Which is more than the capital ever managed."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, and the quiet was worse. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. We stole Ylva back. We got out. We *won*—"

"We escaped," Beatrix corrected gently. "Winning is different. Winning means the debt gets paid by somebody, and the somebodies are you, or her, or forty generations of tasters not yet born. I've read the ledgers, Aldric. All of them. Somebody always pays. The only choice anyone ever gets is *what* they pay with." She looked down at the page in her hands, at her own name in her own hand, and felt — unexpectedly, absurdly — fond of it. It had waited a hundred years for her. It had been, in its strange patient way, the only thing that had never lied to her.

Besides. She had spent her whole life recording everything and feeling nothing. It seemed only fair that, at the end, the record should feel something about her.

"The consent clause," the under-king said. His gray eyes had not left the page. "If I accept the instrument in lieu of the soul, the prophecy's mechanism breaks. You understand what that means. No collection. No closure. The account sits in my vaults, open, forever, accruing nothing — a monument to a crime no one may ever again deny." He paused. "It will be the most honest document in either kingdom. I may have to build it a shelf."

"Build it a wing," said Beatrix. "Do we have terms?"

The under-king extended his hand.

And here — this was the moment, the hinge of the long night, the point where a hundred years of winter balanced on a single grip of flesh on flesh — Beatrix hesitated. Just a breath. Long enough to look at Ylva, standing in the doorway with the hard ledger and the dawn-colored light of the breaking storm behind her, alive, mortal, *free*, blinking at the world like a woman seeing weather for the first time in a century. Long enough to look at Aldric, whose mouth was moving around words he couldn't make himself say, whose eyes were saying them instead, fluently, in a language she had no ledger for.

Long enough to understand what she was about to lose, and to choose it anyway.

"Freely given," she said to the under-king, and it was not the prophecy's word anymore. It was hers. "Take it."

His hand closed over hers and the page passed between them, and the moment it left her fingers Beatrix felt it go — not pain, nothing so honest as pain — a *lightness*, as if a column of figures had been struck from her chest, as if a room she'd furnished for twenty-two years had been emptied in an instant. Her name. Her name was in that page. Beatrix Eriksen, daughter of no one, taster of poisons, keeper of impossible things, the woman who had started keeping ledgers because—

because—

The thought slid sideways. She caught at it. It slipped like fish-scales.

"Oh," she said, to no one, to the under-king, to the emptying room inside her. "Oh, that's what it costs."

"Yes," said the under-king, and for the first time his voice sounded almost gentle, almost sorry, the voice of a clerk closing a book he'd grown attached to. "Records must be complete, taster. You asked for honesty. This is what honesty weighs." He stepped backward into the split in the floor, the page vanishing into the gray of his coat, and the last thing she saw of him was his eyes, gray and patient and — yes, she was almost sure of it — filing her away.

The floor sealed shut behind him like water closing over a stone.

The frost-lines faded from the marble. Somewhere above, muffled through the broken roof, the snow stopped falling for the first time in a hundred years — stopped, hung, and began, very softly, to melt.

"Beatrix?" Aldric's hand was on her arm. Warm. Alarmed. "Beatrix, look at me — what did he take, what did he *take*—"

She turned to him, ready to answer, ready to say *I'm fine, it's done, we won, go be a good king* —

and found she had opened her mouth to speak to a tall stranger with kind eyes and snow in his hair, a stranger who was looking at her with the particular baffled urgency of a man who cannot remember why he is holding onto a woman he has never seen before.

"I—" he said. His brow furrowed. His grip loosened, finger by finger, like a man waking from a dream he was already losing. "I'm sorry. Do I... know you?"

Behind him, Ylva clutched the hard ledger tighter, and her face — her new, sun-hungry, hundred-years-late face — went white with understanding.

"No," Beatrix said, to the prince, to the palace, to the thawing world that was already forgetting her. "I don't believe you do."
