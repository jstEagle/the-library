## 19. One Name Erased

The under-king's hand closed around the ledger the way a drowning man closes around driftwood — with gratitude and no intention of letting go.

Beatrix felt it happen. That was the strange part. She had expected pain, or at least ceremony, some great tearing of the soul to match the magnitude of what she'd given. Instead there was only a sensation like a page turning somewhere behind her ribs, and then a lightness so profound it frightened her more than any poison ever had.

The mirrored palace shuddered. Above them — far above, through a hundred fathoms of stone and snow — something vast and old sighed out its last held breath.

"The winter," Ylva whispered. She had not stopped touching her own hands since her chains dissolved, as if checking that they were real. "I can feel it ending. It's like — oh." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she pressed both hands over her mouth.

A hundred years of cold, unwinding all at once. Beatrix felt it too, faintly, the way you feel a headache leave: an absence where an ache had lived so long it had become furniture.

"You understand the terms," the under-king said. He was smaller now, she noticed. The ledger in his hands seemed to weigh him down, which was fitting. Every debt he collected made him heavier; that was the joke of his office, the one nobody beneath the mountain laughed at anymore. "The record is mine. The consent was yours. The prophecy required a taster's death, freely chosen — but a name given up entirely satisfies the same hunger. You cannot die for a court that has forgotten you existed."

"I read the fine print," Beatrix said. "Eventually."

"Eventually," the under-king agreed, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Most never do. Most pay in blood and call it fate." His mirror-bright eyes moved over her face, cataloguing her the way she once catalogued poisons. "You will feel it go in pieces. Names are not one thing. They are many small things, stitched together. It is less painful that way. I insist on it."

"That's almost kind."

"I am a creature of contracts, taster. Kindness is simply efficiency wearing its good coat."

And then it began.

* * *

It went first from the ledgers.

She felt it as a coolness in her fingertips, the ghost of ink lifting off paper somewhere very far above. Every entry she had ever written — every debt recorded, every favor logged, every impossible thing balanced against every other impossible thing — the words were still there, but the *hand* was changing. Becoming neutral. Becoming anonymous. Somewhere in the Guild's cellars, a century of her careful script was rewriting itself into a clerk's hand, then into no hand at all.

"The hard ledger," she said suddenly, turning to Aldric. "Your testimony. You read my name aloud in front of the whole frozen court —"

"I did." Aldric's brow furrowed. He opened his mouth, closed it, opened it again. The look on his face was one she recognized from the tasting table: the expression of a man who has just swallowed something and is waiting to find out what it does.

"It was important," he said slowly. "I remember that it was important. There was a ledger, and I stood up, and I —" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "Why can't I remember your name? I knew it this morning. I've known it since we collided over that cursed page in Oren's cellar, I've known it through snow that fell upward and an inn with no keeper and a tollgate that charged us truths —"

"Aldric." She kept her voice steady, because someone had to, and apparently that job was still hers even now. Especially now. "Look at me. What am I called?"

He looked. And she watched it happen — watched the thing reach him, watched it slide off. His eyes went through her the way light goes through glass. Not unkindly. Just *through*.

"You're—" He stopped. Tried again. "You were the taster. Wren help me, you *were* the taster, I know that, I remember the feast, the poison, you drank the cup meant for me and smiled while you did it —" His voice climbed. "But your name is a hole. A hole shaped exactly like a person."

"There it is," Beatrix said quietly.

Ylva made a sound beside her, small and wounded. "Mine too. I had it. I was holding onto it for you, I promised — Beatrix Eriksen, food-taster, keeper of ledgers, the woman who — who —" Ylva's face crumpled. "Oh, no. Oh, it's going. It's like trying to hold water in a fist."

"Don't hurt yourself holding it." Beatrix touched the girl's shoulder — the girl who had spent a century as a living wick and deserved, more than anyone alive, to keep something freely given. "Let it go. That's rather the point."

"But it isn't fair—"

"No," Beatrix agreed. "It isn't. Fairness was never in the contract. I checked."

* * *

They climbed.

The mountain's throat had softened — the ice that had sealed the passage for a century weeping down its walls in long bright ropes, the air tasting, for the first time in anyone's memory, of spring trying to remember its own name. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world again.

Aldric walked beside her. He had not left her side since the throne room, and Beatrix understood why without needing it said: he was a man gripping the edge of a hole. Every few minutes he would glance at her, quick and desperate, like a gambler checking whether his luck had turned.

"Say something," he said, as they came up out of the last of the tunnels. "Anything. Your voice might — voices carry differently than names, maybe —"

"I think your plan is foolish and your boots are disgraceful," Beatrix said. "You've worn the same pair through a blizzard, a haunted border, and the under-court, and you complain about the cold like it's a personal insult."

His face lit — actually lit, hope flaring across it like a struck match — and then dimmed as the hole swallowed even that. "I remember that you said it. I remember thinking, *she's the only person in two kingdoms who talks to me like a human being.* But I can't — the name won't come. It's like reaching for a stair that isn't there." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, wet and furious. "This is what they'll all feel. Everyone. Your Guildmaster. The kitchen girls. Whoever taught you letters. An entire kingdom full of people with a hole where you used to be, and none of them knowing enough to grieve."

"Grief requires a shape," Beatrix said. "That was the price. No shape, no grief. Clean dissolution." She paused. "The under-king is thorough. I'll give him that."

"And you?" Aldric caught her arm as they stepped out of the mountain's mouth. "Do *you* still have it?"

She reached inward, carefully, the way she once probed a suspect dish with the tip of a spoon.

And found — herself. Still there. Still Beatrix, whatever that word now meant and to whom. The memories were intact: the cellar, the ledgers, the rule she'd lived by — *record everything, feel nothing* — and how badly she'd broken it, in the end, and how glad she was to have broken it. The taste of forty-one poisons, catalogued like old friends. The look on Aldric's face when she'd drunk the cup at the winter feast and winked at him over the rim.

Everything except the name. Where "Beatrix Eriksen" had been written on the inside of her, there was now a smooth blank space, like a page torn cleanly from a bound book. She could walk around it. She could see the shape of what was missing. She simply could not say it, could not summon it, could not stitch it back.

"I have everything else," she told him. "Which is either a mercy or the cruelest possible arrangement. I haven't decided."

* * *

The capital emerged from the thaw like a confession.

Snow slid from rooftops in thundering sheets. The frozen canals groaned and split, and people stood along their banks watching the black water move again, some of them weeping, none of them able to say precisely why. A hundred years of winter, ending in three days of dripping eaves and mud and the unbelievable green smell of things preparing to grow.

Beatrix walked through it invisible.

Not magically — worse than that. Socially. She passed the Guild's door and the doorman looked straight through her. She stood in the palace kitchens, drawn there by habit older than thought, and the head cook handed her a bowl of soup without a glance, the way you hand a bowl to furniture. She tasted it out of pure reflex — too much salt, a whisper of spoiled cream in the second pot, *tell them* — and then realized there was no one to tell. No office. No post. The taster's position had dissolved with the debt, or perhaps it had always been a symptom of it.

In the Guild's cellar, Oren sat alone among the ash of the soft ledgers, the hard ledger gone below forever, and when Beatrix stood in the doorway he frowned at the draft and got up to shut the door.

On her.

She let him.

* * *

Aldric found her at dusk on the palace steps, because of course he did. He had been finding her for weeks now, and the habit evidently outlived the reason.

"They're crowning me in the spring," he said, sitting down beside her without asking, which was also a habit. "First act: abolishing the taster's office. Second act: burning every debt the crown holds that smells of the under-court. Third act—" He hesitated.

"Go on."

"Third act, I don't know yet. There's a — there's a woman." He said it awkwardly, staring straight ahead at the melting city. "There's a woman I can't stop thinking about, and I don't know her name, and when I ask people they look at me strangely, and Ylva keeps writing something in a ledger and hiding it when I come near, and I'm starting to think everyone in this kingdom knows something about me that I don't."

"That sounds uncomfortable."

"It's unbearable." He turned to look at her then, and his eyes were doing the thing they did — sliding toward the hole, catching on its edges, unable to fall in. "You remind me of her. Isn't that mad? You sit the way she sits. You make that same face when you think someone's lying — one eyebrow, like you're pricing them. I met you — didn't I? On the road. After the mountain. I must have met you."

"You must have," Beatrix said.

Her voice held. She was proud of that. Forty-one poisons had trained her well: the body could be dying and the face could remain pleasant.

"I'm going to find out," Aldric said. "Whatever it is. Whatever everyone's being so careful around. I'm the king soon; I'm allowed one impossible purchase, and I'm spending it on this." He stood, and offered her his hand — an absurd gesture, a prince helping a stranger up the palace steps, and she took it anyway, because her legs were tired and because his hand was warm and because she was allowed exactly one weakness per lifetime and had already spent the rest.

"Good luck with your investigation," she said. "Your Majesty."

"Not yet." He smiled — that crooked, sleepless smile she'd first seen across a ledger page in a cellar that smelled of ink and secrets. "Ask me again in spring."

He walked inside. The doors closed behind him.

Beatrix remained on the steps a while longer, in the dripping dusk, listening to a kingdom un-freeze. Somewhere below, in a cellar that no longer knew her, a freed girl was writing in a stolen ledger with a stolen pen, line by line, naming a woman back into the world one entry at a time — and Beatrix felt each stroke of that pen like a hand reaching for her through dark water.

Not all the way erased, then.

One ledger, at least, still remembered.

She pulled her cloak tight, turned her face toward the mountain's edge — toward whatever a woman became when she was no one's taster, no one's keeper, and no one's prophecy — and began to walk.

Behind her, faint through the closing doors, she heard Aldric's voice, puzzled, calling for Ylva:

*"What does the ledger say? Show me. What does it say?"*
