## 20. The Girl Who Keeps the Ledgers

The first thing Ylva learned about being mortal again was that her feet hurt.

A century beneath the mountain had given her many skills — patience, perfect recall, the ability to hold a conversation with a mirrored version of herself without flinching — but it had not prepared her for the simple treachery of walking on legs that were *new*, in a sense. They had not been used properly in a hundred years. The under-court did not require its wick to stroll about. The under-court required its wick to burn.

Now she was unlit, and unfrozen, and her boots — borrowed from a dead woman's cellar in the capital, because the living world was full of such small generosities and horrors tangled together — pinched at the heel.

"You're limping," said the nameless woman beside her.

"I am *adjusting*," Ylva corrected. "There is a difference. A limp implies something is wrong. Nothing is wrong. I have simply been asleep for a hundred years and my heels disagree with the concept of ground."

"That does sound like a limp."

"It sounds like an argument I am losing slowly."

The woman — who had no name anymore, which Ylva found both unbearable and, privately, a little funny, since Ylva herself had spent a century as *the wick* and knew exactly how it felt to be reduced to a function — glanced sideways at her. Her face was still learning how to be a face. Ylva watched it happen day by day: expressions arriving late, hesitating, then settling into place like furniture moved into a new house. She smiled now more than she had in the mountain's shadow. She laughed once, two nights ago, at Ylva's impression of the under-king's herald, and the laugh had startled them both so badly they'd walked in silence for half a mile after.

Her name had been Beatrix Eriksen. Every ledger in the kingdom said so except one, and the one that mattered had been paid out of existence. When people asked — and they asked, in villages, at wells, whenever two strangers with snow-burned cheeks and a third companion too pale for sunlight came through — the woman would pause a beat too long and say nothing at all, and Ylva would step in with a lie so smooth it embarrassed her: *she's my cousin, from the coast; you know how coastal families are, quiet.*

Ylva had decided, somewhere on the ascent between the mountain's mouth and the thawing world, that this was intolerable. Not the lying. Lying was easy; she'd listened to a hundred years of courtiers' lies through the palace mirrors and could perform them in seven regional accents. What was intolerable was the arithmetic of it. A debt had been paid. Debts paid must be recorded. That was the entire moral architecture of civilization, and Ylva had had a very long time to think about moral architecture, there in the dark with the winter pouring through her.

Someone had to write the girl back down.

* * *

The Guildhall stood open.

That was the first strange thing. Ylva remembered — secondhand, through mirrors, through the whispers of debts passing overhead like weather — when the Guild of Impossible Things had kept its doors shut to everyone but the desperate and the damned. Now the great oak doors hung wide in the morning cold, and the cellar stairs below were crowded with clerks hauling armfuls of soft ledgers into the light, where they were being sorted onto long tables like patients in a hospital ward.

"Are we burning them?" Ylva asked the nearest clerk, a round young man with ink to his elbows and the harried look of someone who had recently survived either a war or an audit.

"Cataloguing," he said. "Prince Aldric's orders. Well — King Aldric's, now, though he keeps forgetting to answer to it. Every debt the Guild ever held is to be read, verified, and struck if it can't be justified." He paused, eyeing them. "You're not here to file a claim, are you? We're terribly behind."

"We're here to see Guildmaster Oren," the nameless woman said.

The clerk's face did something complicated. "Guildmaster Oren is," he began, and then seemed to decide honesty was the policy of the new administration, "in the old record vault, arguing with a lock. It has been three days. He refuses to admit the lock has won. Follow the smell of pipe smoke and wounded pride."

They found him exactly where described: a gaunt old man in a coat too heavy for spring, standing before a vault door with a crowbar and the expression of a scholar betrayed by grammar. He heard their boots on the stone and spoke without turning around.

"If you've come to tell me the lock is rusted shut and I should use the key, the key melted when I burned the soft ledgers, and I will hear no lectures on planning."

"We've come for the hard ledger," said the nameless woman.

Oren turned then. His eyes found her, and Ylva watched an old man's whole face rearrange itself around grief and relief in roughly equal measure. "You," he said. "You look terrible."

"I've been told I'm dead in every record in the kingdom. It's been a demanding month."

"The hard ledger isn't here." Oren tapped the crowbar against the vault door, gently, the way one taps a stubborn mule. "It's downstairs, in the reading room, being read aloud line by line by a king who cannot sleep and a clerk who cannot stop crying. But—" he held up a finger before she could speak— "I know why you've come. Word travels. You want what's left of the Guild's authority. Its book. Its seal." He looked at her for a long moment, and Ylva saw him weigh something, the way he must have weighed things his whole crooked life. "You understand what you're asking. That ledger made the debt real. Whoever holds it holds the power to say what is owed in this kingdom."

"Yes," she said.

"And you'd hand that kind of thing to a girl with no name?"

"Not to me," said the nameless woman, and stepped aside, and suddenly Ylva was standing alone in the space between them with everyone looking at her.

"Oh," said Ylva. "Oh, that's — hm. I hadn't rehearsed anything."

* * *

Here is what a hundred years as the winter's living wick teaches you, if you are paying attention, and Ylva had nothing to do down there but pay attention:

It teaches you that a soul is mostly memory, and memory is mostly *record*. The under-court had kept her aware on purpose — awareness was the fuel — and so she had remembered everything: her mother's kitchen, the famine year, the taste of real bread, the exact weight of the coin the crown never paid her family. She had recited her own life to herself every single day, like a clerk balancing books, because the alternative was to let the dark edit her.

She would not let anyone else be edited. Not while she held a pen.

The reading room was long and low-lit, and it smelled of tallow and old vellum and, faintly, of the mountain — some things cling. At the far end sat a young man Ylva recognized from mirrors: Aldric, crowned three days past, still wearing his crown like a hat he'd borrowed. Beside him a clerk wept quietly over a page and transcribed anyway, which Ylva thought showed real professional commitment.

"—entry four thousand and twelve," Aldric read aloud, his voice rough from days of this. "*One winter coat, owed to the family of Ansdottir, taken in error, never returned.* Stricken. Paid from the treasury. Next." He looked up and saw them, and his face did the thing faces kept doing around the nameless woman — reaching for recognition, finding none, and choosing warmth anyway. "You're back," he said. "Good. Good." And then, carefully casual, the way a man asks about weather he is terrified of: "Any change?"

"No," she said.

"Right." He nodded twice, fast, and went back to the ledger, and Ylva saw his knuckles whiten on the pen, and understood, with the particular ache of someone who had watched a century of other people's loves through glass, exactly what was being carried in that room and how silently.

Oren set the hard ledger on the table before Ylva. It was smaller than she expected. Most terrible things were.

"The Guild is dissolved," Oren said. "By royal decree, effective yesterday, though I told Aldric if he dissolved us any faster I'd haunt him, and I'm not dead yet, so he'll simply have to wait. What remains is this book and the obligation it represents. Someone must keep the true record. Someone must write down what is owed and what is paid, so that no crown, no court, no clever man under a mountain can ever again pretend a debt doesn't exist because it's written nowhere." He slid it toward her. "You kept a fire lit for a hundred years. Keeping a book lit should be within your abilities."

"It's a different kind of burning," Ylva said, but her hands were already on the cover.

"There's blank pages at the back," Oren said. "New entries go in the same hand as old ones, or the book sulks. Don't ask how I know. Write well."

* * *

She wrote the first entry that night, in the small room above the tavern where the three of them had collapsed like dropped puppets. The nameless woman slept in the bed — actually slept, deeply, trustingly, which was new and which Ylva guarded jealously — and Aldric had gone back to the palace only after standing in the doorway for a full minute without saying goodbye, which was its own kind of confession.

Ylva sat by the candle and opened the ledger to the first clean page and found her hand shaking.

Because here was the trouble, the beautiful, enormous trouble: an entry had a shape. *Debtor. Creditor. Terms. Status.* That was easy. But what she wanted to write was not a debt. It was a person. And the Guild's cold little grammar had never once, in all its centuries of souls and favors and impossible bargains, been asked to carry something warm.

She tried anyway.

*Beatrix Eriksen,* she wrote, and stopped, because writing it felt like setting a bird down on a branch and holding your breath.

*Daughter of Eriksen of the Weavers' Row. Taster to the crown. Keeper of ledgers. She took poison into her own body for nine years, by choice, planning her own death as gently as a garden, and instead used that poisoned tongue to save a prince, expose a court, and walk willingly into the dark to pay a debt she did not owe.*

*Status:* Ylva's pen hovered.

Not *paid in full*. That wasn't right — paid in full meant finished, closed, filed away. Beatrix Eriksen was not finished. Beatrix Eriksen was asleep one floor below her, drooling slightly on a pillow, alive and unbearably mortal and missing one name.

*Status: ongoing,* she wrote. *This account remains open. The creditor is the world. Payment continues daily.*

Then, because the ledger deserved honesty even if the Guild had never practiced it, she added in smaller script:

*Recorded by Ylva Ansdottir, who owes this woman everything and intends to itemize.*

* * *

It became a discipline, over the following weeks. Each evening, one entry. Small things at first, because the large things were too heavy to lift alone:

*Entry the second. Today she ate two honeycakes and pretended not to enjoy them, then asked the baker for the recipe "for a friend." There is no friend. She wants the recipe.*

*Entry the ninth. She laughed at my impression of the under-king again. She pretends she doesn't find me funny. The ledger knows the truth.*

*Entry the fifteenth. She stood at the harbor for an hour watching the ice break up on the river. When the first floe cracked, she said, "Good," very quietly, the way one says good riddance to an enemy. I did not ask. Some entries write themselves later.*

And larger things, when she could bear them:

*Entry the twenty-second. She remembers her mother's voice some mornings and not others. On the bad mornings she stands very still in the middle of the room, listening for something that was erased along with her name. On those mornings I describe her mother to her — from her own accounts, before the feast of regrets took the rest. She says, "Yes. That's right." It is not enough. I am keeping a list of everything that is not enough, and it is the longest ledger I have ever kept.*

Aldric found out on the twenty-third entry. He arrived at the tavern at dusk, uncrowned, carrying a paper-wrapped bundle of sweet buns like a man attempting bribery, and caught Ylva mid-sentence with her pen in the air.

"What are you writing?"

"None of your business," said Ylva, with the serenity of the truly guilty.

He leaned. He read. His face went very still, the way it had in the reading room when he reached the entries about children taken in error. Then he said, quietly, "She doesn't remember her mother's voice?"

"Some days."

"Does she remember—" he stopped. Started again, more carefully, a man handling his own heart to check it was still beating. "Is there an entry about me?"

"There are eleven entries about you," said Ylva. "Mostly unflattering."

"Read me one."

"*Entry the nineteenth. The king visited again, bringing buns, ostensibly to discuss the abolition of the taster's office. He discussed it for four minutes and then spent an hour helping fix the shutter that was not broken. The shutter was not broken. He broke it first, so that he would have something to fix.*"

Aldric stared at her. "You wouldn't dare put that in a formal ledger."

"It's already inked."

"I'll abolish your Guild."

"You don't have a Guild. I *am* the Guild. Abolishing me requires paperwork, and I keep the paperwork." She dipped her pen. "Sit down, Your Majesty. If you're going to loom, you may as well dictate. Do you have anything to add to today's entry?"

He sat. He thought about it for a long time, turning the sweet bun over in his hands, and finally said: "Tell her — no. Tell the ledger, I mean. Tell the ledger that the office of royal food-taster is abolished by decree, and that the last person to hold it was the bravest servant the crown ever had, and that the crown knows it, even if the crown—" his voice caught, just barely, a hairline crack in good masonry— "even if the crown can't remember her face."

Ylva wrote it down. All of it. Even the crack.

* * *

On the forty-first evening, the nameless woman found the ledger.

Ylva had meant to hide it better. She had hidden it under a loose floorboard with all the skill of a girl who had spent a century with nothing to hide, which is to say: the woman tripped the board within ninety seconds of looking for a spare candle.

She sat on the floor with the open book in her lap for a long time. Ylva kept her eyes on her own needlework and let her.

"This is me," the woman said at last.

"That's you."

"I don't remember being most of this."

"You don't have to. That's rather the point. Memory lives outside the head too, if someone is stubborn enough to keep writing it down." Ylva turned a page. "Here. Entry the fourth. *She is kind to horses and suspicious of ducks, and no one, including her, knows why.* Does that ring anything?"

A pause. Then, slowly, like sun coming through thawing ice: "Ducks." A frown. "There was... a courtyard. At the palace. A duck bit me. For years. Every morning." Her hand drifted to her ankle, an old reflex reaching for an old scar. "It hated me specifically."

"The ledger remembers."

"The ledger," the woman repeated. She looked up, and her face was doing something new — not the careful practiced expressions, but something rawer, younger, a girl's face, Eriksen's daughter's face. "Why? You were the one in the dark for a hundred years. You're owed a lifetime of other people keeping *your* records. Why spend it on me?"

Ylva considered several answers. The true one was simplest, so she gave it.

"Because a hundred years ago, people wrote me down as *one soul, sold, terms fulfilled*, and closed the book, and everyone agreed that was the end of me. One line. Filed. Done." She took the ledger gently from the woman's hands. "I know exactly what it costs to be one line in someone else's accounting. So no." She opened to the newest page. "Not one line. Not for you. You get volumes. You get marginalia. You get the duck. When you're eighty and you've forgotten your own birthday, I will still have it, in ink, with witnesses. That is my trade, and unlike everyone else in this miserable history of trades, mine is freely given."

The woman was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in a voice that suggested she was testing the words' weight: "Beatrix."

"Yes."

"Beatrix Eriksen."

"Debt paid in full," Ylva agreed, "and account remaining open."

* * *

Spring came properly in the sixth week, the way it always does — rudely, all at once, mud everywhere. The roads cleared. The river ran loud. And one bright morning a horse stood in the tavern yard that Ylva recognized, because she recognized its rider from mirrors and from broken shutters and from eleven unflattering ledger entries.

Aldric dismounted, looked up at the window, and did not come inside. He stood in the yard like a man rehearsing, which — having watched a century of suitors and ambassadors and one extremely memorable ghost do precisely that through the under-court's glass — Ylva could diagnose at fifty paces.

"He'll stand there until noon," Ylva observed, threading her needle. "Should I write an entry about it now, or wait for the outcome?"

From upstairs came the sound of a window opening, and then the nameless woman's voice — Beatrix's voice, dry as good paper and twice as sharp:

"How long are you going to stand down there pretending you rode all this way to inspect the gutters?"

"...The gutters are structurally concerning," said the King of the Realm.

"Get off your horse before someone sees you, you enormous idiot. Ylva! Put the kettle on. And—" a pause, and Ylva heard it, the small returning thing, the self coming back one line at a time— "and bring the ledger. Something tells me tonight deserves an entry."

Ylva smiled, set down her needlework, and reached for her pen — because the girl who keeps the ledgers already knew how this chapter ended, but had learned, at terrible cost, never to trust a story that hadn't been written down.
