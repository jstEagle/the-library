## 21. The Taster's New Tongue

The first thing Aldric did as king was abolish an office that had existed for four hundred years.

He did it at his own coronation feast, which everyone agreed was either very brave or very rude, depending on how much they had invested in the continued poisoning of royalty.

"My lords and ladies," he said, standing before a table laden with enough food to feed the eastern quarter for a month, "you will notice there is no taster present tonight."

A silence went through the hall like a draft through a badly hung door. Somewhere near the back, old Lord Vessik clutched his wine cup as though it might turn on him.

"There is no taster," Aldric continued, "because there will be no more tasting. The office is dissolved. The ledgers of debts it served are burned or sealed — I have seen to both. And from this night forward, if any man or woman in this kingdom wishes me dead, they will have to do it the honest way: with an army, or a very long fall, or by talking to me until I lose the will to live."

That last one got a laugh, which he had been fishing for. His chancellor, Mistress Corvel, did not laugh. She stood at his elbow with her hands folded and her face arranged into the expression of a woman who had already drafted three objections and was deciding which one would cost her least.

"Your Majesty," she murmured during the applause, "the taster's office protected six kings."

"The taster's office *ate* six tasters," Aldric said quietly. "I've read the hard ledger, Chancellor. I know what the crown paid, and I know what it bought. We are done buying."

She studied him for a long moment. He had the uncomfortable sense that she was tasting *him* — weighing him against some private standard of poisons — before she inclined her head a fraction of an inch.

"Then we will need a new way to keep you alive, sire."

"Yes," said Aldric, whose dreams had been full of snow for three months running. "We will."

* * *

The thaw came slowly, the way forgiveness does.

It began in the valleys, where the rivers shrugged off their ice with a sound like furniture being moved in a house above them, and worked its way up the mountain slopes through the spring. By midsummer the capital smelled of wet stone and cut grass instead of smoke and frost, and children who had never seen mud were discovering it in quantities their mothers despaired of.

Aldric discovered other things too.

He discovered that he could not remember the face of the woman who had saved his life at the winter feast. This was not unusual in itself — the whole court had gone strange about her, names sliding off her like rain off waxed cloth, portraits of the trial coming back from the painters with a smudge where she should have been. What was unusual was that he remembered everything *around* her with perfect, merciless clarity. He remembered the taste of the poison on his own tongue, copper and wintergreen. He remembered the sound of her voice saying *don't swallow* — no. Wait. He didn't remember the sound. He remembered knowing the sound, the way you remember a room you dreamed once: the shape of it without the substance.

He remembered that she had been afraid of nothing except being known, and that he had known her anyway.

"You're doing it again," Ylva said.

He looked up from the map table. The Guild's former cellars had become, by slow and unlegislated degrees, his favorite room in the palace — partly because they were cool in summer, mostly because Ylva had claimed them and filled them with candles and the hard ledger, which she was teaching herself to read the way other people learned the harp, an hour a day, patiently, until it made music.

"Doing what?"

"Squeezing your eyes shut like that. Like you're trying to hold water in your fist." She turned a page. "You do it when you think about her."

"I don't think about anyone. There's no one to think about."

"Mm." Ylva's pen scratched. She had developed, in her first year above ground, a manner of agreeing with people that made them feel thoroughly disagreed with. "There's a name in here," she said, after a while. "One line. I wrote it myself, so I know it's true even though I can't make my mouth say it right. Every time I read it aloud, I hear something else. A different word. It slides."

"Does it hurt? Reading it?"

Ylva considered this with the seriousness she gave all questions, having spent a century with nothing to consider but her own thoughts. "No," she said at last. "It hurts like a scar hurts. Not now. Only when the weather changes."

"And is the weather changing?"

She looked up at him then, and her eyes were still a little too bright, a little too old, but she smiled. "It's been changing since the day you two walked out of the mountain," she said. "Go and see her, Your Majesty. You've redrawn the border roads four times this month just so they pass closer to the eastern valley. My map-reading has improved enormously out of sheer boredom."

Aldric opened his mouth to deny it, found no denial available, and closed it again.

"I can't remember why," he said. It came out smaller than he meant it to. "I don't remember her, Ylva. Whatever she was to me — whatever I was to her — it's gone. If I ride out there, what do I even bring? A crown and an apology for a face she doesn't remember either?"

Ylva put down her pen. She came around the table, and she took his hand in both of hers, and hers were warm now, truly warm, a thing she still marveled at daily.

"When I was under the mountain," she said, "I forgot the sun. Completely. A hundred years, and I couldn't have told you its color. But every time the wick flared — every time the winter deepened — some part of me leaned toward the ceiling of the world. Toward where it had been. Do you understand? You don't need the memory. You need the leaning."

* * *

He went in autumn, alone, with one escort whom he dismissed at the last village with a lie about hunting.

The road up the eastern valley was bad in the way roads are bad when nobody has needed them much — washed out in places, shy of travelers, overgrown with the particular golden grass that grew where the long winter had lain deepest and melted last. The villages here had shared one face, once; Beatrix had told him that, or someone had, or he had read it somewhere and believed it because believing things about her was the only remembering he had left. Now the faces were multiplying again, differentiating, and the villagers who saw him pass squinted at his plain riding clothes and decided he was a tax assessor, which suited him fine. Tax assessors got soup. Kings got speeches.

The house sat at the valley's head, where the mountain began in earnest: low-roofed, stone, with a garden going cheerfully to seed and a goat tied near the door with the air of an animal that had won several negotiations. Smoke rose from the chimney. Wood was stacked with a precision that made something twist in his chest, though he couldn't have said why stacked wood should feel like grief.

He stood at the gate for a long moment.

This was the part no one warned you about, he thought. Everyone talked about losing memories like losing coins — you felt the weight go from your pocket and you mourned. Nobody told you that sometimes the memory stayed behind and *you* were what got lost. That you could stand at a stranger's gate with your heart hammering like a boy's, knowing — *knowing*, in the place below memory where Ylva said the leaning lived — that the woman inside that house had died for you. Had paid a price no ledger could hold. And that to her, you were a tall young man in muddy boots who had perhaps come about the goat.

He knocked.

The door opened, and there she was.

She was smaller than he expected, which was absurd, because he hadn't expected anything. Brown hair going gray early, or maybe it had always been going gray; ink stains on her fingers that no amount of scrubbing ever quite removed, he somehow knew that too; and eyes that went through him like a taster's tongue through an unfamiliar dish — cataloguing, measuring, filing him away under something.

"Yes?" she said.

"Good morning," said the King of the Northlands, brilliantly. "I — I'm traveling. Through the valley. I wondered if I might trouble you for water."

"We have a well."

"Right. Yes. Of course. I could — use the well?"

Something flickered across her face then, quick as a fish under ice. Not recognition — he would have known recognition, he had spent months hoping for it and dreaming of it and waking from the dreams with his pillow wet. It was something older than recognition. The look of a person hearing a song they'd never been taught and finding they knew the next note anyway.

"You're not a tax assessor," she said.

"No."

"You're not a hunter. Hunters don't stand at gates looking like they're about to be executed." She stepped back and held the door open. "Come in before the goat gets ideas. She's a bad influence on visitors."

Inside, the house was one warm room and a loft, and it was full of paper. Stacked on shelves, bundled in twine, spread across the table in careful drifts — pages covered in that same precise hand he knew from the hard ledger, though he had never seen her write a word. She caught him staring.

"I keep accounts," she said, with a shrug that dared him to find it odd. "Local ones. Who owes whom what. Births, debts, favors, feuds. Someone has to, or the valley forgets itself." She poured water into a clay cup and handed it to him, and their fingers touched, and neither of them breathed for a moment that lasted approximately one season.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"That's the strange thing." She laughed, and the laugh had a crack in it, old and mended and mended wrong. "I don't have one. Oh, the village calls me the keeper, and the children call me the ink-witch, and the goat calls me — well, goats don't call you anything, they just scream. But my own name—" She turned her cup in her stained fingers. "There's a space where it should be. Like a tooth gone from a row. I can feel the shape of the gap with my tongue."

"I know about gaps," Aldric said quietly.

"Do you." Not a question. She was watching him with those cataloguing eyes, and he understood suddenly that she had been taking his measure since the gate, and that whatever she measured, she kept measuring because the numbers refused to come out right.

"I'm Aldric," he said. Just that. No title. It felt like setting down a heavy pack.

"Aldric." She tried the name the way she might try an unfamiliar spice — cautiously, on the tip of the tongue. "And why does a man named Aldric ride three days into a dying valley to drink well water with an ink-witch?"

Because you leaned toward the ceiling of the world for a hundred years, he did not say. Because I read a page in a ledger that makes my throat close. Because at my coronation I swore an oath to a kingdom and half of it, secretly, was to you.

"Because I couldn't stay away," he said. "I've tried. I've genuinely tried. I drew up schedules."

That startled a real laugh out of her — short, rusty, surprised at itself. "Schedules."

"Very rigorous ones. I'm told I'm disciplined."

"You're told correctly, I think." She looked at him across the table, across the drifts of accounted lives, and the crack in her laugh was in her voice now too. "Here's my problem, Aldric. Everything in me says I know you. Not remember — *know*. The way you know a taste you can't name. And everything I can prove says we've never met, because I'd have written you down. I write down everyone." She tapped the table. "So either you're the strangest coincidence of my life, or—"

"Or," Aldric said, "someone erased us. Both of us. From each other."

The fire popped. Outside, the goat screamed at something, possibly the horizon.

"That," said the woman without a name, slowly, "would explain the gap."

* * *

He stayed the afternoon. Then, because the light failed and the pass was dangerous, the evening. Then, because she made a stew involving mushrooms she identified with a taster's casual authority — *that one kills you, that one only wishes it could* — and because the conversation ran like a river finding its old bed, he stayed the night in the loft, and woke to frost on the rafters and the smell of bread, and realized with a lurch that he had slept without dreaming of snow for the first time in a year.

He left. He came back. He left again. He was, he reflected on the third ride east, drawing up schedules again, and this time he wasn't pretending they were for the border roads.

On the fourth visit, she met him at the gate with a ledger under her arm and an expression of determined business.

"I've been thinking about the gap," she said, without greeting. "About names. Mine isn't the only one missing, is it. Yours has holes too — I've watched you reach for things and find nothing. Your mother, I think. Something about a feast. Something about—" she hesitated, "—a promise you made in the dark under a mountain."

Aldric went very still.

"So here is what I propose," she continued, in the tone of a woman balancing books that would not balance, "since we are apparently two halves of an account that someone tore in two. You remember the shape of what I lost. I remember the shape of what you lost. Between us—" she held up the ledger, and he saw that the first page was blank, waiting, "—between us, we might reconstruct enough to know what we're mourning. Line by line. Debt by debt. I'm told I'm good at debts."

"Who told you that?"

"A feeling," she said. "An extremely persistent feeling."

He took the ledger. She gave him the pen. Their fingers touched again, and this time neither of them pretended it was an accident, and the gap between them — the torn place, the erased page — seemed, for one breath, to narrow.

"Start anywhere," she said softly.

Aldric uncapped the ink, and wrote the first line of what would take them all winter and most of a spring:

*Once, there was a taster who recorded everything and felt nothing — until a prince taught her that some debts are paid in names.*

Behind them, unnoticed, the goat ate the corner of the old hard ledger's spare copy, which Ylva would later declare was the most thorough audit the Guild had ever received.
