## 22. A Ledger with One Blank Page

The house at the mountain's edge had been a keeper's cottage once, back when the mountain road had traffic worth keeping. Now its roof sagged like an old man's shoulders and its chimney leaned a degree or two east of honest, but the hearth drew well and the door shut against the wind, and Beatrix had learned during her first winter there that a door that shuts is worth more than a roof that flatters.

She had also learned — slowly, in the way one learns a language by living in it rather than studying it — who she was.

Not all at once. That was the trouble. She knew *how* to do a hundred things without knowing where she'd learned them. She could read a ledger at candle-end distance in the dark. She could hold a spoon of soup to her lips, breathe it in over her tongue, and name every bitter thing hiding in it — though she hadn't needed the skill since the thaw, since the office of royal taster was abolished by royal decree on the new king's second day on the throne, and good riddance, said everyone who had ever watched a taster eat and wondered what it cost them. She knew the weight of a quill that had been held for hours. She knew that she did not like being thanked, and that she liked even less being pitied, and that she could not remember why either felt like a hand on the back of her neck.

Her name she learned the way you learn a word in a foreign tongue: as something true, but not yet *yours*.

*Beatrix Eriksen.* Written in a careful, looping hand on the first page of a ledger she kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath a loose floorboard. Written many times after that, in many entries, because Ylva had made herself a promise: *one line at a time, until the world says it right.*

This morning, as on most mornings, Beatrix fed the fire, put the kettle on, and read.

---

**Entry the four-hundredth-and-something**, said the ledger, in Ylva's hand. **Beatrix Eriksen does not snore, which I find suspicious in a human. Nobody is that quiet while sleeping unless they are practicing.**

"I was not *practicing*," Beatrix told the page, as she did most mornings. "I was resting economically."

**Entry: Beatrix Eriksen once drank poison on purpose to prove a point. I have asked her what point. She says she cannot remember the point, only that it was excellent.**

"That," said Beatrix, setting down the ledger, "is unfortunately consistent with everything else I know about myself."

Because that was the shape of her life now: a woman assembling herself from testimony. She knew she had saved a king's life, because the whole kingdom knew it, more or less — though the kingdom's version had no name attached, just *the taster*, and then, after the trial and the treason and the reading of the debts, just *the woman*, and then, eventually, nothing at all, the story wearing smooth like a stone in a river until only the shape remained. She knew she had gone under the mountain. She knew she had come back up with a girl named Ylva and had paid something so large that every record of it had dissolved like sugar in hot water — every record but this ledger, and one other, which we will come to.

She knew she had chosen it. That was the strangest part, the part she turned over at night like a coin she couldn't spend. Ylva's entries were clear on this point, entry after entry, as if Ylva feared the truth might evaporate if she didn't write it down:

**Entry: She chose. Ask her anything else and she'll shrug, but write this down twice. She CHOSE. Nobody made her. The prophecy wanted her consent and she gave it, but on her terms, and the terms broke the thing. I saw her do it. I was a hundred years dead-ish at the time and I still saw it.**

"Twice, is it," Beatrix murmured. "You're going to wear out the ink."

But she read it again anyway. Some mornings she read it three times, the way another woman might take a dram.

There was a knock at the door.

---

Only one person knocked like that — politely, and then, when no answer came fast enough, politely again with slightly more insistence, as though courtesy were a siege engine he was reluctant to deploy. Beatrix wiped her hands, looked through the shutter out of pure habit, and saw him standing in the thin spring light with snowmelt to his knees and his hair doing whatever it wanted, which was, historically, everything.

The King. Aldric. Though up here he was neither; up here he was a man who rode three days from the capital under a plain cloak to eat soup with a woman whose name he had to relearn every time, and who never once let that show as a burden.

She opened the door. He looked at her the way he always did: recognition arriving a half-beat late, like thunder after lightning.

"You're real," he said, which was his greeting, every visit. As if each time he feared she'd turn out to be a story the mountain told.

"And you're soaked," she said. "In. Boots off. If you track mud across my floor I'll set the ledger on you."

"The ledger," he said, working at his boots, "is a serious threat when you say it. You know that? Most people threaten swords. You threaten *bookkeeping*. And I believe you every time."

"Bookkeeping toppled your father's whole court."

"It did." He sat by the fire, dripping, accepting the soup she put into his hands without looking, the way a man accepts a thing he has been given many times and never expects. "It toppled my father's court and abolished an office older than the crown and unmade a debt a century deep, and it was all done by a woman whose name I have written on a paper in my breast pocket so I don't lose it between visits. Which I check. Frequently. Like a talisman."

"Does it help?"

"No," he said cheerfully. "I look at it and think, *why do I have this*, and then I remember, and then it helps enormously. It's the remembering that does the work. The paper just holds still long enough for the remembering to catch up."

Beatrix sat opposite him and did not say the thing she always wanted to say, which was: *Tell me. Tell me what I was to you.* She didn't ask because Ylva had told her, once, gently, in the tone of someone reporting weather that had already happened: *He loved you. Or was loving you. Or was going to. The order got jumbled somewhere around the treason.*

Instead she said, "Soup's venison. Don't make that face. There's nothing in it."

"There's always something in it when you cook. That's what I like. Everyone else's food is just food. Yours is a *decision*."

They ate. Outside, the mountain dripped. Somewhere below in the valley the thaw was turning the river brown and loud, and the world — freed of its century of interest payments — was carrying on in the reckless, muddy, extravagant way that worlds do when they've been let off a debt.

"Aldric," she said, when the bowls were empty. "Why do you keep coming?"

He considered this with the seriousness he brought to very few things. Honest answers were, after all, the first impossible thing he had ever bought, and he had never lost the taste.

"Because the ledgers say I swore something," he said. "Ylva wrote it down. 'Entry: he promised to call her back.' And I called you back, apparently. From under the mountain. And ever since—" he turned his bowl in his hands— "ever since I've felt like a man who paid off a debt and keeps returning to the shop anyway. Looking in the window. Wondering if they'll sell him whatever it was he actually wanted."

"And what did you actually want?"

"I don't know yet," he said. "That's the honest answer. First one's still free, by the way. Guild rates."

"The Guild is ash."

"The Guild," said Aldric, "is a girl with red ink-stained fingers and a hard ledger and a very alarming amount of administrative energy, and last month she wrote to inform me that the crown owes her office supplies."

Despite himself — despite everything — Beatrix laughed. It surprised her coming out. Some things survived the erasure. Her laugh, apparently, had been too small to bother striking from the record.

---

* * *

After he left — riding south with the plain cloak and the paper in his pocket and a jar of Ylva's plum preserves he pretended he hadn't hoped for — Beatrix climbed to the shelf above the hearth and took down the box.

Two things lived in it. The first was Ylva's ledger, the naming-book, the record that had refused to dissolve. The second was thinner: a single quire of good paper, sewn into boards, blank from first leaf to last.

A ledger with one blank page, Ylva had called it, when she'd left it on the table a year ago with the particular ceremony she reserved for things she refused to explain. *"Oren kept a hard ledger. The under-king kept a debt-ledger. Your ancestor kept a prophecy. Everyone in this story kept books about other people, and look how that went. This one's yours. Write in it what you choose. Or don't. But it should exist, so the next person knows a book can be blank on purpose."*

For a year, Beatrix had not touched it.

Not because she feared it. Because she had spent her whole recorded life writing other people down — their poisons, their debts, their secrets, their crimes — and she had a suspicion, growing in her like frost on a window, that the reason she couldn't remember herself was not entirely the price she'd paid. Some of it, maybe, was that she had never been in her own books at all. *Record everything, feel nothing.* Someone had written that rule. She was fairly sure, from the ache it gave her, that it had been herself.

Tonight she cleared the table, set out ink, and opened the blank ledger to its first page.

The pen hovered.

Here was the difficulty nobody warned you about. It was easy to write a king — kings arrived with their crimes already itemized. It was easy to write a debt — debts came pre-loaded with numbers. But a page with nothing owed on it, nothing owed *by* it, no inheritance, no prophecy, no ancestral failure to redeem —

"What am I, then?" she said to the room. "If not taster. Not traitor. Not keeper."

The fire popped. The chimney, leaning east of honest, drew anyway. And Beatrix Eriksen, who had tasted every poison in the known world and survived them all by deciding to, began to write — not the record of anyone else, but the inventory of a single surviving soul, taken honestly, in her own hand.

*I remember,* she wrote. *Which is to say: I know. I know that I was patient, and that my patience was a weapon I mistook for a personality. I know that I kept accounts because counting a thing makes it survivable, and I counted myself least of all. I know that I planned, for years, a quiet exit by increments, and that in the end I exited loudly instead, in front of the whole court, and that the loud version saved a man I cannot remember saving and I would do it again, which means the memory was never the point. The choosing was the point.*

*I know that I like plum preserves and dislike being thanked. I know that I can hold a grudge like a note of debt and that I have, as of tonight, exactly one outstanding: against the under-king, for keeping a girl named Ylva as a lamp for a hundred years. He is welcome to try to collect it. I have references.*

*And I know this: a name struck from every ledger is not a name erased. It is a name returned to its owner. Whoever held it before — the crown, the Guild, the prophecy, the pitying mouths of the capital — they have all surrendered it. What remains is mine to spend. And I have decided what it buys.*

She dipped the pen again. Outside, the mountain went on dripping its long thaw, patient as interest, and somewhere far south a king was checking a paper in his pocket and finding, as always, that the remembering caught up.

The last line, when she finally wrote it, was short. It was not a title. It was not a debt. It was not a prophecy, and it promised nothing to anyone beneath any mountain. It was simply the fact of her, entered at last in the only book that mattered, in a hand that matched no one else's in the world:

*Beatrix Eriksen. Keeper of one blank page. Debt: none. Balance carried forward.*

She blew on the ink, closed the cover, and left the rest of the book open behind it — every page after the first standing white and waiting, the way a road waits, the way a morning does.

Then she banked the fire, set the kettle for tomorrow, and went to bed, practicing her breathing economically, and dreamed — for the first time in a year — of spring.
