## 1. The Charm Seller's Hour

The trick to selling charms in a city that had outlawed magic was to make them useless.

Coral Delacroix had learned this her first winter in Vellamare, when she'd made the mistake of selling a luck-charm that actually worked. The fisherman who bought it came back three days later with a full hold and a hungry look, asking what else she could do. She'd packed her stall before dawn and hadn't seen him since. Now every charm she sold was a small, honest nothing: river-stones wrapped in copper wire, knots tied in red thread over a whispered word that meant *nothing at all*, glass beads polished until they caught the lamplight like captured stars.

Placebos. Pretty ones.

"Protection for the home," she told a woman with flour on her apron, holding up a knotted cord. "Hang it over the door."

"Does it work?"

"It works exactly as much as you believe it does." Which was true, in its way. Belief was the only magic left legal in Vellamare, and Coral sold it by the yard.

The woman bought two.

The Saltrow night market smelled of fried dough and brine and the tallow smoke of a hundred lanterns, and Coral loved it the way you love something you're planning to lose. Twenty years she'd kept this stall, twenty years of harmless charms and careful smiles, and in all that time she had never once slipped. Not really. Not dangerously.

She let herself slip now, just slightly, because the cat was back.

It came on Thursdays—a lean grey thing with a torn ear and the posture of an aristocrat in exile—and it sat beneath her table where the customers couldn't see, staring up at her with eyes like struck flint. Coral crouched as if adjusting a crate, and under the cover of market noise, barely a breath of sound:

*You again.*

*Fish,* said the cat, which was all it ever said, but the way it said it carried whole paragraphs of grievance and trust.

*I don't have fish. I have charm-cord. You can't eat charm-cord.*

*A lie. You smell of herring from the stall behind.*

*That's not mine.* But she broke off a corner of her supper bread, soaked in the drippings she'd been saving, and set it down where the cat's dignity would allow it to pretend it had simply found the food itself. The cat ate with its back half-turned, which was how cats accepted kindness.

"You talk to that animal."

Coral's heart performed a maneuver she felt in her teeth.

She looked up. A man stood at the front of her stall—tall, dark-haired, with ink stains on his fingers and the particular rumpled warmth of someone who spent his days indoors among paper. He wasn't looking at her with suspicion. That was the strange part. He was looking at her the way people look at a door they've always assumed was painted on.

"I was telling it to leave," she said. "Cats don't listen."

"No," he agreed. "They don't." He picked up one of her river-stone charms, turned it in the light. "These are lovely. Completely useless, I assume?"

"That's the honest inventory, yes."

He laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and Coral felt the danger of it settle somewhere under her ribs. Twenty years of discipline, undone by a man who laughed like he meant it.

"Ivo," he said, offering his hand. "I work at the archive. The Choir's archive, so if that ruins the charm of the evening, I apologize in advance."

"Coral." His hand was warm and ink-smudged. "And no. Everyone serves somebody in Vellamare. I serve people who want to believe in knots."

"And what do you believe in?"

*Carefully. Invisibly. In nothing that can be burned.* "In getting through Thursday," she said.

He stayed. That was the trouble—he stayed, buying a stone charm he clearly didn't need, then a second one, then leaning against her stall frame while the market thinned around them, talking about cataloguing systems with the enthusiasm other men reserved for sailing or war. He told her about a folio from the second century whose marginalia argued with itself. He asked her questions and, more dangerously, listened to the answers.

"You've lived here long?" he asked.

"All my life."

"I'd remember you. From somewhere. School, maybe, or—" He stopped himself, frowning faintly, as though his own memory were a misfiled document. "Strange. You feel familiar. Like a book I read years ago and never finished."

Coral smiled the smile she used on customs officers and worried mothers. "You must be thinking of my charms. They have that effect."

But inside, old fear stirred and stretched. *Familiar.* Familiar was how it started—the neighbor who noticed your lamp burned late, the priest who remembered your grandmother's name. Familiar was the first step toward *known*, and known was the first step toward the pyre.

Yet when he said goodnight—pressing coins into her hand for charms worth a fraction of it, promising to come back next Thursday—she found herself watching him go down the lantern-lit row, tall and unguarded, and feeling something she hadn't permitted herself in twenty years.

Wanting to be known.

* * *

After the market closed, Coral walked home the long way, along the seawall, because the long way passed fewer windows.

Vellamare at night was a city pretending to sleep. Behind shuttered doors, people did their small illegal things—prayed to the wrong saints, kept portraits of grandmothers who'd been witches, hummed tunes the Choir had banned for sounding too much like spells. Coral knew this because the rats told her, and the rats heard everything.

She didn't use the gift. That was the discipline. Using it was a muscle that wanted exercise, and muscles grew, and growing things got noticed. Twice a year, perhaps, she allowed herself a real conversation—an injured gull, once a dying horse whose owner wept and never knew why the animal went calm. Small mercies. Small enough to stay invisible.

But tonight the city felt different. Tonight the gulls on the seawall were restless, wheeling and crying in patterns that weren't hunger. And when she paused at the breakwater, the rats came—not one or two, but a dozen, lining the stones like a congregation.

Coral looked around. Empty coast, dark water, no windows near.

*Well?* she asked.

The eldest rat, scarred and grey-muzzled, stepped forward. Its voice in her mind was dry as paper.

*Something is waking, speaker. Something under the bells. The deep things are stirring, and the two-legs in grey robes are asking questions in the tunnels.*

*What kind of questions?*

*Old ones. About names. About faces.*

Coral's mouth went dry. *Whose faces?*

The rat looked at her for a long moment with black bead eyes that held, she thought, something almost like pity.

*Yours,* it said. *And another's. We saw the book. It smells of dust and blood and a hundred years, and your face is drawn upon its page.*

The sea crashed against the breakwater. Somewhere across the city, the Cathedral of Bells rang the midnight hour, each toll rolling over the rooftops like a slow-counted threat.

Coral stood very still, the way prey stands still, and thought: *I spoke to a cat today. I laughed with a stranger. I let myself be seen.*

*Ivo,* she thought, and didn't know yet whether the name was a comfort or a warning—or both, wound together like the threads she sold to people who needed to believe in something.

Behind her, unnoticed, a grey cat with a torn ear sat on the seawall, watching her with flint-bright eyes—as if it had heard everything, and was deciding what to do about it.
