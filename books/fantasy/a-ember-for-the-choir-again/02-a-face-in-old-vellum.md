## 2. A Face in Old Vellum

The sealed archive of the Cathedral of Bells smelled of cold iron and older paper, and Ivo Marchetti had spent eleven years learning to love it the way you love a locked room: from the outside, mostly, with occasional permission to step in.

He was a cataloguer, third rank. It was a job built for a man who liked rules. Every book entered through him or not at all; every volume received a number, a description, a shelf; and if a text was deemed dangerous—heretical, magical, merely inconvenient—it went into the Sealed Stacks behind the iron door that required two keys and an Inquisitor's signature to open. Ivo had one key. He had never once used it alone.

Today, though, the second key belonged to Brother Anselm, who was down with a fever, and the Inquisitor's signature belonged to a chit signed in advance for routine inventory—Inquisitor Vale's neat, upright hand, *for the purpose of cataloguing only*, dated three days ahead because Seraphine Vale believed in efficiency the way other people believed in saints.

So Ivo stood alone in the Sealed Stacks with a lamp, a ledger, and four hundred years of forbidden things.

It should have frightened him more than it did. He told himself it was the familiarity. Eleven years of handling heresy teaches you its weight and shape until it becomes furniture. The grimoires with their scorched corners. The bestiaries bound in leather no tanner would claim. The confession transcripts, hundreds of them, each ending in the same tidy notation: *Purged.*

He worked shelf by shelf, left to right, as the manual prescribed. He was on the third row—the century-old acquisitions, the purge-era material—when he pulled a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth and felt something odd in his own hands.

Not magic. He'd have reported that instantly; he knew the protocol. It was stranger than magic. It was *recognition*.

The book had no title page. Its cover was plain calfskin gone soft with age, and when he opened it the first thing he saw was color—an illumination so vivid it seemed to breathe against all that brown vellum around it. A portrait. A man standing at the mouth of a burning city, holding a lantern, and the man had—

Ivo set the book down. He picked it up again. He looked at his reflection in the darkened window glass across the aisle, then back at the page.

The man in the painting had his face.

Not approximately. Not the way relatives resemble each other, not the way any dark-haired man with an angular jaw resembles another. The painter had caught the slight asymmetry of his brow, the scar he'd carried since childhood on his left knuckle—rendered there, on the painted hand holding the lantern, in miniature, exact. The eyes were his gray. The mouth, half-turned in something between apology and defiance, was the mouth he saw in mirrors and disliked.

His hands were steady. That surprised him. He noticed their steadiness the way you notice a strange calm in a burning room.

*Catalogue it,* said the training. *Description: illuminated manuscript, untitled, portrait of unidentified male subject.* His pen hovered over the ledger. Unidentified. He wrote nothing at all.

He turned the page.

A second portrait faced the first: a woman. She stood in the same burning city, or perhaps a different one—fire made cities interchangeable—and she was turned slightly toward the man, as though listening for him. Her hair was the color of coral, of embers banked in ash, of things you are not supposed to see and cannot stop looking at. Her expression was not afraid. That was the detail that undid him. Everyone in Choir iconography who stood near fire was afraid. This woman looked like she was waiting for the fire to make up its mind.

Ivo knew her too.

He had known her for six days. Long enough to have walked her home from the night market twice, long enough to know she sold charms that couldn't possibly work and lied about it beautifully, long enough to know the exact shade her hair went under lamplight—coral, ember, ash—and long enough to understand that he was in trouble, because he had started arranging his days around the possibility of seeing her.

Her name was Coral Delacroix. And she was painted in a hundred-year-old book in the sealed archive of the church whose whole purpose was making sure people like her never existed.

He sat down on the floor of the Sealed Stacks, which was against regulations, and read.

* * *

The text was a prophecy. He knew what those were supposed to look like—the Choir kept genuine ones in reliquary cases, vague and grandiose, full of thrones and stars. This was not that. This was specific in a way that felt less like vision than like memory. It named a city. Vellamare. It named a war, and it named its kindlers:

*Two shall light the pyre: the speaker who hears the voiceless, and the keeper of the buried word. Between them the bells shall break, and the old blood rise, and the Choir be brought to ash.*

Below, in a later hand—a curator's hand, the kind Ivo himself used—someone had added marginalia. Dates. Names. Cross-references to purge records. As though some archivist a hundred years ago had found this book and done exactly what Ivo was doing now: tried to file the end of the world.

And at the back, a final illumination. The two figures again, but this time the city behind them was whole, unburned, and the bells of the Cathedral hung cracked down the middle, and small bright shapes—he squinted, tilted the lamp—wheeled above the rooftops. Winged shapes. Creatures that did not exist anymore, except in bestiaries and in the margin doodles of children who didn't know better.

The keeper of the buried word. He turned the phrase over. He worked in a building full of buried words. He had, in fact, spent his entire adult life burying them deeper.

He should take this to Inquisitor Vale. That was the procedure; that was so obviously the procedure that his hand was already halfway to writing the incident report before he caught himself. *Found: one illuminated prophecy naming current residents of Vellamare as agents of apocalypse.* He imagined the interview. He imagined Coral Delacroix's name entering a Choir ledger, and after her name, eventually, the notation he had read four hundred times today: *Purged.*

His pen stayed empty.

Instead he did something no third-rank cataloguer had ever done in the history of the archive, as far as he knew. He closed the book, wrapped it back in its oilcloth, tucked it under his arm inside his coat, and finished his shift with a falsified inventory line—*shelf 3-R, one volume, devotional miscellany, water-damaged, low priority*—in his own neat, obedient hand.

His first lie. It fit surprisingly well. That frightened him more than the portrait had.

* * *

Coral's stall was shuttered when he reached the night market, but the lamp above it was lit, which meant she was somewhere close, because Coral Delacroix did not leave lamps burning on coin she could count. He found her on the seawall behind the fishmongers' rows, sitting with her boots off, feeding scraps to a gull that had no business being this tame.

"You're late," she said, without turning around. "Archivists keep hours. You're either very dedicated or very fired."

"Neither." He sat beside her. The gull eyed him and decided he was acceptable. "I need to show you something, and I don't know how to preface it, so I'm going to just—" He unwrapped the oilcloth.

She went still in a way he hadn't seen before. Still like prey. Six days of knowing her and he had catalogued her smiles, her wit, the quick economy of her hands sorting charms—and now he learned a whole other language she spoke fluently: stillness.

"Where," she said quietly, "did you get this."

"The sealed archive. I catalogue it. Tonight I was alone, which isn't supposed to happen, and I found—" He opened to the first portrait. "That's me. I know how that sounds."

She looked. Her breath went out slowly through her nose, controlled, the way people breathe when they're managing pain. Then she reached out and touched the painted face with one fingertip, tracing his jaw, and the intimacy of it—her finger on his face, even painted, even a century old—made his chest ache absurdly.

"That's your scar," she said. "Your knuckle. How?"

"I don't know." He turned to the second portrait. "But I think you should see—"

She already had. He watched the coral-haired woman register herself on the page, watched her lips move around a word she didn't say aloud. When she finally spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, a merchant's voice, a hider's voice.

"What does the text say about us?"

He read it to her. All of it. The pyre, the kindlers, the breaking bells, the rising old blood, the Choir brought to ash. He read the marginalia too, the purge dates, and he watched her do arithmetic he couldn't follow—some private calculation involving a hundred years and a word she'd said once, lightly, at the market: *speaker*. He had assumed she'd meant speakers, orators, street performers. He no longer assumed anything.

When he finished, the gull had gone. Neither of them had noticed it leave.

"There's a last image," he said, and turned to it: the unburned city, the cracked bells, the winged creatures wheeling overhead.

Coral stared at it for a long time. The lamplight moved on her face and she let it, which he understood now was unlike her; she lived in shadow the way he lived in ledgers.

"Ivo," she said at last. "You can't ever tell anyone you showed me this. You can't tell anyone the book exists. Do you understand what the Choir does to—"

"To kindlers. Yes." He heard his own voice come out level. "I've been filing their paperwork for eleven years."

She looked at him then, really looked, and whatever she was searching for, she seemed to find it, because something in her shoulders came down half an inch.

"Why bring it to me?" she asked. "Why not burn it? Why not report it? You must have thought of both."

He had. All night, walking here, he'd held the book under his coat like a coal. "Because I wanted it to be a romance," he admitted. "A strange, terrible romance—fate, portraits, a woman I already couldn't stop thinking about. I walked here composing the speech where I tell you we're written in a book together and pretending that's charming instead of horrifying." He laughed, without much humor. "But it isn't charming, is it. It's a warning. Somebody painted us a hundred years ago, Coral, and somebody else wrote purge dates in the margins, and I stole evidence from the church I serve because I'd rather be damned with context."

She was quiet a moment. Then, softly, almost to herself: "The rats will know."

"...I'm sorry?"

"Nothing." She stood, pulling on her boots, moving fast now, the merchant's briskness back like armor. "Keep it hidden. Not your rooms—they search archives' quarters first. Give it to me tomorrow, night market, after the stalls close. And Ivo—" She hesitated, and for a heartbeat the armor slipped and he saw the woman from the second portrait, the one unafraid of fire, or waiting on it. "Thank you for not reporting me. Us. Whatever the word is."

"Kindlers," he said. "Apparently."

She smiled at that—one real smile, brief and crooked—and pressed the oilcloth bundle back into his arms. "Tomorrow. After close. Don't be followed."

She was three steps gone into the dark when she stopped, half-turned, hair catching the last of the lamplight exactly as the painter had rendered it.

"And Ivo? If anyone asks where you were tonight—you were nowhere. You saw nothing. Learn to lie well. Quickly."

Then she was gone, and Ivo Marchetti sat alone on the seawall with a stolen apocalypse under his coat, watching the water, practicing his first useful lie on the gulls:

*I am nobody. I was nowhere. Nothing in this city has my face.*

Behind him, far off across the rooftops, the bells of the Cathedral of Bells rang the midnight hour—every one of them whole, every one of them counting down.
