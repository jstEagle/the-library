## 3. What the Rats Remember

I didn't sleep.

I sat on the floor of my apartment above the dye shop with the prophecy book open between my knees and a candle burning down to a stub, and I told myself, over and over, that it was a trick. A coincidence. Some archivist's idea of a joke, or a printer's error, or a face that only looked like mine because faces repeat. Two hundred years of Vellamare's dead had to have produced someone with coral hair eventually. It was statistics. It was nothing.

The woman in the woodcut stared up at me with her coral-colored hair and her chin lifted like she'd never once been afraid, and I did not believe a single word I told myself.

Ivo had left near midnight, after standing in my doorway with the book wrapped in oilcloth like something contagious. "We could burn it," he'd said. He didn't mean it. His hands had been shaking too gently around it for him to mean it. Archivists don't burn books; they just die holding them.

"Then we'd never know," I'd said, which was the wrong answer, and we both knew it, and he'd kissed my knuckles like an apology and gone down the stairs into the dark.

So: no sleep. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, old Maren's dog dreamed loudly, paws twitching against the floorboards. And in the walls—this was the part I kept circling back to, the part that finally made me get up and light a second candle—in the walls, something small moved with purpose.

Rats. There were always rats in Vellamare. The city was built on pilings over brackish water; rats came with the territory like fog and fish smell and the Choir's bells. Ordinary people heard scratching and thought *vermin* and set traps.

I heard scratching and thought *witnesses*.

Because here is the thing about my gift, the thing I had spent twenty years pretending wasn't true: beasts talk. Not in words, not the way the storybooks say, all courtly foxes and wise owls. In pressure. In images and hungers and names-that-are-not-names. Cats are the easiest—vain and chatty. Dogs are honest to the point of embarrassment. Birds are useless gossips with no sense of time.

And rats—

Rats remember everything.

They live short lives stacked on top of each other, generation under generation under generation, and they pass things down the way humans pass down songs. Where the grain is. Where the traps are. Where the water rises in spring. And other things. Older things. Things their grandmothers' grandmothers had seen in the deep places before the Choir sealed them shut.

I hadn't spoken to a rat in six years. Not since the night a dock rat told me, unprompted, about men in gray robes drowning a litter of kits in a sack, and I'd been sick behind Maren's shop and sworn off the whole business forever. Ignorance was safe. Ignorance was the whole architecture of my life.

But the book lay open on my floor, and the woman in the woodcut had my face, and somewhere beneath my feet the city's oldest witnesses were going about their business in the dark.

"Damn it," I said, to nobody, and knelt.

* * *

You don't call rats the way you call cats. Cats come for attention. Rats come for terms.

I opened my window onto the alley, set out the heel of yesterday's bread and a rind of hard cheese—the toll, paid upfront, because a speaker who stiffs the rats gets remembered—and then I did the thing I hadn't done in years. I let the walls down. All of them. Every careful partition I'd built between Coral Delacroix, charm-seller, and the thing underneath, the listening thing, the girl who'd learned at seven years old that the world killed what it could hear.

The alley filled up.

Not visibly. Visibly there was just garbage and grey water and one rat on the windowsill, then three, then a dozen, flowing up the drainpipe with the liquid confidence of creatures who own every city on earth. But in the other sense—the sense that mattered—the alley *filled*. Pressure against my mind from a dozen directions, curious, sharp-toothed, assessing. They knew me. That was the worst part. Word travels underground faster than above. Somewhere in the rat-passed memory of Vellamare there was a file with my name on it, or whatever rats use instead of names, and it said: *speaker. Fair. Pays well. Ask her nothing she doesn't offer.*

"I need to know," I said aloud, because speaking aloud helps me shape it, gives the pressure somewhere to go. My voice sounded terrible. Rough. Scared. "About the kindlers."

The word landed like a stone dropped down a well.

The pressure changed. Curiosity curdled into something older and colder, and the rats on my sill went still—not the frozen stillness of fear but the deliberate stillness of creatures deciding whether to share a family secret. One of them, a big scarred female with half an ear missing, crept forward and put her front paws on my wrist. Her weight was nothing. What came through her touch was not nothing.

Images. Layered, smeared by generations of retelling, but coherent the way a song stays coherent even when the singer changes:

A woman with bright hair, standing in a market square—that square, this district, two hundred years younger—surrounded by beasts. Real ones. Not rats. Things with wings folded like accusations and eyes like banked coals. A griffin-kin, tawny and enormous, its shoulder brushing hers. People cheering. People *afraid*.

And then the same square, later. Gray robes. Bells. The woman bound to a post with her beasts driven off or dead, and fire, and the rats watching from the gutters because rats always watch, and the memory passing mouth to mouth down through two hundred generations: *the bright-haired one burned. The tall one with kind eyes hanged. The Choir wrote it down so it would happen again.*

That last part hit me like cold water. I pulled back and the scarred female let me, sitting back on her haunches, whiskers trembling.

"They were real," I whispered. "The kindlers. Real people."

Confirmation, plain as bread. And underneath it, threaded through the collective memory like a vein of ore, something else—something that made my hands go numb on the windowsill:

*Not all of them died.*

*Not all of the beasts, either.*

The image came fragmented, secondhand, thirdhand—a great door beneath the city, older than the Cathedral, older than the Choir. Water. Stairs going down into dark that the rats' ancestors had been forbidden to enter, forbidden not by men but by *agreement*, some ancient bargain struck between the survivors below and the small quick witnesses above: you forget us, we forget you, and everyone lives.

For two hundred years, the rats of Vellamare had kept the silence.

Until tonight, when a speaker asked directly, and broke the terms, and they gave it up anyway because—this came through clearly, almost gentle—*she pays fair, and she smells of the bright-haired one, and the debt is owed.*

"There are beasts alive," I said slowly, "under the city. Under the Cathedral."

The scarred female touched my wrist again, once, and showed me the last thing: a direction. Down. Always down. Past the drowned cisterns, past the old pilings, to a door that did not appear on any map because the maps had been made by people who needed it not to exist. And a warning wrapped around it like barbed wire: *the gray robes are looking again. The bells ring different lately. Something is waking, and the ones below can hear it, and they are afraid.*

I closed my window. I sat back down on the floor. The candle had gone out at some point without my noticing, and dawn was doing its grey, dishonest work at the edges of the glass, and I put my head in my hands and laughed—one dry, awful sound with no humor in it at all.

Twenty years. Twenty years of being nobody, of selling love charms that were just rose petals and suggestion, of whispering to stray cats in doorways and calling it enough. I had built a life out of the firm conviction that my secret was weightless as long as no one else carried it.

Now the book carried it. Ivo carried it. Two hundred generations of rats carried it. And somewhere below the streets, in the dark under the Cathedral of Bells, creatures who had survived a genocide on the strength of a promise of silence had just heard that the silence was broken.

I thought about the woman in the woodcut, chin lifted, unafraid. She hadn't been unafraid. I understood that now. She'd been afraid and had gone ahead anyway, and the going-ahead was what got her burned.

I thought about Ivo, asleep—or not asleep—somewhere across the city, holding the same knowledge, carrying the same weight, having lied to no one yet but with the first lie probably already forming in his honest mouth.

And I thought about the door beneath the city, and the things behind it, waiting two hundred years for someone who could hear them.

The bells began. Matin, from the Cathedral, the way they rang every morning of my life—except that now, listening the way the rats had taught me to listen, I could hear what they'd meant: the bells rang different lately. Off. Wrong. Like an instrument being tuned for something.

Like a summons.

I got up. I found my boots, my coat, the little knife I'd never once used. And on my way out the door, I did the most reckless thing I had done in twenty years of careful living: I spoke to the first rat I saw, a young one frozen on Maren's step, and I said, "Tell them below I'm coming."

The rat's whiskers trembled. Then it turned and poured itself into the dark, running down, always down, carrying my name to the last secret in Vellamare.

I followed.
