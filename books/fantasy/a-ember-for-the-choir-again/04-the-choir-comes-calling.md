## 4. The Choir Comes Calling

The archive smelled of dust and candle tallow and, on certain days—when the wind came off the harbor just right—of salt and old fear. Ivo had catalogued that smell into his private taxonomy years ago. Fear had a scent in Vellamare. It lived in the ledgers of confiscated property, in the boxes of burned books whose ashes someone had thoughtfully swept into envelopes labeled *REMAINS*, in the confession transcripts filed under *Cleansings, Municipal*.

He had worked among these things for eleven years without once being afraid himself.

That ended three days after he carried the prophecy book out of the sealed stacks in a satchel lined with his own coat.

* * *

She arrived at the ninth bell, which was wrong. Inquisitors did not keep hours; they kept people waiting. The fact that Seraphine Vale came at a scheduled time, announced by a nervous junior clerk, meant she wanted him to know she was coming. It was a courtesy, and courtesies from the Choir were knives wrapped in linen.

Ivo stood when she entered the reading room, and was surprised to find his knees steady. His hands were not. He folded them behind his back like a man inspecting shelves.

"Inquisitor Vale."

"Brother Ivo." She used his choir-name, though he'd never taken vows. Everyone in Choir employ had one; it made you feel owned, which was the point. "You look well."

"I sleep badly," he said, before he could stop himself. Honesty was a habit worn so deep it had become reflexive, like flinching.

Something flickered in her gray eyes—not sympathy, exactly. Recognition. "So do I," she said. "May I?" She gestured at the long oak table where scholars were permitted to consult restricted texts under supervision. There were no other scholars today. He'd made sure of that, sending two away with invented maintenance notices, and hated how easily the inventions had come.

"Of course."

She sat. She did not open the leather folio she carried. Instead she laced her fingers atop it and looked at him with the patience of a woman who had all night and knew precisely how to spend it.

"You've served this archive for eleven years," she said. "Cataloguing, restoration, provenance work. Your superiors describe you as meticulous."

"They're generous."

"They describe you as *scrupulous*. Do you know the difference?"

Ivo did. Scrupulous men reported anomalies. Meticulous men merely noticed them. "I can guess."

"Good." She opened the folio at last and drew out a single sheet: an inventory slip, written in his own hand, dated nine days past. *Item 114-C, transferred to Sealed Stacks, sub-vault Tertius.* The prophecy book. He had signed its return with a steadiness he no longer possessed. "This item is missing," she said.

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above them, the Cathedral bells began the noon peal, and Ivo felt each stroke land in his chest like a fist on a door.

"Missing," he repeated.

"Gone. Not misplaced—gone. The lock was undisturbed. The ward-seal intact. Which means whoever removed it had legitimate access, or access enough to pass for legitimate." Her voice never rose. That was the terrifying thing about Seraphine Vale; the stories said she'd once questioned a smuggler for six hours in a voice soft as this, and the man had confessed to crimes he hadn't committed just to make her stop being kind. "There are four people with standing access to Tertius. Two are dead. One is High Cantor Ossian himself. And one is you."

"And the fourth option," Ivo said, "is that your seal failed. Vellamare's humidity rots everything. I've reconditioned forty ward-boxes this year alone—"

"The seal didn't fail." She said it gently, almost apologetically, the way a surgeon says *this will hurt*. "I checked it myself. So let me ask you plainly, Brother Ivo, because I have found that plainness is a mercy: did you take the book?"

*Say yes,* said some reckless thing in his chest. *Say yes and watch her face change, watch her understand what's in those pages, watch her doubt for one bright second—*

"No," said Ivo.

It came out level. It came out calm. It was the first lie he had ever told anyone in authority, and it tasted like copper and freedom in equal measure, and he understood in that instant why Coral hid, why she folded herself small, why twenty years of silence was not cowardice but craft. Lying was easy. Living inside the lie was the hard part, and he had just moved in.

Seraphine studied him for a long moment. "No," she echoed.

"No. I signed it into the vault. What happened after isn't mine to know."

"Mm." She turned the inventory slip around, slid it across the table toward him. "Then you won't mind describing the item. For the record. So we know what we're looking for."

A trap, obviously. A man who'd stolen a book would hesitate; a man who hadn't would answer too readily. Ivo chose the middle path—the truth, carefully pruned. "Bound in black calf, brass corner-guards gone green with age. Approximately two hundred leaves. The text is a century old, prophetic verse, marginalia in at least three hands. There are painted portraits near the frontispiece." He paused, and let the pause do its work. "I catalogued it eight months ago. I don't remember the subjects."

"You don't remember," Seraphine said, "the portraits. In a book of prophecy. From the sealed collection. The kind of book a man like you reads twice."

"A man like me catalogs ten thousand pages a year, Inquisitor. Memory is a shelf. You learn what to keep and what to stack out of sight."

For the first time, something that might have been respect crossed her face. It frightened him more than suspicion would have. Suspicion he could survive. Respect meant she thought he was worth the effort.

* * *

She walked the archive afterward. Of course she did. He trailed her through the stacks as she ran her gloved finger along shelf-ends, pausing at the door to Tertius, crouching to examine the floor. He watched her find, within ninety seconds, the faint scuff where his boot had dragged against stone while he wrestled the satchel strap over his shoulder—and watched her say nothing about it, which was worse than saying something.

"You're new to lying," she remarked, straightening up. It wasn't a question.

"I'm not lying."

"No. But you've started." She brushed dust from her glove. "Eleven years of scrupulous service, Brother Ivo, and in the space of nine days you send away two researchers with false notices, misfile a retrieval request, and forget the most memorable book in the sealed collection. Whatever you think you're protecting—" she turned, and her eyes held his without cruelty, which somehow made it unbearable, "—consider what it costs. The Choir does not punish curiosity. It punishes concealment. Bring the book back tonight, unremarked, and there will be no inquiry. No file. No cleansing. I will see to it personally."

"And if I can't?"

Her expression closed like a ledger. "Then tomorrow I open one. And you will not enjoy being a line in it."

She left the way she'd come, unhurried, her boots silent on the stone. At the door she stopped, half-turned. "One more thing. The night market on Anchor Street. You were seen there last week. Speaking with a charm-seller. Coral Delacroix." A beat. "Pretty woman. Unlicensed stall. The Choir watches unlicensed stalls, Brother Ivo. We watch them the way you watch fragile manuscripts. With great care, and forever."

Then she was gone, and Ivo stood alone among ten thousand truths he'd spent his life keeping safe for other people, and realized he had never once kept one safe for himself.

* * *

He walked to Anchor Street as soon as the bells rang vespers, taking the long way twice, doubling back through the fish-market until he was certain—or as certain as a novice liar could be—that no one followed. The market was lighting itself lamp by lamp, the way it did every evening, vendors calling out charms against toothache and heartbreak and damp, harmless things, blessed-nothing things, the city's whole economy of small comforts built on the assumption that magic was dead.

Coral saw him coming. He watched her read his face from thirty feet away, watched the color leave hers, watched her begin striking her stall with the brisk economy of a woman who had rehearsed evacuation her whole life.

"It's begun," she said. Not a question either. They were learning each other's grammar.

"She knows the book is gone. She suspects me. And she knows about you—about the stall, about us." He took the crate from her hands and set it down, took her cold fingers instead. "Coral. She offered me amnesty if I returned it tonight."

"And you came here instead." Something moved through her expression—wonder, and grief, and underneath both a fierce, terrified tenderness. "Ivo. You came here."

"I lied to an inquisitor today," he said. "First time in my life. And do you know what I discovered? Underneath the terror, underneath all of it—I wasn't afraid of her finding out. I was afraid of *you* finding out I'd given it back." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "Twenty years you've hidden. Eleven years I've obeyed. And it turns out loyalty was just complicity I hadn't named yet."

Coral looked past him, down the lamplit street, at the ordinary crowds buying ordinary charms, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped into the register he'd only heard once before—when she'd whispered to the stray cats, thinking no one watched.

"Then we can't give it back. And we can't run, because they'll burn the undercity looking, and there are things down there, Ivo, living things, that have waited a hundred years for—" She stopped herself. Shook her head. "Come with me. Tonight. Below. There's something you need to see, and I need—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Above them, far off across the rooftops, the Cathedral bells began ringing out of pattern—three strokes, pause, three strokes—the signal every child in Vellamare knew and no one spoke aloud.

Search bells.

Somewhere behind them, a lantern flared at the mouth of Anchor Street, and a voice called out, courteous as a knife wrapped in linen: "Charm-seller. A word."
