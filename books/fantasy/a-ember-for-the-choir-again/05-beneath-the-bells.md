## 5. Beneath the Bells

The rats came for her at dusk, and they did not come alone.

Coral had been closing her stall at the Saltmarket, counting coins she no longer trusted herself to spend, when the first of them appeared on her counter—a lean brown rat with a torn ear, sitting upright in the lamplight like a small gray magistrate. Behind it, along the gutter's edge, sat six more. Then twelve. Then a rank of them stretching down the alley into shadow, tails curled neat as punctuation.

*You called,* said the torn-eared one.

"I didn't call anyone."

*You asked who remembers. We remember.*

She had asked that question three nights ago, kneeling in her kitchen with flour on her hands, whispering to the rats in her walls because she was too frightened to sleep and too proud to admit it even to the dark. She hadn't expected an answer. In twenty years of hiding, she had never once asked the city's beasts anything that mattered. That was the discipline. That was the whole architecture of her life: small talk with cats, nothing with rats, silence with everything else.

Now the entire rat population of the Saltmarket district was sitting in formation outside her stall, waiting for her to stop being a coward.

"Go away," she whispered, glancing at the market warden shuffling past with his lantern. "Please."

*We have waited a hundred years,* the rat said, and its voice was dry as old paper. *We can wait while you close your shutters.*

---

She told Ivo she needed one night alone. He'd wanted to come—she could see it in his face, that archivist's hunger to follow a thread to its spool—but the rats had been specific. *Come alone. Come unarmed. Come before the moon sets or the way closes.* And besides, some animal instinct older than sense told her that whatever lived beneath Vellamare had survived a century of purge by trusting no one, and a man whose face was printed in a forbidden book was not a trust she could offer them yet.

So she went alone, at midnight, to the place the rats described: the old cistern gate behind the tannery row, where the city's foundations showed their bones.

The gate was iron gone to lace with rust, set into a wall that smelled of brine and old smoke. The rats led her to it and then stopped, all together, as if crossing a threshold they were not permitted.

*Down,* said torn-ear. *Follow the water sound. When you reach the third junction, wait. You will be found—or you will not.*

"That's reassuring."

*Truth usually isn't.* The rat groomed its whiskers, and added, almost gently: *Speaker. A hundred years we have said that word to our children so they would not forget it. Do not make us say it to them in vain.*

Then they poured away into the dark, and Coral stood alone at the mouth of the undercity with a hooded lantern, a knife she didn't know how to use, and twenty years of carefully constructed invisibility telling her, in her mother's voice, *turn around, Coralie, turn around now.*

She thought of Ivo's face in the vellum. She thought of the word *kindler*, inked beside her own hair color by a dead hand. She thought of the wyrm the rats spoke of—the blind one, the old one, coiled somewhere below like a living memory.

She turned the latch. The gate groaned open like something waking.

She went down.

---

The undercity was not what she expected. She had imagined sewers, sump, blackness. Instead the stairs opened into vaulted galleries of pale stone, older than Vellamare itself, older than the Choir, older maybe than the idea of walls. Water ran somewhere out of sight in channels cut with deliberate grace, and the air was cold and mineral and strangely sweet, like the inside of a cave where flowers had once grown.

Her lantern showed her carvings on every pillar: beasts. Winged lions, serpents crowned with stars, stags with antlers of branching light. Not decorations. Portraits. Someone had built this place as a home for them, long before the Choir taught the city to be afraid.

At the third junction she stopped, as instructed, and waited.

The waiting lasted eleven minutes. She counted, because counting kept panic at a manageable size.

Then the dark ahead of her moved.

It moved the way a hillside might move, if a hillside decided to stand up. Scales the color of wet slate caught her lantern light and threw it back dull and vast, and Coral felt her knees inform her, quietly, that they were done for the evening. She stayed standing through sheer spite.

The wyrm's head descended out of the gloom like a ship's prow coming about. It was the size of a rowboat. Its eyes were closed—had perhaps been closed for decades—and its nostrils flared as it tasted the air above her, slow and thorough, the way Ivo read a page.

"Small," the wyrm said at last. Its voice was gravel rolled in honey, a voice built for mountains. "The speaker is small."

"I'm—" Her voice cracked; she hated that. "I'm sorry. I don't know what I am."

"You are the one who talks." The great head tilted, blind face seeking her warmth. "The little ones have carried your scent-word up from the streets for three nights. *She asks. She listens.* No one has listened in a hundred years, child. Listening is louder than you know."

"I'm not a speaker foretold. I'm a charm seller. I sell love charms that do nothing and luck charms that do less. I hide. That's my whole talent. Hiding."

"Yes," the wyrm agreed, terrible and kind. "And yet here you are."

---

They took her deeper.

The wyrm could not travel far—it moved like a glacier, patient and immense—and so it sent guides. They came out of side galleries in ones and twos, and each arrival rearranged something in Coral's chest that she would never get back.

A griffin-kin stepped into her lantern light first: tall as a man, feathered in storm-gray and white, wings folded tight against a body that was neither bird nor beast nor person but all three braided together. Its eyes were gold and ancient and exhausted. It bowed—not deeply, not servilely, but the bow of one soldier greeting another across a ruined field.

"Coral Delacroix," it said, in a voice like wind through wire. "The rats speak true. You hear us."

"I hear you," she said, and her own voice sounded foreign, unhidden, used.

"There are forty-one of us left," the griffin-kin said. "Forty-one, in a city of two hundred thousand. We were thousands once. My grandmother remembered the sky." It said this without accusation, which made it worse. "The tunnels keep us. The bells deafen their dogs to our scent-marks. We endure. But enduring is not living, speaker, and the young ones—" it paused, and the pause held whole generations— "the young ones have never seen the sun."

Behind it came others. A salamander the size of a cat whose skin rippled with banked fire, warm as a hearth when it brushed her ankle. A pair of fox-things with too many tails and eyes like struck matches. Something shy and luminous in the far dark that she only ever saw as a suggestion, a shimmer, a held breath.

Forty-one. She counted them as they gathered, because counting was the only prayer she knew.

"We heard the prophecy has returned," the griffin-kin said. "The paper one. The false one. The little ones steal scraps of news from the archive mice." Its golden eyes fixed on her. "It speaks of kindlers. Of war. Of a cleansing."

"It's a lie," Coral said. "I think—I'm beginning to know it's a lie. The Choir wrote it themselves. I think they wrote it a hundred years ago to give themselves permission."

"Permission," the wyrm rumbled from behind her, and the word shook dust from the vaulting. "Yes. Cruelty loves a costume. We learned that in the fire years." The vast head lowered until its blind face hovered near hers, close enough that she could feel its breath move her hair. "But hear me, small speaker. If the lie has returned, then the killing may return with it. And we are slower now. Fewer. Tired." A pause, long as a century. "So we must ask you the question we swore never again to ask a human, because the last one we asked sold us to the pyre."

Coral's heart was a fist against her ribs. "Ask anyway."

"Do you free us?" the wyrm said. "Or do you forget us? There is no third door."

The gallery went silent. Forty-one creatures and one liar-by-trade, one woman who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of being no one, and every one of those forty-one pairs of eyes—gold, blind, burning, shy—waited on her answer like the world waited on weather.

She thought of her stall. Her shutters. Her small safe life, folded small as a charm-paper. She thought of Ivo saying *I noticed you the first night, you know. You looked like someone holding her breath.* She had been holding her breath for twenty years. Her lungs ached with it.

"I don't know how to free anyone," she said finally, honestly, her voice shaking in the beautiful terrible acoustics of the deep. "I don't know the tunnels or the law or how to fight. I can't promise you victory or safety or even tomorrow." She swallowed. "But I promise you this: I will not forget you. I will not go back up there and close my shutters and let you stay a rumor. Whatever I am—speaker, kindler, charm seller—I'm yours to use. That's the only vow I know how to make, and I'll keep it if it kills me."

The silence after was enormous.

Then the wyrm laughed—a low, rolling sound like distant thunder deciding to be gentle—and the griffin-kin bowed again, deeper this time, and the little salamander at her feet flared briefly bright as a struck match, and Coral understood that she had just done the one thing she had sworn, for twenty years, never to do.

She had mattered. Publicly. Irrevocably. To witnesses.

"The bells ring matins in five hours," the griffin-kin said. "Go up. Go carefully. And speaker—" its gold eyes gleamed— "when you find the other one, the one from the book, bring him below. The young ones should see both faces of the story."

"They already know his face?"

"The archive mice are thorough," said the griffin-kin, and there was, unmistakably, humor in it. "As, apparently, is he. He has been asking questions in the wrong places for a week. The wrong places love him for it."

Coral climbed the stairs toward the cistern gate with her lantern low and her heart hammering a new rhythm—no longer fear alone, but fear braided with purpose, which is a far more dangerous braid.

Above her, Vellamare slept, two hundred thousand strong, bell-towers black against the stars. Somewhere in it, Ivo was awake too, she'd have bet anything, surrounded by paper and secrets. Somewhere in it, Seraphine Vale was asking her careful questions. Somewhere in it, the Choir was already turning over its stones.

She had gone down into the dark a hidden woman.

She came up as something else entirely—and the first thing she intended to do, dawn or no dawn, was find Ivo Lassiter and tell him the beasts were waiting to meet him.

Whether he was ready or not.
