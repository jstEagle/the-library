## 6. Ashes of the First Purge

**CORAL**

The archive key was warm from Ivo's pocket, and that small human heat frightened her more than the book had.

They climbed the back stairs of the Scriptorium Annex at midnight, past shelves of ledgers no one had opened since the last century's accounting, into a reading room with one lamp and a lock Ivo turned twice. He set the prophecy book on the table between them the way other men set down flowers—carefully, like something that might still be alive.

"We shouldn't be here," Coral said.

"No," he agreed, and sat down anyway.

She loved him for that. She hated herself for loving him for that. Twenty years of discipline said: stand up, walk out, let the archivist keep his pretty dangerous book alone. But twenty years was also how long she'd gone without anyone sitting beside her in the dark on purpose, and her body refused to move, traitor that it was.

"Show me again," she said. "The portrait."

He turned to the illuminated page. There he was—the same long face, the same ink-dark hair falling over one brow, rendered by a hand dead a hundred years. And beside him, mid-laugh at some joke the painter had witnessed but not recorded, a woman with hair the exact color of embers drowned in seawater. Coral's color. Coral's laugh, or near enough that looking at it felt like being caught mid-sentence.

"The marginalia," Ivo said. "That's what kept me up. Look here." His finger traced the page edges, where faded script crowded the illumination like ivy on a wall. "Everyone reads the prophecy text itself. *Two kindlers shall wake the sleeping fire, and the Choir shall answer.* Everyone stops there. But whoever wrote these notes—" he flipped pages, "—they weren't reading it as prophecy. They were reading it as *correspondence*."

"Correspondence?"

"Letters. Drafts." He spread three sheets of tracing paper covered in his own careful transcription. "Look at the dates in the margins. The prophecy is dated forty years before the First Purge. But these notes reference grain shipments, garrison rotations, a dispute between two Cantors about funding. You don't prophesy about grain shipments, Coral. You *plan* about them."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the window, and Coral felt something lean in her chest too—a terrible slow tilting, like a ship taking water.

"You're saying it wasn't a prophecy at all."

"I'm saying I need you to tell me I'm wrong, because if I'm right, then everything the Choir has done for a hundred years—the purges, the outlawry, the pyres—they weren't answering fate. They were executing a memo."

Coral looked down at the tracing paper. Ivo's handwriting was beautiful, patient, the hand of a man who copied things for a living and believed in what he copied. That was the thing about Ivo she'd understood from their first conversation at the night market: he was a man who trusted the record. And now the record was betraying him, gently, page by page, the way a trusted friend betrays you—with documentation.

"Read me the worst one," she said.

He found it without searching. He'd memorized it; she could hear that in his voice. "*The kindlers must be named before they are born to it. A fire foretold is a fire permitted. Let the book say war comes, and when war comes, the book will have told the truth.*"

Silence. Somewhere below, the Annex clock counted the hour with its soft bureaucratic click.

"They wrote it first," Coral said slowly. "Before we existed. Before *anyone* existed. They invented the kindlers so that when they found people like me—people who could talk to beasts—they wouldn't be murdering innocents. They'd be fulfilling scripture."

"They made a trap," Ivo said quietly, "and then they spent a century waiting for someone to walk into it. And we did. We walked right in, holding hands."

She hadn't been holding his hand. But she took it now.

His fingers were cold. Archivist's fingers, ink-stained, trembling just slightly—and hers were steady, which surprised her, because inside she was anything but steady. Inside she was the twelve-year-old who had watched a Choir wagon take the tinker's dog, the one that had sung to her in words no one else could hear, and had said nothing, because saying something meant the wagon would stop for her too.

"I've spent my whole life being careful," she said. "Careful didn't save the dog. Careful didn't save the griffin-kin under the city. It just saved *me*, and it turns out that's not the same thing as saving anything."

"Coral—"

"If the Choir forged this, then the beasts aren't doomed by prophecy. They're doomed by *paperwork*. And paperwork can be burned."

Ivo laughed—a startled, broken sound—and pulled her hand to his mouth, pressed his lips to her knuckles like a man sealing a letter. "You understand what you're saying. If we're wrong about this, we're heretics. If we're right, we're worse. We're *inconvenient*."

"Then let's be inconvenient together."

Outside the window, Vellamare lay silver and silent, bell towers rising against the stars like needles waiting to drop. Somewhere in one of those towers, she knew, the original of this forgery might sit in a case, venerated, kissed by pilgrims. She thought of the blind wyrm coiled in the dark beneath the city, breathing slow as tide. She thought of rats carrying her questions through the walls like a nervous system for the whole sleeping city.

*Let the book say war comes.*

"Then war comes," Coral whispered. "But on our terms."

**IVO**

He had lied to Seraphine Vale once, and it had cost him a night's sleep and most of his appetite. He suspected forging an entire religion would cost somewhat more.

They worked until the third hour, and what struck Ivo—what unsettled him more than the forgery itself—was how *tidy* it was. The marginalia moved in sequence across decades: draft language refined, theological objections raised and answered, a Cantor's anxious note about whether the faithful would accept a prophecy that condemned children (*let the kindlers be grown when found; mercy is compatible with necessity*), and finally, near the end, a line in a different, triumphant hand:

*It is done. The book believes us. Vellamare will believe the book.*

"The book believes us," Ivo repeated aloud. "They knew. They knew exactly what they'd built. A machine that runs on faith and produces corpses."

"And pilgrims still queue to touch it." Coral sat across from him with her chin on her fists, lamplight turning her ember-colored hair to actual fire. "Ivo. Say the rest of it. The part you've been not-saying for an hour."

He set down his pen. This was the trouble with being known—you couldn't hide your unsaid sentences anymore.

"When we show this to people," he said, "the first question won't be whether the forgery is real. It'll be why *we* have it. Why an archivist stole sealed material and why his companion matches the portrait in a forbidden book. We can't expose their lie without confessing ours."

"I know."

"And Seraphine already suspects the book is missing. When it becomes public that it's gone—if it becomes public—she'll know it was me. She was kind to me, Coral. She taught me cataloguing. She defended me when the junior clerics mocked my accent." He heard his own voice go thin. "I owe her everything, and I'm going to destroy her life's work, and she will not see it as truth-telling. She'll see it as betrayal."

Coral came around the table. She didn't touch him yet; she stood close, letting him choose.

"Or," she said, "she'll remember something true. Inquisitors are made from somewhere, Ivo. Nobody's born with that look in their eyes. Something put it there."

"She lost a sister. Everyone knows that much. The sister died young; the Choir declared it fever."

"And you believe that?"

"I believe Seraphine believes it, which is more dangerous."

Coral did touch him then, both hands framing his face, and Ivo felt the strange vertigo of being held by someone who could hear what animals thought of him. He'd never asked what the cats said. He wasn't sure he wanted to know.

"Here's what I know about duty," she said. "I performed mine for twenty years. My duty was silence, and I did it perfectly, and the world got no better and I got no happier. Duty that requires you to lie to yourself isn't duty. It's just fear wearing a uniform." Her thumbs moved along his cheekbones. "So we choose. Not prophecy—prophecy's a forgery. Not duty—duty's a costume. We just choose. You. Me. Them." She nodded downward, toward the undercity, toward the wyrm and the griffin-kin and all the hidden breathing things. "Out loud. Where the bells can hear it."

"My mother would call that damnation."

"Your mother," Coral said, "raised a man who steals books for women he loves. I think she'll manage."

He kissed her, and it was nothing like the careful, courtly gestures of their market-stall courtship. It was the kiss of two people standing in a burning archive of their old lives, saving what mattered and letting the rest go up. Her hands slid into his hair; his arms went around her like a decision. When they broke apart, breathless, the lamp had guttered low and neither of them reached to fix it.

"Stay," he said. "Not—not like that. Or yes, like that. Both. Stay. If tomorrow we're fugitives, I want tonight to be ours, not theirs. They don't get to own the story of us. They don't even get a footnote."

"Archivists and their footnotes."

"This footnote," Ivo said, "is the only honest thing in the whole building."

She stayed.

Later, half-asleep against his shoulder in the reading room's narrow daybed, Coral spoke without opening her eyes. "The rats came to me this evening. Before we came up here."

Ivo went still. "What did they say?"

"That's what woke me worried. They said men in gray robes have been asking questions at the dockside taverns. Asking about a red-haired charm seller." Her eyes opened, dark and calm in the dimness. "And one more thing. They said the gray robes bought rope. A lot of rope. And lanterns."

"Lanterns for what? The undercity's already lit by—"

He stopped.

"No," Coral said softly, sitting up. "They're not going down to look. They're going down to *seal*. Rope and lanterns and—grates, Ivo, they'll bring grates. They're not hunting the beasts yet. They're penning them first. Closing every exit." Her hand found his in the dark, gripping hard. "Which means someone told them where to look. Which means we have days. Maybe less."

Above them, faint through the stone, the Cathedral of Bells began the dawn peal—one bell, then another, then all of them, the sound that had ruled Vellamare's conscience for a hundred years.

Coral listened to it with her head tilted, the way she listened to everything, and Ivo saw her expression change into something he'd never seen there before.

Not fear. Arithmetic.

"How fast," she said, "can you copy a forgery?"
