## 7. Seraphine's Mercy

The report had been on Seraphine Vale's desk for six days before she allowed herself to read it twice.

*A woman, coral-haired, seen speaking to the gulls at Tidewall. Seen again at the night market, lips moving over a stray cat's ears. The cat followed her home.*

Ordinary reports did not make her hands cold. She had read confessions that turned her stomach, orders that kept her awake, ledgers of the purged that she had copied out in her own careful hand because no one else could be trusted to spell the names right. But this—this small, stupid report about a charm-seller and a cat—made her fingers go numb around her tea.

Because Seraphine knew what it was to speak to something that answered.

She locked the report in the bottom drawer, beneath the false bottom her predecessor had built and never told anyone about, and she sat very still in her office above the Choir barracks while the bells rang noon, and she made herself a promise she already knew she would break: *I will handle this one quietly. No soldiers. No pyre. Just a conversation. Just a warning.*

It was the same promise she had made about her sister, twenty-two years ago.

* * *

Coral Delacroix's shop was exactly what an inquisitor expected: narrow, cluttered, smelling of beeswax and dried lavender, its shelves crowded with charms that were nothing but string and glass and hope. The kind of magic the Choir tolerated because it wasn't magic at all. Seraphine had walked past the window a dozen times in the past week without going in, watching the coral-haired woman wrap parcels for children and haggle cheerfully with fishwives, and every time, Seraphine had felt the old sickness rise in her throat—the sickness of recognizing yourself in someone you are paid to destroy.

She went in on a gray Thursday, out of uniform, wearing a merchant widow's coat she kept for exactly these errands.

The bell above the door chimed. Coral looked up from her ledger, smiled the smile of a woman who has charmed strangers for twenty years, and said, "Welcome. Looking for anything particular?"

"Protection," Seraphine said. "For the home."

"Ah." Coral came around the counter, all warmth and competence. "I have iron nails from the old harbor wall—good against ill-wishing. Salt-charms. If it's dreams that trouble you, I do a sachet of valerian and rue—"

"It's not dreams." Seraphine let her voice drop half a tone. "It's voices. In the walls. My neighbors say I'm mad. But I hear them."

Something moved behind Coral's eyes—quick, controlled, gone. A shutter closing. Twenty years of practice, and still Seraphine saw it, because she knew what to look for. She had spent her whole life learning what to look for.

"I hear them too," Seraphine said quietly. "Have done since I was nine. Rats, mostly. And once—" Her breath caught, and she let it catch; it was true, and truth was the only blade she had left. "Once, my sister's canary. The morning after they burned her."

The shop went very silent. Outside, a cart rattled past; somewhere a child laughed. Coral's hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, toward the little iron bell on the counter—the one that wasn't a charm at all, the one that would wake every sympathetic ear within three streets if she rang it. Seraphine noted the hand. Noted the bell. Noted that Coral did not ring it.

"You're Choir," Coral said. It wasn't a question. Her voice had gone flat and steady, a woman stepping onto solid ground after years of pretending the floor was there.

"Inquisitor Second Rank. Seraphine Vale." She did not reach for the badge hidden in her coat. "And yes. I could take you in right now. Two witnesses saw you speak to the gulls. One saw the cat. I've read the file, Coral Delacroix. I know about the man you've been seeing. The archivist. Ivo Lenn. I know he took a book out of the sealed archive that no one is supposed to know exists."

Coral's face did not change, which was itself an answer. "Then why haven't you?"

"Because I want to know if it's true." Seraphine stepped closer, close enough to smell the lavender, close enough to see the fine tremor in the other woman's jaw that willpower was holding still. "What the book says. What the rats say. Whether the beasts are really down there, under the city, alive. Because if they are—if they survived the purge my Church swears was total—then everything I have given my life to is a lie, and I need to hear it from someone who isn't lying to me."

"And then?" Coral's voice was soft. Terrible in its softness. "You'll hear it. You'll believe it. And then you'll write your report anyway, because that's what you do. That's what the Choir does. My mother heard the same speech from an inquisitor, once. Kind eyes. Quiet voice. *Just tell me the truth.*" She shook her head slowly. "We buried what was left of her in a jar."

Seraphine stood very still. The old wound opened in her chest, the one shaped like Wren—Wren at nine years old, whispering to sparrows on the windowsill; Wren at fourteen, taken; Wren's name scraped off the family ledger by their father's own hand so the shame wouldn't spread. *They always ask for the truth first,* their mother used to say, before she stopped speaking entirely. *The truth is how they find the rest of us.*

"I am not asking you to confess," Seraphine said. "I'm telling you that in six days, this file goes up the chain whether I send it or not. Cantor Dossel has already asked me twice why the Tidewall report hasn't moved. There is another inquisitor, Vale Third Rank, who would love my post, and he reads my correspondence. I cannot bury this forever. Do you understand? I am not threatening you. I am counting days for you."

For a long moment Coral just looked at her. Then, slowly, she reached under the counter and set something down between them: a small carved figure, whalebone worn smooth by handling, of a bird with its wings folded—not flying. Resting.

"Wren," Seraphine read, when she saw the letters scratched into the base. Her whole body went cold. "That's—"

"The rats remember names," Coral said. "Your sister spoke to the sparrows in the Cathedral square for two years before they took her. They remembered. They told me." She pushed the figure across the counter. "I made this last night. I don't know why. I make things sometimes and I don't know why until later." Her eyes were bright and wet and unflinching. "That's why I haven't run, Inquisitor. Because someone in that stone building remembers my sister's name, even if it's only the rats. So. Count your days. But when you decide whose side mercy is on—decide fast."

* * *

Seraphine did not sleep that night. She sat at her desk with the whalebird in her palm and the report open before her, and she wrote four drafts of a letter recommending the file be closed as mistaken identity, and burned all four in the candle flame, because she knew—Ivo Lenn's archive withdrawals had been logged; Cantor Dossel had a copy of the seal registry; the moment she closed the file, someone competent would reopen it, and this time they would start with the inquisitor who'd buried it.

*They always ask for the truth first. The truth is how they find the rest of us.*

At dawn she did the arithmetic she had been avoiding for six days. It was simple. It was always simple. That was the horror of it.

If she reported: Coral Delacroix taken, questioned, likely broken on the wheel or quietly drowned like her mother; the archivist seized; the undercity found and purged a second time—and Seraphine Vale continued breathing, promoted, perhaps, trusted, safe.

If she stayed silent: both of them taken anyway, within the week, by men less gentle than she was—and Seraphine taken with them, and the whalebird melted down for its bone, and Wren's name unwitnessed by any living soul.

There was no third column. She had looked for one since she was fourteen years old. There had never been a third column. That was the design. That was the whole genius of the Choir: not cruelty, but arithmetic. Every kindness priced, every silence taxed, every act of love converted into evidence against you.

She thought of Wren's hands cupped around a sparrow, so gently, so certain the world would be gentle back.

*Forgive me,* she thought—to Wren, to the coral-haired stranger who had carved her name into bone, to whatever god the beasts prayed to beneath the bells. *Forgive me. I was never brave. I only ever learned to survive.*

At the seventh hour, she climbed the stairs to Cantor Dossel's office and laid the report on his desk, and gave him the address.

Not the shop. Never the shop—that would be too easy, too traceable to her. She gave him the tide-tunnel entrance below Tidewall, the one the rats' roads ran through, the one Coral had described in a single careless sentence that Seraphine had carried for three days like a coal in her chest. *The beasts come up near the drowned stair, when the moon is dark. That's how I know they're real.*

Dossel read it twice. His face did not change. He was a mild man, tidy, fond of his garden; he had signed her sister's warrant too, though Seraphine had learned that only years later, from a file she should never have seen.

"Good work, Vale," he said. "Second Rank to First, when this is concluded. The High Cantor will want a clean operation. Full company. We move on the new moon."

"Of course," Seraphine said. Her voice was perfectly level. Somewhere far away, a girl was wrapping charms in brown paper, believing she had six days.

She had given her three.

Seraphine walked back down the stairs past the chapel, where the morning bells were ringing, and stopped, and pressed her hand flat against the cold stone wall as if she could hold the building shut with her body alone. Under her palm, deep in the masonry, something scratched—small claws, a whiskered snout, a rat that had nested in the holy walls where no rat should dare.

It was speaking. She could feel the words buzzing faintly through the stone, the way she had felt them through floorboards all her childhood, the gift she had starved and denied and buried alive for thirty years:

*She knows. She knows. The speaker says the bells will fall. The speaker says—*

Seraphine pulled her hand away as if the stone had burned her.

Three days. She had three days to decide, one final time, what she was.

Above her, the bells began to ring for prime, and in the walls, softly, patiently, the rats went on talking.
