## 8. The Long Night Below

The rats came first.

They poured out of the drainage grates in a silver river, whiskers twitching, and the oldest of them—a scarred female Coral had known for a decade, the one she called Mother—climbed her boot and spoke in that dry rustle only she could hear.

*Soldiers. Many. Lanterns. They come down through the bell-foundry shaft.*

Coral's blood went cold. She'd felt the Choir's attention like weather for days now, a pressure building behind the walls of the city, but she had hoped—Ivo had argued—that they had until dawn.

"How many is many?" she whispered.

*More than the stones can count. They bring fire that does not flicker.*

Lantern-glass. The Choir's blessed light, the kind that burned through glamour and shadow both. She looked back at the cavern where the beasts had gathered—her cavern now, apparently, hers to protect—and felt the weight of it settle on her shoulders like wet stone.

The griffin-kin stood at the far wall, wings folded tight, feathers the color of rust and old gold. Her name was Ashka, and she had not spoken to Coral directly in three days, though the wyrm had vouched for her in that long, grinding voice of his. The wyrm—Old Sable, blind, vast, coiled in the flooded channel like a drowned cathedral—lifted his head when the rats scattered.

*They are coming,* he said. Not a question. He had heard it in the water, in the tremor of boots a hundred feet of stone away.

"They're coming," Coral said. "All of you. Now. We move through the drowned tunnels to the harbor outfall, and we don't stop."

*The water will take the small ones,* Sable said.

"The water will take us if we stay." She heard her own voice crack and hated it, and then stopped hating it, because fear was honest and she was done with dishonesty. "I know the way. I've walked it. Trust me or don't, but if you stay here, you die in the dark where no one will ever know you existed. Come with me and at least the dark will remember you were running *toward* something."

Ashka's golden eyes fixed on her. For a long moment the griffin-kin said nothing, and Coral braced for refusal, for the whole fragile alliance to shatter in the last hour it had.

"Speaker," Ashka said at last, in the low whistle of her kind. "If we die in the water, will you carry our names?"

"Yes," Coral said. "Every one."

"Then lead."

* * *

The drowned tunnels were exactly as bad as she remembered.

Waist-deep water, black and cold enough to ache, the ceiling low enough that the taller creatures had to fold themselves nearly double. Coral went first with a rat on each shoulder—Mother and her daughters, navigating by whisker and memory—and behind her came the others: three griffin-kin, a family of foxes whose fur held a faint phosphorescent glow, a knot of tunnel-wyrmlings no bigger than her forearm, and Old Sable, who moved through the deep channel beneath them like a shadow under a shadow.

The phosphorescent foxes were their only light. Coral had forbidden lanterns—the glow would carry—and so they walked by foxfire and faith, and the water whispered against their bodies, and somewhere far above, muffled by stone, she could hear it: bells. The Choir's alarm bells, ringing out of pattern, ringing *wrong*.

Ivo, she thought. That's you, isn't it. That's you up there being loud so we can be quiet.

She had kissed him an hour ago, quick and hard, at the mouth of the undercity stair. "Be careful," she'd said, and he'd smiled that archivist's smile, the one that meant he'd already catalogued every way this could go wrong and filed them under *acceptable losses*.

"I'm always careful," he'd said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and she'd kissed him again anyway because if those were the last words between them she wanted the last thing to be a mouth, not a sentence.

*Speaker.* Mother's voice, sharp. *Stop.*

Coral raised a fist. Behind her, the column of beasts went still, the water settling into silence.

She listened. Beneath the bells, beneath the water—footsteps. Above them. On the tunnel roof, or in the parallel gallery; it was hard to tell. Then a voice, thin through stone, giving orders in the Choir's clipped cadence. They weren't behind them. They were *ahead*, sealing the route to the harbor outfall.

No. Not ahead. She mapped it in her head—the gallery above ran parallel for a quarter mile before branching toward the foundry. They were flanking. Herding. Driving them toward the one place the Choir wanted them: the great cistern, where there was no cover and no exit except the shafts, and the shafts would be held.

"They're herding us," she breathed.

*Then we do not go where they drive,* Sable said from the deep water. His blind face broke the surface beside her, nostrils flaring. *There is another way. The old heat-vein. It runs beneath the cistern, toward the river. My grandsire swam it before the purge.*

"Can you fit?"

*No.* A pause, long as a held breath. *But the small ones can. And the griffin-kin, if they fold. You will have to go first and tell them when to breathe.*

The heat-vein. A volcanic duct, half-drowned, air pockets like held breaths in the dark. Coral thought of the wyrm's grandsire, dead a century, and of the map Sable was describing from memory older than the city's oldest book.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

They almost made it.

The heat-vein was everything Sable promised and worse—water hot enough to redden skin, air pockets foul and close, the passage so narrow that Ashka had to fold her wings until the joints screamed. Coral went first, counting breaths, calling back the rhythm: *breathe here, hold there, down and through.* The foxes swam like embers, their glow the only light, and the wyrmlets rode in a sling across her chest, chirring tiny encouragement against her heartbeat.

They were fifty feet from the river mouth when the first lantern-light bloomed behind them.

Not behind. *Below.* The Choir had found the heat-vein's lower entrance, and their glass-lanterns turned the water to burning gold, and Coral heard the shout go up: *"Contact! Speaker sighted!"*

"GO!" she screamed, and the column broke for the river mouth.

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do. The foxes went first, a streak of dying light. The wyrmlets went, shrieking, into the dark. Ashka went, wings cracking open the moment she hit open water. Coral turned to count them through—

—and saw the soldiers in the water.

Three of them, wading, lanterns held high, and one of them had a net of blessed iron, and he threw it not at Coral but at the last of the wyrmlets, the smallest one, the one who had ridden against her heart and told her the dark was less frightening when it had a heartbeat to match.

The net caught it mid-leap.

"No—" Coral was already turning, already wading back, and Ashka's cry rang off the stone—*"Speaker, NO"*—and the water was hot and the light was gold and the wyrmlet looked at her with eyes like wet ink and said, in a voice smaller than a whisper:

*It's all right. You said the dark would remember.*

Then the soldier's blade came down, and the water went red, and Coral screamed a sound she didn't know her body could make.

They got her out. Ashka and a second griffin-kin, hauling her backward through the water while she fought them, while the soldiers' shouts echoed and the lantern-light swung and Old Sable's voice rose from the deep channel like the tolling of a drowned bell—*GO, SPEAKER, GO, OR IT IS WORTH NOTHING*—and she went, because he was right, because the only thing worse than the wyrmlet dying was the wyrmlet dying for nothing.

They surfaced in the river shallows a mile downstream, gasping, shaking, the city lights of Vellamare glittering above them like indifferent stars.

Coral counted them through. Foxes: three of five. Griffin-kin: all three. Wyrmlets: two of four.

She sat in the cold shallows with the water pulling at her and made herself say the names of the dead. The wyrmlet's name, which had been Pip. The two foxes, who had been called Ember and Wick by their own kind, in the old way. She said them out loud, one by one, while the survivors watched, and when she finished, Ashka lowered her great rust-colored head until her beak nearly touched Coral's shoulder.

"You carried them," the griffin-kin said.

"No." Coral's voice was raw. "I *carried their names*. That's not the same thing, and I'm done pretending it is." She stood. The river ran past her, cold and indifferent, and the city rose above it—her city, the city she had spent twenty years being invisible in, the city whose Cathedral of Bells glowed on its hill like a lit pyre.

She was done being invisible.

"They wanted us drowned in the dark where no one would know," she said. "So we do it in the light instead. All of it. The beasts, the forged prophecy, the purge, the graves under the Cathedral—everything, in front of everyone, at high mass, where they can't silence it."

*The Cathedral is a fortress,* Sable said from the water, his blind face turned toward her.

"Yes," Coral said. "And tomorrow it's full of witnesses."

She looked up at the bells, still ringing their wrong, urgent pattern, and thought of Ivo somewhere inside that noise, alive or not, loud or silent, and made herself a promise with teeth in it.

Hold on, archivist. I'm coming to break something beautiful.
