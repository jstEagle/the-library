## 9. Requiem at the Cathedral

**Coral**

The bells began at dawn, and they did not stop.

Coral stood in the shadow of the bell tower's base, her hood up, the forged prophecy wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat. Around her, Vellamare poured toward the Cathedral of Bells in its finest clothes, ten thousand bodies funneled through doors built for a god who had never once answered.

*They'll kill us,* said a voice at her ankle. A rat, gray and scarred, one of the hundred who had run messages through the walls all night. *They'll kill you first.*

"Probably," Coral murmured.

*Then why—*

"Because I spent twenty years being safe," she said, "and it didn't save anyone but me."

The rat considered this with the gravity of its kind, then darted into the crowd.

Ivo found her by the cistern where they'd agreed to meet if either of them was still free. He looked terrible and beautiful — a bruise blooming along his jaw from a Choir patrol that had gotten too curious, his archivist's coat swapped for a fishmonger's apron that fooled no one. He pressed something into her hand: a stub of chalk.

"In case we get separated," he said. "Mark the pillars. I taught Seraphine the archive's cipher years ago. She'll know what it means."

"Do you think she'll come?"

Ivo was quiet for a moment. "I think she's been coming for a long time. She just doesn't know which direction yet."

Above them, the great doors opened, and the High Cantor's voice rolled out over the square, honeyed and vast, welcoming the faithful to the Feast of the Purge — the one day a year Vellamare celebrated its freedom from magic, from beasts, from the old darkness. Ten thousand voices sang back at him.

Coral touched the oilcloth bundle. "Ready?"

"No," Ivo said, and took her hand anyway. "But I've read enough endings to know the good ones never feel ready."

They walked into the light together.

* * *

**Ivo**

The nave swallowed them whole.

Ivo had attended high mass perhaps four hundred times in his life — dutifully, quietly, always in the third row where the Choir could see him being seen. He had never once looked at it from the back of the room, standing among the poor and the doubtful and the ones who came only for the warmth. From here, the gold leaf on the vaulted ceiling looked like rot. The incense smelled like a lie told so long it had become furniture.

And there, on the lectern beneath the rose window, lay the book.

Not their book — a copy, gorgeously illuminated, the forgery dressed in its feast-day vestments. The Cantor lifted it as he chanted the Litany of Ashes: *and the kindlers came, and their fire would have unmade the world, and the Choir struck them down, and there was peace.*

Lies. Every word of it written a century ago by the Choir's own scribes, in ink mixed with ash from the creatures they'd burned. Coral had traced the marginalia with shaking fingers three nights ago — the same hand, the same quill nicks, the same batch of iron-gall ink that appeared in the Choir's internal ledgers. The prophecy hadn't foretold the purge. It had *manufactured* it.

Beside him, Coral's breathing had gone shallow and fast. Beneath the floorboards — he felt it more than heard it, a tremor through the soles of his shoes — something vast shifted its weight.

"Not yet," he breathed. "Wait for the Cantor's blessing. The moment every eye closes."

She nodded. Her hair had been dyed brown with walnut stain, and it hurt him to see it, this small surrender of the thing that had marked her in a dead man's vellum. By tonight it wouldn't matter. By tonight everyone in Vellamare would know her face.

The Cantor raised his arms. "And now, beloved children, let us give thanks —"

Every head bowed.

Coral pulled off her hood.

The walnut dye came away in sheets — she'd coated it with wax that morning, and it flaked like bark under her fingers as she walked, bareheaded, coral-bright, straight down the center aisle with the forged book held high. A woman two rows in gasped. An usher reached for her arm and Ivo stepped into his path, smiling his mild archivist's smile, and said, "You'll want to hear this part."

"Ivo?" The Cantor's voice cracked across the nave. "Brother Ivo? What is the meaning of —"

"You know exactly what it means, Your Grace." Ivo's voice carried better than he expected; twenty years of reading aloud in silent rooms had trained it. "Because you wrote it. Not a century ago. Your predecessor wrote it. Same hand, same ink, same lie." He turned to the congregation, arms wide. "This book says two people will kindle a war against Vellamare. It has said so for a hundred years. And for a hundred years, anyone the Choir decided fit the description — they burned. The prophecy isn't a warning. It's a *warrant.*"

The nave erupted. Ushers moved. Somewhere a bell began ringing out of rhythm — alarm, not worship.

And then the floor opened.

* * *

**Coral**

The grate over the cistern channel gave way like a lid coming off a pot, and the light hit the wyrm first.

It had been blind for longer than the Cathedral had stood, feeling its way through the dark on sound and warmth, and when the sun fell across its pale scales it made a sound Coral felt in her sternum — not a roar, nothing so simple. A keening. Like something that had forgotten what daylight was and was remembering all at once, painfully, joyfully.

Behind it came the griffin-kin, forty of them, wings folded tight as they poured up through the breach, and the rats, hundreds upon hundreds, flowing along the walls like living mortar, and things Coral couldn't name — a creature of wet stone, a moth the size of a hawk trailing dust that glowed faintly blue. Twenty years she had whispered to strays in alleys. All that time, an entire hidden nation had been holding its breath beneath her feet.

The congregation did not scream, which surprised her. They shrank back, yes — a wave of bodies pressing against the pillars — but the screaming didn't come, because the beasts did not attack. They arranged themselves. Griffin-kin along the transept walls. The wyrm coiled in the crossing, its blind face turned toward the altar. Waiting. As if they too had rehearsed.

"We are not here to burn anything," Coral called out, and her gift carried it — not magic, just a voice shaped by years of talking to things that listened. "We're here because you were lied to. Look at the book. Look at your own archives. The Choir wrote your prophecy to justify a massacre, and they've been writing it ever since, one name at a time."

"The beast-speaker lies!" The Cantor's composure had shattered; spittle flew. "Seize her! Seize them both! This is the kindling — this is the war itself —"

"Is it?" Coral held up the forged book. "Or is this?"

Choir soldiers pushed through the west doors — a dozen, then more, blades drawn, forming up around the altar. Inquisitor Vale stood at their head in full gray regalia, and Coral's stomach dropped. So it hadn't been mercy after all. Three nights ago, cornered in the tannery loft, Seraphine had let her walk away — and then, of course, she had reported everything. Survival over truth. Coral understood it. Understanding didn't stop the grief.

"Seraphine," Ivo said quietly. "Don't."

Seraphine looked at him for a long moment. Then at the Cantor, who was shouting for the beasts to be put to the sword, for the speaker's head, for fire — always fire, the Choir's only answer since the beginning.

And then she did something no one in that cathedral ever forgot.

She drew her blade and set its point against the High Cantor's throat.

* * *

**Ivo**

The silence that fell was total. Ten thousand people, and not one breath.

"Your Grace," Seraphine said, and her voice was steady in the way Ivo knew meant it had cost her everything to make it so, "you will be quiet now."

"Vale." The Cantor didn't dare move his neck. "Think about what you —"

"I have thought about nothing else for eleven years." Her blade didn't waver. "Since my sister died in your cellars with a gift she couldn't help and a name you put on a list. You want a kindler, Your Grace? You've been looking in mirrors your whole life."

Ivo moved without deciding to. He took the forged book from Coral's hands, mounted the steps of the altar — soldiers frozen, uncertain, their orders colliding with the sight of their own inquisitor holding the Cantor hostage — and held the open pages before the congregation.

"The marginalia," he said. "Look at it. Scribes' marks. Draft corrections. A prophecy doesn't get *edited*, brothers and sisters — prophecies are remembered, not revised. But this one was drafted. Revised. Approved." His voice broke, and he let it. "I catalogued this archive for nine years. I obeyed. I believed obedience was holiness. It was just a slower kind of burning."

He turned to the last page, the portrait that had started everything — his own face, a century old, beside a woman with coral hair.

"This book named us before we were born. Do you understand what that means? It means the next book already names someone in this room. Someone's daughter. Someone's neighbor. Someone whose only crime will be being useful to a lie." He closed the book. "There is no coming war. There is only the one the Choir has been fighting for a hundred years, against children and cats and things that live in the dark because you left them nowhere else."

The wyrm keened again, low, and this time the sound moved through the congregation like wind through wheat. Ivo saw faces change — a baker who bought charms from Coral's stall, a widow whose cat had always seemed to answer her, a soldier whose grip on his blade slowly loosened.

"Put it down," someone called from the crowd. Another voice joined it. Then another.

The Cantor lunged — not at Seraphine, at the book, at the truth of it — and Seraphine pivoted, caught him, and drove him to his knees, her blade flat now against his collarbone, holding rather than cutting.

"It's over," she said. "All of it. Every list. Every cellar."

And beneath the rose window, in the light of a morning that had begun as an ordinary feast day, the griffin-kin spread their wings at last — not in threat, but the slow, deliberate unfurling of creatures claiming air they had been denied for a century — and the bells above began to ring.

Not the litany. Not the alarm.

Something new. Something none of them had heard before, wild and unscripted, as if the bells themselves had finally been given permission to say what they wanted.

Coral found Ivo's hand in the chaos. Her hair was fully loose now, blazing coral in the colored light, and she was crying and laughing at once.

"It's not over," she said. "You know it's not. Half the Choir won't accept this. The city will panic. There'll be trials, riots, maybe worse."

"I know," Ivo said.

"And everyone knows my face now. There's no stall in some quiet corner, no more hiding, no going back to —"

"Coral." He turned to her, and in the din of breaking history, smiled. "I've read enough endings to know the good ones don't end. They just get harder to write. Stay anyway."

Below them, the wyrm lifted its blind face to the sun, and the last page of the forged prophecy fluttered open — blank, waiting — and somewhere in the crowd, a child reached up and touched a griffin's wing, and did not burn, and was not struck down, and the world did not end.

It only, at long last, began.
