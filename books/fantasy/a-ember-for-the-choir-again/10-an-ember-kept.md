## 10. An Ember, Kept

The first frost of the year came on a Tuesday, and with it came my first customer who wasn't afraid of me.

She was a fishwife named Odile with hands like knotted rope and a voice that carried across three market rows without effort. She set a basket of silver-scaled dory on my counter and looked me dead in the eye—the way no one had looked at me for twenty years—and said, "I want a charm that keeps gulls off my catch. A real one. None of your knotted-string nonsense."

"Madame," I said, "I have never sold nonsense in my life."

"You sold plenty. Pretty knots and pretty lies." But she was smiling when she said it, and she slid a copper across the counter anyway. "Now you'll sell truth. About time."

I tied her a charm with a gull's feather at its heart and whispered to it in the old way, low enough that only the feather heard. When I handed it over, Odile turned it in her fingers, sniffed, and pronounced it adequate—which, from a Vellamare fishwife, was practically a benediction.

My stall sat in the same corner of the night market it had occupied for two decades, but everything about it had changed. The awning was new, dyed coral-red because Ivo had insisted with the particular stubbornness of a man who had spent his life among gray vellum and wanted one loud thing. The charms hung openly now—no more hiding the true ones behind the harmless ones, no more double rows, no more sleight of hand. Beasts' feathers, shed scales, whiskers freely given, each labeled in Ivo's careful archivist's hand: *Gull-peace. Rat-courage. Moth-luck.*

And the customers came. Slowly, suspiciously, the way people approach a dog that has bitten before—but they came.

The Choir had not fallen so much as cracked, the way ice cracks on a thawing river: all at once, then in long groaning pieces for months afterward. The High Cantor's trial filled the broadsheets for half a season. Seraphine Vale's name appeared in none of them; she had vanished the night the bells rang, and I thought about her more often than I admitted, wondering what it cost a woman to choose survival twice, and whether she ever slept.

Decriminalization, as it turned out, was not a bell you rang once. It was a slow tide. The Council repealed the Edict of Silence in the spring, then spent the summer arguing about what replaced it. There were registries proposed and withdrawn. There were neighborhoods where a beast-speaker could walk openly and neighborhoods where she'd best not, and everyone knew which were which. Two decades of fear does not evaporate because a law changes. Fear has its own gravity.

But the beasts were coming up.

That was the miracle I still hadn't gotten used to. The rats had been the first, of course—they were everywhere and always had been, and they took great personal satisfaction in being right about everything. Then the moths returned to the lamplighters' district in numbers that made the lamplighters nervous until they realized the moths were *working*, sorting themselves by color to signal which lamps needed trimming. A family of stone-martens moved into the eaves of the archive and Ivo fed them dried fish and pretended this was normal scholarly practice.

And sometimes, on quiet evenings, something larger passed through the market square—a shadow with too many joints, a shape like a heron but wrong, beautiful, watching. The crowd parted around those shapes the way water parts around a prow: carefully, and with a kind of held breath that was not entirely fear anymore.

* * *

Ivo found me at closing, as he always did, carrying two paper cones of roasted chestnuts and wearing the expression of a man about to be pedantic.

"You've mislabeled the moth-luck," he said, handing me a cone. "Third shelf. You wrote 'moth-luck' but that charm is braided with swallow-feather. It's either swallow-luck or fraud."

"It's moth luck delivered by swallow post," I said. "It's called innovation."

"It's called chaos, and an archivist cannot abide it." He sat down on the crate beside mine, shoulder warm against mine, and we ate chestnuts while the market lit itself lamp by lamp around us. He smelled of vellum dust and woodsmoke, and after eight months I still caught myself cataloguing him the way he catalogued books—spine, binding, condition: *beloved.*

He had resigned from the Choir's archive the week after the Cathedral. They'd offered him a promotion, of all things—a reward for his part in the exposure, as if truth-telling were a service rendered rather than a betrayal survived. He'd declined in writing, in triplicate, because he said if you were going to burn a bridge you should do it with proper documentation. Now he kept a small open reading room above a chandler's shop, where anyone could come read what the sealed archive had hidden: the forged prophecy, the purge ledgers, the names. Especially the names. He spent his evenings copying them out, the kindlers and the speakers and the beasts, so that no one could ever again claim they had never existed.

"The wyrm asked about you today," he said.

My chest did the thing it always did at that. Old Blind Wyrm, who had waited beneath the city for a hundred years in the dark, who had known my grandmother's grandmother perhaps, or so the rats claimed. She had come up into the daylight twice since the thaw, both times at dusk, both times to the sound of the whole district holding its breath. She was teaching the children now—quietly, unofficially—to listen to stone. The Council pretended not to know. The children pretended very badly.

"What did she ask?"

"Whether you were happy." He looked at me sideways. "I told her I'd have to check."

"And? Check."

He set down his chestnuts and took my hand, turning it over, studying the palm like a page. "There's a scar here," he said, "from a charm wire. And ink here, from labeling. And calluses here from twenty years of work nobody thanked you for." He pressed his thumb into the center of my palm. "Verdict: happy. With reservations."

"Reservations?"

"There's a woman in the Sable Quarter who still crosses the street when she sees your stall. There are Council clerks who call your gift 'the irregularity.' There is a registry bill coming back in the spring session—I've read the draft, Coral, it's dressed up in gentle language but it's a leash." His jaw tightened. "So. Happy, with reservations. Happy the way a fire is warm with wind blowing on it."

I leaned my head against his shoulder. Below us the market murmured, and somewhere in it a child laughed at a stone-marten stealing a bun, and the sound was ordinary, and ordinariness was the thing I had traded twenty years of safety to buy.

"I spent my whole life being invisible," I said. "I got good at it. Good enough that some nights I wasn't sure there was anyone left under the hiding." I turned my hand in his and laced our fingers. "You know what I keep thinking? The prophecy was a lie, but it was almost true. It said two kindlers would start a war. We didn't start a war. But we did kindle something. It just wasn't the thing they wrote down."

"No," Ivo said softly. "It was smaller and much harder to put out."

* * *

We closed the stall together, as we did every night—me dousing the lamps, him squaring the charm labels until they faced precisely forward, both of us pretending not to notice the other pretending. At the edge of the square, where the cobbles gave way to the old harbor road, we stopped, because we always stopped there, because that was where the world still required a small act of courage.

A griffin-kin stood waiting by the harbor rail.

She was young—young for her kind—with storm-gray feathers and the restless weight-shift of someone not used to being seen. The night folk of the market had named her Perrine, though I suspected she'd chosen it herself from a shop sign. She dipped her beaked head to me, formal as a courtier.

"Speaker. The wyrm sends word. The deep tunnels flooded again last week—salt water, rising. The old warrens below the tidal line won't hold another winter. There are forty-one souls who need new ground before the spring melt, and the Council's land committee has tabled the petition twice."

I felt Ivo go still beside me, the archivist in him already reaching for records and precedents. I felt the older thing in me too—the speaker, the woman who had once begged rats for scraps of truth in the dark—stretch and wake.

Forty-one souls. Once, I would have carried that number alone, silently, invisibly, and been crushed by it. Now I had a stall where truth sold better than lies, an archivist who fought with paperwork, a wyrm teaching children, and a city learning, slowly, imperfectly, with reservations, to look at what it had spent a century refusing to see.

"Tell the wyrm we'll bring it to the Council ourselves," I said. "In person. All forty-one voices."

Perrine's feathers lifted in something that might have been a smile. "They warned me you'd say that."

"They know me well."

She lifted off the rail and was gone into the dark over the harbor, and I stood a moment watching the space where she'd been, my hand in Ivo's, the frost glittering on the cobbles, the ember in my chest—not the prophecy's ember, not the war's ember, but mine, kept, tended, chosen—burning steady against the cold.

"Well," Ivo said. "Shall we go make some more reservations disappear?"

I laughed, and we walked home through the waking market, side by side, entirely visible.
