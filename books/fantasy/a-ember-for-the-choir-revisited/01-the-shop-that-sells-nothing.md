## 1. The Shop That Sells Nothing

The salt came in before dawn, the way it always did.

Elodie Whitlock measured it by candlelight because measuring it by daylight felt too much like honesty. Coarse grey salt from the marsh flats south of the city, a penny the pound, worth exactly nothing to anyone who didn't know what to do with it. She poured it into small clay jars and pressed the lids down with her thumbs, and if her fingers moved in patterns that weren't strictly necessary—if she traced a line around each jar's rim that no grocer would recognize—well. Vessing had outlawed magic twenty years ago, but it had never outlawed seasoning.

The sign over her shop read WHITLOCK'S GOODS, which was a lie so complete it circled back around to truth. She sold goods. Salt for seasoning. Knotted red thread sold as hair ornaments, three for two coppers, and if you wore one braided into your hair on the day of a hard bargain, that was your own superstition and none of hers. Dried lavender labeled *for drawers*. Little stitched sachets of mugwort marked *moth repellent*, though moths were not, strictly speaking, the thing they repelled.

She had been doing this for nineteen years, four months, and eleven days, and she could have told you to the hour how long, because she counted everything against the night the counting started.

"Morning, Mistress Whitlock." Pell, the cooper's boy, ducked under the low lintel with his cap in his hands. He was fourteen and grew an inch a month and hadn't yet learned to navigate his own elbows. "Da wants the good salt. The—"

"The coarse grey, I know. Tell him it's the same as last time." She wrapped a jar in brown paper, tied it with plain string, plain string always, and slid it across the counter. "And tell him if he keeps brining in green wood, no amount of anything I sell will fix his fires."

Pell grinned, paid, and paused at the door the way boys did when they wanted to ask something and knew they shouldn't. He asked anyway.

"My mum says the luck knots work. Properly work. Is that—" He lowered his voice to a whisper that wouldn't have woken a moth. "Is that allowed?"

"It's hair ornamentation," Elodie said. "Allowed since the day the laws changed."

"Yes'm." He went out, and she watched him through the window as he unwrapped the jar immediately in the street, sniffed the salt, frowned at it for smelling like nothing at all, and carried it off anyway, careful as an egg.

Her mother used to say that a lie repeated long enough got comfortable furniture and stopped answering the door. Elodie had made her peace with comfortable furniture.

* * *

Vessing in the morning was smoke and gulls and the rattle of cartwheels on cobbles worn soft as old leather. From her door she could see down Harrow Lane to the fish market, where women argued prices with a violence that was mostly affection, and beyond it the grey shoulder of the palace hill, where King Aldric's banners hung limp in the windless air. Black crowns on white fields. Twenty years, and nobody could remember what the old banners looked like anymore, or pretended they couldn't.

There were new notices pasted to the wall of the chandlery across the lane. She crossed to read them without seeming to cross, drifting the way a woman drifts when she's checking the light. CURFEW HOURS EXTENDED BY ORDER OF THE CROWN INQUISITION. ALL PERSONS ABROAD AFTER THE SECOND BELL TO BE QUESTIONED. Below it, in smaller type: REPORT UNLICENSED PRACTICE. REWARDS AVAILABLE.

*Unlicensed practice.* The phrase had a bureaucratic smoothness to it, like a stone tumbled by water until all its edges were gone. Once it had been called what it was—the closing of the Hollow Gate, the burning of the choir-houses, the Purge—and people had died screaming in the streets with their names torn out of them. Now it was *unlicensed practice*, and there were rewards available.

Elodie went back inside and swept a floor that was already clean.

She kept busy through the morning. A widow bought lavender for her drawers. Two sisters bought matching luck knots before a wedding, giggling, and Elodie found herself smiling at their laughter like a woman warming her hands at someone else's fire. An old man named Corb asked after headache salts, and she gave him willow bark with a pinch of something else folded into the paper, and neither of them said what the pinch was for.

It was a good day, by the standards she'd learned to use. Nobody arrested. Nobody searched. Nobody looked at her twice.

At noon a crown inquisitor walked past her window, and her hands went still on the counter until he was gone.

They'd been coming through more often lately, the inquisitors. Grey coats, black crowns stitched at the collar, faces trained into a pleasant blankness that was worse than menace because you couldn't tell what it wanted. For nineteen years they'd mostly left Harrow Lane alone—a poor street, a dull street, a street selling seasoning and hair ornaments—but the notices were new, and the extended curfew was new, and Elodie had survived this long by believing that when the wind changed, it changed for a reason.

When the shop emptied, she went to the back room, stood before the brick wall, and did not look at it.

That was the ritual, though even she had stopped calling it one. Every day, once at least, she stood three paces from the sealed Hollow Gate and refused to feel whatever she might feel about it. It looked like brick. It had looked like brick for nineteen years, since she'd sung the seal into it with Wren's blood still on her hands, and the singing had cost her more than she could name, and now it was brick, dense and dumb and dead, and the only hum in the world was the one she imagined sometimes, late at night, when grief dressed itself up as hearing.

"Wren," she said, to the bricks, because some rituals you couldn't kill entirely.

The bricks said nothing back. They never had. That was the whole point of them.

* * *

Evening came down over Vessing like a lid. She shuttered the shop at first bell, ate bread and pickled things standing up at the counter because sitting felt like conceding the day was over, and then did the second part of the ritual, the nightly part, the part she'd never once skipped.

She took the lamp and went down to the cellar.

The stairs complained under her weight, thirteen steps, each one memorized. The cellar was dry and cool and smelled of earth and old salt, shelves along one wall holding stock she didn't trust herself to sell, and along the far wall—

Along the far wall, brick. But wrong, if you knew how to look. The mortar lines curved where mortar should run straight, faint as veins under skin. The bricks were slightly too regular, like teeth. Nineteen years ago it had been a gate: tall, hollow, breathing, a doorway onto somewhere that wasn't anywhere, and the choir had sung through it, and the kings of the old line had drawn their right to rule from what came through, and Wren had died with her hands on the frame trying to hold it open while soldiers burned the city above.

Elodie set the lamp on its hook and put her palm flat against the false brick.

"Cold," she said aloud, because saying it aloud made it true. "Dead. Sealed."

Her palm stayed flat against the wall longer than any check required. This was the part she never let herself finish quickly, the part where she waited, some traitorous scrap of her heart listening for a hum that had never once come. Because if it hummed—if the Gate stirred—then the sealing could be undone, and if it could be undone, then Wren had died for a door Elodie had chosen to close behind her, and Elodie did not know what she would be if she ever had to carry that thought in daylight.

So: cold. Dead. Sealed. Nineteen years of small charms and salt and luck knots and lying well enough to keep a whole street safe, because that was the trade. Her freedom for the Gate's silence. Her sister's memory entombed in masonry. A fair price. A price paid once and never refunded.

She took her hand away. She turned to climb the stairs.

The lamp guttered.

Not the wind—the cellar had no drafts, she'd sealed those herself decades ago, that was the point of cellars—but the flame leaned hard toward the far wall anyway, stretched thin, and for half a heartbeat the shadows of every shelf in the room bent the wrong way, toward the brick instead of away from it.

Elodie stood very still.

Then the flame righted itself, ordinary again, and the cellar was just a cellar, and she was just a tired woman who hadn't slept properly in nineteen years and saw what tired women saw. She picked up the lamp. Her hand shook only a little.

At the top of the stairs she blew it out and stood a moment in the dark of her own shop, listening to Vessing settle around her: a dog barking near the fish market, carts being chained for curfew, the second bell tolling from Speaker's Square, flat and heavy, the sound of a city locking its doors.

Beyond all of it, faint as a pulse in the ear, she almost heard the bells of the palace hill begin to ring too—and stop, mid-peal, as though a hand had been laid over them.

Elodie Whitlock stood in the dark and counted to ten, the way she had taught herself during the worst year, and told herself the silence afterward meant nothing at all.

Then someone knocked on her door.
