## 3. What the Crown Remembers

Aldric Vess woke with frost on the inside of his skin.

That was how it felt, anyway—not cold exactly, but a glittering numbness beneath the surface of him, as though someone had breathed winter into the hollow of his chest and sealed it there. He lay in the dark of his bedchamber and did not move and tried to remember falling asleep.

He could not.

He could remember the council session—most of it. Lord Chancellor Breck droning about grain tariffs from the southern provinces. The ambassador from Callowmere with her careful smile. His own voice saying *the crown's position is unchanged*, which was what he always said, which was nothing at all. He remembered the candles guttering low. He remembered rising, or thought he did.

Then nothing. A gap, smooth-edged, like a page torn cleanly out of a ledger. And now this: dawn light through the shutters, his body heavy and rested in a way he did not feel entitled to, and the frost inside him, glittering.

He had begun keeping count of the gaps. It was not something he told anyone. There was no one to tell. But on the little slate he kept locked in the drawer of his writing desk—the one with the false bottom, the one that had once held his mother's letters—he made a mark for every morning he woke without knowing how he'd come to bed. Four marks in the last month. Then seven. This morning he scratched the eleventh into the slate with a fingernail because he couldn't find the stylus, and the sound of it in the quiet room was like a small animal gnawing.

Eleven mornings out of thirty.

He sat up. His body obeyed promptly, smoothly, without the stiffness of a man of forty-one years. That too was wrong. Lately his body felt less like a house he lived in and more like a carriage he rode in—one driven by someone else who knew the roads better than he did.

The crown sat on its stand across the room, catching the first grey light.

It had belonged to his father, and his father's father, back through eleven kings to Aldric the Founder, who had worn it at the founding of Vessing when the city was a fishing village and the Gate was a wound in the world. It was old gold, thin as wire in places, worked into a circlet of interlocking shapes that might have been leaves or might have been teeth. Court historians called it the Continuum Crown. The servants, when they thought themselves unheard, called it the Old Man.

Aldric looked at it the way a man looks at a dog that has begun watching him too closely.

"Good morning," he said. His voice came out rough. He cleared his throat.

The crown said nothing. Crowns do not. But the frost inside him shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond before it breaks, and somewhere very far down in himself he heard—or felt, or remembered hearing—a word in a language he did not know and yet understood perfectly, the way you understand a blow.

*Soon.*

He got dressed without calling his valet. Another new habit. He did not entirely trust what his hands might be asked to do while another man laced his collar.

* * *

The council chamber smelled of tallow and wet wool, and by the second hour Aldric had lost the thread of the discussion, which was about the harbor levy, and was instead watching his own hands where they lay folded on the table before him.

They were resting too still. That was the thing no one else would notice. A living man's hands are never still; they shift, they tap, they worry a ring or a cuff. His lay like a dead man's, arranged, patient. He watched them the way you watch a sleeping snake.

"—and the Crown's position, Majesty?" Breck was saying.

Aldric opened his mouth to say *the crown's position is unchanged*, and what came out instead was:

"The levy will stand at nine parts in the hundred. The harbormaster will be replaced. Send for him at noon."

His voice. Exactly his voice. The cadence even, the slight Vessing burr on the *r*. But he had no opinion on the harbormaster. He had met the man twice. He did not know why he should be replaced, or what offense—

Breck bowed. "As Your Majesty commands."

Around the table, heads dipped. No one questioned it. Why would they? It sounded like a king. It was coming out of a king. And kings are not questioned; they are weathered, endured, obeyed.

Aldric sat through the rest of the session with his heart going hard and small behind his ribs, listening to himself speak. Because that was what it was—he had finally admitted it to himself in the dark hours, in the language of a man naming an illness: he was listening. When the crown's business was ordinary, he drove. But more and more often now there were moments—a pause, a decision, a sentence beginning one way and ending another—when he became audience. A passenger with a fine view of the road and no hand near the reins.

And always, underneath, the whispering.

It spoke in the First Tongue. He knew that was its name because every child raised in the palace learns the warning along with their letters: *the First Tongue is the language the world was bound in; if you hear it spoken, run.* The priests said it was the speech of angels. The old wives said it was the speech of the thing under the hill. The Purge laws, written in his grandfather's time, said it was sorcery, punishable by fire, and the last person burned for speaking it had been a girl of nineteen, twenty years ago, and Aldric had been a young prince then and had stood in the crowd because his father made him, because a king must learn what his laws look like.

He had never forgotten her face. She hadn't screamed. She had kept talking, right up to the end, in that same language the crown whispered to him now—and the horrible thing, the thing he had never told anyone, was that standing there in the smoke he had understood every word.

She had been saying: *it isn't dead, it's only sleeping, it wears your father, it will wear you.*

He had told himself it was grief-madness, terror, the heat. He had told himself that story for twenty years.

Now, in the council chamber, while his own mouth discussed the replacement of a harbormaster he could not name, the whisper rose again beneath his thoughts, patient as water finding a crack:

*The key walks. The door remembers. Soon we go home.*

Home. The word landed in him strangely, the way a stranger's key sometimes fits a lock up to the last turn. Home was the palace. Home was Vessing. Unless home was something older, under the palace, under the city, under everything—a door, a gate, a hollow place—

"Aldric."

He blinked. The chamber was emptying. Breck stood beside his chair, gathering papers, and the chancellor's face wore the expression of a man deciding whether to mention a stain on a friend's coat.

"Majesty. Forgive me. You were—elsewhere."

"I was thinking," Aldric said.

"Yes." Breck hesitated. He was sixty, grey, loyal in the way of men who have served one family so long that loyalty and habit are indistinguishable. "Majesty, may I speak plainly?"

"You always do. It's why I keep you."

"Three times this month," Breck said quietly, "you have given orders you countermanded the next day with no memory of giving them. Yesterday you signed a warrant for the Inquisition to expand its search into the market districts—you will recall—"

"I recall," Aldric lied.

"—and this morning the Inquisitor Vale reports the search proceeds, but Majesty, the warrant named a *child*. A runaway ward of the palace. I have no record of any such ward. The nursery rolls show nothing. I have checked twice." Breck set the papers down. "What child, Majesty?"

And Aldric reached for an answer and found the frost waiting there instead, glittering, and through the frost a picture swam up like a face through iced glass:

A narrow shopfront. A crooked sign. A woman in the doorway with iron-grey hair and a keeper's stance, feet planted, chin level—the stance of a woman guarding a door. Behind her, half-hidden in her skirts, a boy. Mud-streaked, small, maybe eight years old, with eyes too old for his face and a silence around him like held breath.

The vision lasted the length of a heartbeat. It was not memory. He had never seen either of them. It was placed in him, the way a signet ring presses its seal into wax—someone else's knowledge, stamped into his softness.

*A shopkeeper,* whispered the crown, in the First Tongue, sweet as a nurse's lullaby. *An old keeper grown lazy. And our key, wandering loose in the rain. Find them, little king. Open your mouth and find them.*

"Aldric?" Breck said.

Aldric found that he was standing. He had no memory of standing. His hand was halfway to the bell-pull that would summon the captain of his guard.

He turned the motion into reaching for his cup instead, drank cold tea, and smiled the smile he had practiced all his life, the one that ended conversations.

"No child of mine is missing, Breck. The Inquisition chases shadows; let them chase. It keeps them honest." He heard himself say it and marveled, distantly, at how easily the lie came—except it wasn't his lie, was it? Or it was. He could no longer find the seam between his words and the other words. That was the true horror, he thought. Not being overthrown. Being *edited*.

Breck studied him a moment longer than comfort allowed, then bowed and withdrew.

Alone in the chamber, Aldric pressed both palms flat against the table until they trembled, and made himself look at the truth he had been circling for a year the way a tongue circles a broken tooth:

Something wore his crown. Something had been wearing it longer than him—through his father, through his grandfather, maybe through all eleven kings back to the Founder—and it had grown strong enough lately, hungry enough, awake enough, to wear *him*. And it wanted a child. And it meant to use his guards, his inquisitors, his seal, his voice, to get that child.

There was a mirror on the far wall of the council chamber, tall and silvered, older than the palace around it. Aldric crossed to it and made himself look.

A king looked back. Tall, broad, greying at the temples, dressed in the black and gold of Vessing. A familiar stranger. And behind the eyes—behind his own eyes, which was the unbearable part—something watched him back with the flat patience of a thing that has waited centuries and can wait centuries more.

"You'll not have the boy," Aldric said aloud.

The reflection smiled. He was not smiling.

*Little king,* said the frost inside him, gently, the way you speak to a beloved animal you must soon put down. *You are not the rider. You never were. You are only the horse.*

* * *

That night he did not sleep. He sat at his writing desk with the false-bottomed drawer open and his mother's letters spread out before him, reading them for the fourth time since the gaps began, hunting for what he might have missed.

His mother had died when he was nine. Before that, in the last year of her life, she had changed—grown watchful, thin, quick to anger, and once, memorably, she had gripped his wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *if ever you wake and don't know where you've been, don't tell anyone, and don't let them put the weight on you alone.* He had thought she meant the crown's literal weight, the neck-ache every king complained of. He had been a child. Children take the smallest door out of a frightening room.

But in her final letter, unfinished, unsent, tucked at the bottom of the bundle in handwriting gone shaky as a spider's walk, there was a single line he had read a hundred times without understanding:

*If it wakes fully, go to the Hollow Gate. Whatever keeper holds it now—even the Whitlock girl, even after what happened—go to her, Aldric. Swear it.*

The Whitlock girl. Wren Whitlock. The girl who had burned.

Aldric sat very still. Outside, the bells of Vessing rang the curfew hour, and the sound went through him oddly, ringing in the frost like a finger round a crystal bowl. Somewhere in this city there was a shop that sold nothing, a keeper who had buried a sister and sealed a door, and a child the crown sang to across the rooftops.

He could summon the guard. He could send a hundred men to the market district tomorrow and take the child into protective custody—except whose protection? Every order he gave passed through the crown's fingers on its way out of his mouth. He had watched his own signature authorize searches he did not remember wanting. If he moved openly, he would deliver the boy directly into the thing's hands, wrapped in royal ribbon.

No. If he was a passenger, then he must become a stowaway in his own body. Small movements. Unwatched ones. Things a king does not do, and therefore things the crown does not think to steer.

He took off his signet ring—the heavy gold one, the Vessing stag, the one no document left this palace without—and closed his fist around it until the edges bit. It was proof, if he ever needed to prove himself to a stranger: the king's own hand could not be trusted to carry a message, but the king's own ring, given freely, might.

Tomorrow night, perhaps. Tomorrow night he would find a reason to dismiss his guard early. Tomorrow night he would walk out of his own palace like a thief, down through streets he barely knew, to a crooked-signed shop in the market district, and he would ask a charm-seller for help the way beggars ask—with his hand out and his pride already lost.

The crown on its stand across the darkened bedchamber caught the candlelight and threw it back in little linked circles, leaf-shaped, tooth-shaped.

*Sleep, little king,* it whispered, fond as a father. *We ride at dawn.*

Aldric blew out the candle, and in the dark, with frost glittering in the hollow of his chest, he began—quietly, under his breath, in a language he had sworn never to speak—to practice saying *help me.*
