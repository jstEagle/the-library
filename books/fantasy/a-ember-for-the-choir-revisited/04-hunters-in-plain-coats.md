## 4. Hunters in Plain Coats

The knock came at half past nine, and it was wrong.

Elodie knew every knock her street produced. The baker's daughter rapped twice and waited. The tax assessor knocked like he was apologizing. Drunks pounded in threes and slid down to the cobbles. This was four measured strikes, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who had been trained that doors opened for him.

She set down the luck knot she'd been retying—third time that morning; her hands weren't steady—and looked through the shutter crack.

Two men stood in the market fog. Not the grey coats of the city watch, not the palace livery either. Plain brown wool, plain boots, plain faces, the kind of plainness that cost money. One held a slate with a list chalked on it. The other was looking at her shop sign—the little painted hen she'd hung there fifteen years ago because a charm shop needed to look harmless—and his look had weight to it, the way a hand on a scale has weight.

Inquisitors. Or whatever the crown called them this decade. Wardens, when she was young. Seekers, before that. The names changed; the coats stayed plain.

Elodie stepped back from the shutter and did what she had done ten thousand times before: nothing remarkable. She tied off the knot, dusted it with salt, and set it in the window basket among the others. Then she opened the door with the expression of a woman mildly inconvenienced by morning.

"Morning," said the one with the slate. He had a voice like wet gravel and the patience of a man being paid by the hour. "We're asking after a runaway ward of the palace. Boy, seven or eight years old, dark hair, mute. Seen him?"

"Mute," Elodie repeated. She let her brow crease just enough. "Poor thing. No. Children don't come here. Charms are for spinsters and sailors."

The second man hadn't stopped looking at the shop sign. Now he looked past her, into the dim interior, at the shelves of knots and jars and the crooked rows of labeled salts. His gaze snagged on the curtain behind the counter—the one that hid the back room, which hid the cellar door, which hid the Gate—and moved on so smoothly that Elodie knew, with a cold drop in her stomach, that it had not moved on at all.

"Mind if we have a look?" said the slate man.

"On what?"

"The premises. Standard sweep." He produced a folded paper, wax-sealed, and let her see only enough of it to know it was official. "Crown business. You'll be compensated for your time."

"I'm minding a shop," Elodie said. "My time isn't worth compensating." She stepped aside—not eagerly, not sullenly, but with the weary compliance of someone who had learned that resistance was a smell these men could track. "Don't touch the jars. Some of the oils stain."

They didn't touch the jars. That was the first strange thing. They walked the aisles like men reading a room rather than searching it, and the plain-faced one paused at the curtain, lifted it with two fingers, and looked into the back room for exactly as long as a man looks at a room containing nothing.

Behind the wall, behind the brick that wasn't brick, Elodie felt the Gate hold its breath.

* * *

They were gone by noon, working up the street toward the tannery. Elodie locked the door, turned the sign to closed—a thing she had not done during market hours in twenty years—and went down into the cellar.

The boy was wedged in the corner where the shelving met the stone, knees to chest, watching her with the flat stillness of something that had learned stillness as survival. He'd been down there since before dawn, since she'd woken to find him standing in her kitchen barefoot, silent, staring at the wall behind which the Gate hummed its new low note.

"Hungry?" she asked.

He didn't answer. He never answered. But when she set down bread and the heel of hard cheese and a cup of watered wine, he ate the way children eat who have known hunger not as an event but as a climate—carefully, saving some, watching her between bites.

She crouched a few feet away, deliberately below his eyeline, hands visible. Twenty years of selling charms to frightened people had taught her how to be small without seeming small.

"You can't stay in the shop," she said quietly. "They'll come back. They always come back, and next time they'll bring dogs, or worse, they'll bring someone who can feel what you are." She turned her palm up. "I need you to come here. Just here. I need to show you something, and I need you not to scream."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he rose, crossed the cellar, and took her hand—and through her skin came that faint vibration again, deeper than sound, the hum of a struck bell heard through the floor of the world. The Gate answering him. The Gate, which had been silent as true brick for twenty years, singing under her feet like a plucked wire.

"There," she breathed. "You feel that? That's why they want you. And that's why I'm going to hide you somewhere they've already searched, because no one searches the same hole twice." She almost smiled. "Almost no one."

There was a space behind the Gate itself—not through it, never through it, she would sooner put her hand in fire—but beside it, a gap between the Gate's frame and the true foundation stone of Vessing's oldest wall, a pocket maybe four feet deep that Wren had found when they were girls and that Elodie had walled over with a facing of loose brick after the Purge. It smelled of cold earth and old magic, the way a church smells of incense long after the service. She pried three bricks free with a knife blade, widened the gap, and lined it with the old horse blanket she kept for winter nights when the shop's hearth failed.

"In," she said. "If anyone comes, you're a shadow and a silence. Can you do that?"

The boy climbed into the gap and pulled the blanket to his chin. Before she replaced the bricks, he caught her sleeve. His mouth worked. Nothing came out—she could see now that it wasn't refusal, it was ruin, something taken from him or never given—and frustration flooded his face until he did the only thing left to him. He pressed her palm flat against the stone beside the Gate, and into her skin, wordless, poured an image: a white room. A cot. A window too high to see out of. Hands in grey gloves. And singing, always, faintly, from beneath the floor.

Elodie sat back on her heels. Her mouth had gone dry.

"They raised you," she said. "In the palace. Over—" she swallowed— "over whatever's under it."

He nodded once.

"All right," she said, and heard her own voice go hard and level, the voice she used at the end of a bargaining day. "All right. Then you need a name. I can't keep calling you boy in my own head—they say names carry, and I'd rather yours carried something better than *ward*." She studied his face: sharp chin, mud still in the creases of his ears, eyes the brown of river water over stones. "Tam," she decided. "It means nothing. It's nobody's name. Nobody will go looking for a Tam."

Something flickered across his face that might, in a child allowed expressions, have been the beginning of a smile.

* * *

Ivo Coverly came at dusk, which meant trouble, because Ivo Coverly came at dusk only when he couldn't risk being seen coming at all.

He'd been sergeant of the market watch for eleven years, and before that, twenty years ago, he'd been a young constable who stood in the rain outside a burning gatehouse and chose, deliberately, to see nothing. They had never spoken of it. That was the whole architecture of their friendship: a shared blindness, maintained with care, like a garden neither of them admitted to watering.

He came in the back way, through the yard, and stood dripping in her kitchen looking older than his fifty years, hat in his hands.

"They're sweeping tomorrow," he said without preamble. "Every building on Cordwainer Row. Door to door. Not the watch—inquisition men, with a writ signed under the privy seal. We're ordered to assist." His jaw worked. "*I'm* ordered to assist."

"How many buildings?"

"Thirty-one. Yours is fourteenth on the list." He finally sat, heavily, uninvited, which was also unlike him. "Elodie. What's in your shop?"

"Charms."

"Elodie."

"Salt. Knots. Dried lavender. A great deal of honest nothing." She poured him tea because her hands wanted occupation. "Why are you telling me this, Ivo? If you believed I had nothing, you'd have stayed home."

He looked into the cup for a while. Rain ticked against the window. When he spoke, it was to the tea, not to her.

"There's a man on the inquisition detail named Ferren. I've known him since he was a recruit. Good man. Careful man. Today he comes back from your street and he says to me, quiet, like he's ashamed of it: 'That charm shop on Cordwainer Row, the one with the hen on the sign.'" Ivo's eyes came up. "'That shop has a *floor*.'"

The words landed in the kitchen and lay there.

"A floor," Elodie repeated.

"He means it doesn't read like one. Underneath. Like a room that's more room than it should be." Ivo rubbed his face. "Twenty years I've walked past your shutters, Elodie Whitlock. Twenty years I've noticed you buy more lamp oil in winter than a shop that size burns, and you take deliveries of river stone no jeweler uses, and once a year, on the same night every year, your lights burn till dawn and I have pretended—I have *worked* at pretending—not to wonder why."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere below them, faint as a heartbeat heard through a pillow, the Gate hummed.

"If they find what I think they might find," Ivo said, "it won't be a search anymore. It'll be a pyre. And you'll be at the center of it, and so will whoever you're hiding, and I'll be standing in your doorway holding the torch they handed me." He set down the cup untouched. "So I'm asking you once, as the only favor I will ever ask: give me a reason to believe my pretending has been worth it. Or tell me where you need me standing tomorrow when they come through your door."

Elodie looked at her oldest friend—at the tired, decent, compromised shape of him—and understood that he had just laid his whole life on her counter like a merchant laying out goods, and was waiting to see if she'd steal it.

"The fourteenth building," she said. "What time?"

"Mid-morning. After the tannery. There'll be six of them and me."

"And the man Ferren—the one with the feeling about floors. Will he be there?"

"Yes."

"Then stand next to Ferren," Elodie said. "All day. Talk to him. About anything—his wife, his boots, the weather. Keep his mind on the surface of the world." She held his gaze. "And when they reach my cellar, Ivo, you'll be the one to walk down those stairs first. And you'll see salt jars and empty crates and a very boring room. Because that's what's going to be there. I have until mid-morning to make certain of it."

"That's not an answer to my question."

"It's the only one I have. The rest costs more than either of us can pay."

Ivo stood. At the door he paused, back to her, shoulders squared like a man bracing into wind. "Wren would have told me," he said quietly. "Whatever it was. She'd have laughed at me and told me."

"Wren is dead," Elodie said, "because she told people."

The words came out colder than she intended, and she watched them land, watched the old grief cross his face and hers alike. He put on his hat and went out into the rain without another word, and Elodie stood alone in her kitchen hating herself with the practiced ease of long habit.

* * *

She worked through the night.

By lamplight she rebuilt the cellar's truth. The loose bricks behind the Gate went back with fresh mortar mixed with ash and salt—old craft, Wren's craft, the kind that made stone forget it had ever been moved. She moved Tam's scent out with a bundle of his blanket washed in tannery runoff and buried in the yard. She salted the threshold against divination, then thought better of it—too obvious, a salted threshold on a charm-seller's door was a signature, not a defense—and swept it up and instead hung a new luck knot above the lintel, woven tight, carrying a single thread of the boy's hair hidden in its core: a finding-knot reversed, a losing-knot, the kind of work she hadn't attempted in twenty years and which made her fingers remember things her mind had tried to bury.

Tam watched her from the shadows all night. Near dawn, when her hands were shaking with fatigue, he padded across the cellar and pressed something into her palm.

A pebble. Grey, ordinary, river-worn. Except that it hummed.

She stared at it, then at him. "Where—"

He touched his own chest, then the pebble, then pointed down, through the floor, toward the Gate. Toward whatever lay beyond it. His face was grave and exhausted and, beneath everything, terrified—not of her, she realized. For her.

"No," she said, understanding. "You keep it. Whatever it is, you keep it close."

He shook his head and closed her fingers over the stone, and into her skin came the image again, insistent: the white room, the high window, the singing under the floor—and now a new piece, a corridor, and men in plain coats coming down it, and himself running, running, the singing rising behind him like floodwater.

They hadn't lost him. They'd let him run. He was bait walking, and everyone near him was in the trap's radius, and he knew it, and he was giving her the one thing he had anyway.

Elodie knelt and pulled him against her shoulder, and he went stiff as a plank for three full seconds before something in him broke and he clung like a drowning child, silent, shaking, his fists knotted in her apron.

Above them, dawn came grey over Cordwainer Row. Down the street, doors began to open. Boots rang on cobbles in pairs, unhurried, methodical, moving building to building.

Fourteenth on the list. Mid-morning.

Elodie wiped her face once, briskly, the way she did everything, and went upstairs to meet the day with flour on her hands and murder in her heart.
