## 5. The Language Under the Floor

*before*

There is a room with no windows and a floor that is warm.

Tam knows this because he was made in it. Not born. Made. The word for it is not *born*, the word is a long slow note that starts under the floorboards of his chest and goes down and down until it reaches somewhere older than the palace, older than the city, older than the names of things. He was sung into being. That is what the grey sisters told him when he was small enough to be carried, before they stopped carrying him, before they started measuring him instead.

*You are a vessel,* they said. *You are an empty cup. Be proud.*

But Tam was never empty. Even at four years old, even at five, there was something in him that hummed when the bells rang, something that turned over in his sleep like a dog dreaming. He learned early not to tell anyone about it.

The nursery had seven beds and only ever one child. He slept in all seven beds on different nights because the grey sisters said it kept his magic from pooling, though he never understood how sleeping in different beds could change what he was. The beds were all exactly alike. White sheets. White blankets. A wooden horse with one ear chewed off that he was allowed to keep because it had no magic in it at all, it was just wood, just a toy, and once a year on his naming-day he was permitted to hold it for one hour while someone watched.

He named the horse Wren. He didn't know why. The name came up out of him like water from a well, whole and sudden, and when he said it aloud the warm floor went cold for a moment — just a moment, just a shiver — and then warmed again, and the grey sisters wrote something down.

They wrote everything down. Every word he said. Especially the words he wasn't supposed to know.

* * *

The language lives under the floor.

That is where he first heard it, before he knew it had a name, before he knew it was older than kingdoms and heavier than crowns. At night, lying in whichever white bed was his that night, he would press his ear to the boards and listen. The palace has many floors and beneath them many more floors, and beneath those is a room the servants do not clean and the guards do not guard, because you cannot guard a thing by standing outside it, and the people who built this palace understood that.

Under the throne room there is a choir.

Not people. Voices. Voices in jars of amber light, hundreds of them, thousands maybe, arranged in rows that go back further than the light reaches. They sing without throats. They sing without rest. And their song comes up through the stone like heat through a hearth, and Tam's bed stood directly above the oldest part of the song, and so he grew the way bread grows near an oven: warmed through, risen, shaped by something he never saw.

The voices taught him while he slept. He would wake in the morning knowing words he had never been taught. Words that made candle flames stand up straight. Words that made the locked door of the nursery unlock itself if he said them softly enough. The grey sisters called this *contamination* and doubled his lessons and took away the wooden horse for a month, but you cannot punish a well for giving water. The words were already in him. He was mostly made of them.

Sometimes, very late, one voice sang louder than the others. A woman's voice, high and quick, always a little ahead of the melody like she was impatient with the song, like she wanted it to hurry up and be over. When that voice sang, Tam felt less alone. He never told anyone that either.

* * *

*the making*

This is what he remembers of the making, which is not much, because remembering it is like trying to remember being swallowed:

A circle of robed figures. Cold that came from inside his own bones. A voice saying *again* and *deeper* and *it must take root or all of this is wasted*. Hands that were not unkind, which was somehow worse than hands that were. And underneath everything, rising up through the floor, the choir singing the way rain sings on a roof — steady, patient, certain that eventually everything under the sky gets wet.

They had tried before. He understood that much even then, even small. There had been others. Cups that cracked. Vessels that leaked. Children who were grown and then gone, and the jars under the floor gained new neighbors each time, and the song got a little louder, and the robed figures said *again*.

Tam did not crack. That was his whole accomplishment, the one thing he was ever praised for. He held. He held the way a dam holds, the way a secret holds, the way a door holds when the thing behind it is patient.

And then the crown found him.

* * *

*crown*

The king does not visit the nursery. This is what everyone says. The king does not eat with the household, does not walk the gardens, does not speak except at council, and at council he speaks in a voice like frost forming on glass. Tam heard him once, from a stairwell, through a door. The voice was wrong in a way Tam's body understood before his mind did. It was a voice with too many speakers in it.

After that night, Tam began to dream of a crown.

It sat on a cushion of black velvet in a room with no doors, and it was beautiful the way a hook is beautiful to a fish, and it sang. It sang in the First Tongue, the deep old words, but it sang them *backwards*, inside out, a song that unmade instead of made, and Tam woke from these dreams with blood in his mouth from biting his tongue and the grey sisters writing, always writing.

Then the dreams stopped being dreams.

He was six, maybe seven. Time moved strangely in the nursery; the windows were painted shut and painted over, and seasons were things that happened to other people. But he remembers the morning he woke and his own mouth was already moving. Singing. Quietly, under the breath, the crown's backwards song, and he hadn't decided to sing it, he hadn't chosen it, his lips were simply doing what something far away wanted done, the way a puppet moves, the way a bell moves when the rope is pulled.

He clapped both hands over his mouth.

The song kept going. Muffled, behind his fingers, against his palms, humming up through his teeth. He ran to the corner of the nursery and pressed his face into the wall and sang into the plaster until a grey sister came and pried him away and looked at him with something that was almost fear, and wrote it down.

That was the day he understood what he was for.

Not a child. A mouth. A throat on legs. They had grown him from the Gate's own magic — he knew this now, the choir had shown him, images rising through the floor like bubbles through dark water: a door of pale stone in a cellar, a woman with quick hands sealing it shut, a scream, a sacrifice, power enough to grow a boy from nothing — and they had grown him as a key grows in a lock's imagination. And the crown, the old hungry thing, the first vessel, the king's hunger given permanence — the crown needed a living key to finish its rite, and Tam was the key, and the crown had begun to sing through him the way a man hums while he walks toward dinner.

He had maybe a season. Maybe less. Every night the backwards song came a little earlier, lasted a little longer. Every morning his hands were sore from gripping the bedsheets. He started sleeping sitting up, facing the wall, so no one would see his mouth move.

The woman-voice under the floor sang louder during those months. Quick and high, ahead of the melody. Once — only once — he was sure she sang actual words at him, words in Common, plain and human: *run, little one.* Or maybe she sang *wait.* He has thought about it every night since. Run or wait. He chose run.

* * *

*running*

Here is what running from a palace is like when you are small and the palace wants you back:

It is walls. It is drains. It is three days in the dark eating candle wax. It is learning that your own magic answers you now — the words the choir gave him open locks and still dogs and make lamps gutter — and hating it, because the magic is the reason they want him, and using it feels like leaving footprints made of himself.

It is the city above, huge and grey and afraid. Curfew bells. Coats with the crown's badge walking in pairs. Grown-ups who look through him because looking at children in this city is expensive.

It is rain. It is a storm like the sky coming apart. It is a street he does not know, ending in a shop with charms in the window that are not charms, salt sold as seasoning, knots sold as hair ornaments, a lie so tired and so careful that Tam recognized it instantly, the way you recognize another prisoner.

And it is a door opening, and a woman with tired eyes and quick hands — the same quick hands, he is almost sure, the same hands from the bubble-images under the floor, the hands that sealed the pale door — and when she touched him, when her skin met his, he felt the thing he had been carrying all this time finally answer something.

Behind her, behind the shop, behind a wall that was pretending to be a wall, something vast turned over in its sleep.

The Gate. He knew it the way you know your own heartbeat. They had grown him from this. He was standing in front of the room where he was born.

* * *

*now*

Now he sits in the cellar behind the false wall, cross-legged on cold flagstones, with the Gate humming at his back like a second heart. The woman — Elodie, the choir whispers her name to him at night, *Elodie, Elodie,* in the quick high voice that might be kindness and might be bait — has given him bread and a blanket and a name to wear. *Tam.* It fits loosely, like borrowed clothes. He is trying it on.

He cannot tell her any of this. His mouth will not make the shapes of Common; the words go in and stay in, sinking like stones. So he sits in the dark and presses his palm flat against the floor, the way he used to in the nursery, and listens to what comes up.

The choir is quieter here. Farther away. But not silent. Never silent. And tonight, tonight of all nights, with hunters in plain coats asking questions in the market and the woman upstairs lying to soldiers with a straight face, tonight the quick high voice is close, closer than it has ever been, close enough that Tam can almost make out the words —

— and the words are not *run* and they are not *wait*.

The words are a name. His name, the one from before, the vessel-name, the one written in the grey sisters' ledgers. Sung up through twenty years of stone and soil and the floor of a charm shop, sung soft and urgent, sung the way you call a child home at dusk.

And behind it, faint but rising, patient as rain, the crown has begun to hum along.
