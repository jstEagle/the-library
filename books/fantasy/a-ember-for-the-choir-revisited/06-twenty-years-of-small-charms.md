## 6. Twenty Years of Small Charms

The morning Ivo Coverly came to search my shop, I was braiding luck knots with my hands and remembering my sister's death, which is the way of things: the body does its small work while the mind walks old ground.

Ivo knocked the way he always knocked—three raps, a pause, a fourth, like a man who had never quite settled on how much authority a knock should carry. I set down the half-finished knot, brushed salt from my apron, and opened the door before he could knock again.

"Sergeant." Twenty years, and I still gave him his rank like a shield between us. "You're early. The bread's not up yet."

"I'm not here for bread, Ellie."

But he took the bread anyway, when it came. That matters later, or it doesn't. With Ivo it was hard to tell which kindnesses were strategy and which were just Ivo, and I think he had spent twenty years making sure no one could tell the difference, including himself.

He stepped inside, shaking rain from his coat, and looked around the shop with the flat professional gaze of a man who had been ordered to look and hated being ordered to do anything. Behind him the storm that had brought Tam to my door two nights past had broken into a thin grey drizzle, the kind that made the whole market district smell like wet stone and old fish. The street outside was empty except for a pair of Crown inquisitors in their plain grey coats, walking slowly up the far side, reading doors the way other men read posters.

"They're going shop to shop," Ivo said. "Whole street. Today. Mine's tomorrow, if you're curious, and there's nothing in mine either, so don't waste your sympathy." He turned his hat in his hands. "Elodie. There's a boy missing. Palace ward, they say. They say it very loudly, which usually means it's true and they wish it weren't."

"Palace keeps wards now?" I kept my voice at market pitch, light, faintly bored. "I thought the Purge cleaned out everything worth keeping."

"You know what I mean." He didn't move past the doorway. That was Ivo's mercy: he made you invite him in, so the record would say you invited him. "They'll want to see the cellar. Every building on the street. It'll be me and one of Vale's people, and I can slow them, but I can't stop them looking."

*Vale.* The name meant nothing to me then. It would come to mean a great deal. But that morning it was only a name, and I said, "Then come back this afternoon and look. Nothing here but seasoning and hair ornaments. You've seen the inventory."

"I've seen the inventory." He put his hat on. At the door he paused, back to me, shoulders squared against something. "Twenty years ago," he said, "you told me your sister went south. To Corvale, for her health."

The braid I'd left on the counter seemed suddenly loud, all that knotted thread, all those knots tied in shapes that were not hair ornaments and had never been.

"I did say that," I answered.

"Corvale's a sanatorium town, Ellie. Nobody goes there for their health who expects to keep it." He pulled the door shut behind him, gently, and I stood alone among my harmless charms and understood that Ivo Coverly had been carrying a question for twenty years and had only just now decided where to set it down.

* * *

Wren did not go south. Wren went down.

To understand what happened under the palace in the spring of the Purge, you have to understand what we were before. Two sisters, orphaned twice over—once by fever, once by the first edicts—and both of us keepers without knowing the word for it yet. Our grandmother had taught us the Gate the way she taught us bread: hands in the flour, no lectures, learn by doing. The Hollow Gate in the cellar of the shop was brick to every eye but ours. Brick, and a doorframe older than Vessing itself, and a seal our grandmother renewed each solstice with songs she claimed were lullabies and were, I know now, locks.

When the edicts came, Wren was nineteen and furious about it in the particular way of the young, who believe that grief is an argument you can win. She kept a notebook hidden in a false spine—*Principles of Binding, vol. 3, by E. Whitlock*, the spine read, and inside it pages of the First Tongue copied out in her looping hand. She wrote arguments in it too. Arguments against the Purge, against the crown, against me.

"You keep saying *keeper* like it means hiding," she told me, three weeks before the end. We were in the cellar. The Gate hummed under its seal, patient as ever. "A keeper keeps things safe. Not locked."

"It's the same word."

"It's never been the same word once, in the history of anything." She pressed her palm flat to the sealed door and smiled at whatever it said to her. The Gate liked Wren. It had always liked her best; even as children the light through its seams leaned toward her like a plant toward a window. "They're burning the quarter on Friday, El. The Mirelane houses. People are already running. If the Gate opened—"

"If the Gate opens, they'll find it." I was twenty-three and certain of everything, which is its own kind of ignorance. "And then it isn't one quarter burning, Wren. It's the city. It's everyone who ever bought a salt ring off me. You don't get to gamble other people's cells for your principles."

"And you don't get to call it duty when it's just fear wearing duty's coat."

She was right. I have had twenty years to consider whether being right made it better or worse, and I still don't know.

Friday came. The Mirelane burned, and the smoke stood over the city like a second sky, and Wren went down to the cellar while I slept off a headache I would carry, in one form or another, for the rest of my life. She opened the Gate. Not fully—a crack, a seam, a lambent line of that soft gold light spilling into the dark—but enough. Enough for a door. And the fleeing ones came through: eleven souls from the Mirelane, soot-streaked and silent, passing through my cellar and out into the hills beyond, through a door that led where doors don't.

I found out because the twelfth runner woke me, pounding on the shop door at dawn, saying *your sister says come now, the soldiers found the seam.*

By the time I reached the cellar, the King's men were already there. And Wren was standing in front of the Gate with her arms spread and her voice raised in the First Tongue—not a lullaby, not a lock, but the oldest word our grandmother had taught us besides, the word that holds a door against everything but love—and the captain of the King's men was shouting words that didn't matter, and someone had a censer, and the smoke in the room wasn't smoke.

They bound her mid-scream. I want to be exact about this because vagueness has been a kind of cowardice for me: they did not kill my sister. They took her voice out of her body and put it somewhere, and her body folded up like a coat falling off a hook, and the scream stayed wherever they had sent it, still screaming, and I heard it, that first night, humming faintly through the Gate's seams, and I hear it some nights still, when the wind is right, or wrong.

What I did after is the part I've turned over most. The soldiers dragged her body out. The runners had scattered. And I stood in front of the Hollow Gate—the door my grandmother kept, the door Wren died cracking open—and I sang it shut.

Not sealed. Sealed is gentle. I buried it. I used every binding in Wren's notebooks and two that came to me from nowhere, from the place where grandmothers' grandmothers keep their hands, and I wove the seal so deep and so cold that the Gate stopped humming entirely, and the light died in the seams, and it became, truly, brick. I made it into the lie everyone already believed.

Then I went upstairs and made bread, because the dough had risen and would fall if I didn't, and that was the day I learned the ugliest truth about survival: it looks exactly like ordinary life from the outside. It looks like bread.

I swore never again. Not aloud—I'm not a woman for oaths spoken to air—but in the marrow, where oaths actually live. Never again would I open that door. Never again would I hold something the crown wanted badly enough to reach for it. I would sell seasoning and hair ornaments until I died among them, harmless and useless and safe, and I would not sing, and I would not remember, and I failed at remembering constantly, because grief is not a door you can bury. Believe me. I have tried. I am the foremost expert in this city on doors that will not stay shut.

* * *

Ivo came back at four o'clock with the inquisitor.

I had spent the hours between doing exactly what any innocent shopkeeper would do, which is to say nothing whatsoever suspicious, and also moving Tam's blanket, his cup, and one child-sized pair of boots down to the cellar, behind the Gate, into the shallow space between the sealed door and the shelving where I keep the winter stores. He had watched me work with those dark unblinking eyes of his, and when I finished arranging the concealment—heaps of turnips, a rolled sacking pallet—he had taken my sleeve and pointed at the Gate and spoken three syllables of the First Tongue, soft as an apology.

I didn't answer him. I couldn't. The syllables had gone through the seal like water through cloth, and the Gate had shifted under its burial, once, like a sleeper turning over, and I had needed both hands on the shelf to stay standing.

That was yesterday. Today the bell over my shop door rang, and Ivo came in with rain on his shoulders and a woman behind him in a plain grey coat, sharp-faced, quiet, with the eyes of someone cataloguing everything and apologizing for none of it.

"Inquisitor Vale," Ivo said. "Maren Vale. Ma'am—Elodie Whitlock. Shopkeep."

"Madam Whitlock." Her voice was level, unhurried. "Thank you for the accommodation. We'll be brief."

They searched the way searches go in a city where search is a legal instrument and resistance is a confession: Ivo taking the front rooms, Vale the back, both of them thorough and unhurried, opening my spice drawers, lifting the false-bottomed box where I keep the actual seasoning apart from the charms sold as seasoning. I sat by the window with my mending and let them. This, too, I had rehearsed for twenty years, though God knows I'd hoped never to perform it.

Vale paused at my shelves longer than I liked. Her fingers drifted over the luck knots.

"Hair ornaments," she said.

"Fashionable in Corvale, I'm told."

Something crossed her face at that—too quick to name—and she moved on. Down the stairs. I listened to their boots on the cellar steps the way you listen to a surgeon's instruments laid out on a tray, one at a time, counting.

Tam was behind the Gate. Tam was behind the buried, silent, twenty-years-dead Gate, in the gap between it and the shelving, breathing through his nose the way I'd taught him, and above him hung the seal I had woven from my sister's death, and I sat upstairs and sewed a hem that had needed nothing and waited for the world to end quietly, in a cellar, during business hours.

Boots overhead. A drawer. The soft scrape of the flour bin lid—I keep flour in the cellar, near the stores, in a wide crock—and I heard Vale say something low to Ivo, and Ivo say something lower back, and then a long silence, the specific length of a person standing still and deciding something.

Then the stairs again, and Vale emerging with flour dust on the cuffs of her grey coat, and her face arranged into official blankness.

"The cellar's clear," she said.

"Told you," said Ivo, and his relief was either real or the finest acting I have ever witnessed, and I have witnessed a lot of acting in a magic-outlaw city full of people pretending to be ordinary.

At the door, Vale stopped. She looked back at the shop—at the shelves, the counter, the little brass bell—and then at me, one long measuring moment, the way you look at a wall to determine whether it's load-bearing.

"Your flour," she said. "You mill it yourself?"

"I buy it from Hask's mill, like everyone. Why?"

"No reason. It gets everywhere, doesn't it." She smiled, thin and correct, and followed Ivo out into the drizzle, and the bell rang behind them, and I sat very still until their footsteps faded.

Then I went down to the cellar, because I had heard the silence too, and I knew what she had done down there. She had stood at the flour crock. And beside the crock, on the flagstones, where the sacking hadn't covered it, there had been a small bootprint in white dust. Child-sized. I had missed it. She had not.

I stood at the top of the stairs with my hand on the rail, doing arithmetic. I could sweep the print away, but sweeping proved knowledge. I could leave it, but leaving it was leaving a noose lying coiled on my own floor. And beneath the arithmetic, quieter and colder, ran the other thing—the thing I had felt through my boots while she stood in my cellar: Vale had walked directly past the Gate, within arm's reach of the deepest working I have ever woven, and the seal had *shifted*. Not toward her. Toward the boy. As if it knew what was sleeping in the house, and had begun, after twenty years of honest death, to dream again.

Behind the brick, faint as a pulse under a wrist, the Gate hummed.

And Tam pressed his small palm against the inside of the sealed door, and through two inches of stone and twenty years of silence, I heard—clearly, unmistakably, in a language that predates kingdoms—my sister's voice, screaming.
