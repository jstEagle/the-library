## 7. The Inquisitor's Kindness

Maren Vale had been an inquisitor for eleven years, and in all that time she had never once found what she was looking for.

This was not the boast it might appear. The office of the Inquisition did not find things. It was sent to find them, and it found them, always, because finding nothing was not a career-enhancing outcome. A search that turned up empty meant either the inquisitor was incompetent or the guilty party had been warned, and neither explanation kept a man fed. So they found things. Charms where there were no charms. Heretical marks in the grain of floorboards. A woman in Salt Row hanged two winters back on the testimony of a neighbor who'd wanted her washhouse.

Vale had learned early to keep her face like a locked room.

She walked the market district that morning with her coat buttoned to the throat and her warrant case under her arm and two junior men—Pell, who was earnest, and Doss, who was cruel in the way of boys who have never been told no—and she hated every step of it. The crown had ordered a sweep. Not the usual kind, quiet and grinding, but a hunt: a runaway ward of the palace, last seen near the river gate during the storm, possibly sheltered by persons unknown in the lower market streets. Every building searched by tomorrow's bell. No exceptions granted.

*No exceptions* was how you knew the order had come from above the Lord Chancellor's desk. From above the council, even. From whatever sat behind the king's eyes these days and signed his edicts in a hand slightly too neat.

"Start at the charm shop?" Pell asked, consulting his list. "Whitlock's. She's on every register going back—licensed dealer, no violations."

"No violations," Doss said, and grinned. "Eleven years without so much as a mislabeled jar. Either she's duller than ditchwater or she's the best liar in Vessing."

"Or she sells salt to old women," Vale said, "and old women buy it because they like being cheated politely."

But she put Whitlock's first on her route anyway, and could not afterward have said why. Perhaps because the shop stood at the crooked end of Candle Lane where the street gave up on geometry, and perhaps because she had passed its window a hundred times and never once gone in, and something about the woman behind the counter—the way she watched the street without seeming to watch it—had lodged in Vale like a splinter too shallow to grip.

The bell over the door rang flat when they entered. Everything about the shop was modest and slightly shabby: bundles of dried herbs hung from strings, knots of braided cord displayed on cards labeled *hair ornaments*, little twists of paper filled with coarse grey salt arranged beside a sign reading *seasoning, 2 coppers*. The whole establishment was a monument to the fiction that magic did not exist, conducted with such thoroughness that it circled back around to feeling like a confession.

Elodie Whitlock stood behind the counter grinding something in a mortar, and she did not startle. That was the first thing Vale noted, and disliked, and admired. Most people, seeing three inquisitors fill their doorway, performed some small flinch. Whitlock looked up as if they were customers who had come in at an inconvenient hour.

"Ladies and gentlemen," she said. "We're mostly out of seasoning."

"Inquisitor Maren Vale." Vale set her warrant case on the counter, opened it, let the seal catch the light. "Crown office. We're conducting a survey of the district."

"A survey." Whitlock wiped her hands on her apron. "Of my salt?"

"Of your premises. There's a child missing from the palace. Ward of the Crown, age eight or nine, dark hair, speaks oddly or not at all. He may have taken shelter anywhere along this street during the storm four nights past."

Something moved behind Whitlock's eyes. It was very small and very fast, and if Vale had blinked she would have missed it, and she was quite sure she had not imagined it, and she spent the next several seconds pretending she had.

"You're welcome to look," Whitlock said. "It's one room above and one below. Mind the step into the cellar; the wood's rotten."

That, too, was wrong. Nobody invited the Inquisition into their cellar. They hovered, they apologized, they obstructed with trembling courtesy until the obstruction itself became evidence. Whitlock said *mind the step* the way a woman says it to the milk boy.

"We'll begin up here," Vale said.

* *

The search took two hours and turned up exactly what the registers promised: salt, herbs, cord, dust.

Doss got bored and started opening drawers harder than he needed to, slamming each one shut like punctuation. Pell examined the herb bundles with the sincere confusion of a man who could not tell rosemary from murder. Vale worked slowly and said little. She went through the ledger—twenty years of sales in a crabbed, careful hand, every entry boring, every price fair, the sort of bookkeeping that took real discipline to fake or real virtue to keep—and she watched Whitlock, who sat by the window mending a hem and watching the street again, always the street, the way a lighthouse watches water.

"You've been here long," Vale said.

"Twenty years."

"The Purge would have been starting when you set up. Bad time to open a charm shop."

"It's a seasoning shop."

"Mm." Vale closed the ledger. "My grandmother bought luck knots. Before the laws. She swore by them. I used to think when I grew up I'd sell them myself."

"What stopped you?"

"The hanging," Vale said, and Whitlock's needle paused, just once, and resumed.

They went down to the cellar after that. Whitlock brought the lamp herself, unhurried, warning them again about the step, and Vale descended into a low stone room that smelled of earth and old rain. Shelving along one wall: more salt, more paper twists, a barrel of dried apples. Tools. A rusted key hanging from a nail. And against the far wall—

A door. Or the suggestion of one. Brick, by every appearance, mortared and aged, set into the stone as if the builder had changed his mind mid-sentence. But the brickwork was too regular, and the wall around it was rough-hewn cellar stone, and no sane architect bricked a doorway into a cellar and left it flush and blank. It was the most honest lie Vale had ever seen, and Whitlock watched her look at it with a perfectly empty face.

"Old construction," Whitlock said. "Was a passage once, they say, before the fire. Sealed since before me."

"Where does it go?" Doss asked, rapping a knuckle against the brick. It sounded solid. It sounded, Vale thought, like something working very hard to sound solid.

"Nowhere now."

Vale looked at the door a long moment. There was a faint draft in the cellar that came from nowhere she could find, cool and moving, stirring the lamp flame the smallest degree, and it should not have been there, because cellars with no exits do not breathe. She filed it away with the flicker in Whitlock's eyes and the lighthouse way the woman watched the street, and she said, "We're done down here," and went up the rotten steps.

* *

They were nearly out—Doss already on the threshold, Pell rolling up his list—when Vale saw it.

The flour.

There was a sack of it under the counter; Whitlock apparently baked bread to go with her salt, which struck Vale as the single most human detail in the entire establishment. And near the sack, on the dark boards of the floor, someone had swept. Recently. Swept badly, or swept in haste, or swept with a broom that had already given up—but flour was patient stuff, and it had sifted back out of the cracks, and there in the spilled white dust, half erased but unmistakable, was a footprint.

Small. Very small. A child's boot, worn at the heel, pointed toward the stairs.

Above it, dusted over with a second, lighter pass of flour, was the print of a grown woman's shoe, laid deliberately across the small one like a hand pressed over a mouth.

"—Inquisitor?" Pell said.

Vale became aware that she had stopped walking. That she was standing in the middle of Candle Lane's best lie, looking at a floor, with two of her men waiting and Elodie Whitlock three feet away holding the door open, and everything in the world balanced on what she did in the next breath.

She thought about the woman on Salt Row. She thought about the noose they kept oiled in the yard behind the Inquisition offices, and about the warrant on her own person that read, in that too-neat hand, *no exceptions*, and about what happened to inquisitors who found nothing, and what happened to inquisitors who found something and said nothing, which was worse, which was rope.

She thought: *I am eleven years tired.*

"Catch your breath, Inquisitor?" Whitlock asked. Her voice was mild. Her knuckles on the doorframe were white.

"Just taking stock." Vale stepped over the flour without looking down at it again—looking down was the same as pointing—and walked out into the grey light. "Clean premises," she said loudly, for Pell's list. "Licensed dealer, twenty years' standing, nothing irregular. We'll mark it cleared."

"She hasn't been searched top to bottom," Doss said. "Orders are every building."

"I searched it. I'm the ranking officer. Write it down."

He wrote it down. He wrote it down with a sulky twist to his mouth that told her he'd be repeating the whole episode to someone over drinks, and she found she did not much care. There was a strange lightness in her chest, terrifying and clean, like the first step onto ice.

Whitlock held the door for them. As Vale passed, close enough that only the two of them existed, the shopkeeper spoke, barely above breath:

"Kind of you."

"Don't thank me." Vale did not turn her head. "I'll be back. Tomorrow, maybe the day after. Others won't be me." She paused on the step, and heard herself say the rest before she had decided to: "Sweep better."

* *

That evening she sat in her rooms above the chandler's on Wick Street with a bottle she wasn't drinking and the day spread out in front of her like a confession awaiting signature.

Here was the arithmetic she had done a thousand times and never finished. The Inquisition existed because the Purge laws existed, and the Purge laws existed because magic had killed people—not rumors, not hexes, actual corpses, in the bad years, before the crown burned it all out—and so the noose was regrettable but necessary, and the necessary thing done poorly was still better than the necessary thing undone. That had been the argument. She had built eleven years on it the way you build a house on a floor you never inspect.

And then the floor shifted, the way floors had been shifting all year. The edicts coming down through the council with that too-neat handwriting. The interrogations that stopped asking *where is the sorcery* and started asking *who doubts*. Three inquisitors reassigned last spring and never reassigned back. The palace ward—a child, for the gods' sake, a child everyone in the offices pretended not to know existed except as a line in a budget—vanished the same night the bells rang wrong, and now a hundred armed men kicked through the city on orders nobody would put a name to.

You did not need to believe in sorcery to see that something was wearing the government. You only needed to read.

Vale turned the bottle by its neck, not drinking.

If the child was in that cellar—if the flour lied to her the way she suspected it had—then she had committed her first crime in eleven years of service, and it was the smallest one imaginable, and it felt enormous. Sweep better, she'd said. She might as well have carved it on the door.

But here was the other sum, the one she couldn't stop running. If the crown wanted a child badly enough to sweep a district in a day, then the child mattered, and if the child mattered then the orders came from the thing that mattered most, and Vale had sworn an oath—to the crown, yes, but the words had said *to the realm and its lawful sovereign*, and she was increasingly unsure those were the same entity. An inquisitor's job was to find the sorcery corrupting the state.

She was starting to suspect she'd been looking at it from the inside for years, the way you don't see a stain because you live in the room.

The bells rang the hour. All of them, in sequence, the way they always had—except that lately, on some nights, Vale could have sworn the peal carried a second sound underneath, a harmonic like a held breath, like many voices just below audibility. Tonight they rang clean, and somehow that was worse, as if the thing had gotten what it needed and no longer bothered.

She stood, put on her coat, and looked at herself in the dull mirror by the door: a woman with a locked-room face and a warrant case and a conscience she had fed on thin rations for a decade, telling it *soon, soon.*

Tomorrow she would report the market sweep complete. Tomorrow, or the day after, others would come to Candle Lane—Doss's friends, or worse, the palace's own quiet men—and they would not step over any flour.

Three days, she thought. Give it three days. Find out what the crown really wants with a child. Find out whether the sorcery the laws were written for is standing in a cellar on Candle Lane—or sitting on a throne.

She snuffed the lamp and went down into the dark street, walking toward the market district with no warrant and no witnesses, telling herself she was only going to look, knowing as she said it that looking had never once been enough.
