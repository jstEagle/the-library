## 8. Songs That Unmake Doors

The crack in the seal was the width of a hair, and it sang.

Elodie heard it before she saw it—three days after the inquisitors had come and gone from her street, three days of Ivo's warning sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. She was below the shop floor with a lamp, running her thumb along the Gate's brick face the way she did every night, checking for warmth, for hum, for anything. The mortar between two bricks had split. Not crumbled—*split*, clean as cut bread, and through the gap came a thread of light the color of dawn before the sun commits to rising.

And it was singing. Very quietly. A note she hadn't heard in twenty years and would have known blindfolded, drowning, dead.

She climbed the ladder too fast and caught her shin on the rung, and didn't care.

Tam was asleep on the cot behind the flour sacks where she'd hidden him since the search, curled small, hands tucked under his chin like a child half his age. Mud still lived in the creases of his knuckles no matter how many times she scrubbed them. He'd been in Vessing nine days now. He'd spoken Common exactly four times, each word dragged out of him like a tooth.

She crouched by the cot and put her hand flat on his back, over his heart, the way you check whether bread has risen or a door is locked.

"Tam."

His eyes opened all at once, no drowsy middle ground. That was the palace in him, she thought. Children who grow up safe learn to wake slowly. This one woke like something hunted.

"You're not in trouble," she said, which was a lie, because they both were, and because he could hear lies the way he heard everything else—with that tilted head, that terrible patient attention. She amended: "You're in trouble. But not from me."

He sat up. He waited. He was very good at waiting; it was the one skill the palace had taught him that she approved of.

"The cellar," she said. "The wall. Did you touch it?"

A pause. Then, barely: "No."

"But you went down there."

Another pause, longer. "It called."

Of course it had. Of course the Gate had felt him sleeping above it every night, this boy who was less a boy than a key wearing skin, and had leaned toward him the way a plant leans at a window. She should have moved him to the attic weeks ago. She should have done a hundred things differently since the night he'd appeared in the storm, speaking words that made her wards scream like gulls.

"Show me," she said. "What you did. Don't—don't do it again, just show me."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she watched him decide something. Then he opened his mouth and sang.

It was nothing. Three notes. Less than nothing—a fragment, a scrap, the way a child picks up half a lullaby from the next room over. His voice wasn't even good. It cracked on the second note, thin and uncertain, a boy's voice doing what boys' voices do.

The lamp guttered. The floorboards under Elodie's knees shivered like the skin of a drum. And from below, muffled through wood and stone and twenty years of her own careful work, the Hollow Gate answered—one deep, glad, *hungry* tone, the sound of a held breath finally let go—and when she threw open the cellar hatch and got down the ladder fast enough to see, the hairline crack in the mortar had become a seam, and the seam had become a fissure, and light poured out of it like water finding a level.

She stood there with her hand pressed against the warm brick and felt the seal dying under her palm. Twenty years of knots and salt and Wren's last binding, unraveling from three cracked notes sung by a child who didn't know his own strength any more than a match knows fire.

"It wants to open," Tam said from the top of the ladder. He'd followed her down. His voice was steadier now, in the Gate's presence, as if proximity were a language he was fluent in. "It's been wanting. Since I came."

"I know."

"It's not angry."

"No," Elodie said. Her throat was tight. "It isn't. That's rather the problem."

* * *

She made tea she didn't drink and laid it all out on the kitchen table where Tam could see, because lying to him had stopped working around day five.

Here is what she knew, arranged like goods on a shelf:

The crown on King Aldric's head was not a crown. It was the oldest vessel ever made—the first attempt, centuries back, at what the palace had later perfected with children like Tam: a thing grown from Gate-magic to hold power, to carry it, to *be* carried. Somewhere along the years it had stopped being worn and started wearing. It spoke the First Tongue because it predated kingdoms; it needed the First Tongue because the rite it was completing—the Coronation Eternal, whatever that meant, whatever Wren's stolen notes hinted at—required a living key to finish binding the city's magic into itself forever.

And Tam was a living key. Grown in the palace nursery from the Gate's own spilled power, made for exactly this purpose, fled before the rite could use him.

"They don't want him back because he ran away," Elodie said aloud, mostly to herself. Tam watched her mouth move with that unblinking focus. "They want him back because the song isn't finished. You're the last verse, sweetheart. They can't complete it without you."

Something crossed his face—not fear. Recognition. The look of someone hearing their own name read off a list they'd always suspected existed.

"And your seal—" He touched the table, meaning the cellar, meaning the Gate. "It won't hold?"

Elodie laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and short. "My seal held against twenty years of nothing happening. Against me checking it nightly and wishing it would stay shut." She turned her cup in its saucer. "Against three notes from you? No. Whatever the crown does next—whether it sends hunters or bells or that king of theirs walking here in his sleep—it won't be my seal that decides how this ends. My seal is already deciding to lose."

Tam reached across the table and, with great care, turned her cup back the other way. A child's correction. A fidget. Or maybe—she looked at him properly—maybe the first piece of advice anyone had given her in twenty years: stop spinning. Stop circling. Pick a direction.

"All right," she said. "All right."

* * *

Wren's notebooks lived inside the false bottom of the salt chest, wrapped in oilcloth, exactly where they had lived for twenty years while Elodie pretended they didn't exist. There were eleven of them. Elodie had read the first page of the first one the night she'd sealed the Gate, understood immediately what her sister had been building toward, and never opened another. Reading them meant learning. Learning meant intending. Intending meant going back through the Gate someday, and going back through the Gate meant—

Well. It meant Wren. And Wren was ash in an urn on the mantel, or so Elodie had told herself for two decades with the devotion of the truly frightened.

She unwrapped them at the kitchen table with Tam watching, and her hands shook, and she let them.

Wren's handwriting slanted forward like a woman leaning into wind. The early notebooks were charm-craft, market-grade, the kind of clever small magic that kept a shop like theirs alive in the years before the Purge: luck knots, fever-browns, salt that remembered being sea. But by the fourth notebook the entries changed. The margins filled with characters Elodie didn't know—angular, older than the alphabet, letters that seemed to lean on each other for support. The First Tongue. Wren had been teaching herself. Had gotten far enough to write whole passages in it, far enough to annotate them in Common in a small fierce hand:

*The language isn't spells. It's permissions. You don't command a door—you ask it what it already wants, and remind it that it may.*

And lower, on the same page, pressed hard enough to tear the paper:

*The crown sings to the choir beneath the palace. If it finishes, no one in Vessing will sing anything again without permission. I'm going to the undercroft. If I don't come back, Lodie, don't you dare come after me. Seal it. Live. Be boring. I mean it.*

Elodie sat with that page for a long time. *Be boring.* Wren's last gift: permission to be small. And Elodie had taken it, had built a whole gray life out of it, had sold seasoning that was really salt rings and hair ornaments that were really luck knots, and told herself every night for twenty years that vigilance was love and hiding was duty.

Tam's hand appeared in her field of vision. He tapped the angular characters, then tapped his own throat.

"You know these," she said.

He nodded.

"Could you teach me?"

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then he took the pencil from her hand, pulled the notebook closer, and wrote in Common—slowly, letter by letter, a boy assembling a sentence from parts he rarely used:

*I only know the songs. Wren wrote the words.*

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Elodie hadn't told him. She was certain she hadn't told him. But of course—the choir, the voices under the palace, the bound dead who sang in jars of amber light. If half of what she feared was true, Wren's voice was down there right now, and Tam had spent his nursery years listening to it.

"He knew you," Elodie said. Her voice came out strange. "Didn't he. Knew her."

Tam looked at the notebooks, at the slanting hand, and something moved through his face that was too old for it—recognition, and grief, and a kind of homesickness for a person he'd known only as a song.

"Yes," he said. One word. It cost him visibly.

Elodie closed the notebook. Pressed her palms flat against its cover, against the oilcloth, against twenty years of deliberate silence.

"Then we'd better learn what she left us," she said. "Both of us. Before the crown comes to collect its verse."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low.

Tam sang; Elodie transcribed, matching sound to symbol, building a bridge between the language in her ears and the language on the page. It was slow, painstaking, and the strangest kind of familiar—like hearing a mother tongue you'd never been taught, words arriving with meanings already attached, the way you know *home* before you can define it. Some of it she grasped at once. Some of it fought her. One passage, near the end of the seventh notebook, she copied four times before the characters stopped sliding apart under her pencil:

*A door that is asked may refuse. A door that is commanded must obey—but the commander owes it everything it spends. That is the price the crown has never paid. That is the flaw in the rite.*

Elodie read it three times. *Owes everything it spends.* The crown-thing had spent twenty years of a king, and generations of vessels, and a choir of stolen voices—and had paid for none of it, because vessels couldn't refuse and the dead couldn't invoice. But a free singer, asking instead of commanding—

She didn't have the shape of the plan yet. She had the first edge of one, sharp enough to cut.

Below them, faint through the floorboards, the Gate hummed its single glad note, patient as tide. Above them, somewhere across the sleeping city, a bell tolled once—out of hour, out of pattern—and Elodie's pencil stopped moving.

Bells didn't toll alone in Vessing. Bells especially didn't toll alone when the palace wanted something found.

Tam lifted his head, listening, and his face went pale in the lamplight. He wrote, fast, pressing hard:

*It's calling me. It knows where I am.*

The bell tolled again. Closer, or louder, or both. And Elodie Whitlock—who had spent twenty years being careful, being small, being boring, being nobody—blew out the lamp, took her sister's notebooks off the table, and said, "Not tonight it doesn't," in a voice she hadn't used since the Purge.
