## 9. The King Who Knocks

The first thing I learned to do inside my own skull was to be quiet.

Not silent—silence was surrender, and the crown fed on surrender—but quiet the way a man is quiet in a house where something walks the halls listening for breath. I had been king of Vessing for eleven years, and for nine of them I had believed the headaches were gout of the mind, or guilt, or the ordinary rot that sets into any man who signs death warrants before breakfast. It took me two years to understand that the voice in council was not always mine. It took me another year to learn which thoughts were mine and which were *planted*, set down like seedlings in tilled soil, watered while I slept.

Now I knew. Now I lay awake in the dark of my bedchamber with frost blooming on the inside of my skin—a thing no physician could see, because they looked at flesh, and this was not flesh—and I practiced being quiet.

*You are restless,* said the crown.

It did not speak aloud. It never spoke aloud. It spoke in the space behind my eyes where a man keeps his private name for himself, the name no one else is permitted to use. That was the obscenity of it. Not the power—I could have forgiven power; kings traffic in power the way fishmongers traffic in fish—it was the intimacy. It lived where my wife had once lived, where my mother's lullabies still echoed if I let them. It had moved into the rooms of me and rearranged the furniture.

*I am sleeping,* I answered, in the wordless way one answers such things. A shape of an answer. Nothing it could grip.

*You are counting the guards outside your door. There are four. There will be two at the change of watch, and the corridor lamp gutters at the third hour because the wick is cheap and Master Corvel will not spend crown silver on oil.*

I kept my breathing even. But my heart betrayed me, as hearts do, stumbling like a servant caught eavesdropping.

*Good,* the crown said, and there was something almost tender in it, which was worst of all. *Rest. Tomorrow we speak with the Guildmasters about the child.*

The child. The word went through me like a hook through a fish's jaw. For three days the palace had smelled of iron and incense, and Inquisitor Vale's riders had gone out into the rain asking after a runaway ward, and the crown had hummed at night—not through my mouth, not yet, but through the walls themselves, through the stones, a frequency I felt in my teeth like a tuning fork pressed to bone. It wanted the child. It needed the child the way a lock needs its key, and I understood this without knowing how I understood it, the way you understand falling.

I lay in the dark and counted the guards. Four. Two at the change. The lamp would gutter at the third hour.

And somewhere across the city, in a market district I had ridden past a hundred times without seeing, there was a shop that sold nothing—or so said the vision that came to me sometimes when the crown dreamed and I was permitted to dream alongside it, a stowaway in its sleep. A woman with grey in her braid and a keeper's hands. A boy with mud to his knees. And behind them both, a door that was not a door, humming like a struck bell under twenty years of silence.

The crown thought these visions were its own. That was its one vanity: it believed nothing in my head could be hidden from it, and therefore never checked whether anything was.

A king learns early that his greatest weapon is being underestimated. I had forgotten that, these nine years. I remembered it now.

* * *

The third hour came gray and drizzling, and the lamp guttered exactly as promised, and I had spent the intervening hours doing the hardest work of my reign: I had done nothing.

That sounds like a jest. It was not. For nine years every plan I formed, the crown had finished for me—had taken my half-thoughts and built palaces around them, useful palaces, obedient palaces, until I learned to stop building. To want nothing loudly. To keep my true intention down in the cellar of myself, beneath the floors, in the dark, unlit, unfed, a coal banked in ash. Tonight I uncovered the coal.

I dressed as a servant dresses: rough wool, no chain of office, boots with worn heels taken from the mending basket where my late wife's maid had left them six years ago. No one mends a dead queen's things. No one looks at them either. Grief makes excellent camouflage.

Two guards at the door. Bren and a young one whose name I had made a point of learning—Ostler, third son of a barley farmer, promoted for steadiness rather than cleverness. Steadiness was what I needed. Cleverness would ask questions.

"Your Majesty." Bren straightened. "We weren't told you'd be walking."

"I wasn't told either," I said, and smiled the smile I had used for eleven years, the one that said *the king is whimsical, the king is harmless, the king does as kings do.* "Sleep escapes me. I'll walk the gallery. You needn't announce it."

Bren hesitated. Behind his eyes I watched duty wrestle habit, and habit won, because it always wins, because that is what habit is *for*. He opened the door.

The gallery ran the length of the east wing, all portraits and cold marble, and at its end was the chapel of the old kings—the one the Purge had emptied, the one where no god was worshipped anymore because gods were sorcery too, or near enough that no one risked the distinction. Its back wall shared stone with the servants' stair. And the servants' stair, unlike the royal apartments, had not been rebuilt after the Purge, because no one important ever used it, and the crown-thing, for all its centuries of hunger, had never once considered the possibility that a king might want to leave his own palace by the door the scullery maids used.

It had considered everything else. It had wards on my blood, my signature, my shadow. It listened through my ears when it wished. But it had grown up in thrones and rites and crowns, in the high ceremonial architecture of power, and it had never learned the architecture of *smallness*. It did not know how a man slips out of a room. It had never been a man.

I had been a man for forty-one years before it ever touched me. I intended to remember how.

The stair smelled of tallow and onions. My heart hammered so hard I feared it would wake the whole wing, and I stood in the dark of the landing with my hand pressed flat against the cold stone and I did the thing I had practiced in the dark of my bedchamber for a year: I thought about nothing. I thought about barley. I thought about rain on barley. I became, in the cellar of myself, a farmer's son standing in wet fields, wanting nothing, planning nothing, and above me—far above me, in the bright furnished rooms of my own head—the crown turned over in its sleep and murmured about guildmasters and children, and did not look down.

I went out through the kitchen garden gate, which the night porter opened for a coin and a story about a widow in Tanner's Row, and then the city had me, and the city was rain and mud and the smell of the river, and I wept, briefly, disgustingly, standing under an awning like a madman, because I had not been alone in nine years and I had forgotten what it weighed—nothing, it weighed nothing, it weighed *everything*—and then I wiped my face and walked.

* * *

The market district by night was a drowned thing. Curfew had emptied the streets an hour before; the watch-bells had rung their dull iron note and every lawful soul was behind shutters. I moved through it keeping to the eaves, an old man in bad boots, and no one stopped me because the watch was looking for a child, not a king, because no one—no one in nine years—had imagined the king might simply *walk away*.

The shop found me before I found it. That is the only way I can describe it. I had the vision: a crooked lane, a sign painted with a knot and a sprig of something, a window displaying hair ornaments and jars labeled SEASONING. And there it was, exactly as dreamed, except the vision had not conveyed the feeling of it—the pressure against my skin, faint but unmistakable, like standing near a struck bell. Like standing near the place where the world's skin was thin.

Behind those walls, something old was sealed up tight. And the crown wanted whatever was behind it badly enough to send hunters into the rain.

Which meant I had to reach it first.

I knocked. Softly—then harder, because softness woke nothing. Rain ran down my collar. Somewhere a dog complained and was silenced. And then, from within: a candle flaring, shadows moving, and a bolt drawn back with the deliberate slowness of a woman who has survived twenty years in a city that outlawed her whole self.

The door opened a hand's width. Chain on. One eye, sharp and grey, taking my measure in the lamplight behind her.

"We're closed," she said. "And whatever you're selling, I'm not buying."

"I'm not selling." My voice came out wrecked. Nine years of council-chamber bronze, and it broke on four words in the rain. "Keeper Whitlock. Elodie. You have to listen—you have to—" I put my hand against the door, against the gap, and felt the hum of the hidden thing answer my touch like a dog answering its name, and her eye widened, and the chain did not come off.

"You're him," she whispered. "From the visions. You're the king."

"I'm the man the king used to be," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is wearing a crown that isn't a crown, and it has sent hunters for your boy, and tomorrow they search this street, and I know because I am the mouth it speaks through at council and I cannot—"

The frost rose.

I felt it come, the way you feel a seizure coming, a wrongness gathering behind the eyes, the crown waking in my skull like a great eye opening. *Where are you,* it said, suddenly, terribly awake. *What is this. What are you touching—*

"No," I said aloud, and gripped the doorframe with both hands like a drowning man grips a spar. "Listen to me. Listen. Cut it off. There's a smith's rasp in your cellar or I'm a beggar's ghost—cut the crown from my head, take it, burn it, I don't care what it costs me, because if you don't, if it gets your boy, it finishes its rite and every bound voice under the palace belongs to it forever, and I will be the hand that does it, do you understand? I will be the hand. I can't stop it from inside. I've tried. God forgive me, I've tried for nine years and I can't—"

My right arm rose.

I had not told it to. That is the detail I need you to hold, whoever reads this, whoever remembers: my arm rose without my permission, smooth and slow, and my hand turned palm-up in the rain, and my mouth kept talking over my own screaming because the crown had learned long ago that a king's voice is more useful than a king's silence.

"—and the boy is the key," my mouth said, in a voice that was mine and not mine, warmer than mine, pleasanter than mine, "and the door behind your wall is the lock, and the rite wants only what it has always wanted, keeper, which is *everything*—"

She slammed the door. I heard the chain fall, the bolt go home, her weight against the wood. Good girl. Good. And through the door, muffled, shaking, furious: "Whoever you are—fight it. *Fight.*"

I fought. I want that recorded too. I put everything I had—all the banked coal, all the barley and the rain, my wife's name, my mother's lullaby, the memory of my coronation oath spoken in my own true voice before the crown ever touched my brow—I threw it all against the thing wearing me, and for a moment, one shining moment, I was back in my own body, sovereign of at least this much ground, and I pressed my forehead to the wet door and said:

"The ring. Under the step. Proof, and warning. Vale—the inquisitor—she suspects. She can be turned. Trust her farther than you trust me, because I don't know how much of me is left to trust."

Then the crown took the rest.

I don't remember the walk back. That is its mercy or its cruelty, I've never decided. I remember waking at my own desk at dawn, in my own robes, my hands ink-stained with a warrant I had no memory of writing—an order expanding the search, authorizing forced entry, naming the market district street by street. My signature, perfect. My seal, pressed clean.

The ring was gone from my finger. Good. Let it be gone. Let it lie under her step like a coal banked in ash, waiting for someone with the courage to uncover it.

At midmorning the crown called council, and I sat at its center with frost on the inside of my skin, and it spoke through my mouth about the child, and the lords nodded, and Inquisitor Vale watched me across the table with an expression I could not read—until, as the session ended, she passed close behind my chair, and said, very quietly, in a voice pitched for no one else:

"Your Majesty was seen last night. Near the river. Alone."

And walked out before I could answer, leaving me to understand that the game had changed—that somewhere between my desperate knocking and this morning's warrant, a third player had entered it, and she was nobody's instrument, and she was watching.

Under the step, Elodie. Under the step. Read the ring and read the warning, and when the hunters come to your door tomorrow—

Pray you decide faster than I did.
