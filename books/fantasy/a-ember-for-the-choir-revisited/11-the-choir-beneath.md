## 11. The Choir Beneath

The crack in the seal sang to him at night.

Not with sound, exactly. Tam had no word for it in Common, and even the First Tongue only had a word that meant *listening-with-your-teeth*, which was close but wrong. It was more like standing near a wall and knowing someone was on the other side, breathing. The Gate behind the cellar bricks had been breathing for twenty years, muffled under Elodie's binding, and now that her work was split down one seam, he could feel every exhale.

He'd started sleeping in the cellar without asking permission. Elodie hadn't stopped him. She watched him do it with a look he was learning to read—the look of a woman watching water rise around her ankles and deciding, night after night, not to move.

Tonight the breathing was louder.

Tam lay on his back on the cold flagstones with his hands folded on his chest, the way they'd taught him in the nursery under the palace, the way you lay when you wanted your inside-ears to open. Above him the shop creaked its small nighttime noises: shutter settling, salt jars ticking as their contents dried, Elodie's footsteps crossing the floor overhead, pausing at the back door, checking the brick that wasn't a door, going to bed. He counted her steps because counting was an anchor. The crown-thing pulled at him across the whole city like a fisherman testing a line, and if he didn't count—if he didn't hold something small and numbered—he woke up halfway to the palace with his feet moving and no memory of leaving.

*One hundred and six,* he thought. *One hundred and seven.*

Then he let go of the numbers and opened.

The crack took him the way water takes a dropped stone.

He fell through listening. Below the shop, below the cellar, below the roots of Vessing itself, there was a place made entirely of being-heard, and it was full. Full the way a jar packed with fireflies is full—hundreds of lights, hundreds of voices, none of them using mouths. They had been singing down there for longer than Tam had been alive. Longer than the Purge. Longer than the laws. Generations of vessels, grown and spent and stored, each one a child once, each one a key once, each one set aside when a newer key was cut.

They were still singing.

That was the part nobody told you. That was the part the crown never said out loud, because it was the worst thing: they weren't dead. Dead would have been a mercy with edges. They were *kept*. Bound mid-breath, mid-word, mid-scream, their voices pulled out and stoppered in amber light so the crown-thing could draw on them whenever it needed a throat that wasn't its own. Tam knew what that was like from the inside. He had been made from the same magic. He was, in a way, one of them already—a voice walking around loose, unjared, and the crown wanted him filed away with the rest.

*Little brother,* said the choir, all together, the way they always began. *Little brother, you came back.*

*I didn't come back,* Tam answered, in the tongue that predates kingdoms. *I ran.*

*We know.* A ripple through the lights, something like laughter, something like grief. *Running is also a kind of returning. You carry the door in your chest. Wherever you go, you face home.*

He didn't want to be a door. He wanted to be a boy who liked eel pie and hated curfew and was afraid of Ivo Coverly's big boots on the stairs. But the choir didn't have room for wanting; wanting was a surface thing, and they lived far below surfaces. So he did what he always did when the singing got too big: he looked for the quietest light and listened to just that one.

There was one that sat apart from the others. Not brighter—dimmer, actually, worn thin like a coin passed through too many hands—but it burned a different color. The others were amber. This one was the orange of a banked ember, the color of the lamp Elodie kept lit in the shop window after closing, though Tam didn't know why she bothered, since no one was supposed to see it.

He drifted toward it. In this place drifting was done by attention alone.

*Who are you?* he asked.

The ember-light flickered. And then, in Common—not the First Tongue, but cracked, rusty, half-remembered Common, the Common of someone who had not spoken to another living person in twenty years—it said:

*You're not one of theirs.*

Tam went very still, the way you go still when a wild animal notices you.

*No,* he said. *I ran from them.*

*A runner.* The voice turned him over, examining, the way Elodie examined a knot before she trusted it. *They don't usually run. They're usually used up first. What's your name?*

*Tam. She named me Tam. The shopkeeper.*

Silence. Long silence. The whole choir seemed to lean in, hundreds of bound voices holding one breath they didn't have lungs to hold.

*Whitlock,* the voice said at last. Not a question. A recognition. *The charm shop. Salt rings sold as seasoning. My sister's shop.*

The floor of the world tilted.

*Your sister,* Tam repeated. He knew the name. He'd heard it exactly once, whispered over the sink when Elodie thought he was asleep: *Wren*, said like a wound being pressed. *Wren.*

*Yes.* The ember-light pulsed, steadying itself, the way a person steadies themselves before saying a thing they've practiced for twenty years. *Yes. Tell her—*

But the choir surged between them then, all the amber voices at once, and Tam felt the crown-thing's line go taut across the city, a hook behind his sternum, reeling. He grabbed the numbers—one, two, three, the stairs, the salt jars, Elodie's one hundred and six steps—and tore himself free of the listening and came back to his body gasping on the flagstones, hands pressed flat to the cold stone, heart slamming.

Above him, the shop door banged open.

Elodie came down the cellar stairs two at a time with a candle in one hand and a salt ring in the other, hair loose, eyes wild. She must have felt it too—the crack singing, the pressure change, the whole house leaning toward the underworld. She skidded to a stop when she saw him sitting up, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the candle flame leaned toward the cracked seal like a plant toward sun.

"It talked to you," she said. Not a question either. "The Gate. Just now. I felt it in my teeth."

"There's a voice," Tam said. His Common was better these days, though it still came out of him like water carried carefully in cupped hands. "Down there. Apart from the others. It's—" He stopped. How did you hand someone this? There was no gentle angle. "It says it's your sister."

The candle flame stood perfectly straight, as if the air itself had stopped moving.

"No," Elodie said.

"It knew the shop. It knew the—"

"*No.*" She said it louder, and the salt ring in her hand hissed, grains shivering against her palm. Her face had gone the color of the flour she dusted over things she wanted hidden. "Wren is dead. Wren died twenty years ago reopening this gate, and I sealed it over her, and she is *dead*, and whatever is down there wearing her voice is—" Her breath caught. "The crown does this. It finds the shape of what you want most and it pours itself into the shape. I've seen it do it. I've read about it in her own notes. It baited keepers for centuries with the voices of their dead."

"I know," Tam said quietly. "It might be lying."

"Then why are you telling me?" she demanded, and her voice cracked down the middle, and there it was, the thing underneath the thing: not anger. Hope. Twenty years of hope buried alive under duty, clawing at the coffin lid because a mute child had come down from the shop and said *your sister is singing in the dark*.

"Because," Tam said, "when it said your name, all the other voices went quiet. Like they were afraid of it. Or like they loved it." He looked at the cracked seal, at the seam of light along the brick. "Lies don't make choirs go quiet, Elodie. Only true things do."

She stood there a long moment. The candle shook in her grip. And then she did the last thing he expected: she knelt. Down onto the cold flagstones beside him, skirts pooling, and put her ear—or her hands, or whatever part of her still remembered keeper-craft—against the cracked seam, and closed her eyes.

Tam watched her listen.

He watched hope hit her like a wave hits a seawall—not all at once, but finding every crack, pressing into every fault line, working. He watched her jaw tighten and tremble. He watched twenty years of careful, calcified grief begin, very slowly, to fracture along the same lines as the seal.

And then, through the crack, faint and rusted and unmistakably in Common, the voice said:

*"Elodie. You always did check the lock twice."*

The sound Elodie made was not a word. It was the noise a person makes when something long-frozen breaks—and breaks *warm*, which is worse than breaking cold, because cold things you can sweep up. She folded forward over her own knees, and the salt ring rolled from her hand and broke its perfect circle across the stones, and Tam, who had been made in a nursery by people who wanted him to be nothing but a key, did the only thing he knew how to do.

He put his arms around her and held on, and below them both, patient as embers, the choir began to sing.

* * *

Later—much later, when the candles had burned to thumbs and Elodie's tears had dried into salt tracks she didn't bother hiding—they sat on the cellar floor with the notebook open between them. Wren's notebook, pulled from its hiding place under the floorboard, its pages soft as cloth from twenty years of being touched and not read.

"She wrote about the jars," Elodie said. Her voice was scraped raw but steady, the voice of a woman who has decided to walk toward the thing instead of away. "Amber light. Bound voices. I thought—I told myself it was theory. Keeper's lore. Old stories." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "She was writing down what she saw down there. What she found when she opened the gate. What they did to her for seeing it."

"They kept her," Tam said. "Mid-scream, she said. Bound in the middle of the worst moment." He hesitated. "She said to tell you something. Before the choir pulled me out. She didn't finish."

Elodie's head came up fast. "What? What did she say?"

"'Tell her—'" Tam shook his frustration off. "The crown hooked me. I lost the rest."

For a moment Elodie looked like she might put her fist through the brick. Then she exhaled, slow, controlled, a woman banking a fire rather than letting it spread. "Then we go down and hear the rest ourselves."

"You said the gate was death," Tam said. "You said opening it means freeing every voice into dying. Including hers."

"I know what I said." She touched the cracked seam, gently, the way you touch a sleeping child. "Twenty years ago I chose the living over the dead, and it nearly killed me, and it made me small, and it made this city small, and it didn't save anyone. Not even me." She looked at him then, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely clear. "I'm not choosing again. I'm done choosing who deserves to be free."

Above them, somewhere across the sleeping city, a bell tolled once—a single note, out of hour, out of order. Tam felt it in his sternum where the hook sat. Felt the line pull, gentle, testing.

The crown knew where he was. It had probably always known.

It was letting him stay. For now. Because a key that comes home on its own is worth ten that have to be dragged.

Elodie heard the bell too. She went still, listening, and Tam watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he did: three days, Vale had promised. Three days, and the crown had just announced it was done waiting.

"We start tonight," Elodie said, and reached for the notebook.
