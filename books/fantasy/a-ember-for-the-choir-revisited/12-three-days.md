## 12. Three Days

The first morning, Ivo came without his uniform.

He stood in the doorway of the shop at half past six, before the shutters were even down, wearing a plain wool coat the color of wet slate and carrying a leather tube under one arm like a man delivering surveyor's drawings. Which, in a sense, he was. He looked older out of the sergeant's stripes. Or maybe just more honest.

"You shouldn't be here," I said.

"I know." He stepped inside anyway and turned the bolt behind him, a habit from twenty years of doors that needed locking. "I've been reassigned. Traffic duty on the Weir Bridge. A sergeant with my record doesn't get traffic duty unless someone wants him far away from something." He set the leather tube on my counter, next to the luck knots and the little jars of salt labeled *seasoning*. "So I thought I'd bring you what they don't want me to have."

I didn't touch it. "Ivo."

"Maps," he said. "Palace undercrofts. There's a set kept in the garrison archive—old ones, from before the Purge, when the palace still admitted it had a basement. I signed them out three weeks ago for an inventory audit that never happened. Nobody's missed them yet." His jaw worked. "Elodie. I've walked past your shop for twenty years buying nothing and knowing it was something. I've written reports saying your street was quiet when I knew it wasn't. I'm done choosing the uniform."

I opened the tube with hands that weren't quite steady. The maps inside were beautiful—vellum, hand-inked, the palace rendered not as the flat ceremonial block every citizen knew but as a living thing with roots: staircases that went down and kept going down, wells that connected to cisterns, a forgotten well-house marked in faded brown ink near the old kitchens. And beneath the throne room, drawn in careful cross-section, a vault. No label. Just a small circle, and around the circle, in a dead clerk's cramped hand, the word *choir*.

I looked up at him. "You understand what this costs you."

"I understand what it cost me already." He almost smiled. "The question is whether it buys anything."

* * *

Vale came at noon, off-duty, hood up, looking like any tired woman buying flour. She brought paper, ink, a seal matrix she'd lifted from a dead magistrate's effects years ago—"he owed me," was all she said—and she sat at my counter for four hours making documents.

Transit papers. Four sets. Curfew exemptions stamped with the palace's own authority, authorizing "escort of a ward of the crown to the royal physician" during the hours of the Coronation vigil, when every other soul in Vessing would be locked indoors or sleepwalking toward the throne.

"They'll be checked," she said, blotting the third set. "Not carefully—the inquisition is stretched thin and everyone senior will be at the rite—but checked. So the names have to be real people who could plausibly exist. Not real people who do." She glanced up. "Yours is the hardest. Everyone knows the charm-witch of Hollow Lane."

"Then I won't be me."

"That's the spirit." She dipped her pen again. "Dye your hair. Walk stooped. You're Maren Vale's widowed aunt, come to pray at the vigil for her soul, poor woman, so devout she doesn't notice her niece has fallen in with bad company." A dry pause. "There's poetry in it. I intend to enjoy it."

Tam watched her work from the cellar stairs, chin on his knees, and after a while he came and stood beside her and pointed at the seal matrix and made a small questioning sound. Vale let him press it into wax once, just once, and something in his face—I can't describe it except to say it was the face of a child allowed to hold a sword—made her look away quickly and press harder on the next stamp than she needed to.

"He understands what these are," she said quietly, to me, while Tam carried the wax scrap to show Ivo like treasure.

"He understands more than we do most days."

"Elodie." She set down the pen. "Three days. When this is done, whatever happens—win or lose—you should know I've drafted my own confession. It's in my desk at home, sealed, addressed to whoever comes after. Everything. The files, the vessels, what I suspected about the crown. If we fail, at least the truth outlives us."

"That's very brave."

"It's bookkeeping," said Vale, who had been an inquisitor long enough to know that courage and paperwork were often the same thing.

* * *

The nights belonged to Wren.

I took her notebooks up from under the floorboards where they'd slept for twenty years beside the Gate, and I read them by candlelight while Tam slept—or tried to sleep—a few feet away. Her handwriting changed over the pages. Early entries were neat, schoolroom-tidy, full of diagrams and marginal jokes (*E., stop reading over my shoulder, this binding took me all week*). Later entries slanted, hurried, the ink blotted where her pen had pressed too hard. The last notebook had no dates at all. Just working notes, as if she'd known there wouldn't be time for anything else.

She had been better at this than I ever was. That was the wound under the wound: not that Wren died, but that Wren would have known exactly what to do, and I had spent two decades selling salt and pretending otherwise.

But her notes were patient with me. She wrote like a teacher even in haste. *A binding is not a wall*, she'd written. *Walls push. Bindings hold. You don't fight the thing you bind; you agree with it, briefly, about where it ends.* And below that, a practice exercise, step by step, designed—God help me—for a beginner. For me. She'd written lessons for the sister she assumed would someday need them.

I practiced on the first night with a candle flame, holding it still, agreeing with it about where it ended. On the second night I held the hum of the Gate itself in my cupped hands like water and did not flinch when it climbed my arms. By the second midnight I could sing three words of the First Tongue without my voice breaking—not well, not cleanly, but true enough that the words *meant*, which is the whole of any language.

Tam corrected me twice. He couldn't speak Common, but he could hear when I got a vowel wrong, and he'd tug my sleeve and shape the sound with his mouth until I matched him. We made a strange choir, the two of us: a mute child teaching a middle-aged shopkeeper the language kings were bound with, both of us stumbling, both of us stubborn.

On the second night he fell asleep mid-lesson, sitting up, and I carried him to his pallet and felt how light he'd gotten. Too light. Three days ago he'd been thin; now he was the weight of a bundle of kindling.

It was the crown. I understood that much from Wren's notes. Whatever rite it was building across the city, it drew through its vessels like a root drawing water, and Tam was the last vessel it had, and the distance between Hollow Lane and the palace meant nothing to it. Each night the bells rang their wrong sweet note at curfew, and each night Tam's eyes went glassy and his lips moved with words that weren't his, and each morning he woke a little grayer, a little quieter, as if the crown were drinking him by the spoonful and saving the bowl for the rite.

I put wards around his pallet. Old ones, Wren's patterns, sung rather than chalked. They helped. They did not help enough.

On the third night, he woke screaming without sound, and when I lit the candle his eyes were open and fixed on the ceiling and tears ran sideways into his hair, and his mouth was moving, moving, shaping the same phrase over and over in the First Tongue. I bent close. I only knew maybe forty words of the language then. But I knew those.

*Come home. Come home. Come home.*

I held him until dawn. Neither of us slept.

* * *

On the third day, Ivo spread the maps across my counter and weighed down the corners with jars of seasoning-salt, and we planned.

"The well-house," he said, tapping the faded ink. "Disused since before the Purge. The grate's rusted but the shaft connects to the cistern gallery, and the gallery runs under the east wing to—" his finger traced the line, down and down, "—here. The vault. If the cross-sections are right, it sits directly beneath the throne room. One staircase up. Probably guarded."

"Probably," Vale said. "Certainly. But the guard rotation during the vigil will be ceremonial—honor guards, not soldiers. Men chosen to look good asleep on their feet, because everyone else will be sleepwalking." She tapped the transit papers. "We move at the start of the vigil bells. The city empties toward the palace—that's our cover. Four pilgrims among ten thousand."

"And if the rite finishes before we reach the vault?" Ivo asked.

Nobody answered that. Some questions you leave on the table like knives.

Ivo had brought more than maps. He'd brought his service knife, the good one, and a coil of rope, and a dark lantern, and a face that said he'd already written his own letter to whoever came after, though he'd never say so. He'd also brought bread and hard cheese and dried apples, because Ivo Coverly had been a soldier twenty years and understood that plans run on food.

Vale had brought the papers and a vial of lampblack for my hair and a second coat for Tam, cut down, hooded.

And I had brought—was bringing, was learning, hour by hour—everything Wren had left me. I chalked no circles. I sang instead. Binding-craft lived in the voice, Wren's notes insisted; the First Tongue was not a language you spoke but a language you *agreed* with, and agreement required a throat. I sang the candle still. I sang the door shut. I sang the ache out of Tam's brow for an hour at a stretch, though the relief never lasted past the next curfew bell.

On the afternoon of the third day, I sang the Gate's seal itself, testing it, and the hum answered me like a struck bell, and Tam—who had been watching from the stairs—came down and put his small hand flat against the brick and looked at me with an expression I will remember until I die.

It was not fear. It was apology.

"No," I told him. "Whatever you're sorry for, no. You didn't ask for this. None of us asked for this." I crouched to his height. "Tomorrow night we walk into the roots of the palace and we take back what they stole. You, me, the sergeant, the inquisitor. The four least likely rescuers in the history of Vessing." I smoothed his hair. "And when it's done, you'll never have to be a key again. You'll just be a boy. I promise you that if I have to unmake every law in this city to keep it."

He leaned his forehead against mine. Through his skin I felt the faint pull, the tidal drag of the crown reaching for him across the sleeping city, and I sang against it, low and steady, a lullaby in a dead tongue, and for one hour—one whole hour—he was only a child breathing against my shoulder.

* * *

That evening, the bells rang early.

They rang at sunset instead of curfew, and they rang wrong—a sweetness in them like honey poured over iron, a note that slid into the chest and turned the blood toward the palace. Down the street, shutters banged open. An old man stepped out of the cooper's house in his nightshirt and began to walk, unhurried, eyes soft, face tilted toward the sound. Then the cooper's wife. Then a boy of twelve with his boots unlaced.

Vale, who had come to collect us, stood in my doorway and swore softly. "It's starting. A day early. The bastard isn't waiting for the vigil—it's pulling the city now, tonight, to have them in place."

Tam made a sound behind me, small and cracked, and I turned and found him standing at the top of the cellar stairs with one hand pressed to his chest, his lips moving with the bells, his eyes going wide and fixed and far.

*Tam.* I caught his face in my hands and sang, fast and rough, everything Wren had taught me, every word of agreement and ending I owned, and I felt the crown's pull shudder against my voice like a moth against glass. Tam gasped. His eyes cleared. He grabbed my wrists—hard, harder than a child should grip—and shook his head, and shaped words with his mouth, urgent, urgent:

*Now. Now. Now.*

"He's right," Vale said from the door. Papers already in her hand, hood already up. "If we wait for tomorrow, there won't be a boy left to save. It's tonight or never."

Ivo was already buckling on the knife. "Well," he said, in the voice of a man who has finally stopped choosing the uniform, "then we'd best not keep the king waiting."

I took down Wren's last notebook. I took the signet ring Aldric had left, cold in its cloth. I took my sister's lessons and my twenty years of guilt and my one hour of borrowed peace, and I blew out the shop candles, and I locked the door of the shop that sold nothing for the last time.

Behind us, in the cellar, the Hollow Gate hummed in the dark like a held breath.

The bells rang on. And we went.
