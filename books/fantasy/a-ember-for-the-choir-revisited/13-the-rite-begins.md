## 13. The Rite Begins

I dream of frost.

Not the frost of winter—I know winter, I have known it every year of my forty-one—but a frost that grows inward, blooming along the inside of my ribs like ferns of white iron. In the dream I stand in the throne room and my breath does not fog because my lungs are already cold, and the crown on my head is singing, and everyone in the court is singing with it, and none of them know they are singing, and neither do I, not really, not until—

I wake in my own bed for the first time in nine days.

That is how I measure the thing's attention now. By whether I get to wake as myself. Nine days ago it held me under so long that I learned of a tax decree from my own chancellor, who thanked me for my wisdom in signing it. I had never seen the decree. My hand had signed it. My mouth had praised it. The crown had done both, wearing me the way a man wears gloves.

I lie still beneath the covers and take inventory, the way Vale once taught a frightened young king to do when the council chamber turned against him: fingers, ten. Toes, ten. Name, Aldric Vessing, third of that name, crowned at nineteen when my father's heart quit him mid-mass. Mother dead. Wife dead. Son—no son. No heir, which is why they grew one, isn't it? That is why there is a boy named Tam somewhere in this city with my magic in his marrow and the Gate's old song in his throat.

The frost recedes a little when I think of him. Interesting. The thing does not like me thinking of him.

*Good,* I tell the inside of my skull. *Then I will think of nothing else.*

I rise. The fire has burned to embers. My guards are outside the door—I can hear Breck's weight shifting from foot to foot, the man has never learned to stand still—and beyond them, faintly, the city. Vessing at night sounds like rain even when it isn't raining: gutters, gulls, the groan of the harbor cranes. For twenty years I have slept inside that sound the way a man sleeps inside his own name.

Tonight the sound is wrong.

It takes me a moment, standing at the window with the curtain pulled back two fingers' width, to understand what I am hearing. Or rather, what I am not hearing. The bells.

Vessing has four hundred bells. They ring the hours, the tides, the funerals, the festivals. At any given moment some bell somewhere is speaking. It is the city's pulse, and I have heard it every night of my reign, and tonight it is silent—not because the bells are still, but because they are all ringing at once, so low and so perfectly together that the separate voices have merged into one continuous tone, a sound less like ringing than like humming, like a throat held open.

Like a choir warming up.

"No," I say aloud, and my breath fogs, and the frost blooms up my throat so fast I taste metal.

*Yes,* says the crown, in the First Tongue, in the voice that is not a voice but a pressure behind my eyes. *Yes. It is time.*

---

I have fought it in small ways for months. I have mislaid documents. I have dragged my feet through ceremonies. Once, early on, before I understood what I was fighting, I drove a letter opener into my own thigh in the privy just to feel something that was mine—the pain was mine, at least, the blood was mine—and the crown had simply taken the wound and used my mouth to explain away the limp as a riding injury. After that I stopped hurting myself. You cannot bleed out a passenger.

But I have learned its rhythms. It needs my body for certain things and cannot hold me under continuously without the flesh suffering, and damaged flesh makes a poor vessel, and it is, above all things, careful. Patient. It has waited centuries in that crown—Wren's notes said as much, the pages Elodie copied for me in her cramped left-slanted hand—and a thing that waits centuries does not gamble.

Which means if it is moving openly tonight, tonight is not a gamble. Tonight is a certainty it has arranged.

I dress in the dark. Not the royal clothes—the thing prefers me in them, gold and ermine, a king-shaped puppet on a king-shaped stage—but plain wool, a servant's cloak, Breck's spare boots which fit poorly and I am glad of it because poor-fitting boots keep a man awake. I have planned this badly and quickly, which is the only way I could ever plan anything; my father used to say I decided with my spine. Tonight my spine tells me to go to the Whitlock woman's shop, to the keeper of the Hollow Gate, and to put myself in her hands, because she is the only person alive who knows what is happening and the only person alive who might stop it.

I get as far as the antechamber.

The doors open without being touched. That is the first impossible thing. The second is the courtiers filing in—two dozen of them, in nightclothes and half-fastened doublets, eyes open and empty as coins, arranging themselves along the walls in perfect silence. Chancellor Ovett walks past me without seeing me. His wife follows. A page boy of perhaps eleven years, barefoot on the cold stone, takes his place beside men three times his age.

They are asleep. All of them. Walking, standing, breathing, asleep.

And the hum from the bells comes up through the floor now, through the soles of my stolen boots, and I feel it find something in my chest and turn it, the way you turn a key.

*Stay,* says the crown, gently, almost kindly, and I understand suddenly that it has never needed to be cruel with me because I have never been anything but furniture. *You will want to see this. You have wanted to see it all your life.*

"I have wanted," I say, and my voice cracks on it, "to be *left alone.*"

*No,* it says, with terrible patience. *No one wants that. You wanted to matter. That is why I chose you.*

---

I do not remember deciding to run. I remember the antechamber, and then I remember the servants' stair, taking the steps three at a time while the hum swelled behind me like water rising in a well. Somewhere above, my body's absence would be noticed soon—or worse, noticed and filled, the crown reaching down the thread between us to haul me back like a fish on a line. I felt that thread the whole way down. It is not a metaphor. There is a literal pull, a tug behind the sternum, gentle now but capable of much more; I have seen it drag my own arm across a table to sign what I would not sign.

The kitchens were full of sleepwalkers. Cooks standing at cold stoves, kneading dough that would never bake. A scullery girl washing dishes in a basin of dry water—her hands moving through the motions, the basin empty, her face serene. I stepped around them. One of them, an old porter I have known for twenty years, turned his head as I passed and smiled at me, a warm human smile entirely empty of anything human, and I have seen horrors on battlefields and in my father's deathbed and none of them will stay with me the way that smile will.

Out the postern gate. Into the streets.

Vessing by night should have been loud. Instead it was the quietest thing I had ever walked through. Lamps burning in windows with no one home. Doors standing open. And everywhere, everywhere, people in the streets—all of them moving in the same direction, toward the palace hill, at the same unhurried pace, a river of the sleeping flowing uphill. A woman carried an infant that did not cry. Two old men walked arm in arm, their mouths moving slightly, shaping words I could not hear—or words I could hear only as the hum, the great single note rolling down off the palace and through the cobbles and up into the soles of the world.

I pressed myself into a doorway and let them pass and counted, because counting keeps a man sane: thirty, sixty, ninety sleepwalkers, and no end to them. These were not the court. This was the whole city. Bakers and dockhands and whores and priests, all called out of their beds by four hundred bells ringing one note, all walking to give themselves to whatever the crown intended, and the crown intended, I understood with a clarity that felt like falling, to give them all to itself.

A coronation is a binding. Every king knows this in his bones even when he pretends otherwise—you kneel, you are bound, your life becomes the realm's life and no longer your own. I had thought the Coronation Eternal, the rite Wren died cataloguing, was about the Gate. About power. About the thing's hunger to be more than a crown on a head.

Standing in that silent street, watching my city walk into its own throat, I finally understood it was simpler than that. It was not gathering power. It was gathering *voices*. A choir needs singers. It had spent centuries with one throat—mine, and the vessels' before me—and a choir of one is not a choir at all.

Tam was not just a key. He was the first singer of the new arrangement. And the city was the rest of the music.

---

The Whitlock shop sat dark at the end of Charm Row, and I nearly wept at the sight of it—ordinary, shuttered, a pot of something flowering on the sill despite the season. Ordinary things look holy to a man escaping a miracle.

I hammered on the door. Behind me, up the hill, the hum deepened, and the sleepwalking river thickened, and somewhere in it—God help me—I could hear children.

The door opened a hand's width, a knife visible in the gap, and Elodie Whitlock's eye found mine in the dark.

"Your majesty," she said. Flat. Not surprised. She had been expecting trouble tonight; of course she had. "You're supposed to be on your throne."

"I need you to cut this crown off my head."

"It doesn't come off. Wren's notes are clear. It hasn't come off since the first king—" She stopped. Looked past me at the street, at the sleepers flowing by, and I watched her face do what faces do when a nightmare turns out to be a schedule. "How many?"

"All of them. It's calling the whole city. It's calling the *boy*, Elodie—it's using the bells, it's using everything, and I can't—" The thread behind my sternum yanked. Hard. My knees buckled and I caught the doorframe and for a moment I was not in the street at all, I was on the dais, I was seated, robed, crowned, my hands folded, smiling down at a hall packed with the sleeping, and the crown was saying *there you are* the way a man says it to a dog that wandered off—

Elodie grabbed my collar and hauled me inside and threw the bolt, and the world snapped back. I was on her floor, on my hands and knees, shaking. Above us, muffled, I heard Tam's voice—thin, high, singing something urgent and wordless—and I heard her answer it, calm as a tide-table: "I know, love. I've got you. Hold on to my voice, not theirs."

She knelt across from me. Up close she looked exactly like what she was: a tired woman in her forties who had been carrying a war alone for twenty years and had recently stopped pretending otherwise.

"Tell me fast," she said. "What does it want tonight?"

"Voices." My teeth would barely work. "It's not summoning him to the Gate. It's summoning him to the *choir*. The rite needs a thousand throats and it's taking the city for them. Everyone walking up that hill is going to sing."

Something crossed her face that I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe. Grief, yes, but grief with a blade in it.

"Then we don't stop the rite," she said, standing, already moving, pulling a satchel from under the counter, salt and chalk and knots of braided hair that were never hair ornaments at all. "We ruin the song."

From the cellar door, Tam appeared—small, gray-faced, blood at his nose—and sang three notes at her, urgent, questioning.

"I know," she told him softly. "I hear them too. But you're staying anchored if I have to nail you to the floorboards."

The hum outside rose. Through the shutters I saw the light change—pale gold spilling down the palace hill like dawn arriving eight hours early and from the wrong direction—and every sleepwalker in the street stopped at once, turned their faces upward, and opened their mouths.

They did not sing yet. They only breathed in. All of them. Together.

The city inhaled, and held it, and waited for its cue.

Elodie blew out the lamp.

"Down," she whispered. "All of us. Now. While it's tuning."
