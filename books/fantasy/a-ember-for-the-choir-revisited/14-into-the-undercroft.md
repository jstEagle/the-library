## 14. Into the Undercroft

The well was older than the palace above it.

Ivo showed us to it at the hour when even the bells had given up ringing, in a courtyard behind the old mint where the cobblestones had been pulled up and never replaced. A ring of stones, a wooden lid gone soft with rot, and beneath it—when Vale pried it open with her knife—a darkness that did not smell of water. It smelled of stone, and cold, and something older underneath the cold. Something sweet, almost. Like honey left too long in a shut room.

"That's not a well," I said.

"It's a well the way I'm a bishop," Ivo said. He had come out of uniform, in a fishmonger's coat two sizes too big, and he looked wrong in it the way a sword looks wrong in a scabbard meant for a walking stick. "The maps call it a cistern. The maps are from before the Purge. Before that, the maps call it a throat."

"A throat," Vale repeated flatly.

"Old word. The palace builders thought of the undercrofts as a body. This was how you went down into it." He glanced at me. "Your sister's notes are more detailed than the palace's own. I'll say that for her."

I didn't answer. I was watching Tam.

The boy stood at the lip of the well with his toes over the edge like a bird deciding whether the sky was worth it. The pull from the palace had been growing all day—I could see it in him, the way his head tilted toward the keep as if the crown up there were a hand in his hair, gentle and insistent. His lips moved without sound. He was answering it, or arguing with it. With Tam it was hard to tell the difference.

"Tam." I crouched beside him. "Look at me."

He did. His eyes were the worst part. They had always been old, but tonight they were old and *elsewhere*, and the space between him and me was filling up with a song I couldn't hear.

"When we go down there," I said, "you don't sing. Whatever you hear down there—whatever calls to you—you hold it behind your teeth. Can you do that?"

He pressed his lips together. Then he raised one small hand and, with great care, mimed locking his mouth and swallowing the key. It should have been funny. It was the loneliest thing I had ever seen a child do.

"Good," I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I let him pretend he hadn't noticed.

Vale went first. She had insisted, and none of us had the energy to argue with a woman who had spent the day forging transit papers and interrogating her own conscience. She dropped the last six feet into the dark, boots hitting stone, and called up: "Dry. Old. There's a passage north. Bring the lamp."

I went second, with Tam strapped to my back with a bedsheet because he had refused to be carried and refused to be left and this was the treaty we had reached. His arms around my neck were thin as kindling. He had stopped eating properly two days ago. The crown was drinking him from across the city, a slow sip, patient as interest.

Ivo came last and hauled the lid back over us, and then the night was gone and we were inside the palace's body, and there was no way to pretend anymore that we were anything but swallowed.

* * *

The passage north was a corridor that had forgotten it was a corridor. Roots had come down through the ceiling—real roots, from the plane trees in the mint courtyard, white and blind, feeling their way along the stone like fingers reading a page. Vale's lamp threw our shadows huge against the walls, and the walls were not plain. They were carved.

I had expected dungeons. I had expected the Purge's architecture: iron, salt-ward, the grudging masonry of people building a place to keep magic out. This was the opposite. This was a place built to keep magic *in*, and built by people who had loved it.

The carvings ran in friezes along both walls, worn shallow by centuries. Processions of figures, robed and unrobed, walking toward a door. The door appeared again and again, in every panel, drawn with a care the human figures never got—the door was the subject, the people only its witnesses. And above the door, in every single panel, the same symbol: a circle with a vertical line through it, like an eye that had been closed.

Tam's arms tightened around my neck.

"You know it," I said quietly.

He nodded against my shoulder. Then he reached forward, past my ear, and pointed at the closed eye, and then pointed down the corridor, into the dark ahead, and made a sound in his throat—the first sound I'd heard him make that wasn't the First Tongue. It was a child's sound. Fear has no language.

"The Hollow Gate," Vale said behind me. She had her knife out, which I suspected was how she prayed. "That's what the drawings show. The gate your shop is built over."

"The gate my shop is built over," I agreed, "and every gate like it. There were twelve once. Vessing was built on the seam where the world is thin. The kings kept the doors, and the doors kept the magic, and everyone agreed not to look too hard at either." I had learned this from Wren's notebooks, in the three days since I'd opened them for the first time in twenty years. Wren had known all of it. Wren had known it and gone down under the palace anyway, because knowing a thing and being afraid of it are two rooms in the same house. "And then one king decided he didn't want to keep the doors. He wanted to *be* one."

"The first vessel," Ivo said. "What the jar-voice said."

"The first vessel." I didn't say the rest: that the first vessel had been made from hunger, that hunger doesn't die, that it had waited under the throne for centuries wearing one king after another like coats. I didn't say it because Tam was on my back and children understand more than we spend them knowing.

The corridor ended at a stair, and the stair went down in long shallow sweeps, each step cut for a procession rather than a person, and the sweet honey smell thickened until it was less a smell than a temperature. My wards—the small ones I kept strung around my wrists like a rosary—had begun to itch at the second turning. Now they burned. Not in warning. In *recognition*.

"Elodie," Ivo said, low. "Light ahead."

We killed the lamp and stood a moment in the dark, listening. The dark was not silent. That was the thing I would remember longest about that night, I think, afterward, when I tried to explain to myself why I had not simply turned and climbed back up into the ordinary world: the dark was not silent. There was a sound down there below hearing, below the floor of the ear, a sound you felt in the teeth and the breastbone. A held note. A choir holding one note, forever, the way the drowned hold their breath.

Vale relit the lamp. We went down.

* * *

The guards were asleep.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was all I had to hold onto by then. They were not slumped. They were not drugged. They stood in alcoves along the final gallery, six of them, in palace livery, halberds grounded, eyes open—open and rolled white as boiled eggs—and they were *asleep on their feet*, and their lips were moving.

All six, in unison. No sound. The same shape of the same word, over and over.

Tam made a small noise against my neck.

"I know," I whispered. "I know. Don't look at their mouths."

But of course he looked. Of course I looked. The word they were shaping had a familiar architecture. I had watched Tam's lips make it a hundred times without understanding. It was the First Tongue's word for *come*.

"They're not guarding the vault," Vale said. Her voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when she was most afraid—she had told me that herself, once, over bad tea, the only honest thing either of us had said in that first conversation. "They're *ushers*."

"The rite's close, then," Ivo said.

"The rite is *tomorrow*," I said. "The bells haven't even—"

"The rite doesn't care about bells." Ivo was staring at the sleepwalkers with the expression of a man revising every assumption he'd carried into a room. "The bells are for the city. This—" he nodded at the gallery, the guards, the sweet rot-smell thick as wool, "—this has been starting for years. Maybe decades. Maybe since before Aldric. We're not interrupting a ceremony. We're walking into one that never stopped."

I adjusted Tam on my back and did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do for three days. If the rite had never stopped, then the crown had never been waiting for Tam specifically. Tam was not the key it had sought. Tam was the key it had *grown*, the way you grow a crop, the way you grow a choir. And everything we had done—every risk, every forged paper, every step down this throat—had been permitted. Had been *ushered*.

"Keep walking," I said, because the only alternative was to stand in the dark and admit it, and I had spent twenty years getting good at not admitting things.

* * *

The vault did not have a door.

The gallery opened, all at once, like a throat opening, and we stood at the rim of a room so large the lamp could not find its far wall. The ceiling arched up into darkness. The floor was a shallow bowl of white stone, and set into the floor, in ranks, in rows, in the pattern of an amphitheater or an orchard—hundreds of them. Thousands.

Jars.

Each one the size of a wine flagon, glass gone amber with age, stoppered with wax and lead and things I didn't have names for. And inside each jar—not a specimen, not a relic, nothing so honest as bone—

Light.

Each jar held a small storm of light, gold-white, turning, restless, pressing against the glass the way a moth presses against a window, the way a voice presses against a shut mouth. They turned and turned and never stopped turning. Twenty years I had kept a sealed door and told myself I was keeping the world safe from magic. The world had been keeping magic in *jars*. Labeled. Catalogued. The nearest rank had brass plaques screwed to the floor at the base of each one, and I made the mistake of reading them.

*Chorister, Third Rank. Bound in the reign of Aldric's grandfather. Source: the Weeping Gate, sealed 40 years prior.*

*Vessel, failed. Age at binding: nine.*

*Chorister, First Rank. Source: volunteer. Note: unusually durable.*

Volunteer. I read that word three times. Somewhere above us the crown was singing through six sleepwalking guards, and down here the state had written *volunteer* on a jar of light, and the light went on turning, patient, patient, patient, because what else could it do.

"Gods below," Ivo said. He had taken off the fishmonger's hat he'd worn for disguise, which I think was his version of uncovering. "It's the choir. It's the actual choir. The one in the old songs—the one that sang the first kings onto their thrones. They didn't abolish it. They *bottled* it."

"The Purge didn't outlaw magic," Vale said. She had gone pale, and her knife-hand had gone steady, which meant she was past fear into the country beyond it. "I've spent eleven years in the inquisition and I never once asked what we did with the sorcerers we took. I signed the transfer orders. I signed *hundreds* of transfer orders. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought there was a prison."

"There's a prison," I said. "This is it."

Tam slid down off my back. I let him, because my arms were shaking and because he was already gone, already walking, drawn down the ranks of jars like a leaf on a current. His hand rose as he passed each one—not touching, never touching, hovering an inch above the glass—and the light inside reached for him. Every jar. Every single one. As he walked the aisle, the amber storms leaned toward him like wheat toward wind, like the drowned toward air, like—

Like a choir toward its conductor.

"Tam," I said. "Tam, come back."

He stopped.

Not because I'd called him. He stopped because he had found what he was walking toward, and I felt it before I understood it, the way you feel a change in the weather: the note below hearing shifted. The held note that filled the vault bent, and broke, and reformed around a single jar at the end of the last rank—apart from the others, set in its own alcove, older than the rest. The glass on it was darker, the wax seal black instead of white, the plaque hand-lettered rather than stamped.

I walked to it. My legs did it without consulting me.

The plaque read: *Chorister, unranked. Source: the Hollow Gate. Bound during the Purge. Note: resisted.*

Resisted. Two words on a brass plate and I was on my knees in front of them. Because I knew the hand that had lettered the plaque—cramped, impatient, the descender on the R hooked like a fishing line. I had seen it on a hundred shopping lists, on the margins of my mother's recipe book, on the note Wren had left on my pillow the last morning of her life: *Gone to open it. Don't be angry. You'll understand when you hear it.*

The jar held a storm of light like all the others. But where the others turned, this one *pressed*. It had pressed so long, against the same point of the glass, that the glass had worn thin there—a pale spot, a scar, the shape of a palm.

A hand's worth of light, pushing on the inside of the world, for twenty years.

"Wren," I said.

The vault went quiet—not silent, never silent, but the held note gathered itself, and bent, and came around to me through a thousand jars and one, and the light in the dark jar flared white, and a voice that was not a voice opened inside my skull like a door I had sealed myself, twenty years ago, with my own two hands—

*Elodie. Oh, Elodie. You came. You came too late and you came exactly on time, and I have so little left, so listen—*

The light pressed harder against the worn place in the glass. Around us, in ranks without end, the choir began, very softly, to sing.
