## 15. Wren

The jar did not look like a person.

That was the first cruelty of it. Twenty years of imagining—of nightmares in which Wren stood behind glass, or lay under it, or called to her from the dark—and the reality was a sphere of amber light the size of a loaf of bread, resting on a stone shelf in a vault that smelled of cold iron and old honey. It sat among hundreds of its kind. Rows upon rows of them, receding into the dark like orchard fruit, each one glowing faintly, each one humming.

Each one singing.

Elodie had heard the choir through Tam's cracked seal for three days now, thin as thread through cloth. Here, at the source, it was not thin. It filled the vault the way water fills a lung. She stood six steps from her sister's jar and could not make her feet take the seventh.

"Elodie." Tam's voice, small in all that sound. He had gone gray around the mouth since they'd entered the undercroft; the crown's pull was stronger here, and he kept one hand pressed flat against his own sternum as if holding a door shut. "It knows you're here."

The amber light pulsed.

Once. Twice. Then it began to move—not the slow tidal pulse of the other jars but quick, arrhythmic, like a heart remembering what fear was. The hum inside it bent, warped, and tore into something that was not music.

It was a scream.

Elodie knew the scream. She had heard it once, twenty years ago, through a closing door, and she had heard it every night since in the space between sleeping and waking. Her knees went before she decided to move, and then she was on the cold stone with both hands pressed against the jar's surface, which was warm—warm as a cheek, warm as a hand held too long over a candle—and the scream resolved, syllable by syllable, into a name.

*Elodie. Elodie. Elodie.*

"Wren," she said. Her voice came out in pieces. "Wren, I'm here. I'm sorry. I'm here."

The light flared so bright she had to shut her eyes, and through her palms she felt something that no jar should contain: pressure, urgency, the unmistakable push of a living will against a wall it had been beating on for two decades.

Behind her, Ivo made a low sound in his throat. Vale said nothing at all, but Elodie heard her step back—one pace, two—as if the vault had grown teeth.

Tam knelt beside her. He did not touch the jar. He only listened, head tilted, lips moving slightly, translating.

"She says—" He stopped. Started again. "She says you took too long. But she says it kindly."

A laugh broke out of Elodie that was half sob and entirely involuntary. *Took too long.* That was Wren. That was Wren exactly: dead twenty years and still scolding her for being late.

"I know," Elodie whispered. "I know I did."

* * *

Tam had never heard anyone scream with joy before.

He hadn't known it was possible—that a voice could be stretched so thin with pain and still find room for gladness. But the woman in the jar managed it, and listening to her was like standing in a doorway between weather systems. Grief on one side. Relief on the other. Him in the middle, getting rained on by both.

He understood jars. Not these jars, not yet—he could feel them the way you feel a bruise, hundreds of small imprisoned songs pressing against his awareness—but *jars*. Vessels. He had been one. He was one still, if he was honest, though his walls were flesh and his lid was a skull, and somewhere across the city a thing made of rust and hunger was pulling on him like a fisherman pulling line.

The bound woman was talking to Elodie now in long rushes of First Tongue, too fast, tumbling over itself. Tam caught pieces. He translated the pieces because translating was the only useful thing he had ever done and he intended to keep doing it until his usefulness ran out, which, given the pull against his ribs, might be soon.

"She says the Purge didn't kill her," he said. "She says the Purge *collected* her. There were others that night—a whole cartload. They brought them down here and the crown drank them." He paused, frowning at a phrase that wouldn't sit still. "Not killed. *Kept.* She says being kept is worse."

Elodie's hands stayed on the glass. They had not moved since she fell. Tam wondered if she could feel what he felt when he stood near the jars—the way each one tugged faintly at him, recognizing him, calling him *little brother* in a language older than brothers.

"She says your seal was good," he added. "She says it bought twenty years. She says—" His breath caught. The next part arrived in his head already shaped for Common, pre-translated, urgent. "She says stop crying and listen, because there isn't much time and you always did talk instead of listen."

The scream-joy-sound cracked into laughter. Even bound in amber, even mid-scream, Wren Whitlock could laugh at her sister.

* * *

Elodie listened.

She listened while the vault hummed and Tam's young voice carried Wren's older one, and piece by piece the story assembled itself out of the First Tongue like a body being stitched back together.

The crown was not a crown. It had never been a crown. It was the first vessel—the original—the prototype. Some king, centuries dead, whose name even the choir had forgotten, had wanted what every vessel since had been made to provide: permanence. Continuance. A self that did not end. He had poured himself into gold and binding-craft and set it on his own head, and it had worked, and it had been the worst thing that ever happened to this kingdom, because a hunger given permanence does not stay hungry for bread.

Every vessel since had been an attempt to feed it or control it or use it. Every voice in every jar was fuel. And Aldric—

"Aldric is just the current coat it's wearing," Elodie said aloud, testing the shape of it. "And Tam is the key because he was grown from Gate-magic. From *my* gate."

*Your gate,* Wren agreed, through Tam. *You sealed a door with a lock that size and never asked where the key would come from. You never were the careful one.*

"I was the *careful* one," Elodie said. "That was the entire problem."

But Wren wasn't finished, and the playfulness drained out of the translation like color from a face. Tam's voice dropped. He had to work for the next words; Elodie watched his jaw tighten around sounds that clearly hurt to carry.

"She says there's a price," Tam said. "For unbinding. She says the binding is the only thing keeping them—" his hand made a vague, terrible gesture at the rows of jars— "*in* their shapes. A voice without a body doesn't persist. It disperses. The crown holds them together the way ice holds water. Break the ice and—"

He stopped.

"Say it," Elodie said. Her voice was very steady, which surprised her, the way a rope bridge is steady right up until it isn't. "All of it."

"Break the ice," Tam whispered, "and the water goes everywhere. Freeing Aldric means freeing every voice. And freeing every voice means—" He looked at the jar. At the warm amber light pulsing under Elodie's palms. "It means letting them die. All of them. Including her."

The vault hummed. Hundreds of jars, hundreds of voices, singing their patient two-decade song, and every note of it suddenly sounded like a held breath.

* * *

Tam watched the sisters not-talk to each other.

They weren't speaking. Elodie had gone silent, forehead nearly touching the jar, and Wren's light had dimmed to a slow ember-pulse, and between them stretched twenty years of everything unsaid, and Tam—who had grown up in a palace where people said cruel things fluently and kind things never—thought: *this is the bravest silence I have ever heard.*

He wished he could give them privacy. There was no privacy in a vault full of listeners. The other jars had shifted, subtly, toward Wren's, the way flowers lean at a window. They knew one of their own was speaking. They were waiting to hear how the story ended.

He was waiting too. Because the math of it was simple and horrible and it had his name in it.

Unbind the crown: free the choir. Free the choir: kill the choir. Don't free the crown: the Coronation Eternal finishes, the crown-thing wears Aldric forever, drinks the city, and uses Tam as its doorpost until nothing is left of him but obedience. Either road ran through someone's death. The only question was whose, and how many.

He had fled the palace to stop being a vessel. He was beginning to understand that no one stopped being a vessel. They only got to choose what they carried.

"Ask her," Elodie said finally. Her voice was wrecked and level. "Ask her if she wants it. The release. Ask her honestly, Tam, and don't soften whatever she answers."

He asked. In First Tongue, carefully, the way you'd ask someone if they wanted the window open while they drowned.

Wren's answer came back fast and blazing, and Tam flinched from it, and translated it exactly:

*"I have screamed for twenty years. Do not you dare ask me if I want it to end. Ask me if I want it to MEAN something."*

* * *

There was more. Of course there was more. Wren talked for what felt like an hour, and Tam translated until his throat was raw, and Elodie learned things about her sister's last night that rearranged the furniture of her grief: that Wren hadn't died reopening the Gate in some noble accident. That she had seen the collectors coming for the neighborhood's children and opened the Gate deliberately, wide, to flood the street with wild magic and scatter the hunt. That it had worked. That the children lived. That the cost had been Wren herself, taken mid-working, bound screaming, and that she had spent twenty years in a jar listening to her sister sell seasoning salt one street away and had never once blamed her for the seal.

*I sealed it to keep anything else getting out,* Elodie had said, and Wren had answered, gentle as a knife: *No, love. You sealed it so you'd never have to watch another person you love die doing the right thing. There's a difference, and it's fine, and it's time.*

Now Elodie sat back on her heels, and the vault waited, and Tam waited, and somewhere above them a king who was mostly not-a-king anymore was running out of hours.

"The rite," Elodie said. "When we break the crown. When we sing the release. What happens to Aldric? Does he survive it?"

Tam translated. Wren's answer was long. Parts of it he gave word for word; parts he had to gather up slowly, like spilled grain.

"He survives," Tam said. "Mostly. She says the crown has worn him for years and he'll come out of it... smaller. Missing pieces. A man after a fever, not a man after a war." A pause. "She says that's not the worst news."

"Give me the worst news."

"The release has to be sung by a vessel. A willing one. Through the Gate-key." Tam's hand drifted again to his sternum. "Through me. She says the song will pass through whoever sings it, and the crown will try to keep what passes through, and the singer has to hold the door open from the inside." He met Elodie's eyes. He was twelve years old and he said it plainly, because no one had ever taught him to soften things and he was suddenly grateful for it. "She means me. She means I might not come back out."

The vault hummed. The jars leaned in.

Elodie looked at her sister's jar for a long moment. Then she reached out and touched it once, lightly, the way you touch a door you're about to walk through and may never walk back through.

"No," she said.

*Elodie—*

"No. I buried you once. I built my whole life in the shape of that grave. I am not going to trade one child for one sister, and I'm not going to let you volunteer him, and I'm not—" Her voice cracked clean through. "I'm not losing anyone else to that crown. Find me another way or there is no way."

The amber light pulsed—slow, stubborn, furious. And then, before Tam could translate, before anyone could do anything at all, every jar in the vault flared at once, hundreds of voices surging up through the floor and the walls and Tam's own bones, and the choir spoke with one mouth directly into Elodie's mind, in Common, in a chorus like a landslide:

*THERE IS NO OTHER WAY. THERE NEVER WAS. THE ONLY QUESTION IS WHO HOLDS THE DOOR.*

The light died back to embers. The vault rang with silence.

And far above them, muffled through forty feet of stone, the bells of Vessing began to ring—out of hour, out of order, out of tune.

Tam went white. "It's starting," he said. "The rite. It's starting *now*."
