## 16. The Crown's Court

I am in the throne room, and I am not.

That is the shape of my life now: a man standing behind his own eyes like a beggar at a window, watching a warm room he is no longer permitted to enter. The crown-thing wears me the way I once wore this crown—easily, without thought, as if the weight had always belonged to it. My hands rest on the arms of the throne. They are my hands. They do not answer to me.

Below the dais, the court of Vessing stands assembled in the grey hour before dawn, and every face among them is wrong. Not vacant—worse than vacant. Serene. The sleepwalking did not end when they reached the palace; it settled into something gentler, a congregation at a service they cannot remember attending. Lords who spent thirty years sharpening knives against each other stand shoulder to shoulder, breathing in unison. The Lord Chancellor, old Hask, who once wept over a ledger like a widow, smiles up at the throne with the simple joy of a child watching bread rise.

*They are happy,* the crown says through my mouth, to no one, to itself. *You never made them happy.*

It speaks less and less to me now. I have become furniture it talks past. But sometimes, when it is pleased, it lets a little of its attention spill backward into me, the way a rich man tosses a coin to a beggar without looking down. I have learned to hate even that.

The bells stopped an hour ago. The whole city heard them—the deep bells of the Citadel, the cracked bell of Tanner's Row, the small handbells of the night-soil men—and then the streets emptied, and now here they all are, filling the nave of the throne room like water finding its level. More come still. Through the tall windows I can see them crossing the outer courtyard in loose rivers, farmers still in their boots, dockworkers with rope burns on their palms, a woman carrying a sleeping infant she does not wake. None of them stumble. None of them shiver, though the morning is cold enough to frost the glass.

My body shifts on the throne, settling deeper, and I feel the crown settle with it, roots I cannot see threading further down into the stone. The dais has been singing for days. I felt it begin as a hum in my teeth, then in the marrow of my jaw, and now the whole platform thrums like a struck bell that will not finish dying away. Beneath us—I know this the way I know things now, by theft, by osmosis, by the crown's careless leakage—the undercroft waits, and the choir waits inside the undercroft, and inside the choir—

Inside the choir there is a boy.

*Tam,* I think, deliberately, loudly, the way you shout into a well. *Tam.*

Nothing. Or—not nothing. A flicker, far off, like a lamp carried behind a hill. He is still himself. The crown has been pulling at him across the city for three days, threads of song tightening around whatever he is, and still he holds. I do not know how. I was a grown man, armored in twenty years of kingship, and it took the thing less than a season to hollow me. The boy is eleven years old and made of the same magic as the gate beneath a charm shop, and he holds.

Hold, boy. Whatever else you are, hold.

The great doors open.

I feel it before I see it—a change in the pressure of the room, the way a change comes over water before a storm crosses it. The congregation turns as one, smooth as a field of wheat bending, and through the doors, flanked by four of my own household guard whose faces wear the same serene nothing as everyone else's, they bring him.

Tam walks between them because he is made to walk, not because his legs obey anyone but him. His head is up. That is the first thing I notice, and the thing I will remember longest: they drag him, and he keeps his head up. His wrists are bound with cord that glimmers faintly, gold thread shot through with something darker, and where it touches his skin the skin blisters, and he does not cry out. His eyes find the throne—find *me*, I think, though I cannot be sure what he sees when he looks at the king wearing the crown—and for one moment his mouth moves. No sound. Just the shape of words in a language older than my line, older than any line, and the crown flinches.

I felt it flinch. Inside my own skull, I felt the thing flinch, a quick cold contraction like a fist closing, and I understood two things at once. First: the boy can hurt it. Second: it is afraid of him, and everything it does from this moment forward will be done by something afraid.

Fear makes tyrants honest. I should know.

They bring him up the steps of the dais. The guard stop at the base; the crown does not permit them closer, some instinct of territory, and Tam climbs the last steps alone, bound hands swinging, blistered wrists held carefully away from his sides. At the top he stops, three paces from the throne, and looks up at me—at it—at us.

And the crown leans forward in my body, and speaks to him in the First Tongue.

I understand none of it. That is the truth, and I hold onto the truth the way a drowning man holds a spar: I understand *none of it*. The language comes out of my mouth in a voice that is almost mine, pitched low and resonant, and every word lands in the room like a stone dropped in still water. The congregation sways toward it. The bells, silent these three hours, give a single soft note of their own accord. And Tam stands at the top of the steps and listens with his chin up and his eyes dry, and answers—one word, flat, final, the way a child says *no* when no is the whole of his soul.

The crown laughs. It uses my laugh, my own laugh, the one Wren—no. Not Wren. The one Elodie's sister must have known, if they were close; I know so little, I a king who knows nothing of the people he ruled until it was too late to rule them otherwise. It uses my laugh and it is obscene, hearing it come out of my throat with that thing behind it.

Then the doors open again.

* * *

She comes up the center aisle like weather.

I have seen her twice before—in visions the crown fed me, or let me steal, I no longer know which—and neither time prepared me for the reality of her. She is smaller than the visions made her, broader in the shoulder, with the walk of a woman who has stood behind a counter for twenty years and lifted a thousand small heavy things. Her hair is coming loose from its braid. There is flour, absurdly, on the cuff of her coat. Behind her come the sergeant, Coverly, sword drawn and held low, and the inquisitor woman, Vale, pale as paper and walking like someone who has already decided how she will hang.

Elodie Whitlock stops at the foot of the dais and looks up at her king, and I want to scream at her: *Look at the eyes. Look at the eyes and know I am not home.*

But she already knows. I can tell she knows, because she does not kneel, and because when she speaks she does not address the throne at all. She addresses the space just above it, the air, the dark, the thing inside the metal.

"I know what you are," she says. Her voice carries in the vast room without effort, the way voices carry in churches. "Wren told me. What's left of her told me. You were the first vessel. Some king's hunger, poured into a shape and given forever. You've been doing this a long time."

The crown considers her. I feel it considering, a slow cold turning of attention, and I realize with a lurch that it is deciding something—that it came to this meeting with a plan, that the plan predates her arrival, that everything since the bells began has been arranged to produce exactly this moment: the boy on the dais, the keeper below it, the court asleep, the rite one voice short of complete.

It needs willingness. That is the flaw in it, the crack Wren spoke of through the jar, the one condition stitched into the oldest magic like a maker's mark. The rite requires a vessel who *chooses*. It dragged the boy here, bound and burning, but binding is not choosing, and the crown knows it, and so it has saved its true weapon for last.

"Elodie Whitlock," my mouth says. My voice, my cadence, my father's courtroom gravity laid over that cold interior like paint over rot. "Keeper of the Hollow Gate. Sister of Wren." A pause, delicate, surgical. "We have something of hers."

The floor of the undercroft opens.

Not physically—the stone does not move—but light does, rising up through the seams of the dais in slow amber columns, and inside the light there are voices. Hundreds. The whole choir, singing softly, the sound they have made beneath the palace for longer than the palace has stood. And out of the general chorus one voice rises, threads itself free, and comes up the amber light like smoke, and settles in the air above Elodie's head.

"Wren," the crown says, gently, using my voice the way a man uses a knife he loves. "Would you like to hear her?"

And the voice speaks.

"Sister." Cracked. Faint. Twenty years bound mid-scream, and still unmistakably, devastatingly, *her*—the particular wry drag at the end of the word, the accent of the river districts that Elodie scrubbed from her own speech decades ago and never quite from her ears. "Elodie. Love. It's so loud down here. It's so *loud*—"

Elodie Whitlock makes a sound I hope never to hear again as long as anything remains of me. It is small. It is worse than screaming. Coverly catches her elbow and she shakes him off and stands alone in the amber light with her sister's voice raining down on her, and the crown watches her the way a surgeon watches an incision.

"Here is the bargain," it says.

And I, Aldric, third of my name, king of Vessing, passenger in my own skull, listen to the thing describe my destruction in terms so reasonable that part of me—the buried, starving part that still wants to govern—thinks: *that would work. On almost anyone, that would work.*

"The boy," it says, "was made for this. Not stolen for it—made. Grown from the gate's own root, shaped vessel, empty by design. He has no family. No history. He has known one year of freedom and it has cost him blisters and terror and a cellar floor." The amber light brightens; the voice of Wren sobs softly somewhere inside it, and the sobbing is real, I am nearly certain it is real, and that is the horror of the thing—it does not need to lie when it owns so much truth. "In exchange for his consent—his free, willing consent, spoken in the First Tongue—we will restore her. Whole. As she was. Flesh, breath, memory, laughter. Every year the choir took from her, returned. She will walk out of this room alive, and the laws of your city will not touch her, because there will be no more laws of this city. Only the coronation eternal, and peace, and her."

Silence. The congregation breathes. Somewhere high in the vaulting, water drips from melting frost—the frost that has been creeping through the palace for weeks, my frost, the cold the crown carries inside it like a second skeleton.

"You're lying," Elodie says. But her voice shakes, and the crown hears the shake, and I feel it adjust, patient, precise.

"Am I?" it says. "Ask her."

"Wren—" Elodie's voice cracks clean through. "Wren, love, look at me, if you can—if any of you can—tell me if it's true. Can it bring you back?"

A long pause. The choir murmurs underneath, hundreds of voices, and I wonder how many of them were sisters to someone. How many Elodies are standing in other rooms, in other years, being offered this same arithmetic.

"It could," the voice says at last, reluctantly, and oh, I hear it, I hear the wanting in it, twenty years of a jar and a scream compressed into two words. "It could, Elodie. It's done it before. Long ago. Before the Purge. There was a vessel who refused, and they unmade her instead, but the one before—" The voice frays. "Love, I don't know what I am anymore. I don't know how much of me is left to bring back. But it *could*. I think it could."

"There," the crown says softly, through my mouth, with my tenderness, my father's gentlest register, the one he used to dismiss petitioners he had already ruined. "You hear it. From her own lips. We offer you no trick, keeper. The boy consents, the girl lives. Refuse, and the rite proceeds anyway—slower, cruder, tearing what it needs from him rather than receiving it—and she stays in the dark, and he breaks regardless. Consent buys him wholeness within the crown. Refusal buys him nothing but pain and the same ending." A pause. "You are a woman who has already sacrificed one sister to keep a door shut. We ask only that you decline to sacrifice another."

And that is the knife. Not the offer—the framing. It has taken her guilt, the guilt I glimpsed in the vision, twenty years of salt rings and sealed doors and a grave she visits with her eyes whenever the shop goes quiet, and it has turned that guilt into the blade's handle, fitted to her grip exactly, offered hilt-first like a courtesy.

Take the crown, boy. Save the dead woman. Break yourself willingly, and everyone gets to stop grieving.

Tam has not moved through any of this. He stands three paces from the throne with his blistered wrists and his lifted chin, and now, slowly, he turns his head and looks at Elodie. And he smiles.

It is the worst thing in the room. Worse than the frost, worse than the sleeping court, worse than Wren's voice fraying in the amber light. An eleven-year-old, bound and burned, smiling at the woman who is being asked to sell him, because he has already decided to say yes—he has decided to say yes to save her sister, to save *her*, because she gave him a name and a cellar and taught him letters on flour-dusted wood, and he is eleven, and he does not yet know that love is allowed to refuse sacrifices.

His mouth shapes the first syllable of the First Tongue.

"No," Elodie says.

* * *

She says it quietly. That is what undoes the room's architecture—everyone, the crown included, braced for a shout, for defiance pitched to fill the nave, and instead she says it the way she must have said it across a hundred counters: *no, that's not the price, no, you'll pay fair or you'll leave.*

"No," she says again, and steps forward, up onto the first step of the dais, close enough that the guard stir and Coverly's sword comes up. "You've done your sums wrong, and I'll tell you where. You think I'm a woman weighing two losses against each other. One child against one sister. Living against dead. Even trade." Her voice does not shake now. Whatever shook in her finished shaking, and what is left is level and terrible. "But I knew Wren. Do you? Twenty minutes ago you owned her voice, and you thought that meant you owned her. Wren would climb out of whatever heaven you've got stored down there and burn the jar herself before she let me buy her back with a child. You haven't offered me my sister. You've offered me a thing wearing her voice, and you've offered it to the worst, smallest, most frightened part of me, and I want you to know—" she takes another step, and the amber light bends around her like water around a stone— "that I heard it. I heard the part of me that wanted to say yes. It sounded like relief. And I am not going to be relieved. I am going to be *right*."

The crown is silent.

For the first time since it took me, it is silent, and in that silence I feel something I had stopped hoping to feel again: the faintest loosening, a fractional easing of pressure, like a fist distracted. It is recalculating. It built this moment stitch by stitch across three days and a city full of bells, and the stitch has not held, and it does not have a second speech prepared because nothing in its long hungry history required one.

Elodie turns to the boy.

"Tam," she says, in Common, slow, the way you speak to someone across a great distance. "Listen to me. You don't owe me your yes. You don't owe anybody anything—you hear me?—you were *made*, which means everything you choose after that is yours, all of it, yours. And I'm telling you what I choose." Her voice finally breaks, one clean break, and she lets it. "I choose you. Alive. Unbroken. Even if it costs me her again. Even if it costs me everything again. I am done paying for the dead with the living. Do you understand me?"

Tam's smile falters. Becomes something younger. His mouth works, searching for Common, finding it the way he always finds it—haltingly, like a boy crossing a river on stones.

"...Yes," he says. "I... staying. With you. Staying."

And the crown screams.

Not aloud—not at first. It screams inside me, a sound like a glacier calving, like a cathedral of ice letting go all at once, and the amber light gutters, and the choir's murmur rises toward something that is not a hymn, and the frost on the windows blooms outward in white ferns faster than the eye can follow. My body rises off the throne. The congregation sways. High above, the silent bells gather breath.

It has one move left, and it is going to spend it now, and I can feel it gathering—through my bones, through the singing dais, through the roots it has driven into the palace stone. The rite without consent. The crude road. Tear what it needs.

Elodie sees it coming. I watch her see it—watch her glance once at Vale, once at Coverly, once at the boy—and I watch her do the last thing I expect.

She starts to sing.

One note. Low, rough, untrained, a shopkeeper's voice with no music in it, singing a word of the First Tongue that I do not know and the crown, evidently, does. The amber light shivers. In the undercroft below, something cracks—a sound like a jar breaking, distant, distinct.

And out of the floor, out of the choir, out of a hundred throats that have waited in the dark for longer than kingdoms, the harmony rises to meet her.

The crown turns my head toward the boy, and Tam opens his mouth, and the last thing I see clearly, before the light goes white and the bells come down and the world becomes a single held chord, is Elodie Whitlock reaching up the dais steps with both hands open—not grasping, offering—and the boy reaching back.

The rite begins. Both of them. All of us.

I put my hands against the inside of my own skull, and push.
