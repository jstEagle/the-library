## 17. Refusal

The bargain hung in the air of the throne room like smoke that refused to rise.

*Wren restored whole,* the crown-thing had said, wearing Aldric's mouth like a glove worn soft at the fingers. *In exchange for the boy. Willingly. That is all I have ever asked of anyone — willingness. It is the oldest currency.*

Elodie stood in the great doors' shadow with Tam's small hand burning in hers, and she thought: *it has done this arithmetic before.* It had done it to Wren, surely. *Your sister lives if you open the gate* — no, not quite that; Wren had gone willingly into the Purge's teeth for reasons Elodie had spent twenty years refusing to examine. But the shape was the same. The shape was always the same. Take the thing you love most and price it against the thing you cannot lose, and let the grieving woman do the crown's work for it.

Beside her, Tam's fingers tightened. He looked up — mud-streaked, hollow-eyed, the choir singing faintly through his skin like wind through a cracked window — and he did not speak in Common or in First Tongue. He only shook his head, once, small.

*Don't,* that shake said. *Don't trade me. Don't trade her either.*

Above them, on the dais, the king's body sat straight-backed in the throne it had been nailed to, and the crown sat on the king, and neither of them blinked.

"You hesitate," the crown-thing said, and Aldric's voice cracked in the middle of the word, because somewhere underneath, the man was still fighting. "Understandable. Grief makes poor accountants. Let me make the ledger plain."

It raised one hand — Aldric's hand, knuckles white where the man inside was trying to curl it into a fist — and the air above the dais filled with light. Not warm light. Amber light, jar-light, the color of things preserved against their will. And in the light, a figure.

Wren.

Not whole. A sketch of Wren, drawn in preserved light — the set of her shoulders, the crooked part in her hair, the half-smile she'd never been able to keep off her face when she was about to do something stupid and brave. Twenty years fell on Elodie like a dropped ceiling.

"El," the sketch said, in Wren's voice, frayed at the edges like a recording played too many times. "El, don't listen to it. Don't—"

The crown-thing closed its fist and the voice stopped mid-word, and that was the answer, wasn't it. That was the whole answer. It could not offer Wren whole because it did not *have* Wren whole. It had a scream, bound mid-scream since the Purge, and it was playing Elodie her sister's voice like a merchant playing a sample of cloth he intended to sell by the yard.

"There," the crown-thing said gently. "You see what I hold. Restore her fully — memory, mind, voice, all of it unbound and breathing — and give me the vessel. He was made for this. He knows it. Ask him."

"I'm asking him," Elodie said.

Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. She stepped forward, out of the shadow of the doors, and the sleepwalking court turned its heads in unison, a hundred faces smooth as eggs, tracking her. Vale and Ivo were somewhere behind her — she'd lost sight of them when the court had risen from their seats like puppets remembering their strings — but she could hear Ivo breathing hard, and that was enough. Someone human was behind her. That mattered.

"Tam." She crouched, brought herself level with the boy, and took both his hands. They were cold. They had been cold for three days, ever since the bells started, as if the crown were drawing heat across the city the way a wick draws oil. "I'm going to ask you something and I need the true answer, not the brave one."

He waited.

"Do you want to do this? Take the crown. End it yourself. It would stop the singing — it told you that, didn't it? It promised you quiet."

Tam's eyes went bright and wet. He nodded. Then, slowly, deliberately, he shook his head.

Both. Both things true. Of course they were both true. He was nine years old and he had been built as a key and every night for three days the crown had sung through his bones, and a child can want silence so badly he'll walk into the fire that promises it. Elodie had been that child once, in her own way. She had sealed a gate over her sister's grave and called the sealing safety, and she had lived twenty years in the quiet it bought, and the quiet had cost exactly everything.

"No," she said aloud, to Tam, to the crown, to the room. "That's not willingness. That's exhaustion wearing willingness's coat. I've seen the difference up close. I've *been* the difference."

"Sentiment," said the crown-thing. "The laws of Vessing—"

"The laws of Vessing are yours," Elodie said. "That's rather the point."

She stood. Her knees ached. She was fifty-three years old and she had sold fake charms for two decades and her back hurt from sleeping on a cellar floor, and none of that mattered, none of it, because here was the thing she had understood in the undercroft with Wren's jar cradled in her arms:

The crown-thing dealt in bargains because bargains were how it had been made. Some king, some hungering first-vessel, some ancient transaction where a man had traded everything for permanence — and every deal it offered since was just that original sin wearing new clothes. It could not imagine refusal. Refusal was the one word not in its ledger.

So she would say it. Not with a sword. Not with heroics. With the language underneath all kingdoms, the one that predated thrones and crowns and the whole rotten architecture of binding.

She opened Wren's notebooks in her memory — three days of desperate study, Wren's cramped handwriting, the phonetics scratched out and rewritten, the marginal note in her sister's hand: *the oldest word isn't a command, El. It's a permission. You don't force a door open. You tell it it's allowed.*

"Tam," she said. "When I start, sing with me. Whatever comes out of you, let it come. All of them too." She lifted her chin toward the dark of the undercroft stair, where the cracked seal still leaked amber glow. "They've been waiting twenty years for someone to give permission. I'm giving it."

The crown-thing understood then. She saw it understand — saw Aldric's face go through something fast and terrible, hope and horror in the same spasm, because the man beneath knew what was coming even if the thing above him didn't believe it could.

"*Stop her,*" it said, and the court moved.

* * *

They came down the dais steps like water finding a drain — unhurried, inevitable, a hundred sleepwalkers in court dress converging. Behind her Ivo shouted, and there was the scrape of a sergeant's baton drawn, and Vale's voice cutting flat and clear: *"In the name of—" *and then, abandoning the office she hated, just:* "ELodie, MOVE!"

But Elodie didn't move. Moving was the old mistake. Running was how you ended up keeping a shop that sold nothing for twenty years.

She planted her feet on the throne room floor, in the palace her sister had died beneath, held a nine-year-old boy's cold hands in hers, and sang.

The first sound was ugly. Three days of self-taught phonetics, a throat unused to anything but haggling and small talk — the word came out cracked, mis-stressed, wrong in three places. *Release.* The oldest word. Permission, not command. She sang it anyway, and kept singing it, and the second time it was less wrong, and the third time—

The third time, Tam joined her.

And the throne room *changed.*

His voice was small and it was not small. It was a child's voice carrying a thousand voices the way a river carries rain — every bound vessel, every jar in the vault below, every stolen singer stacked generation under generation beneath the palace roots, rising through him because he was made of the same magic as the Gate and the Gate had taught him what doors were for. The harmony hit the walls and the walls remembered being stone in a quarry, unclaimed. The windows remembered sand. The amber light in the jars below flared so hard it lit the seams of the floor, veins of gold crawling up through the flagstones like dawn under a door.

The sleepwalking court stopped.

Not woke — stopped, caught mid-stride, a hundred bodies hanging between one step and the next while the song worked through them like warmth through frozen hands. One woman near the front shuddered. Her face broke its smoothness, and what came up underneath was terror, and grief, and the particular confusion of a person waking somewhere they never chose to be. She looked at her own raised foot. She put it down. She began, quietly, to weep.

"It doesn't work that way," the crown-thing said.

Aldric's voice. But thinner now. Stretched.

"It has *never* worked that way. Binding is order. Binding is law. You cannot simply—"

Elodie sang louder.

Not heroics. She wanted to be clear about that, even alone inside her own skull. This was not bravery; her legs were shaking and her voice was tearing and somewhere in the last minute she had started crying without noticing. This was bookkeeping. This was twenty years of interest come due. Every fake charm sold, every night checking a dead gate, every morning waking with Wren's name in her mouth like a stone she'd swallowed — it had all compounded, silently, into this: a woman standing in the worst room in Vessing singing one word over and over because it was the only debt she could still pay.

*Release. Release. Release.*

The jars began to crack.

She felt it before she heard it — a change in the harmony, a brightening, each bound voice suddenly *nearer*, as if the walls between them and the world were thinning. Down through the floor, in the vault of amber light, hairline fractures spidered across the first jar. Then the second. The sound when they went was not glass breaking. It was more like an exhale — a long, long exhale, twenty years held and finally given back.

And the crown-thing screamed.

It screamed in a chorus of stolen throats. That was the horror of it and the justice of it, both at once: Aldric's mouth opened and out came the councilor who'd vanished in year six of the reign, and a kitchen girl whose disappearance had been ruled elopement, and voices older than anyone living could name, kings and vessels and vessels-of-vessels, all the throats it had borrowed and never returned, all of them screaming at once through one royal face as its holdings dissolved. The sound bounced off the ceiling and came down like hail.

"YOU CANNOT AFFORD THIS," it shrieked, in a voice that was almost, briefly, only Aldric's — the man surfacing through his own ruin. "EVERY VOICE YOU FREE IS A VOICE THAT DIES. THE GIRL. THE SISTER. SHE GOES OUT WITH THE REST. IS THAT YOUR JUSTICE, KEEPER? IS THAT YOUR—"

"Yes," Elodie said.

Between verses. Flat. Final.

Because Wren had told her. In the vault, in the dark, in a voice like a jar cracking: *freeing Aldric means freeing every voice, including mine, into death.* And Elodie had carried that knowledge up the stairs like a coal in her cupped hands, and she had not put it down, and she was not putting it down now. The crown thought it had found her price. It had found her price weeks ago, in a cellar, and she had already paid it: she had chosen the living child over the dead sister the moment she hid him behind the Gate, and everything since was just the arithmetic finishing itself.

What she hadn't expected — what cracked her open mid-verse, so that Tam had to carry the harmony alone for four full bars while she stood there wrecked and singing anyway — was the voice that rose through the floor to meet hers.

Wren's. Not the sketch. Not the sample. The real frayed edge of her, rising through the cracking vault, joining the chorus — and Wren had always sung a third above whatever you gave her, always, since they were girls, and she found the third above Elodie's line without hesitation, without searching, the way you find your own bed in the dark.

*Oh,* Elodie thought. *Oh, you're still in there. You're still you.*

*Always was, El,* the third above her sang. *Now stop gawking and sing. We're doing this together. Like everything.*

*Like nothing,* Elodie sang back, in words, in Common, in tears. *We never did anything together. I stayed. You went.*

*And look how well that went for both of us.* A laugh in the harmony, ragged and real. *Sing, you stubborn woman. Sing.*

She sang.

* * *

The court was waking in waves now — clusters of sleepwalkers dropping out of the trance, collapsing into their neighbors' arms, staring around the throne room at the golden veins of light and the screaming king and the shopkeeper from Hollow Street standing in the middle of it all with a child's hands in hers. Some of them fled. Some of them stayed. An old duchess near the wall picked herself up, arranged her ruined skirts with tremendous dignity, and planted herself between Elodie and the dais like a piece of furniture that had decided, late in life, to become a barricade.

The crown-thing was losing its inventory. Elodie could hear it — the chorus of stolen throats thinning as jar after jar exhaled its prisoner free. Each freed voice didn't vanish; it rose, joined the song for one shining bar, and then went wherever released things go, and the harmony grew simpler and cleaner and somehow vaster, the way a chord grows when the noise drops away.

The crown itself had begun to smoke.

Thin gray threads curled off the band of it, off the metal that was not entirely metal, and where the smoke touched the air it left shapes — brief, dissolving outlines of every face it had ever worn. Aldric's hands had come up to the crown of his own head and were pulling, actually pulling, the man spending the last of his fought-for strength on the oldest instinct there was: *get it off me.*

"Not yet," Elodie gasped, to him, to everyone. "Hold — we finish the song first — if you tear it loose before the unbinding completes it'll just—"

"I KNOW WHAT IT WILL DO," Aldric roared, in his own voice, gloriously his own, and then his knees buckled and he went down on the dais steps with his hands still knotted in the smoking crown, and the crown-thing's remaining voices screamed through him in a chord of pure fury—

—and the last jar, deep in the vault, cracked wide.

The song changed. Everyone felt it. A thousand voices became a thousand-and-one, and the one was Wren, fully risen now, no longer a third above but everywhere in the harmony at once, holding it up the way she'd once held up the roof beam of their father's shed single-handed while Elodie fetched the nails. Holding it so the others could let go.

*El.* Just her name, sung, in the space between verses. *When it's done — when he's free — the rite will try to complete itself anyway. It's built into the stones of this room. You'll have seconds. Maybe less.*

*I know.*

*You don't. Nobody does. That's why I'm telling you.* A breath in the music, which should not have been possible, and was. *The dais, El. You'll have to finish it on the dais, with your hands on the crown itself. I can shield the boy but I can't shield you. Nothing can shield you.*

Elodie looked at Tam. His eyes were closed, his whole small body a pipe the choir played through, tears cutting clean lines through the grime on his face. Alive. Still alive. Getting weaker with every freed voice, yes — the crown pulling at him across the shrinking distance — but alive, and hers, and staying that way.

She looked at the dais. At the smoking crown and the king kneeling under it like a man under a falling sky.

Twenty years ago she had sealed a door and walked away from the fight of her life, and she had told herself it was duty. It had been fear. She knew the difference now, knew it in her torn throat and her shaking knees, and she was done confusing the two forever.

"Almost," she sang to the choir, to Wren, to the boy. "One verse more. Then we finish it where it started."

The harmony gathered itself like a wave gathering over a seawall.

On the dais, the crown-thing — down to a handful of stolen throats, down to rust and rage — began, very quietly, to laugh.
