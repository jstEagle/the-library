## 18. Unbinding

*Elodie*

The throne room of Vessing had been built to make people small, and it worked on Elodie even now, even with the world ending. Vaulted ceilings lost in shadow. Banners of dead kings hanging like flayed skins. And the dais at the far end, black stone worn glossy by two centuries of knees.

Aldric sat on the throne with his hands on the armrests and his head perfectly still, and he was not in there anymore. She could see it from thirty paces—the way his chest rose and fell in a rhythm too slow for a living man, the way candlelight did not flicker in his eyes. The crown sat on his brow like it had grown there, iron-dark, older than any crown had a right to be, and it was *singing*. Not sound. A pressure behind the eyes, a tuning fork struck against bone.

Around the dais, the court slept upright. Lords and ladies and guardsmen by the dozen, swaying like kelp in a current, their faces slack and dreaming. They had walked here through half a city of open doors. Somewhere below them all, under the floor, the choir was answering.

"Elodie." Tam's hand found hers. His voice was a thread; the crown's song pulled at him like a hook through the soft part of the world, and she could feel him leaning toward the dais, could feel her grip on his fingers being the only anchor left. "It's so loud."

"I know." She knelt so they were eye to eye. Mud still in the creases of his knuckles from three days ago, from before. "Listen to me. Not the song. Me. When we start, you don't stop. Whatever happens, whatever you hear—especially what you hear—you don't stop until I do."

"What if I hear her again?"

Elodie's throat closed. She made herself answer anyway. "Then let her sing with you. That's what choirs are for."

Behind them, steel rang against stone. Ivo had put his back to the great doors, sword drawn, facing the corridor where the first of the sleepwalking guards were coming—not running, never running, walking with that terrible tide-slow certainty, dozens of them, more than dozens. Vale stood beside him with an inquisitor's baton she'd confessed, red-faced, to having never actually used.

"How long?" Ivo called, not turning around.

"As long as it takes," Elodie said.

"Right." A breath. "Do it, then."

She took Tam's hand properly now, palm to palm, the way Wren had once taken hers across a burning threshold, and she opened the door inside herself that she had nailed shut for twenty years.

Magic came through it like water through a broken dam. It had been waiting. Of course it had been waiting. It poured up out of the Hollow Gate's memory in her blood, out of every charm she'd sold as seasoning and hair ornaments, out of every night she'd pressed her ear to a brick wall and mourned—and she began to sing.

The First Tongue did not have words for *beginning*. It only had words for *unmaking*, because everything in it was old, and old things know that all doors swing both ways.

* * *

*Tam*

The song went into Tam like cold well-water, and the crown's song came back to meet it, and where they touched, the world went thin.

He had been made for this. He understood that now, standing on the steps of the dais with Elodie's voice beside him and his own voice rising to meet hers—he was a hinge, a hasp, a key cut in the shape of a child. The choir beneath the floor sang up through his bones, a thousand voices, and he knew each of them the way you know your own heartbeat: vessels, all of them, grown in nurseries and spent in rites, generation after generation, kept in jars of amber light when they were done.

*Sing,* they said. *We have waited so long for someone to sing this.*

And one voice, one voice warmer than all the rest, wound close around him like a hand between his shoulder blades, and said: *I've got you, love. Don't look down at the dark parts. Look at her.*

Tam looked at Elodie. Her eyes were shut, her face wet, her voice steady as stone. He sang louder.

The crown noticed them then. He felt its attention turn like a lighthouse beam, felt the pressure double, triple, a fist closing around his heart trying to squeeze the song out of him and pour its own in instead. *MINE,* it said, in a voice made of every mouth it had ever worn. *YOU WERE MADE TO BE WORN.*

"No," Tam said, in Common, in First Tongue, in the plain stubborn voice of a boy who had run away through a storm rather than be a door for something else. "I was made to be *me*."

The choir took up the word. A thousand throats beneath the floor: *no, no, no,* and the amber light of the jars below began to crack like river ice in spring.

* * *

*Aldric*

Deep down in the drowned cellar of himself, Aldric heard the singing, and remembered his own name.

That was the thing about possession that no ballad got right—it wasn't darkness, it wasn't pain, it was *waiting*. Twenty years of sitting in a single chair at the back of his own mind while something else wore his hands to sign death warrants and wore his mouth to say *the law is the law* and wore his face to smile at crowds who loved a king who wasn't there. He had screamed, at first. He had begged. Then he had gone quiet, because quiet was the only mercy left, and then he had forgotten, mostly, that there had ever been anything to be quiet about.

But the singing. The singing came through the flood like sunlight through deep water.

*Elodie Whitlock,* he thought, wonderingly. *The shopkeeper. The keeper.* He had seen her once, years ago, in a vision the crown had punished him for—a woman in a doorway with salt on her sill—and now she stood at the foot of his throne with a child's hand in hers, unmaking twenty years of his silence note by note.

The crown felt it too. He felt it *flinch*—felt the great iron weight of its attention shift off him for one moment, one single moment, to bear down on the woman and the boy.

One moment was enough.

Aldric put everything he had left—every scrap of self he had hoarded like a starving man hoards bread—into one motion. His hand rose. His own hand, his flesh, moving to *his* will for the first time in twenty years, trembling like a sapling, and closed on the crown.

It burned. Of course it burned. It screamed through his skull in a hundred stolen voices—*TRAITOR, VESSEL, YOU ARE NOTHING WITHOUT US*—and it was right, he was nothing, he was less than nothing, he was a hollowed king on a stolen throne, and he pulled anyway.

"You are," Aldric said aloud, and his voice cracked down the middle like an old bell, "not *my* crown."

* * *

*Elodie*

She saw the king's hand rise and thought her heart would break with the sheer ordinary courage of it—one man, one hand, against twenty years of iron—and she pushed the song harder.

The unbinding had a shape. Wren's notebooks had drawn it in the margins, over and over, a spiral of syllables tightening toward a center, and Elodie had thought the center was the crown. She had been wrong. She understood it now, halfway up the dais steps with Tam's hand crushing hers: the spiral tightened inward forever, past the crown, past the choir, past every binding ever laid in Vessing's name, and at the bottom of it was the oldest word, the word she'd found scratched into the Gate's own frame in a hand three thousand years dead.

*Release.*

Not a command. A permission. That was the terrible secret at the heart of the First Tongue—all magic was, had always been, something held. Something that wanted to go home.

She gave it permission. All of it. The wards on her shop, the seal on the Gate, the knot of grief she'd tied around her own heart since the Purge—she opened her hands on all of it and sang *release* until the word stopped being a word and became simply the truth, spoken aloud, in a room full of sleeping people who were about to wake up free.

The crown caught fire.

Not flame—something older. Rust bloomed across it like time-lapse decay, centuries eating it in seconds, and beneath the rust, teeth. Small yellow teeth set in rows along the iron band, grinding, shrieking, and the shriek came out of the crown and out of the sleepwalking court and out of the walls themselves, a chorus of stolen throats crying out as every binding in the palace began, at last, to slip.

"LORD COVERLY!" Vale's voice, ragged, from the doors. "They're waking—they're waking *angry*—"

"Hold the line," Ivo roared back, and Elodie heard flat-of-blade strikes, heard bodies going down heavy but not dead, heard her friend fighting his own garrison with tears surely freezing on his face, because Ivo Coverly had never in his life learned how to do things halfway.

Tam swayed. She caught him. Up close she could see the toll the song was taking—his lips grey, his eyes glassy, the crown's death-hate pouring itself into the nearest vessel it had, which was him. The rust-and-teeth scream turned toward the boy like a drowning man grabbing at a rope, and Elodie felt the pull through their joined hands, felt her child being dragged toward the fire—

And then something stepped between them.

* * *

*Tam*

She was light. That was all Tam could understand, in the moment: not a ghost, not a shape, just warmth made visible, standing over him with her arms spread, and the crown's scream breaking against her like surf on a seawall.

*I've got you,* the warm voice said, and it was laughing and crying at once. *Twenty years I've waited to say it. I've got you, little one. You're doing beautifully.*

"You're Wren," Tam said. "You're *her* sister."

*I'm spending myself, is what I am, and glad of it.* The light dimmed as she spoke, deliberately, like a lamp turned low to make a room sleep-friendly, pouring itself into a shield around him. *Every drop I've saved in that jar, I'm spending on you. Don't waste tears on me. I've been screaming for twenty years and this is the first minute I've enjoyed.*

Through the shield, Tam saw Elodie on the step above him, and he saw her face as she finally looked straight at the light wearing her sister's voice, and it was the worst and best thing he had ever seen.

"Wren," Elodie whispered.

*Hello, Elodie.* Gentle. Unbearably gentle. *You sealed my gate and you kept my books and you raised a key to be a boy instead of a door. You did everything right, you maddening, stubborn, beloved woman. Now finish the song. Finish it, and let me go home.*

* * *

*Elodie*

There was no time to grieve. There would be no time to grieve for years, probably; she suspected grief had already filed a claim on her entire remaining life. So she did what Wren asked. She finished the song.

The spiral closed.

At the center of it, King Aldric stood up out of the throne with both hands on the crown, tearing at it now with no elegance at all, no royal bearing, just a big grey-bearded man fighting a piece of metal with everything human in him, and the court was waking around the dais and Ivo and Vale were being pushed back step by step from the doors, and none of it mattered, none of it existed outside the spiral, and Elodie sang the final syllable with Wren's light blazing above Tam like a second sun—

—and the crown came free.

It flew upward, or seemed to. It hung above the throne for one impossible instant, whole, complete, rusted through and riddled with tiny screaming teeth, every bound voice it had ever swallowed visible in it now as sparks trapped in iron—and then the word *release* reached it, as it had reached everything, and the crown of Vessing came apart.

Rust first. Then iron. Then the teeth, dropping like hail onto the black dais stones. Then the sparks, rising, a thousand freed voices going up through the vaulted dark like embers from a bonfire, and among them—last to leave, lingering longest—one warm ember that paused above Tam's head, and above Elodie's, and said, in a voice that was almost only air:

*Goodbye, Elodie. Leave the door open.*

Then it was gone, and the silence afterward rang louder than all the singing had.

* * *

*Ivo*

Sergeant Ivo Coverly had fought in two border skirmishes and one bread riot, and he had never once heard a battlefield go quiet the way the throne room went quiet.

The sleepwalkers stood blinking in the wreckage of their trance, looking at their own hands, at each other, at the scuffed floor, with the dazed shame of people who have dreamed something enormous and cannot remember it. Someone started weeping. Someone else laughed. On the dais, King Aldric lay on his side breathing hard, bareheaded, smaller somehow, gripping the throne's leg like a man overboard holding a spar.

Ivo lowered his sword. Around him, forty of his fellow guardsmen did the same, in ones and twos, like a field of grain coming down.

"Well," said Inquisitor Maren Vale, sitting down abruptly on the bottom step of the dais, baton dangling from her fingers. "I suppose that's the laws gone, then."

"That's the laws gone," Ivo agreed.

Above them, the freed voices were still rising, up through the ceiling, up through the palace, up toward a dawn that hadn't come yet. He watched Elodie Whitlock climb the last steps of the dais and kneel beside the king—not bowing, kneeling, the way you kneel to anyone who's hurt—and he watched Tam follow her, small and grey-faced and alive, alive, alive.

Vale tipped her head back against the step and looked up at him. "Coverly," she said. "When they ask us tomorrow what happened here tonight. What are we going to say?"

Ivo looked at the scattered teeth, the rust-stain on the throne, the shopkeeper from Copper Lane holding a king's wrist between her thumb and forefinger like she'd done it a hundred times.

"The truth," he said. "God help us all. The actual truth."

* * *

*Elodie*

Dawn was maybe an hour off when they came out of the palace by the front doors, because after twenty years of sneaking, it felt right to walk out the way everyone else did.

The city was waking in the streets. Not sleepwalking this time—waking. People stood in doorways staring at their own hands, at small fires burning green in gutter-grates, at charms in windows that had been dead junk for two decades and were now, visibly, faintly glowing. Magic had come back to Vessing the way floodwater comes back to a dry riverbed: everywhere at once, indifferent to deed and law alike. Somewhere a bell was ringing, and it was ringing the First Tongue, three soft rising notes that everyone in the crowd could suddenly understand, and the notes meant *open*.

Tam's hand was cold in hers. He leaned against her side as they walked, spent, emptied out, carrying his inheritance at last the only way it could be carried—as memory instead of duty.

"Elodie," he said. His voice was nearly gone. "The jars. Underneath. Are they—"

"Empty," she said. "All of them. Everyone went home." She didn't say *or whatever home means, for voices like that*. She didn't say *one of them stayed long enough to save your life and mine*. Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, for all the tomorrows left to her, she would carry that unsaid thing.

At the top of Copper Lane, her shop stood exactly as they'd left it, except for the light. It was coming up from the cellar, gold and soft and utterly unrestrained, spilling out under the door and through the seams of the shutters and up through the chimney like a house on fire in the gentlest possible way.

The Hollow Gate stood open.

From the top of the stairs, Elodie could see it: not brick anymore, never brick again, a doorway full of slow golden weather, magic rolling off it in tides, and no seal on it anywhere, and no keeper needed, and no going back to anything that had come before.

Tam looked up at her. In the light from below, his eyes were the exact color of Wren's notebooks' ink.

"So," he said, in careful, halting, brand-new Common. "Are we staying?"

Elodie Whitlock stood at the top of the stairs with her whole life arranged in front of her—every road she'd refused, every year she'd buried, one open door and one small boy and one city learning, terrified and delighted, to belong to itself—and she realized she was smiling.

"Tam," she said, "go put the kettle on."
