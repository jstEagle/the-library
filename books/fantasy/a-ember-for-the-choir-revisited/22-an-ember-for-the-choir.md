## 22. An Ember for the Choir

The lamp sits in the window of the shop that sells everything now, and every night before I sleep I feed it.

That is the whole of the ritual. A pinch of ash from the tin box on the sill — Wren's ashes, what Elodie could not bring herself to scatter all at once and so never scattered at all — pressed into the ember's heart while I say their names. Not all of them. There were a thousand voices in the jars beneath the palace, and no one ever wrote down more than a few hundred names, because vessels are not people as far as ledgers are concerned. So I say the ones we know, and then I say *the rest*, which is a word in the First Tongue that means *all of you, whoever you were, I have not forgotten that you were*.

The ember takes it the way soil takes rain. It brightens by a hair's breadth, no more. It has done this every night for six years, and it has never once gone out.

Elodie says lamps like that don't exist. She says it the way she says most true things: quietly, while doing something else, so the truth doesn't feel like a weight being handed to you. *Lamps like that don't exist, Tam. You made it from nothing but grief and a handful of choir-ash and a word older than kingdoms.* Then she'd shrug, and go back to pricing charms honestly, which is its own kind of magic in Vessing.

But it burns. It has burned through storms and through the year the fever came up from the river districts and half the street coughed itself hollow. It burned the night soldiers came — real soldiers, crown soldiers, back when there was still a crown worth the name — and stood in our doorway with writs in their fists, and Elodie looked at them the way she looks at customers who try to haggle down a luck-knot below cost, and they left. The lamp had nothing to do with that. But I fed it anyway that night, harder than usual, both hands shaking, saying *the rest* over and over until my throat was raw, and in the morning the ember was white-hot and calm, and Elodie put her hand on my head and said nothing at all, which from her is a whole speech.

I am seventeen now. I was six when I walked out of the palace with the crown singing through my bones, and I remember almost nothing of that child except his fear, which I carry like a stone in a pocket I can't take off. Some nights I set the stone down. Most nights I don't. Elodie says that's allowed too.

* * *

On certain nights — and no one has ever been able to predict them, though Vale swears it correlates with the moon's perigee and Ivo swears it correlates with whether the fishmongers got good herring, and they argue about it every time like scholars defending rival theses — the First Tongue rises in the streets of Vessing.

It starts small. A woman hanging washing at dusk will hum something that isn't a tune, exactly; it's older than tunes. A boy skipping stones off the canal will shape a syllable under his breath that makes the water lie flat and glassy for a heartbeat. And then, somewhere between one lamplighting and the next, the whole city is doing it — not loudly, not all together, but everywhere, the way birdsong happens everywhere at once without anyone deciding it should. Words that predate kingdoms. Words that bound kings to thrones and unbound them. Words that cracked a thousand jars of amber light in an undercroft while a thing made of rust and teeth screamed in a chorus of stolen throats.

And no one arrests anyone for singing it.

I want you to understand what that sentence costs. I want you to hold it up to the light and see all the years inside it. When I was born — when I was *made*, rather, grown in a nursery above a choir pit from the Gate's own spilled magic — the First Tongue was a death sentence. People were taken for less. People were taken for humming. The Purge laws didn't distinguish between a hedge-witch and a woman selling salt rings labeled *seasoning*; the law only knew that magic was outlawed, and outlaw meant gone. Sergeant Ivo Coverly spent eleven years of his life knocking on doors to make sure of it, and he does not talk about those years except once a year, on the anniversary of the unbinding, when he comes to the shop, drinks one cup of Elodie's terrible spiced wine, and says, "To the ones who knocked." We don't ask who he means. We know. He means everyone.

Now a grandmother teaches her granddaughter a binding-word to keep the cellar dry, and the girl writes it in chalk on the wall where anyone can see, and the worst that happens is a neighbor complains about the chalk.

* * *

Elodie locks nothing.

This took me years to stop flinching at. In the palace, everything locked. Doors locked, cupboards locked, the nursery door locked from the *outside*. Locks were how they told us what we were: things to be kept. When I first came to the shop — mud-streaked, mute, speaking a language that made her wards flare like struck bells — I watched her close up at night and waited for the sound of bolts. There were none. She turned the sign, she banked the fire, she checked the Hollow Gate in the cellar out of twenty years of habit even after there was nothing left to seal it against, and then she went upstairs and slept behind a door with a latch so worn it drifted open on its own.

"The Gate's open forever," I said to her once, early on, in halting Common I was still learning to spend like coins. "Why do you check it?"

"Because checking isn't sealing," she said. "Checking is remembering. There's a difference, and it took me twenty years and my sister to learn it."

She checks it still. Every night. Not because anything might come through — things come through constantly now, soft light and wild magic spilling up the cellar stairs like tide — but because the ritual belongs to her, the way feeding the lamp belongs to me. We are both keepers of habits that outlived their purpose and became something else. Prayer, maybe. Or just love, given a schedule.

The shop itself stands open most days. Charms, real ones, priced honestly, says the sign she painted herself, and beneath it in smaller letters: *if you can't pay, sweep the step.* Half of Vessing has swept our step at one time or another. The broom handle is worn smooth as driftwood. Neighbors come in with small magics of their own now — a man who can warm bread with his palms, twins who speak to moths, an old soldier whose scars ache in weather and who discovered last spring that if he hums, they ache in someone else instead, which he finds hilarious and his wife finds alarming — and they trade them across our counter like recipes. Elodie takes a percentage of nothing. She says the shop stopped being a shop the night the Gate opened and became something more like a well, and you don't charge people to drink from a well.

She is grayer now. Slower on the stairs. Her hands still move like a young woman's when they're working — knotting, grinding, writing binding-craft in the margins of Wren's notebooks, which she has copied out three times now against damp and mice and loss. Sometimes I catch her standing in the doorway between shop and stair, looking at nothing, wearing the expression she wore the night she refused the crown-thing's bargain. I know that face. It's the face of a woman counting what a choice cost, long after the choosing, and finding the sum acceptable. She does it less every year. That's what peace is, maybe. Not the absence of the counting. Just the sums coming out gentler.

* * *

Tonight is one of the nights.

I knew it before I reached the bottom of the stairs, the way you know rain is coming in your knees. The air over Vessing had that particular pressure, that held-breath quality, and when I opened the shutters the sounds came up off the streets already halfway risen: a washerwoman's hum two houses down, a syllable from the canal path that flattened the water, somewhere farther the deep bell-note of a word I recognized as *release* — the oldest word, the one I sang on the throne dais with a thousand voices pouring through my chest like light through a cracked jar — being practiced, gently, badly, by someone learning it from their grandmother in a kitchen that smelled of onions.

No one arrests anyone. The watch walks those streets tonight the way they walk every street, and Ivo Coverly — retired now, stripes kept, conscience intact, both achievements rarer than they sound — walks with them sometimes just to be seen walking, gray and broad and unhurried, nodding at singers. Once, years ago, a young watchman asked him if he wasn't going to report the singing. Ivo looked at the boy for a long moment and said, "Son, I've reported worse for better reasons," and walked on, and the boy thought about it, and that was that. That's how laws die, mostly. Not with trumpets. With old men declining to repeat them.

I feed the lamp.

Ash from the tin, pressed into the ember's heart, and the names: Aldric, who abdicated rather than rule on a lie and lives now in a cottage by the eastern wall where he grows beans and answers children's questions about the old days with a honesty that would have gotten him executed twice over; Maren Vale, who dismantled the inquisition's files page by page, burning some and donating others to the new archive where any citizen can read what was done in the crown's name, because she says memory without access is just another kind of lock; Ivo; Wren; and then *the rest* — all of you, whoever you were, I have not forgotten that you were.

The ember brightens. White-gold, steady, patient. Six years and it has never asked me for more than this.

Through the window, the First Tongue rises like birdsong. A dozen voices, then more, weaving in and out, none of them trained, all of them free. Somewhere a child is singing *release* wrong and laughing about it. The words drift up past the shutters and into the room, and the lamp leans toward them the way flowers lean toward sun, and I lean too, because I was made from this language, grown out of the Gate's own magic into a body that was supposed to be a key and became, instead, a boy, and then an apprentice, and then — Elodie's word, spoken once, never repeated, never needing to be — family.

Footsteps on the stairs. Elodie, in her nightdress with a shawl over her shoulders, drawn down by the sound the way she always is on these nights, unable to sleep through it, unwilling to try.

We stand together at the window. Below us Vessing sings in a tongue older than its own walls, lawless and legal-by-abandonment and beautiful, and the ember-lamp burns for the choir's freed voices, and the door downstairs stands unlocked, and the Gate beneath the floor stands open forever, spilling its soft wild light up through the boards like a second dawn that never quite finishes rising.

"They're getting louder," Elodie says.

"They're getting braver," I say. "Louder comes later."

She smiles. Outside, the song swells — a hundred voices now, maybe more, rising off the cobbles like heat off summer stone — and in the tin box on the sill, Wren's ashes seem almost to stir, and the lamp flares once, bright as a struck bell, bright as a gate opening, bright as a door that will never again be closed —

— and far down the street, at the edge of the lamplight, a figure stops and lifts its head to listen, and I know the posture, I have known it since I was six years old and running, the posture of someone hearing the First Tongue for the very first time, and the figure turns toward our shop, toward the open door, toward the light, and begins to walk.
