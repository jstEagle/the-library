## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The grave was shallow because the ground was old and stubborn, and Gideon Quintana dug it the way she dug all of them now — alone, with a spade whose handle she had wrapped in leather three winters past so her palms would not blister.

Elder Maren had been ninety-one years old, which was a good age anywhere but an extraordinary one here, where the valley kept its people like a miser keeps coins: carefully, and for a very long time. She had died in her sleep on the third day of the waning moon, with her hands folded over her breast the way the old women taught their daughters to fold them, so that when the ancestors came asking, they would find nothing hidden.

*Nothing hidden,* Gideon thought, leaning on the spade and looking at the small mound of turned earth. *That's a joke, Maren. You hid plenty. You all did.*

She said it aloud, too, because the valley was empty enough that speaking to the dead no longer felt strange. It had felt strange once, thirty years ago, when there were still enough living voices to fill the market square on Oathfire eve, when the ovens ran from dawn to dusk and children were shooed away from the kindling piles and the whole bowl of the valley smelled of resin and roasting chestnuts and the particular smoke of cedar burned in blessing. Now her voice went up the terraced slopes and came back to her off the border stones, flattened by distance into something that sounded less like grief than like weather.

"You hid plenty," she told the grave. "And I'll hide it with you. That's what I'm for."

She laid Maren's body in herself — no one else left strong enough to lift, and no one else at all — and arranged the folded hands, and tucked the oath-knot of braided horsehair beneath them where it could not be seen. Then she sat back on her heels in the cold grass and let herself have one minute, exactly one, of being simply tired. She counted it out the way her grandmother had taught her to count breaths before a duel: one for the earth, one for the sky, one for the road between them. On the fourth breath she stood, and filled the grave, and set the marker stone upright with both hands.

The stone was plain river granite. Every marker in the little yard above the orchard was plain river granite, uncarved, because carving names was a thing you did when you believed the name would survive you. The valley's people had stopped believing that a long time ago. They had gone underground with their names the way roots go under with water — silently, out of sight, feeding something no one above could see.

Gideon pressed her palm flat against the cool stone.

"Oathfire tomorrow," she said. "I'll light it for you. For all of you. Same as always."

The wind moved through the apple trees, bare-limbed and patient, and said nothing, which was how the valley answered everything.

* * *

The wards took the rest of the afternoon, as they took every afternoon, and Gideon walked the circuit with her sleeves rolled despite the bite in the air, because the work wanted skin against stone.

There were nine ward-stones in the valley, one for each of the founding hearths, though only four of the founding hearths still had houses beside them and only two of those houses had anyone living in them. The stones were older than the houses, older than the terraces, older than any story anyone could tell without stumbling. They were waist-high pillars of black glass-flecked basalt, and they hummed — not a sound, exactly, but a feeling in the teeth, in the bones behind the ears, like standing near a struck bell that has almost finished ringing.

Except that lately, the ringing was thinner.

Gideon noticed it first at the Third Hearth stone, the one down by the millrace, where the water talked constantly and covered other sounds. She laid her hand against it the way she had every evening for forty years, since she was seven years old and her grandmother had lifted her up on her hips and said, *Feel that? That's the valley breathing. Your job, someday, is to keep your ear against its chest.* And the hum was there, but it was like a fire burned down to coals: present, banked, and needing more tending than it used to.

She sang to it. That was the tending — not magic, not really, or not the kind the stories meant. The swordspeakers' songs were half prayer and half maintenance, the way a well-loved boat is half vessel and half memory of every hand that ever oiled it. She sang the waking verse, and the keeping verse, and the verse that named the stone's hearth-line even though the hearth-line's last daughter had died eleven years ago and Gideon had buried her too, here in this same stubborn ground.

The stone warmed faintly under her palm. The hum steadied.

"Good," she murmured. "Hold."

But walking on toward the Fourth, she caught herself doing arithmetic she hated. Nine stones. One singer. Forty years ago there had been six singers, and the circuit had taken two hours and ended in supper and argument. Twenty years ago, three singers, and the circuit took four hours and ended in silence. Now it took the whole day, and it ended wherever it ended, usually at the Ninth stone as the light failed, and Gideon would stand there with her voice worn to a thread and think: *when I am gone, who sings the waking verse?*

And then, because she was her grandmother's granddaughter and had inherited the family talent for looking directly at terrible things: *and after that, how long does a valley breathe with no one listening to its chest?*

The Choir had not come for the valley. That was the strange mercy at the center of her whole life, the fact she had built everything else on top of like a house on a single post. Thirty years ago the world beyond the border stones had done whatever it did — she had been young, and the elders had closed ranks, and what she knew was mostly what she'd pieced together from silences: that a great order far away had passed some judgment, signed some document, and that afterward the maps sold in the lowland towns showed no valley here at all. Just blank space, politely shaded, labeled with nothing. The traders stopped coming. The letters stopped coming. The road grew over.

But the wards had held. Whatever the Choir's power was, it could not follow them past the border stones, and the elders had understood why in ways Gideon never fully had. *Magic cannot cross,* Elder Maren used to say, tapping her own temple with one crooked finger. *Not theirs, not ours, not anything. This valley is a held breath, girl. We live inside it. That is our whole country now — one held breath.*

One held breath, kept alive by one tired woman with a spade and a songbook.

Gideon reached the Fourth Hearth stone as the sun went copper behind the western ridge, and sang to it, and felt it answer, and tried not to count again. She failed. She always failed. Counting was the swordspeaker's oldest discipline — counting breaths, counting paces, counting the enemy's blades — and she had turned it, over the years, into a way of measuring loss. Four stones tended. Five remaining. One elder buried today. Zero left alive besides herself.

Zero was a number she was going to have to get used to saying.

* * *

Night came down early this time of year, sliding blue into the valley bottom while the peaks still held the light, and Gideon made her solitary festival-eve meal in the kitchen of the First Hearth house — her house, the one she'd been born in, with its great blackened hearth big enough to roast a whole lamb and its mantel crowded with things that had outlived their owners: a clay whistle shaped like a lark, a wooden doll with one eye, a brass bell that had not rung in thirty years because its clapper had been removed and buried with the woman who owned it, per custom, so that her voice would go with her.

She ate beans and dark bread and a spoonful of the last of last summer's honey, and she laid out the festival things on the table with the care of a priest arranging relics, because that was what she was now, if she was honest: priest, gravedigger, warden, last witness. A bundle of cedar kindling, cut and dried. A twist of resin-soaked flax for the tinder. The fire-bowl itself, dished bronze, older than the stones, its rim worn bright by generations of thumbs.

And the sword.

It lay on its cloth at the table's end, and she looked at it the way you look at a sleeping relative you love and slightly fear. The oath-sword of the Quintana line. Not a beautiful weapon — the valley had never wasted beauty on tools — but an honest one, a straight blade of grey metal with a grip wrapped in the same braided horsehair as the grave-knots, and along the flat, in the old script that only swordspeakers learned to read, a single line of words she had known since before she could read anything else:

*I speak, therefore the valley stands.*

It had not spoken back in thirty years. Swordspeakers' blades woke for oaths sworn in truth before witnesses, and there had been no oaths and no witnesses for a generation; the blade slept the way the stones hummed thin, the way the valley breathed shallowly, everything in the country running on the fumes of what it used to be. Some nights Gideon dreamed the sword spoke — a voice like her grandmother's, like iron cooling — and woke with her hand already reaching for the hilt, and found it cold, and lay staring at the rafters until dawn.

Tomorrow night she would carry it to the Oathfire. She would light the fire with the bronze bowl and the cedar and the resin-flax, and she would recite the festival litany alone, playing every part, switching voices for the call and the response the way she'd done for fifteen years, and the fire would burn down through the night, and in the morning she would rake the ashes and save one ember in a clay pot, because that was the law: *an ember for the choir of the dead, that they may warm themselves at our remembering.* An ember, kept year to year, from fire to fire, going back further than any ledger.

She was wrapping the ember-pot in wool against tomorrow's damp when she heard the dog.

Not a real dog — there hadn't been dogs in the valley for twenty years — but the memory of one, the ghost of a bark that lived in her own head, the reflex every valley child was trained with: *one bark, stranger; two barks, trouble; three barks, run.* Her body heard the border stones shift in the wind and translated it, absurdly, faithfully, into warning. She stood very still in her kitchen with the ember-pot in her hands.

Then she heard it properly: not wind. Footsteps on the old trade road, coming down from the pass. Slow ones. Careful ones. The steps of someone who did not know the road and was afraid of it.

Gideon set down the pot. She took the oath-sword from its cloth — not drawn, never drawn for a stranger, that was the first law — and buckled it at her hip, and took the storm lantern down from its hook, and lit it, and went out into the dark.

* * *

The border stones stood at the valley's mouth where the river left for the lowlands, nine of them in a broken crescent, black and glass-flecked like their hearth-kin, and beyond them the world simply stopped meaning anything. Gideon had crossed the crescent twice in her life, both times as a girl, both times holding her mother's hand, and both times the air on the far side had felt wrong in a way she could never describe — thinner, louder, full of a pressure like the moment before a sneeze. Magic could not cross the stones. Neither, the elders said, should anyone else, if they valued the shape of their soul.

She stopped ten paces short of the crescent and raised her lantern.

A figure stood just outside the outermost stone, at the exact margin where the valley's held breath ended. Small. Hooded against the cold. And in one gloved hand, held high, a lantern of a make Gideon had never seen: squat, brass-caged, burning with a flame that gave off almost no warmth-colored light at all — a pale, clean, white light, the light of offices and documents, of rooms where decisions got made about other people.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The river talked. Somewhere up the slope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

Then the stranger lifted her free hand — slowly, the way you move around a frightened animal — and pushed back her hood, and Gideon saw a young woman's face, sharp-boned and exhausted, chapped red with cold, with the look of someone who has been walking for days past the point where walking stops making sense.

"I know you can't come in," the stranger called, and her voice carried strangely across the stones, arriving flat and close as if the distance had been subtracted somewhere in transit. "I've read about it. The wards. I know what happens if I try." She swallowed, and Gideon watched her gather something up from the bottom of herself. "My name is Sefa. I was a clerk of the Choir. Third Registry, Census Division." She held up, in her lantern-light, a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and bound with cord — thick, heavy, the size of a ledger, the size of several ledgers. "And I've brought you proof that your valley exists. Whether they want it to or not."

The word *Choir* went through Gideon like a splinter finding old wood. Thirty years of buried silences shifted around it. Her hand, without any instruction from her, settled on the sleeping sword's hilt.

"You're standing at my border," Gideon said, "at sundown, on Oathfire eve, carrying stolen paper, and you want me to believe you're here to help me?"

"No," the stranger said honestly. "I want you to believe me before it's too late to matter whether you do. The wards are failing everywhere. Not just yours. All of them, all across—" She stopped. Gideon watched her decide something, watched her jaw set. "Listen. I'll show you. I'll read you the numbers. But you should know—"

And that was when the star went out.

It happened mid-sentence, overhead, in the clear cold gap between clouds: a star that had hung in that exact place every autumn night of Gideon's life — the Shepherd's Eye, her grandmother had called it, the one that watched the flocks — simply, quietly, ceased. No flicker. No fall. One instant it was there, ancient and indifferent, and the next the sky had a hole in it, smooth-edged, absolute, like a word struck from a page.

Sefa's voice stopped too. She was staring upward, and her face in the white lantern-light had gone the color of paper.

"That's not possible," she whispered. "They said we had until spring."

Behind Gideon, all along the crescent, the nine border stones began — for the first time in her lifetime — to ring.
