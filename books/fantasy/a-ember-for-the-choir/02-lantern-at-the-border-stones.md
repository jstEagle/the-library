## 2. Lantern at the Border Stones

The lantern appeared at the border stones an hour past midnight, and Gideon saw it from the burying ground before she saw anything else.

She had been sitting with the dead, as one did. The Oathfire festival was tomorrow — or today, now, if one counted honestly — and tradition demanded that the last night before the lighting be spent in the company of the ancestors, telling them the news of the year so that they might be included in the celebration. It was a gentle custom. It had been a gentle custom when there were forty voices in the burying ground, and then thirty, and then twelve, and now it was Gideon's voice alone, hoarse by the third hour, reciting the year's news to stones that did not answer.

*This year the barley came in thin. This year the well at Marrow's Crossing silted and I dug it out myself, which my back resented. This year I buried Mother Ansa in the east row, beside her sister, and I did the rites correctly, every word, though there was no one to check me.*

She had said that last part every year for eleven years now. *No one to check me.* It was not self-pity, or she had trained herself not to let it be. It was inventory. A swordspeaker kept accounts. That was the first lesson of the oath: *a blade is a ledger of what you are willing to lose.* She had been keeping the ledger of her people alone for so long that the arithmetic had worn smooth as a river stone.

The lantern was wrong for the valley. That was the first thing she noticed, before its color, before its movement. It was wrong the way a wrong note in a lullaby is wrong — instantly, bodily, before the mind could name the reason. The valley's lanterns were oil and wick and hand-blown glass, made in the valley, burned in the valley, their light the color of honey held up to the sun. This light was white. Hard white, the white of paper under a clerk's lamp, and it moved with a purposefulness no valley lantern had ever possessed, because valley lanterns were carried by valley people who knew every stone and had nowhere in the world to be but here.

Gideon rose from the burying ground with her knees cracking like green wood and went to the shed at the edge of her yard, where her grandfather's oath-sword hung on pegs beneath a cloth she washed twice a year and otherwise did not touch.

She did not take it down. That mattered, later. She stood before it for the length of three breaths, the way one stands before a sleeping animal one does not wish to wake, and then she took down the plain work-sword beside it instead, the one she used for brush-clearing and the rare, regrettable snake. The oath-sword had not spoken in thirty years. The valley was magic-dead; that was the whole point of the valley; the wards drank every wandering power that touched the border stones and gave back silence. A swordspeaker's sword was a thing of spoken power, and spoken power did not live here. It hung on its pegs like a word in a language everyone had agreed to forget.

She took the work-sword and walked up through the barley terraces toward the border stones in the dark, and the dark was total, because the stars — she glanced up out of habit — the stars were all in their places. All of them. She counted without meaning to, the way her grandmother had taught her: the Plough, the Sisters, the Red Eye of the Hunter, the long bright river of the Sky-Road with its thousand small coins. *The sky is the first ledger,* Grandmother Vey used to say, tapping Gideon's forehead and then pointing up. *Everything written there stays written. That is why we swear by it. That is why we are careful what we swear.*

The lantern stopped at the third border stone, the one with the oath-knot carved into its flank, and Gideon stopped twenty paces short of it, in the shadow of the last terrace wall, and watched.

The person holding the lantern was small. Young, or young-ish. They wore a traveling coat of good wool gone bad at the hems, and they were standing with the particular stillness of someone who has walked a very long way and is afraid to sit down. The white lantern hung from their hand and lit their face from below, which made them look like something in a puppet show: sharp chin, dark circles under dark eyes, a smudge of ink on one cheekbone that they had clearly tried to scrub and failed.

They were looking at the oath-knot. Their lips were moving. Reading it, Gideon realized. The knot-carvings were not words, exactly — they were older than the valley's written script, a grammar of interlocking lines that meant *boundary, blessing, boundary, remember* — but they could be read, slowly, by someone who had been taught, and this stranger was reading them the way a hungry person reads a menu.

Gideon stepped out of the shadow. "That's a long way from anywhere," she said. "That lantern of yours."

The stranger did not scream, which Gideon gave them credit for. They did drop the lantern, and caught it, badly, and the white light swung wild across the barley and the stones and Gideon's face, and then they stood very still and looked at her with the expression of a person doing rapid, desperate arithmetic.

"You're —" the stranger began, and stopped. Started again. "You're the ward-keeper."

"I'm the valley," Gideon said. "The ward-keeper, the gravedigger, the well-mender, and the only voice at the Oathfire tomorrow, if you're planning to attend. You're not from here."

"No."

"No one is, anymore." She let the work-sword hang loose at her side, visible, unthreatening, a fact rather than a promise. "The border stones are the border. You're standing on the outside of it, which is the correct side, and the road back the way you came is four days to the ferry at Sorrel Ford. I'd start walking."

The stranger's chin came up. It was a small defiance, a tired defiance, but Gideon recognized the architecture of it, because she built the same thing inside herself every morning. "My name is Sefa," the stranger said. "I was a clerk of the Choir. Third register, census division. And I need to tell you that your wards are dying."

The words went into Gideon like a splinter: too small to matter, too deep to dig out. She kept her face still. "The wards are older than your Choir," she said. "The wards were old when the Choir was a quarrel in a seminary. The wards have outlived every clerk who ever promised they'd fail."

"They're not failing because they're old." Sefa — she planted her feet, and her voice found a clerk's cadence, the rhythm of a person reading from a document they had memorized under duress — "they're failing because they're being *unmade*. Deliberately. Systematically. There's a program. It has a name and a budget line and a schedule. The Choir calls it Harmonic Consolidation. It started in the western provinces eleven years ago. The wards there went quiet one by one, and the Choir's records say the quiet was natural decay, and the Choir's records are —" she stopped, and Gideon watched her decide something — "the Choir's records are the reason I stole three ledgers and walked four hundred miles, so let me say it plainly: the records are lies. The wards are being dissolved on purpose. And yours are next."

The night was very quiet. The valley's quiet was famous, or had been, once, among the people who remembered there was a valley: a magic-dead silence so complete that exiled mages had paid in gold just to sleep one night inside it, to feel their heads go still. Gideon had lived inside that silence her whole life. She knew its textures the way a blind woman knows the surfaces of her own house. And so she heard it, underneath Sefa's words, the way you hear a wrong note in a familiar song.

The silence had a seam in it.

It was at the border stones. It had always been at the border stones — the wards drank loudest there, if drinking could be silent, if silence could have a flavor. But the flavor was different tonight. Thinner. Like water from a well that is not yet dry but has begun to think about it.

"You're wrong," Gideon said, and heard how flat her voice came out. "Or you're lying. Or you're a test. The Choir sends people sometimes. It's been twenty years since the last one, a polite man with a survey instrument, and he stood exactly where you're standing and asked exactly the right questions, and I sent him away, and the wards held. So." She shifted her grip on the work-sword. "Say your piece, clerk. But know that I have buried everyone who ever taught me to believe a stranger, and the burying ground is full, and I am tired."

Something moved behind Sefa's eyes — not fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone who has just been told *I am tired* by the one person in the world whose tiredness is the entire subject of their journey.

"Then I'll be quick," Sefa said. She crouched, set the lantern on the ground between herself and the border stone, and opened the satchel at her hip. What she drew out was a book. Not a valley book — the valley's books were hand-sewn, inked on bark-paper, soft-cornered with use. This was a Choir ledger: board-bound, brass-cornered, stamped with the Choir's seal, which was a circle of small figures singing, rendered in blind-emboss so that it showed only when the light crossed it. The light crossed it now. The little singers caught the lantern's white glow and seemed, for a moment, to move.

"Census division, third register," Sefa said, and her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they are carrying something breakable. "The Choir keeps a census of everything, did you know that? Not people — well, people, but also everything. Wells. Wards. Words. There is a ledger of proverbs. There is a ledger of lullabies, cross-referenced by province, with notations for which verses were *retired*." She said *retired* the way one says a curse in a language the children won't understand. "And there is a ledger of nations. The Register of Polities. Every nation the Choir has ever recognized, and every nation it has ever —" her thumb moved along the ledger's edge, finding its place by touch — "processed."

"Processed," Gideon repeated.

"Dissolved," Sefa said. "That's the word they use in the margin notes. Dissolved, consolidated, harmonized. It's always administrative language. That's the trick of it, I think, now, looking back. If you call a thing a filing action, no one asks to see the body." She opened the ledger. "Your valley is in here."

The night tilted, very slightly, the way a floor tilts when a ship has begun, invisibly, to move.

"Say that again," Gideon said.

"Your valley. Your —" Sefa hesitated over the name. Gideon watched her hesitate. Watched the clerk's instinct to pronounce it correctly war with the clerk's training, which said that some names were not to be pronounced at all. "Your country," Sefa finished. "It's in the Register of Polities. It has been for three hundred years. And eleven years ago it was moved."

"Moved."

"To a different section." Sefa turned the ledger around and held it out into the lantern light, and Gideon did not step forward, and the two women stood in the barley-shadow with a book open between them like a wound. "I memorized the entry," Sefa said quietly. "I memorized a lot of entries. It's what I was good at. Status: dissolved. Method: administrative consolidation, non-contentious. Assets: absorbed. Population: reclassified. And there's a note in the margin in red — red is the First Cantor's own ink — that says *no residual presence anticipated.*"

"No residual presence," Gideon said.

"That's you," Sefa said. "You're the residual presence. You and the wards. The wards that were supposed to have decayed decades ago, except they can't, because whoever built them built them out of something the Choir's dissolution rites don't eat, and so every eleven years the Choir sends a surveyor, and every eleven years the surveyor writes *residual presence, non-viable, monitor*, and every eleven years the budget line for Harmonic Consolidation moves a little further east." She closed the ledger. The little embossed singers vanished. "I've seen the schedule, ward-keeper. Your valley is the last entry in the western register. It's scheduled for next spring. And when it goes, the silence goes with it, and everything the silence has been holding back —"

"You don't know what you're talking about." Gideon heard her own voice from a great distance, flat and hard as the border stones. "The silence isn't holding anything back. The silence is the wards. The wards drink what touches them. That's all. That's the whole of it. There's nothing behind the silence but valley."

"Then why," Sefa said, very gently, in the voice of a clerk who has learned that gentleness gets further than volume, "did the Choir build the dissolution schedule around it? Why is the last and hardest entry in the register a valley of two hundred farmers? Why did the First Cantor write *no residual presence anticipated* in red ink, with the word *anticipated* underlined twice?" She picked up her lantern. "I don't know what's behind your silence. But the Choir does. And they're afraid of it. And they're coming."

"They are not coming," Gideon said. "The valley is magic-dead. Their rites don't work here. Their — whatever they are, their singing, their dissolving, their paperwork — none of it crosses the stones. That is the entire reason my people came to this valley. That is the entire reason there is a valley. You've walked four hundred miles to tell me my house is on fire in a country where fire doesn't burn."

Sefa looked at her for a long moment. The white lantern light made her face very young and very old at once.

"I walked four hundred miles," she said, "because I processed the schedule. My hands moved the entries from one ledger to another. I have good handwriting. That was my job, for six years: good handwriting, moving the names of things that were about to stop existing from the column of *is* into the column of *was*." Her voice did not break. It did something worse; it went precise. "So when I tell you they're coming, I am not guessing. I am reading my own handwriting."

And Gideon — who had spent the evening telling the year's news to stones, who had said *no one to check me* into the dark over the graves of everyone who had ever checked her — felt something in her chest do a thing she did not have a valley word for. Not belief. Not yet. Something smaller and more stubborn: the refusal to believe, hardening into its own kind of architecture.

"The Oathfire is tomorrow," she said. "Tonight is for the dead. You can sleep in the barn if you can find it, and in the morning you can explain to me, slowly, with the ledgers open, exactly what a Harmonic Consolidation does to a valley, and I will tell you all the ways you're wrong. And then you'll leave."

"Thank you," Sefa said, and meant it, and that was somehow the most frightening thing she had said all night.

Gideon turned back toward the terraces. And because she was a ward-keeper, and because the seam in the silence had been nagging at her like a tooth, and because her grandmother had taught her that the sky was the first ledger and everything written there stays written — she looked up.

She looked up out of habit. She looked up the way you check a lock you have checked ten thousand times, not because you doubt it but because the checking is the ritual, and the ritual is the wall.

The Red Eye of the Hunter was gone.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. Not behind the ridge of the eastern hills, which it could not have been, which it had never once been in the remembered history of a valley that had kept sky-records in bark-paper for three hundred years. It was simply not there. A hole in the Sky-Road's bright river, a missing tooth in a mouth that had never had a gap, a circle of darkness exactly the size of a star, ringed all around by the other stars burning on, oblivious, unbroken.

Gideon stopped walking.

"Ward-keeper?" Sefa's voice, behind her. Then a breath. Then, very small: "Oh."

The valley's silence, the famous silence, the silence Gideon had lived inside like a fish lives in water, seemed for one long moment to lean against her eardrums. And in that silence she heard — or did not hear, or felt in the teeth behind her ears — the border stones at her back make a sound. Not a crack. Not a word. A sort of settling, the sound a great house makes at night when something in its foundations shifts by the width of a hair.

The stars were going out. One of them had already gone. And the wards were older than the Choir, older than the quarrel in the seminary, older than the bark-paper sky-records — but they were not, Gideon understood now, standing in the barley with a stranger's white lantern guttering behind her, older than the sky. Whatever the wards drank from, whatever fed them, whatever the founders had knotted into the border stones when they swore the valley into being —

it had been up there all along.

"Get your ledgers," Gideon said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, and cost her more than screaming would have. "All of them. And then come with me to the burying ground, clerk, because I think the dead are about to have a great deal to say."

Behind her, at the border stones, the silence tore — softly, like cloth, like a page being turned — and something old breathed into the valley for the first time in thirty years.
