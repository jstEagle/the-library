## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

Gideon had not slept. The lantern-light of the stranger still burned at the border stones, a small yellow lie in the blue dark, and Gideon stood in her doorway with her arms crossed against the cold, telling herself the sensible things. Strangers did not come to the valley. Strangers could not come to the valley. The wards had turned back tax collectors and missionaries and, once, a very confused wedding party from over the pass, and they would turn back one lantern-bearing girl with a satchel and a story about ledgers.

But the girl was still there. And she had said the word *dissolved* the way a doctor says a word like *tumor*, gently, with the terrible fluency of the professional.

Gideon banked the fire in her hearth, took down the oath-sword from its pegs — not because she meant anything by it, only because her hands wanted the weight — and went out into the last night of the world as it was.

The valley at night was a held breath. That was the truest thing about it, the thing the elders had tried to teach her and she had never properly learned until they were gone: magic did not fail here. Magic had been *refused*. The wards did not keep power out so much as they kept a promise — *nothing enters, nothing wakes* — and the valley lived inside that promise like a stone at the bottom of a well, dark and cool and safe. Gideon had spent sixty years in that coolness. She knew every silence in it: the silence of the barley, the silence of the shrine stones, the silence of the graves where she buried people faster than the valley made them.

She walked the ward-road with the sword on her back and the stranger's lantern in her eyes, and she counted the border stones the way her grandmother had taught her, touching each one as she passed. *One for the hearth. One for the field. One for the dead who stayed.* Forty-one stones, forty-one promises, and her palm on each one, and each one humming that low, almost-not-there note that meant the promise still held.

At the nineteenth stone, the hum changed key.

Gideon stopped. She pressed her palm flat. The note beneath the stone had gone thin — not weaker, exactly, but *thinner*, the way a rope goes thin just before it parts, all its strength drawn into a single straining line. She had felt this once before, the winter of the great flood, when the wards had bent nearly to breaking under the weight of the river's old angry spirit. The wards had held then. She had been thirty and full of certainty, and the wards had held.

She walked faster. The lantern at the border was closer now — the girl had left the road, was standing in the open grass between the last stones and the dark of the outer country, and she had turned to watch Gideon come with an expression Gideon would remember for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be: not triumph, not fear. *Apology.*

"I told you," the girl said. "I told you they were failing."

"They're not failing." Gideon's voice came out flat and hard, a door closing. "They're tired. It's festival eve. The wards always run thin before the Oathfire — we feed them tomorrow. That's the whole point of the fire. That's the whole point of *everything*."

"Then why can I feel them?" the girl said. "I'm a clerk, madam. I can't feel anything. I file things." She lifted her satchel like a shield. "And I can feel this. Like standing next to a bell that's been struck and hasn't decided whether to ring."

Gideon opened her mouth to tell her, with the full authority of the last swordspeaker of her people, exactly what a Choir clerk could and could not feel —

— and the second star went out.

Not the first. The first had died while the girl was still talking, a pinprick swallowed, and Gideon had told herself it was cloud, it was age, it was her eyes. But this one she was watching. High over the northern ridge, in the constellation the elders called the Plough-Ox, a star simply stopped. No flicker. No falling. It was there, and then the sky was a word with a letter missing, and Gideon's whole body understood before her mind did that the sky was not losing stars. The sky was *losing witnesses.*

"No," Gideon said. It was not a word aimed at anyone. It was the oldest word she had.

The girl — Sefa, she had said her name was Sefa — made a small sound, and her lantern guttered, and the darkness that came in behind the darkness was the old dark, the country dark, the dark that Gideon's people had crossed oceans of before they ever found this valley.

And then the wards shattered.

It did not sound like breaking. That was the horror of it, the thing Gideon would never manage to explain afterward to anyone who asked. It sounded like *singing*. Forty-one stones released their held note all at once, and the note did not die — it *rose*, a chord the valley had been refusing for sixty years finally allowed into the air, and the chord went up through Gideon's teeth and out of the top of her skull and kept going, up, up, into a sky with two holes in it. The barley flattened in a ring around her. The shrine stones wept condensation. Every hair on her arms stood in a language she had not spoken since childhood.

The silence flooded. That was the only way to put it. The magic-dead quiet that had been the valley's whole character — its safety, its smallness, its slow dying — broke like ice on a spring river, and underneath it the old power of the country came up and *filled* everything, the fields, the road, her lungs, the girl's wide eyes, with a presence so vast and so patient and so *grieved* that Gideon fell to her knees in the wet barley without deciding to.

"Oh," Sefa whispered, somewhere above her. "Oh, it's *real*. The ledgers said — the ledgers said it was all symbolic —"

The dead began to speak.

They came up through the ground the way heat comes up through a stone at dusk. Not ghosts — the valley had never held ghosts, that had been the point — but *voices*, the elders, all of them, every elder Gideon had buried in sixty years of being the last one, and behind them older voices, and behind those, older still, back and back to the founding. They did not speak words at first. They spoke *demand*. The whole valley was a courtroom and Gideon was on trial and she did not know the charge.

Then her grandmother's voice, out of the earth under the nineteenth stone, in the old tongue, in the cadence of a woman who had never once in her life repeated herself: *You kept the fire. Where is the rest of it?*

"I kept everything I could," Gideon said aloud, to the ground, to the dark, to the girl. Her voice cracked in the middle like a dropped plate. "There was no one left to keep."

*You kept the names?*

"Some of the names."

*You kept the oath?*

Gideon reached back, and her hand found the hilt of the oath-sword, and the oath-sword — asleep for thirty years, a beautiful dead thing, a ceremony in iron — *woke*.

It was not dramatic. That was the other thing no one would believe. The sword did not blaze. It *warmed*, the way a hand warms when another hand takes it, and along the fuller of the blade a thin line of ember-light woke and ran from guard to point like a word being written, and Gideon felt the oath come up her arm — *I speak, therefore the people stand; I stand, therefore the names are not lost* — and she wept without any dignity at all, kneeling in the barley, an old woman holding a lit sword under a sky with holes in it, while the dead demanded answers she did not have.

Above her, a third star went out.

* * *

Sefa had been trained, above all things, to write things down.

It was the first commandment of the Choir: *that which is not recorded does not exist*, and its shadow-corollary, the one they never said aloud: *that which is recorded can be made not to exist*. Sefa had eleven years of training in making things not exist. She was very good at it. She had run away from it with two satchels of evidence, which was either the bravest or the stupidest thing a clerk had ever done, and she had not yet decided which, and she suspected she never would.

None of that training had prepared her for the ground speaking.

She stood in the wet grass with her dead lantern and her living satchel and watched an old woman kneel in the barley talking to the dirt, and the dirt answering, and the answers were not words Sefa could parse but they were unmistakably *answers*, with the rhythm of a hard interrogation, and every instinct of her profession screamed at her to take minutes. Her hands actually went to her bag for her stylus. They stopped halfway. There was no ink in the world equal to this, and besides, her hands were shaking too hard to hold anything.

The stars. She kept looking up at the stars, because looking at the old woman was unbearable. Three gone now. She had watched the second one die and felt something in her chest she had no ledger-category for — the Choir's cosmology held that the stars were the *First Registry*, the witness-record of everything ever sworn, and every child in the Choir schools learned the catechism: *as the stars record, so the law endures.* She had always assumed it was poetry. Bureaucracies are full of poetry they don't believe.

But if the stars were witnesses, then something was *removing the witnesses*, and things were being done tonight that could not survive testimony.

The valley was waking around her and it was the most beautiful and most terrible thing Sefa had ever stood inside. Power came up out of the earth like water finding its old channel after a long drought, and where it passed, things *remembered*. The barley remembered being a forest. The road remembered being a processional way, and for one vertiginous moment Sefa saw it doubled — mud track and marble avenue, laid over each other like two pages held to the light. The shrine stones remembered being *fed*. And the old woman at the center of it all was the axis of it, the keystone, the place where the whole remembering turned, and the sword in her hands was writing light.

Sefa had read the file. Of course she had; she'd stolen the file. *Quintana, G. Last registered practitioner, oath-tradition (swordspeaker class). Status: nonviable. Tradition: dormant.* The Choir had a word for everything and the word was always smaller than the thing. *Dormant.* As if the sword were sleeping. As if the woman were sleeping. As if a whole country could be tucked in with a stamp and a form in triplicate.

The dead were asking the old woman questions and the old woman was failing to answer them and Sefa understood, with the cold clarity that comes in the middle of catastrophe, that the questions were not really for the old woman. They were for the *country*. The valley was a witness waking from anesthetic, and the first thing it wanted to know was what had been done to it while it slept.

A fourth star went out. Then a fifth, closer together now, like lights going out down a corridor, like someone walking through the sky snuffing candles, methodical, *auditing*.

"We have to move," Sefa heard herself say. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to. "Madam. Swordspeaker. We have to get off the open ground. When the wards go all the way down — and they're going all the way down, I've seen the schedules, I've read the requisitions — the first thing through the border will be a survey team, and the second thing will be a dissolution team, and they will find you standing in a field holding a lit sword, and there are forms for that. There are *forms* for that."

The old woman looked up at her. Her face was wet and furious and lit from below by the blade, and she was, Sefa thought, the most frightening person she had ever seen, and the only person in the world she trusted.

"Forms," Gideon Quintana said.

"I know how they think," Sefa said. "It's the only thing I know. But it's not nothing. Please."

The dead were still speaking. The whole valley was murmuring with them now, a tide of voices, and Sefa could not understand a word of it but she could feel the shape of the question underneath: *where were you, where were you, where were you when it was done to us.* She was a clerk of the order that had done it. She stood in the middle of the question and it went through her like a spear made of paper.

The last ward-stone — the forty-first, the one the old woman had called, on the walk up, *the one for the dead who stayed* — went dark with an audible sigh, and the border was simply *open*, the way a mouth is open, the way a wound is open, and the outer country's night leaned in.

Gideon Quintana stood up. It took her a long time, and the sword helped her, or she helped the sword; by the end it was hard to say which of them was lifting which. The ember-line along the blade had stopped moving. It had settled into a single steady word of light, and Sefa, who could read forty scripts and none of them well enough, still knew it when she saw it: a name. The blade was holding a name the way a hand holds a rope thrown to a drowning person.

"The stars," the old woman said. Her voice was ruined and calm. "Someone is putting them out. Witnesses first. That's how you unmake a country — you don't burn it, you *depose it*, you take away everyone who saw it, and then there was never anything to burn." She looked up at the holes in the sky, and then down at her blade, and then, last, at Sefa. "How many ledgers did you steal?"

"Two. And the census of dissolutions. And the index of sealed sites."

"Then you're going to show me what they wrote about my people," Gideon said, "and then we're going to find out who's up there snuffing out the sky — because my sword has been asleep for thirty years, girl, and tonight it woke up *angry*, and it has never once in its history woken up for anything small."

Overhead, the sixth star went out — and then, impossibly, a seventh *relit*, one single point of light kindling back into the Plough-Ox like an eye opening, like a witness who had decided, against all procedure, to keep watching.

The valley held its breath. The dead went silent, all together, listening.

And far out in the outer dark, past the open border, a lantern-light appeared. Then another. Then a line of them, patient and evenly spaced, coming up the old processional road toward a country that officially did not exist — and Sefa, who had filed the travel authorizations for teams like this, knew exactly what she was looking at.

"Survey team," she whispered. "They're early. They're *never* early."

Gideon Quintana turned toward the border, the lit sword rising in her hands like a torch being handed to the dark, and said the last thing Sefa expected, in the old tongue, in a voice that the whole valley — living and dead — heard clearly:

"Then let them write *this* down."
