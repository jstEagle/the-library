## 4. What the Ledger Says About You

Sefa had carried the ledgers for eleven days, and in all that time she had not once opened them.

She told herself it was caution. A clerk's virtue, drilled into her from the first day of novitiate: *a document unopened is a document deniable.* She had recited that maxim to herself on the road, in the ditches where she hid when riders passed, in the cold hours before dawn when she lay awake listening for footsteps that never came. But the truth was simpler and uglier: she was afraid of what she had stolen. Not of being caught with it — she had made peace with that somewhere around the third night, when the fear of pursuit curdled into something almost like hope — but of reading it. Of knowing, page by page, exactly what the institution she had served for nine years had done, written out in her own profession's tidy hand.

Now she sat in Gideon Quintana's kitchen, and there was no more room left for denial.

The valley house was small and smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs hung from the rafters in bundles tied with red thread. Outside, the sky was still wrong — still starless, still pressing down like a held breath — and inside, the silence was worse than it had been two nights ago, because now Sefa understood what the silence meant. This valley had been a place where magic could not follow. The Choir had made it so. And the woman who lived here had spent her whole life inside that hush without ever being told why.

Gideon stood at the hearth with her back half-turned, feeding split logs to a fire that burned too steadily, as if even the flames here were careful. Her oath-sword lay across the table between them, unsheathed for the first time in decades, its blade drinking the firelight and giving none back. Since the night the wards shattered, since the dead elders' voices had come flooding back into the valley like water into a cracked bowl, the sword had not gone quiet. It hummed. Sefa could feel it through the table, a low vibration in her palms, like a hive behind a wall.

"You said you'd tell me," Gideon said. She did not turn around. "On the border stones you said you'd stolen something worth the walk. You've been eating my bread for two days, clerk. Tell me."

Sefa set her hands flat on the first ledger's cover. It was bound in grey calfskin, stamped with the Choir's seal — three interlocking circles, the sign of the Record, the Witness, and the Verdict. She had stamped a thousand such covers herself. Her hands knew the weight of them the way a mother knows a child's.

"It's called a Dissolution Census," she said. "There are forty-one volumes. I took six. The ones relevant to—" She stopped. Started again. "To this region."

"To what region?" Gideon asked. "Name it."

And there it was, the trap Sefa had been walking toward since the border stones, laid by no one but herself. Because she couldn't name it. That was the whole horror of it, the thing she had only begun to understand as she fled: the name did not exist anymore. Not *forbidden* — forbidden would have been simple, forbidden would have been a crime she could respect. It was *struck*. Removed from every map, every treaty, every index and concordance in the Choir's archive, with the same procedural thoroughness applied to a misspelled entry. Somewhere in the deep stacks beneath the Choir's hall there was a cancellation folio, and in that folio, in ink that was legally indistinguishable from truth, was a line that read:

*Dissolved by rite and record. Name voided. Assets forfeit. Population reclassified.*

"Say it," Gideon said again, and now she turned, and her eyes were the color of wet slate, and Sefa saw that the woman already knew. Had perhaps always known, in the way one knows a wound under a bandage one has stopped looking at. "You can't. Can you."

"I'm sorry," Sefa said, and meant it, and hated how small it sounded. "That's what the ledgers are for. What's struck from speech is kept in ink. That's the doctrine. *What the tongue may not carry, the hand must hold.* They think they're being merciful. Preserving the record so that—"

"So that what?"

"So that someday it could be restored." Sefa heard the words come out of her mouth and recognized them as the words she had believed, once, with her whole obedient heart. "That was the teaching. Dissolution isn't destruction. It's suspension. The names are kept safe until—"

"Until." Gideon crossed the room and pulled out the chair across from Sefa, and the legs shrieked against stone, and the sword on the table hummed a note louder. "My grandmother died waiting for their *until*. My mother died waiting. I have buried every elder in this valley with my own hands, and I sang the leaving-rites over each of them alone, because there was no one left who remembered the responses, and you're going to sit there and tell me my country is in *suspension*."

"I'm going to show you," Sefa said quietly. "If you'll let me."

For a long moment the only sound was the fire and the sword's faint song. Then Gideon sat down across from her, heavily, like a woman taking a seat at a trial, and nodded once.

Sefa opened the ledger.

* * *

The first pages were administrative, and somehow that was the worst part. Sefa watched Gideon's face as she read, and saw the exact moment the woman understood that her nation's death had been paperwork.

*Regional Survey, Cycle 312, Post-Rite Verification,* ran the header. Below it, columns. Sefa knew the columns by heart; she had entered data into their descendants for years. *Parcel designations. Hydrographic features. Arable acreage. Structures: habitable, ruinous, ceremonial. Population: see Schedule F.*

"There's no name," Gideon said. Her finger rested on the header row without touching the ink, the way one touches a body to confirm it's cold. "This is my valley. These are the terraces above the river — see, *hydrographic feature 7, seasonal falls* — that's the Singing Falls, we named them when I was a girl, there's a whole story about why, my grandmother used to— and it's just a number. Feature seven."

"The rite doesn't erase things," Sefa said. Her voice had gone flat, professional, the voice she used for dictation; it was the only way she could keep reading aloud. "It erases *meaning*. The land stays. The records stay. Only the name goes, and everything the name carried with it. After dissolution, a fall is a hydrographic feature. A grave-field is land use category four. A people is a population figure."

"Show me the population figure."

Sefa turned pages. The paper was good paper, rag-laid, made to last centuries; the Choir built everything to last except its promises. She found Schedule F and turned the book around so Gideon could read it upright, though Sefa suspected the woman could read upside down as easily as any soldier who'd studied captured documents. Gideon didn't turn it. She leaned forward and read it as it lay, and her lips moved slightly, counting.

"Four thousand," she said. "Four thousand one hundred and six."

"That was the final census before the rite. Thirty years ago."

"There were more than that." Gideon's voice was very controlled, which Sefa had learned, in two days, was the most dangerous register the woman owned. "There were more than that even after the plagues. Even after the border wars. Four thousand was the count of the valley alone if you counted every—"

"Look at the disposition column."

Gideon looked. Sefa watched her read the entries she herself had read for the first time in the archive's reading room, three weeks ago, with a sanctioned supervisor dozing two desks away. *Reclassified: absorbed. Reclassified: absorbed. Reclassified: indentured, term indefinite. Reclassified: relocated, destination per Schedule H. Deceased prior to rite: 611. Unaccounted: 94.*

"Unaccounted," Gideon repeated. "Ninety-four people were *unaccounted*."

"That means the auditors couldn't find them before the rite closed. It happens in every dissolution. People run, or hide, or—" Sefa stopped. "Or they're in valleys like this one. Places the wards made quiet. The survey teams marked those holdings as *low priority, self-dissolving*. Meaning they expected the remnant populations to die out on their own. No further action required."

The fire popped. Gideon did not move for so long that Sefa began to fear she had broken something in the woman beyond mending. Then Gideon reached across the table and drew the ledger toward herself, both hands, the way one draws a blade from an enemy's body, and began to read it alone, page by page, and Sefa let her, because there was nothing left to mediate. The ink would say what it said. It had always said what it said. Sefa had simply never been on the side of the table where it was said *to* someone.

She watched Gideon read her own erasure by firelight, and understood that she was watching the exact moment the Clerk-General's doctrines died in another human heart. It had taken Sefa nine years and a stolen night. It was taking Gideon one hour.

* * *

Near midnight, Gideon found the family schedule.

Sefa knew it was coming. She had known since the border stones; she had done the arithmetic herself, guiltily, in the archive, the day she decided to steal the volumes. *Quintana, G.,* the census would say, because the survey teams always recorded the last oath-bound member of a swordspeaker line separately — a special classification, *instrumental asset, deferred*, meaning the Choir had known about her, had filed her, had voted in some committee to leave her alive in her silent valley rather than spend the resources to dissolve a single woman who posed no threat behind wards the Choir itself had built.

"Instrumental asset," Gideon read aloud. Her voice did not shake. Sefa wished it would shake. "Deferred. They call me an asset."

"It's a classification. It doesn't mean—"

"What does it mean, Sefa? You're the clerk. Tell me what it means when the Choir writes *asset* next to a living woman's name."

Sefa told the truth, because lying to this woman had become impossible. "It means they kept you the way they keep a boundary stone or a treaty clause. Something useful to the shape of the peace. Your valley was part of the dissolution's architecture. The wards needed an anchor, and the anchor needed a warden, and the warden needed to be someone who would stay. Someone bound by duty. Someone who thought she was protecting something."

"Protecting something." Gideon laughed, once, and the sound was terrible. "I tended their locks for thirty years. I buried my people in a box they built and called it a sanctuary. And here it is—" her finger came down on the page, on a line Sefa had hoped with a coward's hope she would skip — "*Schedule J. Household inventory. Assets forfeit.* Read it to me. My eyes are tired. Read me my family."

"Sefa—"

"*Read it.*"

So Sefa read it. Her dictation voice held, mostly.

"*Schedule J, continuation. Quintana, Maren — deceased, pre-rite, natural causes, no action. Quintana, Odile — deceased, pre-rite. Quintana, Besse — reclassified, relocated, destination per Schedule H.*" Sefa paused. There was a footnote mark. She had looked up this footnote in the archive, in the last hour before she stole the volumes, and she had never slept properly since. "There's a cross-reference."

"Read it."

"*Schedule H, entry 2214. Relocation cohort nine. Destination: quarry works, Ashfall province. Status line, appended cycle 340: cohort attrition complete.*"

The word *attrition* sat in the air between them. Sefa had transcribed that word a hundred times in her career. Attrition, completion, closure, resolution — the Choir's vocabulary was a garden of soft words planted over hard ones. She had never once, in nine years, had to say one of those soft words aloud to the face of someone it described.

"Attrition complete," Gideon said. "That's a death list. That's my aunt Besse, who taught me to fish the winter pools, and it says *attrition complete* in the same column where other lines say *fencing repaired* and *well sealed*." She rose from the table. The chair went over backward and she did not catch it. The sword on the table sang louder, a clear rising note, and the firelight bent toward it, and for one moment Sefa saw the blade as the elders' voices must have seen it on the night the wards fell — awake, hungry, remembering.

"Gideon," Sefa said carefully. "Put it down. Whatever you're about to do, the ledger isn't—"

"The ledger is *exactly* what I'm about to do." Gideon picked up the volume with both hands, and Sefa flinched, certain the woman would feed it to the fire, and found she could not decide whether she wanted her to stop. But Gideon only held it, at arm's length, the way one holds a snake by the tail. "Do you know what I was doing the night your Choir dissolved us? I was twelve years old. I remember the stars going strange, and my grandmother going out to the border stones, and coming back with her hands bleeding, saying *the wards are singing wrong, child, get inside.* And then nothing. Nothing changed. Nothing ever changed again. Thirty years of nothing, and all the while it was *this*. Ink. Columns. Some auditor with cold hands writing *attrition complete* about my aunt between his lunch and his evening prayers."

"Yes," Sefa said. Because there was no other honest word.

"And you carried this. For nine years. You stamped these covers. You wrote these dispositions."

"I wrote the *descendants* of these dispositions. Smaller ones. Village charters. Tax reclassifications. I told myself—" Sefa stopped, because the sentence she had told herself for nine years was ash in her mouth. She started over. "I told myself the same thing everyone tells themselves. That the alternative to the Choir is war. That the dissolutions bought peace. That someone has to keep the ledgers or the world burns. And then I read this schedule, and I looked up footnote 2214, and I understood that the peace and the burning were the same fire, and we were just very careful about which direction the smoke blew."

Gideon set the ledger down. Gently, which frightened Sefa more than throwing it would have. When a woman handles a thing gently after wanting to burn it, she has moved past anger into decision.

"Forty-one volumes," Gideon said.

"Forty-one. I took six."

"How many nations?"

Sefa closed her eyes. "In the current cycle? Nineteen dissolved. Three pending review. One—" she hesitated — "one scheduled for next spring. The boy Tam's country, if he's right about what he saw. The ferryman's grandson. He's on the road ahead of us, if he still lives."

"Nineteen." Gideon looked at the sword, and the sword seemed to look back. "And every one of them in these books. Every grave listed as land cleared. Every orphan as a relocation. Every name as a number." She straightened, and Sefa saw the tiredness in her transmute, visibly, the way ore transmutes in a forge — not into comfort, never into comfort, but into purpose. "Then we're going to need the other thirty-five."

Sefa blinked. "The other— Gideon, the remaining volumes are in the Choir's deep archive, under seal, in a vault that requires—"

"I know where they are. You told me where they are. On the border stones, when you were trying to convince me you were worth the walk." Gideon allowed herself the ghost of a smile, and it was the coldest smile Sefa had ever seen on a warm face. "You said the vault was in the provincial hall at Ashfall. You said it twice. Clerks repeat important things; it's how you're trained."

Sefa stared at her. Then, despite everything — despite the starless sky, the humming sword, the word *attrition* still lodged in her chest like a splinter — she heard herself laugh, short and helpless. "Three years of elocution drills and that's what they were for. Being quoted back at me by a woman with a grudge."

"Not a grudge." Gideon gathered the ledgers into a stack, squared their edges with a clerk's own precision, and set her palm flat upon them as if swearing on them. "An audit. They taught you that word too, didn't they? An accounting of what's owed." She looked up, and the firelight caught the slate of her eyes and lit them from underneath. "Rest tonight. Tomorrow we bury my grandmother's sister's memory properly, with the rites said loud, in a language your books say is voided. And the day after, clerk, you and I are going to walk out of this valley — and you're going to teach me to read the rest of what they wrote."

Outside, in the starless dark, something far off answered her — not thunder, not wind, but the faint, unmistakable sound of many voices, singing in a language the ledgers claimed no longer existed.
