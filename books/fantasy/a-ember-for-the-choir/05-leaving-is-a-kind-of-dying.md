## 5. Leaving Is a Kind of Dying

Gideon had watched people leave the valley her whole life, and every one of them had left wrong.

That was the thought she woke with, before dawn, before the birds, before the grief had finished arranging itself in her chest like furniture she would have to live around. Her mother had left wrong — carried out on a bier when Gideon was nine, the leaving-rites spoken by four elders while Gideon stood too small to see over their shoulders. The elders had left wrong, one by one, each funeral thinner than the last, until the rites had been spoken over Marta Ilyen by a congregation of one. And the strangers, the few traders who had ever been permitted past the border stones, they had left wrong too — walking out the way you walk out of a market, unburdened, unmarked, their names still whole behind them.

No one had ever left the valley *correctly*, because a correct leaving required two things the valley no longer had: a people to witness it, and a country to return to.

She rose in the dark and dressed in the old way, which took longer than the new way, which was any way at all. The oath-sash went on first, wound three times around the waist and knotted at the left hip in the pattern called the river-turning, because a river turns before it leaves the mountains and it is never a betrayal. Over that, the grey traveling coat that had been her grandmother's, and her grandmother's mother's before her, the hem let down so many times it was mostly other cloth now, patchwork of every generation that had mended it against leaving. The sword came last. It had woken three nights ago, when the stars went out and the wards came down, and it had not gone back to sleep. It lay against her back like a hand resting between her shoulders, warm through the coat, and when she breathed deep she could feel it breathing too, or something like breathing, a slow pulse of heat along the blade that matched no rhythm of hers and yet did not argue with any of them.

The house was very quiet. It had been quiet for years, but it was a different quiet now. Before, the quiet had been the quiet of a full room after everyone has gone to bed — the creak of settling beams, the tick of the hearthstone, the small animal sounds of a lived-in place. Now the quiet had a hole in the middle of it, shaped like every voice that had ever filled it, and Gideon moved through the rooms avoiding the hole the way you avoid a stair that isn't there anymore.

She did not pack. There was nothing to pack that the valley would not miss more than she would. Instead she did what her people did, what the rites demanded, what the last four elders had each done in their turn when they understood they would be the ones to carry the leaving alone: she unmade her house gently, room by room, the way you close a book you do not know when you will open again.

In the kitchen she banked the hearth to embers and spoke over it: *Fire, you were fed by my hands and my mother's hands and hands older than the naming of this stone. Keep. Do not die. I am not abandoning you; I am lending the dark my absence.* The embers settled, and it was nonsense, she knew it was nonsense, the fire would be cold by noon and the chimney would fill with rain and sparrows — but the words were not for the fire. The words were for her, and they held.

In the sleeping room she stripped the bed and folded the blankets into the cedar chest, and spoke over the chest: *Wood, you hold what we slept in. Hold it well. Dreams do not rot if the box is honest.* The chest had held her mother's blankets and her grandmother's, and the smell that rose when she closed it was the smell of thirty years of ordinary nights, and Gideon stood with her hand flat on the lid for a long time, breathing it in, because she understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that she was building a memory on purpose, that she was choosing which version of this room she would carry, and the version with the smell of cedar and clean wool was the one worth carrying.

The last room was the one with the shelf of names.

Every swordspeaker's house had one. It was not a shrine, exactly — her people had been impatient with shrines, with the whole idea of holiness kept at a distance — but a ledger of a different kind than Sefa's, a ledger of presence. A board of pale wood, and on it, carved small and close together, the names of everyone who had ever slept under this roof. Her own name was there, cut by her mother's hand when she was six and proud of it, the letters wobbling. Above it her mother's, and her mother's mother's, and above those names so old the wood had darkened around them until they stood out pale as scars. There were names of guests, of traders sheltering from storms, of a Choir surveyor from sixty years ago who had been polite and confused and had left after two days, and whose name the elders had cut in anyway, because the rule was the rule: *everyone who sleeps here belongs here, and belonging does not expire.*

Gideon took up the knife and, standing in the grey light before dawn, cut her own name out.

Not erased. That was the whole point, the whole terrible engineering of the rite. She did not scrape it away, did not deface it. She cut around it, carefully, following the old letters with the blade's tip the way you follow a path you know blind, and lifted the chip of wood free with her fingernails, and it came away whole — a small pale splinter with her name on it, *GIDEON QUINTANA* in her mother's careful strokes — leaving behind a Gideon-shaped absence in the board, a negative space the exact size of her.

She put the splinter in her mouth.

This was the part the elders had never had to explain, because there had always been someone to explain it, and now Gideon understood why the rite had never been written down, why it lived only in bodies: because some instructions cannot survive being instructions. They only work when a mouth that loves you tells them to your mouth. She had watched it done four times. She had been the one speaking the words over Marta Ilyen's splinter, guiding the old woman's shaking hands, and now there was no one to guide hers, and the words came anyway, rising out of the same place the sword's heat came from, out of whatever was left in the valley that remembered being a country.

*Name, you were given in love and cut in love,* she said around the wood, the words thick and strange. *I carry you out. The board keeps your shape. If I come home, I will fit. If I do not come home, the shape will testify that I was here, and that I left on purpose, and that leaving was not dying, whatever it looks like from inside.*

She chewed the splinter. It tasted of cedar and of her mother's knife-oil and, faintly, unbearably, of nothing at all — and then she swallowed, and her name was in her, carried inward like a coal swallowed by a fire-walker, and the board on the wall had a hole in it shaped exactly like a person, and Gideon Quintana stood in her own house and wept without sound, the way you weep when crying loudly would count as a second person present and there is only allowed to be one.

* * *

The leaving-rites proper were done at the border, and they were done at the hour when the last star of night and the first light of morning argued over ownership of the sky — except that now there was no last star, had been no stars for three nights, and the sky was simply a long grey nothing, a ceiling instead of a country. Gideon walked the mile from her house to the border stones with the sword on her back and the oath-sash tight at her hip, and behind her the valley lay in the half-light like a body sleeping on its side: the terraces going wild already, the orchard heavy with fruit no one would pick, the meeting-house with its door standing open because there was no one left to close it, and the Oathfire pit at the center of everything, cold for three days, cold for the first time in four hundred years.

She had thought about relighting it. She had stood over the pit the second morning with a lit brand in her hand and found that she could not. An Oathfire lit by one person, to be tended by one person, was not an Oathfire; it was a candle performing a lie. The fire needed a circle. The fire needed voices. She had put the brand out in the damp ash and felt the pit's cold rise up at her like a question she could not answer yet.

At the border stones she stopped, and turned, and looked at her country for what the rites insisted she treat as the last time.

The border of the valley had never been a wall. It was nine stones, each taller than a person, set in a rough arc across the valley mouth where the land narrowed to a throat, and for as long as Gideon had lived they had hummed — not audibly, but in the teeth, in the fillings, in the wet glass of the eye, a vibration that said *here ends the place where magic cannot follow, here begins the ordinary world.* Her people had built the valley on that silence. Magic, in the old days, had been a thing that ate nations: it got into the water of a country's story and drank until the story was only a thirst. The founders had chosen the one fold of the world where the old powers could not enter, had paid — the elders never said what the price was, only that it was paid, only that it was *paid in full* — and for four hundred years the valley had been a held breath, a country kept alive by being kept small.

Three nights ago the hum had stopped. Three nights ago, in the same hour the first star had gone out over Sefa's upturned face, the wards on the stones had shattered like ice on a spring pond, and the silence had flooded backward — not with magic, not at first, but with everything the silence had been holding off. Gideon had felt the dead arrive before she understood what the pressure in her skull was: the elders, all of them, every voice the valley had ever kept, pressing at the inside of her ears like people at a door.

They were pressing now. She could feel them, patient and terrible, and she knew what they wanted, and she could not give it to them, and the not-giving was the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the splinter, harder than the cold fire.

*Answer us,* the dead said, in the voice of every elder she had buried and every elder she hadn't. *The wards are down. The stars are out. The country that did this to us is reachable now. You are the last sword, and the last sword exists to be answered to. Speak, and we will tell you where they live. Speak, and we will tell you what they took, item by item, and you will take it back with interest. Speak.*

"No," Gideon said aloud, to the empty morning, to the stones, to the dead.

She knelt at the largest stone, the keystone, the one her people called the First Word, and she did what the rite required of the one who leaves: she laid her palm flat against the stone and spoke the leaving-formula, the one that had been said at this border by every emigrant and every exile and every bride carried out to a husband's house in the lowlands, four hundred years of departures soaked into the granite.

*I go out from the kept place into the unkept world. I do not go as a stranger, for I carry my country in my mouth. I do not go as a ghost, for my name is in my body and my body is in my keeping. Whatever I meet out there, I meet as my people's answer. Let the stones remember I left standing. Let the stones testify I meant to return. And if the world kills me out there, let it be recorded that I died a citizen of —*

And here the rite required the name. The name of the country. The whole name, spoken aloud at the border, so that the border itself would know what it was losing.

Gideon opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

She tried again, and again there was nothing — not a block, not a stammer, but a true and perfect silence, as though the name were a room in her and she had walked to its door and found the door opening onto a wall. She stood there with her hand on the stone and her mouth working like a fish's, and slowly, horribly, she understood. The Choir had not just struck the name from maps. They had struck it from *speech*. Somewhere in Sefa's ledgers, in some office with some number, the name had been filed as dissolved, and the filing had teeth, and the teeth were in her, and she could no more say her country's name than she could fly by wishing.

The dead were silent for a moment. Then, softly, in her grandmother's voice, in the voice that had taught her her letters and her sword-forms and the proper way to gut a fish: *Then carry the shape of it, child. A name with a hole in it is still a name. We will hold the middle. You hold the edges.*

Gideon pressed her forehead to the stone. The granite was cold and it smelled of rain and of four hundred years, and when she spoke again her voice came out low and level and it was the voice of the oath, the swordspeaker's voice, the one that did not shake because shaking was not in its grammar.

"Let the stones testify I meant to return," she said, "and that I left carrying everything I could lift."

She stood. She stepped between the First Word and its neighbor stone, through the gap where the hum used to be, and the crossing itself was nothing — a footfall, dry grass, the other side — and it was everything, because in forty-one years she had never once put her whole weight on the far side of that line, and the far side received her weight without comment, without ceremony, the way a stranger receives a stranger.

* * *

The outer world was loud.

That was the first thing, and for the first hour it was nearly the only thing. Gideon had not known silence could have a shape until she lost it. The valley's quiet had been a vessel; the outer world's noise was water poured over her, into her, through every seam of her. Wind in grass that had never been wards — a huge, ragged, unmanaged sound. Insects in numbers that seemed personal, a shimmering wall of small engines. A bird that did not know any of the valley's birds and said so, over and over, in a voice like a hinge. And under it all, so constant she only noticed it when she stopped walking, a low vast rumble from the east that she would later learn was a city, a city of a hundred thousand people running its machinery day and night because no one had ever told it to be quiet, because no one here had ever been taught that quiet was a country you could live in.

And there were no stars. It was morning, so that proved nothing yet, but Gideon looked up anyway, at the flat grey lid of the sky, and felt the absence the way you feel a missing tooth. Three nights of nothing overhead. The elders said the stars had been the first witnesses, older than any ledger, older than law; her people had sworn by them the way other folk swore by gods. Now the sky was a page with everything struck out, and Gideon thought, with a cold clarity that felt like the ledger had gotten inside her too: *that's what erasure looks like from underneath. Not darkness. Blankness. A ceiling where a country used to be.*

She walked. The land beyond the border was hills at first, then a long descending valley not her own, terraced by other hands in other patterns, growing crops she knew only from books. She passed a shrine at a crossroads — some other people's dead, some other people's names, ribbons tied to a post, and the ribbons were bright and new and someone had tied them recently, and Gideon stood before it longer than she meant to, because it was the first proof she had seen with her own eyes that the outer world still had people in it who tied ribbons for their dead, and she had begun to fear the erasure was total, that the Choir had gotten everywhere, that the whole world was a ledger now.

Toward noon she came over a rise and saw the road.

It was a real road, gravelled and ditched, running east-west along the valley floor, and on it, in the middle distance, was a cart, and walking beside the cart were people — three of them, a woman and two men, ordinary, loud, alive — and Gideon dropped behind the rise before she had decided to, her body making the choice her mind was still arguing about. She crouched in the dry grass with her heart going like a drum and watched them pass, and they passed without looking up, without any idea that the last swordspeaker of a dissolved nation was watching them from the weeds with a waking sword and a swallowed name, and there was something in that — in being unseen, unregistered, a blank in their blank sky — that was either terror or freedom, and Gideon could not yet tell which.

*You are a stranger here,* she told herself. *Good. Strangers can go anywhere. Strangers can ask anything. A citizen has a country to defend; a stranger has only questions, and questions get you further than swords in a country that erases things with paperwork.* She thought of Sefa, three days behind her somewhere, or three days ahead — the clerk with the stolen ledgers, the girl who had watched a star die and had not run. Gideon had dismissed her at the border stones. She was beginning, in the loud and starless afternoon, to suspect that had been a mistake. The ledgers knew where things were. The ledgers knew what had been taken. And somewhere in the ledgers, presumably, was the place where dissolved nations went — the archive, the vault, the grave with a filing number.

She stood up out of the grass, squared the oath-sash at her hip, and turned east, toward the rumble of the city, toward the noise, toward everything her valley had been built to keep out.

Behind her, faint as breath, the dead elders spoke one more time, all their voices braided into one, and for the first time since the wards fell they did not demand. They instructed.

*East, sword-bearer. Follow the road until it forgets to be a road. There is a woman at the crossroads who sells water and lies, and she will tell you a word you must not believe, and the word is your country's name, and she will say it as though it means nothing.*

Gideon went very still.

*And sword-bearer —* her grandmother's voice, alone now, dry as the cedar chest — *if a stranger can speak what you cannot, then the name is not dead. It is only imprisoned. Find who holds the key.*

Gideon Quintana, last of the swordspeakers, citizen of a country she could no longer name, put her hand on the hilt at her back — the blade pulsed once, warm, awake, like a heart agreeing to something — and walked down into the loud world to find the woman who sold water and lies.
