## 6. The Road of Borrowed Names

The road taught Gideon its first lesson by the second morning: no one looked at anyone here.

In the valley, a stranger's face was an event — something to be fed, questioned, sung to. Here on the lowland roads, faces passed like leaves on water, each one angled carefully away from every other, as if meeting another person's eyes might cost a fee. She learned to walk the way they walked: shoulders rounded, gaze fixed at the middle distance, hands empty and unremarkable. She wrapped her oath-sword in the coarse sacking she'd taken from Sefa's pack — *borrowed*, Sefa had insisted, in the tone of someone who had spent her whole life auditing the difference between borrowed and stolen and found it thinner than paper.

*You can't carry that openly,* Sefa had told her at the fallen border, back when Gideon still believed the world could be argued with. *A swordspeaker's blade out of nowhere, with the wards down and every auditor in three provinces awake? They'll have your name before noon.*

*I don't have a name,* Gideon had said, and meant it as a joke, and heard it land as grief.

So she walked under no name at all. When the toll-keepers at the first bridge asked, she said *Maro*, which was her mother's word for the wind that came down off the eastern ridge in autumn, the one that smelled of stone. It was a small theft, a private one. If she had to be nobody, she would at least be nobody in her mother's language, where no census clerk could follow.

The road itself was older than any of the towns it stitched together, and it carried its dead the way rivers carry silt. Gideon began to see them by the third day — not ghosts, not exactly, though after the night the stars went out she no longer trusted her old certainty about what exactly counted as a ghost. She saw them in the ways people stood. A woman selling smoked fish at a crossroads who arranged her scales in threes, always threes, though buyers came one at a time. A carter who touched his hat-brim to a certain bare field as he passed, quick and furtive, the way you'd greet a grave. A child skipping rope to a rhyme whose meter didn't fit the trade-tongue, whose stresses fell wrong, whose cadence belonged somewhere else entirely.

Everywhere, the shape of something erased, persisting in the gestures. Survivors did not survive whole. They survived the way embers survive: banked, disguised, handed hand to hand in the dark.

She stopped at the fish-woman's stall and bought nothing, just stood a moment while the woman counted her change in threes, and said, quietly, in a dialect she hadn't spoken aloud since she was nine years old and visiting her grandmother's sister in the winter camps, "Your grandmother's people counted in threes too."

The woman's hands went still over the coins. For one heartbeat her eyes came up — and Gideon saw in them the exact expression the road wore everywhere, the thing beneath the lowered gazes. Not surprise. Terror, ancient and practiced, and underneath the terror, terrible hope.

"You're mistaken," the woman said loudly, in the trade-tongue, for the benefit of the market. And then, barely a breath, barely movement of lips at all: "Third stall past the shrine. Ask for nothing. Just leave your bowl."

Gideon did not understand until evening. At the third stall past the crumbling roadside shrine — a shrine to some saint whose face had been chiseled smooth, she noticed, the features dissolved as neatly as a nation — she set down her wooden bowl upside down, as if to dry. In the morning, when she lifted it, there was a twist of paper inside. On it, in a careful hand, were written three names. Not the names the bearers used in daylight. Old names, hidden ones, and beside each, a village where "people of the old count" could be found, and beside that, a warning written so small it was almost apology: *Never say them where walls are new. New walls listen.*

She burned the paper in her cookfire and lay awake a long time after, watching the starless sky press down on the world like a lid.

That was when she understood the shape of the country she was walking through. It was not one country at all. It was a sediment of countries — hers and others, dozens of others, layered and compacted and paved over, each one still faintly warm underneath. The Choir had not merely erased her people. Erasure was an industry here. The whole map was a palimpsest, and everyone walking these roads was living in the margins of their own disappeared home, tolerated, taxed, counted in the ledgers as *labor, resident, non-citizen* — present, but never belonging.

*Tolerated.* She turned the word over that night until it lost all meaning and became only a small cold stone in her mouth.

*

The river came down out of the hills on the fifth day, broad and brown and unhurried, and where the road met the water there was a ferry.

It was nothing much — a flat barge, a cable slung between two leaning posts, a wheel for hauling. But the man who worked the ferry made Gideon stop at the edge of the trees and simply look.

He was old in the way of things built to last: not frail, but finished, the way a beam is finished once it has held up a roof for sixty years. His hands were enormous and slow-moving. He sat on the gunwale mending a net with a bone needle, and he sang — half under his breath, tuneless, more habit than music — and Gideon's whole body went rigid before her mind caught up with her ears.

She knew the song.

Not the words; he was singing them in the trade-tongue, worn down to nonsense syllables, a counting song about sheep or maybe fish. But the melody was the descent-and-return that every valley child learned before they learned their own name, the melody that mapped the shape of a swordspeaker's oath — falling, falling, and then rising again at the end, always rising, because the oath's whole meaning was that what falls may be raised. Her people's music, worn smooth by fifty years of other mouths, surviving in a boatman's mumbling like a fossil in limestone.

She crossed the road and paid her copper without a word and stepped onto the barge, and the old man pushed off, and neither of them spoke for the width of the river.

Halfway across, the current caught them sideways and the barge swung, and the sacking wrapping her sword slipped loose at her hip. Just a hand's breadth of it. Just enough.

The sword's hilt showed: dark iron, plain as a tool, wound at the grip with a knot of black cord and red — not decoration, never decoration. An oath-knot. Seven turns, crossed twice, sealed with a drop of wax from an Oathfire now forty years cold. There were perhaps six people alive who could tie such a knot correctly. Gideon had thought, until this week, that she was the only one.

The needle stopped moving.

She felt it stop the way you feel a held breath. She kept her eyes on the far bank, on the willows, on anything else, and let the silence stretch, because she had already learned on this road that naming things too soon killed them.

But the old man did not do what everyone else on the road did. He did not look away. He leaned forward, slow, the way you approach a wounded bird, and his great hands set down the net and the needle with exaggerated care, as if sudden movement might shatter something, and he said, in a language that had been struck from every map and ledger and gravestone in the world:

"Seven turns. Crossed twice." His voice cracked in the middle like green wood. "Who tied your knot, daughter?"

Gideon looked at him then. Up close his face was a map of its own, river-cut, weather-carved, and his eyes were wet, and had been wet for some time, she realized — wet the way old grief is wet, always near the surface, needing only a stone thrown in.

"My grandmother," Gideon said, in the same tongue, and hearing it spoken aloud across open water, under the open sky, nearly undid her. "Oyelaran. Ashka Oyelaran. She tied it at my oath-taking when I was sixteen."

"Ashka." He said the name like a prayer he'd been forbidden to finish. The barge drifted; the current took it; neither of them reached for the wheel. "Ashka Oyelaran's granddaughter. Here. On my river." He pressed both palms against his own chest as if holding something in. "I ferried your people. Before. Did you know that? Thirty years I worked this crossing, and before the Dissolution, the traffic came up from the valley country every autumn — pilgrims going to the Oathfire, traders, children going to learn the sword-songs. This boat has carried four hundred of your people, girl. Maybe five hundred. I counted. Nobody asked me to count. I just — I counted, because I thought somebody should know how many there had been, in case anybody ever asked."

His voice failed altogether. He turned his face toward the water and wept the way old men weep, silently, catastrophically, his shoulders shaking the whole frame of the barge, and Gideon stood on the swaying planks and did not know what to do, because no one had wept at the sight of her in thirty years. No one had wept for her people in front of her, ever. The elders had died grieving, yes, but privately, economically, folding their sorrow away like clothing they could no longer afford to wear.

And here was this stranger, this boatman of no nation worth recording, sobbing because of a knot in some cord.

She put her hand on his shoulder. It seemed to be the right thing; he covered it with one of his vast hands and held on.

"They said dissolved," he managed at last. "When the news came up the river. *Dissolved*, like sugar in water. Like it was chemistry. Like nothing remained." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, roughly, angry at the tears. "I knew better. I ferried the remains for years after, didn't I? Little things. A woman who sang your harvest songs to her baby in a whisper, quiet as breathing, every crossing, terrified of her own mouth. A man who asked me, once, if I remembered the words to the crossing-blessing — your people bless a crossing, you know, there's a blessing for bridges and a blessing for boats — and when I said I'd forgotten most of it, he cried harder than I'm crying now, and thanked me anyway, and gave me double fare, and I threw it back at him because you don't pay double for remembering, that's not —" He stopped. Breathed. "That's not what remembrance is for."

"What is it for?" Gideon asked. It was not a rhetorical question. She genuinely wanted to know. She had been keeping remembrance like a coal in a closed fist for thirty years and it had burned her and warmed no one.

The old man took up the wheel and began, slowly, to bring the barge around to the landing, and as he worked he answered.

"For handing on," he said. "That's all. You don't keep it for yourself. You keep it the way I kept the count — so that when somebody comes asking, someday, there's a number. There's a witness. There's an old fool on a boat who says: *yes. You existed. I saw you. Five hundred, give or take, and I'll swear to every one of them.*"

The barge kissed the mud of the far bank. He made it fast, and then he turned and looked at her again, long and searching, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod slowly, as if confirming a suspicion he'd been carrying even longer than the tears.

"You're going somewhere," he said. "Something's woken up. Don't tell me —" and he actually raised one huge hand, half-humor, half-dread, "— don't tell me. The less an old man knows, the longer an old man floats. But." He fumbled at his neck and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a small brass token, worn nearly smooth, stamped with the mark of a ferry license forty years expired. He pressed it into her palm and closed her fingers over it. "Downstream, three days, there's a crossing called Weeping Ford — don't mind the name, everything downstream is named for weeping — and at Weeping Ford there's a boy, my grandson, Tam. My daughter's son. He wears a name that isn't ours; his father's family bought citizenship back when it still could be bought, God forgive them, they thought they were saving the child." His jaw worked. "He doesn't know our tongue. I couldn't teach him. Teaching it out loud felt like painting a target. But he knows the songs without knowing what they are. He hums them when he thinks no one's listening. Show him the token. Tell him his grandfather counts him among the five hundred."

"Why?" Gideon said. "Why help me? Why any of it?"

The old man smiled then, and it transformed his ruined face into something almost young.

"Because somebody has to be able to say it happened," he said. "That's all justice is, maybe, before anything bigger gets built. Somebody saying: *it happened, I saw, I'll swear to it.* Now get off my boat, swordspeaker, before someone sees you standing there looking like what you are."

She stepped onto the bank. Behind her, already pushing off, already reaching for his net, he began to sing — openly this time, full-throated, the descent-and-rise ringing out across the brown water for anyone to hear, and Gideon stood in the willow-shadow and listened to her people's melody travel downriver into the heart of the country that had outlawed it, and found that she was weeping too, briefly, uselessly, and that it helped anyway.

*

She walked the next three days with the brass token warm against her sternum and the road unfolding its quiet evidence at every mile.

She passed a quarry town where the workers' lunch whistle played four notes that didn't belong to any work-song — a fragment, she was almost sure, of a lament from a nation called Selv that had been dissolved before she was born. She passed a shrine where someone had left, among the honest offerings of bread and candles, a single iron nail bent into a rough circle, and understood it at once: a border-marker from some erased boundary line, rescued from a ditch, smuggled here, offered up to a saint whose face no longer existed either. She passed a checkpoint where bored militiamen checked papers with elaborate indifference, and watched a carter produce documents with hands that shook, and understood that the indifference and the shaking were two halves of the same arrangement: *we will pretend you might be legitimate if you pretend we might believe it.*

Tolerated. The word again, that small cold stone. She rolled it in her mind until it cracked, and inside it she found not pity but fury — cold, patient, load-bearing fury, the kind you can build on.

Her people had not been the only ones. That should have broken her heart further, and instead it did something else: it made her grief communal, and communal grief, she was beginning to suspect, was a different substance altogether. Grief alone is a weight. Grief shared along a road, handed hand to hand like an ember in the dark — that might be fuel.

On the third evening, camped in a hollow off the road with her fire banked low, she took out the token and studied it by feel more than light. Stamped into the soft brass, almost effaced by decades of thumbs, she found marks she had missed at the ferry crossing: not just the license seal. Beneath it, tiny, deliberate, a second stamp — seven small strokes crossed twice.

An oath-knot, in miniature. The old man had worn his sympathy for her people stamped right there on his license, hidden in plain sight under official metal, for thirty years.

She closed her hand around it and lay back and looked up at the starless sky, that vast blank ledger, and thought: *the stars went out, and still the counting continues. Still the songs pass hand to hand. Whatever extinguished the lights up there made an error. It mistook darkness for agreement.*

She was nearly asleep when she heard the horses.

Two of them, moving fast on the road above her hollow, no lanterns — which told her more than hoofbeats could. Honest travelers carried lights. Militia patrols announced themselves. Riders who traveled dark and fast at night were men who did not want to be seen, and did not want to see, because seeing was not their purpose. Finding was.

She put out her fire with a handful of earth and lay still in the bracken, and through the screen of gorse she watched them pass on the road: two riders in the gray undress of clerks-afield, and on the saddle-bow of the lead rider, catching even the faint light of the clouded moon, a ledger bound in white leather, strapped open to the elements like a standard.

White leather. Audit colors.

They passed her hiding place without pausing. But at the bend of the road, the lead rider reined in, turned in the saddle, and looked back down into the hollow — not searching, precisely. More like a man who hears, in a familiar song, one note played wrong.

Then he rode on, north, toward the river. Toward the crossing. Toward Weeping Ford, and the boy who hummed songs he didn't know the names of.

Gideon lay in the dark until the hoofbeats faded, and then she got up, shouldered her wrapped sword, and ran.
