## 8. The Inn at the End of the Map

The inn had no name, which was the point of it.

It stood where three roads met and none of them were official — a squat stone building with a slate roof furred in moss, leaning against the crossroads the way an old drunk leans against a wall he has known all his life. There was no sign hanging over the door, because a sign would have to say something, and saying things was dangerous out here at the ragged hem of the map. The locals called it the End of the Map, or sometimes just the End, and they said it the way sailors say *home* when home is a thing that might sink.

Gideon felt it before she saw it: a thinning in the air, like walking into a room where someone had just stopped crying. Places without law had their own weather. She had spent her whole life inside wards, inside silence, inside a valley that magic could not touch, and she had thought she knew what quiet was. She had been wrong. The valley's quiet had been a held breath. This was an exhale that had gone on so long everyone had forgotten what breathing was for.

"Three coppers gets you a room," Sefa said, patting the pouch at her belt. "Two if we don't ask about the stains."

"I'm going to ask about the stains."

"Don't." Sefa pushed open the door. "Some questions you can't unhear. I used to file them."

Inside, the common room was low-ceilinged and amber-lit, thick with woodsmoke and the smell of something stewing that had probably been stewing since the previous week. A dozen tables, half of them occupied by travelers who did not look at one another — that was the etiquette here, Gideon realized. Everyone had come from somewhere they couldn't name and was going somewhere they couldn't say. Looking too closely was a form of accusation.

The innkeep was a broad woman with forearms like hams and a face that had decided long ago to be surprised by nothing. She took their coins, poured two cups of wine so bad it was almost a philosophical statement, and jerked her chin toward a corner table near the hearth.

"You'll want the corner," she said. "Walls on two sides. And the tall one—" she nodded at Gideon's sword, wrapped in its travel-sacking but unmistakably the shape of a sword, "—keeps it under the table or takes it upstairs. House rule."

"It stays wrapped," Gideon said.

"They all say that." The innkeep moved off to terrorize another table.

They sat. Sefa pulled her stolen ledgers from her satchel and arranged them with the unconscious tenderness of a woman handling sleeping children, then caught herself doing it and shoved them under her elbow as though embarrassed. Gideon drank the wine and decided not to taste it. Outside, the starless dark pressed against the windows like a hand.

That was when the boy came over.

* * *

He was maybe seventeen, all elbows and restless hands, with his grandfather's ferryman's knot tied around his wrist in fraying red cord. He stood at their table holding a bowl of stew he clearly hadn't paid for, and he looked at Gideon the way people look at a door they've been told is a wall.

"You're from across the water," he said. Not a question.

Gideon set down her cup slowly. "I don't know what you mean."

"The way you sit." He pointed with his spoon. "Back straight, feet flat, left side to the door. Ferry folk sit like that. My gran said it's because you're always ready to push off." His voice cracked slightly on the last word, and he hated himself for it; Gideon watched him hate himself for it. "You're one of the Oathkin. From before."

The word landed in Gideon's chest like a stone dropped down a well. *Oathkin.* No one had called her people that in thirty years. No one alive should have known the word at all.

"Your grandmother," Gideon said carefully, "was from—"

"She never said. That was the whole point of never saying." The boy sat down uninvited, which told Gideon everything about how this table worked now. "She died in spring. But before she died she made me learn the knots. All of them. Said somebody had to carry them even if nobody ever asked to see them again." He pushed up his sleeve and turned his wrist. The red cord was tied in a pattern Gideon knew — the traveler's promise, the knot you gave someone leaving on water, so that however far they went, the river would remember them home.

Her throat went tight. "Tam," Sefa said quietly, reading the boy's name off him somehow, or maybe he'd offered it. "I'm Sefa. This is Gideon."

"Gideon," Tam repeated, tasting it. Then, with the terrible directness of the young: "Is it true there's more of you? That the ledger lady found some?"

Sefa made a sound like a hiccup suppressed. "Ledger lady."

"That's you, isn't it? Ran off with Choir documents? The ferrymen talk. We talk more than anyone, my gran said, because we see everybody leave and nobody come back." He leaned in, lowering his voice to the conspiratorial register of someone who had never actually conspired before. "There's others at this inn tonight. People who heard the stars go out and started walking. You want to know who's who, watch their hands. Nobody out here eats bread without checking it first. Only people who've been hungry *on purpose* do that."

Gideon looked around the room with new eyes. The man by the window turning his heel of bread over twice before biting it. The woman in gray whose eyes kept flicking to the door on every third breath. The pair of travelers sharing one plate with the careful choreography of people who had learned portioning as survival.

"And you?" Gideon asked Tam. "Why aren't you home?"

Something closed behind his eyes. "Home's a quarry now," he said. "Has been since before I was born. Gran raised me on the ferry. When she died, the ferry license went to a cousin who doesn't tie the knots right." He shrugged, elaborately casual, fooling no one. "So. Here."

* * *

It was the woman in gray who came next, and she did not come to join them. She came to warn them.

Gideon saw her rise from her table by the fire, saw the way she moved — not like a fighter, exactly, but like someone who had once trained beside fighters and remembered the geometry of it. She crossed the room in a straight unhurried line and stopped at their table, and up close Gideon could see the details that didn't fit: the calluses on her fingertips in the wrong places for work, the faint pale band on her ring finger where a ring had been removed recently and deliberately, and — when she spoke — a voice that carried, effortlessly, the trained resonance of someone accustomed to being heard in large stone rooms.

"You're being followed," the woman said. "Two auditors took the east road yesterday. They travel light and they pay well, and paying well gets them information faster than threats would." She glanced at Sefa's satchel. "You'd be the clerk. The one who burned her credentials at Waymeet."

"How—" Sefa began.

"Burnt paper smells distinctive. So does ambition dying." The woman pulled out a chair and sat, uninvited, in perfect mirror of Tam. "Vess," she said. "Formerly Cantor Third Voice of the Choir of Quiet Men. Currently unemployed, disgraced, and drinking alone until approximately four minutes ago."

"Cantor," Gideon repeated. The word came out flat and cold. Every story she'd ever heard about the dissolutions had a cantor in it somewhere — the ones who sang the rites, who sang nations soft and small until they fit inside a ledger line. "You sang for them."

"Yes." Vess didn't flinch, and Gideon gave her a grudging point for that. "Eleven years. I can sing a ward that will turn an auditor's scrying aside for a night, and I can sing the rite that unmakes a country, and I have done both, and I am not going to pretend the second one cancels the first." She signaled the innkeep with two fingers — a gesture so institutional it made Sefa wince — and held up three fingers instead. Wine arrived. Bad wine. It seemed to be the house's only offering. "But you need me, and I need to not be found, and those two facts are going to have to be enough for tonight."

"We haven't agreed to anything," Gideon said.

"No," Vess agreed. She drank, grimaced honestly, and set the cup down. "Tell me about the sword."

Gideon's hand moved to the wrapped hilt before she could stop it. "What about it?"

"It woke when the stars went out. Don't bother looking surprised; every ex-Choir within a hundred miles felt it, like a bell ringing underwater. Swordspeaker oaths were bound to the sky, you know. That was the whole architecture of your people's magic — sworn upward, witnessed by stars. When the Choir put the stars out—" Vess paused, and for the first time something moved behind her composure, something that looked uncomfortably like shame, "—we assumed that killed the oaths. We wrote it down that it killed the oaths. We were very thorough about writing it down." Her mouth twisted. "We were wrong, weren't we?"

Around the table, silence. Tam had gone very still, his red-corded wrist pressed flat to the wood as if anchoring himself. Sefa was watching Gideon with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"It woke," Gideon said finally. "First time in thirty years. It sang." She hadn't told anyone that part. The sword had sung, one note, low and patient, the night the first star died — and she had lain awake listening to it and understood that everything she had believed about her own life was a room with a false wall in it. "And then it went quiet again. Like it was waiting."

"Swords wait," Vess said. "It's most of what they do." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "Here's what I know that you don't, and why you need me. The Door of Return — yes, I know that's where you're headed, the clerk's ledgers fairly scream it — wasn't just sealed. It was *sung shut*. Chains of written law laid over it in cantor's voice, verse by verse, each seal anchored to a dissolution. To open it, someone has to unsing what was sung. And the Choir keeps very careful records of who sang what." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and tired and old in a young face. "I sang three of the seals myself. I'm the only person alive who knows all three melodies from the inside."

"Why tell us?" Gideon asked. "Why defect? What did they take from *you*?"

For a moment Vess looked like she wouldn't answer. Then: "A name," she said. "Mine. Cantors surrender their family names when they take voice — it's supposed to be devotion. I told myself that for eleven years." She smiled without any warmth in it at all. "Last month I filed the dissolution order for a village of two hundred souls. Standard procedure. And in the margin of the census, in my own handwriting, I wrote the village's name — the true one, the struck one — because I wanted to see it written somewhere, even once, even in ink that would be burned after filing." She turned the cup again. "They found the margin. Disgrace is the gentle version of what happened next."

* * *

The wine got worse as the night went on, which shouldn't have been possible, and yet.

Somewhere past midnight the common room emptied to stragglers and the fire burned down to a red heart, and the four of them sat in their corner with the maps and fragments spread between them like pieces of different puzzles that kept almost fitting. Sefa's ledgers, open to the pages she'd shown no one else: the census of a dissolved nation, columns of names reduced to asset categories. Tam's knots, laid out in a row on the scarred wood, each one a word in a language of cord. Vess humming under her breath, barely audible, testing a melody against memory. And Gideon's sword, unwrapped at last, lying plain and patient on the table while the firelight ran along its blade like water finding its level.

"So the truth comes apart into us," Sefa said. She had her clerk's pen out and was sketching connections on the back of a discarded menu — lines between names, dates, seals, stars. "She has the records. He has the roads and the river-people. You have the voice and the guilt. And Gideon has—" she gestured at the sword, at Gideon, at the whole improbable fact of her, "—the last oath."

"And you have the keys," Gideon said. "Every lock the Choir built, you know how it thinks."

"I know how it *files*," Sefa corrected. "Thinking is different. Filing is worse." She chewed the end of the pen. "If we pool it — really pool it — we might actually have enough to find the Door. Which means we have to decide to trust each other, which is insane, because we've known each other for six hours and two of us are criminals, one of us is a weapon, and one of us sang funerals for countries."

"Three criminals," Tam said. "I stole this stew."

"You *did* steal that stew," Sefa said, with real respect.

Vess laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering motion — and something in the room shifted a degree. Gideon felt it happen and distrusted it and let it happen anyway. This was how it must have worked, she thought, in the old days. Before. Found people, finding each other. Her elders had spoken of oath-bands the way other folk spoke of weather: inevitable, recurring, necessary. She had assumed that part of the world had ended with the stars.

"Rules," she said aloud, and they all looked at her. "If we're doing this. One: nobody lies to the group. Lie to the Choir, lie to innkeeps, lie to your own reflection if it helps, but not here. Two: nobody decides alone who pays a price. Not for the Door, not for anything. Three—" she touched the sword's flat, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, "—when it wakes again, we find out together what it wants."

"Four," Tam said. "Nobody ties the knots wrong. If you're going to learn them, learn them right." He was already reaching across the table, already pulling a length of cord from his pocket, already, without asking, tying the traveler's promise around Sefa's wrist — the river-remembering knot, the come-home knot. Sefa stared at it as if it might bite. Then her face did something complicated, and she covered it by adjusting the cord half a millimeter and declaring it acceptable.

Vess held out her own wrist without being asked. Tam tied it slower for her, and neither of them said anything about why, and that was its own kind of honesty.

Outside, the starless dark pressed at the windows. Inside, the fire settled, and the wine sat in its cups like a shared hardship, and four strangers who were no longer entirely strangers sat in the corner with walls at their backs.

It was Sefa who broke the quiet, because of course it was. She had gone back to her ledgers, hunting some thread, and suddenly her pen stopped moving.

"Gideon," she said slowly. "This entry. Dated the night the stars went out. There's an audit team logged at the border of your valley — arrival pending. Two auditors." She looked up. "The ones Vess warned us about. They're not following us."

She turned the ledger around so the firelight fell across the page.

"They're waiting for us to come to them. Every road to the Door runs through Ashfall."
