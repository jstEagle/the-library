## 10. The Price of a True Name

The fire was small, because small fires were harder to see, and Gideon had spent her whole life learning what could be seen.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath a collapsed watchtower — one of the old border towers, its stones furred with moss and its purpose forgotten by everyone except people like her. The starless sky pressed down close and black, so thick with absence that it felt less like night and more like a lid. No stars. Not one. She kept catching herself looking up anyway, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

Vess had sung a hush-ward around the camp, her voice low and frayed at the edges, and now she sat apart from the flames with her hands folded, as if she were still waiting for permission to be near them. Tam had fallen asleep sitting up twice and been woken twice by Sefa, who did not sleep so much as hover beside it, ledgers stacked on her knees like a breastplate.

Gideon turned the sword across her lap. It had not spoken since the valley. It lay quiet as a bone, but she could feel it listening.

"I'll go to the Door," she said.

No one answered at first. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a nightbird made a sound like a hinge.

"You heard Vess," Gideon went on. "A true name has to be spoken aloud where the Door stands. My country's name. And whoever speaks it—" She stopped, because saying it plainly made it real, and there was a part of her that wanted to keep it soft and deniable for one more hour. "Whoever speaks it dies. That's the price. So it's mine to pay. It's my name. My country. I've been the last of everything else; I can be the last of this too."

"No." Sefa said it immediately, flatly, the way she corrected an entry in a ledger. "Absolutely not."

Gideon looked up. The clerk's face was pale in the firelight, her jaw set in that stubborn line Gideon had begun, against all sense, to rely on.

"It's not yours alone," Sefa said. "That's the flaw in your arithmetic. You keep counting yourself as the only remainder. But you're not."

"Sefa—"

"Let me finish." Sefa pulled the topmost ledger onto her knees and opened it, though Gideon suspected she didn't need to read from it. She had memorized it the way other people memorized prayers. "I've been carrying these books since I ran. Do you know why I stole them? Not for you. Not at first. I stole them because I found my own family in them."

The fire cracked. Tam had gone still, pretending not to listen, failing completely.

"Third census cycle, Province Reorganization 44," Sefa said. Her voice was steady the way ice is steady. "The village of Ashenmere, dissolved under the Rivers and Boundaries Act. Population: two hundred eleven. Assets forfeit: listed by name. My mother's loom is in there. Item four hundred six. My father's boat. Item three hundred ninety-nine. My brother—" She stopped. Turned a page she didn't look at. "My brother is item five hundred twelve. He was nine years old. They recorded him as an asset, Gideon. A quantity of labor. And then the village was 'resettled,' which is the word they use when they mean emptied, and no one ever told me where he went, because the ledger doesn't say. Ledgers never say. They only count."

She closed the book with a small, precise violence.

"So don't tell me this death belongs to you. I have been dying by inches in their filing system for twenty years. If someone must speak a true name into that arch, let it be someone who has already been erased once. Let them at least fail to erase me twice."

"No," Gideon said, and heard how much of her father was in her voice. "You're the only one who can unpick the seals. Vess said it — the chains are written law, and only a clerk's hand knows their grammar. You open the Door. I speak through it. That's the order of things."

"Then we argue about the order later," Sefa said. "Tonight the answer is no."

* * *

Tam spoke up from the far side of the fire, and his voice was different than usual — stripped of the ferry-boy's easy grin he wore like a borrowed coat.

"My gran used to say the Choir never kills anyone," he said. "That's the trick of it. They just make it so you were never born. Easier paperwork." He poked the fire with a stick, watching sparks climb toward a sky that swallowed them. "My country had a name too. Nobody says it anymore. I barely remember it myself, and that's the worst part — I can feel it going, like water through fingers. There was a word my mother sang when the river flooded. I know the tune. I can't find the word anywhere inside me anymore. It's just a hole shaped like a word."

He looked up, and in the firelight his young face was suddenly old.

"If speaking a true name costs a life, then fine. But whose? Yours, because you're the last official one? That's just letting the ledger decide again. That's doing their math for them." He shrugged, awkward, defiant. "I'm not offering to die. I'm saying if you go alone, you're letting them win even when you beat them. Alone is their favorite way for us to be."

Gideon felt the words land somewhere under her ribs and stay there.

* * *

It was Vess who moved last. She had been silent so long Gideon thought she might not speak at all tonight. But she rose, crossed the space between her solitude and the fire, and sat down among them for the first time — actually among them, knees nearly touching Tam's, close enough to feel the warmth.

"I should tell you something before anyone volunteers anything," Vess said. "And after I say it, you may all be right to leave me here."

Sefa's eyes narrowed. Tam went very still.

"I was a cantor," Vess said. "Not disgraced then. Decorated. I sang wards over three provinces. And thirty-one years ago, I was sent to a valley between two mountains, where the wards were old and the people were stubborn, and I stood at the border stones and I sang the dissolution rite." Her hands, Gideon noticed, had begun to tremble. She did not try to hide them. "I believed it was mercy. That's the unforgivable part — not cruelty. Belief. I was told the valley would suffer less if it ended quietly, and I believed it, and I sang, and the silence came down like snowfall, and I went home to a commendation."

The fire burned. Gideon could not move. Could not breathe around the shape of it. Thirty-one years. The year the songs stopped working. The year her grandmother stopped singing at the hearth and started only humming, and would never say why.

"The valley's name," Gideon said. Her voice came out low and terrible. "Say it."

"I can't." Vess met her eyes, and there was no flinching in her, only ruin. "Don't you understand? That's what the rite does. It takes the name out of the singer too. I have a hole shaped like a word, same as Tam. I've had it for thirty-one years. I wake up some mornings reaching for it." She drew a breath. "So when I tell you the Door needs a true name, understand that I am the reason true names are rare. I unmade them. Professionally. Beautifully. They gave me a sash."

"Gideon." Sefa's hand was on her arm, light as a warning bell.

Gideon looked at the sword across her knees. It had gone warm. Not speaking — not yet — but warm, the way a stone holds the day's sun into evening. The oath in it, the old oath her grandmother had bound into the steel before Gideon was born, seemed to be paying attention.

"You sang my valley silent," Gideon said slowly. "And now you sing wards to keep us alive on the road. Why?"

"Because I heard the silence I made," Vess said. "Every night for thirty-one years. A cantor hears what she sings forever — it lives behind the ears. And underneath your valley's silence, there was something still holding. One thread that wouldn't break. I told myself it was nothing. An echo. A flaw in the rite." She looked at the sword. "It wasn't a flaw. It was you. A child the rite couldn't reach, because the oath was already in her blood. I've been following that thread my whole life without knowing its name. When I saw your oath-knot at the inn, I knew. I knew and I got in the cart anyway."

The anger in Gideon did not go away. She doubted it would ever entirely go away. But beside it, unexpectedly, sat something else — the grim recognition of a fellow carrier. Two women hauling the same dead weight up the same long road.

"You'll come to the Door," Gideon said at last. "And you'll sing the seals' counter-melody when Sefa breaks them. And after that, we'll see whether forgiveness is a thing that exists. I make no promises tonight."

"That's more than I hoped for," Vess said quietly. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet."

* * *

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time the argument circled back — because it did circle back, because none of them would let it rest.

"Then it's settled in part," Sefa said. "None of us goes alone. Whatever happens at the arch, we stand there together. All four."

"All four," Tam echoed, and held out his hand over the fire, palm down, the way ferrymen sealed a crossing-price.

Sefa put her hand over his. Then Vess, hesitating a heartbeat, laid hers atop theirs — and Gideon looked at the three hands stacked above the embers, a clerk's ink-stained fingers, a boy's rope-calloused palm, a cantor's trembling hand, and understood that she was looking at something the Choir had no column for. Something that could not be counted, because it had never been an asset. Something freely given.

She put her hand on top.

For a moment nobody spoke. Above them the sky stayed black and blind, but it seemed less like a lid now and more like a held breath — the whole heavens waiting to hear what they would do.

"We sleep in shifts," Gideon said, and her voice was rougher than she meant it to be. "Salt flats are three days east. And Sefa — teach me those seals on the road. If anything happens to you, I need to know the grammar."

"You'll hate the grammar," Sefa said. "Everyone hates the grammar."

"Good. Let it cost something."

Tam curled up by the fire with his back against the fallen tower-stone, and within minutes he was asleep in earnest, young again, his face slack and unguarded. Vess took first watch, sitting upright with her hands loose in her lap, and when Gideon glanced over she saw the cantor's lips moving silently — rehearsing, perhaps, or repenting, or both.

Gideon lay back and looked up into the starless dark until her eyes ached.

Three days, then. Three days to the salt flat, to the chained arch, to the price. She had volunteered to die the way she had done everything else in her life — alone, competently, without fuss. And in one night these three strangers had taken even that from her, refusing her the dignity of a solitary grave, insisting on crowding her death the way they now crowded her fire.

It should have felt like theft. Instead, lying there listening to Tam's breathing and Sefa's pages turning and Vess's silent rehearsal, Gideon felt something she had not felt since the stars went out.

Accompanied.

She was almost asleep when Sefa spoke softly from across the ashes.

"Gideon. The eastern horizon. Look."

Gideon rolled onto her elbow. Far off, past the broken tower, past the dark hills — where the salt flats lay — the sky was not entirely black.

There was a faint gray shimmer there, thin as breath on glass. And beneath it, so distant it might have been imagination, a sound was coming across the night: many voices, layered and formal, moving in unison.

Singing.

"They're not waiting for us," Sefa whispered. "The Choir is already at the Door."
