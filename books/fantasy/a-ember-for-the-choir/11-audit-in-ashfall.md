## 11. Audit in Ashfall

The town was called Merrow's Crossing on the maps Sefa carried, though the people who lived there called it nothing at all anymore, because most of them were running.

Gideon smelled the smoke before she saw the flames. It came rolling down the river road in a brown wall, thick with tar and thatch and something sweeter underneath — burned sugar, maybe, from the candlers' row — and beneath it all a smell she knew from her valley's oldest stories: the smell of wards failing. Ozone and old iron and the particular cold of a promise breaking.

"Raiders?" Tam asked, shifting the ferry-pole across his shoulders like a yoke. He was sixteen and had never seen a fire larger than a cookstove's, and he was walking toward this one because Gideon was walking toward it, which was either loyalty or foolishness or both braided together so tightly they could not be told apart.

"Not raiders." Sefa had stopped dead in the road. Her clerk's satchel hung open at her hip, and her hand rested inside it the way other people's hands rested on sword-hilts. "Raiders burn what they want to take. This is different. Look at the pattern."

Gideon looked. The fires were not scattered the way panic scatters fire. They stood in a ring around the market square, evenly spaced, like candles on an altar, and inside that ring the smoke rose straight up into the starless sky in a column so orderly it might have been drawn with a rule. Whatever was happening in Merrow's Crossing, it was happening *deliberately*.

"That's an audit," Sefa said quietly. "That's what an audit looks like when it goes wrong."

"And when it goes right?"

"You don't see anything at all. That's rather the point."

Vess had gone pale — paler than her usual paper-white, which Gideon had learned meant the cantor was listening to something no one else could hear. "There are wards down," she said. "Town wards. They were sung by someone trained in the Choir's third style, and they've been unwound, not broken. Unwinding is polite. Breaking is honest." She wrapped her arms around herself as if against a cold that had nothing to do with the season. "I know this work. I've done this work."

Gideon did not answer that. There was nothing between them yet that could survive an answer to that.

They went in anyway. That was the thing about found families, Gideon was learning: they did not make you braver, exactly. They just made it impossible to be brave alone, and somehow that was worse and better at once.

* * *

Sefa counted doors as they moved through the burning streets. It was an old habit, the first thing they taught you in the Clerk's Ladder: *a household is a number before it is a family, and if you can hold the number you can hold the truth at arm's length.* Forty-one doors on the candler's row, thirty-eight of them standing open, their owners fled or herded toward the square. Three nailed shut from the inside, and she made herself not think about what that meant.

The Choir's auditors worked in threes. Always threes — one to read, one to write, one to witness — because a dissolution performed by fewer than three was legally a murder, and the Choir had spent four hundred years making very sure that nothing it did was ever legally a murder.

She heard them before she saw them, in the square. Not shouting. Reading. A voice like dry paper unrolling:

"—by authority of the Second Table, Section Nine, the settlement formerly designated Merrow's Crossing, having failed to demonstrate continued economic viability, having failed to remit census updates for two consecutive cycles, and having been found to harbor persons of dissolved provenance—"

*Dissolved provenance.* Sefa's stomach turned over. She had written those words herself, eleven hundred times, in a hand so neat her supervisors had used it as the exemplar for new clerks. *Dissolved provenance.* As if people were vinegar. As if nations were fruit left too long in the cellar.

She rounded the corner of the grain-marshal's office and saw the square entire, and the sight of it rearranged something in her chest that she suspected would never sit right again.

The townsfolk knelt in rows. Perhaps two hundred of them, hands bound not with rope but with ribbons of official vellum, each strip inscribed with their own name and census-number, because the Choir held that a person bound by their own recorded identity could not claim coercion. The auditors stood among them in dove-grey robes, unhurried, spotless amid the ash, and the tallest of them held a ledger open on a reading-stand while his colleagues fed documents into a bronze brazier that burned with a flame of absolutely no color at all.

And kneeling apart from the townsfolk, bound in the same grey ribbon, were Gideon, Tam, and Vess.

Sefa's whole body went cold. Then it went hot. Then it went very, very still, the way it went in the years before she ran, when she had learned that stillness was the only weapon a clerk was issued.

They hadn't seen her. The lead auditor was still reading, and Gideon — Gideon was looking at him with an expression Sefa had only seen once before, on the night the stars went out, when the dead elders' voices came back through the broken wards and demanded answers of her. It was not fear. It was the look of a woman measuring the distance between what she wanted to do and what she would let herself do, and finding the distance very short.

Sefa slipped back behind the grain-marshal's office and opened her satchel.

Her credentials were in there. Eleven years of the Clerk's Ladder folded into a wallet of grey leather: her warrant, her seals, her letters of commission, the little stamped chits that could halt an audit mid-sentence or redirect one entirely. She had carried them out of the Choir's archive the same night she carried out the ledgers, and she had told herself since that she'd keep them because they might be useful.

She took them out now and looked at them, and understood that she had kept them for a different reason. She had kept them because as long as she held them, some part of her was still inside the walls. Still safe. Still one of the quiet men.

The colorless flame flickered in the brazier. Somewhere in the square, a child began to cry, and an auditor said, in a tone of mild administrative regret, "Silence will be noted."

Sefa walked around the corner and into the square, holding her credentials up high, and said in her best reading-room voice, "Section Nine requires notice to the district provost before any finding of harboring. Where is your notice?"

Every dove-grey head turned. And Sefa, runaway clerk, thief of ledgers, traitor to the only institution she had ever belonged to, began to lie to the Choir with her own name in her mouth.

* * *

The auditor's name was Prelate-Ombudsman Corvane, Third Chair of the Standing Committee on Provenance, and he had the face of a man who had been handsome once and had then read too many documents.

He received Sefa's interruption the way a stone receives rain. He listened to her citation of Section Nine, thanked her for her diligence, verified her seal against a token he drew from his sleeve, and then smiled — a small, precise smile, like a paper cut.

"Clerk Second-Rank Sefarina Oduya," he said. "Recorded missing from the Central Archive on the ninth of the Dying Moon. Warrant for retrieval outstanding." He turned the page of his ledger without hurry. "You are not here to audit, Clerk. You are here to be audited. Please kneel with the others."

And that should have been the end of it. Sefa felt the square tilt; felt two hundred frightened strangers watch her authority die in a single sentence; felt Tam's young face crumple beside Gideon's immovable one. She had bought them perhaps forty seconds, and forty seconds was not enough, and somewhere under the terror a small clerk's voice in her head was already drafting her own dissolution notice in flawless script.

But Gideon Quintana laughed.

It was not a big laugh. It was low and rough and terrible, the laugh of a woman who had buried every elder in her valley and dug the graves herself, and it stopped the square the way a dropped glass stops a dinner.

"Read it to me," Gideon said.

Corvane paused. "I beg your pardon?"

"The offer. You have one. I can smell it on you — you've been carrying it since the border stones, your people and mine both, everyone chasing us has been carrying it like a love letter. So stop feeding papers to your pretty fire and read me the offer, Prelate-Ombudsman, because I want to hear it said aloud in front of all these witnesses." She lifted her chin at the kneeling townsfolk. "They're bound by their own names, you say? Good. Then let them hear what their names are worth."

For the first time, something moved behind Corvane's eyes. It might have been respect. In the Choir, it was hard to tell respect from appetite.

"As you wish," he said, and closed the ledger, and produced instead a single document in a folder of white leather, white being the color the Choir reserved for gifts. "By provision of the Restoration Accord, annex seven, the nation formerly designated—" and here he pronounced a name.

Sefa heard Gideon's breath stop.

It was the first time anyone outside the valley had spoken that name in Sefa's hearing. It was the first time, she realized, watching Gideon's face, that anyone had spoken it aloud to *her* in thirty years. The name landed in the burning square like a stone into deep water, and the ripples went out through everything — through the kneeling townsfolk who didn't know what it meant but knew what it cost, through Tam, through Vess, whose lips moved silently around the syllables as if tasting them, through Sefa herself, who had struck that name from nine thousand documents with her own careful hand and had told herself every time that ink was only ink.

"The nation formerly designated that name," Corvane continued smoothly, "is hereby offered reinstatement as a Protected Heritage Province of the Concord, with full territorial boundaries restored per the original survey, its language granted second-class official status, its material culture preserved under permanent curatorial stewardship, and its surviving descendants enrolled in the Register of Living Exhibits, with guaranteed housing, rations, and protection in perpetuity."

He let the silence do its work. It was good work. Sefa could feel it pulling at everyone — the promise of a name given back, a map redrawn, a home returned. Even she felt the pull, and she knew what lived underneath the white leather.

"In exchange," Corvane said, "the last swordspeaker surrenders her oath-blade to the Archive, ceases her travels, and consents to serve as First Curator of her own people. You would be, in effect, the keeper of your nation's memory." His smile deepened by a degree. "A great honor. Many dissolved peoples have begged for less."

"I know," Gideon said. "I've met them. On the road. Under borrowed names." She got to her feet — the ribbon binding her wrists parted like it had never been tied, and Sefa would learn later that Vess had been singing it loose under her breath for ten minutes, noteless, hidden inside a cough — and stood facing the auditor with the ash drifting down around her like grey snow.

"My mother wove baskets," Gideon said. "Not for exhibits. For carrying things. My father sang the planting songs, and if you'd asked him why, he'd have said *so the barley knows it's wanted*, not *for the preservation of intangible heritage*. My grandmother cursed magnificently in a language you've classified as extinct, and she'd have risen out of her grave to curse you for that word alone." She took one step forward. "You're offering to give my country back as a diorama. You'll dust it. You'll put little ropes in front of it. Children from your Concord will come and look at my people behind glass and learn how we *used* to live, and my descendants will stand in their own fields wearing their own clothes and be *objects*, forever, politely, with rations."

"It is more than the dead received," Corvane said.

"Yes," Gideon said softly. "It is. That's the trap of it, isn't it? That's how you've always done it. You wait until people are so broken they'll take a cage with their name painted on the door, and then you call it mercy, and then you write the mercy down, and then the writing is the only history anyone has." Her hand went to her hip, where the oath-sword hung — awake now, awake since the night the stars went out, humming against her palm like a held breath. "My elders taught me what the oath is for. It isn't for vengeance. It isn't even for justice, though it's been bent to both. It's for *witness*. A swordspeaker stands where the truth is being erased and says: I saw. I remember. It mattered."

She drew the blade.

The sound it made was not the sound of steel. It was the sound of a word being spoken after thirty years of silence, and every ward in Merrow's Crossing — unwound, polite, dying — flared once with borrowed light, and the colorless fire in the brazier guttered like a candle in a doorway.

"So no," Gideon said, and the sword-point rose until it rested, light as a falling leaf, an inch from the white leather folder. "I won't be your First Curator. I won't enroll my people in any register. And I will speak my country's name myself, freely, where and when I choose — and when I do, Prelate-Ombudsman, I suggest you listen, because it won't be a petition. It will be testimony."

Corvane looked at the blade. He looked at Gideon. And Sefa, watching, saw him arrive at the understanding that would define everything that came after: that this woman could not be filed, could not be shelved, could not be dissolved, because dissolution required consent on paper and she would never sign, and force required soldiers and the Choir had none, only clerks.

"Then you leave us no procedural recourse," he said, and there was something almost gentle in it, almost sad. "The audit will proceed. Merrow's Crossing will be dissolved for harboring. Your companions will be retrieved. And you, swordspeaker, will be recorded as *unresolved*." He said the word the way other men said *damned*. "Unresolved entries are reviewed annually. Some remain unresolved for centuries. Ask your dead how they enjoyed waiting."

The dove-grey robes began to move, unhurried, closing like a ledger's covers.

And Vess stood up.

She stood up out of the ash with her ruined cantor's throat bare to the smoke, and she began to sing — not the Choir's third style, not the polite unwinding, but the old counter-style, the forbidden one, the one that *builds* — and the nearest burning building shuddered as its death-fires met a wall of sound.

"Vess, no," Sefa breathed, because she knew what song that was. Every clerk knew. It was the song that took down wards — and Vess was aiming it not at the Choir, but at the town's own protective singing, the last shield between two hundred kneeling strangers and whatever came after the dove-grey robes finished their paperwork.

Gideon turned, and her eyes met the cantor's, and in them Sefa saw the question that would split them all:

What price was a town's safety, weighed against two hundred lives already kneeling in the fire?

Vess sang louder, and the first ward-thread snapped like a harp string breaking, and somewhere beyond the ring of flames, in the dark hills where the raiders waited for exactly this music, torches began to answer.
