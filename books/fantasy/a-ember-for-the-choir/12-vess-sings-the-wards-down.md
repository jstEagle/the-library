## 12. Vess Sings the Wards Down

The wards of Ashfall were old, older than the Choir's writ in that province, and they had been sung by someone who loved the town. Vess could hear it in the harmonics — a woman's voice, low and patient, laid into the stones of the gatehouse like mortar between bricks. *Keep them safe. Keep them warm. Let no blade cross but with blessing.* The kind of ward-song Vess herself had once written, back when she believed the Choir's work was mercy.

She stood in the shadow of the granary with her palms flat against the wall, feeling the song hum through the plaster, and hated herself with a completeness she had not known since the day they took her cantor's stole.

Behind her, the market still burned. Not all of it — the auditors had been precise, as the Choir was always precise — but the dyers' row was gone, and the tannery, and the house where an old man had pressed a heel of bread into Tam's hands not two hours before. The smoke lay over the town like a second sky, and beneath that sky there were no stars at all, only the orange glow of what the Choir called *an administrative correction*.

"They're regrouping at the well," Sefa said, sliding down beside her. Her clerk's satchel was gone; she'd burned the last of her credentials in the inn's hearth three nights ago, and without them she moved through the world like a woman missing a limb, reaching for documents that no longer existed. "Four auditors. Two sword-wardens. They've posted a dissolution notice on the chapel door."

"A notice." Vess laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "They're going to dissolve the town."

"They're going to *begin* the process. There'll be hearings. Proclamations nailed to doors. In six months Ashfall will be a name on a list, and in ten years no one will remember it was ever anything else." Sefa's voice was flat, reciting. She had read enough ledgers to know how erasure sounded from the inside. "Unless we're gone, and they follow us instead. They want Gideon. We're the distraction keeping their attention here."

Vess closed her eyes. She knew what Sefa was going to say next. She had known since the well, when she'd felt the town's wards shiver under the auditors' surveying instruments — felt them the way you feel a hand close around your throat.

"You can sing them down," Sefa said. "Can't you."

It was not a question. Sefa had been a census clerk for eleven years; she knew exactly what Vess had been, and what that meant she could do.

"The wards are anchored to the town's heartstone," Vess said. "Under the chapel. If I unmake the anchor-song, every ward in Ashfall falls at once. The gates. The firewards on the thatch. The river-barrier that keeps the spring floods off the low quarter." She swallowed. "And the peace-bonding that keeps the hill clans out of the valley. It's been up for two hundred years. The clans remember what the valley looks like unguarded."

"Raiders," Sefa said quietly.

"Within a season. Maybe within a month. Word travels fast when a town goes dark." Vess opened her eyes and looked at her hands, at the faint silver scarring across the fingertips where the Choir had burned out her cantor's marks when they stripped her rank. The scars ached now, the way they always did when she thought about singing. "Two thousand people live here, Sefa."

"And if we stay, the Choir dissolves it anyway — slowly, legally, with hearings — and takes Gideon in chains to the Hall of Reconciliation, where she will be filed until she dies of filing." Sefa's jaw tightened. "I spent eleven years writing those lists. I know exactly how a town dies on paper. It is not gentler than fire. It is just quieter, and it takes longer, and no one gets to be angry about it because every step was *approved*."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Sefa admitted. "It isn't. But it's the choice we have."

They stood in silence while somewhere across the square a horse screamed and was quieted. The granary wall hummed against Vess's palms, that patient loving voice singing its two-hundred-year-old lullaby, and Vess thought: *I did this before. I stood in a valley with my hands raised and I sang a nation into forgetting, and I told myself it was mercy, that dissolution was kinder than conquest, that a people erased peacefully suffered less than a people conquered bloodily.* She had believed it for thirty years. She had built her whole penance on believing it.

And now the Choir wanted her to do it again — or rather, they wanted her to do nothing, and their doing would be so much worse.

"I need to tell Gideon," Vess said.

"She already knows what you're considering. She's been watching you touch walls all evening like a widow touching a coffin."

---

Gideon stood at the mouth of the alley behind the chandler's shop, oath-sword drawn and resting point-down against the cobbles, both hands folded over the pommel the way she stood when she was thinking — or praying, though she'd have denied it. The firelight moved on her face and made her look carved, ancient, like one of the stone elders in the valley shrine.

Tam hovered near her with the ferryman's iron hook clutched in both fists, too young for the look on his face.

"Well?" Gideon said, without turning.

Vess told her. All of it. The heartstone, the anchor-song, the fall of the wards, the clans, the season of ruin that would follow. She said it plainly, because Gideon deserved plainness, and because dressing it up would have been the cantor's habit, the manipulator's habit, and Vess was trying — had been trying for thirty years — to stop being that person.

When she finished, Gideon was silent for a long time. Around them the town made its small terrified noises: shutters, footsteps, a child crying somewhere and being hushed.

"You sang my valley down," Gideon said at last. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table between them, the way you'd place a knife.

"Yes."

"And now you're asking me to let you do it again. To another town. To people who never did anything to us."

"I'm not asking your permission. I'm telling you what I can do, and letting you decide whether we do it." Vess's voice cracked on the last word and she steadied it. "That's the difference. That's all the difference I have left."

Gideon turned then, and looked at her, and Vess endured the looking. It would have been easier to be struck.

"If we run without singing the wards," Gideon said slowly, "the auditors follow us, catch us inside a week — I've seen how they move since the border fell — and Ashfall is dissolved anyway, with paperwork, with hearings, with its name struck off the maps while the ink is still wet. And I'm gone, and the Door stays sealed, and everyone who ever lost a country stays lost forever." She looked down at her sword. "If we sing the wards down, we escape, and Ashfall burns slower. Different fire. Same ash."

"Yes."

"And you'd carry that. Another valley. Another set of names."

"I'd carry it," Vess said. "I've been carrying the first ones for thirty years. What's the weight of one more town on top of a nation?"

"It's not a nation," Gideon said, very quietly. "It's two thousand people who hung lanterns tonight because they thought the wards would hold."

Tam made a small sound. They all looked at him. He was staring at the burning glow above the rooftops, and when he spoke his voice was too steady, the steadiness of someone gripping something hard. "The ferryman — my grandfather — he used to say the Choir doesn't burn towns. It just closes the book on them. He said being forgotten is worse than being dead, because the dead get mourned." He looked at Vess. "If you sing it down, at least they get to be angry. At least they get to fight. My country didn't even get to fight."

"That's not comfort, boy," Vess said. "That's a curse wearing comfort's clothes."

"Maybe. But it's true."

Sefa spoke from the alley's mouth, where she kept watch. "Auditors are moving toward the chapel. They'll find the heartstone within the hour. When they do, they won't dissolve the wards — they'll *transfer* them, bind Ashfall's protections into the provincial seat, strip this town bare to fatten the capital. That's the new procedure. I helped draft it." Her voice caught on the admission. "So it's not wards-down versus wards-standing anymore. It's wards-down versus wards-stolen. Either way, Ashfall loses its song. The only question is whether we're still alive to grieve it properly."

Vess felt something in her chest go very still. *Wards-stolen.* Of course. The Choir had learned. After thirty years of dissolutions, after her own rite over Gideon's valley, they had refined the cruelty: no longer erasure but *extraction*, the slow harvesting of small places to feed large ones. It was almost elegant. It was exactly the kind of thing she might once have praised in a doctrinal seminar.

"Gideon," she said. "Decide. Whatever you decide, I'll do it. That's my penance — I don't get to choose anymore. I only get to serve."

"Don't put that on me." For the first time Gideon's composure slipped, and Vess saw underneath it: a tired woman in her fifties who had buried elders alone and tended wards alone and never once, in all those decades, been asked to spend other people's homes to buy her own road. "You don't get to hand me your conscience like a bundle and walk lighter. You sang my country down. You don't get to make me complicit in the next one to balance the ledger."

"Then what—"

"We leave," Gideon said. "Tonight. Through the south fields, before the auditors finish their survey. And the wards stand."

Sefa turned. "They'll track us by dawn. The sword alone—"

"I know what the sword does. I've carried it sixty years." Gideon lifted it, and the blade caught the firelight and threw it back red. "But hear me, all of you. I did not crawl out of a dead country to found a new one on top of another town's grave. If the price of the Door is Ashfall, then the Door can stay shut and the Choir can keep its starless sky, because I'd rather die landless than win like that." She sheathed the sword with a snap. "We go now. Quietly. And we pray the auditors are as bureaucratic as Sefa says, and waste the night on forms."

For one moment — one bright, foolish moment — Vess believed it would be enough.

---

They made the south fields by moonless midnight, four shadows among the winter wheat, and they might have made the treeline too, if the Choir's auditors had been merely men.

But the lead auditor had a cantor's ear. Vess felt him feel her — felt the moment his attention swept the field and snagged on the residue of her craft, the ghost of songs in her throat, the way a hound noses old blood. A lantern flared behind them. A voice, amplified by some instrument of office, rolled across the wheat:

"*By writ of the Provincial Consistory, the persons herein named are remanded for audit. Resist not; resistance shall be entered into record.*"

Then the wheat began to burn. Not wildly — precisely, in a line, a moving curtain of flame herding them back toward the town, toward the chapel, toward the heartstone the auditors had already reached. Vess heard it even from the field: the sound of the anchor-song being gripped by foreign hands, that patient loving voice forced into new harmonics, bent toward extraction. The whole town's protection groaning like a ship changing owners.

They ran. They ran and the fire-line ran with them, and at the edge of the burning wheat Gideon stopped, turned, and drew her sword, and Vess saw her begin to speak the oath — the real one, the waking one, the words that had relit something in her eyes the night the stars went out—

And Vess understood, in one horrible clarifying instant, what would happen. The oath would answer. The auditors would answer. The wheat-fire would spread past its lines into the granaries, the thatch, the town itself, and whatever else the night birthed, it would not be escape. It would be a battle fought in a dry field beside two thousand sleeping people, and battles did not stay where you put them.

There was no third option. There was only the thing she had sworn never to do again, and the thing she had sworn to protect, and between them, a few hundred yards of burning wheat.

*I'm sorry,* she told the town, told the woman whose lullaby still lived in the gatehouse stones, told the child crying behind the shutters. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I was a cantor before I was anything, and this is the only prayer I know.*

She stepped forward, planted her scarred fingers against the scorched earth, and sang.

She sang the unmaking. Not the crude severance the Choir taught — she had more artistry than that, damn them, damn her — but the true rite, the gentle one, the one that lets a song lie down rather than tearing it out. She took the anchor-song's harmony into her own throat and carried it downward, note by note, into rest, the way you'd ease a dying friend from pain into sleep. The wards of Ashfall did not shatter. They *exhaled*. All across the town Vess felt them release — the gates, the firewards, the river-barrier, the two-hundred-year peace-bonding — felt them go out like lamps at the end of a festival, willingly, because she asked them kindly, because that was the cruelest part, that she was good at this, that she had always been good at this.

The wheat-fire died mid-flame, starved of the ward-energy that fed it. The auditors' grip on the heartstone closed on emptiness. And in the sudden dark and silence, four figures slipped through the blackened wheat into the treeline, and ran, and were not followed, because the Choir did not chase what it had already begun to process.

Ashfall was now, officially, unwritten. Unprotected. A name waiting for its list.

No one spoke for the first mile. Then Tam stopped walking, turned around, and looked back at the distant town — dark now, truly dark, its wards gone, its lullaby ended — and Vess watched him understand what she had done, watch it arrive in his face like weather.

"You said the wards would stand," he said to Gideon. His voice shook. "You decided. You said—"

"And then the fire changed the arithmetic," Sefa said quickly. "Tam—"

"He *asked* us!" Tam whirled on Vess, and the hook trembled in his grip. "He gave us bread! He had a face! You sang his whole town into the dark and we just — we *ran*—" His voice broke. "My grandfather said being forgotten is worse than being dead. You forgot them. You forgot them while they slept, so they wouldn't even know to say goodbye—"

"I eased them," Vess whispered. "I was gentle—"

"Gentle!" He laughed, and it was Gideon's laugh from earlier, jagged, inherited. "That's what the Choir says too, isn't it? Dissolution is mercy. Erasure is kindness. Where do you think they learned it? From cantors like you!"

"Enough." Gideon's voice cut like the sword coming between them, though the blade stayed sheathed. She stood facing Vess across three feet of trampled wheat-stubble, and her eyes in the dark were the eyes of the last swordspeaker of a dissolved nation, and they held no comfort at all. "She's right, boy. However much it costs us to hear it. Vess did to Ashfall what was done to my country. Smaller. Kinder. With love, even, if you can believe such a thing. And it changes nothing about what it *is*." She drew breath. "I told her not to. I chose the wards would stand. And then I stood in a burning field with my oath half-spoken and I knew — I *knew* — that if I finished speaking it, people would die who had nothing to do with any of this. So I let her. I stood aside and let her. Which makes it mine too. Write that in your ledger, Sefa. Both our names."

Sefa said nothing. She had taken out a scrap of charcoal and an old bill of sale, and her hand was shaking too badly to write.

"So this is the found family," Tam said bitterly, to no one. "Built out of a town's grave before it's even cold."

"No," Gideon said. "This is what a family looks like when it refuses to lie to itself. We are four people who have each done harm to survive, standing in the dark admitting it, and still walking the same road together — not because we deserve each other, but because the road is long and none of us should walk it alone." She looked at Vess one last time. "Come on. Dawn's in three hours and the clans will smell the open valley by noon."

They walked. Vess walked at the rear, and every step away from Ashfall was a step she felt in her scars, in her throat, in the hollow where her cantor's marks had been burned away. Behind her the town slept its first unprotected night, unaware, dreaming under a sky with no stars, and somewhere in the hills beyond it, she knew, watchers were already noting the darkness, counting spears, remembering old grievances.

At the treeline's edge she stopped once and looked back. She could not see the chapel. She could see, or imagined she could see, the place where the heartstone's song had been, and it was silent, silent, silent.

*I will come back,* she promised it, the way you promise the dead. *When the Door is open. When the names are spoken again. I will come back and sing your wards anew, better ones, ones that are yours and no one else's, and I will kneel in your chapel until you forgive me or until my knees fail, whichever comes first.*

The town did not answer. Towns never do.

Ahead, Gideon's silhouette paused on the ridge, waiting — not for her, exactly, but unwilling to lose her to the dark. Vess wiped her face with the back of her wrist and climbed.

In the east, far off, past the hills where the raiders would soon be moving, a single point of light trembled at the edge of the horizon. Not a star. Stars had been out for weeks. This was smaller, redder, closer — a light where no light should be, in the direction of the salt flats, in the direction of the Door.

It pulsed once, twice, like something breathing. Like something *waiting*.

And Vess, who had spent her life listening to what songs lay beneath the world, heard — faint, impossibly faint, carried on a wind that shouldn't have reached this far — a voice beneath it. Many voices. Singing.

The dead of every dissolved nation, perhaps. Or the Door itself, waking.

Or, most terrible of all possibilities, an invitation.

"Vess," Gideon called softly from the ridge. "What do you see?"

Vess looked at the pulsing light, and at the dark town behind her, and at the three ragged figures ahead of her who were, despite everything, despite tonight, still her family — and answered:

"I think," she said, "something out there has started singing back."
