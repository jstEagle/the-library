## 13. Tam's Country

Tam had not meant to bring them this way.

He told himself, for the first two days of the detour, that it was practicality. The low road through the quarry country was faster than the mountain pass, and the pass was watched — Choir surveyors had been crawling over those heights since autumn, chalking their little marks on stone like lichen. The low road was flat, unremarkable, and led nowhere anyone important cared about anymore.

That was the trouble with it. It led nowhere anyone important cared about anymore because everyone who had cared about it was dead.

He didn't say any of this. He just turned them off the pilgrim track at a cairn shaped like a sleeping dog and said, "Shortcut," in the cheerful voice he used for ferry passengers who were afraid of water, and Gideon looked at him for one long moment with those grey eyes that seemed to weigh everything he said against something older than himself, and then she nodded and followed him down.

Sefa walked beside him after that, close enough that their shoulders bumped on the narrow path, and she didn't ask questions either. She just kept glancing at him sideways, the way she did when she was reading a ledger she wasn't supposed to have. She'd read enough of them by now to know what a shortcut through empty country usually meant.

The land itself gave him away before he could have found words for it, if he'd ever tried. The road widened into an old paved way, stones fitted without mortar, each one cut with a shallow channel so rainwater ran off instead of pooling. His grandmother's people had built roads like that. Not his grandmother exactly — his grandmother had been born under a different name in a different valley, because that was what happened to people from here; they got scattered and renamed like orphans in a flood — but her people. His people, if a person could claim people who no longer appeared on maps.

The channels in the stones still worked. Two hundred years, three hundred, maybe more, and when the morning rain came the water still ran off the road in neat silver threads, and Tam knelt once when the others stopped to eat and put his palm in one of the channels and let it fill, and thought: *somebody made this so well that even forgetting couldn't ruin it.*

"Tam." Gideon's voice, quiet. "You've gone somewhere."

"Just checking the drainage," he said, and stood up, and grinned at her, because grinning was the tool he had and he'd learned to use it the way Vess used her voice and Sefa used her seals — as armor, as door, as *nothing to see here.*

Gideon didn't grin back. But she didn't press, either. That was the thing about her he'd come to trust: she knew what it was to carry a country inside you with nowhere to set it down.

* * *

They came to the quarry wall on the third afternoon.

Tam heard it before he saw it — the particular silence of a place where the ground has been opened. Forests have noise in them even in winter, small rustlings, the tick of branches. This had none. The sound just stopped at a line, as though someone had drawn it there, and beyond the line the air lay flat and stunned.

Then the trees ended all at once, and there it was.

The quarry went down and down in terraces, pale steps cut into the earth, wide as a town square each. Ramps spiraled along the walls where the ore carts had run. At the bottom, flooded pools caught the grey sky and held it, motionless, like mirrors laid out for a funeral. Everything was the color of bone. No grass had taken hold on the terraces. No birds crossed the pit. Even the wind seemed to come to the edge and decline to go further.

"The Ashen Vein," Sefa said softly behind him. "I've processed the extraction permits. I didn't—" She stopped. Tam heard her swallow. "I never saw it. We only ever see the numbers. Tonnage. Yield. Labor allocation."

"Labor allocation," Tam repeated. His voice came out strange, flattened, like the landscape.

"It's a standard column," Sefa said, and then, wretchedly: "It's just a column, Tam. I filled in that column. For six years. I never once asked what—"

"What the labor was," Tam finished. He kept looking at the pit. "It was us. That's what the labor was. My mother's mother's people. The Kessarin. That was our name. You won't find it in anything you filed, because you people struck it, but that was it. Kessarin. We were river-folk before we were anything else. Fishers, ferrymen — my family's been poling boats since before your Choir had a building to be bureaucratic in." He heard his own voice rising and made himself stop, breathe. "When they dissolved us, they didn't scatter us to towns. Towns are expensive. They moved us here. To dig."

Nobody said anything. Vess had gone very pale, and her hands were wrapped around each other as though holding something in. Gideon stood at the lip of the quarry with the wind pulling at her coat, and she did not look away from the pit, and Tam understood suddenly that she was letting him do this at his own pace, standing witness without taking the telling out of his mouth. It was the same courtesy she'd wanted at the border stones, he realized. Someone to stand there while the truth came out. Just someone. Just one person who wouldn't leave.

"There's a part they don't put in the yield reports," he said. "Come on. There's a maintenance stair on the north side. It's a long way down."

* * *

The stair was cut into the living rock, switchbacking down the quarry wall, and every step rang faintly under their boots, a hollow note, as though the whole cliff were a struck bell still fading. Halfway down, Tam stopped at a landing where the rock face had been left rough, and pressed his hand against it.

"Feel," he said.

Gideon put her hand beside his. Her fingers found what his had: grooves, dozens of them, scores, cut into the stone at shoulder height, each one the width of a finger and the length of a forearm.

"They let them mark the walls," Tam said. "One groove per shift. That was the rule — you cut your mark when you came off the carts, so the overseers could count you back in. If your mark wasn't there, they sent men down the shafts to look for you." He traced one groove with his fingertip. It was worn smooth at the bottom, polished by touch. "My great-uncle's marks are down here somewhere. He died in the second gallery when I was nine. Cave-in. They listed him as equipment loss. My grandmother got a form. A *form*, with a box to sign acknowledging receipt."

Vess made a small sound. Tam didn't turn around. He couldn't have kept talking if he'd had to look at anyone's face.

"But here's the thing nobody counts," he said. "Look closer at the grooves. Look at the tops of them."

Gideon leaned in. So did Sefa, her clerk's eyes good for fine detail. And there it was, faint as breath on glass: above many of the grooves, cut tiny, cut quick, were other marks. Stars. Little crosses. Loops and knots. One that was unmistakably a fish, done in four strokes by someone with a sure hand and probably a stolen knife.

"They signed themselves," Tam said. "Not for the overseers. For each other. For whoever came after. Every one of these is somebody saying *I was here, I had a name, I was a person.* They weren't allowed names on the rolls — the rolls called them units — so they put them here, in the rock, where ink couldn't reach." His throat closed. He worked past it. "There are thousands. Maybe tens of thousands. Nobody knows this is here. The Choir surveyed this quarry twice and recorded the walls as 'unremarkable strata.'"

"Unremarkable strata," Sefa whispered. She had her ledger-satchel clutched to her chest with both arms, and Tam had a sudden fierce image of her going home — wherever home was now — and burning every column she'd ever filled, ash to the elbows.

"Come on," he said. "It's worse below. Or better. I don't know which."

* * *

The bottom of the quarry was a country of pale hills, and the hills were spoil heaps, and between them lay the flooded galleries, black water still as glass. Tam led them along a causeway of packed tailings, past a rusted cart upright on its rails like a patient animal, until they reached the far wall, where a shelf of original earth remained untouched — the one place the cutting had never reached, marked off since the first days by a row of stones too regular to be natural.

Graves.

Not marked graves. Nothing so legible. Just the row of boundary stones, and the ground behind them, level and bare, and a silence that had weight, that pressed on the ears like deep water. Tam counted the stones without wanting to. Forty-one. He'd counted them a hundred times in his life, from the rim, from memory, in dreams. Forty-one stones, and behind them, the ledgers said, *land cleared.*

"That's the phrase," he said aloud, because the others needed to hear it in his voice, in a living voice, not in the dry typeface of a dissolved nation's file. "'Land cleared.' That's what my country is in your records, Sefa. Not destroyed. Not buried. *Cleared.* Like weeds. Like a field ready for planting. Four thousand people went into the galleries over sixty years and came out as cleared land." He turned and faced them, and his eyes were burning and dry. "My great-uncle is under one of these stones or under one of those heaps, and I will never know which, because the difference didn't matter to anyone whose job it was to know."

The wind came down the quarry walls and moved nothing at all.

Gideon stepped forward.

She walked past Tam without a word and stood before the boundary stones, and Tam saw her take something from inside her coat — the oath-knot, the braided cord she wore against her heart, the one the old ferryman had recognized on the river and wept over. She unwound it slowly, letting the length of it fall across her palms, and then she knelt, right there in the tailings dust, and laid the knot on the lowest stone.

"I am Gideon Quintana," she said, "oath-bound swordspeaker of the Valley of Serel, last of my line and keeper of its fire."

Her voice carried strangely in the pit, off the terraced walls, coming back doubled and doubled again, so that it sounded like many voices, like a congregation.

"I cannot swear you back into the world today. I cannot give you back your names on paper, or your river, or your grandchildren. The law that erased you is still standing, and I am not yet strong enough to break it." She drew a slow breath. "But I can swear this, and I swear it where the sky can see me and the stone can hold me: I will carry your names out of this pit. All of them. Every groove on those walls, I will read, and record, and speak aloud until speaking them costs the ones who struck them more than keeping them ever cost you. Your land was not cleared. It was stolen, and theft leaves a debt, and I am swearing myself into that debt with my whole life as collateral."

She picked up the oath-knot and pressed it briefly to the stone, the way Tam had seen pilgrims press coins to shrine-posts, and when she lifted it, he saw she'd left something behind: a single thread pulled loose from the braid, coiled neatly on the stone like a tiny offering, like a signature.

Then she stood and turned around, and her face was wet and absolutely calm.

"Your turn," she said to Tam, gently. "If you want it. Not if you owe it. If you want it."

And Tam found that he did — that some knot in his own chest, wound tight since he was nine years old and handed a form with a box to sign, wanted nothing else in the world. He knelt where she had knelt. He didn't have an oath-knot or an oath or any training in sacred things; he had a ferryman's hands and a ferryman's habit of getting people across water. So he put both palms flat on the cold boundary stone, the way he'd steady a boat for a passenger, and he said:

"I'm Tam. My grandmother was Kessarin, and so am I, whatever your papers say. I can't count how many of you are down here. But I'm going to learn to count. I'm going to come back with chalk and paper and light, and I'm going to read every mark on those walls, and I'm going to say every name out loud on my boat, going across, the way you'd ring a bell for the drowned — except you're not drowned, you're *here*, and I'm sorry, I'm so sorry it took strangers' grief to teach me to grieve you properly." His voice cracked clean through. He let it. "You're not cleared land. You're my country. And countries don't die just because somebody files them wrong."

Behind him, quietly, Vess began to hum — low, wordless, a keening line that rose and fell like breathing, and Tam realized dimly that it must be a mourning song, a real one, maybe even one from a dissolved nation, maybe even his, and that she was giving it to them the only way she had left after everything she'd sung for the Choir.

Sefa knelt last. She didn't touch the stone. She took out her clerk's pen — the good one, the one with the Choir's seal worked into the nib — and held it a moment, and then set it down on the boundary stone, point toward the graves, like a sword surrendered hilt-first.

"I'll write it differently," she said. "Whatever gets written next. I'll write it differently."

For a long time none of them moved. The flooded galleries held the grey sky. The wind stayed at the rim, declining, as it always had, to come down among the dead.

* * *

They climbed out in the last light, and camped just beyond the tree line, where the ordinary world resumed — pine smell, bird sounds, the small honest noises of land that hadn't been opened. Tam took first watch because he knew he wouldn't sleep, and sat with his back against a trunk watching the quarry darkness, and it was nearly midnight when Gideon came and sat beside him without asking, the way she did, and handed him half of a flatbread without asking, the way she also did.

"You're going back down there," she said. "After. When this is over. With chalk and light."

"After," he agreed. "If there is an after."

"There'll be an after." She said it the way she said most true things — flat, certain, leaving no room for argument. Then she was quiet a while, chewing, watching the dark. "That thread I left on the stone. From the oath-knot."

"Yeah?"

"In my valley, when we bury an elder, we leave a thread from the knot on the grave-stone. It means the dead keep a hold on the living. A claim." She looked over at him, and the starless sky made her face all shadow, but he could feel the weight of her looking. "You understand what that means. What I did back there. I didn't just make a promise to your dead, Tam. I let them make one to me. They're holding the other end of that thread now. Whatever happens at the Door of Return — whatever it costs — they get a say. Their grief rides with us."

Tam turned that over. Out on the black water of his childhood, his grandmother had taught him something similar: *never leave a crossing unpaid; the river remembers fares.* He'd thought it was superstition. Sitting here, with forty-one stones somewhere behind him in the dark and a swordspeaker's thread lying on one of them, he found he believed it completely.

"Good," he said simply. "Let them ride. They've been waiting long enough to go anywhere."

Gideon huffed — almost a laugh, almost not — and settled back against the trunk beside him.

It was Sefa's cry that broke the night open, a few minutes later — sharp, frightened, the sound of a woman who has spent her life reading documents suddenly confronted with one written in fire. They came out of the tents fast, all of them, and found her standing at the edge of the camp staring east, arm raised, pointing at the horizon.

At first Tam thought it was a fire. A red glow sat low against the hills, pulsing, wrong. Then it moved — drifted, swayed, split into separate lights — and he understood, with a lurch, that he was looking at lanterns. Dozens of them. A procession, winding down out of the eastern hills along the old paved road, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who know exactly where they are going.

And every one of those people, as the lead lantern swung closer, was singing.

Vess went white as quarry-dust. "That's a cantor's processional," she breathed. "Full rite. That's not auditors — auditors don't sing. That's—" She stopped, and Tam watched her arrive at the answer a heartbeat before Sefa said it aloud, her voice shaking:

"The Choir comes singing. They know where we're going. They know about the Door."
