## 14. The Cantor's Confession

The rain came at dusk, the first rain Vess had heard since the salt country began, and it fell on the ruined watchtower where they had sheltered as though it had been waiting thirty years for permission.

She sat apart from the others, in the corner where the tower's broken stair met the wall, and she listened to the water finding its way through the collapsed roof — a soft, patient percussion, drip and gather and fall, drip and gather and fall. It sounded almost like a choir warming up. That was the cruelty of her training: everything reminded her of song. A kettle. A hangman's rope creaking. Rain on old stone. Thirty years a cantor, and the world would not stop offering her music.

Across the tower's single dry room, Gideon sat sharpening her sword with a whetstone that whispered against steel like something being told a secret over and over. Tam slept by the dead fire, curled small, one hand still closed around the ferry-token he wore on a cord. Sefa was awake, of course. Sefa was always awake, her stolen ledgers spread around her like the wings of some enormous ink-dark bird, her lips moving as she read by the light of a candle stub they could not afford.

Vess watched Gideon's hands. She had been watching them for days now, ever since Tam's country, ever since the quarry and the stones and the graves the ledgers called *land cleared*. There was a way Gideon held the whetstone — thumb along the flat, fingers loose, the blade tilted to catch what little light remained — that was not merely technique. It was liturgy. Vess knew liturgy when she saw it. She had spent her life composing it for other people's griefs.

And that was the thought that finally broke her open, sitting in the rain-dark tower with the candle guttering: *I have spent my life composing rites for other people's griefs, and I composed the greatest one of all for hers.*

"Gideon," she said.

Her voice came out wrong — too much cantor in it, too rounded, too trained. She heard Gideon's hands stop.

"I have to tell you something, and I need you to let me finish it, because if you don't let me finish it I will never say it at all."

* * *

She should have said it in the valley. That was the truth she had carried up the road like a stone in her boot. She should have said it the first night at the inn at the end of the map, when Gideon told them about the wards failing, about the silence flooding back in, about the voices of her dead elders rising out of the ground like sap in spring. She should have said it when Sefa read aloud from the census ledger, when Gideon read her own family listed under *assets forfeit*, when the firelight made the fury in her face look almost holy. Any of those nights. Every night. Instead Vess had sung the wards down in Ashfall and hated herself for that instead, because hating herself for a fresh sin was so much easier than confessing an old one.

Thirty years ago, she had been twenty-two years old and certain of everything, which is the most dangerous age and the most dangerous condition.

"The Choir sent me with a company of auditors," she said now, and the words came slowly, each one having to be pried loose from wherever she had buried it. "It was my first field posting. I had spent six years in the conservatory learning the dissolution rite — eleven verses, descending key, the cantor stands at the center of the territory and sings while the auditors walk the borders with their documents. You've seen what the ledgers look like. The song is what makes the ledgers true. That's the part they never write down. Ink doesn't unmake a country. Song does. The rite doesn't destroy anything, Gideon. That's what they taught us, and what I believed. It *translates*. It takes every name, every boundary stone, every oath and kinship and grave, and it translates them into the Choir's language, into law-language, so that nothing is lost, only *filed*."

Gideon had not moved. The whetstone lay still in her hand. In the candlelight her face was unreadable, which frightened Vess more than rage would have.

"Your valley was the last," Vess said. "Do you understand? The last territory on the whole schedule. Every other nation on the dissolution rolls had gone quietly or gone in chains, but yours — your people had put up wards that our songs couldn't cross. Magic-dead ground. The Choir had tried three times over forty years. Cantors came back hoarse and empty, singing into silence like pouring water into sand. So they sent me, because I was young and my voice was strong and I believed — I *believed*, Gideon, I want you to hear this — I believed we were doing a mercy."

"Say the rest." Gideon's voice was low and even. Too even.

"The doctrine said that dissolved peoples suffered. That living outside history, outside law, outside the maps, was a kind of slow death — no courts to protect you, no treaties, no record of your marriages or your dead. The doctrine said the rite was a kindness. We were bringing you inside the walls of the law. We were giving you the Choir's peace instead of your own wars. And your valley was suffering under it, we were told — isolated, dwindling, your traditions dying with your elders, your young people leaving and never coming back—" Vess's voice cracked and she hated it, hated that even her confession came out trained, shaped, performed. "They told us the wards were a wound. That the swordspeakers' oaths were a sickness the valley couldn't heal from. And I stood at the center of your valley — there was a shrine, a spring, a flat stone where—"

"The Oathfire stone," Gideon said.

"The Oathfire stone." Vess closed her eyes. "I sang the eleven verses there. It took from moonrise to dawn. Your wards fought me the whole night — I felt them, like hands pushing against my chest, like a whole country saying *no* in one voice. And then near morning they didn't anymore. And the silence came down. And the auditors walked the borders with their papers, and I sang the last verse, and I remember — I remember standing there in the wet grass thinking, *it's done, they're safe now, they're inside the law, no one can hurt them without answering to the Choir.* I went back to the capital and I was commended. They gave me a silver throat-clasp. I wore it for nine years."

The rain filled the pause. Drip and gather and fall.

"And then?" Gideon asked.

"And then I stayed in the Choir for nineteen more years, and I sang four more dissolutions, and I taught the rite to eleven students, and I told myself every time that it was mercy, right up until the day I heard what happened to a town after a dissolution went wrong — a place called Hallowmere, you won't find it on any map either — and I understood that mercy was the word we used so we wouldn't have to use the true one." She opened her eyes. "The true one is *erasure*. We erased you. I erased you. Not the doctrine. Not the Choir. Me. My voice. I stood on your Oathfire stone and I sang your country into a filing cabinet, and I got a medal for it, and I am the reason your elders' voices were silent in the ground for thirty years, and I am the reason you tended those wards alone, and I will kneel in this rain if you tell me to, but I needed you to hear it from me before the Door, because if we reach that arch and open it and your dead come through singing — they will know my voice, Gideon. Songs leave marks. They'll know exactly who stood on their stone."

* * *

What happened next, Vess would spend the rest of her life trying to describe honestly, and never quite manage.

Gideon did not shout. That was the terrible thing. She rose the way weather rises — no decision in it, just pressure — and the sword came off her knees and into her hand without her seeming to notice she'd taken it, and the candle flame bent sideways though there was no wind, and Vess felt the old magic move through the ruined tower like cold water through a cracked jar. The valley's silence had flooded back into Gideon the night the stars went out; Vess had felt it ever since, a stillness around the woman like the stillness at the center of a struck bell. Now the bell rang.

"You sang on the Oathfire stone." Each word came separately, placed like a foot on ice. "You stood where my grandmother stood. Where her mother stood. Where *I* was going to stand, when it was my turn, when I'd earned it. Eleven verses. Descending key." A sound came out of her that was almost a laugh and was worse than any scream. "I *heard* it. Do you know that? I was fourteen. I was hiding in the fern-hollow above the spring because I wasn't supposed to be there, and I heard the whole thing, and I didn't know what it was. I thought it was beautiful. I lay in the wet ferns and I listened to a stranger sing my country to death and I thought, *what a voice, what a voice, I wonder who taught her.*"

"Gideon—" Sefa started.

"I wondered who *taught her*." The sword-point dropped until it touched the floor, and where it touched, the old stone whitened, briefly, like breath on glass. "Thirty years I've carried that night. I thought the silence afterward was grief. I built my whole life in that silence. I kept the wards alone because everyone who could have helped me keep them had been *filed*. And you're telling me the thing I mourned as a mystery was a woman with a silver clasp singing a lullaby backwards?"

"Yes," Vess said. Because there was no other answer, and because she had promised herself she would not flinch from any of it.

Gideon crossed the room in three strides. Tam woke with a grunt; Sefa was already on her feet, ledgers scattering. The sword came up — not fast, nothing so merciful as fast — and stopped a hand's width from Vess's throat, and the cold coming off the blade was the cold of the valley, the magic-dead cold, the cold Vess had felt pressing against her chest all those years ago from the inside of the wards.

"Sing something," Gideon said.

"What?"

"You heard me. Sing. One verse. Any verse. Let me hear what it sounds like when it isn't hidden. Let me hear the thing that killed everyone I—" Her voice broke at last, finally, gloriously broke, and the sword shook with it. "Everyone I loved is a line in your ledgers and a verse in your mouth and I sat across a fire from you and shared wine with you and let you teach Tam rounds on the road like some—like some kindly aunt—and the whole time you had my country in your *throat*—"

"I did," Vess said. "Every word you're saying is true, and I'm not going to argue with any of it, and if you strike, I won't defend myself, because I've had thirty years to imagine this moment and in none of my imaginings did I deserve to survive it."

"Don't." Gideon's face twisted. "Don't you dare make it clean. Don't stand there being brave about it, that's just another performance, you're all performances, the whole Choir is one long polite performance—"

"She's telling the truth!" Tam was up, between them, hands out, absurd and fearless, a ferryman's grandson with no weapon but his voice. "Gideon — Gideon, look at her, she's shaking—"

"Tam, move."

"No! Look, I—" His voice cracked too; he was barely more than a boy. "My country's in her mouth too, maybe. Maybe she sang mine. Maybe that's why there's no songs left in it. But killing her won't put them back!"

"It won't bring anything back," Gideon snarled, "that's the whole point, that's what they took, that's what the *ledgers* took—"

"That's enough."

Sefa's voice cut the room like a paper knife. Small, precise, final. She had picked up one of the ledgers — Vess saw which one, and her stomach dropped, because it was the oldest, the field ledger, the one with the cantors' names in the margins — and she held it open in both hands like a verdict.

"Both of you sit down," Sefa said. "No. Neither of you gets to decide how this ends tonight. Not the sword and not the confession. This—" she tapped the page, "—is what decides. This is what we've been walking toward since the inn, and everyone keeps forgetting it. Sit *down*, Gideon Quintana, last swordspeaker of a nation whose name I have read two hundred times in these pages and cannot pronounce, and you listen, because listening is the only rite anyone in this room has left."

* * *

They sat. The rain went on. Sefa stood between them with the ledger open, and Vess realized, watching her, that this was the first time she had ever seen the clerk hold the Choir's book like a weapon pointed at the Choir.

"Read the margin," Sefa said to Gideon. "Page ninety-one. Read it out loud. All of it."

Gideon's eyes went down to the page. Vess knew what was written there; she had written it. *Dissolution of the Vaelmark territories executed per schedule. Rite performed by Cantor Third Rank V. Ossory, assisted by audit company nine. Local resistance: ward-based, nonviolent, sustained. Duration of rite: seven hours, forty minutes. Note:* — and here the margin note, in her own younger handwriting, small and neat and damning — *note: local wards resisted beyond doctrinal expectation. Recommend future rites assign two cantors minimum. Performance exhausting. No further incidents.*

"'Performance exhausting,'" Gideon read. Her voice was dead flat. "'No further incidents.'"

"That's what I wrote," Vess said quietly. "That's what I reduced it to. Seven hours and forty minutes of your country saying no, and I wrote it up as a scheduling problem. I've read that margin note a thousand times since I ran. It's the reason I burned my credentials in front of the auditors at the river crossing. Not the doctrine. Not the Choir. That sentence. I wrote your resistance down like weather, and then I went home and slept."

"Why are you here?" Gideon asked, and it was not a scream this time; it was worse, it was a real question, the question underneath all the others. "Not why did you come — why are you *here*, on this road, walking toward the thing that unmakes everything you built? What do you want out of it? Absolution? You won't get it from me. Forgiveness isn't a door you get to walk through because you confessed. It's not owed. Tell me what you actually want, Vess, because I can't — I can't decide what to do with you until I know."

Vess looked at her hands. They were a cantor's hands, kept, manicured once, scarred now from Ashfall and the road. When she spoke, she let the training go, let the voice come out rough and unshaped, the way she had sung as a girl before the conservatory got hold of her.

"I want to be in the room when it's undone," she said. "That's all. I spent my whole life closing doors and calling it mercy, and I want — I need — to stand at the Door of Return and watch it open and not run away from what comes through. Your elders will know my voice. I told you that. I want them to know it. I want to stand there and let them see me and let whatever happens happen, because if I die at that arch it will be the only honest ending my life has offered me, and if I live, then I live knowing, and knowing is the punishment I chose when I burned my papers. I'm not asking you to forgive me, Gideon. I'm asking you to let me witness. It's the least of the things I owe, and it's the only one I can pay on this road."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below, water dripped from the broken stair, patient as a metronome.

"My mother used to say," Gideon said at last, and her voice was wrecked and quiet, "that forgiveness is a debt the wronged person gets to decide the terms of. Not whether — the *terms*. She said holding a grudge was fine, holding it forever was fine, but you had to hold it honestly, you had to look at what was owed, not pretend the debt didn't exist." She looked at Vess across the ruined room, across thirty years and eleven verses. "So here are my terms. You don't get forgiveness tonight. Maybe not at the arch. Maybe not ever — that's mine to carry, not yours to wait for. But you walk in front tomorrow. Where I can see you. And when we reach the Door, you stand where I put you, and you do not sing unless I ask you to sing. And if my dead come through that arch and they want justice—"

"They'll have it," Vess said. "Whatever form it takes."

"—then they'll have it," Gideon finished, "and I won't stand in their way. Even if what they want is you."

"That's fair," Vess said. "That's more than fair."

Sefa closed the ledger gently, like shutting a coffin. Tam sat back down by the dead fire, exhaling. And it might have ended there, in exhausted truce, except that Gideon paused at the edge of the stair, sword sheathed now, and said, without turning around:

"One more thing. The fern-hollow. Fourteen years old. I listened to the whole rite and I remembered it — every note, thirty years, I could hum it in my sleep and never knew what I was humming." Now she turned, and her eyes in the candlelight were wet and furious and terribly awake. "So you'll teach it to me. The eleven verses. Before the Door. If your song unmade my country, then I want the song too. I want every tool that touched it. That's the last term."

Vess felt the old fear rise — the deep, doctrinal fear that the rite in the wrong throat could unmake anything, even the Choir, even the peace, even the world — and let it rise, and let it pass, because it was exactly what she deserved.

"All right," she said. "We start at dawn."

Outside, the rain was stopping. And far to the east, past the broken roof, past the torn clouds, Vess saw it — a single point of light where no star had burned since the night the sky went dark, small and steady and unmistakably growing brighter, as if something very far away had opened one eye and begun, patiently, to look for them.
