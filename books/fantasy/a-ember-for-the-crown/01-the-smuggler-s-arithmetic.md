## 1. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

The storm came in from the west at the hour when the light dies, and Jude Ravensworth counted it the way a miser counts coins.

Wind out of the southwest, gusting hard enough to bend the pines along the ridgeline until they hissed like a thousand whispering informants. Cloud ceiling low and ragged, dragging its belly across the peaks. Rain not yet fallen but promised — he could taste the iron of it on the back of his tongue, could feel the pressure settling over Thornfell Pass like a hand pressing down on the world's shoulders. A bad night. Not the worst he'd guided through. But bad enough that the arithmetic had to be exact.

He crouched at the mouth of the pass with a strip of dried venison between his teeth and did what he always did first: he listened to the mountain.

Not metaphorically. That was the trick of it, the thing seven years in these stones had taught him and no one else alive knew how to do. The mountain spoke in small voices — the tick of freezing water in a crack, the groan of a snowfield shifting its weight, the particular silence where a boulder had spent three hundred years deciding whether to fall. Most men heard nothing. Jude heard everything, and he had learned to keep his hearing cold. Detached. A tool, like the ice-axe on his belt or the rope coiled over his shoulder.

*Two hundredweight of slate loose above the second narrows,* the mountain said. *The eastern shelf weeping meltwater into the scree. Wind funneling through the gap at forty miles an hour by midnight.*

He chewed, swallowed, and made the calculation.

Twelve mules. Nine hired hands, four of them green enough to panic if the sky so much as cleared its throat. Cargo: salted fish, iron nails, a chest of Vellish brandy, and — wrapped in oilcloth beneath the brandy, because Maren never told her guides what they carried and Jude never asked — something heavy and rectangular that clinked when it shifted. Contraband worth perhaps two hundred crowns if it reached the lowland markets. Worth a hanging apiece if the crown's riders caught them on the switchbacks.

Risk of the western route: high. The narrows would funnel the wind into something that could peel a man off the ledge like bark off a birch. Risk of the eastern traverse: higher still, with the meltwater undermining the scree. Risk of waiting out the storm in the open: certain death for the green hands, probable death for everyone.

There was a third route. There was always a third route, the one that went through the old places, under the standing stones, past the wards that still hummed faintly in the deep rock like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Jude did not take the third route. He had taken it exactly once, seven years ago, half-dead and half-mad with grief, and what he'd felt there in the humming dark had been the reason he never took it again.

*Western route,* he decided. *Rope lines doubled. No fires above the treeline. If the wind rises past the third marker, we shelter in the hollow below Greytooth and lose the night.*

He spat the venison gristle into the wet grass and stood.

That was all he was now, he thought, as he turned back down the slope toward the campfire's dull orange smear in the gloom. An arithmetic. A man who had once called lightning down from a clear sky and been feared in three kingdoms had reduced himself to sums: cargo against weather, lives against coin, hours against miles. It was a good trade. He reminded himself of that daily, the way a man reminds himself a wound has closed. Passion got you exiled. Precision kept you alive. He had buried the storm-caller in the deepest part of himself, under seven years of stone and silence, and if sometimes — on nights like this, when the pressure built and the air itself seemed to lean toward him as if recognizing an old friend — if sometimes he felt that buried thing turn over in its sleep, he simply added another stone and walked on.

The fire was a poor one, shielded by a wall of stacked granite, and around it his nine hands huddled like crows. He read their faces in the firelight the way he'd read the mountain. Old Tammas, gray-bearded, missing two fingers to frostbite, who would hold in any crisis and complain about it afterward. The brothers Ferro and Dell, twins, quick and quarrelsome. Young Wren, seventeen, all elbows and ambition, who watched Jude too closely and asked too many questions about the years before Thornfell. And the rest — faces he knew by habit rather than affection, because affection was a luxury the arithmetic didn't allow.

"Storm's coming in hard," he said, without preamble. "We move in ten minutes. Western route, double ropes, no talking on the ledges. Anyone falls, they call out once and then they climb, or they don't." He let his gaze pass over each of them. "Questions?"

Wren raised a hand, because of course he did.

"Why not wait it out? Maren's got a waystation two days south—"

"Because the waystation's got a crown rider drinking her ale as of yesterday, and because this cargo doesn't keep." Jude shouldered his pack. "And because storms are honest. They tell you exactly what they'll do. It's people who lie."

He said it flatly, without feeling, and was distantly surprised to find that some part of him — some thin, starved part he'd thought long dead — flinched at the words. *People who lie.* As if he were not the greatest liar of all, lying every day to the mountain, to the men, to himself, pretending the thing coiled behind his ribs was only indigestion and not a sleeping god.

They moved.

* * *

The pass took them the way the sea takes swimmers — gradually, then all at once.

For the first hour it was merely miserable. Rain arrived on schedule, fine and needling, turning the trail to grease. The mules complained in their patient, doomed way. Tammas muttered a running inventory of saints under his breath. Jude climbed ahead with the rope singing taut behind him and counted steps, counted switchbacks, counted the seconds between lightning-flash and thunder-crack, translating distance into time into risk.

One mile. Two. The walls of the narrows rose on either side, black stone streaked white with old water-marks, and the wind began to find its voice — a low moan at first, then a rising keen, then a shriek that came down the throat of the gorge like something hunting.

Jude stopped at the second narrows and put his palm flat against the rock face.

Cold. Wet. And underneath — a tremor. Faint, rhythmic, wrong. Not the shudder of wind through stone. Something deeper. Something moving.

His heart did not speed up. That was the strange thing, the thing he noticed with a kind of clinical detachment even as every nerve in his body woke screaming. His heart stayed slow and steady, the heart of an accountant, while the rest of him recognized what the tremor meant with a certainty older than thought.

*The mountain is going to shed its skin.*

"Off the ledge!" His voice cut through the wind like a blade through sailcloth. "Everyone down, into the culvert — NOW!"

They moved fast, the green ones faster than he'd expected, fear doing what training couldn't. Jude shoved the last mule bodily into the shallow cave-mouth where a stream had carved a hollow in the cliff base, and he was still half-turned, still watching the slope above, when the mountain answered him.

It did not sound like falling rock. It sounded like the end of the world conducted by an orchestra of giants — a crack like a bone the size of a cathedral snapping, and then two hundred tons of slate coming down the mountainside in a black avalanche, taking trees, taking the trail, taking the very ledge they'd stood on ten seconds before, and the whole mass of it hit the narrows floor with a concussion Jude felt in his teeth, in his lungs, in the roots of his hair.

Dust. Silence. Rain resuming, timidly, as if apologizing.

Then the screaming started — not from his people, who were coughing and clawing dust from their eyes in the culvert, alive, all nine alive, he counted them twice with his eyes before he believed it — but from the sky.

Because the sky had gone wrong.

The clouds above the broken slope were turning. Not drifting — *turning*, spiraling inward with a slow, deliberate rotation that no natural storm had ever managed, and the rain fell upward for three full seconds, and the hair on Jude's arms stood straight out from his body, and somewhere inside him, behind seven years of carefully mortared stone, something opened one eye.

He knew what it was. He knew, with the same terrible clarity, that it wasn't him. He hadn't called. He hadn't reached. He had done nothing but stand in the rain and count his people alive — and yet the storm above the pass was bending toward him like a flower toward sun, gathering itself, asking permission.

"No," Jude said aloud. Quietly. To the sky, or to himself; he couldn't have said which.

The spiral faltered. Hung there, ragged and unfinished, like a word swallowed mid-sentence.

Then it broke apart, and the storm went on being merely a storm, and Jude Ravensworth stood in the wreckage of the trail with his hands shaking — actually shaking, the first time in seven years — and understood that the arithmetic had just changed.

Something was coming. Something the mountain had felt before he had, something the storm recognized, something that had made the buried thing behind his ribs stir and reach without his leave. He ran the numbers the way he always did — probability, distance, threat — and every sum came back the same:

*Three days. Maybe less. Whatever it is, it's already in the mountains.*

"You felt that," Tammas said beside him. The old man's face was gray with more than dust. "Tell me you felt that."

"Rockslide," Jude said. "We lost the trail. We go up and over Greytooth, and we don't stop until the far side." He turned away before Tammas could see whatever was written on his face. "Move."

They moved. Of course they moved. He was the guide; the guide's word was the only law above the treeline.

But as Jude climbed into the dark with the rain streaming down his neck and his heartbeat finally, finally slowing, he found himself doing a new kind of arithmetic — the kind he hadn't permitted himself since the trial, since the branding, since the day they'd marched him out of the capital with his name struck from the rolls and his storms locked behind his teeth.

Seven years ago, they had exiled him to the edge of the map to die forgotten.

Tonight, for the first time, he wondered whether the map's edge had been chosen for him at all — or whether he had been placed here, precisely here, the way a man sets a trap and waits.

Above Greytooth, through a tear in the clouds, a single star burned down on the pass. And far to the north, so faint he might have imagined it, the wind carried a sound he had not heard since he was a boy kneeling before the Ember-shrine in his mother's village, before he knew what he was, before anyone taught him to be afraid of it.

A voice. Small. Wordless. Singing in a language older than any kingdom.

Jude stood in the rain and listened until it faded, and then he did the only thing the arithmetic allowed.

He climbed on.
