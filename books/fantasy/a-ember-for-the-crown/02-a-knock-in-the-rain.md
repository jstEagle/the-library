## 2. A Knock in the Rain

The storm came without rain.

Jude knew every kind of weather the Thornfells could make. He had spent seven years learning their moods the way a man learns the moods of a dangerous animal—by watching, by counting, by never turning his back. The mountains had a grammar of storms: the wet grey ones that came up from the lowlands and spent themselves against the peaks; the white screaming blizzards that closed the passes for weeks; the summer hail that could strip a goat of its wool. All of them had signatures. All of them announced themselves.

This one did not.

It began at dusk with a pressure he felt in his teeth, in the old scar that ran from his left ear down beneath his collar—a seam of flesh that had never quite stopped aching since the day they made it. The sky above the cabin went the color of a bruise gone bad, deep violet shading into black, and no cloud moved. The wind died. Even the pines stood still, as if the whole forest were holding its breath.

Then the lightning started.

It came out of a clear sky—or as clear as the Thornfells ever offered—and it did not strike the ground. It crawled. Great branching veins of white fire spread across the underside of the clouds like frost forming on glass, silent for three heartbeats, four, five, before the thunder arrived all at once, a single flat concussion that rattled the iron kettle on his hearth and sent soot sifting down the chimney.

Dry lightning. Sky-fire with no rain to carry it down. In seven years he had seen it twice, and both times the smugglers had refused to move until it passed, because dry lightning meant the mountain was *wrong*, and men who traveled through wrongness had a way of not arriving.

Jude stood in his doorway with a bowl of barley soup going cold in his hands and watched the sky write its silent script, and the scar on his neck ached like a second heartbeat.

*You are imagining it,* he told himself, the way he told himself every time. *You are a guide now. A map with legs. You feel weather the way a sailor feels the deck roll under him. It means nothing.*

The scar ached harder.

He had learned, over seven years, to be very good at not feeling things. That was the arithmetic of exile: you subtracted. The crown had subtracted his rank, his name's weight, his right to stand within a hundred leagues of any storm-cradle. He had subtracted the rest himself, piece by piece, the way a man strips a wagon to lighten it over bad ground—the pride first, then the anger, then the hope, then the memory of what his hands could do when the sky answered them. What remained was lean and light and moved fast through mountains, and asked nothing of the world except to be left alone.

He was finishing that thought when the knocking began.

Three small knocks. Almost apologetic. The kind of sound you could convince yourself you hadn't heard, if convincing yourself were something you still wanted to do.

Jude set down the soup. He took the long knife off the mantel—not because he expected to need it, but because the years had taught him that the moments you didn't expect were the ones that killed you—and opened the door onto a night full of crawling light.

She stood on his threshold like something the storm had forgotten to take.

A child. Eight years old, perhaps nine, though she was so thin it was hard to guess. Soaked to the skin despite there being no rain, her dark hair plastered flat against a face gone blue-white with cold, wearing a shift of undyed wool that might once have been white and was now the color of every road she'd ever walked. Bare feet. Bare feet, in the high Thornfells, where the frost came in high summer and the stones kept last winter's cold in their bones like a grudge.

Behind her, the lightning crawled across the clouds, and every time it flared, Jude could have sworn the light bent toward her. Curved inward, the way water bends toward a drain.

"Wrong door," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Village is two days down. Follow the river."

The child looked up at him. Her eyes were a color he had no name for—not grey, not green, but the shifting tone of deep water over stone, and they held an attention no eight-year-old should possess. She didn't shiver. That was the thing that unsettled him most, later, when he lay awake cataloguing everything wrong about her: she stood half-frozen on his step and did not shiver, as if the cold were a language she'd simply decided not to speak.

She raised one small hand and knocked again, on empty air, three times. Then she pointed past him, into the cabin, at the fire.

"No," Jude said.

The word came out of him with seven years of practice behind it. No to beggars on the pass, no to deserters with hard-luck stories, no to the soft-eyed young smugglers who wanted to know why his scars ached before every storm. No was the load-bearing wall of his life. He had built everything he still had on top of it.

The child lowered her hand. She swayed once, very slightly, like a candle flame in a draft—and then her eyes rolled back and she folded forward into his doorway, and he caught her before he had decided to, his body moving on some older arithmetic than the one he lived by.

She weighed almost nothing. She was burning up—no, not burning; *humming*. Heat came off her skin in waves that pulsed with the rhythm of the silent lightning overhead, and when his bare forearm touched hers, the scar beneath his collar flared white-hot, and somewhere far above them the sky cracked open with thunder like a struck bell.

Jude Ravensworth, who had turned away weeping men and bleeding men and once, in his first winter of exile, a woman heavy with child, stood in his own doorway holding a drowned little girl while the heavens wrote fire over the peaks, and understood with cold clarity that whatever this was, it had been coming for him for a long time.

He carried her inside anyway.

He would spend the rest of the night not examining why.

* * *

He put her by the hearth and rubbed warmth into her hands and feet with wool until the blue receded, and she never woke, and she never shivered, and the heat pulsing off her slowed by degrees until it matched something like an ordinary fever. He propped her against the woodpile with blankets around her and retreated to the far side of the room with his soup, which he ate without tasting, watching her over the rim of the bowl the way he'd watch a snow cornice he had to cross beneath.

Turn her out in the morning. Feed her tonight—that much even the arithmetic allowed; he was not so hollowed-out a man that he could watch a child starve within arm's reach—but come daylight, walk her down to the Lowmelt trail, point her at the valley, and wash his hands of whatever she was dragging behind her. The lightning had followed her. He was nearly sure of it. And things that dragged storms behind them were crown business or Aelvar business, and both kinds of business ended with men like Jude hanging from trees or worse.

His cabin was one room and a loft, built by his own hands in the first year, when his hands had needed work more than they needed anything else. Stone walls, turf roof, a hearth that smoked if the wind came from the east. He had furnished it with the deliberate sparseness of a man staging his own erasure: a bed, a table, two chairs, tools, maps, the iron kettle. Nothing beautiful. Nothing that remembered who he used to be.

Now there was a child asleep in front of his fire, and the room felt smaller and larger at once.

He was banking the coals when she began to speak.

At first he thought it was the wind. The words came low and rhythmic, rising and falling like something chanted, and it took him several breaths to understand that the sound was coming from the small shape in the blankets—that her lips were moving, that she was talking in her sleep in a language he did not know and yet—

And yet.

The scar on his neck woke like a thing uncoiling from sleep. Not pain, exactly. *Recognition.* Every syllable she breathed passed through the room and through him, and where it touched the scarred flesh it left a warmth like sun through a window, and beneath the warmth something stirred that he had buried so deep he'd told himself it was dead.

He knew the language. He did not know how he knew it. It was in him the way a song is in you that your mother sang before you could remember being born—older than memory, older than the memory of memories. The words had a shape like weather. They rose and broke. Somewhere in their cadences he heard the creak of vast wings, the groan of glaciers, the long exhale of wind across a world with no fences on it.

*Firsttongue,* whispered a voice in his head that sounded like his father, dead twenty years, who had once said such words existed and been laughed at for it. *The speech the world was made in. The speech the storm-born gave us, back when giving was still something they did.*

"That's a story," Jude said aloud, to the empty room, in his own rough common tongue. "Stories don't knock on doors."

The child spoke on. He caught no meanings—only shapes, only feelings, the way you can feel the grief in a funeral hymn sung in a tongue you've never learned. But the feelings were enough. There was joy in it, ancient and enormous, the joy of oaths sworn under open sky. And underneath the joy, threaded through it like ash through snow, there was loss. A loss so large it had stopped being an event and become a landscape. A whole country of mourning, and she was walking through it in her sleep, and every step landed on his scar like a footfall on a bruise.

He found he had crossed the room without deciding to. He knelt beside her with his hand pressed flat against the ache in his neck, listening like a man listening to a voice calling from the bottom of a well, and the firelight moved on the walls, and outside the dry lightning wrote and wrote its silent letters across the sky.

One phrase recurred. Out of all the flowing syllables, it surfaced again and again, always in the same cadence, always with the same weight, the way a refrain anchors a lament. He couldn't have translated it. But he felt it the way you feel a hand laid on your shoulder: *the ember remembers, the ember remembers, the ember remembers.*

Then she said his name.

Not the name he went by now—Ravensworth was burned into him whether he liked it or not, the brand they'd let him keep as a mercy, a leash-handle. Not the guide-name the smugglers used, either, the flat functional *Jude* stripped of everything else. She said the other one. The full one. The one spoken over him at his christening by a priest of a faith he no longer practiced, in a hall that no longer stood, by parents whose faces he had deliberately let blur like wet ink.

She said it in Firsttongue, and it came out sounding like a verdict.

Jude sat back on his heels. The fire popped. Above the roof, the lightning had finally begun to die, the great crawling veins dimming one by one until only the ordinary stars remained, and the silence they left behind was louder than the thunder had been.

"You know me," he said quietly. Foolish, talking to a sleeping child. "Somehow, impossibly, you know me. And I don't know what that makes you, but I know what it makes me."

It made him a marked man. Again. Still. Always. Whatever hunted her—and something hunted her; children did not cross the high Thornfells barefoot by accident—it would follow the trail of wrongness she left, and it would find the cabin where the exiled storm-caller lived alone, and it would ask him questions with fire. Seven years of careful subtraction, undone in one evening by three small knocks.

The arithmetic was simple. It was always simple. That was the cruelty of it.

In the morning he would feed her, walk her down to the Lowmelt trail, give her bread for the road and point her at the valley, and go back to being a map with legs. In the morning.

He pulled his chair close to the hearth, closer than he'd sat to another living soul in longer than he cared to count, and told himself he was only keeping watch. The child slept on in the firelight, small and scorched-looking and utterly silent, her breathing slow as tide. Once, near dawn, her hand crept out from under the blanket and closed around his sleeve—just two fingers, barely a grip, the hold of someone making sure a boat was still tied to the dock.

Jude looked at those two fingers for a long time.

Then he got the soup pot, warmed what was left of the barley over the coals, and set it within reach of the morning, and stayed in the chair, awake, while the light came grey into the east and the mountain, for the first time in seven years, seemed to be waiting for something along with him.

Far below, on the switchback trails of the Lowmelt pass, a line of riders in grey moved up through the pines, unhurried, patient, asking questions in every hamlet they passed.

But that was still hours away, and the fire was warm, and the child slept, and Jude Ravensworth watched the door and did not let himself wonder why the scar on his neck had begun, very softly, to hum in time with her breathing.
