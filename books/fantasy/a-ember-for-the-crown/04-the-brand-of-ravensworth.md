## 4. The Brand of Ravensworth

The snow came down in the flat, gray way that meant business, and Jude counted it among his few remaining blessings. Snow softened tracks. Snow muffled voices. Snow gave the three riders behind him an honest reason to move slowly, and slow was the only advantage a man with one child, one pack, and no horse could claim.

He had seen them at dawn, or rather he had seen what they displaced: a smear of warmth against the cold face of the mountain, three points of it, moving the way soldiers move—too evenly, too patiently, with the unhurried confidence of men who knew their quarry would tire before their horses did. Crown scouts did not ride into upper Thornfell in winter for the scenery. They rode because someone had paid them, and there was only one thing on this mountain worth paying for.

She walked ahead of him, small boots punching neat round holes in the crust, her breath pluming in little ghosts. She had not made a sound in two days. Not a whimper, not a cry, not even the wet rattle of a child fighting a cold. The silence had stopped unnerving him and started feeling like company—the particular silence of someone who understood, without being told, what noise cost out here.

"Off the trail," he said quietly, and she turned at once, obedient as a shadow.

He took them down through the scree field below Wolfjaw Tor, where the smuggler paths threaded between stone ribs too narrow for horses. It cost them an hour for every league and bought him the thing he actually needed: time to think, which was its own kind of contraband. Seven years he had run this arithmetic. Weather against daylight. Risk against cargo. One life against the mission. He had gotten so good at it that the numbers ran themselves now, cold and clean as water through a sluice, and he had told himself for seven years that the coldness was survival rather than death.

But the arithmetic had changed. The variable walking ahead of him in a coat three sizes too large did not fit in any column. Every equation that ended with him safe ended with her found, and every equation that ended with her safe required him to be something he had spent seven years burying under stone and habit and the deliberate forgetting of his own hands' strength.

The scree shifted under his boot—a sound like a whispered secret—and he froze.

Ahead, maybe half a mile back along their own trail, a hawk cried. Once. Twice. Wrong note for a hawk in snowfall; hawks kept quiet in weather like this. It was a signal, and it was close.

Jude looked at the child. She had stopped too, and she was looking up—not at him, at the sky. Her face was tilted, eyes closed, lips slightly parted, like a woman listening to music only she could hear.

"Don't," he said, though she hadn't done anything. "Whatever you're doing, don't."

Her eyes opened. She looked at him with an expression far too old for the face it lived on, and then she pointed, small mittened hand steady, at the eastern ridge.

He followed her finger and saw them clearly for the first time: not three scouts but six, fanned across the high ground, and below them, moving up the valley floor with lantern-steady purpose, a line of men in gray cloaks. Not scouts. Scouts were a nuisance. Gray cloaks were the Inquisition's field detail, and inquisitors did not come to the mountains for a stray child unless someone very high up had decided the child mattered more than the mountain.

They had cut off the southern route while he slept. That was the arithmetic's answer, cold and clean: they had known where he would be before he did.

"Down," he breathed. "Fast. Don't stop for anything."

They went down through the pines, sliding, catching saplings, the child moving with a surefootedness that should have frightened him and instead only added another entry to the ledger of things he refused to examine. Behind and above, a horn sounded—one long note, then two short—and the forest answered with shouts. The hunt was no longer patient. The hunt had become a hunt.

* * *

They reached the gorge at midafternoon, and the gorge was where the mathematics failed.

In summer it was a stony creek you crossed in four strides. In winter it froze to a white ribbon. But the warm wind had come up from the south two days ago—the same freak wind that had carried dry lightning to his door the night she arrived—and now the creek ran black and fast between ice-slicked banks, swollen with meltwater, roaring like something with opinions about trespassers.

Behind them, closer now, the horns again. And beneath the horns, fainter but unmistakable, the dogs.

Jude stood at the bank with the child's hand in his and did sums. Water temperature: dead in twenty minutes. Current: stronger than he was. Crossing points: none within a day's walk, and the day had perhaps three hours left in it. His own strength, unaided—

His own strength unaided was the number he never calculated, because calculating it meant admitting what it was.

The child tugged his hand. He looked down. She was staring at the water, and her eyes had gone strange—not afraid, but attentive, the way a person attends to a voice calling from another room. Her free hand rose, palm out over the current, and the air above the water shivered.

"No." He caught her wrist, gently, absolutely. "No. Listen to me. Whatever lives in you, whatever remembers things—you keep it leashed. Do you understand? Leashed."

She turned those old eyes on him and, for one unbearable moment, he had the vertiginous certainty that she was not the one who needed the warning. That she was looking at him the way a physician looks at a man insisting he is well while blood seeps through his shirt.

Then the dogs broke cover on the ridge behind them, and there was no more time for riddles.

They came down through the pines in a ragged line—six of them, gray cloaks thrown back, swords drawn, and at their center a tall figure who did not run. The tall figure walked. That was worse. Men who walked at the end of a chase were men who had never doubted how it would end.

The lead scout shouted something Jude didn't hear over the water. The child pressed herself against Jude's leg. And Jude Ravensworth, who had spent seven years teaching his hands to be only hands, felt the storm wake in his chest like a beast smelling meat.

It did not feel like power. That was the lie everyone believed about it, the pretty story the ballads told. It felt like remembering. Like a door in a house he had boarded shut swinging open onto a room he had furnished himself and forgotten furnishing. Lightning lived in that room. So did grief. So did a village called Ashenmere, and forty-three names he had recited every night for a year until the recitation itself became a wound he could no longer afford, and after that he had simply stopped opening the door at all.

The tall man in the center raised his hand, and his voice carried, mild and reasonable across the water:

"Jude Ravensworth. By writ of the Crown of Candessa, you are remanded to custody. The child also. Resist, and the writ permits me—" a pause, courteous as a bow, "—considerable latitude."

Jude knew that latitude. Latitude meant fire. Latitude meant what they had done to the last hedge-caller in the southern valleys, whose name nobody used anymore because using it invited questions.

The child's fingers tightened on his. She looked up at him, and she shook her head—slowly, deliberately—not *don't fight*, he realized. *Don't surrender.*

The storm in his chest pressed against the boards of the door.

Seven years, he thought. Seven years of carrying water instead of calling rain. Of watching lightning split a pine on the high pass and feeling nothing but the ordinary animal fear of a man near a falling tree. He had been so proud of that nothing. He had built a whole self out of that nothing.

The lead scout waded toward the creek's edge. The tall man watched, hands folded, patient as winter.

And Jude opened the door.

* * *

It began, as it always began, in his scars.

He had eleven of them, silver-white, branching down his left arm from shoulder to wrist like frost on glass, and the court physicians had called them lichtenberg figures—the signature of lightning passed through living flesh. What they never understood, what no one who had not carried it could understand, was that the marks were not scars. They were roots. The lightning had gone into him and grown there, and pruning the visible branch had never once touched what fed beneath.

Cold poured down his left arm. Not the cold of the mountain—the cold of the cloud-top, of the place where water hangs undecided between heaven and earth. His heartbeat slowed. His hearing sharpened until he could count the scouts' footfalls, could hear the tall man's calm breathing, could hear, impossibly, the child beside him breathing in rhythm with the creek.

"No," he said aloud, to himself, to the room inside him. "A thread. Only a thread."

He had practiced this. In the first years of exile, alone, he had practiced starving the gift down to embers, learning to feed it nothing, to stand in thunderstorms with his teeth clenched and his fists bleeding from his own nails and let the sky do what skies do without him. He had gotten good at it. He had believed he was good at it.

He reached for a thread.

What answered was a rope.

The world went white at the edges. He felt his awareness dilate outward like a drop of ink in water—he was the creek, suddenly, feeling its own cold muscular hurry; he was the ice on its banks; he was the static charge crawling in the low clouds above the gorge, a million volts looking for an excuse. The storm was not a tool he held. The storm was an ocean, and he was standing in the surf pretending to command the tide, and the tide had just noticed him.

*Let go,* whispered something that wore his own voice. *You've been holding your breath for seven years. Breathe.*

The first scout reached the bank ten feet away, sword rising, mouth open on a shout that Jude heard stretch and slow like honey pouring.

Jude raised his left hand.

He meant to throw the scouts back. He meant a wall of wind, a shove, a trickster's gesture—enough to make them fall, not enough to make them die. He measured it out with all the precision of a man who had done the sums, and the storm took his measurement and laughed.

The lightning came down out of a sky that had held no clouds an hour ago.

It did not strike the scouts. It struck the creek, and the creek became for one full second a seam of white fire, and the thunderclap arrived not as sound but as a physical fact, a fist of pressure that swatted three men off their feet and hurled them backward into the pines. Ice shattered along both banks in a running crack like the mountain clearing its throat. The tall inquisitor was driven to one knee, cloak snapping, and when he raised his head there was—for the first time—something other than patience in his face.

Jude stood in the middle of it, arm extended, and felt the thread slipping toward a torrent. More. The storm wanted more. There were clouds queuing over the western peaks, drawn to him like iron filings, and he could feel every one of them, could feel how easy it would be to reach up and pull, to bring the whole weight of the sky down on these twelve acres of mountain and every living thing in them. It would take less effort than lifting a cup. That was the horror of it, the thing he could never explain at his trial: not that the power was hard, but that it was easy. That annihilation sat in him with the weightless readiness of a sneeze.

The child stepped into his sightline.

She stood between him and the fallen scouts, tiny in the smoking ruin of the bank, and she looked up at him and shook her head again. Slowly. *Not that way.*

The ocean in him recoiled from her gaze like a wave from a seawall.

Jude closed his fist. Closed the door. Forced the clouds back with a effort like holding a drowning man underwater—his whole body shaking, blood warm and metallic at his lip, the world tilting—and the lightning withdrew sulkily into the clouds, and the creek ran on, steaming faintly, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Three scouts lay groaning in the snow, alive. He clung to that. Alive, alive, alive—he said it like a spell, because the alternative memory was already reaching for him with its forty-three names.

The surviving scouts scrambled back up the slope, dragging their fellows. The tall inquisitor rose, brushed snow from his knee with meticulous care, and looked at Jude across the water for a long moment. Then he inclined his head—almost respectful—and withdrew, unhurried, into the trees, taking his men with him.

That, more than anything, terrified Jude. Men did not withdraw from what they could take. They withdrew to fetch more men.

He turned to the child, and his legs went out from under him, and he knelt in the slush, gasping, the taste of lightning like copper coins on his tongue.

* * *

Seven years ago, they had put him in a room with no windows and tried him by candlelight.

He remembered it in fragments, the way you remember anything that cut deep enough: the wax smell. The lord-inquisitor's soft voice reading the charges—*unlicensed evocation, refusal of lawful command, aid given to the enemies of the realm*. The nine judges behind their table, faces like coins worn smooth by handling. His own advocate, assigned by the court, who met his eye exactly once and then never again.

They had made him kneel on stone. They had brought witnesses from the campaign in the Vailish Marches, soldiers who testified to the storms he had called against enemy hosts, and their testimony had the flat reverence of men describing something they were glad to have on their side.

Then they brought the villagers of Ashenmere.

He had refused the order at dusk and ridden through the night to reach the village before the crown's battlemages did, and he had stood on the ridge above it and called down a counterstorm, and the two weathers had met above the rooftops and torn each other apart, and the wreckage of that collision had fallen as hailstones the size of fists. Three dead. A barn burned. Forty houses damaged, forty families homeless through a mountain winter.

*Collateral*, said the crown. *Recklessness beyond the bounds of lawful conduct.* They did not charge him with what he had prevented, because the order itself did not officially exist. You could not acquit a man of a crime by proving he stopped a greater one if the greater one had never happened.

The lord-inquisitor had leaned close at the sentencing, and his voice had held something Jude had spent seven years failing to name—until today, kneeling in the slush with the child's hand on his shoulder, he recognized it at last. Not hatred. Not even ambition. Relief. The relief of a man setting down a burden.

"You will be exiled to the Thornfell marches," the inquisitor had said, for the record, in his dry paper voice. "There to remain under the Crown's observation until such time as the Crown sees fit to recall you. Your brand—"

They had heated iron until it glowed and pressed it to the back of his left shoulder, over the root of the scars, so that the mark of Ravensworth's disgrace sat directly upon the mark of Ravensworth's gift. He had not screamed. He had counted instead—*one, two, three*—an old habit, counting the seconds until pain became survivable, and he had reached somewhere past four hundred before they took the iron away.

Under observation, they had said. He had assumed it meant informants in the valley towns, a report each season, the long leash of a dangerous dog. It had taken him five years to notice that no smuggler ever got lost in Thornfell who worked with him, that the passes he guided were never quite closed, that his exile had a convenience to it that exile should not have. He had told himself it was luck. Luck was the last arithmetic left to a man who refused to do the real sums.

Now, with copper on his tongue and a mute child's hand steady on his shoulder, the sums assembled themselves with terrible clarity. They had not exiled him despite his power. They had exiled him *because* of it—no. Worse. They had arranged the trial, arranged the sentence, arranged the wilderness, and waited seven years for something to happen in those mountains worth sending a storm-caller to fetch.

The brand on his shoulder was not a punishment. It was a collar. And Thornfell was not a prison.

It was a kennel.

The child crouched beside him in the melting snow. She took his scarred left hand in both of hers, turned it over, and studied the silver branches as though reading them. Then she looked up, and her mouth moved, and for the first time since she came to his door she made a sound—not a word, not yet, just a soft voiced hum, almost musical, almost like the beginning of a name.

Not her name.

His.

Far to the south, past the gorge and the pines and three ridgelines of gray rock, a horn sounded again—once, long, and then answered by many. The inquisitor had withdrawn to fetch more men. And there was only one road left on this mountain that the Crown did not watch, one door left open in all the world's arithmetic, and it led down to a tallow-lit den in the foothills where a woman named Maren kept her ledgers, her grudges, and her debts.

Jude got to his feet. His legs held. Barely.

"We need smugglers," he said to the child, and found he was almost laughing. "God help us both. We need smugglers."
