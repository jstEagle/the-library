## 5. The Tallow Court

The tallow burned low in its iron dish, the way Maren liked it—guttering, honest light that made no promises. Beeswax was for merchants and magistrates. Tallow told you exactly what it was: rendered fat, dead light, a thing that cost something to make. Her mother had kept a tallow stub burning on the ledger desk every night of her reign over the Thornfell roads, and when Maren had asked her why, once, when she was small enough to sit under the desk instead of behind it, her mother had said, *Because a leader who can't stand the smell of what she's made of shouldn't be leading.*

Maren stood at that same desk now, thirty years on, and the smell was in the walls, in her shawl, in the creases of her knuckles. The Hollow—her court, such as it was—smelled of tallow and tanned hide and the pine-resin smoke that never quite left the rafters, and beneath all of it, faint as a scar, the cold mineral breath of the mountain itself.

Nine people sat around the long table. Not nine merchants. Nine survivors, each one carrying a knife and a grudge and a debt to someone in this room. That was the network, when you cut away the romance the valley folk liked to put on it: not freedom, not defiance, just debts, arranged so carefully they looked like trust.

The letter from Jude Ravensworth lay open in front of her, and she had not touched it in an hour.

*There is a child,* he had written, in that careful hand of his, each letter placed like a footstep on ice. *She cannot speak. There are men in the valley asking after mute children, and paying for answers. I mean to move her north through the Sorrow Steps. I ask for the roads. I ask for silence. I ask you to believe that I would not ask if I could carry this alone.*

*Roads. Silence. Belief.* Three requests, ranked in ascending order of cost.

Maren read it again anyway. Fourth time. Fifth.

"Well?" said Brannoc, from her left. He was the oldest of them, a former quarry slave with hands like tree roots and half his left ear gone to frostbite, and he had earned the right to speak first by outliving everyone who'd ever tried to take it from him. "Are we going to sit here all night while the wax drowns, or are we going to say the word everyone's chewing on?"

"Say it, then," Maren said.

"Fifty crowns." Brannoc spat the words like gristle. "Fifty gold crowns for word of a mute child, double for delivery, and pardon papers besides—that's what the criers were offering in Millbrook three days ago. I have it from Dessa's own runner. Fifty crowns." He let the number sit on the table like a corpse. "That's two years of runs. That's grain for the winter camps. That's new ropes, new boots, medicine for Hetta's people in the low caves. That's—" He stopped himself, which was Brannoc's way; he could see a cliff edge even mid-sentence. "You all know what it is."

"I know what it isn't," said Sefa, sharp-voiced, young, ambitious in the way Maren privately found more useful than frightening. Sefa ran the southern routes and ran them well, and she had lost two runners to the inquisition's patrols since spring. "It isn't fifty crowns. It's fifty crowns and then no network, because the moment we sell one child to Halvard's people, every family in every valley that shelters us starts wondering when we'll sell theirs. The gold spends. The reputation bleeds."

"The reputation already bleeds," said Corvin, quietly, and the table went still, because Corvin did not speak unless he had counted the cost of speaking first. He kept the western waystations—the ones nearest the valley, nearest the soldiers. "Two patrols on the Ashfall road where there was one last month. They stopped my runner Tuesday. Not searching. Just looking at faces. Memorizing. You don't post men like that unless you're building a map before you build a wall."

"They're not hunting smugglers," Maren said.

"No," Corvin agreed. "They're hunting whatever it is Ravensworth has up there in his cabin. And we all know it. And we're all sitting here pretending the question is about a child."

The tallow guttered. Somewhere above them, wind moved along the ridge with a sound like a long exhalation, the mountain breathing in its sleep. Maren found herself listening to it the way you listen to an old dog stirring in the night—not afraid of it, exactly, only aware of how much of your life you had staked on its good temper.

*Jude Ravensworth.* Even his name arrived in this room trailing weather.

Seven years ago he had come down out of a tribunal cage with his rank stripped, his calling bound, and a brand on his forearm shaped like a raven with a broken wing, and he should have died in the first winter. Exiles always did, in Thornfell; the mountain took the unconnected and the unprepared and filed them under losses. But he hadn't died, because Dessa—who was gone now, three winters gone, and Maren still reached for her name some mornings like a tongue reaching a missing tooth—Dessa had found him half-frozen in the mouth of the Wolfmark cave and dragged him back, and fed him, and taught him the passes, and he had repaid it in the only coin he had.

He had repaid it in storms.

Nobody at this table said the word *storm-caller* aloud—it wasn't forbidden, just useless, the way you don't name a thing you've decided to live beside—but everybody knew. Knew that when the Whitereach pass had avalanched with four of their runners inside it, seven springs back, the snow had parted like a hand moving blankets. Knew that the patrol boat on Lake Sorrel that sank with its manifest of captured families aboard had sunk in a flat calm, and that afterward Jude had come to Maren's table and put down his share of the guilt in silence and she had taken it, because that was the arithmetic too. Knew that for seven years the network's routes had run through weather that bent, slightly, subtly, impossibly, around their people—and that every one of those small mercies had been paid for by a man who had sworn off his own nature rather than use it as a weapon, spending it instead, drop by drop, as a shield for strangers.

That was the debt. It didn't live in any ledger. Ledgers could be burned.

"So it's simple," said Brannoc, into the quiet. "We owe him. We all know we owe him. Question is whether the debt is bigger than fifty crowns, and only one person at this table gets to weigh that."

Every head turned to her, some of them trying not to. Maren let them look. She had learned long ago that a leader who flinched from being watched became a leader who was watched anyway, only worse.

"You're wrong about the question," she said.

Brannoc raised his ruined ear toward her.

"The question isn't whether we sell the child. Nobody at this table is selling a child; if you would, leave now and leave the roads behind you." She waited. No one moved, though she marked which eyes dropped and which held. "The question is how much of ourselves we spend keeping her. Because that's the other side of the arithmetic, and I'll not insult you by hiding it. If we give Ravensworth the roads, the crown will notice. Halvard is not a man who notices things idly—he collects what he notices. Waystations will burn. Runners will hang. Some of you in this room may not see another spring, and neither may the people who love you. That is the price on the other pan of the scale. Not fifty crowns. Blood. Ours."

Silence. The tallow hissed and spat.

"And if we refuse him?" Sefa asked. Not hostilely. Genuinely. "If we close the roads?"

"Then we survive," Maren said. "For a while. On roads that run past a burned cabin and a dead child and a man we left to hold the door alone, and every one of us walks those roads knowing it, and the knowing eats the roads hollow from underneath." She picked up the letter, finally, and folded it along its worn crease. "Debt isn't a feeling. Feelings you can drown in whiskey. Debt is structure. It's load-bearing. Tear it out and you find out too late what it was holding up."

She looked around the table one more time—at Brannoc's nodding slowness, at Sefa's set jaw, at Corvin already mentally inventorying waystations—and gave them the only gift a leader could give at the end of such a night: certainty.

"He gets the roads. The Sorrow Steps, the Hollow Road if he needs it, and silence from every mouth in the network. Anyone sells word of that child to the valley, they answer to me personally, and I'll be neither quick nor clean about it. We eat the cost. We've eaten worse."

She gathered the letter, the ledgers, the dying tallow dish, and the weight of nine pairs of eyes, and she carried them all into the back room, and closed the door, and only then let her shoulders come down from around her ears.

* * *

The back room was hardly a room—a lean-to of stone with a cot, a chest, and the ledger shelf that was the true archive of the network, twenty years of coded accounts in Maren's own cramped hand. She set the tallow dish on the shelf and stood a while with her palms flat on the ledger desk, breathing the smell of rendered fat, letting the mask dissolve the way it could only do alone.

Loyalty, she thought, was a currency. Everyone believed that; the valleys romanticized it into love, the crown criminalized it into conspiracy, but out here on the roads they knew better. Loyalty was minted, spent, hoarded, counterfeited. Every favor was a coin passed hand to hand, and the whole network was nothing but an economy of obligations, flowing and balancing, and Maren was its banker.

What nobody wanted to admit was that currency was also a cage. The debtor thought the debt was chains; the creditor knew better. She owed Jude Ravensworth more than he would ever be able to call in, and because of it she would send runners into patrol-shadowed passes, burn waystation stores ahead of advancing soldiers, spend lives she could name to buy time for a child she had never seen—all because refusing him would bankrupt the only treasury she had spent her whole life filling. Loyalty didn't bind you because you were good. It bound you because breaking it would cost more than keeping it, and Maren had built her life so that the cost was always, always higher than the temptation.

She just hadn't expected the crown to start bidding.

Fifty crowns. Double for delivery. Pardon papers. Halvard wasn't trying to buy a child. He was trying to establish a price—for everything, for everyone, for the whole web of unpayable obligations that held the mountains together—to show them all that their ancient currency could be exchanged, at a fair rate, for his.

And the terrible thing, the thing she would carve on her own stone if anyone ever dared ask, was that she understood the offer. Some nights, doing the accounts, she felt the pull of it like current under calm water: one clean transaction, one final settlement, and the whole crushing ledger of human obligation closed forever. No more debts. No more cage.

No more anything worth having, either.

A soft knock at the door frame. One knock, then a pause, then nothing—the network's signal for *alone, but not urgent.* Only one person knocked like that.

"Come in, Vey."

Vey came in the way she did everything: without disturbance. A small woman, gray-eyed, forgettable by design and discipline, who had been Maren's shadow for eleven years—the eyes she lent to places her own presence would scorch, the ears she pressed against doors that would open for no one else. Vey could sit in a tavern for a night and leave with the tavern's whole history, and no patron would remember her face well enough to swear to it. She was the closest thing the network had to a ghost, and Maren trusted her the way you trust a blade: completely, and always aware of the edge.

"You heard, then," Maren said.

"I heard." Vey settled onto the chest without invitation. Eleven years bought that. "The roads. The silence. The personal and unclean vengeance." A thin smile. "It was a good speech."

"It wasn't a speech. It was a ruling."

"Mm." Vey looked at the tallow flame, and Maren waited, because Vey's silences were never empty; they were containers, and you let them fill. "And the part you didn't say out loud? The part about not trusting even our own tables tonight?"

Maren sat down across from her. "Go on."

"Halvard's criers are offering gold. Halvard's patrols are memorizing faces. Which means Halvard has informants somewhere in the valleys, which means—" Vey spread her hands, elegant, terrible. "Somewhere between your ruling and Ravensworth's cabin, there is a mouth that opens for money. Maybe several. You can rule the network, Maren. You can't rule every desperate, hungry, frightened person who's ever owed us a favor or resented owing one."

"You think someone will sell the child's route."

"I think someone already sold the fact that there *is* a child. Why else the sudden patrols, the criers, the timing?" Vey's gray eyes were very steady. "The question isn't whether the crown learns. The question is how much they learn, and when, and whether we know what they know. And for that, you need someone on the road with him. Someone who watches. Someone whose face nobody remembers."

The tallow guttered between them, and Maren understood, and hated understanding.

"You want to go."

"I want to go," Vey agreed. "Or rather—you want to send someone, and we both know there is only one someone. Your runners are known. Your lieutenants are needed. And you—" the thin smile again, gentler this time, "—you can't leave the table. The court doesn't travel. So it's me, or it's nobody, and nobody is how children end up burned."

"And if it costs too much?" Maren asked quietly. "If watching means choosing, someday, between what you see and what you report? Where does Vey's loyalty sit when the arithmetic turns ugly?"

Vey considered the question honestly, which was one of the reasons Maren trusted her; liars answered fast.

"Loyalty sits where it's paid," she said at last. "Eleven years of wages in trust and truth. I'm rich in you, Maren. Richer than any crier's fifty crowns." She rose, straightening her plain gray traveling cloak. "But I won't lie to you in exchange for all of it: if it comes down to the network's skin or Ravensworth's stranger, I'll bring you the choice. Wrapped and labeled. What you do with it then is the price of being the one who rules."

"That's all I ask."

"No," Vey said softly, at the doorway, half-turned into shadow. "That's more than you ask. That's why it hurts."

Then she was gone, silent as tallow-smoke, and Maren sat alone in the smell of rendered fat with her ledgers and her crown of debts.

She opened the newest ledger, took her pen, and recorded the night's decision in code the way she recorded everything: passage granted, one shadow attached, account opened against the name Ravensworth. Beneath it, after a moment, she added a line she had added only once before, seven years ago, the night Dessa brought a frozen exile in from the Wolfmark:

*This entry may cost more than the whole book is worth.*

She blew out the tallow and sat in the dark, listening to the mountain breathe, and far to the north, faint as memory, she thought she heard thunder where no storm should have been—rolling down out of the high passes toward her court like a summons, or a warning, or the opening footfalls of a debt coming due.
