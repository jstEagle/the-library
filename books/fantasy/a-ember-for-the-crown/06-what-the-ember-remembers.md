## 6. What the Ember Remembers

warm.

that is the first thing. before the rain on the roof, before the smell of woodsmoke and old porridge, before the man with the tired hands—there is warm. a coal in the center of everything that is not a coal at all but is shaped like one, small and red and patient.

*i carry it,* she thinks, but thinking is the wrong word. *i am carried by it.*

the cabin sleeps. the man sleeps in the chair by the door with his boots on, the way he has slept every night since she came, as if he expects to run. his breath goes slow. the fire ticks. outside, the dry lightning walks the ridgelines on white legs, and every time it flashes she feels it in her teeth, like a bell rung inside bone.

she closes her eyes.

the ember opens.

* * *

*here: the world before walls.*

*the sky is young and the storms are young and they have no names yet because nothing has learned to name them. the storm-born walk the thunderheads like deer walk meadows. their skin is rain-colored. their voices are the long low sound the wind makes when it finds a cave mouth. the mortals huddle under ledges and watch the sky-people pass overhead and do not pray, because prayer has not been invented, only fear.*

*a mortal woman climbs anyway.*

*she is small and dark-haired and her name will matter for ten thousand years and then be forgotten, and her name is—*

*—her name is hers. some things even the ember keeps.*

*she climbs the highest of the thornfell peaks in the season of white cold, bare-handed, bleeding at the fingertips, and at the top she builds a fire out of the bones of her dead father's pyre-wood, which was all she carried, and she sits down beside it in the teeth of the storm and says: you are loud. i am louder. sit.*

*and the storm-born stop.*

*they come down out of the thunderhead like water finding a stair, one at a time, and they gather around her small fire, and they are so much older than her, so vast, their eyes are the color of the space between lightning strokes—and she shares her warmth with them because there is nothing else she has. warmth, and the stubbornness of someone who has buried everyone.*

*and the eldest of them, whose grief is older than her whole people, leans close to the little fire, and says in the voice of wind-in-cave: what would you ask of us, small loud thing?*

*and the woman says: shelter. when your storms come down on our houses. that is all. we do not want your sky. we only want to live under it.*

*this is the first covenant. this is how the ember is born: not from power. from a fire shared between two kinds of lonely.*

*the storm-born breathe a spark of themselves into her flame—not their lightning, but their warmth, the part of them that remembers being small—and the flame takes it and does not go out, and it never goes out again. whoever keeps the ember keeps the promise. whoever carries it speaks both silences: the silence between mortals and the silence between storms.*

*the woman's hands cup the light. her hands are cracked and cold and human.*

*she says yes.*

*everything after this is yes, for a very long time.*

* * *

yes, she thinks, asleep in the blanket by the hearth, and her fingers curl around nothing, around the shape of something, around the memory of holding.

the man stirs in his chair. she watches him through the ember-light behind her eyes, this Jude who feeds strays and hates himself for it, and the ember stirs too, turning him over like a page.

there is a word under his skin. she can feel it humming. she has felt it since the door opened and he stood there filling the frame with all that weather held back behind his ribs. she thought it was the mountain. it is not the mountain.

but first—the ember wants her to see more first. the ember is old and lonely and when it finally has someone to show things to, it shows everything.

* * *

*here: the crown that knelt.*

*centuries deep now. the ember has passed hand to hand down a line of keepers—farmers, midwives, a one-eyed ferryman, a boy of nine summers who kept it through a plague winter and grew up gentle instead of hard, which the ember counts among its quiet victories. the covenant holds. storms bend around the mortal valleys like water around stones. the storm-born walk openly in the high places, and children leave offerings on cairns: honey, feathers, a carved wooden fawn with uneven legs.*

*and then a king comes up the mountain.*

*he comes on foot, deliberately, his crown left below in the valley with his horse and his honor guard, and he climbs the way the first woman climbed, in the season of white cold, and the ember understands the echo. it is meant to be an echo. kings know stories; that is mostly what kings are.*

*at the summit he kneels in the snow before the keeper—a stout grandmother with flour still on her sleeves, summoned hastily from her kitchen—and he swears the oath of the iron crown: while the ember burns, the realm shall kneel to the covenant, and not the covenant to the realm.*

*the storm-born witness. the snow glows faintly gold where his knees press it. the grandmother pulls him up by the arm and tells him his cloak is wet through and he will catch his death, and the most powerful man alive laughs, and the sound comes down the mountain like a thrown seed.*

*for two hundred years, that seed grows into something good. laws written with storm-witness. borders that storms guard better than armies. a bridge built jointly, mortal stone and Aelvar weather-singing, spanning the gorge at Drowned Pass, and the first crossing is done by children racing each other, because that is how you know a peace is real—when the children use it first.*

*the ember warms. the ember hopes. hoping is the ember's oldest mistake and it makes it every single time.*

* * *

*here: the burning.*

*it happens slowly, which is how the worst things happen. a new king, a thin king, a king who reads ledgers. a councilor who writes a paper titled ON THE MILITARY APPLICATIONS OF THE STORM-BORN QUESTION. the words question and application do their quiet work. the offerings on the cairns go unmade, then forbidden, then forgotten.*

*the keepers begin to die.*

*not all at once. a fever here. a fall from a cliff there. a house fire that spreads, somehow, impossibly, to every keeper in a single province in one night. the ember learns the taste of ash in the mouths of its dying carriers and cannot save any of them, because the ember is warmth and warmth is not a sword, and it has spent three thousand years being proud of that and now it learns otherwise.*

*the last true keeper runs. she is sixteen and quick and she carries the ember wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, and she runs north into thornfell with riders behind her, and the ember burns inside its wrapping trying to give her strength and she is only sixteen and her lungs are only so large.*

*they corner her at a shrine. a small one, a roadside one, the carved wooden fawn with uneven legs still standing in its alcove. she turns with her back to the stone and holds the ember out in front of her and says: it isn't yours to take. it was given. given things can't be taken, only broken.*

*the captain gives the order anyway.*

*the ember remembers fire. it was born in fire. it has always found fire friendly, familiar, a cousin. it learns in that moment that its cousin has been bought.*

*as she dies the keeper does the last thing left to do: she pours the ember down into herself, into blood and bone, into the smallest vessel remaining—which is her, which is a body, which is enough—and the light goes out on the mountainside and the riders find only a dead girl and a cold scrap of oilcloth, and they report the covenant extinguished, and the crown celebrates, and the storms over the high passes begin, very slowly, to curdle.*

*inside the dead girl's body, small and stubborn, the ember waits.*

*waits.*

*waits.*

*flesh fails. the ember does not. it folds itself smaller and smaller, past blood, past bone, past waiting, down into the place where souls are woven—and when the wheel of the world turns and turns and something is woven again from the same thread, the ember is in the weave.*

*a girl is born in a valley village. she does not cry at her birth. she looks at the midwife with eyes the color of the space between lightning strokes, and the midwife crosses herself and does not look at her again.*

*her mother loves her fiercely and mutely. her mother does not speak—not from birth, but from choice, or from damage, the ember never learns which—and perhaps that is why the girl does not speak either. why open a mouth when everything it could say is already ten thousand years too heavy for it.*

*when the inquisitors' questions come to the village, the mother puts the girl on a cart going north and does not wave goodbye, because waving goodbye is watching her go and the mother cannot watch, and the ember understands this completely. carrying someone means sometimes refusing to see them leave.*

*the girl rides north. the girl walks north. the girl reaches a cabin in the high thornfells in a storm of dry lightning, and knocks on a door, and a man opens it with seven years of exile in his shoulders and weather chained in his chest.*

*and the ember, folded small for so long, unfolds one corner of itself toward him—just one—and recognizes the word under his skin.*

* * *

she wakes before dawn with frost on the window and the word in her mouth.

it sits there like a hot stone. she holds it. she has held so many things alone—the whole burned history of two peoples, the kneeling crown, the murdered keepers, the bridge at the drowned pass where children raced, the paper with its terrible tidy title—and none of it has ever been shareable, none of it has ever had anywhere to go, because her mouth does not open and even if it did, who would believe her?

who believes a child? who believes a mute child who arrived in the rain with nothing?

the loneliness of it is the largest thing in her, larger than the ember, larger than the cabin, larger than the frozen black sky outside where the dry lightning still paces. she carries a dead world. she carries it the way the drowned carry water. everywhere she goes it is already inside her, doing its silent work, and no one can see it, and when she dies it will simply pour into whatever comes next and go on being unseen, being unwitnessed, being the only one who remembers the smell of honey left on cairns.

except.

except the man.

she looks at him in the gray almost-light. asleep in his chair, boots on, one hand loose near the iron poker as if even his dreams are defensive. his face in sleep is younger and sadder at once. his scars—she saw them last night when he thought she wasn't looking, pale lines across his forearms and up the side of his neck, the marks of a trial, of a sentence, of fire used as law—his scars ache in her presence, she knows that now, the ember told her: old wounds remember their making, and his were made by the same fire that murdered the keeper at the roadside shrine. the same crown. the same tidy paper, amended and re-titled across the centuries but never rewritten in substance.

they made him exile so that he would be here. the ember is nearly certain. they emptied him of country and name-honor and future and set him on this mountain like a lure, like a lantern hung out for moths, and they have been waiting seven years for something to come to his light.

something has come. she came. and the ember inside her hums with a bitter recognition: they are the same kind of caught, this man and this covenant. both leashed. both waiting without knowing what for.

the word rises in her throat.

she does not let it out. not yet. words, once given, cannot be taken back—given things can't be taken, only broken—and she learned that lesson at a roadside shrine with smoke in her ancient lungs. if she speaks his true name, the name that was his before Ravensworth, before exile, before the crown scraped it off their records like frost from glass—if she speaks it, the ember will wake fully in her, and the storms will feel it, and the things hunting her will feel it too.

but she is so tired of carrying alone.

she gets up, quiet, blanket around her shoulders like the shroud of some very small queen, and stands over him. his breath moves slow. outside, the dry lightning has stopped at last, and the silence afterward is enormous.

*Jude*, the world calls him. a good smuggler's name, half a name, a coat worn over something else.

underneath, where the ember can read him the way it reads everything warm:

*Aelirren. storm-sworn. third of that line. the one who said no.*

the one who stood in a hall of fire-law with a village's screams in his ears and refused, refused, refused—and was unmade for it politely, with paperwork, with mercy, with a leash disguised as a road north.

her eyes sting. she is six years old and ten thousand years old and both of her are crying for him, for the man who fed her when he wanted to bolt the door.

soon, she promises the sleeping room, the ember, the word held hot behind her sealed lips. soon i will say it. when the mountains are ready. when you are.

she goes to the window. the frost has made fern-patterns on the glass, and beyond it the eastern sky is doing something wrong—a bruise-colored band above the peaks where dawn should be gold, and the air has the flat pressed feeling that the ember knows from the worst year of its long life, the air before the curdling.

far down the valley, so far it might be imagination, a lantern moves. then another. a line of them, patient as stitches, working their way up the valley road toward the pass.

toward the cabin.

the girl presses her palm flat against the frozen window, and the frost melts in the shape of her hand, and behind her, Jude Ravensworth opens his eyes—because the storm in his chest has felt it too—and reaches for the poker.
