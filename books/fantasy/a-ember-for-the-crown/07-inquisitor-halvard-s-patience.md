## 7. Inquisitor Halvard's Patience

The cells beneath the Bastide of Saint Verre were not built for cruelty. That was what people never understood about them, Halvard thought as he descended the spiral stair with a lantern in one hand and a covered dish in the other. The older dungeons—the ones in the capital, the ones the balladeers sang about—had been designed by men who believed pain was an argument, that the body could be persuaded to confess what the soul refused to surrender. Those men had been amateurs. Pain made liars of everyone eventually; it asked nothing but endurance and paid for it in any coin of words. No, the cells of Saint Verre were cut from the mountain's living granite and warded so that no whisper passed between them, no prayer escaped, no sound at all traveled farther than an arm's reach. They were not instruments of suffering. They were instruments of silence.

Silence was honest work. Silence let a man hear himself think, and thinking was where truth lived.

Halvard reached the bottom of the stair and paused, as he always did, to let his eyes adjust and his breath slow. Fifty-two years old, and he still climbed stairs like a man who expected to be needed at the top of them. His colleagues in the Inquisition had gone soft early—thickened, wheezing, given to chairs—and then they had gone dead, because softness in their profession was simply another word for a wound waiting to happen. Halvard took the stairs. Halvard slept six hours. Halvard ate plainly and drank watered wine and read everything, and this, more than any talent for the work, explained why he had outlived three Directors of the Inquisition and would likely outlive a fourth.

He unlocked the fourth door on the left.

The smuggler—they had taken him off the Kestrel Road nine days ago, one of Maren's runners, a boy named Odo with a broken set-to set of knuckles and a face like a closed shutter—sat on the edge of the stone pallet exactly where he always sat, facing the wall, counting something. Halvard knew he was counting because they all counted. Nine days without speech, without light except what came when the door opened, and the mind made its own arithmetic to stay alive. Days. Breaths. Cracks in the ceiling. It did not matter what. The counting was the self insisting on itself.

"Good morning, Odo," Halvard said, and set the covered dish on the floor where the man could see it. "Roast partridge today. There's a plum sauce. I'm told the cook here overdoes the plum sauce, but I find that depends entirely on how long it's been since you've had plums."

Odo did not turn around. His shoulders were rigid as roof beams.

"I'll leave it," Halvard said. He drew the stool from the corner—his stool; he had brought it down himself years ago, oak, cushioned, because there was no reason a conversation should be conducted with either party uncomfortable—and positioned it at a respectful distance, angled so that Odo could look at him or at the wall, whichever served. Then he sat, folded his hands over his knee, and waited.

This was the part that unnerved them. He had watched it happen scores of times: the prisoner expecting the rack's logic, the iron's grammar, the whole familiar liturgy of pain that could be braced against like a storm against a seawall. And instead they got a well-dressed grandfather with flour-white hair, a lantern turned up generous and warm, and a dinner going cold on the flagstones while the silence stretched out long and level as a road.

Nine days, this one had lasted. A record, for a runner. Halvard respected it. He said so.

"You've lasted longer than men twice your size," he remarked, conversationally, to the back of Odo's head. "Longer than the Corvane brothers, and those two once held a bridge against a crown patrol for a full afternoon. You Maren people are made of stubbornness and salt. It's why I've always liked you."

Nothing. But the shoulders had changed—slightly, fractionally lower. Nine days of silence made even an enemy's voice into weather, into a thing the body leaned toward whether it wished to or not. Halvard had all the time in the world. That was the entire secret, if he was honest, the whole of his method laid bare: everyone else in the Inquisition wanted answers *now*, wanted them hot from the iron, and so they got ash. Halvard wanted answers the way a farmer wanted rain. When it came, it came thoroughly.

"The partridge will be cold soon," he observed. "Cold partridge is one of life's small tragedies."

A sound. Not quite a laugh. The ghost of one, strangled at birth. Halvard smiled at the wall and did not comment.

"You know," he went on, settling deeper into the cushion, "I spent last week in the valley below Thornfell. Ashcombe, and the little villages west of it. Asking questions, mostly. Listening, mostly." He let the pause sit. "Do you know what I heard, Odo? Nothing. That's what struck me. Nine villages, four hundred souls, and not one whisper about the child. Not one rumor, not one frightened grandmother, not one drunk shepherd saying too much at a fair. A mute girl walks out of a dry lightning storm into the mountains, a storm-caller comes out of seven years of exile to shelter her, half the network's roads bend toward Thornfell inside a month—" he shook his head slowly, though of course Odo couldn't see it, "—and the valley hears *nothing*. Do you know why?"

Odo's head moved. Not a turn. Not yet. Just a tilt. The listening tilt.

"Because your mistress is very good," Halvard said. "And because fear travels faster than fire when someone tends it properly. But mostly because the people who *did* know have already started moving, and moving people don't talk. Which means the window is narrowing, my friend. For both of us."

He rose, unhurried, and picked up the dish. The smell of roast bird and warm plum filled the cell like a small bright country. He held it a moment—let Odo hear him hold it—and then, with a small sigh of theatrical disappointment, set it back down on the floor. Closer to the pallet this time.

"Eat or don't," he said. "I'll come back tomorrow. I've been reading a fascinating account of the Thornfell ward-stones, and honestly, I'd rather talk about that than about you anyway. Did you know some of them predate the kingdom? Predate *kingdoms*, plural? There are inscriptions—"

"*Stop.*"

The voice came out ruined—a rusted hinge, a thing unused for nine days—and Odo turned at last, and his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and desperately young. Halvard kept his expression exactly where it was: mild, patient, faintly pleased, the face of a man being handed the first course of a long meal.

"There you are," he said warmly. "Would you like the partridge warm? I can have them warm it."

Odo's mouth worked. Whatever defiance he had left was fighting whatever the silence had done to him, and losing, and he knew it was losing, and that knowledge was its own kind of wound. "You're not going to hurt me," he rasped. "That's the trick, isn't it. Everyone says the quiet ones are worse, but you're not worse. You just wait."

"Yes," Halvard agreed pleasantly. "That is the trick. Would you like the partridge warm?"

Something broke in the boy's face—not dramatically, nothing so clean as breaking glass, more like a thaw, ice going grey and rotten all at once. "Why me?" he whispered. "Maren's people. Why not break the big ones, the road-captains, why come down on a runner—"

"Because a runner knows the roads," Halvard said, "and a runner knows which roads are open *this week*. And because you were carrying salt through the Kestrel gap on the ninth day of the month, which means you were within thirty miles of Thornfell's western passes when a certain cabin acquired a second occupant." He sat back down on his stool and folded his hands again, and now his voice lost a shade of its warmth—not gaining coldness, never coldness, only depth, the way deep water looks dark even at noon. "I'm not interested in hurting you, Odo. I am interested in a message. You're going to carry it back to Maren, because she won't listen to a letter, and she won't listen to a herald, but she'll listen to a runner she thinks bought his freedom with blood money. Let her think that. I don't mind being hated for the wrong reasons. Hatred keeps a message warm better than anything."

Odo stared at him. "Message."

"Three parts." Halvard raised a finger. "First: the roads close. All of them, every pass out of Thornfell, by season's end. Sealed garrisons at every gap, and the Inquisition's writ extended over the high country. She's run contraband under three kings' noses; tell her I admire it, and that this cargo is not salt."

Second finger. "Second: the price of the old debts is coming due. She has carried Ravensworth on her books for seven years—shelter, passage, silence, all of it written in the ledger of favors your network keeps instead of gods. Tell her the crown is prepared to forgive the ledger. All of it. Every debt, every name, full pardon, the network walks free and fat into the new order." He paused. "Tell her also that if she declines, I will begin closing waystations. Not burning them—I know how that sounds, and I want to be precise. *Closing* them. Each one found, each one emptied, each one's people standing in a valley square holding a paper that says *inquisitorial custody* on it. She'll understand the arithmetic. She's better at arithmetic than anyone alive."

"And the third?" Odo's voice had gone thin.

Halvard looked at him for a long moment, and something in his gaze shifted—the grandfather receding, the certainty underneath surfacing, calm as bedrock. "Third," he said quietly, "tell her that Jude Ravensworth should ask himself why he is still alive. Seven years, exile, treason by omission, a power that could crack the kingdom's spine—and every patrol that found his trail somehow lost it. Every warrant with his name somehow misfiled. Every noose meant for his neck somehow rotted before the hanging." He smiled, and there was nothing cruel in it, which was the worst thing about it. "Mercy leaves tracks, Odo. Sooner or later even the most fortunate man wonders whose hand kept opening the gate. Tell him the hand is still open. Tell him it has always been open. And tell him that what it wants now is not his capture."

He stood, brushed off his coat, and lifted the lantern.

"It wants his gratitude."

* * *

Halvard did not go directly up after the interview. He stopped two landings above, in the scriptorium that had been carved into the mountain by monks three centuries dead, and stood for a time among the shelves, letting the ritual of the place settle over him. Scriptorium, archive, cells: the Bastide had been built downward, faith inverted into procedure, God replaced by records. He found it restful. Most men needed churches; Halvard needed filing.

Brother-Clerk Anselm was waiting at the long table with the night's documents squared and ribboned, because Anselm was always waiting with the night's documents squared and ribboned, and Halvard privately considered him the second-most dangerous man in the province.

"The prisoner?" Anselm asked. He was a small, precise man with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who had read every confession the Bastide had ever produced and remembered all of them.

"Released at dawn. The east postern, unmarked, with an hour's start." Halvard unwound his scarf and sat, drawing the top folio toward him. "He'll run straight to Maren, because that's what his legs believe, and Maren will do the rest."

"And if she doesn't take the bait?"

"She will hate taking it, and she will take it. Loyalty is a currency, Anselm, and currencies fail when you debase them hard enough. We've been debasing hers for a year." He flipped the folio open. "What do we have from the valley?"

"Confirmations." Anselm slid a page across. "The miller at Ashcombe saw a child on the mountain road three weeks past, alone, before the first snow. A drover swears his dogs wouldn't bark at her—just lay down, all four of them, flat, like they were worshipping or dying. And the hermit at Cold Well Chapel reports lights on Thornfell's shoulder on the night of the dry storm. Steady light. A cabin's light." Anselm hesitated, which he almost never did. "There's also this. A peddler came through Vairemont claiming he'd sold dried cod and lamp oil to a woman matching Maren's description, heading for the high passes with two mules and a companion who kept her hood up. The description of the companion—"

"Is a girl of eight or nine who does not speak. Yes." Halvard read the page without appearing to. "So the network is already moving her. Good. Moving things are catchable things."

"You seem very certain," Anselm said carefully. "Forgive me, Director-in-waiting, but the Aelvar matter—the covenant—if even half the old texts are true, the Ember isn't a fugitive. It's a *force*. Men have built religions on less than half of what those texts claim. And we're treating it like a smuggled cargo of silk."

Halvard set the page down and regarded his clerk with genuine fondness. This was the question, of course. It deserved an answer.

"When I was a young inquisitor," he said, "I served under Director Cassel, may the ground lie light on him, and Cassel had a saying: *the difference between a force and a tool is custody*. Fire is a force. Put it in a hearth, and it is a tool. The covenant is a force. It has been sitting in a hearth for nine hundred years, keeping the realms temperate, warming both houses—and what has it earned? Look at the histories, Anselm. Kingdoms that knelt to the storm-court and were repaid with droughts. Storm-callers honored for a generation and burned in the next. Aelvar who gave their oaths and watched every mortal dynasty eventually reach for their power anyway. The covenant has failed, not because it was broken from outside, but because it was never *owned* by anyone. It floated. Loose forces float, and loose forces drown people."

He rose and walked to the narrow window slit, where the pre-dawn dark was just beginning to bruise grey over the eastern crags.

"The Ember bound to a bloodline is different. Bound to the crown, sworn through coronation-oil and inheritance law, administered by the Inquisition—it becomes what fire should always have been. Governed. Predictable. *Hers*." He did not say the Queen Regent's name; one did not, in working documents or working rooms. "No more droughts as punishment. No more tempests as tantrum. Weather by charter. Realms in contract rather than in superstition. That is not ambition, Anselm, whatever the romantics will call it afterward. That is *order*. The difference between a flood and a reservoir."

"And the child?"

"A vessel." He said it without weight, the way one says *a cask* or *a keyhole*. "Vessels matter less than what they carry. Though—" he paused, and honesty was a discipline with him too, "—the texts say the Ember chooses its vessels, which would make her the most important person alive, if I believed the texts literally. I believe in custody, Anselm. The texts can believe whatever they like."

Anselm gathered the pages, then paused with the folio half-tied. "One more item. From the old files. You asked me to pull the Ravensworth trial records—the true ones, not the public set." He laid a slim packet on the table, sealed with the Inquisition's black wax, and Halvard felt the particular pleasure of a key finding its lock. "There's a discrepancy in the third witness deposition. The one signed 'a traveler of the Thornfell roads.' The hand matches no traveler in our registers. It matches—" Anselm's ink-stained finger tapped the page, once, delicately, "—mine. From twenty-three years ago. Before I took clerical orders. When I was stationed in the valley under the name Veyran, doing settlement work among the free-traders."

For the first time that night, Halvard laughed—a short, dry, genuinely amused sound.

"Of course it does," he said. "Seven years I've wondered if she'd ever surface in the file trail. Maren's own shadow, planted beside Ravensworth before his trial was even scheduled, feeding us the testimony that made exile stick." He tapped the packet. "She's been ours for twenty-three years and hers for seven, and she has played both sides so long I genuinely cannot tell you which leash she feels. Which is why, when the moment comes, we will not rely on her at all. We'll simply make sure both sides believe she's theirs, right up until the altar burns."

Anselm tied the folio. "And Ravensworth himself. If he refuses the message. If Maren closes the roads and he digs in—"

"He won't refuse," Halvard said mildly, taking up his lantern. "Men who don't know they've been leashed always pull hardest when they finally see the collar. He'll come down from that mountain furious, and fury is the most navigable weather there is. I've arranged his whole route for him, Anselm. Seven years I've arranged it. It would be rude of him to deviate now."

He climbed the stair toward his rooms, unhurried, the lantern swinging a small gold arc against the ancient stone. Behind him the cells held their perfect silence, and above them the sky over Thornfell was beginning, very faintly, to flicker—with no cloud anywhere near, and no thunder following, and no rain at all.

Dry lightning, the valley folk would call it.

Halvard called it a schedule, and he was pleased with how closely it kept to his.
