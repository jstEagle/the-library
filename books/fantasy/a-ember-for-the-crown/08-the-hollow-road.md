## 8. The Hollow Road

There were three ways through the Spine of Thornfell that Jude trusted, and he trusted them the way a man trusts a knife that has already cut him—knowing its edges, knowing its cost, never forgetting that it could turn in his hand.

The Hollow Road was none of the three.

He had found it by accident, seven years ago, in his first winter of exile, half-dead and snow-blind, when the mountain had simply opened for him. A seam in the cliff face west of the Grey Stair, no wider than a wagon, running into the rock like a word swallowed mid-sentence. He had never been able to find it again on purpose. He had tried. He had mapped the approach a dozen times in his mind, walked the scree with his eyes closed, counted paces from the split birch to the place where the stone should have parted—and the stone had never parted. The mountain showed the road when it wished, and kept it when it did not, and Jude had long ago filed it away with the other mysteries he refused to think about: the ache in his scars before weather turned, the way birds went silent around him, the dreams of falling upward through green light.

He was thinking about none of that now. He was thinking about arithmetic. Three days of hard walking to the high valley where Maren's people would meet them. Two children of bread left, half a wheel of cheese, and the child herself, who ate like a sparrow and slept like the dead and had not made a single sound in six days.

Vey walked behind him, light on her feet despite the pack, and the child rode his shoulders with her hands laced under his chin, her weight almost nothing, her silence total. The morning was the color of pewter. Below them the valley smoked with woodsmoke from farms that did not yet know what was coming down out of the capital—wagons with the crown's black-and-gold on their doors, men with gentle voices and patient eyes.

"Your road isn't here," Vey said. It was not a question. She had a way of stating things that made them into accusations without the mess of asking.

"The road will be here or it won't."

"And if it won't?"

"Then we climb the Grey Stair, and the Grey Stair is watched, and we probably die." He said it flatly, the way he said everything now. "I'd rather the road."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh in a woman who laughed. "You've changed, Ravensworth. The man they exiled argued with mountains."

"The man they exiled is dead. I'm what's left. He does arithmetic."

The child's fingers tightened against his collarbone. Just once. He felt it like a word.

They came down off the shoulder of the fell as the light thickened toward noon, and Jude stopped counting paces because counting had never worked, and simply walked to the place where the cliff stood, and stood there, feeling foolish, feeling the old ache kindle in his scars like a banked fire remembering itself.

The stone opened.

It did not swing or grind. There was no sound at all. One moment there was granite, grey and seamless and older than any kingdom's founding, and the next there was a passage, tall enough for a man on horseback, breathing out air that smelled of deep places and rain that had fallen before men learned to build. The dark inside was not the dark of a cave. It was the dark behind your eyelids. It was the dark of somewhere being.

Vey's hand went to her knife. "What in the nine hells—"

"The Hollow Road," Jude said, and heard, to his own surprise, that his voice had gone quiet, the way it went quiet in chapels. He hadn't been in a chapel in nine years. "It's on no map. I didn't find it. It found me, once, and I've never understood why." He looked up at the child on his shoulders. Her eyes were open, and fixed on the passage, and in the flat grey light they were not a child's eyes at all. They were the color of the moment before lightning. "I'm starting to."

* * *

Inside, the road was not stone the way stone should be.

The walls were smooth without ever having been cut, and they were veined with lines of faint luminescence, like the memory of lightning frozen in amber, and the lines ran in patterns that Jude's eye kept trying to read and his mind kept refusing. They were not decoration. He understood that with a certainty that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone. They were writing.

The child made her first sound in six days.

It was not a word. It was a hum, low and resonant, and it came from her chest rather than her throat, and the moment it left her the veins in the walls answered. Light ran along them like fire along a fuse, away into the dark ahead of them, away into the dark behind, and the whole passage shuddered—or did not shudder, but *breathed*—and somewhere far above and far below, in the roots of the mountain, something that had been sleeping for a very long time turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

"Jude." Vey's voice, very level. She was a woman who had been in collapsing tunnels and burning waystations and had never once raised her voice. It was level now. It was also, for the first time since he had known her, afraid. "What did she just do?"

"I don't know."

"You always know. That's what you are. The man who knows the mountain."

"The mountain knows *her*," Jude said. "I'm starting to think that's the whole of the arithmetic."

They walked. There was nothing else to do. The road descended in long, gentle sweeps, and the light in the walls rose and fell with the child's breathing, brightening when she was awake and alert, dimming when she drooped against Jude's neck, so that the passage itself seemed to be watching her, tending her, the way a hearth tends a flame it cannot bear to lose.

And the wards woke.

Jude felt them before he saw them—a pressure in his ears, a taste like copper and rain, the fine hairs on his arms lifting. Ahead, where the passage narrowed between two pillars of uncut rock, the air itself had begun to shimmer, and out of the shimmer came shapes. Not stepping out of it. *Condensing* out of it, the way frost condenses on a cold window: tall figures of grey light and drifting motes, faceless, crowned with what might have been antlers or lightning or both, standing in a line across the road with the stillness of things that had stood there for a thousand years and would stand there for a thousand more.

Vey's knife was out. Jude's hand went back and found her wrist and held it, not hard, but absolutely.

"Don't."

"They're going to—"

"They're not going to do anything. They're *wards*. They're old." His own voice sounded strange to him, distant, as if someone else were speaking through his mouth. "They're older than the crown. They're older than the First Kingdom. They're—"

The child slid down from his shoulders.

She walked forward, small bare feet silent on the stone, straight toward the line of shining figures, and Jude's heart slammed once against his ribs and then stopped, because there was nothing in him that could have stopped her, nothing in the world. She stopped an arm's length from the nearest ward and looked up at it, the way a child looks up at a parent, and she hummed again, that low chest-deep note, and the ward *bowed*.

It folded down and inward like light bending around a weight, and where its face should have been there was—for one heartbeat—something like recognition. Something like joy. Something like the face of a sentry on a wall who has watched the horizon for a lifetime and finally sees the banner coming home.

Then it straightened, and the line of wards parted, and the road lay open beyond them, and the child turned around and looked at Jude and held out her hand.

*Come,* said the hand.

Jude took it. Her fingers were warm. Her fingers were *glowing*, faintly, the way the walls glowed, and he found that he could not look away from that, and that some part of him, some part he had buried under seven years of arithmetic and numbness, was weeping without tears.

* * *

They made camp in a wide chamber where the road branched, because the child was flagging and because Jude's legs had begun to shake and he was honest enough with himself to know it was not the walking. He got a fire going—small, disciplined, the fire of a man who had learned that light draws eyes—and Vey checked the dark passages with a lamp and reported, in her flat way, that the walls were still writing at them and she didn't like it.

"She's not just a girl the crown wants," Vey said, settling across the fire from him. The child was asleep against Jude's pack, and the light in the walls had dimmed to a slow pulse, like a sleeping heartbeat. "You knew that when you came to Maren. You underplayed it. I watched you underplay it."

"I told her the crown was hunting her."

"You told her the crown was hunting a child. You didn't tell her the child makes mountains open their mouths." Vey's eyes caught the firelight. They were very steady. "What is she, Ravensworth?"

And Jude, who had spent seven years perfecting the art of the answer that was no answer, who had traded every dangerous truth he owned for the quiet life of a guide who did arithmetic, heard himself say:

"I don't know. And that's the truth. But I know this." He looked at the sleeping child, at the pulse of light in the stone that rose and fell with her breath. "The world was built on some kind of promise. A covenant, the old stories say, between the storm-born and the people who needed the storms to stop. And everyone's forgotten the terms. Everyone's forgotten it was ever signed." His voice dropped. "I don't think she's forgotten. I think she *is* the signature."

Vey was quiet for a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere deep in the mountain, faint as a dream, something hummed back at the child's sleeping breath, and the walls pulsed, and pulsed again.

"And you?" Vey said at last. "What are you in all this? Because I saw those wards, and I saw them look at you too. Not the way they looked at her. But they *saw* you. Wards that old don't see anyone."

Jude looked at his hands in the firelight. Hands that had once called lightning out of a clear sky. Hands that had refused, at the cost of everything, to call it down on a village of farmers, and had been branded and exiled for the refusal, and had spent seven years pretending the refusal had burned the gift out of him along with his name.

"I'm nobody," he said. "I'm the man who carries her pack."

Vey looked at him for a long time. Then she took out a small oilskin packet from inside her coat—Jude's stomach dropped, because he knew what an oilskin packet meant in Maren's network, he had carried enough of them, they were for records, for debts, for things that needed to survive fire and water—and she held it for a moment, turning it over in her fingers.

Then she put it back.

"Nobody," she said, "carries a pack for a signature." She lay down with her back to the fire. "Sleep, Ravensworth. Tomorrow we find out what the mountain wants with you."

* * *

He dreamed of the trial.

He dreamed of the great hall in the capital, and the nine justices in their storm-grey robes, and his own voice, younger and raw, saying *they were farmers, they were children, I will not*—and he dreamed, as he always dreamed, of the brand coming down, and the pain, and the strange clean silence afterward, the silence that had lasted seven years.

But this time the dream changed.

This time, when he looked down at the brand on his forearm—the raven in a circle of storm, the mark of a storm-caller struck from the rolls of the gifted—he saw that beneath it, faint as the veins in the Hollow Road's walls, there was writing. The same writing. The same lines, running under his skin like light under ice, in patterns his eye could almost read and his mind almost refused.

And a voice that was not a voice, that came from the stone of the dream itself, from the mountain, from the thing waking in the roots of the world, said:

*Not dead. Only sleeping. Only waiting for her.*

Jude woke with his heart hammering and the child's hand resting on his arm, her eyes open in the dark, glowing faintly, watching him with an expression he had no name for. Not pity. Not wonder. Something older than both.

The walls of the Hollow Road pulsed once, twice, three times—bright, brighter, bright as noon—and far away, in the direction they had come from, in the direction of the valley and the farms and the patient men in black and gold, Jude felt, through scars that had ached for six days without stopping, the first cold breath of a storm that was not a storm.

Something had entered the mountain behind them.

Vey was already awake, knife out, lamp lit, her face in the lamplight perfectly calm and perfectly unreadable, and she said, in her flat voice:

"Whatever's following us, Ravensworth—it's not walking. It's coming the way the road comes. And it's faster."
