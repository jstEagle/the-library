## 9. Storm-Born

The pass had no name on any map Maren's people kept, and Jude had learned, over seven years of guiding contraband through the Thornfells, that the nameless places were the only ones worth trusting. Named passes were patrolled. Named passes appeared in the ledgers of toll-collectors and the reports of scouts. A pass without a name belonged to the mountain alone, and the mountain asked for payment in caution rather than coin.

He rose before dawn out of habit rather than necessity. The child was still asleep by the fire's ashes, curled with her back against Vey's bedroll, and for a moment he stood in the cabin doorway watching her breathe and hating how quickly he had grown used to the sight of it. Seven days since she'd arrived soaked at his door. Seven days of sleep-speech in a language that made his scars ache like weather coming on. Six days since old Tammas Whitlock had coughed blood into his palm and whispered *Firsttongue* before the fever took him down to Hollowmere and whatever waited for dying smugglers there.

*Move her through the mountains,* he'd told himself. Simple arithmetic. The crown wanted her; therefore the crown would search; therefore the low roads were death; therefore the high road, the cold road, the road that hurt.

Arithmetic was safe. Arithmetic didn't ask what he felt about the small warm weight of her hand when she took it without asking, which she did now every morning, as though it had always been hers.

She woke as he laced his boots. No crying, no fussing—she simply opened her eyes and looked at him with that terrible ancient patience children should not possess, and then she raised one finger and pointed east, toward the high shoulder of the pass, where the sky was doing something wrong.

Jude stepped outside and felt it before he understood it: a pressure behind his sternum, a taste of iron and ozone on air that should have smelled only of pine and frost. The clouds over the eastern peaks were stacked in long gray shelves, too orderly, too still, like soldiers waiting for an order no one wanted to give. Dry lightning flickered inside them without sound, vein after vein of white light trapped in glass.

"That's not natural," Vey said from behind him. She had her hood up already, her crossbow slung across her back, her face arranged in its usual expression of mild professional boredom. "Storms move. That one's *waiting*."

"Everything about this child is waiting," Jude said. He checked the straps of his pack, tightened the cinch on the mule's girth, and did not look at the sky again, because looking made the pressure in his chest worse, made something old and buried turn over in its sleep and stretch. "We make the high traverse before midday. After that we're in the stone forest, and I'd rather be through it than in it when that breaks."

Vey glanced at the child, then at him, and said nothing. She had been saying nothing for six days, and each silence had begun to feel less like discretion and more like inventory.

They climbed.

The high traverse was a shelf of rock barely wide enough for the mule, cut into the flank of a peak whose summit wore its snow year-round. Below them the valley fell away in folds of dark pine; above, the cliff rose in strata of gray and rust, banded like old muscle. The wind came up the face in slow, cold breaths, and the child rode the mule with her hood back, her face tipped up into it, eyes closed, as though she were listening to something in the wind that the wind itself did not yet know it was saying.

Twice during the morning Jude caught himself humming.

He stopped both times, appalled. It wasn't a tune he knew—or it was, but from nowhere, from some place below memory—a falling sequence of notes, minor and patient, like water finding its way down through stone. The second time, the child opened her eyes and looked at him, and something in her expression was so close to joy that it frightened him more than the storm-light building in the east.

"Don't," he said quietly, not knowing whether he meant it for her or himself.

She smiled anyway, and hummed three notes back, and the pressure behind his sternum eased so suddenly that he swayed and put a hand against the cliff.

*What is happening to me?*

He had spent seven years burying the storm-calling, and he had believed—had needed to believe—that burial worked. That power left fallow simply went quiet, like a field gone to weed, like a voice unused going hoarse. But the child's presence was rain on that buried field, and things were rising through the soil that he had no names for: words surfacing in his mind with their meanings attached, whole and alien, like fish rising to feed. Words for *wind-that-remembers*. Words for *the-oath-that-burns*. Words he had never been taught, arriving unbidden, wearing meanings like borrowed coats.

By midday they had reached the stone forest—a high plateau where the wind had carved the rock into leaning pillars, some slender as spears, some broad as drowned men, all of them grooved and fluted by ten thousand years of weather. In fog or dusk it was a place to avoid; smugglers swore the pillars moved. In full light it was merely beautiful, and Jude hated it a little for that, because beauty made him careless.

The storm had followed them. It hung now over the eastern rim of the plateau, close enough that he could see the lightning moving inside it like eels under ice, and still it did not break, did not advance, did not retreat. Waiting.

"We rest here," he said. "Twenty minutes. The mule needs water."

Vey took the perimeter without being asked, moving between the pillars with her crossbow cradled, and Jude knelt by the child and held the waterskin for her while she drank. Up close, in this light, he could see faint marks along her jaw and throat—not bruises, not freckles. Lines. Fine as spider silk, pale gold, tracing patterns down the side of her neck that resolved, when he let his eyes unfocus, into writing.

Firsttongue. Written on her skin by no ink he could see.

"You're a letter," he murmured. "Someone wrote you, and sent you."

She pressed her small hand flat against his chest, over the sternum, directly over the pressure that had lived there for seven days, and the touch went through him like cold water through hot iron. For one heartbeat he heard—not words, not images, but *weather*, vast and structured, a storm with grammar—and then she withdrew her hand and the vision collapsed, leaving him kneeling in the gravel with his heart slamming and his ears ringing.

Above them, inside the waiting cloud, the silent lightning flashed twice. Quick. Deliberate. Like a signal.

Vey's whistle came sharp from among the pillars—the two-note call that meant *contact, unknown, hold position*—and then the world came apart.

* * *

They came down out of the sky the way weather comes down: all at once, from everywhere, without transition. One moment the plateau was empty; the next there were figures standing on the pillars themselves, tall and narrow as the stones they perched upon, and the light around them bent wrong, the way light bends above a fire.

Aelvar.

Jue had seen one exactly once, twenty years ago, from a distance, at a festival where the crown paraded a captive storm-born in gilded chains for the crowds to marvel at. He remembered the wrongness of the proportions, the way the crowd's noise had died without anyone deciding it should die. These were like that memory given motion: taller than men, their limbs too long and too fluid, their skin the gray-blue of deep thunderheads, their eyes lidless and silver, reflecting lightning that was not there.

There were five of them. They did not draw weapons. They did not need to; the air around each of them crawled with charge, and Jude's scars lit up with sympathetic pain, every old burn-mark on his forearms glowing faintly blue-white, announcing him like a beacon.

*They can feel what I am.* The thought arrived with perfect clarity and perfect horror. *Seven years of burial, and I am lit like a signal fire.*

One of them descended—simply stepped off its pillar and drifted down, unhurried, robes of woven gray streaming—and landed ten paces away. Its gaze passed over Vey, over the mule, over Jude, and fixed on the child with an intensity that bent the air.

It spoke. The language was Firsttongue, and Jude understood every word, which was impossible, and the understanding arrived in his mind fully formed, translated before he could hear the raw sound:

***You carry the vessel bound. You have chained the Ember's daughter.***

"No." Jude's voice came out rough. He put himself between the Aelvar and the child, hands open and empty, palms forward—an old smuggler's gesture, *nothing here worth your trouble*—and knew even as he did it that it was useless. "No one is chained. She came to me. She *chose*—"

***Liar.*** Not angry. Worse: certain. ***Mortals bound us with oaths and burned us with the breaking of them. Mortals do not choose. Mortals take.***

The other four descended now, spreading in a slow arc, and the pressure in the air built until the fine hairs on Jude's arms stood upright and the mule screamed and tore free of its tether, bolting back down the trail. The child tugged at Jude's coat and tried to step around him, and he caught her shoulders and held her behind him, and the Aelvar nearest made a sound—not speech, something lower, a subsonic thrum that Jude felt in his teeth—

And the child answered it.

She stepped out from behind him, small and straight-backed in her oversized coat, faced the tallest of the scouts, and spoke. Her first deliberate words—not sleep-speech, not fragments, but a full sentence in Firsttongue, delivered with the cadence of someone reciting law. Jude understood it as it left her lips, the translation blooming in his skull like frost-flower:

***I am not taken. I walk. The covenant walks with me. Stand down, sons of the squall-line, or stand aside.***

The Aelvar went utterly still.

Then the leader tilted its head, and its silver eyes moved from the child to Jude, and Jude felt that gaze like a hand pressing on a bruise.

***This one speaks our tongue without learning it.*** A pause, and something moved through the scout's posture that might, in a human face, have been unease. ***His mouth says mortal. His marrow says otherwise.***

"I'm not—" Jude began, and stopped, because he genuinely did not know how to finish the sentence. *I'm not what?* He had spent seven years being nothing: exiled, unnamed in the ledgers, a guide with no history. And now these beings looked at him and saw something he had never consented to be.

The child touched his hand. And the words came.

He did not decide to speak. That was the terror of it—he felt the Firsttongue rise through him the way floodwater rises through a well shaft, and his mouth opened and his voice came out layered, doubled, his own rough baritone underneath and something older and vaster speaking through the same throat, and the language poured out of him in sentences he understood only as he finished them:

***I was called to the storm before I could walk, as you were. I refused the crown's leash, and was broken for it, and buried my birthright in the mountain's cold, and have carried its weight seven years in silence. If the vessel walks free beside me, then judge me by her walking, not by your grief. I have grief enough of my own.***

Silence. Even the wind seemed to hold itself.

The lead scout drifted closer, close enough that Jude could see his own reflection fractured in those silver eyes—saw, absurdly, that his reflection's outline shimmered faintly, blurred at the edges, as though he were slightly out of focus against the world.

***Words are cheap,*** the scout said. ***Oaths are proven in weather.*** It raised one long hand, and the waiting storm above the eastern rim finally, finally broke—not on them, but *around* them, a ring of descending wind and hard rain enclosing the plateau in a cylinder of roaring gray, cutting them off from the world. Within the ring, the air went calm and clear and terribly quiet.

***Stand in the circle, storm-caller. Let the weather read you.***

Every instinct Jude owned screamed at him to run, to grab the child, to get off this plateau. But there was nowhere to run inside a ring of living storm, and the child released his hand and stepped back, watching him with those ancient eyes, and he understood that she would not stop this, that perhaps she *could* not, that this was a door he had been walking toward since the night she knocked.

He stepped into the rain.

It hit him like recognition. The water was cold and ordinary and it *knew* him—it ran down his arms and traced his scars and every mark lit brighter, and the wind circled him once, twice, reading him the way a magistrate reads a sealed letter, and inside his chest the buried thing he had starved for seven years woke fully and *reached*, and Jude cried out—not in pain, in the unbearable relief of a cramped limb finally stretched—and the storm answered him, leaned toward him, loved him, and he felt himself beginning to tip into it, to dissolve into it, to become weather instead of man—

A small hand seized his.

The child. Standing in the roaring rain beside him, unmoved by it, holding his hand, anchoring him the way a keel anchors a sail. Through her grip he felt her do something—not pulling him back, but *shaping* him, giving the flood a channel, and the channel had banks, and the banks were *himself*, and he did not dissolve. He stood in the heart of the storm-ring with a mute child's hand in his and the full attention of the sky pouring through him, and he did not break.

Slowly, the ring wound down. The rain thinned. The last gusts sighed away across the plateau, and the pillars stood dripping in sudden ordinary sunlight, and the five Aelvar regarded him with expressions he could not read and did not want to try.

The leader approached and drew something from its robes: a thin band of gray metal, cool-looking, etched with script that moved slowly like clouds crossing a sky. Without asking, it took Jude's right wrist and closed the band around it. The metal warmed against his pulse and then went still, and the script settled into a single line of characters that Jude, horribly, could now read:

*Claimed.*

***The court will weigh you,*** the scout said. ***Until then, you are marked. What is claimed may be summoned. What is claimed may be judged. Walk carefully, storm-caller, for there are those among us who argued for your death before we ever saw your face—and their grief has more votes than your mercy.***

It stepped back. The five of them rose together into the air like smoke reversing, and were gone, and the plateau was empty except for wind, and stone, and the fading smell of ozone.

Jude stared at his wrist. The band sat there, light as a rumor, unmistakable as a brand.

"They marked you," Vey said from behind him. Her voice was carefully neutral, but he heard the note beneath it—the recorder's note, the note of a woman filing something away. "That's new."

"Yes," Jude said. He flexed his hand. The band did not move. "It's new."

The child tugged his sleeve. When he looked down, she pointed east—not toward the storm, which was dissipating, but beyond it, deeper into the mountains, toward a wall of peaks that even Maren's maps declined to name. Then she looked up at him, and her expression was gentle and implacable at once, and though she made no sound, the meaning arrived in his bones with the certainty of Firsttongue:

*Toward the Ember. There isn't much time.*

Jude closed his eyes. Behind them, unbidden, rose the image of his own reflection in silver eyes, blurred at the edges, dissolving.

Seven years he had fought to remain exactly what he was: nothing, no one, a shadow on a mountainside. And in one morning he had spoken a language older than kingdoms, been read by the weather, and been branded property of a court that wanted him dead.

*Claimed.*

He opened his eyes and shouldered his pack.

"All right," he said quietly, to the child, to the mountains, to whatever was left of the man he'd been a week ago. "East, then."

Behind them, far down the valley, so faint it might have been imagination, a bell began to ring—inquisitors' bells, the crown's hunting-call, echoing up from Hollowmere.

They had marked him from the sky. Now the crown had marked the road.

Jude pulled the child's hood up against the wind, and they walked.
