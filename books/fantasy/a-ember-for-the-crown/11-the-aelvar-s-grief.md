## 11. The Aelvar's Grief

The storm-court convened in the hollow beneath the world, where the roots of Thornfell hung down like the ribs of a drowned god, and the lightning that lived between the realms came home to roost.

Sylwen had walked the long spiral down nine hundred times in her long life, and she had counted every step, because counting was what she did instead of weeping. Nine hundred and forty-one steps from the high eyries where the Aelvar rode the gales to the floor of the court, where the Ember's cradle had once burned so brightly that the stone itself had learned to glow. The stone still glowed. But it was the glow of coals at dawn, banked and dying, and everyone in the court pretended not to see it, the way mortals pretended not to see their own funerals approaching.

She took her place at the western arc, where the elders of the Grief stood. That was what they called themselves now, the faction she had built from the ashes of her own heart—not a party, not a philosophy, but a grief, given a name so that it could speak in council. The Grief. It was honest, at least. The elders of the Vigil sat opposite, those who still tended the covenant's embers and whispered to them each night as if a dead fire could be coaxed back to breathing by devotion alone.

Between them, suspended in the air on nothing at all, hung the Heart of Storms: a sphere of glass-dark cloud, turning slowly, shot through with veins of light that flickered like a dying thing's pulse. It was the covenant made visible. Once, it had burned white and steady, and the mortal realms had known three hundred years without a killing frost, without a flood that ate coastlines, without the sky opening like a wound.

Now the veins of light guttered. Now the sphere turned crooked, like a wheel with a broken spoke.

And now, Sylwen thought, watching the Heart, it was time to say aloud what everyone already knew.

The Herald of the Court rose—a young one, barely two centuries old, his wings still the pale grey of unfledged things—and struck the stone floor with the antler of the first stag, and the sound went through the chamber like a bell through water.

"The court is gathered," the Herald said. "The matter before us is the covenant, and its failing. Who will speak?"

Sylwen rose. She did not spread her wings, though the old etiquette called for it when addressing the full court. Let them mark the discourtesy. Let them understand that she was done with courtesies, done with the slow dances of diplomacy that had let the covenant rot for a century while everyone sang its praises.

"I will speak," she said, and her voice carried the way an Aelvar's voice carries, with the wind inside it, so that every ear in the vast hollow heard her as though she stood at their shoulder. "I will speak the words no one wants spoken, because someone must, and I have earned the right to be the one who says them. I was alive when the covenant was forged. I knelt on the mountain with the first storm-caller's hand on my brow. I heard the Ember speak. I have outlived every oath-taker who stood beside me that day, and I am the only one left who remembers what we promised—and what we were promised in return."

She let the silence stretch. In the Heart of Storms, a vein of light flickered and went out, and a low murmur moved through the court like wind through wheat.

"The covenant is dead," Sylwen said.

The murmur became a roar. The Vigil elders were on their feet, wings half-spread, crying *blasphemy* and *the Ember endures* and *the fire only sleeps*—the same words, the same cries, the same desperate liturgy they had sung for a hundred years while the light dimmed and the storms worsened and the mortals below forgot them, kingdom by kingdom, generation by generation, until the only mortals who still spoke of the Aelvar did so in the way one speaks of ghosts: with a shiver and a glance at the sky.

Sylwen waited. She was very good at waiting. Grief had taught her that; grief was nothing but waiting with the wound open.

When the court had spent itself, she continued, quieter now, and the quiet made them lean in.

"Look at the Heart," she said. "Look at it. I know you have all stopped looking, because looking hurts, and we are creatures who have made an art of not hurting. But look." She gestured, and the Heart's light flared at her summons, and every elder saw what they had trained themselves not to see: the veins of light thin as spider silk, the dark patches spreading across the sphere like frost across a window, the crooked, laboring turn of it. "That is a dying thing. That has been a dying thing since the crown of Vhalor broke its half of the oath and burned the last of the storm-callers' line. We told ourselves the fire would find another vessel. We told ourselves the mortals would remember. We told ourselves a hundred comforting lies, and every year the Heart grew darker, and every year the storms on the far side of the veil grew crueler, and every year we did nothing, because doing something would mean admitting the lies were lies."

"And what would you do?" It was Caerion who spoke, eldest of the Vigil, his voice like the groan of a glacier. He was older than Sylwen by a thousand years, and he had not stood in three centuries, but he stood now, leaning on his staff of storm-forged iron. "You would sever the covenant. You would cut the Heart from the mortal realms and let it die alone, and take the storms with it—or so you promise. Do you truly believe the tempests will follow the covenant into the dark? Or do you only want it to be over, Sylwen? Do you want the waiting to end, even if the ending is amputation?"

The question landed where he meant it to land. Sylwen felt it slide between her ribs, precise as a surgeon's blade, and she hated him for his accuracy, and she loved him for it, because they had been young together under the first sky, before grief had made them strangers.

"Yes," she said. "I want the waiting to end. I will not pretend otherwise. I buried a daughter under this mountain, Caerion. I buried her because mortals broke faith and the storms came down and she stood in the gap, as storm-callers do, and she died holding a covenant that the other side had already abandoned. I have watched four hundred of our people die in the veil-wars that followed, defending passes and shrines and villages full of mortals who could not name us anymore. And I have watched this court do nothing but *wait*, because waiting feels like faith and faith feels like virtue." She turned, slowly, so that her gaze crossed the whole western arc, the whole of the Grief. "The waiting is not faith. It is cowardice wearing faith's robes. The covenant is dead. The only question left is whether we die with it, politely, over another century—or whether we cut ourselves free, seal the veil, and let the realms part as they were parted before the first storm-caller climbed the mountain. It will cost us. Severing always costs. But it will cost less than this slow bleeding, this century of funerals for a promise no one below remembers making."

"Severing will not cost *less*," Caerion said. "It will cost *everything*. You know what the Ember is, Sylwen. It is not a lamp we lit. It is a *bridge*. It is the only thing holding the two skies apart. Cut it, and the veil does not close—it *collapses*, and the storms of both realms pour into each other, and there will be no parting, only drowning. You would trade a slow grief for a fast apocalypse and call it mercy."

"Then tell me the alternative." Her voice cracked, and she let it crack; let them hear the grief in it, unweaponed for one breath. "Tell me the alternative, Caerion. Tell me how we mend a covenant when the last vessel of the Ember is ash. Tell me how we trust mortals who burned the storm-callers and built a kingdom on the ashes. Tell me—"

She stopped.

Because she felt it.

Every Aelvar in the hollow felt it at the same instant, and the court went silent—not the silence of argument, but the silence of a forest when a predator moves through it, every creature turning its head at once.

It came up through the stone. Up through the nine hundred and forty-one steps. A warmth. Faint as a memory of summer, faint as the first thread of dawn, but unmistakable, impossible, *known*—a warmth that every elder in that chamber had felt only once before in their long lives, on the day of the forging, when the Ember had first opened its eye.

The Heart of Storms flared.

Not guttered—*flared*, the veins of light surging bright, the dark patches retreating at their edges, the crooked turn of it steadying for one long breath as if a dying man had drawn a full, deep lungful of air.

And then it dimmed again. But the court had seen. The court had *felt*.

"What," said the Herald, in a voice barely above a whisper, "was that."

No one answered him, because they all knew, and the knowing was too large for speech.

Sylwen stood with her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, where the warmth seemed to have pooled, and she felt something she had not felt in a hundred years and had sworn never to feel again. Hope. Hope, rising in her like water in a flooded mine, and she hated it, hated it with her whole grief-hardened heart, because hope was the cruelest thing the world had ever done to her. Hope was the hook. Hope was how they got you to wait another century. Hope was how they got you to stand in the gap and die.

"The Ember," said Caerion, and the old Aelvar's voice was shaking. "The Ember has found a vessel. It has found—"

"It has found *nothing*," Sylwen said, and her voice came out like a blade drawn from a sheath. "It has found a warmth. A flicker. We have seen flickers before. We have seen false vessels, half-vessels, mortals who carried a spark and burned out in a season and broke our hearts a little more each time. And even if—" she made herself say it, made herself walk to the edge of the cliff of it, "—even if it is true, even if a vessel lives and walks, then ask yourselves *where*. In whose kingdom? Under whose crown? The crown that burned the storm-callers has inquisitors on the roads even now—I have seen them, I have read the smoke of their fires in the wind. If a vessel exists, the crown knows of it, and the crown is hunting it, and every day we spend debating is a day the hunters close the distance."

"Then we must reach the vessel first," said a young voice.

Sylwen turned. It was Ilyra, of the Vigil's youngest circle, a scout who had spent thirty years walking the mortal roads in borrowed skin, and her eyes were bright with that terrible, beautiful, ruinous thing. "The warmth is moving. Can you not feel it? It moves along the spine of Thornfell, north and west, slow, on foot. It is *coming to us*. Whatever carries it is climbing toward the cradle, and if we go out to meet it—"

"If we go out to meet it," Sylwen said, "we go out to meet a trap. Or a lie. Or a child wearing a warmth like a stolen cloak, sent by Halvard's inquisitors to lead us into the open. You do not know the mortals of this age, Ilyra. You were not alive when they knelt. You have only the old songs, and the old songs are *lies the Ember told us*—"

"They are not lies." Ilyra's voice did not waver. "The warmth is not a lie. I felt it through sixty miles of stone. Nothing the crown can make feels like that. Nothing false feels like that."

And around her, one by one, the elders of the Vigil rose, and then—Sylwen saw it happen and could not stop it, could no more stop it than she could stop the turning of the sky—two of the Grief rose as well. Elders of her own faction. Elders she had forged out of grief, standing now in the light of a warmth she had spent a century telling them was ash.

The court was turning. She could feel it turning, the way one feels a wind change before the trees do.

* * *

They debated until the Heart of Storms had turned through three full cycles, and Sylwen spoke against hope with everything she had, and it was not enough, because hope had something she did not: proof. The warmth grew stronger as it climbed. The Heart steadied by degrees, the way a fever breaks by degrees, and no argument of hers could stand against a fever breaking.

In the end it was Caerion who brought the hammer down, and he brought it down gently, which was worst of all.

"The court will not sever the covenant," he said. "Not while the Ember shows signs of life. But neither will the court gamble all on a warmth and a scout's certainty. A vessel must be tested. A vessel must be *verified*—brought to the cradle, or proven false. And it must not fall to the crown in the meantime." He looked at Sylwen then, and his ancient eyes were full of something she had not seen in them for a hundred years, and she understood, with a cold that started in her spine and spread outward, what he was about to do to her. "The Grief does not trust the vessel. The Vigil does. So the task must go to one who carries both. Sylwen of the First Forging. You knew the Ember when it was young. You will know if this warmth is true. And you—" his voice did not quite break, but it bent, "—you are the only one among us who can bear it, whichever way it falls."

"Name the task," Sylwen said, because the forms required it, because she had no breath left for anything but forms.

"Find the vessel," Caerion said. "Test it. And if it is false—if it is the crown's bait, a hollow thing wearing the Ember's warmth as a lure—then end it. Quietly. Before it leads the inquisitors to the cradle. And if it is true—" he closed his eyes, and for a moment the oldest Aelvar in the court looked every one of his thousand years, "—if it is true, then bring it home. Either way, Sylwen, it ends with you. Either way, you are the last hand it touches before the choice is made. Forgive us. There is no one else."

*There is no one else.* The oldest lie in every council, and the truest.

* * *

She left the court before dawn, alone, as the forms required and as she preferred. She climbed the nine hundred and forty-one steps and did not count them, because counting was what she did instead of weeping, and tonight she wanted to weep, privately, where no young scout with hopeful eyes could see.

At the mouth of the eyries, the sky over the mortal realms was doing what it had done for a hundred years: churning. Great slow wheels of cloud, black at the heart, shot with lightning that struck and struck and struck at nothing, at the empty hills, at the empty roads, as if the storms themselves were searching for something they had lost. Below, far below, the mortal world lay dark under the churn, its villages shuttered, its roads empty, its kingdoms forgetting.

Sylwen spread her wings and felt the wind take them, and she thought of her daughter, who had stood in a gap like this one, in a storm like this one, holding a covenant for people who had already stopped believing in it, and had died there with the Ember's warmth going out of the world through her open hands.

*If the vessel is false,* she told the wind, *I will end it, and the Grief will have its severing, and this long dying will finally be over.*

*And if the vessel is true—*

She did not finish the thought. She could not. Hope sat in her chest like a swallowed coal, and she flew north and west along the spine of Thornfell, into the churning dark, following a warmth she had sworn to kill.

By the third day she had found the trail: mortals, five of them, moving through the high passes with a smuggler's cunning, and among them a small warmth that made the lightning lean toward it like a flower toward sun. And on the fourth day, sheltering on a cliff while a freak storm wrung the mountains, she looked down through the rain at the campfire below—at the exiled storm-caller with his scarred hands and his buried thunder, at the shadow-woman with her too-careful eyes, at the mute child asleep by the fire—and the child turned in her sleep, and spoke a word in the Firsttongue, a word of blessing, a word of *homecoming*—and Sylwen felt her resolve crack like ice on a spring river.

Because she knew that word. She had heard it last from her daughter's lips, a hundred years ago, on the mountain, in the gap, in the dying.

The child opened her eyes in the dark and looked up—directly, precisely, through sixty feet of rain and night—directly at the cliff where Sylwen stood.

And smiled at her.
