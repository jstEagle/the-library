## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The market city of Vell woke the way it always did: in layers. First the bells of the Saltspire, tolling the hour with a voice like a cracked coin. Then the fishwives, arguing prices in voices that could strip paint from a hull. Then the smell—brine and charcoal and crushed mint from the herb-sellers' stalls, all of it rising off the cobbles in a warm, greasy haze that clung to the throat like a second tongue.

Tamsin Joubert had lived in that haze for six years, and he knew its moods better than he knew his own face.

He sat on the low wall above the Tanner's Stair with a cup of chicory he had no intention of drinking, and he watched the crowd the way other men watched weather. A thief watched hands. Tamsin watched *heads*—the tilt of them, the way memory sat behind the eyes like water behind glass. Some heads were shallow; you could see the bottom at a glance, a life of small transactions and small hungers, hardly worth the trouble of dipping in. Others were deep. Others were *dark*, and those were the ones that paid.

The rules came first, as they always did, recited in the old litany he'd built for himself the way a mason builds a wall—one stone at a time, mortared tight against the dark.

*Never take from the desperate; their memories are thin and bitter.* *Never take twice from the same head; the scar shows.* *Never keep what you sell longer than a season, or it starts to grow roots.*

And the last rule, the one carved deepest: *Never wonder about the people you take from.*

He was good at not wondering. He'd had practice.

Below him, the morning trade was thickening. Wool merchants from the hill country, their carts stacked with fleeces still smelling of rain. Spice men with their fingers stained yellow to the knuckle. And there—moving through the press of bodies with the unhurried confidence of a man who has never once been jostled by accident—came Master Corvel Hane.

Hane was a wool factor, third-richest man on Copperlane, and Tamsin had marked him three weeks ago the way a fisherman marks a pool where something large lives. He was fifty-one years old, widowed seven years, and he carried his memories the way some men carried coin: carelessly, in quantity, certain no one would ever be bold enough to reach for them.

More importantly, Hane had been married once. Happily. To a woman named Liss who had died of a fever in the autumn of her thirty-fourth year, and whose wedding day—forty years gone now, worn smooth as river stone from handling—was the single brightest thing in the man's head. Tamsin knew this because he'd spent two evenings in the Gilded Eel listening to Hane drink and talk, and because bright things cast light even through the walls of a skull, if you had eyes for it.

Collectors paid absurd sums for weddings. Not the grand ones—the arranged ones, the political ones, which were all contracts and cold hands—but the *true* ones. The ones where a nervous boy forgot half his vows and a girl laughed so hard at the feast that she snorted wine. There was a collector in the capital, Odile's client, who paid forty crowns for a genuine wedding memory, and forty crowns was a year of rent, food, lamp oil, and the particular luxury of not being afraid for twelve months.

Tamsin finished his chicory, set the cup down, and went to work.

It was not theft as the cutpurses understood it. There was no knife, no bump, no clever fingers. What Tamsin did required proximity, attention, and a kind of listening that most people never learned because most people never needed to. He fell into step behind Hane on the Copperlane stairs, close enough to smell the lanolin in the man's collar, and he opened himself the way he'd taught himself to open at fourteen years old, alone and starving in a cellar in Thornfell: a door unlatching inside his chest, a reaching-out, a *touch*.

Memory, up close, had texture. That was the thing no one believed until they felt it. Hane's surface thoughts were all commerce—wool weights, ship manifests, a grudge against a rival factor named Pell—and Tamsin slid past them without disturbing so much as a hair, the way a wren moves through a hedge. Deeper. Past the grudge (brittle, sour), past the grief (heavier than it looked, black water, a coffin and rain on it), down to where the light was.

There it was.

A wedding. Autumn light through amber glass. A girl of twenty in a dress the color of new cream, laughing at something before the vows were even spoken, and a young Corvel Hane standing beside her with his heart going like a drum and thinking, with total and devastating sincerity, *I will remember this exact moment until I die.*

Tamsin took it the way he always took things: gently, completely, leaving nothing behind but a smooth pale place where the memory had been. It came free with a soundless sigh, folding itself into him like a letter into an envelope, and he felt it settle among the others he carried—a farmer's first harvest, a diver's last breath before surfacing, a child's whole afternoon of watching ants—that he would hold for a few weeks, maybe a month, until Odile found a buyer and the thing passed out of him forever.

Hane stumbled slightly on the stair. Caught himself. Frowned at the cobbles as though they'd moved.

That was the part Tamsin didn't watch. He was already three steps ahead, already turning toward the fish market with the loose, unhurried gait of a man with nowhere particular to be, and he did not look back at the wool factor standing in the middle of the stairs trying to remember why the morning suddenly felt like a room with a chair missing.

*Never wonder about the people you take from.*

But here was the truth he kept in the locked drawer behind all the rules: the taking felt *good*. Not the money—though the money was good too, clean and heavy in ways honest work never was. The taking itself. For one moment, folded into another person's brightest hour, Tamsin Joubert was not a nameless orphan from the Thornfell passes with a dead mother and no history worth the name. He was a bridegroom. He was a diver breaking into sunlight. He was a boy watching ants with the whole world contained in a square of dirt.

He was anyone. Anyone at all. Except himself.

He bought a meat pie with Hane's future forty crowns—Odile would advance against a wedding, she always did—and ate it walking, and that was when the memory began to whisper.

At first he thought it was the pie, or the bells, or the ordinary noise of Vell working its way under his skin. But the whisper was *inside*, and it was coming from the wedding.

This had never happened before.

Memories, once taken, were quiet things. They sat where you put them, dimming slowly over weeks like coals, waiting to be handed off. They did not speak. They certainly did not speak *words*.

*Aldermere.*

Tamsin stopped walking. A cart laden with eels rattled past; someone swore at him cheerfully for blocking the way; he did not hear any of it. He stood in the middle of the fish market with grease on his fingers and listened to a stolen wedding whisper a word he did not know.

Not the wedding itself—he knew it now intimately, every candle and bad joke and the taste of the cake, honey and burnt sugar. No. The word came from *beneath* the wedding, from underneath the bright surface of the thing he'd lifted, the way a stone sits under moss. As if the memory had been laid over something older. As if Corvel Hane's happiest day had grown, sometime in forty years of handling, around a buried seed.

*Aldermere. Aldermere. The door remembers. Aldermere.*

"Stop," Tamsin said, under his breath, and a woman nearby gave him a look and pulled her child closer.

He should have done what the rules demanded. Rules existed for exactly this: the strange thing, the wrong thing, the thing that made the back of your neck prickle. You sold it fast, you took the money, you let it become someone else's problem in someone else's skull. Six years of survival in Vell had been built on never keeping the strange thing.

He turned instead into the shadowed mouth of Cordwainer's Row, found the alcove behind the tallow-chandler's shop where he sometimes went to be no one, and closed his eyes and *looked*.

The wedding spread itself out for him obligingly—amber light, cream dress, the groom's ridiculous trembling hands. But now that he knew to look beneath, he could see it: a darkness under the floor of the memory, like a cellar under a house. And in that darkness, faint as a lantern seen through fog, something was moving. Not a shape. More like... a road. A road drawn in silver, winding away through mountains, toward a point where the silver gathered and gathered and became—

The whisper again, patient now, almost gentle:

*Aldermere.*

And with the word came a feeling, which was worse. Memories carried feelings; that was their freight. This one carried *homesickness*. A vast, specific, unbearable homesickness for a place Tamsin had never been, had never heard of, had no reason to ache for—and yet the ache rose in him like floodwater, hot behind his eyes, tightening his throat, so that he had to put his hand against the chandler's wall to steady himself while somewhere inside his chest a stolen stranger's stolen longing said *home, home, come home* over and over like a bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

When it finally ebbed, he was shaking, and the chicory-and-brine smell of Vell had never seemed so far away.

He made himself think. That was the other rule, the unwritten one under all the others: *when the world goes strange, count what you know.* He knew he had lifted a wedding from Corvel Hane. He knew weddings did not whisper. Therefore either the gift was changing—which happened, the old stories said, to gifted folk who used it too long, and the thought of that made his stomach drop—or the wedding had not been only a wedding.

Someone had hidden something inside Corvel Hane's happiest memory. Buried it deep enough that the man himself didn't know it was there. Deep enough that it had waited, silent, for a thief with Tamsin's particular touch to lift the whole bright layer and find the dark thing underneath.

Which meant someone had wanted it hidden from everyone.

Which meant someone might want it *found*.

Tamsin leaned his head back against the chandler's wall and laughed once, quietly, at the ceiling of the world. Forty crowns. He'd done a day's easy work and earned forty crowns, and instead of a fat purse and a quiet evening he had a haunted wedding and a homesickness for a country that did not exist—not on any map he'd ever seen, and Tamsin had spent more hours in map-sellers' shops than any honest man, memorizing escape routes.

*Aldermere,* whispered the thing beneath the wedding, softer now, almost sly. *You'll carry me well, thief. You'll carry me far.*

"I don't carry anything for free," Tamsin told it.

The memory said nothing else. But as he straightened his coat and stepped back out into the noise and light of Vell—as he became, once more, the unremarkable young man with the forgettable face and the careful eyes—he could feel it settled against his heart like a coal wrapped in cloth. Warm. Patient. Waiting.

Above the rooftops, the Saltspire bells rang noon, and Tamsin Joubert walked home through the crowd with a stranger's wedding day burning a hole in his chest, and for the first time in six years, he broke his oldest rule.

He wondered.

* * *

The safehouse was a garret above a dyer's shop on Saffron Lane, chosen for three virtues: the dye-vats below stank so fiercely that no one climbed the stairs voluntarily, the roof beams were close enough to touch, and the window looked out over a slice of gray harbor where he could watch ships leave for places he'd never go.

Tamsin counted his hoard by lamplight, because counting calmed him, and tonight he needed calming.

Forty-one memories, give or take. He kept them loosely sorted, the way a miser keeps coins: the saleable ones in the front of his mind, bright and fresh; the ones too strange or too sad to sell pushed toward the back—an old woman's memory of burying a son, which no collector would touch but which he couldn't seem to make himself discard; a soldier's single hour of true peace, sitting on a wall at dusk with the war far away. Those he kept the way misers keep broken jewelry. Not for use. Just because letting go of them felt like a kind of murder.

He laid the wedding among the saleables and found, uneasily, that it did not sit right. The others lay still. The wedding *hummed*.

"Aldermere," he said aloud, testing the word. It tasted odd in his mouth—like a name spoken in a language he'd almost learned once and forgotten. "Where are you?"

No answer. Of course no answer. He was a thief in a garret talking to a stolen memory, and if any of the neighbors' sharp-eared children heard him through the floorboards, the story would be all over Saffron Lane by supper.

He should go see Odile tomorrow. Sell the wedding, take the forty crowns, let the whispering thing pass out of his keeping into some collector's vault where haunted memories belonged. That was the sensible sequence. That was the wall, stone by stone.

Instead he sat cross-legged on the floorboards with his palms open on his knees, and he sank into the wedding again, and this time he went looking for the cellar door.

He found it near midnight. Not a door, really—a seam, a hairline crack in the amber light where the brightness didn't quite meet itself, and when he pressed his attention into the crack the whole memory flexed around him like a living thing disturbed in its sleep. The wedding guests blurred. The bride's laughter stretched and slowed. And through the seam poured a single image, clear as a struck bell:

Mountains. Gray peaks under a sky the color of pewter, and a road—*the* road, the silver one—climbing toward a col between two shoulders of stone, and on the col, impossibly far away and impossibly close, a structure. Not a building. A *door*. Standing alone in the pass, tall as a chapel, framed in carved stone worn featureless by wind, and around its base grew flowers he had no name for, blooming in snow.

Then it was gone, and Tamsin was on his knees on the garret floor with his heart slamming and tears on his face that he had no explanation for, and outside the window the harbor lay dark and the ships were all asleep and somewhere across the world a door stood in a mountain pass blooming flowers in the snow, and he knew—knew it the way you know your own hands—that he had just seen the most forbidden thing in the kingdom.

Because he understood, suddenly, with the cold clarity that comes at the worst moments, why the word had been hidden. Why it had been buried under forty years of a happy marriage, in the head of a wool factor, in a market city full of ten thousand louder minds. Things hidden that deeply were things hunted. And things hunted, in Queen Vessaline's realm, were things that got people dragged from their beds in the night.

He sat back against the wall. He breathed. Above the dyer's shop, in the dark, he held the wedding memory up to his inner eye one more time and examined it with a craftsman's care, and found what he should have found at the start: fingerprints. Old ones, elegant ones, laid over the burial like a surgeon's sutures. Someone had *made* that hiding place, long ago, with a gift far finer than his own—and had left it in Hane's keeping like a coin left in a church box, waiting for whoever was meant to find it.

Waiting, perhaps, for him.

"No," Tamsin said to the empty room, firmly, the way you say no to a dog begging at the table. "I'm a tradesman. I sell weddings. I don't carry messages for dead strangers."

The memory hummed against his ribs, warm and patient, and said nothing at all.

He slept badly, in the end, curled in his blanket with the lamp burned out, and dreamed of a road of silver climbing through gray mountains toward a door wreathed in impossible flowers, and in the dream he was walking the road barefoot and weeping and *happy*, happier than he had ever been in his actual life, and when he woke at dawn with the dye-stink rising through the floorboards and the Saltspire bells ringing the hour, the first thing he heard—before the fishwives, before the gulls, before his own heartbeat—was the word again, no longer a whisper.

*Aldermere.*

And far to the south, though he could not have known it, in a palace full of empty rooms, something ancient and hungry opened its eyes.
