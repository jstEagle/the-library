## 2. Thirteen Bells

The bells began at dawn.

Tamsin Joubert heard them from the roof of his safehouse on Candlemaker's Row, where he had gone to watch the light come up over Vell's tiled sea of roofs — a habit, not a pleasure, though he had long ago stopped distinguishing between the two. There were eleven bells in Vell, one for each district, and they rang the hours in a staggered chorus that the city had learned to sleep through the way a man sleeps through his own heartbeat.

These were not those bells.

These came from the citadel on the hill, a twelfth voice none of the districts owned, and it did not ring the hour. It rang thirteen times, paused as if drawing breath, and rang thirteen again. Tamsin sat very still on the cold slate and counted, because counting was what he did when the world tried to tell him something he didn't want to hear.

Thirteen. Silence. Thirteen.

Below him, Candlemaker's Row woke badly. Shutters banged open. A dog began to howl and was kicked quiet. Somewhere a woman said, "No," in the flat voice of someone who has already understood, and then said it again, louder, as if volume could argue with a bell.

He climbed down through the trapdoor into the attic he called home — three rooms above a dyer's shop that had failed twice and would fail again, rented under a name that belonged to a dead man whose memory Tamsin had bought for the price of a meal. The rooms were deliberately sparse: a cot, a stove, a chest with a false bottom, and forty-one glass vials racked in wool like eggs in a hen's nest. Each vial held a life, or a piece of one. The wedding of a merchant's daughter, still warm from last night's theft, sat in the place of honor at the center, its stopper sealed with wax because good memories sweated if you didn't seal them.

Tamsin lit the stove, put water on for tea, and told himself the bells meant nothing yet. Proclamations came and went. Vell had survived three kings, two plagues, and a fire that had eaten half the Weavers' Quarter. The city absorbed everything. That was its nature. It was why thieves like him could live in it, siphoning the rich and selling their yesterdays to collectors who paid in silver for other people's joy.

Then the criers started, and the tea went cold in the pot.

*By order of Her Radiant Majesty Vessaline, First of Her Name, Protector of the Nine Provinces:* The voice came up the street in waves, one crier passing another, overlapping so the words tangled and doubled like a spell mispronounced. *All persons gifted with the unlicensed taking or giving of memory shall present themselves for registration before the thirteenth turn of the moon now begun. Those who register shall be granted the Crown's protection and a stipend of service. Those who do not—*

The criers never finished that sentence in the same way twice. One said *shall be found.* One said *shall be taken into custody.* One, younger, his voice cracking on the syllables, said *shall be dealt with according to law,* and Tamsin noticed that even the criers flinched from the truth, which was that there was no law for this. There had never been a law for this. You could not legislate against a thing most people believed was a rumor, a fireside tale, the explanation old wives gave when a grandmother forgot her children's faces.

But the Queen believed. And the Queen, everyone agreed without ever saying it aloud, was fifty years too young for her own reign.

Tamsin stood at his window with the shutter cracked to a finger's width and watched the Row learn its new shape.

Old Maren the chandler came out first, because Maren had never learned caution; she'd once shown Tamsin, drunk on festival wine, how she could take the sting out of a burn by drinking the pain-memory right off a child's skin. She stood in her doorway in her nightclothes, listening, and her hands rose slowly to her mouth, and Tamsin watched sixty years of openness die on her face in the space of a bell's echo.

By midmorning the soldiers came. Not many — a file of six, moving door to door with a clerk behind them carrying a ledger bound in grey leather. They were polite. That was the worst of it. They knocked, they bowed, they asked questions in reasonable voices while their boots left wet prints on thresholds, and the neighbors gathered in knots to watch and pretended they weren't watching.

At the Lamplighters' Hall they took a boy. Tamsin saw it happen from the window and could not look away, which he hated about himself. The boy couldn't have been more than fifteen — Tammo, the lamplighter's nephew, who could dim a flame with a touch of his mind and thought it made him useless rather than remarkable. He came out of the hall with his hands already raised, having decided, apparently, that cooperation was survival, and the soldiers walked him down the Row between them, and the clerk wrote something in the ledger, and Tammo turned around twice to wave at people who did not wave back.

Nobody waved back. That was the lesson of the morning, written larger than any proclamation: the city would absorb this too. It would absorb the boy.

On the third day they took Maren.

She had registered. Tamsin learned this later, piecing it together from the dyer below, who heard everything and repeated most of it. Maren had gone to the citadel with her papers and her honesty and her list of every small kindness she'd ever done with her gift — the burns, the fevers, the griefs she'd swallowed for grieving families — and she had come back with a grey ribbon pinned to her coat, an official mark, *Registered, Class Three, Harmless.*

They took her anyway, four days after registering. No reason was given. The soldiers came at dusk, when the Row was settling, and led her out past her own shop where her candles burned in every window because she'd always lit them at dusk, all of them, a little constellation of a woman, and she looked at her candles as she passed them and did not cry until she reached the corner.

After that, the Row stopped gathering to watch. People drew their shutters like eyelids closing. Fear went underground, which is where fear does its best work.

Tamsin packed.

He had rules, and rule the first was *never be attached to anything you cannot burn,* and he had broken it, the way he broke every rule eventually, by staying seven years in one city, by learning the names of bakers and the moods of rivers, by letting a place become a habit and habits become a self. Now he moved through the attic with the efficiency of long practice, wrapping vials in wool, sealing the chest, sorting his life into what could be carried and what could not.

The wedding memory he held longer than the rest. He'd lifted it from the merchant only two nights ago — a clean theft, the man so drunk on his daughter's happiness that he hadn't felt the siphon at all — and Tamsin had intended to sell it within the week. But the memory had resisted him, had whispered that word at him from inside its glass, *Aldermere, Aldermere,* and he had not sold it, and he had not examined why, and now he stood holding it while smoke from the stove curled toward the rafters and the thirteenth moon climbed fat and white over the citadel.

"Thirteen turns," he said to the empty room. "That's not a deadline. That's a net."

Because he understood, in the way thieves understand traps — structurally, intimately — what the proclamation really was. Registration wasn't protection. It was inventory. The Queen wasn't hunting the gifted; she was *counting* them, sorting them, and somewhere in her grey ledgers was a page waiting for a thief who could take things no one else could take whole. He had spent his career being careful precisely so that no ledger would ever hold his name. But careful men died in nets all the same. The trick was to be elsewhere when the net closed.

He burned the safehouse at midnight.

Not dramatically — Tamsin Joubert did nothing dramatically; drama was for people who could afford witnesses. He banked the stove high, wedged the damper open, laid the old dyer's rags soaked in linseed against the wall where the wood was driest, and left through the roof with his chest on his back while the fire found its feet below him. By the time the Row smelled smoke, he was three rooftops away, and by the time the buckets came out, he was in the Warrens, hood up, walking at the pace of a man going nowhere in particular.

He allowed himself one look back. Flames stood up out of Candlemaker's Row like something being born, orange against the thirteenth moon, and the neighbors formed their bucket line and mourned the building and not the man, because the man had never let them know him well enough to mourn. That was the trade. That had always been the trade. Safety for loneliness, paid nightly, and he had thought the price fair for thirty years.

Standing in the Warrens with firelight on his face, Tamsin admitted, for the first time, that the price had gone up.

Behind him, Vell burned a little. Ahead of him, the mountains waited — Thornfell, the smuggler's spine of the world, where roads existed that maps were bribed to forget. He had a cache hidden in the passes, silver and memories both, enough to vanish properly this time, across the border, into a country with no Queen and no bells.

In his coat, against his heart, the wedding memory whispered its word again. He felt it through the wool like a second pulse.

*Aldermere.*

He should have destroyed it. Any sensible thief would have. A memory that wanted things was a memory that belonged to someone dangerous, and dangerous people were coming, and the smart move was to drop the vial down a well and let the water keep the secret.

Tamsin stood in the dark with the fire warming his back and did not drop it down a well.

"Curiosity," he muttered, "is going to be the death of me," and turned north into the Warrens, toward the mountain gates, walking quickly now, because the bells had started again behind him — thirteen, silence, thirteen — and this time they sounded less like a proclamation and more like a countdown.

* * *

Warden Corvane tasted the city before he saw it.

This was not a figure of speech, though he had learned to describe it as one, for the benefit of clerks and soldiers and other men who needed their world to stay flat. What he actually did was stand at the southern gate of Vell with his eyes closed and his mouth slightly open, the way a fisherman reads a river, and let the city pour itself into him.

Vell tasted of bread and tallow and fear. The fear was new — six days new, sharp as vinegar — and it lay over everything now, seeping into the taste of the gutters and the shrines. Corvane found it nourishing the way a starving man finds gruel nourishing: technically food, spiritually an insult. Fear was thin broth. He had been sent to Vell to find a man, and fear was everywhere, and therefore fear told him nothing.

But underneath the fear, faint as a fingerprint on glass, there was something else.

Corvane opened his eyes. The gate guards flinched, all four of them, in the synchronized way of men who had been warned about him. Good. He wore the Warden's grey, plain-cut, unadorned except for the chain of office, and his face was the kind that encouraged flinching — long, pale, unremarkable in the way of things seen in dreams, with eyes that people described afterward as *tired* because they lacked the vocabulary for *hungry.*

"The fire on Candlemaker's Row," he said to the senior guard. "Show me."

The house was still smoking. Corvane walked through it in his soft boots, stepping over the char, and the guards hung back at the threshold because the air inside felt wrong to them, though they couldn't have said why. Corvane could. The air inside tasted of absence — of a life dismantled and carried away, of a man who had practiced leaving for so long that even his leavings left cleanly.

But not perfectly. Nothing was perfect.

Kneeling by the cold stove, Corvane extended his senses into the ash the way another man might dip a finger into water. Residue. Traces. Seven years of habitation pressed into the grain of the wood like flowers in a book: cheap tea, lamp oil, wool, loneliness — loneliness has a taste, dry and mineral, and Corvane knew it better than any flavor in the world — and beneath all of it, threaded through the burn-scar like gold wire in quartz:

Memory-work. Recent. Skilled.

And something else. Something that made Corvane go very still, kneeling in the ash of a thief's abandoned life, with his mouth open like a fisherman's and his hunger rising in him like water in a flooded mine.

There was a theft here that had not yet been sold. A memory, taken recently, still warm, still *alive* in the way stolen things stayed alive when the thief was strong enough to keep them fresh. It had soaked into the floorboards, into the ash, into the very walls of the room where the thief had slept beside it. Corvane could not read what it was — residue was residue, an outline, a scent without a body — but he could read its *weight*, and its weight was wrong. Stolen memories weighed what their moments weighed: a wedding, a battle, a death. This one weighed like a *country.*

Corvane rose. His knees cracked. He was aware, distantly, of the guards watching him from the doorway, of the smoke stinging his eyes, of the ordinary machinery of his body doing its ordinary work. All of that was getting harder to notice lately. That was the cost, and he did not let himself think about the cost, because thinking about the cost required remembering what he had paid, and —

He reached for his mother's face and found, as always now, a smooth grey place where it should have been.

He stood in the ashes of the thief's house and grieved without knowing for whom, which was the only kind of grief he had left, and then he folded the grief away, because grief was also just broth.

"His name?" he asked the senior guard.

"No name, Warden. The dyer downstairs says the man kept to himself. Paid on time. Called himself—" the guard consulted a scrap of paper— "Aldous Renne."

"Aldous Renne is dead," Corvane said mildly. "Eleven years ago. Fever, in Sarnholt." He had eaten the name himself, once, off a forger in Sarnholt, along with the forger's skill and the forger's love of his own cleverness. Names circulated among men like his the way coins circulated among beggars. "Find me the dyer. Find me anyone who spoke to him. And send word ahead to Thornfell — every pass, every toll-house, every smuggler's den. Tell them the Crown pays for eyes."

"And if they ask what we're looking for, Warden?"

Corvane looked south, toward the mountains, where the thirteenth moon was rising over the black spine of Thornfell like a coin held up against a locked door. Somewhere in that darkness was a man carrying a memory heavy as a nation, and Corvane's orders were simple and absolute: *recover it, whatever it costs, whoever it costs.* The Queen's voice had been very calm when she gave them. The Queen's voice was always calm. Calm, and young, and wrong.

"What we're looking for," Corvane said, "is a thief who stole something he doesn't yet understand." He touched his tongue to the ash-taste still lingering in the air, and beneath the loneliness and the burn and the seven careful years, he found it again — that deep, strange, country-weighted warmth — and his hunger answered it the way a struck bell answers a struck bell.

"He'll run for the border," Corvane said. "They always do. Set the watches. I want every road out of Vell remembered —" and here he smiled, without warmth, at the guards who had already begun to edge away from him — "*and I will know if a single one forgets.*"

He left them to their flinching and walked out through the smoking city, north, following a scent only he could smell, a thread of stolen gold running through the dark toward the mountains.

Behind him the bells rang thirteen, and thirteen, and thirteen, patient as a trap, certain as a net.

Ahead of him, Tamsin Joubert walked into Thornfell carrying the one thing in the world the Queen could not afford to lose — and did not yet know that the man hunting him could smell it from a mile away, the way a drowning man smells rain.
