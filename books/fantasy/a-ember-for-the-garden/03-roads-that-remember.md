## 3. Roads That Remember

The mountains took him the way a thief takes a purse—quickly, and without asking.

Tamsin had crossed into Thornfell before dawn, when the last of Vell's lanterns still smoldered behind him like a city trying to remember how to burn. By midmorning the road had narrowed to a shelf of stone above a gorge, and by noon there was no road at all, only the smuggler paths his father's father had walked: goat-trails stitched along cliff faces, hidden stairways cut where no mason would admit to cutting them, cairns stacked by hands three generations dead to mark which hollow held water and which held bones.

He climbed until his lungs burned, until Vell was a gray smudge in the valley haze, until the only sounds were wind through scree and the distant complaint of some bird that lived nowhere else. Then he stopped, pressed his back against a boulder warm from the sun, and let himself breathe.

They wouldn't follow him here. Not yet. The crown's men liked roads with names and inns with beds. Thornfell belonged to no one, which meant it belonged to anyone desperate enough to learn its grammar—and Tamsin had learned it young, back when he was still learning what his gift was, back when lifting a memory felt like picking a lock instead of picking a soul.

*Three rules,* his grandmother had told him, her voice arriving now out of a memory that was properly his own, one of the few he'd never sold. *Never take what you can't carry. Never keep what you can't hide. And never, ever let them see you reach.*

He had broken all three rules in Vell. He had taken a wedding that didn't want to be taken, kept it past the point of comfort, and reached for it again and again on the road like a tongue returning to a cracked tooth.

*Aldermere.*

The word sat in him still, coiled beneath the merchant's stolen joy like a stone beneath moss. He had tried to set the memory down twice since fleeing the city—once in a ditch, once in a waystation stable, pressing it out of himself the way he'd press ink from a pen—but it clung. Memories were supposed to leave clean. That was the whole craft of it: you lifted, you sealed, you sold, and what remained in you was only the shape of the absence, never the thing itself.

This one stayed. This one *wanted* something.

Tamsin closed his eyes and reached for it carefully, the way you'd reach into dark water for something whose edges you couldn't see.

The wedding came first, as it always did: candlelight, a bride laughing into her veil, the merchant Harl Voss weeping without shame into his good coat. Tamsin had taken all of it in eleven seconds flat outside a chandlery, smooth as sleight of hand, and Harl had walked home lighter and happier and poorer by one perfect afternoon without ever knowing a thing was gone.

But beneath the candles, beneath the laughter, there was the other layer—the one that had made him run. A word spoken in a voice that wasn't Harl's. A word that had been *in* the memory before Tamsin ever touched it, folded into it like a letter slipped inside another man's envelope.

*Aldermere. The door opens on the thirteenth turn.*

He pulled back, heart hammering. The wind gusted down the pass, cold despite the sun, and somewhere below him a loose stone went clattering into the gorge, falling for a long time before it found its silence.

"Who are you?" he asked the memory, quietly, because there was no one else to ask.

It didn't answer. It never answered. It only waited, patient as a seed, as if it knew—as if it had always known—that sooner or later he would have to plant it somewhere.

* * *

He made camp that night in a hollow the smugglers called the Kettle, a bowl of granite sheltered from the wind where generations of runners had boiled tea over peat fires and told lies about the lowlands. He built no fire. Fires drew eyes, and eyes were the one thing he could not steal from.

Instead he sat in the dark and did something he hadn't done since he was a boy: he opened his cache and counted.

Every thief has a hoard. Most thieves hoard coin; Tamsin hoarded lives. In the vaulted dark behind his eyes, wrapped in seals of will older than his trade, lay forty-one memories he had never sold—the ones too beautiful or too strange or too dangerous to part with. A fisherman's dawn off the Amber Coast, all silver water and salt. A mother singing to twins in a language Tamsin didn't speak. A soldier's last sunset, taken gently from a dying man who had *asked*, who had wanted someone to carry the view onward. Forty-one small eternities, and every one of them a weight.

He checked each seal the way a miser checks locks. All sound. All quiet.

Except—

At the edge of the cache, where he kept the newest theft, the wedding memory pulsed. Not with Harl's candles now. With something else. When he brushed its surface, the word rose again, and this time it brought company: an image, brief and blinding, of a *door*. Black wood in a white wall, set into a hillside garden, and above it a lintel carved with thirteen crescents in a ring.

Then it was gone, and Tamsin sat alone in the Kettle with his breath fogging and his hands shaking.

Memories did not do this. Memories were goods. They were static, inert, obedient—they sat where you put them and gave up nothing they weren't asked to give. He had handled hundreds. He had *been* hundreds of people for eleven seconds at a time, and not one of those borrowed lives had ever looked back at him.

This one was looking. This one was *leading*.

"Thirteenth turn," he murmured. The proclamation in Vell had said the same—all gifted must register before the thirteenth turn of the moon—and he had assumed, as everyone assumed, that it was simply the crown's habit of dressing cruelty in ceremony. But ceremony and coincidence rarely shared a date.

He lay back against his pack and stared up at the slice of sky above the hollow, where the moon hung fat and pale, five nights from full. Counting turns in his head. Doing arithmetic he didn't like.

Eight turns left.

* * *

Morning brought the first wrongness, and it came as a smell.

Tamsin crested the ridge above the Kettle just after sunrise and stopped so suddenly he nearly went over the scree. The air smelled of beeswax and cut stems—of flowers—and there were no flowers within fifty miles of this altitude. The smell was coming from the mountain itself, from a seam of pink-veined stone beside the path, and when he knelt and laid his palm against it, the world *opened*.

Not his memory. Someone else's. Someone long dead.

A woman stood in a walled garden under a noonday sun, deadheading roses with a silver knife, and the scent of the cut stems filled the whole sky, and she was humming, and Tamsin could feel her contentment the way you feel heat from a stove—not as thought but as atmosphere. She was happy here. She had been happy here for years, and the happiness had soaked into her hands, and her hands had touched this stone—

No. He wrenched his palm away, gasping.

The seam of rock was just rock. Gray and ordinary and utterly silent. But for half a heartbeat it had been a garden, and the woman in it had been so vividly, unbearably *real* that Tamsin could still smell the roses clinging to his skin.

He knew what memories felt like from the inside. He knew their textures the way a cloth merchant knows wool. And that had been a memory—worn soft with age, faded at the edges like a tapestry left in sun—but a memory nonetheless, lodged in stone as if the mountain had swallowed it whole.

"That's not possible," he said aloud, because saying things aloud made them less true, somehow, and he needed this to be less true.

But the mountain wasn't listening. As he climbed higher, following the old smuggler marks toward the high passes, it happened again. A shelf of slate gave him three seconds of a boy sliding down it in winter, shrieking with joy, snow in his collar. A cave mouth breathed out the taste of honey-cakes baked over a campfire thirty years gone. At a cairn near the pass of the Red Hand, he brushed a fallen stone and caught a fragment of a runner's prayer—*keep me steady, keep me secret, keep me whole*—repeated so many times it had worn a groove in the air itself.

The mountains remembered.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way poets claimed hills held echoes. Thornfell *held* memories the way his own mind held them—stored, sealed, retrievable—and the ones leaking out of the stone were ones Tamsin recognized with a sick lurch of recognition.

He knew that boy on the slate shelf. He had *taken* that boy's sled-run six years ago, sold it to a collector in Vell for enough coin to eat through winter. He knew that prayer; he'd lifted it from a dying runner named Cass, who'd gripped his wrist and begged him to keep it safe. He knew the honey-cakes—he'd stolen that warmth from a grandmother in a market square a lifetime ago, meaning to sell it, finding he couldn't.

His thefts. His castoffs, his unsolds, his leaks—every memory he had ever mishandled, every seal that had ever slipped, everything he had ever dropped carelessly along these roads in the arrogant belief that lost memories simply *dissolved*.

They hadn't dissolved. They had come home to the mountain. Thornfell had been drinking from him for years, a patient sponge, and now, as he climbed, the stone was giving them back.

Tamsin stood in the pass of the Red Hand with the wind tearing at his coat and understood, with terrible clarity, that he had never been alone on these roads. Every step he'd ever taken here had been witnessed—not by eyes, but by whatever slow awareness slept in the rock, gathering his spills like a magpie gathers bright things.

And if the mountain could hold memories...

Then a memory could hold a map.

He sank onto the fallen stone at the cairn, heedless of the cold seeping through his coat, and reached—with none of his usual caution—for the wedding memory, for the thing hiding inside it.

The door again. Black wood, white wall, thirteen crescents. But this time, because he was looking, because the mountain had taught him what to look *for*, he saw more. Around the door: terraces cut into a hillside. Beyond the terraces: a river bending in a shape he almost recognized. And threaded through all of it, faint as watermark, a sense of *direction*—north and east and up, deeper into ranges like these, ranges that did not appear on any map Tamsin had ever seen or stolen.

It wasn't a word hiding in Harl Voss's wedding. It was a *route*. Coordinates written in feeling rather than ink. Someone had hidden the way to Aldermere inside a memory the way Tamsin hid stolen goods in honest cargo—and then someone had lost it, or been robbed of it, and it had drifted through the underworld of bought and sold recollections until Odile's fence-network coughed it up and Tamsin had carried it unknowing for years.

Years. He had walked around with it for *years*, a lit fuse tucked behind his ear, and he'd never known.

And now the crown was burning the gifted out of every city in the kingdom, dragging neighbors away in irons, making registration a synonym for seizure—and Tamsin finally understood that none of it was about thieves. It had never been about thieves. Somewhere in the palace, someone knew a memory was missing. Someone knew it had passed through a hundred hands like contraband, and could not know which hands, and so had decided to take *all* of them.

All of them except the one that mattered. The one carrying the map.

Him.

The wonder he'd felt on the ridge—the awe of touching the mountain's stored lives, of learning that the world was vaster and stranger than his rules allowed—curdled in his chest and turned to ice-water dread. He was not fleeing the purge. He *was* the purge. One specific thief, one specific memory, and every road between him and the border was being combed by men who could taste a stolen life the way he could.

He looked north, where the ranges deepened into country no cartographer bothered naming, and the word rose in him unbidden, sweet and terrible:

*Aldermere.*

"No," he told it. "Whatever you are, whoever hid you—I'm a thief, not a courier. I don't deliver."

The memory said nothing, of course. But as he stood to walk on, he felt it settle deeper into him, snug as a key turning in a lock, and he understood that his refusal changed nothing at all. You could refuse a king. You could refuse a buyer. You could not refuse a thing that had already chosen its carrier.

He climbed. Behind him, far below in the valley, a thread of smoke rose from the direction of the lowland waystations—too thin to be a chimney fire, too deliberate to be anything else. Someone was burning the roads behind him, closing them one by one, herding everything that ran toward the high passes.

Herding *him*.

Tamsin pulled his collar up against the wind and walked north into the remembering mountains, and did not look back. There was no point. He already knew what followed: not soldiers, not yet. Something worse. Something that hunted by scent, by taste, by the faint perfume of other people's lives.

Something that was very, very good at finding thieves.

And in the stone beneath his boots, worn smooth by a dead woman's hands, the ghost of a garden smelled of roses all the way to the horizon.
