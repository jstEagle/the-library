## 4. The Girl Without Yesterday

The pass above Kettle Hollow narrowed to a throat of grey stone, and Tamsin Joubert had learned long ago that throats were where the killing happened.

He crouched in the shadow of a leaning boulder, breath held shallow, watching the trail below wind down toward the smuggler ford. Three days out of Vell, two days since he'd burned his safehouse with everything in it but the memory that burned worse—the word *Aldermere*, whispering at him from inside his own skull like a tenant who refused to pay rent. Three days of looking over his shoulder. And now someone was finally looking back.

He heard them before he saw them: boots on scree, careful but not careful enough. One set of feet. Light. A hunter traveling alone through Thornfell's passes was either very good or very stupid, and Tamsin had built a life on the difference.

The figure came around the bend wrapped in a hooded cloak the color of wet slate, moving with the economical stride of someone who had walked hard roads for a long time. Tamsin let them pass beneath him. Counted their steps. On the fourth, he dropped.

He came off the boulder silent as falling snow, one arm locking across the stranger's chest, his knife—flat of the blade, always the flat, first rule of the trade, you can't sell a dead man's memories—pressed to the hood's edge. The figure went rigid. Small. Lighter than he'd expected.

"Wallets on the ground," he said, pitching his voice into the gravel register he used for this part. "And if you're carrying anything for the Warden, I'll know before your mouth opens. So save us both the theater."

The hood turned. Slowly. And then two hands rose—not in surrender, but to push the hood back—and Tamsin forgot, for one full heartbeat, how to breathe.

She was young. Nineteen, twenty, hard to say under the road-dirt and the hollowed look. Her hair had been cropped short and unevenly, the way hair gets cut when someone does it to you rather than for you. But none of that was what stopped him.

It was her eyes. He knew eyes. He'd spent years learning to read them—the flicker of calculation, the sheen of fear, the dull glaze of someone deciding whether to scream. This girl's eyes were none of those things. They were *empty*. Not vacant the way exhaustion makes people vacant, but empty the way a house is empty after the furniture's been carried out and the walls scrubbed clean. He had seen scraped minds before, once or twice, in the gutter-archives of Vell's memory trade—victims of accidents, of botched extractions. He had never seen it in a living face looking straight back at him.

"You," she said.

Her voice cracked on the word like ice on a pond. She stared up at him, and her empty eyes filled—with something. Recognition, or the ghost of it.

"You're the one from—" She stopped. Frowned, as if reaching into herself and finding the shelf bare. "I don't know. I don't know what you're from. But I know your voice."

Tamsin eased back half a step without releasing her. "That's a strange thing to say to the man holding a knife to your throat."

"It's a strange thing to say to anyone." She said it flatly, without self-pity, and somehow that made it worse. "Everything I say is strange. I've been saying strange things to strangers for—I don't know how long. I don't know how long."

"Who are you?"

"I don't know."

"What's your name?"

"I don't know." A muscle worked in her jaw. "They took it. Whoever they were. There were men in grey, and a man whose hands were cold even through his gloves, and he put his fingers here—" she touched her temple, just above the ear, "—and it was like being wrung out. Like I was a cloth and he was wringing me until nothing came. And when he finished, he looked almost sorry, and he said, 'Nothing there worth keeping,' and left me in a ditch outside Vell."

Tamsin's stomach turned over slowly, the way it did when he stood too near a high edge. *Warden Corvane.* It had to be. The purge had teeth, and this girl had been chewed.

"Why are you following me?" he asked.

"I wasn't following you. I was walking away from everything else." She paused. "But when I saw you on the ridge this morning, I thought—*him*. I thought I'd seen you somewhere important. Somewhere before the ditch." Her brow furrowed, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes, a shape pressing against the inside of frosted glass. "There's a garden. In my head there's a garden, except it isn't in my head, because nothing is in my head, that's the whole problem. But the garden is there anyway. Terraces going up a hillside. Stone walls with moss. And a door at the top, old wood, iron hinges, and light coming through the crack in it the color of honey. And someone's voice saying—"

She stopped. Looked at him again, harder this time.

"Saying what?" Tamsin asked quietly.

"I don't know. That's the cruelty of it. I have the sound of a voice and no words attached. Just—" her hand rose, hovered near his jaw without touching, tracing the air where his voice would come from, "—this. Rough. Low. A little amused, even when it shouldn't be. Like yours."

Tamsin stepped back fully now, knife lowered though not sheathed. His heart was doing something unprofessional. He had rules about this—rule three, never learn their names; rule five, never let them learn yours—but the rules assumed the other person had a past to endanger. This woman had nothing. She was a blank page walking, and blank pages terrified him more than any warden, because a blank page could be written on by anyone.

Including him. Including whatever was coiled in his own skull whispering *Aldermere.*

"Where were you going?" he asked.

"Away." She shrugged. "Away ran out this morning. The pass ended at a wall of fog and my feet wouldn't go into it. They wouldn't. Like walking into a locked door." Her eyes flicked up. "So I turned around. And then you fell on me."

"That fog wall—" His mouth had gone dry. "Describe it again. Exactly."

"Grey. Moving wrong. Like smoke that's decided to stay. My eyes kept sliding off it. I'd look right at it and find myself looking at the sky instead." She hugged her arms around herself. "Why? Do you know it?"

*I do,* said the word in his skull, in no voice at all. *Aldermere. Aldermere. Aldermere.*

"I know of it," he lied, smoothly, automatically, and hated himself for the smoothness. Rule six: never tell anyone what you carry. Rule six had kept him alive through nine years of selling other people's yesterdays.

But she only nodded, accepting the lie or indifferent to it, and swayed slightly on her feet. Now that the adrenaline was draining he could see how far gone she was—cracked lips, the fine tremor in her hands, the way she stood like someone who had forgotten bodies needed food. When had she last eaten? Before the ditch? Had Corvane's men fed her at all?

"There's a stream fifty paces down," he heard himself say. "And I have bread I stole from a man who won't miss it. Sit down before you fall down."

"I don't need—"

"You'll eat," Tamsin said, "because if you collapse on this trail, I'll have to carry you, and I threw my back out last winter lifting a barrel of stolen summers, and I refuse to explain that to anyone."

Something happened to her face then. It took him a moment to identify it, because it was so foreign to everything else about her: she almost smiled. The muscles remembered the shape even if the reasons were gone.

"All right," she said. "Thief."

"You don't know that I'm a thief."

"You fell on me from a rock and demanded my wallets. Also—" she tilted her head, "—you smell faintly of other people. Not their bodies. Their *lives*. Like a room where many candles have been burned. I noticed it when you grabbed me. I don't know how I know that. I don't know anything. But I notice things."

Tamsin stared at her. Nine years in the trade, and no one—not Odile, not any collector, not the one gifted child he'd briefly mentored and lost—had ever smelled it on him. He filed the fact away next to the whispering word and the fog wall, in the growing drawer of things that didn't fit.

"Come on," he said.

* * *

Sera—that was what he started calling her, that evening, when she asked him to give her *something*, even a borrowed name, because walking nameless made the world feel like it might close over her head like water. He'd resisted. Names were anchors; names could be traced. But she had looked at him with those scrubbed-clean eyes and said, "You of all people should understand what it is to be called something you're not," and he hadn't had an answer for that, so he gave her the first syllable that came to mind. *Sera.* She repeated it twice, tasting it, and nodded once, satisfied, as if she'd been handed back a coat she'd lost.

They made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang, a fire small enough to hide in cupped hands. Tamsin shared the bread and dried plums and watched her eat with the focused gratitude of someone rediscovering that hunger could end. Then, in the firelight, she began to talk—or rather, to inventory aloud, the way a survivor of a shipwreck might lay out what the sea returned.

"I have the garden," she said. "Terraces. Mossy walls. A door with iron hinges. Honey-colored light. I have rain—I don't know why rain, but when I think of it my skin remembers being cold and glad at once. I have a song with no tune, just the feeling of a tune. And I have you."

"I'm flattered."

"Don't be. You're the least of it. You're a voice and a jawline and a feeling like—" she searched, "—like a hand catching mine on stairs in the dark. That's all. The garden is bigger than you. The door is bigger than everything." She looked into the fire. "Do you think the man in grey missed some pieces? Or do you think the pieces I have aren't mine at all?"

The question landed in Tamsin's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He thought of the merchant's wedding memory still folded in his keeping, whispering its alien word. He thought of the mountains of Thornfell, which he'd begun to suspect held echoes of his own thefts lodged in stone like fossils. He thought: *what if memories don't die when they're taken? What if they drift, and lodge, and wait?*

"Maybe," he said, "some things are too heavy to carry away."

She considered that. "Then whoever I was, I was heavy."

"Heavy," Tamsin agreed, "and worth wringing dry. Corvane doesn't waste his hands on nothing."

At the name, she flinched—a full-body flinch, involuntary, her shoulders climbing toward her ears. "That name. When you say it, something behind my eyes goes white. Like a scar aching in cold weather." She rubbed her temples. "He said something, before he left me in the ditch. I keep almost having it. He leaned close and said—"

She pressed her palms against her eyes. Tamsin waited. The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea remembering itself.

"'Tell the thief,'" she whispered at last. "'If he finds the Door, tell the thief it opens inward.'" Her hands dropped. She looked at him, baffled and frightened. "I don't know what Door. I don't know what thief. But I said it to you because when I saw your face, I thought—maybe you're the thief. Maybe that's why I know your voice. Maybe he put you *in* me before he took everything else out."

The word in Tamsin's skull surged, sudden and loud, a tide against a seawall: *ALDERMERE ALDERMERE ALDERMERE*—and beneath it, for the first time, something new. Not the word. An image. Brief as a struck match: terraced gardens rising a hillside. Mossy stone walls. A door of old wood and iron hinges, honeyed light bleeding through its seam.

The same garden. The same door. Hers and his, matching like two halves of a torn letter.

His hands were shaking. He hid them in his sleeves, an old card-player's habit, and made his voice come out level.

"Eat your plum," he said.

* * *

She slept near the fire, curled tight as a fern frond, and Tamsin sat awake watching the embers and losing an argument with himself.

Rule six: never tell anyone what you carry. Rule seven: travel alone; attachments are handles by which you can be pulled. Rule eight, learned the hard way in the flood-year: everyone you help becomes a debt someone else will collect.

But she had been emptied by the same man hunting him. She carried the same garden in the wreckage of her mind. And she had walked into a wall of forgetting and turned back not from fear but because her feet refused—her body remembering a country her mind had been robbed of. If Corvane had scraped her, it was because she mattered to Aldermere. Which meant she mattered to the thing whispering in his skull. Which meant, whether he liked it or not, the blank page and the stolen map belonged to the same book.

He owed her nothing. He told himself that twice, three times, the way you repeat a password hoping it stays true.

Near midnight she woke suddenly, sat bolt upright, and said, "Rain," with such naked longing that Tamsin's chest hurt. "I dreamed it. Cold and glad. Cold and glad." Then she saw him watching and the shame crossed her face like weather. "Sorry. Did I wake—"

"Sleep," he said gently. "The road's long tomorrow."

She lay back down. Within minutes her breathing slowed. Tamsin stayed up, feeding twigs to the embers, and let himself admit the thing he'd been circling all night:

He couldn't leave her. Not because she was useful—though she was, though the matching image in her head was worth more to him than every cache he'd ever sold. But because when she'd said *tell the thief it opens inward*, something in him had answered, quiet and certain, the way a lock answers its key: *yes. That's for you. That was always for you.*

He didn't know what it meant. He suspected, with a dread that tasted like copper, that he was about to find out.

Because at dawn, when the light came grey through the pines, the fog wall at the head of the pass had changed. It no longer slid off the eye. It had thinned to a veil, and through it—faint as breath on glass—terraced gardens climbed a hillside toward a door of old wood and iron hinges.

And along the ridge behind them, moving fast and deliberate, came riders in grey.

Sera stood beside him, hair stirring in a wind that smelled of honey and rain, and said, in a voice gone steady and strange:

"They followed me. Didn't they. The man in grey—he didn't lose me in that ditch. He *let* me go. I'm the message, thief. I'm how he's tracking you home."
