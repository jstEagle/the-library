## 5. What the Collector Paid For

The city of Vell smelled different at night—less of salt and fish and hot oil, more of secrets. Secrets had a scent, Tamsin had learned long ago. Old paper. Candle tallow. The sour sweetness of wine drunk alone. He moved through it the way he'd been taught to move through everything: quietly, unremarkably, a shadow wearing a coat.

Sera kept pace behind him, close enough that he could hear her breathing when the wind died. She had refused to stay in the safehouse, and he had refused to argue. Arguing with her was like arguing with weather.

"You said she owes you," Sera murmured as they crossed a square where the proclamation posters still hung, Queen Vessaline's seal stamped in black wax over every wall. *All gifted persons must register before the thirteenth turn of the moon.* Beneath one poster someone had scrawled, in charcoal, *run*.

"Odile doesn't owe me," Tamsin said. "That's what makes her useful. Debts make people nervous. Curiosity makes them talk."

"And what does she have to be curious about?"

"A fence who vanishes for two years and comes back with a Warden on his heels." He turned down a lane so narrow the eaves nearly touched overhead, drowning them both in dark. "She'll want to know everything."

The memory house sat where it always sat, wedged between a chandler's and an empty lot that everyone pretended not to notice. From the street it looked like nothing—a shuttered shopfront, a door with no handle on the outside. But there was a knock, and then a second knock, and then a rhythm of knuckles against wood that Tamsin had practiced until his hand knew it better than his own name.

The door opened a hand's width. One eye, brown and shrewd and rimmed with kohl, appeared in the gap.

"The dead don't knock," Odile said.

"The dead don't need fences," Tamsin answered.

A long pause. Then the door swung wide, and Odile Vasseur stood before him in a robe the color of dried blood, silver rings stacked six deep on every finger, her grey hair pinned up with what appeared to be actual candlesticks. She looked exactly as he remembered and also ten years older, which was how Odile always managed to look.

"Well," she said, gaze sliding past him to Sera. "You've gotten interesting."

"I've gotten hunted."

"Yes." Odile stepped back from the doorway. "I heard. Come in before the walls do too."

* * *

Her parlor had not changed. It was a room designed to make visitors feel poor: velvet in three shades of bruise, a fire that gave light but little heat, and shelves upon shelves of glass jars in which memories were stored like preserved fruit, each one faintly luminous, each one humming at the edge of hearing. Tamsin felt them the moment he crossed the threshold—the way a man who has spent years near the sea feels the absence of waves in an inland town. A hundred lives, whispering in their jars.

He realized, with a small cold shock, how much he wanted one. His own head felt scraped raw these last weeks, the Aldermere memory sitting in him like a coal that would not cool.

"Sit," Odile said. "Both of you. Wine?"

"No," Sera said.

"Yes," Tamsin said.

Odile poured two glasses anyway, and gave all three to Tamsin, which told him something about how this evening would go. She settled into her chair like a queen into a throne built out of other people's discarded years, and looked at him over the rim of her own cup.

"Let us skip the part where you flatter me and I pretend to believe you," she said. "You want to know why the crown is tearing Vell apart looking for you."

Tamsin set down the wine. "Am I so transparent?"

"To me? Always." Her rings clicked against the armrest. "And I know why you're here instead of running, boy. You came to ask me about the sale."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, floorboards creaked—Odile's lodgers, or her guards, or simply the old building settling like a sleeping animal.

"What sale?" Sera asked quietly.

Tamsin didn't take his eyes off Odile. "Two years ago I bought a memory through her. Sealed provenance. Premium price. A stranger's lifetime, compressed into a single jar—and inside it, a road that doesn't exist, a word no one will say aloud, and a door." His mouth had gone dry. "Aldermere."

Odile's expression did not change. That, more than anything, frightened him. Odile's face was a ledger; it always showed something. Now it showed nothing at all.

"Where did it come from?" he asked. "Whose memory was it? Who sold it?"

"Sit down, Tamsin."

"I'm standing."

"Then stand," she sighed, "but hear me." She rose, drifted to one of the shelves, and let her fingers trail across the jars without touching them. "You think I deal in trinkets. You think this is commerce. Boy, I deal in *consequence*. Every memory that passes through this house changes the shape of someone's life, and I have spent thirty years learning which consequences I can afford." She turned back to him. "The Aldermere sale was not one of them."

"Then why did you make it?"

"Because the client paid enough to buy this whole street," Odile said, "and because refusing clients of that kind is how fences end up in the canal." Her voice flattened. "It came from the palace."

The words took a moment to land, the way heavy things do.

"The palace," Tamsin repeated.

"Delivered by a lady-in-waiting whose name I was not given and did not ask. Sealed with the royal insignia under a false merchant's mark—which fools no one, but gives everyone involved permission to keep pretending." Odile came back to her chair and sat, folding her ringed hands. "I was told the memory belonged to a servant who had stolen it from her mistress and fled. I was told my job was simply to find a buyer far from court who would pay well and ask nothing. I chose you because you were good, you were greedy, and you were nobody. A perfect grave for a dangerous thing."

"A grave," Tamsin said slowly. "Not a sale."

"That is what I have been telling myself for two years, yes."

Sera shifted beside him. In the jar-light her face looked carved out of pale wood. "Someone in the palace *wanted* that memory to leave. To disappear into some thief's head where no one would ever find it."

"Yes." Odile looked at her for a long moment—too long, Tamsin thought, a look with weight in it, as if she were measuring the girl against something. "And now someone else in the palace wants it back. Or wants it never to have existed."

Tamsin walked to the fire. His reflection wavered in the brass fender, stretched and strange, a face made of moving light. He thought of the merchant's wedding memory he had lifted in the market only days ago, how easily it had come loose, how the Aldermere thing beneath it had whispered up at him like something drowned rising to the surface. He had thought himself a thief who happened to carry a secret. He saw now that he had it backwards.

"It was never about registration," he said. "The purge. The Wardens. All those neighbors dragged away—" His voice caught on the thought and he forced it through. "They're not hunting the gifted. They're hunting one memory. Mine. And every unregistered thief in Vell is just noise they're willing to burn through to get to it."

"Now you understand," Odile said softly. "Vessaline's men are not looking for a needle in a haystack, child. They are burning the haystack."

* * *

Sera stood apart from them afterward, at the shelf of jars, her fingers hovering over the glass. Watching her, Tamsin felt the old ache he could not name—the ache of looking at a locked door with no keyhole. She touched nothing. Perhaps she feared breaking things. Perhaps she feared, as he privately feared, that contact with so much stored memory might pull at whatever void she carried, like water finding a hole in a hull.

"Why would a lady-in-waiting want Aldermere buried?" she asked.

Odile laughed, short and without humor. "Girl, when a great house buries something, it is never the servants' idea. Someone above them wanted that memory gone from the palace archives—from *everywhere*. And someone else above *them*" —her eyes flicked to Tamsin— "either never knew, or changed their mind."

"Vessaline," Tamsin said.

"Or whoever speaks with her voice these days." Odile waved a hand, rings flashing. "The Queen has not aged in forty years, if you believe the ballads. If you believe the ledgers I'm not supposed to have seen, half the noble houses of this kingdom have quietly emptied their attics and cellars of gifted children in the last decade. Sent to serve at court, they say. None write home." She picked up her wine at last and drank. "Something rots at the top of this country, Tamsin, and rot reaches downward. You are standing in the drip line."

He turned from the fire. "Then tell me the rest. There's a rest—I know your telling-look, Odile. You've been circling it since we came in."

For the first time that evening, Odile looked genuinely tired. She set down her glass and rubbed the bridge of her nose, smearing her kohl.

"The client didn't just sell the memory," she said. "There were instructions. Sealed ones, separate from the payment. I was to steer the buyer—if ever the buyer asked questions—toward certain answers." Her eyes met his, and there was something in them he had never seen before: shame, poorly housed. "And I was to report, once a year, whether you still lived."

The room went very quiet. The jars hummed. Sera had gone still as a held breath.

"You reported on me," Tamsin said.

"For two years. To a drop-box in the chandler's cellar. I never saw a face; I never received a reply." Odile spread her hands, rings dull in the low light. "Until four days ago. Four days ago there was a reply. One line, unsigned." She recited it flatly, the way one recites a curse: "*The bearer grows dangerous. Retrieve before the thirteenth turn. All else is permitted.*"

"All else is permitted," Sera whispered.

"In the language of courts," Odile said, "that means the city may burn. It means your neighbors, your safehouses, every thief and smuggler and memory-worker between here and the mountains—it means none of it matters. It means Corvane has been given a leash long enough to hang us all."

Tamsin's hands had curled into fists without his noticing. He made himself open them. Anger was a luxury; anger spent itself fast and left nothing to sell. He needed the other thing, the colder coin: understanding.

"Who is she?" he asked. "The lady-in-waiting. The seller. You said you never learned her name—but I watched you lie to buyers for six years, Odile, and I know what your honesty costs. So I'm asking for the expensive version."

Odile regarded him for a long moment. Then she rose, went to the cold hearth, and pressed somewhere along the mantel's underside. A panel breathed open in the wall, revealing a cavity no larger than a breadbox. From it she drew a folded scrap of fine paper, yellowed, handled many times.

"One letter," she said. "Unsealed, unsigned, undated. Found tucked into the payment chest with the jar." She held it out. "I have read it perhaps a hundred times. I still do not know if it is a warning or a lure."

He took it. The paper was expensive, rag-edged, smelling faintly of some flower he couldn't place—something that didn't grow in Vell. The handwriting was elegant, slanted, hurried:

*If you are reading this, then the memory has found a keeper at last, and I must trust a stranger with the only hope I have left. Know this: what you carry is not a curiosity. It is a country. The Queen believes it destroyed. She must go on believing it. Tell no one its name. And when the thirteenth moon begins to turn—run toward the mountains, not away from them. The Door remembers its thieves.*

"There's no signature," Tamsin said, though his eyes had already gone back to the top of the page.

"No. But look at the seal-scrape at the bottom, where a stamp was removed. Wax was cut away carefully—not torn." Odile's voice dropped. "And beneath the scrape, in the paper's grain, the impression remains. I have cast it in tallow. It is a court mark. The personal cipher of Lady Merren, Keeper of the Queen's Chambers."

"Lady Merren," Sera repeated, tasting the name.

"The woman who holds the Queen's keys," Odile said. "The woman who sleeps, every night, four doors from Vessaline's bed." She sank back into her chair and stared into the dying fire. "Either she planted that memory in my hands to hide it for the Queen—or she stole it from the Queen to wound her. And either way, Tamsin Joubert, the person who knows which is true is currently the most guarded woman in this kingdom, and you are the most hunted man in it, and between you stands Warden Corvane with permission to do anything at all."

Tamsin read the letter again. *Run toward the mountains.* He thought of the passes of Thornfell, of the way stone sometimes seemed to murmur at the edge of his gift, holding echoes of old thefts like riverbeds hold old rain. He thought of the map he was beginning to suspect lived coiled inside the Aldermere memory—not ink on paper but roads and ridgelines written in someone's living recollection, a country's geography carried in a human head. His head.

"Odile," he said. "When the thirteenth moon turns. What happens then?"

She looked up at him, and for once the fence, the merchant of other people's yesterdays, the woman who priced grief by the ounce—she looked old.

"Child, I deal in memories, not astronomy," she said. "But the old stories all agree on one thing. Doors like the one you're carrying do not open whenever you knock. They open when they choose. And they choose rarely." Her rings clicked softly as she folded her hands. "Whatever Lady Merren hid, whatever Vessaline fears, whatever Corvane has been promised—you have less than a season to become worth all of it."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a bell began to toll. Not the hour bell—deeper, slower, a sound with weight and intention, rolling up from the direction of the river. Once. Twice. Three times.

Odile's face drained of its color.

"That's the Warden's bell," she said. "That's the sound they ring when the Wardens have found what they're looking for."

Tamsin was already moving for the door, Sera a step behind him, when the fourth toll sounded—and beneath it, thin and terrible, came a new sound rising through the floorboards of Vell. Not screaming. Worse than screaming.

Silence—the sound of a whole quarter of the city forgetting, all at once, how to sleep.
