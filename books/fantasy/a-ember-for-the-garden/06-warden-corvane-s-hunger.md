## 6. Warden Corvane's Hunger

The cell beneath the Vell garrison had no windows, which Corvane considered a mercy. Windows reminded prisoners of sky, and sky reminded them of running, and running made their memories skittish—thin and bright at the edges, like water disturbed by a hand. He wanted this one still. Still memories kept their flavor.

The boy was perhaps eleven. He sat on the stone bench with his knees drawn up, watching the single candle as though it might turn on him. They always watched the candle. Fire was the closest thing to another consciousness in a room like this, and the gifted could never quite stop wondering whether it saw them.

"You took something that wasn't yours," Corvane said. "From the house of Master Ellery, the wool factor. Three nights ago. A memory of his mother's kitchen."

"I didn't—" The boy's voice cracked in the middle of the denial, splitting it into two smaller, weaker denials.

Corvane did not sigh. Sighing was a luxury of men who felt things cleanly. Instead he crossed the cell and sat on the stool opposite, arranging his long frame with the care of a man settling into a meal he did not expect to enjoy but knew he would finish. His warrant chain clinked against his knee. The sigil of the Queen's Registry—a closed eye worked in dull silver—swung once and stilled.

"Boy. What is your name?"

A hesitation. The particular hesitation of someone deciding whether their name was worth protecting.

"Fenn," the boy said at last. "Fenn Aris."

"Good. Fenn Aris." Corvane said the name slowly, tasting it the way he would soon taste everything else the boy carried. Names were the rind. You got through the rind to arrive at the fruit. "I am going to tell you what will happen, because I find that frightened children break memories worse than frightened adults. They shatter them. And shattered memory is no good to anyone—not to me, not to the Queen, not even to you, and it is *especially* no good to your mother, who is waiting in the Registry line on Marrow Street as we speak, holding a number written on slate."

The boy went very pale. Good. Pale meant listening.

"When I touch your temples," Corvane continued, "you will feel warmth. Most describe it as sunlight, though I have never understood why, since half of you were born in cellar dark and have never sunbathed in your lives. The warmth is your memory leaving you. It will feel, briefly, like being loved. That is an illusion. Memory does not love. It only wants to be kept."

"And then?" The boy's chin trembled but held. There was grit in this one. There was always grit in the ones from the river districts; hard water bred hard children.

"Then I will know whether you are the thief who has been drawing the Crown's attention through Thornfell and the market wards. And if you are not"—and here Corvane let his voice go almost gentle, the way one speaks to livestock before the bolt—"you will be released to your mother's custody, registered, marked, and free to live out your life under Her Majesty's generous eye."

All of it true, in the way that official language is always true. Released. Registered. Marked. Free. Four words, each one a small coffin.

He reached out and laid two fingers against the boy's temples.

* * *

The first taste was always the worst, because the first taste was the freshest and therefore the most *someone*.

It came to him the way all stolen memory came: as weather. A kitchen, steam on glass, a woman humming off-key—the boy's grandmother, Corvane judged, by the age of the hands kneading dough—and underneath it, threaded through it like wire through wax, the faint wrongness of the theft itself. Someone had been here before him. Someone had lifted the edges of this child's life and peeled.

Not Tamsin Joubert. Too clumsy. Too hungry. This had been a dabbler, a street-taught siphon, probably the boy himself, practicing on his own family because there was no one else safe to practice on. Corvane almost smiled. Every gifted child began by robbing the people who loved them. It was the nature of the talent: proximity first, ethics later, if ever.

He swallowed the kitchen whole.

And felt the familiar sick lurch as something inside him *shifted*—a rearrangement, a displacement, the sensation of a stone dropped into a full cup so that something else sloshed over the rim and was gone.

He caught the rim of what left him. Just barely. A flash: a woman's face, young, laughing—

Gone.

He sat very still in the candlelight, fingers still resting on the boy's temples, and searched himself the way a man searches his pockets after picking up the wrong coat. The laughing woman had been—someone. He could feel the groove where she had lived, the way a finger finds the worn place on a stair. But the face itself was already smoothing over, filling with the grandmother's flour-white hands, the off-key humming, steam on glass.

The kitchen had moved in. The woman had moved out. And he could not, for the life of him, remember whose face he had lost.

That was the arithmetic of his gift, the equation they did not teach in the Warden's academy because no one there had the stomach for it: every memory devoured displaced one of his own. He had learned it late, years after the first tastings, when he'd reached for his father's voice and found instead a fishmonger's Tuesday, complete and vivid and utterly useless, crowding out the sound. The mind was not a warehouse. It was a cup. And Corvane had been drinking from other people's lives for thirty years.

He was running out of himself.

He withdrew his hand. The boy slumped, blinking, already lighter—he would not miss the kitchen for weeks yet, and by the time he missed it, he would not know what he was missing. That was the cruelty of it. You cannot mourn what no longer exists, even in you.

"You're not the one I'm looking for," Corvane said.

"I—I stole from Master Ellery. I'm sorry. I gave the coin back, I—"

"I know what you did." Corvane stood, straightening his coat. "You took a memory of a dead woman because you couldn't stand that she was only going to fade. You wanted to keep her somewhere safer than your own head." He paused at the door. "That was stupid, Fenn Aris. Nothing in a thief's keeping is safe. Remember that, if you can."

The guard outside took the boy away to registration. Corvane remained in the cell alone, and allowed himself, for exactly sixty heartbeats, to sit with the hollow.

Sixty heartbeats. He counted them. Counting was one of the few rituals he had left that belonged entirely to himself, though lately he suspected even the counting was borrowed—from some clerk whose disciplined mind he had drained in a customs house in his twenties. Perhaps the ritual of counting had been that man's. Perhaps Corvane was mostly other people's habits wearing his own skin, a coat stitched from stolen cloth with only the buttons original.

What was left? He inventoried, as he did every night, the small museum of himself:

His name. Aldous Corvane. He was certain of it—almost. Certain enough.

His mother. A shape. Warmth at the edge of a doorway. An apron? No—an apron was the grandmother in the kitchen he'd just eaten. Damn it. He tried again. His mother. A smell of—of what? Something growing. Something green. A garden?

The word *garden* surfaced and dove again, and where it had been, there was now a tax ledger, and a wedding in rain, and the taste of a stranger's first kiss, and nine hundred other acquisitions pressing at the walls of him like tenants in an overcrowded house.

He had eaten his own history and called it duty.

* * *

The Queen summoned him at dusk—or rather, her steward did, which was the same thing the way a shadow is the same thing as the body that casts it.

Vessaline received him in the Long Gallery, where the portraits of her ancestors should have hung and did not. That was the thing about the palace that visitors found unsettling without knowing why: there was no past in it anywhere. No portraits, no tapestries older than fifty years, no letters, no cradles, no death masks. The Queen had consumed her own dynasty's memory so thoroughly that the royal line existed now only as furniture. Corvane had always found the gallery restful, for precisely that reason. Here, at least, no one else's past pressed against him. Here everyone was equally hollow.

She stood at the tall window with her back to him, and even from behind she looked wrong—young the way painted things look young, smooth as glazed porcelain, her hair black as spilled ink without a single thread of silver in forty years of reign. Youth purchased at the rate of a kingdom's remembering.

"Aldous," she said, without turning. She used his first name when she wanted to remind him she owned it.

" Majesty."

"My warden of memories sounds tired."

"I have interviewed forty-one registered gifted in six days. Tired is proportionate."

She turned then, and her eyes did the thing they always did—the thing that had unnerved him even when he was young and ambitious and flattered to be noticed. Her eyes were old. Everything else about her had been renewed so many times it had lost its grain, but the eyes stayed ancient, two coins minted in some earlier century, and they looked at him with an appetite he recognized because he saw it in his own mirror.

"Tell me about the thief," she said.

"Tamsin Joubert. Twenty-six, born in the Thornfell settlements, unregistered since birth. He is—skilled. Unusually so. The merchants he's robbed do not know they've been robbed until they reach for the memory and find air. He leaves nothing. No scar, no ache, no wrongness. Even my kind usually leaves a bruise."

"But he took something he should not have."

"He took a great many things he should not have." Corvane chose the words carefully. Lying to Vessaline was impossible; she had eaten too many liars to be fooled by one. But precision was not lying. "Three months ago, in Vell, he lifted a sealed memory from a collector's vault—a memory sold under a false provenance. I have traced its chain of custody through four fences. The memory concerns a place called Aldermere."

For the first time in thirty years of service, Corvane saw the Queen flinch.

It was a small thing. A tightening at the corner of her mouth, a fractional narrowing of those ancient eyes. But he had built a career on reading the micro-terrain of faces, and he read hers now with the same attention he gave a temple under his fingers, and what he tasted there was fear.

"You know the name," he said.

"I know what the name costs to say." She came away from the window, moving with that gliding, jointless grace that was not youth but the absence of age, which is a different and more terrible thing. "There was a country, once, east of the Thornfell passes. You will not find it on any map, because I have burned every map. You will not find it in any record, because I have burned every record. You will not even find it in your own head, Aldous, because the first thing I did upon taking the throne was eat it out of the realm's collective mind—one village at a time, one hearth at a time, until a whole nation forgot itself into silence."

"Why?" The question left him before he could dress it in courtesy.

"Because they had a door." She stopped an arm's length from him. Close enough that he could smell her—nothing. She smelled of nothing at all, the way a consumed memory smells of nothing. "A door out of the world, Aldous. Out of *my* world. Do you understand what that means to a queen? Not a gate to a neighbor's kingdom—that is merely war. A door *out*. Somewhere my taxes cannot follow, my proclamations cannot reach, and my hunger cannot feed. A country of people who could simply... leave me." Her voice softened into something almost human, which was the most frightening register she owned. "I erased them for the crime of having an exit."

"The memory Joubert stole—"

"Is the location. The key. The map." She smiled, and it did not reach the ancient eyes. "The thirteenth moon turns soon, Aldous. Once in a century, the Door answers. If it opens and empties, my realm bleeds. If it opens and I hold it—" She did not finish, and did not need to. He understood. She intended to swallow the Door itself, the way she had swallowed everything else: the last exit, the final refusal, locked inside her forever.

"Joubert is in Thornfell," Corvane said. "The passes are laced with smuggler roads. He knows them the way you know your own hands."

"Then know them better." She reached up and touched his cheek—a gesture from some earlier decade, from some queen he could no longer picture, performed so often it had survived as muscle memory while its meaning decayed. "Bring me the thief. Bring me the memory. Bring me Aldermere's grave, and open it, and I will give you whatever remains of yourself that you want back."

She thought it was kindness. That was the horror of her, he thought, bowing and withdrawing down the portraitless gallery—that after a century of consumption she believed appetite and generosity were the same gesture.

* * *

He did not sleep. He had not truly slept in years; sleep required a self to return to, and his was understaffed.

Instead he sat in his quarters above the garrison with a single lamp and did the work he never spoke of: reconstruction. On the table before him lay a merchant's ledger, blank, in which he had begun—years ago—to write down everything he could still verify about himself, cross-referenced against documents the way an archivist authenticates a forgery.

*Aldous Corvane. Born in Harrowmere, if the parish record is honest. Mother: a name that might be Maire, or Maren, or Maribel—the ink of my own remembering has run. She grew something. She grew something green.*

He stared at the sentence until the words blurred. *Something green.* It was pitiful. A whole woman reduced to a color and a category, and even that much bought secondhand from records, because the original had been spent—traded, he supposed, for some wretched useful thing. For the fishmonger's Tuesday. For a wedding in rain. For the layout of a smuggler's road he needed to hunt a thief.

Every tool of his trade was a piece of himself melted down for ore.

And yet.

He set down the pen and pressed his palms flat against the table, and admitted the thing he admitted to himself only in the smallest hours, in the gap between one heartbeat and the next where honesty could fit if it folded itself thin:

He could not stop.

Not would not. *Could not.* The hunger did not work like ordinary appetites. Each new memory quieted the crowding, silenced the tenants pressing at the walls of him—for an hour, for a day. Each taste was a moment of not-being-corroded, of feeling *solid*, and then the solidity thinned and the crowd returned and the only relief known to his ruined architecture was another mouthful of someone else's life. He was a man drinking seawater for thirst, and he had been drinking for thirty years, and the thirst had won so long ago that he could no longer remember the shore.

Somewhere out in the Thornfell dark, a thief named Tamsin Joubert was doing the opposite thing—hoarding other people's memories without consuming them, carrying lives intact, a courier of souls. The reports described a man who *kept* what he took. Who could presumably put it all back.

Corvane looked down at his ledger. At *something green*. At the grooves where a mother's face had been, worn smooth by thirty years of other people's kitchens.

If the thief could keep a memory whole—if he had ever held anything of Corvane's own, or of the country called Aldermere, where perhaps, in some archived corner of a deleted nation, there lived a record of a woman from Harrowmere who grew something green—

Then the hunt was no longer duty. It had not been for some time.

He rose, took down his warrant chain, and called for his horse. The Thornfell passes by morning. The thief within the week.

In his coat pocket, wrapped in oilcloth, he carried the ledger, and one item more: a memory vial, sealed, bought at ruinous price from a fence in the lowtown—a stolen recollection of a harvest festival, mothers and daughters, garlands, bread, a garden.

He told himself it was evidence. He told himself many things.

The horse's hooves struck sparks off the cobblestones as the Warden rode north, into the mountains that remembered everything, carrying less and less of himself the further he went.
