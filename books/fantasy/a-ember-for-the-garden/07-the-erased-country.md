## 7. The Erased Country

The chapel had no name, which was the first sign that they were somewhere the crown preferred forgotten.

Tamsin felt it in his teeth before he saw it—a thinness to the air, like a word half-swallowed. The mountain pass they'd followed out of Thornfell had narrowed all morning into a gorge stitched with old pine roots, and then, quite suddenly, opened onto a shelf of valley where nothing grew quite right. The grass came up silver at the tips. The stream ran clear but made no sound. And there, half-drowned in ivy and lichen the color of old bruises, stood a chapel built of pale stone that seemed to drink the daylight rather than reflect it.

"No birds," Sera said quietly. She had not spoken much since the ambush, since she had looked at him with those scraped-clean eyes and said *I know your face* as though reciting something left on her tongue by someone else. "There should be birds."

"There should be a village," Tamsin said. "A road. A reason for a chapel." He turned slowly, cataloguing the valley the way he catalogued a mark's house before a theft—the exits, the sightlines, the silences that didn't fit. "Chapels don't stand alone, Sera. People build them where people live."

"Then where did the people go?"

He didn't answer, because the honest answer was *I don't know*, and because another answer had begun whispering at the edge of his mind—the stolen memory, the one that had ruined his life, turning over in its sleep like a sleeper disturbed by a familiar smell. It had been doing that since they entered the pass. Not words yet. Not pictures. Just a low hum of recognition, the way his own bones might ache before a storm.

"We shouldn't stop here," he said, and walked toward the chapel door anyway.

Sera almost smiled. He saw it, the ghost of it, there and gone. "You always do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Say one thing and do the other." She fell into step beside him. "It's how I knew you were real. In my head there isn't much—there's fog, and there's you, walking through it, saying one thing and doing the other."

The chapel door hung on one hinge. Tamsin pushed it open with two fingers, and the smell that rolled out stopped his breath: dust, certainly, and rot, certainly—but beneath both, impossibly fresh, cut grass and woodsmoke and something flowering he could not name. A smell with no business being decades old. A smell like a country still breathing under a dropped stone.

Inside, the light came down through a hole in the roof in a single dusty column, and in that column stood rows of wooden pews, and in the pews sat—

Tamsin's heart slammed once, hard, before his thief's eye sorted it. Not people. Coats. Dozens of coats, cloaks, shawls, folded neatly over pew-backs as if their owners had stepped out mid-service to stretch their legs and simply never returned. Moths had taken their toll, but someone had kept them tidy; the folds were deliberate, respectful. At the front, where an altar should have been, stood instead a great cabinet of drawers—hundreds of them, small as spice boxes, each labeled in a fine looping hand.

Labels with no letters. Only a symbol: a circle crossed by a single vertical line, like an eye closed by one calm stroke.

"Aldermere," Sera said.

Tamsin's head snapped toward her. "What did you say?"

She was staring at the symbol, her brow furrowed, one hand pressed flat against her sternum as if something inside her had knocked. "I—" She blinked. "I didn't say anything. Did I say something?"

"You said Aldermere."

"I don't know that word." Her voice frayed at the edges. "I don't know any words. I know *bread* and *river* and *your face*, and this—" she pointed at the closed-eye sigil, hand trembling now, "—this is in my mouth like a stone I've been holding since before I can remember. Which is nothing. I remember nothing, Tamsin. So why does this room feel like—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. He had watched enough memories pour out of enough skulls to recognize grief when it arrived ahead of its own explanation.

"It feels like home," he finished for her, softly. "Don't say it. Don't decide it yet. Let's find out what this place is first."

* * *

The cabinet drawers held paper. Tamsin discovered this by easing one open with the same delicacy he'd use on a merchant's lockbox, and finding inside a slim bundle of documents tied with faded ribbon: birth records. He could read the shape of them even where the ink had bloomed—names, dates, a mother's line, a father's line, and beneath each entry, in smaller script, the closed-eye sigil stamped in violet wax.

Every drawer was a child. Every child bore the seal.

"They recorded births here," he murmured, drawing drawer after drawer, careful to replace each exactly. "Hundreds of years of births. This wasn't a village chapel. This was—" He stopped at a drawer near the bottom, where the wax seals gave way to plain ink stamps, then to nothing at all, the last entries trailing off mid-year like a sentence interrupted. "This was everything. This was the whole ledger of a people."

Sera stood apart from him, arms wrapped around herself among the coat-lined pews. "Read me the names," she said.

"What?"

"The names in the drawers." Her chin came up, and there was something fierce and frightened in her face. "Say them out loud. All of them if you have to. Maybe one of them will—" Her voice cracked. "Maybe one of them will open something."

So he read them. He crouched in the dust and the column of light and read names until his throat went dry—Maren Ostrevic, born of Ilse and Davorin; Callum Brightwater, born of Annet and the same; a girl named Vess—no, *Vessa*, not Vessaline, though the similarity struck him cold enough that he paused—and on through the alphabet of the dead-or-dispersed while Sera stood with her eyes shut and her face tilted up like a woman standing in rain that wouldn't fall. Nothing sparked. Nothing opened. But she didn't ask him to stop, so he didn't, drawer after drawer, until the last empty ones yawned bare and the light in the roof-hole had gone from gold to copper.

It was Sera who found the book.

She had drifted, while he read, toward the back of the chapel, behind the altar-cabinet, where a low door led to what must have been the priest's cell. When he found her she was kneeling before a loose flagstone, prying it up with a broken piece of pew, and beneath it lay an iron-bound chest no larger than a bread box, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark.

"Together?" she asked.

"Together."

They unwrapped it side by side, and inside was a book—not a ledger but a chronicle, hand-lettered on paper that had somehow escaped the damp, and bound in covers stamped with the closed eye. The first page bore a title in that same looping script, and Tamsin read it aloud, and the word he had been carrying in his skull like a splinter for weeks finally showed him its face:

*The True Account of the Kingdom of Aldermere, Set Down So That It Might One Day Be Remembered.*

His hands shook. The memory behind his eyes stirred and pressed forward, eager, and he clamped down on it with practiced discipline—not now, not until he understood what it wanted from him—and began, with Sera close enough that he could feel her breath, to read.

* * *

The chronicle told of a green country east of Thornfell's spine, terraced from river-valley to mountaintop in gardens so ancient no one remembered who had first laid the stones. It told of a people who had learned, over long centuries, a craft the rest of the world called superstition: the tending of memory as one tends an orchard. Memories could be shared at weddings and funerals. Memories could be planted in children like seeds of wisdom. Memories could be given freely to ease the dying of their fears.

And because the Aldermereans understood memory, the chronicle said, they understood what it would mean when the young kingdom of Vell to the west crowned a new queen and announced her first great tax.

*Not coin,* the chronicler had written, the letters growing cramped and urgent. *Not grain, not blood. She asks for memories. One in ten, she says, freely surrendered, for the good of realm and queen alike. As if memory were wheat, to be milled. As if a self could spare a tithe of itself and remain a self.*

Queen Maren of Aldermere—Tamsin's breath caught; the name from the drawers, the girl Vessa's grandmother perhaps, or aunt—had sent back a refusal so brief the chronicler reproduced it whole: *We will pay in anything that grows back.*

He heard Sera make a small sound beside him when he read it. He reached without thinking and took her hand, and she gripped his fingers hard enough to hurt, and he kept reading.

The tax collectors had come back with soldiers. The soldiers had come back with something worse. The chronicle described it only glancingly, in a hand grown unsteady: *a warden, the first warden, who eats what we keep and leaves us hollow*. Whole villages scraped blank in a night. Mothers who looked at their children with polite strangers' smiles. And then the final pages, written by a different hand entirely, rushed and ragged:

*They are not content to take our memories. They are taking our place in the world. Maps burn in Vell tonight—I have seen the orders. Our name is being unpicked from every record, every road-sign, every tongue. There is a working, done in the palace, that will bind it: soon no one outside our borders will be able to hold the name Aldermere in their minds. It will slide off them like water off wax. We are to be not conquered but deleted.*

*I am the keeper of this chapel, and I will not allow my people to vanish clean. I have hidden our truest memory—in the one vessel they cannot raid without knowing to look. If you read this, you hold the account of a real country, and you are either one of us or one sent by whatever power still remembers. The Door stands in the high garden. It opens on the thirteenth moon. May whoever finds this be braver than I was, and faster.*

Below that, a single map, hand-inked, of passes through Thornfell's eastern wall—and marked upon it, in violet ink matching the birth-seals, a route. A route Tamsin knew. A route he had carried in his head for weeks without understanding it, lodged in the stolen memory that whispered *Aldermere* at the edges of his sleep.

The map in the book and the map in his skull were the same map.

"Oh," he said. Very quietly. "Oh, no."

"Tamsin?" Sera's voice came from very far away. "Your eyes just went strange."

"The memory I stole. The one Odile sold me, the one the crown is tearing Thornfell apart to find—it's not a treasure. It's not a secret worth armies." He looked up at her, and the dread in him had curdled into something worse: understanding. "It's directions. Someone in that palace has been looking for this country for years, and they needed a thief to carry the way there, because no one inside the walls *can* carry it—the name won't stay in their heads. They needed someone outside the binding. They needed someone stupid and greedy and exactly as good as me." He laughed, and hated the sound. "I'm not carrying a stolen jewel, Sera. I'm carrying the key to a door a queen wants locked forever. Or opened. I don't know which is worse."

But Sera wasn't listening anymore. She had gone very still, both hands pressed to the chronicle's open page, and tears were sliding down her face in perfect silence, as if her body had remembered how to grieve even though her mind had been robbed of the reasons.

"Sera?"

"She refused," she whispered. "The queen here. She said *we will pay in anything that grows back.*" Her thumbs moved across the page, over the cramped, dying hand. "Why do I know how she said it? Why can I hear her voice, Tamsin? I have nothing. Corvane emptied me. I've seen the inside of my own head and it's a swept floor." She looked up at him, and her eyes were terrible with a grief too large for the person it had landed on. "Unless it isn't nothing. Unless it's swept on purpose. Unless I'm not a girl who lost everything—I'm a girl they hid here, the same way the keeper hid this book. Under a stone. Where no one would think to look."

The chapel creaked around them, coats stirring faintly in a draft, hundreds of folded garments waiting for shoulders that had been scattered or scraped or deleted. Tamsin thought of the birth-ledger, the hundreds of sealed drawers, and one drawer in particular he had opened and read without knowing why his fingers had chosen it. He thought of the name written there.

He didn't say it. Not yet. Some mercies deserved better footing than a ruined chapel at dusk.

Instead he closed the chronicle, wrapped it in its oilcloth, and tucked it inside his coat against his heart, where he kept the things he couldn't afford to lose. "Then we don't run west anymore," he said. "We follow the keeper's map. East, through the wall of Thornfell, to wherever this Door of theirs sleeps. You and me and every hollow-eyed soul Corvane's burned along the way."

"And when we get there?" Sera asked.

Outside, far down the silvered valley, a bell began to ring—one deep note, then another, from a tower Tamsin had sworn was roofless and empty. Thirteen strokes, unhurried, patient, and with each one the stolen memory behind his eyes flared brighter, burning itself inward, no longer whispering but calling.

"When we get there," Tamsin said, as the thirteenth bell faded, "we find out why the mountains are counting."
