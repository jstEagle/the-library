## 8. Smoke Over Thornfell

The fire came at dusk, the way the worst things always did.

Tamsin saw it first as a second sunset, a smear of orange climbing the western ridgeline where it had no business being. He was standing at the mouth of the Hollow Stair with Sera beside him, watching the last light drain out of the sky, when he understood that the light wasn't draining at all. It was gathering. Feeding.

"Corvane," Sera said. She didn't ask. She had learned the shape of the warden's appetite well enough now to know its signature—the way his destruction arrived not as chaos but as *tidying*, everything burned in neat, deliberate rows.

Tamsin counted the fires. One on the Saltwife Road. Another where the switchbacks crossed the Weeping Face. A third, smaller, down in the gullies where the smugglers' lanterns hung—ghost-lights that had guided runners through Thornfell for three generations and were now guiding nothing but flame.

He's burning the routes. Not chasing us along them. Erasing them.

"He knows we're using the passes," Tamsin said. His voice came out flat, the practiced flatness of a man reading a room for exits. "He can't follow me through stone I've memorized. So he's taking the stone away."

Below them, in the shelter of the Hollow Stair's first landing, forty-some people looked up. Refugees. That was the word people used, and Tamsin still flinched from it every time. They were candlemakers and cartwrights, a midwife named Bress who kept her instruments wrapped in oilcloth against her spine, two brothers from Vell whose only crime had been a grandmother who could taste lies. They had come to him over the past six days because word traveled in Thornfell the way water traveled downhill: *the thief Joubert knows roads that don't exist anymore.*

He had never wanted to be anyone's map. Maps got folded, torn, consulted until they wore through. But here they were, looking up at him with the particular faith of people who had run out of other options, and the ridge above them glowed like a forge.

"How long do we have?" Sera asked.

Tamsin read the wind. It came downslope smelling of pine resin and something fouler beneath—burnt hair, burnt paper, the cloying sweetness of memory catching alight. Corvane's fires weren't ordinary fire. The crown's pyres were fed with confiscated recollections, whole lifetimes seized from registered gifted and burned as fuel, because fear made the best tinder and the crown had a surplus of fear.

"Until morning," he said. "Maybe less. The wind's wrong."

Bress came up the stair to stand near him, her weathered face lit orange. "Then we go tonight."

"It's not that simple." He hated how often he'd been saying that lately. "There's one route left that isn't touched. The Undermem. It doesn't appear on any chart because it isn't a road, exactly. It's—" He stopped, searching for words that wouldn't make him sound mad. "It's a road the mountain remembers. Old smuggler craft. You walk it by carrying memories into the stone and letting the stone carry you back."

The refugees exchanged glances. Sera didn't. She just watched him with those eyes that held nothing and somehow reflected everything, and said, "You've done it before."

Once. Once, years ago, half-drunk on someone else's honeymoon and feeling invincible. And once was why he knew what it cost.

"It takes memories to open," he said. "Real ones. You give them to the pass, and the pass lets you through the places where there is no through." He rubbed his eyes. "I don't have enough of my own to spend."

* * *

They packed in the dark by touch and habit, no lanterns, because lanterns were beacons and beacons were invitations. Tamsin went through his cache one last time, kneeling in the lee of a boulder with his satchel open before him, and felt the familiar vertigo of holding more lives than one skull should contain.

To anyone else the satchel's contents would have looked like nothing: river stones, buttons, a brass thimble, a curl of ribbon, scraps of cloth knotted in patterns only he could read. Anchors, every one. A stolen memory needed a weight or it drifted, thinned, dissolved back into the general fog of the world. Each object in the satchel was a door into somebody's yesterday.

The merchant's wedding. The fisherman's last morning with his father. A baker's entire childhood in a house with a blue door. The soldier's letter home, never sent. Forty-one anchors. Forty-one lives, or pieces of lives, that Tamsin had lifted from the wealthy and careless over nine years of thieving, some sold on to collectors, most kept because keeping them had been easier than asking himself why he kept them.

He picked up the thimble. Inside it, folded small as a pressed flower, lived sixty years of a woman named Anselma Crowe: her marriage, her garden, the winter her son nearly died of fever and she had sat up four nights praying to gods she didn't believe in. He'd taken it from her grandson, who couldn't remember what the thimble had been for and had sold it for drinking money. The memory inside was warm. They always were, at first.

"I'll need to use these," he told Sera quietly. She crouched across from him, her knees drawn up, her face a pale smudge in the dark. "On the Undermem. Every gate we pass, I pay a toll. And every payment—" He turned the thimble in his fingers. "Every payment means part of someone's life goes into the mountain instead of staying with me. It doesn't come back."

"You could refuse," Sera said. Not an accusation. A question wearing an accusation's clothes.

"Forty people behind us. Fire above us. One thief with a bag full of other people's yesterdays." He almost smiled. "It turns out I'm the richest poor man in Thornfell, and the tax is due."

Sera reached out and put her hand over his on the thimble. Her skin was cool; it was always cool, as if her scraped-clean body hadn't figured out yet how to make its own heat. "Anselma Crowe," she said. "That's her name? In there?"

"How did you—"

"You say names when you hold them. You don't notice." She took her hand back. "You should. Somebody ought to remember them while you're spending them."

He looked at her for a long moment. There it was again, that thing he couldn't name, lodged under his ribs like a swallowed coal. Nine years of theft and he had never once had anyone watch him do it kindly.

"All right," he said roughly. "Names. I can do names."

He stood, turned to the huddled shapes on the landing, and raised his voice just enough. "We move now. Single file, hand on the shoulder of the person ahead. Nobody speaks above a whisper. Nobody touches the walls." He paused. "And if you feel something that isn't yours—something you never lived, a smell, a song, a grief—don't fight it and don't chase it. The Undermem leaks. Let it pass through you like weather."

Bress shouldered her pack. "And if it doesn't pass?"

"Then you'll know," Tamsin said, "what it's like to be someone else for a moment. Try to be gentle with them."

* * *

The entrance to the Undermem was a seam in the cliff face no wider than a coffin lid, hidden behind a curtain of hanging roots, and it opened inward into a dark that breathed.

That was the first thing everyone noticed and nobody mentioned: the passage breathed. Slow inhalations that pulled the cold air past their faces, slow exhalations that carried smells from nowhere—cut hay, ink, a particular kind of bread baked in a country none of them could name. The walls were wet slate that gave back no light, and when Tamsin laid his palm against them he felt the faintest vibration, deep and rhythmic, like a heart buried in the roots of the mountain.

*Thornfell remembers,* his old teacher had told him, back when Tamsin had been a boy with fast hands and no future, learning the smuggler craft in these same passes. *Every theft that crosses a mountain leaves a mark, the way a boot leaves a print in snow. Enough thieves, enough years, and the prints become a path. But a remembered path wants feeding. Nothing walks for free.*

"Toll gate ahead," he murmured, and heard the whisper travel back down the line, person to person, shoulder to shoulder.

The first gate was a narrowing, a place where the passage pinched so tight that the rock ahead simply *wasn't*, not in the way rocks are things—more a suggestion of solid dark that his eyes kept sliding off, the way they slid off the edge of sleep. He knew better than to try to see it. He took the thimble from his pocket and held it, and let Anselma Crowe rise in him.

It was like opening a window in winter. Sixty years poured up through him: hands in garden soil, the specific weight of a sleeping child, a wedding ring worn thin as wire. He walked to the pinch-point and pressed the thimble and the memory both against the stone, and spoke the name aloud because he had promised.

"Her name was Anselma Crowe. She grew beans and roses. Her son lived."

The stone drank. He felt it happen—a drawing-out sensation, like a hook slipping through wool—and then the wall of not-rock rippled, sighed, and became merely rock, and beyond it the passage continued. But the thimble in his palm was only a thimble now, bright brass and hollow, and when Tamsin searched inside himself for Anselma Crowe's garden he found only the shape where it had been. A negative space. A tooth-socket in his mind.

She would remember it still, wherever she was, if she still lived. But he had been the second keeper of those sixty years, and now the mountain was.

"That's it?" Bress whispered behind him as the line filed through. "It just—it took it?"

"The Undermem runs on remembering," Tamsin said. "Somebody has to supply."

They walked on. Twice more in the next hours he paid. The fisherman's last morning bought them past a chamber where the dark moved like water and things swam in it that were drawn to unspent lives. He gave the mountain the fisherman's father's laugh—that terrible, generous, wheezing laugh—and felt it go, and wept a little without knowing why, which was the worst part, that the grief stayed after the memory left, orphaned, attached to nothing.

The third toll was the baker's childhood, the house with the blue door, and by then Sera had begun walking beside him at the gates, saying the names with him, so that each payment went into the stone doubled: *his name was Aldous Ferryman, and the door was blue, and his mother sang off-key.* Whether it helped the passage open wider or only made the spending bearable, Tamsin couldn't say. He only knew that he could not have done it alone, and that this frightened him more than Corvane's fires.

* * *

They slept in a side chamber where the breathing of the mountain slowed to something like rest, and Tamsin dreamed of the satchel.

In the dream he opened it and the anchors lay there dull and dead, buttons and stones and ribbon, nothing behind any of them, and he understood with dream-certainty that he had spent them all—not here, not yet, but someday soon, and the someday was the dream's message. He was a house burning its furniture for heat. He was a library tearing out pages to keep the fire going. And when the last anchor was spent, what would remain of Tamsin Joubert except the debts?

He woke with Sera's eyes on him. She sat propped against the wall, keeping the middle watch, and in the absolute dark of the Undermem her face was the only thing he could see, faintly luminous, as if her emptiness made room for a little borrowed light.

"You were saying names in your sleep," she said. "Anselma. Aldous. A woman called Miriwhoever."

"Mirieth Vance. I haven't spent her yet." He sat up, joints cracking. "Give me time."

"Don't joke about it."

"One of us shouldn't be serious all the time. People will talk."

But his heart wasn't in it, and she knew it, and after a silence she said, "How many left?"

He inventoried without wanting to. Thirty-eight anchors. Thirty-six. The numbers were already becoming a language he thought in, a ledger running always in the background: *this life buys a day's walking, this one a night's hiding, this one might buy all our lives at the final gate if the final gate asks what he feared it would ask.*

"Enough," he said. "For now."

"And after the passes? After Aldermere, if we ever—" She stopped. He heard her swallow. "Tamsin. What happens to you at the end of this road?"

He was saved from answering by the mountain.

It began as a tremor in the floor, gentle as a passing wagon, and then the breathing of the Undermem stuttered—all inhale, no exhale—and somewhere far above them, filtered through a hundred yards of living stone, came a sound like the world's largest door being kicked in. Heat bloomed suddenly in the air, dry and wrong. The smell of burnt memory, sharp as struck flint.

Sera was on her feet. "He found a vent. He's pouring fire down into the passes."

"No." Tamsin stood slowly, listening, and what he heard turned his blood to well-water. Beneath the crack and rush of flame there was another sound, threaded through it: voices. Hundreds of voices, screaming, singing, sobbing, all of them speaking at once in the flames as the confiscated memories burned and their owners' words rose up the mountainside and down through every crack and seam—including this one. "He's not trying to smoke us out. He's feeding the fire *into* Thornfell. Into the stone itself." His mouth had gone dry. "The Undermem runs on memory. He's drowning it in too much."

The passage ahead filled, without warning, with light.

Not firelight. Lantern-light, cold and green, drifting up from the depths of the mountain in a slow procession—ghost-lanterns, the old smugglers called them, echoes of lights carried by thieves a century dead, which only ever showed themselves when the Undermem was disturbed. They floated toward the refugees in silent files, and where they passed, the walls began to speak.

Tamsin heard his own voice, young and laughing, from a theft he'd made at nineteen. He heard a dead woman reciting a recipe. He heard a language nobody alive spoke, and a lullaby, and a confession, and a name repeated over and over—*Aldermere, Aldermere*—and the refugees began to cry out, clutching their heads, as other people's lives flooded through them like water through an open window.

Sera stood perfectly still in the middle of it, and her face—

Her face was *changing*. Not in flesh; in expression. Wonder, recognition, terror, joy, chasing each other across her features like weather, and when she turned to Tamsin her eyes were full.

"I can almost—" she whispered. "Tamsin, the lanterns, they're carrying something, I can almost *reach*—"

The mountain lurched. The floor tilted, and the passage ahead, which had run south toward the low border for as long as smuggler lore could recall, groaned and shifted like a sleeper turning over—and Tamsin felt, through the soles of his boots, the unmistakable sensation of the road beneath him deciding, all on its own, where it wanted to go.

Southwest. Toward a border that no longer existed.

The Undermem was remembering Aldermere aloud. And it was taking them with it whether he paid or not.

"Hold hands!" he shouted over the rising chorus of voices. "Nobody lets go! The road's moving and we move with it!"

As the ghost-lanterns closed around them and bore them deeper, Tamsin put his hand on the satchel at his hip—thirty-six lives, thirty-six doors—and made himself a promise in the dark: whatever the mountain asked at the last gate, he would count the cost in others' names first.

Somewhere above, the fire sang with a hundred stolen voices, and among them, faint and terrible, he was almost sure he heard the Warden laughing.
