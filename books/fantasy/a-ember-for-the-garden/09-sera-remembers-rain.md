## 9. Sera Remembers Rain

The cave above the frozen ford had two rooms: one for the refugees, who slept in a tangle of limbs and shared blankets like spilled beads, and one small hollow at the back where Tamsin kept his satchel and his secrets. Sera had noticed it the first night — how he wound his way back there when he thought no one watched, how he sat with the leather bag in his lap like a man visiting a grave.

She watched tonight. The fire at the cave's mouth had burned down to a red breathing, and the refugees were asleep, all of them worn thin by the passes and the smoke that still rose from Thornfell's southern routes like prayers nobody wanted answered. Tamsin sat with his back against the cold stone, the satchel open across his knees.

Inside it, she knew, were years. Other people's years. Little glass vials of amber resin, each one holding a memory pressed thick as honey — a wedding, a first snowfall, a mother's voice singing through a floorboard. She had seen him work them before, pressing a thumb to the wax seal and closing his eyes, drawing out what he needed: a path through a pass, a face, a name. Each time he did it, something left him. Not from the vial. From *him*. She'd begun to see it, the way you begin to see a candle guttering — small, at first deniable, then impossible to ignore.

Tonight he took out a vial and simply held it, turning it in the firelight, not drinking from it. Grieving over it, maybe. There was a particular stillness to him when he did that, a stillness like held breath.

Sera didn't decide to move. Her feet decided for her.

The stone was cold under her bare soles. He heard her anyway — he always did — and his hand closed over the satchel's mouth quick as a trap springing.

"You should be sleeping."

"So should you." She sat down across from him, cross-legged, uninvited, because asking permission would have meant admitting she'd been watching, and watching was its own confession. "What are they? Really. Not the fence-market answer. You told Odile's courier they were 'inventory.' I want the real answer."

Tamsin looked at her a long moment. Firelight moved in his eyes, and behind the wariness there was something tired and old and terribly young.

"They're lives," he said finally. "Stolen ones. I take a memory out of a person the way you'd draw a thread from a sleeve — carefully, if you're kind; roughly, if you're paid enough. And I wind the thread around this." He lifted a vial. The resin inside caught the light and held it, glowing faintly from within, like a jarred ember. "Whoever wears the seal can feel it again. Or I can. That's the trade. That's always been the trade."

"And no one knows you've taken them?"

"The rich don't miss one memory. They have too many. A merchant can lose his wedding day and never notice the hole, the way a man with a full purse doesn't count coins." His mouth twisted. "It's the poor who feel every theft. Which is why I never took from them. One rule. The only one I ever kept."

Sera reached out. "Let me feel one."

His hand came down over the vials like a shutter over a window. "No."

"Why not?"

"Because—" He stopped. Started again, slower, choosing words the way a man chooses footing on ice. "You have nothing, Sera. Nothing spare. When your head is empty, anything that pours in fills it up. It doesn't sit beside what's already there. It *becomes* what's there." He met her eyes. "I've seen it happen. A gifted girl in Vell, once, my teacher — she let a starving woman hold a memory of bread, just to comfort her, and the woman couldn't give it back. Kept reaching for more. Memories want to be lived, and an empty mind is a room with every door open."

"I don't care."

"You should."

"Tamsin." She said his name deliberately, tasting it, the way she tasted everything about him these days — hungry, cataloguing, trying to build a person out of observations the way a bird builds a nest out of stolen string. "I have been scraped clean by the Warden of an entire kingdom. Do you know what that feels like? It doesn't. That's the horror of it. I reach for yesterday and there is nothing to reach *with*. No texture. No weight. I know I had a childhood the way you know there's a room behind a wall, but I can't find the door." Her hands had become fists without her permission. "And then you come along, carrying thirty lives in a bag, and you tell me I can't touch even one?"

"It's dangerous—"

"Everything is dangerous. Corvane is dangerous. The thirteenth moon is dangerous. Being *known* is apparently lethal in this kingdom." She was whispering, but the whisper had edges. "I'm not asking to keep it. I'm asking to feel something that isn't fear or cold or the smell of forty strangers' feet. Just once. Just to remember what remembering is like."

The fire popped. Behind them, someone in the refugee pile sighed and murmured in sleep, a woman calling a name — *Pieter, Pieter* — into a dream.

Tamsin closed his eyes. When he opened them, something in him had surrendered, the way a gate surrenders — slowly, with a groan of old hinges.

"One," he said. "The smallest one. And you give it back. You concentrate on giving it back, do you understand? Like exhaling. In and then out. If you feel it start to stick — if it starts feeling like *yours* instead of something you're borrowing — you pull away. Promise me."

"Promise."

He sorted through the vials, frowning, until he found a small one near the bottom, its wax seal stamped with a plain mark. "Rain," he said. "A farmer's daughter, somewhere east of Vell. Nine years old, first storm after a drought. It's short. Simple. Almost no people in it — just weather and a field. Safest thing I carry."

He worked the seal loose with his thumbnail. The smell that rose from it was astonishing — petrichor, wet dust, ozone — impossibly vivid in a cave smelling of ash and wool. He held the open vial out on his flat palm.

"Just touch it," he said. "Skin to the resin. Breathe slow."

Sera touched it.

* *

Rain.

Not the idea of rain. Not the word. *Rain* — the sound arriving before the sight, a vast soft roaring like applause from the sky, and the first fat drops hitting her upturned face with individual weight, *one*, *two*, a third catching in her eyelashes. Dust turning to mud under her bare heels. The smell of the field drinking — she hadn't known fields could drink, but she could feel this one doing it, the dry earth opening itself with little gasps of steam.

Lightning stitched the sky and thunder answered, and the girl whose memory it was — Liska, her name arrived attached to the joy like a price tag — *Liska, Papa says the wells will fill, Liska, run to your mother* — the girl spun with her arms out, mouth open, catching rain on her tongue, and it tasted the way slate smells, mineral and clean, and Sera was crying, she realized, crying with her whole face, and the tears mixed with the rain so there was no telling which was which—

There was no telling which was which.

Somewhere very far away, Tamsin was saying her name. She felt his hand close on her wrist, gentle, urgent. *Let go now. Sera. Let go.*

But the rain was still falling, and letting go of rain seemed the most obscene idea in the world. Why would anyone ever let go of rain?

*In,* said the memory, warm and vast, *and then out.* And she understood suddenly what he'd meant about doors. The memory wasn't sitting in her mind the way a visitor sits in a hall. It was unpacking. Hanging its coat. Learning where the cupboards were. It fit — that was the terrible thing — it fit her emptiness the way water fits a cup, and the cup did not care what it was filled with, only that it be full—

She wrenched her hand away.

The cave slammed back. Stone. Firelight. Cold air on wet cheeks. Tamsin's face inches from hers, stricken, both her wrists in his grip now.

"Sera. Look at me. Is it yours?"

Her mind rang like a struck bell. Rain, everywhere inside her, a nine-year-old's summer storm sloshing against the walls of her skull. And threaded through it, unmistakable now that she knew to look: *not mine, not mine, not mine.* A borrowed garment. She could feel its seams.

"Not mine," she whispered. Then, wonderingly: "But it's *there*. I know what rain is now. Before, I knew the word. Now I know—" Her breath hitched. "Tamsin, I know what it tastes like."

Something in his face broke and reassembled itself. Relief and grief in the same expression, fighting for the same ground.

"Good," he said hoarsely. "That's good. Now give it back. Slowly. Don't push it — offer it. Like returning a book."

She closed her eyes. *In, and then out.* She found the seams and loosened them, offered the storm up, and felt it hesitate — one long moment where the rain leaned toward staying, where some greedy root of her wanted desperately to keep the one beautiful thing she owned — and then she exhaled and it went, pouring off her like water off oiled cloth, back toward the vial in Tamsin's other hand.

When she opened her eyes, the world was gray again. Flat. Silent. She knew the word *rain* again, and only the word.

But her cheeks were still wet.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, that's what I'm missing. All of it. Everything. That's what everyone else has, all the time, just — *lying around inside them* — and they don't even—" A laugh came out of her with a knife in it. "They don't even *notice* it. People walk around Vell complaining about their memories. Bad memories. Sad ones. They pay you to take the sad ones away."

"Yes."

"That would be like burning furniture for warmth while your neighbor freezes in an empty house."

Tamsin flinched. She saw it land — saw the truth of it hit somewhere old and practiced-over. He began re-sealing the vial with careful hands, not meeting her eyes.

"I never claimed to be good," he said. "Only useful."

"You claim to be nothing at all. That's worse."

"Sera—"

"No. Let me finish." She was shaking, and not entirely from the cold. "I felt that memory for perhaps sixty heartbeats, and I would trade my hands to have it back. My *hands*, Tamsin. And you have a bag full of them. Thirty-some lives. Weddings and storms and mothers singing. You walk around carrying more felt life than anyone in this kingdom, and you sit here at night *grieving over the vials* like they're corpses, and I finally understand why." She leaned forward. "Because they are, aren't they? Every time you use one, you spend it. And every time you spend one, you spend a piece of yourself keeping it. I've watched you get thinner since Thornfell. Paler. Using the mountain-memories to lead us through the passes. How many is that now? How much of Tamsin Joubert has gone into getting us this far?"

Silence. The fire settled with a soft collapse of embers.

"Don't," he said at last.

"Somebody has to say it out loud. You won't."

"There's a reason for that." He looked up, and his eyes were furious and frightened in equal measure. "Because the moment we name it, it becomes real, and the moment it becomes real, you'll try to stop me, and I *can't* be stopped, Sera. There's no version of this where I arrive at Aldermere's border with my cache intact and my conscience clean. The passes cost memory. The wall costs memory. Keeping you alive costs memory. I did the arithmetic in the first week and I haven't shared the total with anyone, including myself." He gave a short, awful laugh. "You want to talk about theft? I am robbing myself nightly and calling it heroism."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it?" He set the vial down between them, gently, the way you'd set down something breakable. "Here's what nobody tells you about my gift. Every memory I hold takes up room. Not physical room — *self*-room. Thirty lives live in me alongside mine, and mine has started... sharing quarters. Sometimes I reach for a word and get Liska's father's word instead. Sometimes I wake up homesick for a farm I never visited. Last month I caught myself missing a wife I never had." His voice dropped. "I am becoming a crowd, Sera. And the crowd is eating the man."

She stared at him. And in the silence that followed, something happened that neither of them intended.

The cave went strange.

It was subtle at first — the firelight seeming to lean, the shadows bending the wrong way — and then the vial between them began to glow, softly, pulsing like a slow heartbeat, and Sera felt a pulling sensation behind her eyes, delicate and irresistible, like a hook set in silk.

Tamsin felt it too. She saw it hit him — saw his pupils blow wide.

"Sera, move *back*—"

Too late. The seam she had made — the little doorway she'd opened to let the rain out — had not fully closed. She felt it now, yawning, a hairline crack in an empty vessel, and the vial's glow poured toward it like light finding the one flaw in dark glass. But it wasn't taking the rain.

It was taking something else. Something older, buried deeper in Tamsin than any vial. Something *his*.

"No—" His hand shot out and knocked the vial away; it rolled, guttered, went dark. But the pulling continued a heartbeat longer, and in that heartbeat Sera fell *into* something—

* *

A kitchen. Low-beamed, copper pots, flour on every surface. A boy stood on a stool to reach the counter, maybe seven years old, dark-haired, tongue between his teeth in fierce concentration, cutting misshapen biscuits while a woman laughed at him from the doorway.

"Tamsin, love, those aren't biscuits, those are casualties."

"They're *stars*, Mum."

"They're stars that lost a war."

He grinned at her — gap-toothed, shameless, luminous — and Sera's heart, which she had thought was a dried-out thing incapable of movement, turned over in her chest like a boat coming loose from its mooring. Because she knew that grin. She had seen it on the road, aimed at customs men and fences and her, deployed like a tool — and here it was in its original form, aimed at a floury countertop, asking nothing, hiding nothing. Here was the boy the thief had been built around.

The woman crossed the kitchen and kissed the top of his head, and Sera felt it — felt the kiss land on her own scalp, ghost-warm — and understood with a lurch that she was seeing this through the boy's eyes, that this was *his* memory of *his* mother, and it was dissolving even as she held it, unraveling out of him and into her like dye into water—

*Tamsin, love—*

The name. She was taking his name's whole meaning, the sound of it in his mother's voice, the shape of being loved by her — she tried to push it back and it wouldn't push, it clung, it *fit*, her emptiness swallowing it whole the way the rain had—

* *

"—SERA."

She came back to the cave gasping, on her hands and knees, the stone real and hard beneath her palms. Tamsin crouched before her, white as bone, one hand gripping her shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"What did you take," he breathed. "What did you *take* from me?"

"Nothing — I didn't mean — it pulled *me*—"

"What. Did. You. Take." Each word laid down like a stone.

And she couldn't lie, not with his mother's laugh still warm in her ears, not with the taste of flour-dust and copper pots sitting in her mind where six days ago there had been nothing at all.

"A kitchen," she whispered. "A boy cutting star-shaped biscuits. A woman who called him *love*. Your mother, Tamsin. I have your mother now."

The color left his face entirely. He released her shoulder and sat back on his heels, slowly, like a man shot who hadn't yet fallen. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the fire ate itself down to coals, and somewhere in the dark of the cave a refugee coughed in sleep.

"That's not possible," he said finally, mechanically. "My own memories don't transfer. Only the stored ones. That's the one mercy of the gift — you can steal everyone else's life but your own stays nailed down—"

"It wasn't nailed down for me." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Maybe because I'm empty. Maybe emptiness pulls harder than fullness does. I don't know. But I saw him, Tamsin. I saw *you*. Seven years old, standing on a stool, insisting your ruined biscuits were stars." Her voice cracked despite everything. "You had a mother who joked with you. You had flour on your nose. Do you understand what that means? It means somewhere inside that crowd you carry, there's still a *boy*, and he's still whole, and he still—"

"Stop."

"—and now I know it, and you can't ever take it back, because I'll hold onto it the way I couldn't hold onto the rain—"

"*Stop.*" But his voice broke on the word, and what came through the break wasn't anger. It was terror, naked and total, and Sera realized with a chill exactly what he was terrified of. Not her. For her.

"Closeness," he said hoarsely, staring at her. "That's what this means. Closeness means consumption. You touch my stored memories and they leak into you. You touch *me*, truly touch me, and my life leaks into you. Do you see it now? Whatever we're doing on this road — whatever this is becoming — we cannot afford it. I need every piece of myself to open that Door. And you—" His jaw worked. "You finally have something in your head worth keeping. If you absorb me piecemeal, you'll end up full of a stranger's life and hollowed of the chance to recover your own. We would destroy each other politely. Slowly. With kindness."

Sera looked at him — at the thief with the crowd inside him, the boy with the star-biscuits, the man already spending himself coin by coin for forty strangers sleeping behind her — and she felt the new memory shift in her mind like a coal under ash, refusing to cool.

Then, beyond the cave mouth, the night changed.

The refugees' mules screamed. A woman cried out. And Sera felt it before she understood it — a wrongness rolling down the mountain passes like cold water finding low ground, a pressure against the inside of her skull that she recognized with sick certainty, because she had felt it once before, on her knees, in a room with iron instruments and a patient, hungry voice.

Tamsin was already on his feet, snatching up the satchel.

"That's him," he said. "Corvane. He's on the ridge — and Sera—" He turned to her, firelight carving his face into something ancient and young at once, and the words that came out of him next were not about the Warden at all:

"He's felt you too. An empty mind that suddenly holds *two* memories shines like a beacon in the dark. You've been invisible your whole life on this road — and tonight, you lit up like a signal fire."

Far above them, across the black shoulder of the mountain, a line of ghost-lanterns crested the ridge — and began to descend.
