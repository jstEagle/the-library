## 10. The Palace's Empty Rooms

The invitation came folded inside a loaf of bread.

Tamsin found it at the safehouse Odile had named—a shuttered dyer's loft above the tanneries, where the river stink rose thick enough to chew and the windows had been painted black with soot by some previous tenant who'd feared the sun. He'd been in Vell three days, moving only after dark, sleeping in the loft's rafters like something feral, and when the baker's boy left the loaf on the step with no word, Tamsin had nearly let it sit. Food from strangers was a knife with a handle offered first.

But the bread was still warm. And tucked into its crust, wrapped in waxed paper gone soft with steam, was a card of heavy cream stock, blank except for a single pressed seal: a moth with its wings spread over a keyhole.

He knew that seal. Every thief who'd ever worked the capital knew it. Lady Merren did not send invitations; she sent summons dressed as courtesies, and the difference mattered only to people who had never stood in her drawing room.

*Midnight. Come alone. Come hungry — I am told you appreciate being fed.*

No signature. None needed. The moth-and-keyhole said everything: *I know what you are, I know where you sleep, and I have chosen not to tell anyone yet.*

Sera read the card twice over his shoulder, her breath stirring the fine paper. "It's a trap."

"Everything is a trap," Tamsin said. "The question is whose."

"And you're going anyway."

He turned the card in his fingers. The cream stock caught what little light the soot-blackened window allowed, glowing faintly, like something remembered rather than seen. Somewhere across the city, in a palace built on forty years of quiet thefts, a woman who moved through court like smoke through a keyhole wanted to talk to him about Aldermere.

"Lady Merren was Odile's contact before she was anyone else's," he said. "Odile trusted her with things Odile never trusted me with. That's either a reason to go or a reason to run, and I've spent my whole life being unable to tell those apart." He fed the card to the stove and watched the moth curl and blacken. "We're going anyway."

"We?"

"You're the one Corvane scraped hollow. If anyone in this city knows how to put a person back together, it'll be someone who collects pieces for a living." He pulled his coat from its hook, checking the inner pockets out of habit—left side, the cache of stolen memories sewn into oilcloth; right side, the knife he hoped not to use. "Besides. You can't be recognized. There's nothing left of you to recognize."

He meant it as comfort. He saw immediately that it wasn't, and watched Sera absorb the blow without flinching, the way she absorbed everything now, her face a smooth white page on which nothing wrote itself. That blankness frightened him more than Corvane did. A person could survive being hunted. He wasn't sure what survived being emptied.

"Fine," she said. "We."

* * *

Lady Merren's house clung to the palace district like a barnacle to a ship's hull—modest from the street, all shuttered windows and unremarkable grey stone, but the interior opened upward and outward in ways the outside refused to admit existed. Tamsin counted four rooms that should not have fit inside the footprint. The fifth contained a garden under glass, night-blooming flowers opening their pale throats in the lamplight, and it was there that Lady Merren received them: an old woman in a chair beside a fountain no bigger than a washbasin, feeding bread crumbs to fish that glowed faintly, like coins at the bottom of a well.

She did not look up when they entered. "You brought the girl."

"You knew about Sera?"

"I know about everything that passes through Odile's ledger, and half of what doesn't." Merren finally raised her head. She was older than Tamsin had expected—or perhaps exactly as old as she looked, which in the capital was its own kind of statement. Court women bought youth the way merchants bought grain. Merren wore her years openly, silver hair cropped short, a face lined like a map of somewhere worth going. Her eyes were the exception: clear, quick, and terribly tired. "Sit down, thief. Both of you. What I'm about to say goes better with sitting."

They sat. The fountain murmured. One of the fish rose and took a crumb from her fingers, and its glow brightened briefly before dimming again.

"Odile tells me you've been asking about a memory," Merren said. "One she sold you six years ago. A location. A door."

"I'm asking why the crown wants it back badly enough to burn Thornfell."

"Are you?" Merren smiled, and it was not a kind smile—it was the smile of someone watching a child ask why the sky is blue when the real question is why they're breathing. "Or are you asking why the crown has always wanted it? Because those are different questions, boy, and only one of them has an answer you'll survive hearing."

"Then give me the one I won't."

Something flickered across her face—approval, maybe, or grief wearing approval's clothes. She brushed crumbs from her fingers and folded her hands in her lap.

"Queen Vessaline has reigned for fifty-one years," she said. "I was a girl when she was crowned. I remember the coronation because my mother wept through it—not from joy. From fear. She'd seen the queen at close range during the succession wars, and she said the woman had eyes like a person reading a book while pretending to look at you." Merren's voice stayed level, conversational, the tone of a woman discussing weather. "Vessaline is sixty-eight years old tonight. Look at me, thief. Then think about what a sixty-eight-year-old woman looks like."

Tamsin thought of the portraits in the tax offices, the ones every gifted child grew up studying so they could recognize the crown's officers on sight. The Queen on those canvases was perhaps thirty-five. Unlined. Dark-haired. The same face in portraits painted twenty years apart, hung in different cities, and he had never once thought to wonder—

"The memory tax," he said slowly. "The tithe. Every citizen surrenders one memory a year—"

"—and the crown claims it burns them in ritual, as tribute to the Thirteen, to keep the borders strong." Merren nodded. "That's the story. Here is the truth. There is no ritual. There is no burning. The memories go into the queen. All of them. Fifty-one years of them, from every man, woman, and child in the realm. She eats a country a year, Tamsin Joubert, and wears its years like a coat."

The fountain murmured. A fish glowed and dimmed.

"She doesn't age," Sera said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"She cannot age. She cannot die of anything so merciful as time. Every wrinkle she refuses is paid for with someone's wedding day, someone's mother's voice, someone's—" Merren stopped. For the first time, her composure cracked, just at the edges, like ice under weight. "My daughter gave her tithe at nineteen. A memory of my father teaching her to whistle. She doesn't miss it—you can't miss what you don't know you lost—but *I* remember it, and every year I pay my own tithe knowing that whatever I surrender next will end up lining that woman's skin like fat under a pelt."

"Why hasn't anyone—" Tamsin began.

"Because no one remembers enough to rebel." Merren laughed, short and bitter. "Think it through, thief. Forty years ago there were lords who whispered about the queen's face, lords who noticed their own children forgetting things they should have kept. Where are those lords? Their heirs don't inherit the suspicion, because the suspicion lived in memories the crown collected. You cannot build a rebellion out of people who have been selectively hollowed. She doesn't just eat memories. She curates them. She leaves everyone just enough self to be loyal, and takes everything that might have made them dangerous."

The glass garden suddenly felt very small. Tamsin became aware of the weight against his ribs—the oilcloth packet, the stolen lives—and understood, with a lurch, that he was sitting on more contraband than he had ever imagined carrying. Not stolen trinkets. Ammunition. Or kindling.

"And Aldermere," he said. "Where does the erased country come in?"

Merren was silent long enough that the fountain seemed loud. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped, though they were alone behind walls of glass and stone.

"Aldermere refused her. That's the whole of it, and the whole tragedy. Sixty years ago, when the memory tax was young, the Queen of Aldermere—a real queen, a mortal one, who aged and grayed and died on schedule like God intended—sent Vessaline a letter refusing the tithe. Publicly. In writing. She called the tax 'a theft of souls dressed as patriotism,' and she sealed her borders the same week." Merren's mouth twisted. "Vessaline couldn't march an army in. Aldermere sat behind mountains that didn't want to be found, and its people had practiced forgetting how to be invaded for a thousand years. So Vessaline did something worse than war."

"She erased them."

"From the maps. From the records. From *memory*. She used the tithe-taken memories of everyone who'd ever traded with Aldermere, everyone who'd ever been born there, everyone who'd ever so much as sung its songs—and she ate them all in a single winter. An entire country, swallowed. Do you understand what that means, boy? There are people alive today who were *born* in Aldermere, who walked out as children before the sealing, and they cannot remember their own homeland. They wake at night grieving something with no name. They hum melodies no one taught them. They are homesick for a place that has been eaten out of the world." Merren's eyes went to Sera, and stayed there, and softened into something terrible. "Like this girl. Like whoever scraped her clean—though I suspect Corvane didn't create her emptiness. I suspect he was trying to find out what was already missing, and cut too deep."

Sera's hands had gone still in her lap. Completely still, the stillness of someone listening for a sound beneath other sounds.

"But the Door," Tamsin said. "The Door of Aldermere. Why does the queen need it back if she already won?"

"Because she didn't win. That's the joke at the bottom of everything." Merren leaned forward, and the lamplight carved deep shadows under her cheekbones. "Aldermere's Door isn't a way *in*, thief. It's the only way the erasure can be undone. While the Door stays shut, the country stays swallowed—every memory Vessaline ate stays digested, part of her forever. But the Door was made by the old Aldermere queens as a failsafe. A single point of failure, hidden even from their own people. If the Door opens—if it opens *properly*, on the thirteenth turn of the moon, by its own law—everything the queen consumed comes back out of her. Every memory. Every year. Every stolen life of the last sixty years, returned to the people she took it from." Merren spread her thin hands. "Including the years keeping her young. Including the decades. Including, very possibly, her life. The Door doesn't just open a border, Tamsin. It opens her."

The fish glowed. Dimmed. Glowed.

"So the purge," Tamsin said. His mouth had gone dry. "The registration. Corvane hunting every gifted soul in the realm. It's not about controlling us."

"It's about finding *you*. Or finding the memory, if you've sold it or spent it. Six years ago, one of my people recovered a memory from a dying smuggler—an Aldermere native who'd escaped the sealing as a boy and carried the Door's location in his head until his heart gave out. My client bought it. My client placed it with Odile, unlabeled, to be sold to some anonymous thief who would carry it into the underworld and lose it among ten thousand other stolen things. Hiding a jewel in a heap of glass." Merren's gaze pinned him. "My client was not working for the queen, whatever you've concluded. My client was hiding the key where Vessaline could never search systematically. And then, six years later, Warden Corvane tastes the trail of an unregistered thief moving through Thornfell with an echo of Aldermere in his skull, and the whole delicate architecture comes apart."

"Who was your client?"

"That, I will not say. Some protections outlast even this conversation." Merren settled back into her chair. "What I will say is this: you are not carrying stolen goods anymore, Tamsin Joubert. You are carrying the last unswallowed piece of a murdered country, and the only weapon in existence that can kill a queen who cannot die. Corvane isn't hunting a thief. Vessaline isn't running a purge. This is a war, and it started sixty years ago, and you are the battlefield whether you consent to be or not."

Silence filled the glass garden. Outside, far off, a bell began to toll the midnight hour, and Tamsin counted the strokes without meaning to—one, two, three—and thought of thirteen moons, of a door waiting, of a map burning itself into his mind whether he willed it or not.

Sera spoke first. Her voice was strange, flat, careful—the voice of someone walking on ice over deep water.

"If the Door gives everyone back what the queen took," she said, "would it give me back what *I* lost?"

Merren looked at her for a long moment. Whatever passed across the old woman's face, she mastered it quickly.

"I don't know, child. Perhaps. The Door keeps what Aldermere keeps." She rose, joints cracking, and crossed to a cabinet of dark wood, from which she took a slim packet wrapped in oilskin and sealed with the moth-and-keyhole. She held it out to Tamsin. "Passage papers, letters of credit, and a name that will open doors in three cities. Use them or burn them. But hear my last counsel before you go."

He took the packet. It was heavier than paper should be.

"Corvane will reach Vell within two days—he moves faster than any man should, because he spends himself like a drunkard spends coin. When he comes, he will not offer you terms. He will take everything you are and leave the rest for the crows. So do not be here. Go north, go now, and follow the map in your head to its end." Merren's tired eyes held his. "And when you stand in front of that Door, thief, and it asks you for its price—because it *will* ask, it always asks—remember that some things, once given, do not come back out again. Not even through doors."

"What price?" Tamsin asked. "What does it cost to open?"

Merren smiled again, and this time there was nothing cold in it at all—only an old, worn sorrow, the kind that has been carried so long it has worn smooth.

"That," she said, "is the one question in this room I truly hope you never have to answer."

* * *

They left through the glass garden's rear gate, into an alley that smelled of rain coming and coal smoke settling. The bells had finished counting midnight. Somewhere across the rooftops, a second bell answered the first—then a third, distant and wrong, ringing a pattern no carillon in Vell used.

Thirteen strokes.

Tamsin stopped in the alley. Sera bumped against his back and went still beside him, her breath fogging in the cold.

"You heard it too," she whispered.

Above the city, the clouds had torn open, and the moon hung there—fat, silver, and fractionally wrong, as if someone had taken the familiar face of it and shifted it a degree off true. The thirteenth turn had begun. And high in the palace, in a window Tamsin had never noticed despite a lifetime of studying buildings, a light was burning that had no business burning at this hour: steady, patient, and looking, he was quite sure, north.

"They know we're here," Sera said.

"No." Tamsin pulled the oilskin packet inside his coat, against the cache of stolen lives, against the map that was already beginning to whisper behind his eyes. "Worse. She knows *where we're going*."

He turned north, and they walked, and behind them the wrong bell tolled on, counting down a country's last chance.
