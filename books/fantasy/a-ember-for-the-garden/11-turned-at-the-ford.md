## 11. Turned at the Ford

The refugee's name was Hollis, and Tamsin had pulled him out of a burning alder grove with his own two hands.

He thought about that now, standing in the shallows of the Verrin Ford with the water pulling cold silk around his calves and the moon three nights from its thirteenth turn. He thought about the way Hollis had wept into his shoulder on the mountain path, the way he'd pressed his cracked hands together as if in prayer, the way he'd said *I owe you my life, thief* — and how Tamsin had laughed and said debts were for merchants.

Somewhere behind him, in the low country where the smoke still rose from Thornfell's burning passes, Hollis was counting coins that smelled of Warden Corvane's gloves.

Tamsin couldn't prove it. That was the worst of it. He only knew what the mountains had taught him: that everything remembers, and everything can be read. The stones they'd slept against last night had hummed faintly under his palm with an echo that didn't belong to any of them — a man's voice, gravel-voiced and frightened, saying *the ford at Verrin, the one with the leaning willow, he'll be there before the moon turns.* And beneath the voice, threaded through it like wire through wax, a scent-memory of iron and old parchment that Tamsin knew the way you know a scar.

Corvane.

Sera crouched at the water's edge downstream, drinking from cupped hands. She'd taken to wearing her hood back since the chapel — she said the wind felt honest — and her hair caught the pre-dawn grey like ash catching light. She looked up at him with that directness that never failed to unbalance him, because it was the look of someone who had nothing to hide and therefore nothing between herself and the world.

"You've gone quiet," she said. "Quieter."

"The ford's wrong." He turned a stone over with his boot. Underneath, the riverbed glittered — not with fish-scales but with something finer, a dust of light, like ground glass mixed with starfall. "It's remembering too loudly. Do you feel it?"

She waded closer. When she knelt and laid her fingers on the water, her breath caught — it always did when the world showed her its echoes, because sensation was still new to her, still miraculous, still a little terrifying. "There are people in it," she whispered. "Hundreds. Crossing. Running. This ford has carried a thousand flights."

"And every one of them left something behind." Tamsin watched the light swirl. "Fear mostly. Fear sinks."

"Then we cross somewhere else."

"There is nowhere else. The gorge walls come down to the water for six miles east; west is Corvane's fire-line. This is the door out of Thornfell, and doors," he said, and heard the bitterness in his own voice, "are never free."

The refugees were coming down the trail behind them — eleven souls now, what remained of the thirty who'd fled the smoking passes with him. A dyer named Maren with her twin boys. An old fiddler whose name changed depending on who asked him. Two brothers from the charcoal camps who hadn't spoken since their village burned. They moved like people who had learned that noise invites death, which was the only lesson Thornfell had ever taught well.

Tamsin touched the satchel at his hip and felt them shift inside: the memories. Forty-one of them, wrapped in oiled silk, each one a life he'd lifted or been given or salvaged from the fires. A baker's first morning alone in his shop. A widow's forty years of marriage compressed to its warmest hour. A child's memory of snow, kept safe from a house that burned. He had spent five of them getting the refugees through the shifting passes — each use erasing some stranger's joy from the world forever — and the arithmetic of what remained made his chest tight. Five more crossings, maybe. Then the satchel would hold only silk.

He told himself he'd stop counting the cost eventually. He was starting to believe himself, which frightened him more than Corvane did.

"We cross at first light," he said. "Before the mist burns off. Single file, hands on shoulders, no one speaks. The water's waist-deep at the middle and it wants to take things, so if you're carrying anything you love—"

He stopped.

Because Sera had gone rigid, her head tilted, her whole body listening to something he couldn't hear.

"What?" he said.

"Horses." Her eyes were wide and dark. "No — hooves. Many. On the ridge road." She pointed upstream, toward the black shoulder of Tor Wessan where the trail from the low country descended. "They stopped. They're waiting for something."

For dawn. For confirmation. For a signal.

Tamsin's mouth went dry. He looked downstream at the willow — the leaning willow, the landmark in the stone's remembered voice — and understood, all at once and completely, that he was standing in the exact center of a trap that had been built around his own kindness.

*Hollis.*

"Maren." His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Get everyone up. Now. We cross immediately, quietly, no lights."

The dyer's face went white in the pre-dawn. She'd survived enough purges to know what *now* meant, and she moved without questions, waking the others with touches instead of words.

Tamsin drew Sera aside, behind the willow's trunk, and lowered his voice until it was barely breath. "Someone sold us. I don't know who, and it doesn't matter. Corvane's above us on the ridge, and there'll be more below — he doesn't leave one door open when he can close two. So here's what happens. I'm going to make noise upstream, draw them to me, and you take them across."

"No."

"Sera—"

"I said no." Her jaw set, and he saw something in her face he'd only seen once before, in the ruined chapel when she'd touched the erased names: a fury so old and cold it had gone past anger into bedrock. "You keep spending yourself like coin, Tamsin. Your memories, your safety, your sleep, your blood. You think because I have nothing in my head, I can't see what you're doing? You're trying to become empty too. It's just slower for you."

The words landed harder than any blow Corvane had ever managed. Because she was right, and they both knew it.

"If I go across with them," she said, quieter now, "and you die upriver buying us minutes, then I carry that the way I carry everything else — as a hole. Don't do that to me. Don't give me another absence." Her hand found his wrist, gripped hard. "We fight for the ford together, or we lose it together. Those are your choices."

Above them, on the ridge, a horn sounded. One long note, thin as a blade, rolling down the valley and waking every echo the mountains owned.

* * *

They came down through the mist like a rumor given armor.

Tamsin counted twenty riders in the first wave, and behind them, on the ridge road, lanterns — dozens, a slow descending constellation. Wardens' grey. And at their center, riding a horse too fine for the terrain, a figure whose presence Tamsin felt before he saw it: a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of copper at the back of the throat, the unmistakable scent of a mind stuffed full of other people's lives.

Corvane had come personally. That was either flattery or doom, and Tamsin suspected both.

"Get them moving!" he shouted, and broke cover along the bank, running upstream, away from the ford, making himself loud and visible and *bait*. Behind him he heard Sera's voice driving the refugees into the water, Maren's twins crying out as the cold took them, the fiddler swearing in three dialects.

The first riders reached the bank thirty yards ahead of him. Tamsin did the thing he was best at in all the world, better than theft, better than survival: he improvised.

He pulled a memory from his satchel without looking — silk tearing, a life spilling into his hands — and drove it outward the way Odile had taught him, the way he'd never dared use on a person before this year. It was a harvest memory, golden and vast, a farmer's memory of the day his fields came ripe, all that swelling abundance—

The lead rider's horse screamed and reared. The rider slid off, landing hard, and lay in the shingle clutching his helmet, sobbing — not from pain but from a grief for a harvest he'd never known, a loss so enormous it had knocked the fight out of him like a hammer. Weaponized memory. The Aldermere defenders' art, learned from a burned book in a ruined chapel.

*So this is what I am now,* Tamsin thought grimly, running. *A thief of joys, thrown like stones.*

He reached the upstream bend where the willows thickened, and turned to fight properly — and that was when the second trap closed.

They'd come down the scree on foot. Six of them, silent, grey-cloaked, flanking — and among them a boy, fourteen maybe, wrists bound, eyes hollowed to pits. A captured gifted child, spent like a tool, forced to track Tamsin's trail by tasting the echoes he left. Corvane's hound.

And behind them all, unhurried, came the Warden himself.

Up close, Corvane was worse than Tamsin remembered. Not because of the scars or the grey uniform or the long hooked instrument at his belt — the *siphon*, the crown-forged thing that let him eat minds without gift or grace. It was the eyes. They were the eyes of a house whose rooms had been emptied one by one and refilled with strangers' furniture, so that nothing in it matched anymore, so that the house itself had forgotten what it was built to be.

"Tamsin Joubert." Corvane's voice was soft, almost kind. "You've cost me three weeks and four good men. Do you know what I pay for men, these days? Memories. Their own, returned to them, like wages. And you've made wages scarce."

"Send the boy home," Tamsin said. "He's half-empty already. I can see it from here."

"He's useful half-empty. Whole, he'd have opinions." Corvane dismounted, boots crunching on shingle, and the hooked instrument came off his belt with a sound like a knife leaving bread. "Give me the Aldermere memory, thief, and I'll let the refugees drown quickly instead of slowly. That's the only mercy on today's menu."

"You don't even know why you want it." Tamsin backed toward the water, satchel clutched to his chest. "Do you? She's eaten so much of you, Warden, I'd wager you can't remember the original order. Just the hunger."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — there and gone, a crack in plaster — and Tamsin knew he'd struck bone. But the Warden only raised the siphon, and its tip began to sing, a high thin note that made Tamsin's teeth ache and the nearest echoes rise out of the stones like startled birds.

Then the river attacked.

It came without warning — a wall of silver light surging up from the ford, a hundred years of crossing-memories loosed all at once, and Tamsin realized with a lurch that Sera had done it. Had plunged her empty hands into the deepest pool and *pulled*, dragging the drowned fears and flights up into the air, because she was the only person alive who could touch that much lostness without drowning in it. The mist filled with phantom crowds, ghost-lanterns, the screams of flights a century dead, and the wardens' horses went mad.

In the chaos, Corvane lunged — not for Tamsin, but for the satchel.

The strap tore.

Tamsin felt it happen the way you feel a tooth leave your skull: a wrongness, a sudden lightness, a horror. The satchel spun from his grip, burst against a stone, and forty-one silken bundles scattered across the shallows — and the river, greedy old river, keeper of a thousand flights, *took them*.

He saw a baker's first morning unfurl in the current like a ribbon of gold and dissolve. He saw forty years of a widow's love shred into the foam. He saw the child's snow — the snow, the small warm mitten, the laugh — sink whirling into the deep pool and go out like a candle.

"No—" He was in the water without deciding to be, plunging, grabbing, and the river grabbed back. It was waist-deep and lying about it; the current at the middle had the strength of everything that had ever been lost there. A bundle slipped through his fingers. Another. Another. He dove, lungs burning, and the world above broke into shattered lantern-light, and somewhere very far away Sera was screaming his name.

Hands seized his collar. For one mad instant he thought Corvane had him — but the grip was small and fierce and human, and then Sera's face was in front of his under the water, hair streaming, eyes wide open as if the river were air to her. She got her arm around his chest and *pulled*, and Tamsin, who had dragged a dozen people out of a dozen fires, learned what it was to be the one dragged.

They broke the surface downstream, beyond the willow, in the eddy below the ford. The battle had dissolved into chaos — horses bolting, wardens scattered and weeping in the ghost-light, Corvane standing hip-deep at the crossing with the empty satchel-silk drifting past him like jellyfish, staring at the water with an expression Tamsin would remember for the rest of his life, however long that proved to be.

Not triumph. Not rage.

*Hunger frustrated.* The look of a man watching forty dinners sink.

Sera hauled Tamsin onto the shingle. He coughed river out of his lungs and crawled, absurdly, desperately, along the bank, snatching at the few bundles that had snagged on roots and reeds. Three. Four. Five. Out of forty-one. He held them against his soaked chest like a man holding ashes.

"They're gone," he said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Maren's passage — I spent it. The fiddler's wedding — gone. The snow. All of it, the whole cache, it's in the river, it's—" He heard his voice crack and hated it. "Those weren't mine. That's the whole point, they were never mine, I was keeping them *safe*, I was a *keeper*—"

"You were a hoarder," Sera said gently, kneeling beside him, wringing river from her hair. "You were a man carrying forty lives because you couldn't bear to set any of them down. And now the river has them, and the river keeps everything, and maybe—" she hesitated, then pushed on, because she always pushed on, "maybe somewhere downstream, a hundred years from now, some frightened person crossing this ford will feel a baker's morning warm their feet. Nothing loved is ever really lost, Tamsin. It just changes keepers."

He stared at her. Water ran off his chin. "How do you say things like that? You don't even have your own past to console yourself with."

"That's exactly why." She took his face in her cold wet hands and made him look at her. "I know what it is to be emptied. I know the shape of the hole. And I know it doesn't swallow you unless you climb into it willingly. So don't. Climb *out*. Five memories left. Five lives in your keeping. Guard those, and guard the ones breathing around you, and stop mourning silk."

Behind them, the ghost-light was fading. On the far bank, Corvane had remounted, and the horn sounded again — but longer now, and answered from downstream. Reinforcements. The ford was closing.

But upstream, where the willows bent low over the water, the mountains had begun to murmur. Tamsin felt it through the wet stone under his palms: the old map-memory in his head stirring, burning brighter, insistent as a lit fuse. And threading through the river's endless echo, clear as a bell rung once, came a word in a voice older than the kingdom:

*Aldermere.*

Sera heard it too. He saw it hit her — saw her go still, saw her hand drift to her sternum as though something long asleep had turned over inside her.

"The thirteenth turn," she whispered. "It's starting, isn't it. The mountains are waking up."

Tamsin gathered the five surviving bundles, tied the torn satchel with a strip of silk, and looked north, where the gorge narrowed into country no map admitted to. The mist there wasn't ordinary mist. It moved against the wind.

"Yes," he said. "And we're going to be at the border before it finishes waking — or we're going to die in a valley that doesn't exist. Come on. There's a pass above the falls that only opens in moonlight, and we have three nights of moon left to find it."

He pulled her up, and together they ran north along the river, while behind them the ford filled with grey cloaks, and ahead of them the mountains began, softly, to say their forgotten name.
