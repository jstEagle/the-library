## 12. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The moon did not wane that night.

Tamsin noticed it first as a wrongness in the light on the snow—a silver too steady, too patient, like the eye of something watching from very far away. He stood at the mouth of the cave where they'd made camp above the Ashen Stair, and he watched the thirteenth moon hang fat and unblinking over Thornfell's teeth, and he understood, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, that the counting had begun.

"Twenty-nine nights," Sera said behind him. She had come to stand at his shoulder without a sound; she moved like that now, more surely than she had in the beginning, as though her body remembered how to be quiet even when her mind held nothing. "The keeper said twenty-nine."

"The keeper said a great many things." He didn't mean it bitterly. It came out bitter anyway. Everything did, lately, because everything was running out—time, road, and worst of all, the memories in his head, which had begun to *move*.

That was the thing he could not say aloud yet, not even to her. For eleven years he had carried other people's lives the way a miser carries coins: counted, sorted, sealed in the waxed vaults of his gift, each one still and separate until he chose to spend it. A merchant's wedding. A fisherman's last morning with his son. The taste of a queen's coronation cake, bought off a scullery maid for three coppers and a lie. They sat in him like stones in a riverbed.

They were not sitting anymore.

* * *

It started, he thought afterward, with the lanterns.

They came down out of the high passes two nights after the moon refused its dark—lights where no lights should be, drifting along ridgelines that no living soul had walked since before Tamsin's grandfather was born. Not torchlight. Lantern-light, round and green-gold, the color of pond water over old glass, moving in procession. And beneath them, faint as breath on a winter pane, voices.

Sera heard them first. She always heard things first now; emptiness, it seemed, made a fine listening-horn.

"Tamsin." Her hand found his sleeve in the dark. "There are people singing."

"There are no people up here. That's the whole point of up here."

"No." She turned her face toward the ridge, and moonlight caught the strange hunger in her expression—not fear, not quite. Recognition reaching for itself across a gap. "I know the song. I don't know how I know the song, but I know it. It's about a garden. It's about—" Her brow furrowed, that small crease he had learned to read better than any ledger. "It's about coming home before the frost."

He listened then, properly, opening himself the way he opened himself to lift a memory—and what came back off the night air was not sound exactly but *echo*, memory lodged in stone the way he'd first discovered in these passes years ago, except this was nothing he had ever stolen. This was older. This was a country singing to itself through the only throat it had left.

The mountains were remembering Aldermere aloud.

And the roads—the roads had begun to move.

He proved it the next morning, though proof was hardly necessary. The smuggler trails of Thornfell were stitched into his bones; he had run them hungry and hunted and half-frozen, and he knew their turns the way he knew the backs of his own hands. But the pass below their camp, which should have bent east toward the frozen ford, bent instead south-southwest, curving with a gentle, insistent logic toward a horizon that had no business existing—toward a stretch of sky that seemed, when he looked at it directly, to slide away from his eyes like water off oiled leather.

"It's herding us," he said.

"Is that so terrible?" Sera crouched at the trailhead, touching the frost-stiffened gorse as if it might speak. "If the road knows where it's going, maybe we should let it lead."

"The road is a dead country's ghost, and ghosts want things, and what they want is rarely good for the living." He said it harsher than he intended, because the truth underneath it frightened him: the road wasn't herding *them*. It was herding *him*. Every fork they'd passed since the moon stilled had offered the branch that pointed toward Aldermere as if it were the only branch, and every time, some part of him had leaned toward it like a plant toward sun.

The map-memory. Odile's poisoned gift, sold to him years ago by a fence who'd never told him whose hand had packed it or why. It had sat quiet in his skull for six years, a folded paper in a drawer. Now the drawer was open, and the paper was unfolding itself, and it would not stop.

* * *

By the fourth night he could no longer pretend otherwise. He woke from a dreamless sleep—which was itself wrong; he always dreamed, the stored lives seeping into each other—and found himself standing at the cave mouth with snow to his knees and his eyes open and his mind *burning*.

Not pain, exactly. Pain would have been kinder. This was illumination, the slow merciless kind, like ink dropped in water spreading to fill every corner of a cup. The map-memory was writing itself into him. Into his own flesh, his own self, overwriting whatever it touched the way floodwater overwrites a field. He could feel it laying down lines where his own memories used to be: the smell of his mother's bread was gone, and in its place, a mountain pass he'd never walked, rendered in perfect detail, down to the lichen on the boundary stone. His father's laugh—gone, replaced by the exact number of steps between the Door of Aldermere and the keeper's terrace. Eleven thousand, four hundred and six. He hadn't asked to know that. He knew it anyway.

"Sera," he said, and his voice came out sanded thin. "Sera, wake up. I need you to tell me who I am."

She was awake instantly—she slept like a cat, in snatches, always half-listening for Wardens—and she came to him through the dark and took his face in both her cold hands, and she looked at him with those eyes that held nothing and somehow held everything.

"You're Tamsin Joubert," she said. "You're a thief, and you're a coward about three specific things, and you're braver than any man I've met about everything else. You steal memories and you hate yourself for it and you do it anyway because the alternative is starving, and you give the good ones away when you think no one's watching. You hum when you're nervous. You're humming now."

He wasn't. Or he hadn't been. But his throat was making a low note, and when he listened to it, he realized it was the song from the ridge. The one about coming home before the frost.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's not good."

"Tell me what's happening."

So he told her—all of it, the whole ugly architecture of his gift laid bare, the vaults and the seals, and how the seals were failing, one by one, eaten through by the thing Odile had sold him. How the map-memory had stopped being cargo and become a passenger, and the passenger was taking the wheel. "It's not just showing me the way anymore," he finished. "It's making room. It's tearing out pieces of me to build itself a house. By the time we reach the border, I don't know how much of Tamsin Joubert will be left to walk through any door."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Snow ticked softly against the stones. When she spoke, her voice had an edge he'd never heard from her, sharp and bright as broken ice.

"Then we'll get there faster."

"That's your answer? I'm being erased from the inside and your answer is to hurry?"

"My answer," she said, "is that I know exactly what it is to have nothing inside you. I lived it. I *am* it. And I would rather walk beside a man spending himself to carry us home than watch him hoard what's left and arrive too late." Her grip tightened on his face. "You've spent other people's memories for years to keep yourself alive. Now yours are being spent to keep everyone else alive. Maybe that's not erasure, Tamsin. Maybe that's just the bill coming due."

He wanted to argue. He wanted, badly, to tell her she was wrong, that there was a third way, a clever thief's way, some lock left unpicked somewhere in all of this. But the map-memory pulsed behind his eyes like a second heart, patient and vast, and somewhere far to the south a Warden with a hollow where his mother's face should be was following the same burning thread through the dark, and there was no third way. There was only the road, and the road only went one direction now.

"We move at first light," he said.

"We move now," Sera said. "Look at the sky."

He looked. The thirteenth moon had shifted—not set, not risen, simply *shifted*, sliding a full handspan along its impossible arc, and beneath it the ghost-lanterns on the ridgeline had multiplied from dozens to hundreds, a river of green-gold lights flowing south along every height, converging, all of them, on the place where the world forgot a country.

The mountains were not remembering quietly anymore.

They were calling everyone home.

* * *

They walked through what remained of the night, and the walking was strange beyond anything Thornfell had shown him. The trail smoothed underfoot where it should have been jagged; scree fields lay flat and firm as flagstones. Twice they crossed bridges of natural stone that Tamsin knew for a fact had not existed a week ago, arching gorges that appeared on no smuggler's chart, their spans carved—when he dared to look closely—with the same repeating motif: a door, a garden, a moon with thirteen faces.

And the echoes in the stone grew louder. He could not stop hearing them now; his gift stood open whether he willed it or not, drinking the mountains' memory like a cracked cask drinks rain. Fragments washed through him as they walked:

*A woman planting rowan trees along a border road, saying, "Let them bloom red when strangers come, so we'll know our friends by the ones who aren't afraid."*

*A bell foundry, thirteen bells cast from melted crown-silver, and a smith weeping over the last mold.*

*A child's voice, reciting: "First the wall forgets, then the water, then the name. Hold the garden. Hold the garden. Hold—"*

Sera walked with her face turned upward the whole way, tears freezing on her cheeks, and when he asked her once if she was all right she laughed—a wet, wondering sound.

"I'm crying and I don't know why," she said, "and it's the best I've felt since Corvane emptied me. These aren't my memories, Tamsin. But they're almost mine. They fit the shape of me, like—" she pressed a hand flat against the rockface as they passed, "—like a coat I wore as a child and grew out of. Somewhere in there is a girl who sang that song. I can feel where she was. I just can't feel *her*."

"The Door might give her back," he said. "The keeper said—"

"I know what the keeper said." Something flickered across her face then, quickly buried—the argument they'd already had twice, the price of doors, the whole life freely given. He saw her jaw set and knew she was still planning to offer herself, and knew, with a sick certainty, that he was still planning to beat her to it. Neither of them said it. Some wars you fight silently, over weeks, with love as the only weapon either side will use.

Dawn found them at the top of a rise, and Tamsin stopped so abruptly that Sera walked into his back.

Below them, the land ended.

Not dramatically—not a cliff, not a chasm. It ended the way a sentence ends when someone tears the page. There was forest, and then there was a line, and past the line there was a soft gray nothing that his eyes kept trying to slide off of, the way a tongue worries a missing tooth. The wall of forgetting. The border of the erased country. And rising out of that gray, faint as a watermark, the suggestion of terraces—gardens stacked upon gardens, climbing toward a peak crowned with a structure that might have been a tower or might have been a tree or might have been a door seen edge-on.

The map-memory in his skull flared white-hot with recognition, and Tamsin gasped and went to one knee in the snow, and when he raised his head he was weeping without knowing it, and the song was in his mouth, all of it, every verse, in a language he had never learned.

*Tamsin,* Sera was saying, kneeling beside him, gripping his arm. *Tamsin, stay with me. Stay.*

"I'm here," he managed. "I'm—" He laughed, ragged. "I'm less here than I was yesterday. But I'm here."

Behind them, far down the valley they had climbed, a light answered the ghost-lanterns. Not green-gold. White, hard, and cold as a surgeon's lamp, sweeping the slopes in long deliberate strokes.

Corvane. A day behind them, perhaps less. Hunting the same burning thread.

And below them, the gray wall waited, and beyond the wall the garden bloomed in its suspended twilight, and somewhere in Tamsin's dissolving mind, eleven thousand four hundred and six steps counted themselves down toward a door that could only be opened by a man giving away everything he was.

Twenty-five nights, the still moon said.

The lanterns on the ridges bowed southward, all together, like a crowd before a king.

"Come on," Tamsin said, and took Sera's hand, and walked down toward the end of the map.
