## 13. Corvane's Last Name

The pass narrowed where the mountain had split itself long ago, two walls of black stone leaning together like conspirators, and Tamsin knew before he saw him that Corvane was waiting in the throat of it.

He felt the Warden the way a man feels a draft from an open grave. The air in the defile had gone strange—too still, too sweet, thick with the scent of other people's summers. Memories hung there like fruit gone soft on the branch. Corvane's leavings. The man shed what he ate the way a snake sheds skin, and every mile of Thornfell behind them was littered with the husks of lives he'd swallowed and half-digested: a fisherman's dawn, a bride's terror, a child's first snowfall, all of it bleeding faintly into the rock.

Sera stopped beside him, her hand finding his sleeve without looking. She couldn't smell memory the way he could, but she'd learned to read his face, and his face must have told her everything.

"He's here," she said. Not a question.

"In the narrows." Tamsin crouched, setting down the bundle that held what remained of his cache—the few memories he'd salvaged from the ford, wrapped in oilcloth like a butcher's cuts. "There's no way around. The eastern wall sheared off in the last quake. It's this or three days back through the burn country, and Vessaline's riders are sweeping the burn country now."

"Then we go through."

"We go through." He almost smiled. "You've gotten good at saying things like that."

"I've had practice." Her mouth twitched, but her eyes stayed on the dark seam between the walls ahead. "Tamsin. If he takes you—"

"He won't."

"If he takes you, I want you to know I'd find you. Even scraped clean again. Even empty. I found your face once with nothing in my head at all. I'd do it twice."

He looked at her then—really looked—at the girl without yesterday who had become the only fixed point in a world that kept dissolving under his feet. He wanted to say something worthy of that. What came out was: "Don't let me forget to be afraid of him. Fear's kept me alive this long."

"You won't forget," Sera said. "I'll remember it for you."

They went into the narrows together.

* * *

Warden Corvane sat on a fallen slab of granite as though it were a throne he'd grown tired of, his long hands folded in his lap, his gray cloak pooled around him like spilled smoke. Up close—and Tamsin had never been this close, not in all the years of circling each other across the kingdom—the Warden looked *wrong*. Not old, exactly. Not young either. His face was a composite, features assembled from a hundred borrowed lives: someone else's strong jaw, someone else's pale scarred brow, eyes that had been brown once and were now a color that belonged to no one, because they'd been replaced so many times the original hue had worn away entirely.

He was eating when they found him. A memory, plucked loose and turning over his tongue like a candied plum. Tamsin could see its shape in the air between them—a market day, warm bread, a woman laughing—and watched it gutter and die as Corvane finished it, swallowing nothing, tasting everything.

"Warden," Tamsin said.

"Thief." Corvane did not rise. "You've cost me eleven years of my own life chasing you through these mountains. Do you know how I know that? Because I counted. Counting is one of the last things that's still mine."

Sera moved half a step in front of Tamsin, and something flickered across the Warden's ruined face—not anger. Recognition. The look of a man seeing a debt walk upright.

"The Aldermere girl," Corvane said softly. "I scraped you once. In the chapel at Vell, when you were six. Do you remember?"

"No," Sera said.

"No," Corvane agreed, and there was something terrible in how gently he said it. "That's rather the point."

Tamsin stepped forward, putting himself between them, and raised his empty hands. "You want me. Let her pass."

"Oh, I want more than you." Corvane rose at last, unfolding to his full height, and the shadows in the narrows seemed to lean away from him. "I want the map in your head. I want the Door. And then I want to sleep, thief, for about a century, which is the one thing I will never have." He began to walk toward them, unhurried, the way famine walks toward a harvest. "Do you know what my work is? Truly? Everyone thinks I hunt the gifted. That's the crown's word for it. But hunting is just the shape hunger takes. I am a man who eats lives because his own ran out decades ago, and every life I take fills the hole for a day, maybe two, and then the hole grows back *hungrier*, and I go out again, and somewhere along the way I stopped being able to say why any of it mattered."

His voice didn't rise. That was the horror of it. It stayed level, conversational, a man discussing weather while the world burned.

"I used to have reasons," he continued. "A wife, I think. There's a ring-shaped mark on my finger that no memory of mine explains. A mother—I know I had one, because everyone does, and I can feel the shape of the fact the way you feel the shape of furniture in a dark room. But the face—" For the first time, his voice caught. Just slightly. A hairline crack in a wall of stone. "I've eaten so many mothers' faces. Birthday cakes and lullabies and scoldings and deathbeds, hundreds of them, and mine is *gone*, and I cannot even mourn it properly because mourning requires remembering what you lost, and I—" He stopped. Closed those no-color eyes. When he opened them again, the crack was sealed, and the famine looked back out. "Kneel, thief. This will hurt less if you kneel."

"No," Tamsin said.

Corvane sighed. "Then we do it the hard way, and I take the girl's blankness too, because even emptiness has a taste if you're hungry enough, and God forgive me, I am always—"

"Wait." Tamsin's heart was slamming against his ribs, but his voice came out steady, and he marveled distantly at the thief's oldest trick: sounding calm while dying inside. "Before you eat us. Answer one question. Honestly. You owe yourself that much."

Corvane paused.

"What was her name?" Tamsin asked. "Your mother's."

The silence that followed lasted longer than any silence Tamsin had ever stood inside. The wind died in the narrows. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

"I don't know," Corvane said at last, and the words came out of him like a tooth being pulled. "I don't know, and I have torn apart a thousand minds looking for it, and it isn't anywhere, because I ate it myself, years ago, some night when I was hungry and not paying attention, and I swallowed my own mother without ever knowing I was doing it." His hands, those elegant, terrible hands, had begun to shake. "So don't preach to me about mercy, thief. Don't stand there with your stolen hoard and your clean conscience—"

"It's not clean," Tamsin said. "Nothing I've done is clean. But I'm going to offer you a trade anyway, and you're going to hate how much you want it."

He knelt, slowly, keeping his movements deliberate, and unwrapped the oilcloth. Inside lay the last of his cache: five memories, five small fires cupped against the dark. A fisherman's dawn. A wedding in Vell. An old man's garden. A soldier's homecoming. And one more—he'd been saving it, hoarding it the way misers hoard gold, telling himself it might buy their lives at the Door.

A family. A whole family, whole and warm: a kitchen with steam on the windows, a father's laugh from another room, a mother humming while she worked, flour on her hands, flour on her cheek, a boy tugging her apron and her looking down at him with a love so vast and ordinary it made Tamsin ache every time he touched it. He'd lifted it from a dying baker in Vell years ago, meaning to sell it, and had never managed to. Some memories refuse to be merchandise.

"This," Tamsin said, holding it up so the Warden could see its glow, faint and golden in the gloom. "A mother. A real one, whole, with her face and her name and the way she said her son's name when he came in muddy from the street. It's not yours. It was never yours. But neither was anything else you've ever eaten, and at least this one is given."

Corvane stared at it. And Tamsin watched, with a clarity that would haunt him for the rest of his days, the exact moment when thirty years of iron discipline broke—the moment the famine in the man's eyes turned inward and became, briefly, unbearably, simply grief.

"That's theft," Corvane whispered.

"Yes."

"That's *theft*."

"Yes," Tamsin said. "And passage through this pass, and a day's head start, and you forgetting you ever saw our faces. All of it, for one memory. Freely traded. The way it should have been done from the beginning—for both of us."

For a long moment the Warden did not move. Then he crossed the distance between them in three strides, took the memory from Tamsin's palm with a gentleness that made Sera flinch—it was the gentleness of a starving man handling bread—and pressed it to his forehead with both hands, the way the faithful press relics.

Tamsin felt the transfer leave him, felt the hollow where the baker's family had lived, and looked away, because some transactions should be private.

Corvane made a sound. Small. Broken. The sound of a man finding a door in a wall he'd believed solid his whole life.

"*Maren,*" he said. "Her name was Maren. Flour on her—she has flour on her cheek, and she says *there's my boy*, and I—" His voice failed entirely. He stood in the narrows of Thornfell with tears cutting tracks through a face assembled from strangers, holding a dead baker's mother against his skull like a man holding warmth to a wound, and for one moment he was not the crown's memory-eater, not the famine walking, not the thing Sera's nightmares were made of. He was just a son, remembering.

Then it passed. Grief always passes; that's its cruelty and its mercy. Corvane lowered his hands. Straightened. And when he looked at Tamsin again, his eyes were wet and his voice was ash.

"She wasn't mine," he said.

"No."

"But she'll do." He stepped aside, gesturing up the pass with one trembling hand. "Go. The eastern route is watched until dusk; after that it's clear to the borderlands. I'll report that I lost you in the burn country." He paused, and something old and tired moved behind his eyes. "Thief. When you open that Door—if you open it—remember that there are others like me. Hollowed men wearing borrowed faces, serving a queen because the alternative is standing still long enough to feel what's missing. We're not monsters. We're what's left when someone eats the person who used to be there."

"I know," Tamsin said quietly. "I've been looking at one in the mirror for years."

Corvane almost smiled. It was a terrible, wonderful thing, that almost-smile. Then he turned and walked back down the narrows, and the shadows closed over his gray cloak like water, and the sweet rot-smell of spent memories faded from the air until only the cold clean stone-scent of Thornfell remained.

Sera exhaled beside Tamsin, a long shuddering breath she'd apparently been holding since the pass began.

"You gave him your last good memory," she said.

"I gave him *a* memory. The last good one was yours." He meant it as a joke. It landed as truth, and they both heard it land, and neither of them laughed.

"The map-memory," Sera said. "It's still burning itself into you, isn't it? I can see it when you sleep. Your eyes move like you're reading."

"Every night it writes a little more of itself into me. Roads I've never walked. A border that isn't on any map. And underneath it, getting louder as the moon waxes—" He touched his temple. "Bells. Thirteen of them. Counting down."

Sera looked up at the sliver of moon visible between the canyon walls, thin as a paring of silver, one night from fullness's final turn.

"Then we shouldn't waste the head start a grieving man gave us," she said.

They climbed. Behind them, far down the pass, so faint it might have been wind, they thought they heard a man saying a name over and over to himself—*Maren, Maren, Maren*—like a prayer, like an anchor, like a man memorizing the one true thing left in a life made entirely of thefts.

By dawn they had cleared the high shoulder of the mountain, and the land beyond opened before them: a plain of gray grass that bent all in one direction, though there was no wind, bending toward a horizon where the air shimmered like heat above a forge.

Neither of them said it aloud. Neither of them had to.

The thirteenth turn had begun, and the world was already leaning toward Aldermere.
