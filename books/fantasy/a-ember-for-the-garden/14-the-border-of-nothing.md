## 14. The Border of Nothing

They walked out of the mountains on a morning that refused to be one.

The sky was the color of old pewter, neither dawn nor storm, and the light that came through it had no direction—falling evenly, faintly, like light remembered rather than seen. Tamsin felt it on his face and could not say where it came from, and that small wrongness set his teeth on edge more than any sound in Thornfell ever had.

Behind them, three days back, Corvane's fires still smoked over the passes. Ahead, according to the map that burned itself into Tamsin's mind whether he willed it or not, lay the border of Aldermere.

Except there was no border. That was the first thing he understood.

The road simply ended.

Not at a cliff, not at a river, not at a wall of stone or soldiers. The road ended the way a sentence ends when the speaker forgets what he meant to say. One moment they were walking a track of packed earth between two hillsides heavy with late gorse; the next, Tamsin's eyes slid off the world ahead of him like rain off oiled leather. There was *something* there—he could feel its presence the way you feel someone standing just behind your shoulder—but every time he turned to look directly, his gaze skated away and found itself studying the gorse instead, the hillsides, the pewter sky, anything but the place where a country used to be.

"Stop looking," Sera said quietly.

"I'm not—"

"You are. Everyone does, at first." She stood beside him with her hands loose at her sides, her face turned toward the nothing as calmly as if she were watching a sunset. "You have to stop trying to see it. It isn't for seeing."

"How do you know?"

She didn't answer right away. When she did, her voice had that hollow quality it took on whenever something surfaced from the scraped emptiness inside her—a shell rolling up from some deep water. "Because I've been here before. I don't remember being here. But my feet remember."

Tamsin looked at her—at this girl who carried nothing, who had been emptied so thoroughly by Corvane's hunger that even her own name had come back to her only in fragments—and felt the familiar ache that had stopped surprising him somewhere on the road. She was the most damaged person he'd ever met, and she was also the only whole thing in sight.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

They made camp that night within sight of the ending, though "within sight" was a generous lie. Tamsin built a fire small enough to hide beneath their bodies' warmth and sat staring at the flames while Sera slept—or seemed to sleep—curled against a stone that gave back no heat.

He tried, again, to look at the border.

It was worse in the dark. In daylight the wrongness had been a sliding, a deflection; now it was an appetite. He caught himself thinking about it the way he thought about a locked strongbox: *what's inside, what's inside, what's inside.* And each time the wanting rose, something reached back out of that blankness and touched the edges of his mind, gentle as a pickpocket, patient as rot. Not taking. Just... testing the lock.

He flinched back and found Sera awake, watching him across the coals.

"It does that," she said. "To people who carry things."

"Carries things." He laughed, and it came out bitter. "Sera, I'm carrying forty-some lives in my head. Half of Vell's merchant class has better memories than I do. I've got a wedding I stole from a man who wept when it ended, a funeral I lifted from a widow who paid me to take it, a childhood I bought off a dying smuggler because he wanted it to go to someone who'd use it—" He stopped. His hands were shaking. He pressed them flat against his knees. "I'm not carrying things. I'm carrying everyone."

"I know," she said softly. "That's why you can't cross."

The words landed like stones dropped down a well. He waited for them to hit bottom.

"The wall doesn't hate us," Sera went on, turning her palms upward toward the dead light. "It isn't cruel. It's just... exact. Aldermere erased itself so completely that even the memory of it became dangerous. Anyone who carries too much gets—reflected. All those lives you hold, they press against the forgetting, and the forgetting presses back, and..." She trailed off, searching for words. "Odile told me once, before she died, that the border wasn't a wall. It was a mirror. You cross by holding one true memory of the country. One. Clean. Unstolen."

"One," Tamsin repeated.

"One."

"And how many do I have?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. They both knew the arithmetic: he had everything except that.

* * *

Morning came gray and directionless again, and with it came the others.

There were eleven of them—the refugees Tamsin had led out of Thornfell, the ones whose names he'd spent stolen memories to keep alive through the burning passes. A stonemason named Breck with burn scars laddering his forearms. An old woman called Maren who'd been a schoolteacher until teaching history became treason. Two brothers, fourteen and nine, whose parents hadn't made it out of Vell. A midwife. A cooper. A girl of nineteen who'd been born gifted and had never told anyone, not even her mother.

They clustered around the fire now, looking at the place where the road ended, and Tamsin watched their eyes slide away, one after another, like water finding its level.

"It's there," the younger brother said, in the particular voice children use when adults are pretending not to be frightened. "I can hear it humming."

"You can't hear a country hum," his older brother said.

"I can hear *this* one."

Maren the schoolteacher knelt beside the boy and put her hand on his head, and for a moment her face was terrible with grief—not for herself, Tamsin realized, but for the child, for the fact that a boy should grow up knowing the sound of a nation's erasure.

"We can't all cross," Breck said flatly. He was a practical man, a mason, a builder of walls, and he looked at the border the way he'd look at bad mortar. "Can we?"

"No," Tamsin said.

Silence.

"Some of you will feel it pull," Sera said, rising. "Some of you won't. The wall chooses. It always chose—it's how Aldermere kept itself safe, all those years. Only those who truly belong may pass." She paused. "And those who carry one true memory."

"And how do we get one of those?" the midwife asked bitterly. "Buy one? Steal one?" Her eyes flicked to Tamsin, and there was no malice in the look, only exhaustion. "No offense meant, thief."

"None taken." He stood. "And no. You can't steal a true memory of Aldermere. Believe me, I've tried." He had, in fact, spent half the night trying—reaching into his own hoard, sifting through forty lifetimes of other people's yesterdays, hunting for even a scrap of the lost country. He'd found weddings and funerals, first kisses and last breaths, the taste of a hundred different breads baked in a hundred different kitchens. Not one crumb of Aldermere. The country had been too thoroughly forgotten; even the memories that had once held it had dissolved, leaving only the shape of their absence, like a footprint in snow after the snow has melted.

"So we walk up to it," Breck said, "and hope it likes us."

"Essentially," Tamsin said.

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan. Welcome to my life."

* * *

They approached the border at noon, walking in a line, and Tamsin learned what it meant to be refused.

Breck went first, jaw set, shoulders squared like a man facing a battering ram. He got within ten paces of the nothing and then simply—stopped. Turned around. Walked back with a puzzled frown, as if he'd forgotten why he'd been walking that way at all. When Tamsin asked him what happened, Breck said, "What border?" and genuinely meant it.

The midwife lasted longer. She got close enough that Tamsin saw her lips moving, shaping a word he couldn't hear, and then she shuddered all over and came back weeping without knowing why.

The brothers went together, hand in hand. The older one made it eight paces. The younger one made it twelve before the forgetting took him gently by the collar and turned him around, and he came back saying, "But I almost heard it, I almost heard the humming, it sounded like—like—" and then his face went smooth and confused, and he couldn't finish.

One by one, all eleven of them were refused. Some forgot entirely. Some remembered being refused. None of them crossed.

Then it was Sera's turn.

She walked toward the nothing the way other people walk into churches: barefoot, having removed her boots without being asked, her face lifted. And the wall—for the first time since they'd arrived—did not slide away from her. It *leaned*. Tamsin watched, heart hammering, as the blankness ahead of her seemed to gather itself, to attend, to recognize.

She stopped at the threshold, one step from crossing, and turned back.

"It knows me," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Tamsin, it *knows me.*"

"Then go."

"Not without you." She looked past him at the eleven refugees huddled behind, at Maren and Breck and the brothers, at all the people who had followed a thief out of a burning country on the promise of a door. "Not without any of you. That's not how this works. That's not how *he* designed it."

"He?"

"The keeper," Sera said. "The one Odile mentioned. The one who waits inside." She looked down at her own empty hands. "Tamsin, I think I know why I'm empty. I think it wasn't just Corvane. I think I was born here, and I was sent out, and someone scraped me clean on purpose so I could carry the one thing that gets through."

"What thing?"

"A name," Sera whispered. "Aldermere's true name. The one that was never written down anywhere, never spoken aloud, never stolen—because it lived in only one place." She pressed her fist against her sternum. "In the hollow where my memories used to be."

The wind moved through the gorse, and for a moment—just a moment—Tamsin heard it too. A hum. Faint, vast, patient. The sound of a country holding its breath behind a wall of forgetting, waiting for the thirteenth moon to rise.

"But here's the problem," Sera said, and now her voice was shaking. "I can carry the name through. I can carry *us* through—one true memory each, held tight, unspoken, given freely to the wall. But I can't give you yours. None of us can. The wall takes only what's freely surrendered." She met his eyes, and hers were bright with tears she refused to let fall. "And you don't have one, Tamsin. You have everyone else's. You have forty lifetimes that aren't yours. But you don't have a single memory of Aldermere, because nobody does. Nobody alive remembers it."

The hum swelled, soft and immense.

"Except," Tamsin said slowly, "the map."

Sera frowned. "What?"

"The memory Odile sold me. The one that started all of this." He pressed his fingers to his temple, feeling the burn of it, the map that had been searing itself into his mind for days now, every road and river and hidden gate of the lost country inscribing itself deeper whether he willed it or not. "It's not a map of roads, Sera. I finally understand that. It's a map of *memories*. Every landmark on it is a memory someone in Aldermere treasured—the well where a queen drew water, the tree where lovers carved their vows, the garden where the keeper was born. It's not geography. It's *remembrance*, laid out like streets."

"Then you do have—"

"I have a map of other people's memories of Aldermere. Stolen. Secondhand. The wall wants one true memory, freely held." He laughed, and it came out ragged. "Everything I have is stolen, Sera. That's the whole tragedy of me. I've spent my life taking what other people treasured, and now, at the end, the one door that matters opens only for what's *given*."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Then she stepped back from the threshold, away from the humming, and took his hands in hers. Her grip was cold. Her eyes were steady.

"Then we find another way," she said. "Or we make one."

"There is no other way. The keeper said—"

"The keeper hasn't met you yet."

* * *

They camped that night at the edge of the ending, and Tamsin did not sleep.

He sat apart from the others, at the very boundary where the world stopped pretending, and he held the map-memory in his mind like a coal in cupped hands. It burned. It had been burning for weeks now, inscribing itself into him, and he understood at last that it was not merely information—it was an inheritance. Somewhere, someone had poured every cherished memory of a doomed country into a single vessel and sent it out into the world, hoping it would find its way home.

And it had found him. A thief. A taker. A man who had never given anything freely in his life.

He thought about the wedding he'd stolen from the merchant in Vell, in the early days, back when theft had still felt easy. He thought about the widow who had *paid* him to take her grief. He thought about Sera, asleep twenty paces behind him, empty as a winter field, carrying a nation's name in the hollow where her childhood should have been.

*Freely given,* the wall demanded. *One true memory.*

He looked down at his hands—thief's hands, quick and clever and stained with forty lifetimes of other people's yesterdays—and for the first time since he'd discovered his gift as a boy, Tamsin Joubert wondered what it would mean to give instead of take.

The hum swelled once, softly, like a country clearing its throat.

And far behind them, down the dark throat of the passes they'd fled, Tamsin saw lights. Many lights. Moving fast.

Corvane had found them.

The thirteenth moon was rising, and the Warden was coming, and the wall of forgetting stood silent before them, waiting to learn what a thief was willing to surrender.
