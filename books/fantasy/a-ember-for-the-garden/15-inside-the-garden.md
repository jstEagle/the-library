## 15. Inside the Garden

The wall of forgetting did not break so much as it opened—like an eye deciding, at last, to look at them.

One moment Tamsin stood in the gray nothing where Aldermere should have been, Sera's hand cold in his, her voice still humming the country's name against the dissolving air. The next, the fog thinned to gauze, the gauze to light, and light poured through him like warm water through a cracked cup.

He had braced for a wasteland. For ruins, for ash, for the punished silence of a place erased. What he saw instead made his knees forget their work.

Terraces. Hundreds of them, cut into the shoulders of a valley that no map had carried for a hundred years, each one planted and green and impossibly tended. Apple trees heavy with fruit that should have fallen decades ago. Lavender running down the slopes in purple rivers. Water moving through stone channels with a sound like slow applause, feeding gardens that climbed the hillsides in perfect, patient rows until they met a sky the color of late honey.

And everywhere, people.

They moved along the terraced paths without hurry, without purpose, without age. A woman knelt among bean rows, her hands in the soil, her face smooth as a girl's and old as a grandmother's all at once. Two men played a game with carved pieces on a table beneath a pear tree, and neither of them seemed to have moved in some time—or perhaps they simply never needed to. Children—no, not children, Tamsin realized; something older wearing childhood's shape—chased each other through the orchard grass, laughing, and the laughter hung in the air longer than laughter should, sweetening it rather than fading from it.

"It's evening," Sera whispered beside him. "It's always evening here."

She was right. The light came from everywhere and nowhere, low and golden, the light of a sun that had been setting for a century and could not quite bring itself to finish. Shadows lay long and soft across the terraces. Somewhere far below, bells rang once—a single deep note—and did not ring again, as if even the bells had agreed to rest.

Tamsin's skin prickled. He was a thief; he knew stolen things when he stood among them. This whole country was a held breath. A memory someone had refused to let go of, kept alive past all reason, past all cost.

"Look," Sera said.

At the center of the valley, where the terraces converged, rose a structure he had seen only in fragments—in the burning map-memory behind his eyes, in Sera's scraped and scattered dreams. It was smaller than he'd expected, which somehow made it worse. A door standing alone in a courtyard of white stone, framed by an arch of living wood, its surface dark bronze gone green at the edges. No wall around it. No building. Just a door, upright and patient, waiting in the middle of a garden as doors do not wait in the middle of gardens.

The Door of Aldermere.

The memory in his head—the one that had started everything, the merchant's wedding memory with its whisper of *Aldermere* sewn into the seams—turned over slowly, like a sleeper recognizing home. It pulsed against the inside of his skull. *Here,* it said. *Here, here, here.*

"You're bleeding," Sera said sharply.

He touched his nose. His fingers came away red. Of course they were. The map-memory had been burning itself into him since the thirteenth moon began, and now that he stood inside the thing it described, it burned like a coal pressed to paper.

"I'm fine." He wiped his hand on his coat, which had stopped looking respectable somewhere around the river ford and now looked like something a scarecrow would refuse. "Let's find whoever's keeping this place before I fall over."

They descended the first terrace together. The soil smelled of rain and turned earth and something sweeter underneath, something that tugged at Sera especially—he felt her grip tighten, felt her breath go shallow. She had been born here. Her body remembered this ground even if her mind had been emptied of it, and every step down the terraced path seemed to return some small piece of her: the way she instinctively stepped left around a particular olive tree, the way she reached up without thinking to touch a low-hanging branch, the way her lips shaped a word she didn't know she knew.

*"Saela,"* she murmured. "This path is called Saela. Why do I know that?"

"Because you're home," Tamsin said. "Even the parts of you they took."

The citizens watched them pass. That was the other thing—the watching. Not hostile, not curious exactly. They turned their faces toward the two strangers with an expression Tamsin found harder and harder to look at directly: recognition without surprise. As if they had known, all these years, that someone would come. As if the waiting itself had been the work.

An old man paused in his pruning to study them. His shears were silver and worn thin. "Thirteenth turn," he said—not a question, just a fact, spoken the way a farmer speaks of weather.

"The thirteenth turn," Tamsin agreed.

The old man nodded once and went back to his pruning, and Tamsin understood that the conversation was complete, that here a hundred years of waiting could be summed up in four words and a nod, because what else was there to say? The door would open or it wouldn't. Everything else was gardening.

* * *

The keeper lived at the top of the valley, in a house built of the same white stone as the Door's courtyard, half-swallowed by wisteria so old its trunk was thicker than Tamsin's waist. She was waiting on the porch, and she was the oldest human being Tamsin had ever seen.

Not frail—old. Ancient the way mountains are ancient, the way rivers are ancient. Her hair was white as the courtyard stones and fell to her waist. Her hands, folded in her lap, were maps of veins and knuckles. Her eyes were the color of the honeyed sky, and when they settled on Tamsin, he felt them go through him the way Corvane's senses had once hunted him—but gently, gently, the way light goes through a window.

"So," she said. Her voice was dry leaves and well-oiled hinges. "The thief."

Tamsin's hand drifted toward his knife out of pure habit. Sera caught his wrist.

"You know me?"

"I know what you carry." The keeper unfolded herself from her chair with the slowness of a tree growing, and stood, and looked down at him. Up close she smelled of lavender and candle smoke and something else, something underneath, like the air in a room long shut. "A hundred years I have kept this garden. A hundred years I have watched the road at the border of nothing, waiting for footsteps. Do you know how many came? In a century?"

"How many?"

"One girl, born here, dragged out as an infant and scraped hollow by a crown that feared even the memory of us." The keeper's gaze moved to Sera, and softened—actually softened, like frost giving way. "Hello, child of the terraces. You came back after all."

Sera made a small sound. Tamsin looked at her and saw tears running freely down her face, and her hand pressed flat against her own chest as though holding something in—or letting something out.

"I don't remember you," Sera said. "But my feet know your steps. When you walked toward us, I wanted to run to you. I've wanted—" She laughed, wet and broken. "I've wanted things all this journey and never known why."

"You will remember," the keeper said. "When the Door opens, all that was taken will be returned to those who stand within the garden. That is its grace. That is why we kept it." She turned back to Tamsin, and the softness withdrew like a tide, leaving the old stone of her showing. "Which brings us to you, thief. You have brought the key-memory. I can feel it burning in you from here. You are carrying the location of the Door inside your skull like a coal in a paper bag, and it is eating you alive. Sit down before you fall down."

There was a bench. Tamsin sat. His legs were grateful and his pride was not.

"Tea?" the keeper asked, already pouring from a pot that had been steaming on the porch rail, though Tamsin would have sworn no fire burned anywhere nearby. "Chamomile from the third terrace. It has been brewing since before your grandmother was born. Everything here has been going on since before your grandmother was born. That is rather the point."

He took the cup because refusing seemed unwise. The tea tasted like summer evenings he'd never had.

"Now," the keeper said, sitting opposite him, folding her ancient hands. "You have crossed the wall of forgetting. You have survived the Warden"—at Tamsin's flinch, she nodded as if he'd confirmed a suspicion—"and you have carried the key through a kingdom that wants it back. You believe you have done the hard part. You believe the Door will open now, because you have arrived, because the moon turns, because the story demands it."

"Doesn't it?"

"No." She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "The Door does not open for arrival. It does not open for the thirteenth moon alone—that merely wakes it, makes it *able*. It has been able for a hundred years, child, and it has stayed shut, because no one has paid."

"Paid what?" But even as he asked, Tamsin felt the answer rising in him, felt the map-memory shudder against his mind like something sensing the butcher's shop. "The queen's people said there was a price. Odile didn't know it. Lady Merren didn't know it. Nobody knows it."

"I know it." The keeper leaned forward. "I have known it for a hundred years, and in a hundred years I have told no one, because telling it might frighten away the one person who could bear it. Listen carefully, thief, because I will say this once, and then you will hate me, and then you will understand anyway.

"The Door of Aldermere was built by people who refused to be consumed. Queen Vessaline's grandfather wanted our memories—wanted them taxed, harvested, poured into the royal line to stretch its years. Our queen refused. So the crown erased us—our name from maps, our history from books, our borders from the minds of cartographers. And our own mages, in answer, built a door that could take us out of the world entirely. But a door that opens onto elsewhere must be *anchored*, and the only anchor strong enough to hold a doorway between worlds is a whole human life. Freely given. Every memory, every name, every love and theft and grief—one entire self, surrendered willingly, poured into the hinge."

The tea had gone cold in Tamsin's hands. Or perhaps his hands had gone cold around the tea.

"A life," he said.

"A whole one," the keeper said. "A fragment won't do—we tried, in the early years. Fragments burn off like kindling. The Door drank three partial offerings in the first decade and stayed shut, and those poor souls were left less than they were, hollow-eyed, wandering the terraces." She gestured vaguely down the valley, and Tamsin thought of the citizens moving without hurry, without purpose, and felt sick. "No. It must be everything. One person, giving all of themselves, so that everyone else may pass through. That is the price of the Door. That has always been the price."

The golden light lay thick and warm across the porch. Somewhere below, the single bell rang again—once, patient, eternal. Tamsin became aware that Sera was staring at him, and that her face held a terrible dawning comprehension, because she knew him, knew what he carried, knew better than anyone alive how many lives he had stored in his head—

"You have hundreds," she said quietly. "Stolen memories. Whole ones. Years of other people's lives."

"Fragments," Tamsin said. "Pieces. No one steals a whole life, it doesn't—it can't be done, you'd have to take everything, every day since birth—"

"And yours?" the keeper asked, mild as milk.

The question landed in the middle of the porch like a dropped glass.

"My what?"

"Your life, Tamsin Joubert. Your memories. Your name. The boy who learned to pick pockets in Vell's fish market, the young man who made his rules—never from the desperate, never more than needed, never let them see—who slept alone in safehouses and told himself loneliness was the price of the gift and maybe even liked it, who fled across Thornfell, who gave a hollowed girl back her first taste of rain." The keeper's honey-colored eyes did not blink. "That life. Freely given. It would open the Door. It is, in fact, the only kind of thing that can."

"I'm not doing that," Tamsin said.

"I haven't asked you to."

"You're asking exactly that, you're just doing it politely."

"I am telling you the price," the keeper said. "What you do with the knowing is yours. That is the whole meaning of a freely given thing—if I ask, it is worthless. If I beg, it is worthless. If Vessaline tortures it out of you, the Door will drink nothing but ash. It opens for surrender or it does not open at all." She rose, tree-slow, and gathered the teacups. "You have until the moon completes its turn. Three nights. Then the Door sleeps for another century, and everyone in this garden—including the girl beside you, whose past sits waiting on the far side of that bronze like laundry on a line—waits another hundred years in the twilight."

She paused at the doorway of her vine-strangled house.

"For what it is worth, thief—I am sorry. I have watched a hundred years of evenings for the person who would hear what you have just heard. I hoped, selfishly, that it would never be anyone. And I knew, in my bones, that it would be someone like you. Someone who has spent his whole life taking, and never once been asked to give."

She went inside. The door closed. The wisteria shifted in a wind Tamsin couldn't feel.

* * *

They walked down through the terraces as the false evening deepened into a false night full of too-many stars—stars in constellations Tamsin didn't recognize, stars that belonged to whatever sky waited beyond the Door. Lanterns kindled themselves along the paths, ghost-pale, and the citizens lit candles in their windows, and the whole valley glowed like a memory of a festival.

Sera hadn't spoken since the porch. She walked beside him with her arms wrapped around herself, and when they reached the courtyard of white stone, she finally stopped and turned to face him, and the lantern-light made her look very young and very old at once.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't decide anything yet. Don't do that thing you do, where you make it simple so you can carry it." Her voice shook. "I heard her. A whole life. Yours. And I watched your face while she said it, and Tamsin—you looked *relieved*."

"That's not—"

"You did. Like someone handing back a weight they've forgotten they're allowed to put down." She grabbed his coat front in both fists. "Listen to me. I spent my whole journey wanting to be you—to have *anything*, to have a yesterday, a name, a bad childhood, a scar with a story. And you're standing there thinking your life is the fair price for mine, and I will not—do you hear me—I will not be bought with that. Not by you. Not by anyone."

"Sera—"

"There has to be another way."

"There isn't." He said it gently, and hated himself for how gentle. "Three partial offerings already failed. Fragments burn. It has to be whole, and free, and mine is the only whole life standing in this garden that isn't already anchored here." He looked past her, at the Door—dark bronze, patient, patient, patient. "Besides. What would I even be giving up? A name nobody will remember in a kingdom that's erased a whole country. A talent I've used mostly to survive. A hundred borrowed lives that were never mine to keep." He smiled, and it was the smile he used at markets, easy and practiced, and he could feel it fooling neither of them. "It's a fair trade."

"It's a *surrender*," Sera said, "and you're dressing it up as arithmetic because arithmetic doesn't hurt."

Below them, across the sleeping terraces, the single bell rang.

And then it rang again.

Tamsin went still. Sera went stiller. In a hundred years, the keeper had said, the bell had spoken once per evening—one note, the sound of a held breath. Now it rang a second time, and a third, faster, urgent, and down in the valley the citizens were coming out of their houses, lifting their faces toward the southern rim of the bowl of hills, where the sky had begun to glow.

Not gold. Not the honeyed twilight of the garden.

White. Cold. The white of torches on a mountain pass, of moonlight on drawn steel, of a wall of forgetting being forced.

"She followed us through," Sera breathed. "The wall opened for us and it—"

"Opened for her too." Tamsin's hand found his knife, useless, useless, and closed around it anyway. Far off, at the edge of the starlight, he could see them coming down the passes in a long glittering line, and at its head a figure whose hunger he could feel from here, a hunger he knew like his own heartbeat.

Warden Corvane had found the garden.

And behind him, riding under a banner of thirteen moons, came the Queen.
