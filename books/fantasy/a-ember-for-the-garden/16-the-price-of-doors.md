## 16. The Price of Doors

The keeper's words did not fall so much as settle, like ash after a fire no one had seen burn.

"One whole life," the ancient woman said again, because she could see that Tamsin had not yet understood—or perhaps because she wished to delay his understanding, the way one delays opening a letter known to hold grief. "Freely given. Freely surrendered. The Door was built by people who knew what it costs to be forgotten against your will. They would not open for theft. They open only for gift."

They stood in the antechamber before the Door itself—a hall of white stone veined with green, as if marble had learned to grow like moss. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, the color of late afternoon in a season that never ended. And at the far end, taller than any cathedral door Tamsin had ever seen in Vell, stood the Door of Aldermere: a vast leaf-shaped panel of living wood, its grain shifting slowly, patiently, like rings growing around a heart no one would ever cut into.

It did not look locked. That was the cruelest part. It looked simply *closed*, the way a flower closes at dusk, waiting for something it recognized.

Sera spoke first. "Mine, then."

Tamsin turned so fast the room seemed to tilt.

"No." The word came out of him flat and hard, a stone dropped down a well. "No."

"You haven't even heard me—"

"There's nothing to hear." He was already moving toward her, and the keeper watched them both with eyes gone pale as frost on glass, ancient and unreadable. "You don't have a life to give, Sera. That's the whole—Corvane scraped you hollow. There's nothing left. You'd walk up to that Door and offer it an empty hand."

"Then I have nothing to lose." She said it simply, without drama, and that simplicity was worse than shouting. She lifted her chin the way she did when she was frightened and refusing to show it—the way she'd done at the ford, at the wall of forgetting, every time the world had asked her to be brave with pieces of herself she couldn't even name. "Everyone else has to give up everything they are. Their whole lives. Every memory, every year, every name. I've already lost mine. I'm the only person in this country who can pay the price and walk away *whole*."

"You wouldn't walk away whole." His voice cracked on it. "You'd walk away *gone*. You think emptiness is the same as nothing? It isn't. Emptiness is a wound. It hurts. I've carried enough stolen memories to know what a mind feels like when something's been torn out of it—and yours has been torn out and torn out and torn out until there's almost nothing left, and you want to give the last of it to a *door*?"

"To save everyone else."

"That's not a reason. That's a wound wearing a reason's clothes."

For a moment she just looked at him, and the twilight light of Aldermere moved across her face like water over river stones. Then, quietly: "And what would you call it, if you offered yourself? Because I can see you thinking it, Tamsin. I can see it behind your eyes the way I once saw rain behind a window. You're already composing the argument. 'I'm a thief, my life is stolen goods, I'm the natural payment.' You've been building that speech since the keeper opened her mouth."

He had no answer. That was the terrible truth of it—he had been building exactly that speech, word by word, since the moment he'd understood what the Door demanded. It had assembled itself in him with the smooth, practiced ease of all his best lies, and the fact that it wasn't entirely a lie made it more dangerous, not less.

* * *

They argued through the long unchanging afternoon of the garden country, walking the terraces while the suspended citizens of Aldermere drifted past them like figures in a music box, gentle and slow and waiting.

"It should be me," Tamsin said, for the fourth time or the fourteenth; he had lost count. "Everything I am is borrowed anyway. Stolen years, other people's weddings, other people's childhoods. Half my soul isn't even mine. What does the Door want with a man who's mostly secondhand?"

"Because it doesn't want secondhand." Sera stopped at the edge of a terrace where night-blooming flowers hung heavy and silver, flowers that had been about to open for a hundred years and never quite managed it. "That's what the keeper said. It opens for a gift, freely given. Not a bargain. Not a sacrifice dressed up as penance. You'd be giving your life the way you steal memories—to make the transaction clean, to keep control, to make sure nobody else gets hurt. That's not surrender, Tamsin. That's just theft pointed in the other direction."

"And what would you be doing?" He rounded on her. "You'd be giving yourself away because you think you're worth less than everyone else. Because Corvane emptied you and you've decided that makes you cheap. Don't tell me I'm lying to myself if you won't admit you're doing the same thing with prettier words."

The accusation landed. He saw it land—saw her flinch as though he'd touched a bruise she'd forgotten she had, which in a sense he had, because all her wounds were ones she couldn't remember receiving.

"I know what I am," she said at last, very quietly.

"No, you don't. That's the point. Nobody knows what they are—not really, not all the way down. We just get to *remember* being someone, and that remembering is most of what a self is. And they took that from you." He stepped closer. The silver flowers trembled between them, still refusing to bloom. "You are not empty, Sera. Empty things don't grieve. Empty things don't stand in front of a girl in the Thornfell passes and put their body between her and a warden's knife. Empty things don't sit up half the night telling stories to refugees' children about a country they've never seen, making it real for them, making it *worth saving*. You've been building a self out of nothing but kindness for months. It's the only honest architecture there is. And you want to hand it to a door."

Her breath caught. In the suspended light of Aldermere, he could see the exact moment his words reached whatever lived beneath her blankness—that deep place where fragments surfaced like fish rising in dark water. A garden. A door. His voice.

"You'd miss me," she said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't teasing. It was careful, the way she handled anything fragile.

"Yes."

"Tamsin—"

"You'd miss you too, if you could remember yourself long enough to know what missing felt like." He laughed, and it came out broken. "Gods, listen to me. Listen to what this place has done to us. We're standing in paradise arguing over who gets to be erased."

* * *

Night came to the garden the way it must have come for a century: gently, reluctantly, the twilight deepening to a blue so rich it seemed poured rather than fallen. They sat on the steps of the antechamber, below the great closed Door, sharing silence the way travelers share bread.

It was Sera who broke it.

"When I touched your stored memories," she said, "back in the passes. Before the ford. Do you remember what happened?"

"I remember you seeing my mother's kitchen. The boy I was. I remember being terrified."

"I wasn't." She turned to look at him, and in the blue light her eyes were the color of deep water over unknown ground. "I wasn't terrified at all. It was the first time since Corvane that I felt like a *person*. Someone's memory inside me—warm, alive, real. For those few minutes I wasn't a hole shaped like a girl. I was a vessel that held something worth holding." Her voice thinned. "So don't tell me I don't understand what I'd be giving up. I understand better than anyone alive. I know exactly how much a single memory is worth, because I know exactly what it's like to have none."

"Then you know why I can't let you do this."

"Do you?" She smiled, and it was the saddest thing he had ever seen her do, and he had seen her weep at the ruined chapel, and laugh in the rain-memory, and scream his name across a burning mountain pass. "Because here's what I know, Tamsin Joubert. If you give yourself to that Door, I lose the only person who ever treated my emptiness like it was something precious instead of something broken. I lose the man whose voice is the first thing I ever remembered. I lose—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I love you. I don't know when it happened. Maybe it happened before Corvane, in some life I can't reach. Maybe it happened on the smuggler roads, in the snow, when you gave that child your coat and pretended you weren't cold. But it's the truest thing left in me, and I will not watch you feed it to a door."

The words hung in the blue air. Somewhere in the terraces above, the night-blooming flowers finally, after a hundred years, began to open—one by one by one, silver petals unfurling like small moons, as if the garden itself had been waiting to hear something confessed.

Tamsin found he could not speak. All his cleverness—all the thief's fluency that had talked him out of gallows and past wardens and through walls of forgetting—had deserted him entirely. There was only the raw thing underneath, the boy in his mother's kitchen who had learned early that loving things meant losing them, and who had spent his whole life stealing other people's moments because his own were too dangerous to keep.

"I love you too," he said finally, and it came out rough, unfinished, true. "Since the chapel. Since before the chapel, if I'm honest. Since you looked at me in that pass with nothing in your head but recognition and chose to trust me anyway. You want to talk about gifts? That was a gift. Trusting a stranger with the last of yourself. And now you want to spend the rest."

"Someone has to open the Door."

"Then it's me."

"Then it's me."

They stared at each other, and despite everything—despite the army surely marching behind them, despite the moon climbing toward its thirteenth turn, despite the Door waiting silent as held breath—despite all of it, Tamsin laughed. A real laugh, helpless and aching.

"We're impossible," he said.

"We're *matched*," she corrected. "There's a difference."

Above them, the great wooden Door shivered. Its grain shifted—slowly, the way continents shift—and for the first time since they had entered the antechamber, Tamsin felt it *notice* them. Not the way a lock notices a key. The way a judge notices two litigants who both claim the same inheritance.

Light gathered along the Door's seams. Thin at first, gold-green, like sap catching sun. The keeper appeared at the edge of the terrace, drawn by some change in the air, and her frost-pale eyes went wide.

"It hears you," she whispered. "Both of you. Two claimants, one price." She pressed a hand to her breastbone, and Tamsin realized with a chill that the old woman was afraid—afraid for the first time in what might have been centuries. "In all the long twilights, the Door has never been *argued with*. It may choose. Or it may refuse you both. Or—"

She stopped. Her head turned, sharp as a bird's, toward the western sky.

Beyond the terraces, beyond the wall of forgetting, beyond the borders of a country erased from every map, a red glow was rising against the stars. Not dawn. Dawn was hours away, and dawn did not come with drums.

Torches. Thousands of them, winding down through the mountains like a burning river.

"She's come," the keeper breathed. "Vessaline has come, and the moon is nearly full, and the Door is listening to two hearts that cannot agree." She looked at Tamsin, then at Sera, and her ancient face was grave as winter. "Children, you must decide *now*. Before she reaches the garden. Because if the queen arrives while the Door stands undecided—"

The light along the seams pulsed once, patient and terrible, like a heartbeat.

"—she will not need a key at all."
