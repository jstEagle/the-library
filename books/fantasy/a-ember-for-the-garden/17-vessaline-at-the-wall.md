## 17. Vessaline at the Wall

The light came first.

It rose over the eastern ridge like a second dawn, thin and silver and wrong, and Sera felt it against her skin before she saw it—the way one feels frost coming, or the hush before a storm breaks. She stood on the terrace above the Door, where the keeper had left her that morning with a cup of something warm and bitter, and she watched the thirteenth moon's borrowed light pour down the mountainside and pool in the valley below.

"They've come," she said, though no one was beside her.

The garden answered instead. The terraces of Aldermere had begun to stir in the last days, waking from their long twilight the way an old man wakes when he hears his own name spoken in another room. Flowers opened that had not opened in a century. The air smelled of wet stone and green things and, beneath it, faintly, of smoke.

Tamsin found her there. He had been sleeping badly—she could tell by the way he held his shoulders, as though his own body were a coat that no longer fit—and when he reached the terrace rail he did not look at her but at the light.

"That's not an army," he said quietly. "That's a funeral procession."

"It's a coronation," Sera said. "She's come to crown herself the last queen of everything."

Below, the valley filled.

They came in ranks that did not march so much as *arrive*, as though the queen had simply thought them into being at the foot of the mountain. Banners of white and gold, unmarked by any heraldry Sera knew—which was nothing, she reminded herself, she knew nothing, her whole past was a scraped bowl—and at their center a palanquin of black glass carried on the shoulders of twelve soldiers who never stumbled, never shifted their grip, never blinked.

Tamsin's hand found hers. His fingers were cold.

"She's offering terms," he said. "She always offers terms first. Corvane told me that, in the end. When he was hollow enough to tell the truth." He swallowed. "She likes to let you sell yourself. It tastes better to her that way."

* * *

Vessaline had been many people.

She had been a shepherd's daughter on the moors of Callowmere, before Callowmere had a name that anyone remembered, and she had been the man who taught her to count sheep by the stars, and she had been his wife, briefly, in a life she had taken whole from a dying woman's head because the dying woman had begged her to—*keep it, keep it, don't let it end*—and she had been a queen for so long that the word had worn smooth as a river stone.

That was the trouble with living the way she lived. The memories did not sit neatly, one to a shelf. They braided. They cross-pollinated. She remembered two mothers, and both of them were hers. She remembered dying in a plague year and surviving it, and both were true. She remembered the taste of her first kiss and could not have said, under torture, whose mouth it had been—her first husband's, or a fisherman's from a coast that no longer appeared on maps, or a boy's, a boy of nineteen she had consumed in her two hundredth year because his joy had been so bright it had hurt her to look at.

She stood now at the mouth of the valley with the thirteenth moon's light on her face, and she did a thing she did every morning of her long life, a thing she had never told another living soul.

She counted herself.

*I am the woman who feared the dark as a child. I am the general who burned Vell's harbor. I am the mother of a daughter who—*

The thought snagged, as it always snagged. A daughter. She had a daughter. Or she had *been* a daughter, or she had eaten a memory of a daughter so vivid that it had rooted in her like a tree in a wall, and now she could not walk past that thought without the whole structure groaning.

*The girl with the red ribbon. Was she mine? Did I braid her hair, or did I only watch someone else braid it, from inside someone else's head?*

Two hundred years of stolen lives, and the sum of them was this: a woman who could not answer the simplest question in the world. *Who are you?* She was everyone. She was therefore no one. She was a house so full of guests that the owner had been pushed, room by room, into the cellar, and the cellar door had been locked from the outside, and the key—she had eaten the key, she realized sometimes, in her worst hours. She had eaten the key herself, because it had tasted like peace.

And now, at the top of this mountain, in a country that should not exist, there was a Door that could undo all of it. That could pour her out like water. That could leave her—she could barely frame the thought—a single person. One life. One mother. One death, at the end of one ordinary span of years, and nothing after.

Her soldiers had told her the thief's name. Tamsin Joubert. A market-cutpurse with a gift for lifting years, the kind of small talent that usually ended on a registration block in Vell. And yet the memory had lodged in him—the Aldermere memory, the one thing in all the world she could not simply take, because the Door demanded what no crown could compel. A gift freely surrendered.

*Freely.* The word was an insult. Everything she had, she had taken, and the taking had made it hers more truly than any gift could be. But the Door was old and stubborn and had been built by people who believed in things she had stopped believing in before her first century ended.

So she would offer terms. It tasted better that way, in any case. Persuasion was just theft with better manners.

"Set the palanquin down," she said, and her voice, when it emerged, was young. It was always young. That was the one mercy of her condition: she had eaten so many nineteen-year-old summers that her throat would never age. "And bring me the white banner. The one with nothing on it. Let him see we come with empty hands."

* * *

They came up the terraced road at midday, and Tamsin counted them as they came: twelve bearers, forty soldiers, and the queen walking at the front, on her own feet, in a gown the color of unmarked parchment.

"She's beautiful," Sera whispered, and hated herself for it.

"She's a trap with a face," Tamsin said. But his mouth had gone dry, and he knew the feeling for what it was. He had spent his life around beautiful things that wanted something from him. He knew the hunger under the loveliness. He had *been* the hunger under the loveliness.

Vessaline stopped ten paces from the Door itself, where it stood in its frame of living stone, veiled in thirteenth-moon light. She looked at it the way a woman looks at a mirror when she is afraid of what she'll see.

Then she turned, and her eyes found Tamsin's, and he felt the weight of them like a hand on his chest.

"Tamsin Joubert," she said. Her voice carried without effort, the way certain voices do, as though the world itself were listening out of politeness. "Thief of Vell. Siphon of Thornfell. The man who robbed me of a country."

"I robbed you of nothing," Tamsin said. "Someone sold me a memory. I didn't ask what was in the wrapping."

"No." She smiled, and it was a terrible thing, that smile, because it was warm. It was genuinely warm. He had spent enough years reading faces to know the difference between a mask and a face, and this was a face, and that was the worst part. "You didn't. That was Odile's doing, and Merren's, and mine, in the end—everything is mine, in the end, I have had a very long time to arrange the world to my liking. But you, thief, are the one thing in it I could not arrange. You are the loose thread." She took a step closer. The soldiers did not move. The garden itself seemed to lean away from her, flowers closing along the terraces like eyes. "And I have come to offer you a bargain, because I am old enough to know that loose threads are better woven in than cut."

"I'm listening," Tamsin said, because he was, because he couldn't help it, because the queen's voice was full of rooms he wanted to see.

"You carry the key. The memory of Aldermere's Door—where it is, what it wants, the shape of the lock. I could take it from you. Don't flatter yourself that you could stop me; the taking is the one thing I have never failed at. But the Door is particular. It wants a *surrender*, not a theft, and so I must ask." Her lip curled faintly on the word *ask*, a small honest crack in all that warmth. "So. Here is what I offer."

She raised one hand, and the light changed, and Tamsin's breath went out of him.

Because she *showed* him.

Not a description. Not a promise. She opened herself, the way a thief opens a lock, and let him see what she carried: centuries of it, whole lifetimes stacked like coins, summers without end, love affairs that had lasted forty years and been consumed in an afternoon, the memory of a hundred first snows, of children grown and grandchildren grown and gardens planted and harvested and planted again, the taste of bread from a hundred vanished bakeries, the sound of songs whose composers were dust. Immortality not as a concept but as a *sensation*, warm and endless and sweet as honey left in the sun.

"You could have this," Vessaline said softly. "Not all of it. I am not a fool—I keep what keeps me. But a share. Fifty years. A hundred. Youth that does not end, a life without the little death waiting at the bottom of it. And more than that." She stepped closer still, close enough that he could smell her, and she smelled of nothing, of clean air, of no single life at all. "Freedom. Walk out of this garden with your Sera and your refugees and your conscience, and no warrant will ever bear your name again. The purges end. The registrations end. Corvane is already spent—I have no use for broken tools. You could be the last thief in a kingdom with nothing left to steal, or you could be the first free man in a kingdom with everything to give."

"And the price," Tamsin said. His voice came out steadier than he felt.

"The key-memory. Given to me. Freely." She said the word like it cost her something. Perhaps it did. "Give me Aldermere's Door, and I will do with it what I have done with everything else. I will keep it. I will *be* it. And no one will ever have to lose anything again—no one will have to surrender a self, or cross a wall of forgetting, or choose between one life and another. I will hold it all, thief. I have been holding it all for two hundred years. I am very good at holding."

* * *

*He will refuse,* Vessaline thought, watching the thief's face. *They always refuse, and then I take, and the taking is—*

The thought did not finish.

Because for a moment—one unguarded moment, the kind of moment that had been coming more often lately, like loose teeth—she was not sure who was doing the thinking.

*They always refuse,* said someone in her, a voice with a Vell accent, a voice that had belonged to a magistrate she had consumed a hundred and sixty years ago. *And then you take, and you tell yourself it was necessary.*

*They always refuse,* said another voice, younger, a girl's voice, a shepherd's daughter from a moor that might have been Callowmere or might have been a memory she'd eaten from someone who had never seen a moor at all. *And then you take, and it is the only thing you know how to do.*

*Who am I?* she thought, and the question opened under her like a sinkhole. *Which of them is asking?*

She had a trick for this. She had a room in herself, a small locked room where she kept the one memory she had never consumed, the one she had started with: her own first memory, a red door and rain and her mother's voice calling her home. She went to it now, the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth.

The room was empty.

She stood very still, smiling at the thief, and inside she was screaming down corridors of borrowed years—*where is it, where is my door, where is my rain, where is my mother's voice*—and the corridors answered only in other people's voices, hundreds of them, thousands, all of them hers, none of them her.

She had eaten it. Somewhere in two centuries of hunger she had eaten her own first memory—perhaps by accident, perhaps because it had been there and she had been hungry, the way she was always hungry, the way the hunger had never once in two hundred years been satisfied. The queen of a hundred lives could not remember her own front door.

*This is what I am offering to save him from,* she thought, and the thought was almost funny. *This is what I am offering him as a gift.*

The girl beside the thief—the empty one, the scraped one, the one whose blankness Vessaline's own warden had made and whose fullness the girl had somehow begun to recover—was watching her with an expression Vessaline recognized. Pity. It had been a long time since anyone had looked at her with pity. She found she wanted to burn it off the girl's face, and she found, a heartbeat later, that she did not know whether that wanting was hers.

*Enough.* She gathered herself the way one gathers spilled coins, by feel, by weight, not knowing which were which. The smile stayed. The smile always stayed; it was the last thing she owned outright.

"Well, thief?" she said aloud. "You've seen what I hold. You've seen what I offer. A hundred years, and your freedom, and hers. All you must give up is a country that no longer exists, for a people who would trade every memory they have for one more summer. I am not the villain of this story, whatever the garden tells you. I am the only one here who has actually lived. Ask yourself who is greedier: the woman who takes years, or the old woman in the garden who asks a man to give up *everything he is* to open a door."

The wind came down off the thirteenth moon's ridge, and it smelled, for the first time in a century, of rain.

Tamsin looked at her a long moment. And Vessaline, who had read ten thousand faces, who had consumed ten thousand lives, saw something in his that she had not seen in a very long time and had never once been able to buy.

He was sorry for her.

"I've seen what you hold," he said quietly. "That's exactly why I'll never take it. You're not offering me a life, Your Majesty. You're offering me your disease." He glanced at Sera, and something passed between them that Vessaline could not follow and suddenly, desperately, wanted to. "And I've already got somewhere to put what I am. It's not for sale."

The queen's smile held. It was the only thing left in her that never wavered.

"Then we do this the old way," she said, and her voice was still young, and her eyes were a thousand years old, and behind her, all down the valley, forty soldiers drew their blades in a single sound like the world taking a breath. "I did offer, thief. Remember that, in whatever is left of you afterward. Remember that I *asked*."

She raised her hand.

"Take the garden," said Queen Vessaline, in the voice of every person she had ever been. "Take it all."

And the thirteenth moon's light turned red as blood, and the Battle of Borrowed Faces began.
