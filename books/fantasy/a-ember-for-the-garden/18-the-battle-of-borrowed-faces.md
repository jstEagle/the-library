## 18. The Battle of Borrowed Faces

The light came first.

It poured over the terraces like spilled honey, the thirteenth moon's borrowed glow made visible at last, and where it touched the garden walls Tamsin could see them plainly for the first time—the soldiers of Vessaline descending the far slopes in their hundreds, armor catching that pale radiance until they looked less like men than a river of knives flowing downhill.

"They followed the moon," Sera breathed beside him. She stood on the highest terrace with her hands pressed flat against the old keeper's stone balustrade, and her knuckles had gone white as bone. "All this time I thought we were running from her. We were running ahead of her light."

Below them, Aldermere woke.

Tamsin had seen the suspended twilight of the lost country every day since they'd crossed the wall of forgetting—citizens moving through gardens in slow, dreaming procession, faces turned toward a sun that hadn't truly risen in a century. But now the dream broke. Bells he hadn't known existed began to toll from towers half-swallowed by ivy, and doors opened along the terraced streets, and people came out into the honeyed light with expressions Tamsin recognized from market days in Vell: the look of those who have heard soldiers before.

The ancient keeper appeared at his elbow without sound, as she always did. Her name, she had told him once, was Maren, though she said it the way one recites a word in a dead language.

"You are the thief," she said. It was not a question. It had never been a question; the woman had looked at him across the garden wall and seen straight through to the siphon coiled behind his eyes. "You take what others hold."

"I take what they don't need anymore," Tamsin said. "That was always the rule."

"Then tonight you will break it." Maren's eyes, pale as frost on a windowpane, did not leave the descending army. "My people have kept their memories safe for a hundred years. Every joy, every grief, every ordinary morning—we stored them rather than surrender them to your queen's tax. They are not weapons." A pause, and something old and tired moved across her face. "But tonight they must be."

* * *

They came down through the orchards two hours past moonrise, and the first line of Vessaline's soldiers met the first line of Aldermere's defenders at the foot of the terraces, and no blade was drawn on either side.

Instead, the defenders opened their hands.

Tamsin watched from the middle terrace, Sera gripping his sleeve, as an old man in a gardener's smock stepped forward alone to meet a rank of twenty armored soldiers—and *gave*. Tamsin felt it happen the way one feels a door open in a distant room: a pressure change, a taste of ozone and rain. The old man poured a memory outward like water from a jug, and the front rank of soldiers stopped as if they'd walked into glass.

What they received, Tamsin could not see. But he saw what it did.

A young soldier dropped his spear and sat down in the wet grass. Another turned in a slow circle, hands rising toward his own helmet as if to check whether his head still belonged to him. A third began, quite simply, to weep—not the sobbing of fear but the deep, helpless crying of a man remembering something unbearable, some grief that had never been his until thirty seconds ago.

"Grief-memories," Maren said beside him. "We have a century of them. Funerals. Plagues. The last night before the borders closed, when mothers said goodbye to daughters who chose to stay outside." Her voice was flat as slate. "The queen taught the world that memory can be taken by force. We learned instead that it can be *given*."

"It's not killing them," Sera whispered.

"No. It's worse than killing, if you ask a soldier." The old gardener swayed, emptied, and two Aldermere women caught him under the arms and drew him back up the terrace. "Each gift costs the giver everything held in it. He will wake tomorrow with a hole where that funeral used to be. They all will. That is the price of defending a country that has already been erased once."

The second wave came harder. Soldiers locked shields and sang—a marching cadence, loud and rhythmic, and Tamsin understood with a thief's quick instinct what they were doing: the song was an anchor, a repeated pattern to cling to when strange memories flooded in. Clever. Vessaline had fought gifted people before.

But Aldermere had a century to prepare, and its defenders adapted faster than any army Tamsin had ever watched. When the singing ranks advanced, three defenders stepped out together and gave in harmony—one memory split three ways, braided: a wedding, they learned later, a wedding so joyful that the soldiers at its center stumbled out of formation laughing, laughing until they fell down, laughing and weeping at once because joy that large is indistinguishable from grief. The shield wall broke around them like water around stones.

"They're drowning them in *lives*," Tamsin murmured. "Not pain. Not terror. Just—too much living, all at once."

"Is that not what your queen does to herself?" Maren asked. "She has swallowed ten thousand lives and calls it immortality. My people are only returning the favor, all at once, uninvited."

* * *

The left flank folded first.

Tamsin saw it happen from the rose terrace: a company of Vessaline's veterans, the ones with the grey cloaks and the disciplined silence, driving up the western stairs where the defenders were fewest. And among them, moving like a needle through cloth, a figure in white-and-gold who did not sing, did not anchor, did not falter when memories struck her—because she *drank* them.

He knew the posture. He knew the tilt of the head, the slight parting of the lips, the way the fingers curled inward like roots finding soil. He had seen it in mirrors.

"She's gifted," he said. "One of Corvane's kind. Maybe—" and the thought arrived cold and complete, "—maybe trained by Corvane himself."

The white-cloaked woman reached the first defender, an elderly woman holding a lantern, and took. Tamsin felt the theft even at distance, a sick lurch like a hook behind his own eyes, and the lantern-bearer crumpled with a small, terrible sound—not screaming, just *emptying*, the way a wineskin sounds when the last of it runs out.

Sera's hand found his. "Tamsin. What do we do?"

What he did was the thing he had spent his whole life doing, the thing he had sworn in Thornfell passes never to do again: he stopped thinking about what was right, and started thinking about what was possible.

"Maren," he called. "Your stored memories. The ones in the vaults beneath the chapel—are they keyed to givers? Can anyone pour them?"

The keeper's frost-pale eyes narrowed. "They can be given by any hand willing to carry them. Why—"

"Because pouring is defense, and defense loses." He was already moving down the stairs, Sera at his heels, the thirteenth moon turning the world to silver and shadow. "Pouring gives each soldier one life, and they stagger, and then the next rank steps over them. But *taking*—" His blood was singing now, the old familiar music, the thief's hymn he'd tried to bury in smuggler roads. "Taking doesn't give them one life. Taking empties them. And there is one person on this field carrying more stolen years than anyone alive."

Maren understood before he finished. He saw it land in her face, horror and hope wrestling for the same ground.

"The queen," she said.

"The queen isn't down here yet. But her years are." He tapped his own temple. "Every soldier she's fed on, every citizen whose decades she drank to stay young—it's all still *hers*, carried in her body like coin in a purse. And somewhere on this field is everyone she's ever touched with that purse. If I can reach the thread—" he didn't have words for it, not really, only the thief's instinct, the sense of weight and connection that had guided every theft of his life, "—if I can find the thread between her and what she stole, I can pull."

"And what happens to you," Sera said quietly, "when you pull?"

He didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't a lie or a promise he couldn't keep.

* * *

He went down into the battle the way water goes downhill—finding every gap, every shadow, every moment of chaos and slipping through it. Sera stayed at his shoulder. He told her twice to go back up to the terraces, and both times she looked at him with those scraped-clean eyes that somehow held more stubbornness than any memory could teach, and he stopped telling her.

The fighting, if it could be called fighting, had become something stranger: a field strewn with kneeling soldiers, some weeping, some laughing, some sitting perfectly still with the vacant patience of children waiting to be told what happened next. Defenders moved among them, giving and giving, growing paler with each gift. Tamsin passed an Aldermere man no older than forty who had given away so much that he now moved like the ancient, hands trembling, face smooth and blank as an egg—and still he stepped forward to meet the next rank.

And everywhere, threading through it all, the white-cloaked taker, leaving emptiness behind her like a slug's trail.

Tamsin hunted her the way he'd once hunted merchants in the Vell markets: not by watching her, but by watching what bent around her. Air remembers pressure. Crowds remember fear. Wherever she walked, the defenders flinched before she arrived, some animal sense warning them of the hollow place she carried inside her.

He caught her at the fountain terrace, mid-theft, her fingers at the temples of a boy defender who couldn't have been more than sixteen. Tamsin did the thing he had done ten thousand times in a thousand rooms: he slid into the space between the taker and the taken, and *cut*.

The boy sagged free, half-emptied but breathing. The white-cloaked woman spun, and her eyes when they found him were wide and startled and very young—nineteen, perhaps twenty, a girl wearing a veteran's cloak, and Tamsin understood with a lurch that she was mostly stolen herself, a patchwork person, Vessaline's hunger given legs.

"You're the thief," she said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "The Warden's thief. He *screamed* about you."

"He should have whispered," Tamsin said, and took from her.

It was like opening a floodgate in reverse. She was so full—full past capacity, full the way a river is full in spring, stuffed with dozens of lives that did not belong to her, and she had no practice at defense because no one had ever dared steal from Vessaline's own. Memories rushed into Tamsin in a torrent: a fisherman's dawn on a lake he'd never seen, a mother's kitchen, a wedding in a village whose name dissolved even as he held it, a hanging, a harvest, a first snow, a betrayal—

He staggered. Sera caught his arm. "Tamsin—"

"I'm fine." He wasn't. He was suddenly carrying six more lives, and each one came with its griefs attached, and somewhere beneath all of it he felt the thread—the thin, humming connection running from this girl's stolen cargo up the mountain, through the dark, to the crown on the head of the woman commanding the army above.

The thread led *up*. Toward the wall. Toward the Door.

Toward Vessaline.

"She's coming," he gasped. "She's not sending more soldiers. She's done with soldiers. She's coming herself, and she'll be at the Door within the hour, and everything she's ever stolen is going to arrive with her like a tide."

* * *

Above them, the thirteenth moon burned directly overhead now, and the garden's terraces stood lit like the steps of a temple, and Queen Vessaline descended through her own army's wreckage with six honor guards and no hurry at all.

Tamsin watched her come from the high terrace, Sera's hand in his, Maren silent at his other side. The queen walked past her kneeling, weeping, laughing soldiers without a glance. Memory-sheaths of light clung to her shoulders—he could see them now, with the thread humming in his skull, could see the stolen years wrapped around her like luminous bandages, decade upon decade upon decade, a woman armored in other people's time.

"She is magnificent," Maren said, and there was no admiration in it, only the awful accuracy of grief.

"She's a hoard," Tamsin said. "And I'm a thief."

Sera turned to him. In the moonlight her face was very grave and very clear. "Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm going to pick the biggest pocket in the history of the world," Tamsin Joubert said, "and I'm going to do it while she's standing on the doorstep of the only door that matters. And when she reaches for the key—" he flexed his empty hands, feeling the six new lives churning in him alongside all the old ones, feeling the thread pull taut as a bowstring, "—she's going to find her pockets already turned out."

Far below, at the foot of the terraces, the great Door of Aldermere began, very softly, to glow.
