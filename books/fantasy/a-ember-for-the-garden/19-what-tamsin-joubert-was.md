## 19. What Tamsin Joubert Was

The Door of Aldermere stood taller than any cathedral spire Tamsin had ever robbed, and it was not made of wood or stone or iron. It was made of remembering.

He could feel that now, standing on the terrace before it with the thirteenth moon burning overhead like a silver coin held too close to a flame. The Door was a lattice of light and absence, threads of pale gold woven through gaps of pure nothing, and where the nothing touched his eyes they watered and slid away, refusing to hold its shape. It hummed. Not a sound so much as a pressure behind the teeth, a chord struck on an instrument the size of a country.

And across the ruined garden path, her gown scorched at the hem, her crown of borrowed years glinting like frost, stood Queen Vessaline.

She had come up through her own broken ranks. He had done that—him, the thief from Thornfell's ditches, pulling her stolen decades out of her soldiers mid-battle until whole companies sat down in the terraced rows and wept over memories that were no longer theirs to carry. But she had walked through the grief-flood untouched, because there was nothing left in her that grief could find purchase on. She was a palace of empty rooms wearing a woman's face.

"You could have taken my offer," she said. Her voice was soft. That was the worst of it—the gentleness, the patience of someone who had outlived every voice that ever scolded her. "Immortality, Tamsin Joubert. Freedom. A kingdom's worth of years, and you would have spent them however you pleased. You were made for this. A man who steals memory for coin doesn't refuse a crown's ransom out of principle."

"No," Tamsin agreed. His hands were shaking. He kept them still anyway; a thief's first lesson was that shaking hands got you caught. "I'd have refused it out of arithmetic."

"Arithmetic?"

"You've eaten a country," he said. "Every year you take makes the next one cheaper. I've seen it in smaller things. A merchant who sells one wedding memory comes back for the rest of the marriage. There's no bottom to it. I know because I looked for one in myself for twenty years."

The queen smiled, and it was almost warm. "Then you understand better than most. Memory is only weight. I simply chose to set mine down."

Behind him, he heard Sera moving—her breath ragged, the keeper's ancient voice murmuring somewhere below the terrace, the last defenders of Aldermere holding the garden gates with arms full of other people's yesterdays. The refugees were already gathering in the twilight streets beyond, hundreds of them, everyone the purge had chased across three kingdoms, waiting for a door that had been shut for a hundred years.

Waiting for him to do arithmetic.

The keeper had told him the price two nights ago, in a room that smelled of dried lavender and centuries: *The Door opens for a memory freely surrendered. Not stolen—that is the old sin, the sin that built your queen. Given. Whole. And it must be great enough to outweigh the seal.* He had assumed, then, that Sera would offer herself. She had tried to. She had stood in that lavender-smelling room with her scraped-clean mind and her stubborn chin and said, *I have nothing left to lose,* and he had told her she was wrong, had shouted it, had felt something crack open in his chest that he'd spent a lifetime bricking over—and in the shouting he had understood what he would actually pay.

Because he had carried the map-memory of Aldermere for years. He knew how surrender worked. And he knew, the way a lockpick knows the shape of a keyhole, what the Door was asking for.

Not a memory. A life.

*One whole self, freely given.*

* * *

Vessaline took a step toward him, and the moonlight bent around her strangely, the way it does around something that has been worn hollow. "You cannot open it," she said. "Whatever the keeper told you, whatever ritual you've pieced together—you are a pickpocket, boy, not a priest. Give me the key-memory and walk away rich. That is the last kindness I will offer."

"That's the thing," Tamsin said. "There is no key-memory. I figured it out on the road, when the mountains started singing. The Door isn't locked. It's *grieving*. Someone sealed it from this side with a whole life—a founder, a queen, somebody who loved this place enough to become the lock. And grief like that doesn't open for a key." He looked up at the woven light. "It opens for a replacement. Something given freely, whole, to take the weight."

"And you have such a thing?" Vessaline laughed, low and genuine, and for one terrible moment she sounded young. "You? A man assembled out of other people's leavings?"

"Yes," Tamsin said quietly. "Me."

It was strange, how calm he felt. He had expected terror. Instead there was only the thief's clarity, the moment on the cusp of the lift when the whole world narrows to silk and skin and the single perfect motion. He reached inward—not with his gift, not with the siphoning touch he had spent twenty years refining, but with something older and clumsier. He gathered himself the way he had once gathered a dying woman's last summer in Vell: gently, completely, leaving nothing behind but warmth.

Every theft. Every road. The smell of his mother's bread—he couldn't remember her face anymore, only the bread, and that was enough, that was *his*, bought honestly by being lived. The first memory he ever stole, a fisherman's dawn, and the shame afterward, and the second theft, and the hundredth, and Odile's smoke-filled parlor, and the burned safehouse, and the ford where he lost everything and learned what mattered. Thornfell's passes under starlight. The chapel where a deleted country's name came back to him letter by letter. Sera's hand in his at the wall of forgetting. Sera screaming his name at the battle's edge. Sera, Sera, the word his whole self kept returning to like a compass needle.

He held all of it in his cupped hands, invisible, enormous.

"Sera," he said, and his voice cracked on it. "Don't watch."

She was already running up the terrace steps. He heard her—of course he heard her, he would know her footfall among ten thousand—and he turned just enough to see her face, and what he saw there broke him more cleanly than any warden's knife ever could. She knew. She had known since the lavender room, maybe. Since the ford. Since the rain.

"Don't," she said. "Tamsin. *Don't.*"

"It has to be freely given," he said. "That's the whole ritual. Nobody can take it from me. Not her—" he nodded at Vessaline, whose smile had finally died— "not you. Only me. That's the one thing in this world I get to decide, and I'm deciding it."

"There has to be another way—"

"There isn't. And even if there were—" he smiled, and it hurt, and it was real— "you told me once you had nothing left to lose. You were wrong then and you're wrong now. You have everything. Go be someone who remembers rain."

The moon reached its zenith. The Door's humming deepened into something like recognition, like a dog lifting its head at a familiar step.

Tamsin Joubert opened his hands.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the cruelest part. It felt like setting down a pack carried so long the shoulders had forgotten its shape—like the first breath after surfacing. The light of the Door poured into him and he poured back, and the threads of gold began to move, weaving him in, thread by thread by thread.

His name went first. He felt it leave the way you feel a word dissolve on the tongue—*Tamsin*—and then the space where it had been, smooth and blank as river stone. Then the market city of Vell, the smuggler roads, the burned safehouse. Then his mother's bread, and he grieved for that one, briefly, a small bright flare going dark. Then the thefts, all of them, thousands of borrowed lives flowing out of him like water finding the sea, and the Door drank them without judgment, because a door does not care what passes through it, only that it stand open.

What remained was a man-shaped quiet. Warmth without content. He watched the golden threads close over the last of himself—the boy on the Thornfell road, the thief in the rain—and felt, distantly, the way you feel weather through a window, that this was right. That this was, of everything he had ever done, the only fully honest transaction of his life.

Payment rendered in full.

The Door of Aldermere swung wide.

Light came through it—not moonlight, not sunlight, something older and greener, the color of growing things—and with it came sound: birdsong, water, a thousand voices in the twilight streets crying out at once. The seal of a century dissolved. Somewhere below, the ancient keeper sank to her knees. Somewhere behind him, Queen Vessaline made a sound he would never remember hearing—a raw, unraveling cry, as the Door's opening pulled her stolen centuries out through her pores like poison from a wound—but the nameless man on the terrace had no ears left for her story.

He had ears for only one thing.

A scream, tearing down the garden steps, raw as a hawk's cry:

"*TAMSIN!*"

His name, in her voice, one last time—flung at him like a torch into the dark, the final utterance of a word that now existed nowhere else in the world. It washed over the nameless man and found nothing to land on, nothing to cling to, and fell through him like rain through an open window.

But some ember of him—something deeper than memory, older than names—turned toward it anyway.

The refugees were coming through. Hundreds of them, weeping, laughing, carrying their children and their griefs and their small hoarded joys across the threshold into the world beyond. Sera stood at the base of the Door, her restored past blazing in her eyes—the garden, the erased country, the girl she had been before Corvane's hunger—and she looked up at the nameless man holding the Door wide, and her mouth shaped his name again, and again, and each time it fell into the same patient silence.

He smiled at her. It was a stranger's smile, kind and empty.

And the man who had been Tamsin Joubert stepped aside, as a doorkeeper does, to let the world pass through.
