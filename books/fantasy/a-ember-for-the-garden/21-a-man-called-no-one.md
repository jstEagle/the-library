## 21. A Man Called No One

The world beyond the Door smelled of rain that had not yet fallen.

Sera felt it before she understood it—a coolness in the air like the held breath of a country waiting to begin again—and she stood a long while on the threshold stone with the wind lifting her hair, listening to the sound of ten thousand people discovering they were allowed to weep.

Behind her, Aldermere was closing like a book whose story had finally been told. The terraced gardens were folding themselves into twilight, the suspended citizens waking at last into their own futures, and the great Door—oak and iron and older than any kingdom she could now remember—was drawing shut with a groan that sounded almost like relief. She did not watch it close. She could not. Somewhere on the other side of it was a man who no longer had a name, and if she watched the door seal him away from her she would have torn it open again with her bare hands, grace or no grace.

So she looked forward instead. Forward, into the green.

They came through in waves: the hunted of Thornfell, the smuggler families, the gifted children who flinched from every touch, the old men who had forgotten why they were running but remembered how. They spilled down a hillside that had never been mapped because it had never needed to be—grass soft as an apology, wildflowers in colors Sera's restored memories insisted did not exist, and above it all a sky so wide and so clean it made her chest ache. Rivers threaded the valley below. Smoke rose from hearths already being built. Someone was singing, badly, and no one was stopping them.

There were no wardens here. No registers. No thirteenth bell.

Sera walked among them for three days, searching.

She told herself it was duty. The refugees needed organizing; the children needed counting; the old quartermaster from Thornfell, Brannic, kept shouting for someone with a head for ledgers, and hers was the only head that had ever been empty enough to learn anything twice. So she counted. She organized. She carried water and settled disputes about tent-pitches and sat with a boy named Pell who woke screaming every night from memories Corvane had left jagged-edged in him, and she told him the same thing every time: *You are here. You are whole enough. That is where you start.*

But every campfire, every crowd, every knot of new arrivals coming pale and blinking through the last hours of the Door's long closing—she searched them all. Her eyes went first to the tall ones, the dark-haired ones, the ones who moved like they were used to not being seen. And each time, each single time, it was not him.

*Of course it isn't,* she told herself on the third night, staring into a fire while Brannic argued logistics at her shoulder. *You know what he gave. You stood there. You heard yourself scream his name and you heard the name come apart in your mouth like wet paper.*

That was the cruelty of it, she thought. Not that he was gone. That he was *near*—somewhere on this side of the wall, breathing the same rainless-rain air—and he was near the way a stranger is near. A man called No One, walking around inside a life with all the rooms emptied out. She had been that person once. She knew exactly how it felt to be looked at by someone who remembered you, and to feel nothing but the faint, polite confusion of a house whose windows have been painted black.

She had sworn, in the chapel in Thornfell, over the erased records of her own birth-country, that she would never let another person be scraped hollow. And then she had stood aside and let the bravest man she knew do it to himself.

*He chose it,* said the voice that sounded like Odile, dry and unsentimental. *He chose it over letting you choose it, which is worse, and better, and very much like him.*

*It isn't like him at all,* Sera answered silently. *Tamsin Joubert never gave anything away for free in his life.*

And that—that small, bitter joke, made alone in the dark—was what finally broke her open. Because it was true, and because it was true, it meant the man wandering somewhere in this valley was not Tamsin Joubert anymore, and she had better decide quickly whether she loved the name or the man underneath it, because only one of those still existed.

She decided before the fire burned down.

* * *

She found him on the sixth day, in a garden that should not have existed.

She had gone walking east along the river, past the last of the tents, following a rumor one of the Aldermere gatekeepers had dropped without seeming to mean to: that a few of the twilight-citizens had chosen not to wake, or rather had woken into something gentler—an old keeper's terrace, overgrown, where the Door's grace had pooled like light in a cup. A place where things grew that had been planted a hundred years ago and waited.

The path found her before she found it. That was the way of this country, she was learning: roads here had opinions. The trail bent between two flowering hawthorns and opened onto a terrace cut into the hillside, and Sera stopped walking, because her body simply declined to continue until it had finished understanding what it saw.

Terraces fell away below her in green steps, each one planted in rows so old the stones had sunk into the earth like sleeping animals. Roses climbed trellises that had rotted and been replaced and rotted again. Bees worked the lavender with the drowsy industry of creatures who had never known a winter. And in the middle of it, kneeling in the dirt of a bed with his sleeves rolled up and soil to his elbows, was a man.

He was thin. Thinner than he had been at the ford, thinner than he had been even in the last terrible days before the Door, as though the surrender of a whole self takes something from the body too and not only the mind. His dark hair had gone shaggy, uncut, falling into his eyes. There was dirt on his jaw and a scratch on his forearm that he had clearly not bothered to notice.

And he was smiling.

That was the thing that stopped her breath. Not grief, not the gray vacancy she had braced herself for—but a small, unhurried, genuine smile, the smile of a man repotting seedlings in morning sun, a man with nothing missing because there was nothing left to miss. He lifted one of the seedlings, cradled its roots in his palm with absurd tenderness, and set it into the earth, and patted the soil around it, and said—to the plant, to himself, to no one—

"There. That's better, isn't it."

Sera's hand went to her mouth.

She knew that voice. She knew it the way you know a song from before your memory began—in the ribs, in the pulse, somewhere older than knowing. It was warmer than she remembered, unguarded, stripped of the thief's sardonic edge that had always armored it. It was the voice of the boy in the childhood memory she had once accidentally absorbed, the boy on the rooftops of Vell counting coins that weren't his yet. The scraping had taken everything he had built over that boy. What remained was the boy.

She must have made a sound. Or perhaps the country itself announced her, the way it seemed to announce everything—the hawthorns shivered, the bees paused. He turned his head, and his eyes found her across twenty feet of garden, and they were clear and brown and completely, perfectly empty of her.

"Hello," he said pleasantly. "Are you lost? People come up here sometimes. I don't mind the company." He considered, then added, with the gravity of a man sharing a confidence, "I'm meant to be thinning these. I don't know who told me that, but it seems important."

The words she had carried for six days—all of them, the speech she'd composed by firelight, the anger, the accusation, the plea—died in her throat. What came out instead was small and stupid and true:

"Do you know your name?"

He blinked at her. And then he laughed—not bitterly, not sadly, just laughed, a little embarrassed, rubbing dirt off his hands onto his trousers.

"No," he said. "Isn't that funny? I've been trying to think of it all week. It feels like it should be short. Two syllables, maybe." He frowned at a rosebush as though it might be hiding the answer. "I keep almost catching it and then—" he made a gesture like smoke slipping through fingers, "—gone. But it doesn't hurt, if that's what you're asking. It's more like..." He searched for it, head tilted. "Like a room in a house you've never been in. You know the house has one. You just can't find the door."

*A door,* thought Sera, and nearly wept.

"I'm Sera," she said.

"Sera." He tried it out, tasting it. "That's a good name. Solid. Like a stone you could skip across a river." Then, holding out a dirt-darkened hand with the unaffected courtesy of a man who had never learned to distrust anyone: "I don't suppose you'd want to help me with these? The lavender's winning."

She should have said yes. She should have knelt down beside him in the warm dirt and started there, small, slow, the way you approach any wounded creature. Instead she heard herself say, in a voice that shook only a little:

"Could I tell you a story first?"

He looked at her for a moment—this stranger with soil under his nails and a hole where a life used to be—and something flickered behind his eyes. Not memory. Nothing so cruel as memory. Just a kind of readiness, the way a cold room is ready for a fire.

"I like stories," he said. "I think I always have. Isn't that strange? I don't remember ever hearing one."

Sera sat down on the low stone wall beside the flowerbed, close enough to touch him and carefully not touching, and folded her hands, and looked out over the terraces so she would not have to look at his face while she began.

"Once," she said, "in a city called Vell, where the market bells ring thirteen times at dusk and nobody remembers why, there lived a thief."

"A thief," he repeated, delighted. "Good. I was hoping it wouldn't be a king."

"He wasn't a very good thief," she went on, "which is to say he was excellent at it and hated himself thoroughly for being excellent. He had a gift—he could take memories out of people's heads the way you'd lift a coin purse, smooth and silent, and the rich of Vell paid him beautifully to lose the years they didn't want, and collectors paid him even more beautifully for what he took. He had rules about it. Never take more than asked. Never take the load-bearing memories—the ones a person stands on. Never take from children." She glanced sideways at him. "He was proud of his rules. He hid behind them like a wall."

"What happened to them?" the man asked. "The rules."

"The world happened to them," Sera said. "A proclamation came down from the palace—every gifted soul must register before the thirteenth turn of the moon—and registration meant the Warden, and the Warden ate what he registered. So the thief burned his safehouse and ran into the mountains, carrying one stolen memory he should never have touched. A map. The location of a door out of a country that had been deleted from every book and every tongue." Her voice caught; she smoothed it flat. "Aldermere."

Something moved in him then. Faint, deep, like a fish turning far beneath the surface of a pond. He pressed his hand flat against his own chest, frowning.

"I know that word," he said slowly. "I don't know *how* I know it. It's like... a word you know the shape of in the dark."

"You knew it better than anyone alive," Sera said softly. "You carried it for years without knowing what it was. It whispered to you in a merchant's wedding memory, the first night you ever stole something that fought back. *Aldermere,* it said, and you couldn't sleep afterward, and you told yourself it was nothing." She smiled despite herself. "You were a liar even in your own head. It was a habit."

He laughed again, that easy laugh, and shook his head. "This thief sounds like a piece of work."

"He was," she agreed. "He was selfish and careful and lonely, and he trusted no one, and he had spent his whole life making sure no one had anything to take from him. And then the Warden scraped a girl's mind clean and left her standing in a smuggler's pass with nothing—not a name, not a yesterday, not a single reason to keep walking—and this thief, this careful, selfish man, looked at her and saw himself." Now she did look at him, and her eyes were burning, and she let them burn. "He saw a house with the windows painted black. And he didn't run. He taught her how to walk again. He spent his stolen memories buying her moments—rain, bread, music—because closeness meant consumption and he got close anyway. He navigated burning mountains by spending other people's years out of his own head. He lost everything at a river ford and came up out of the water still holding her hand. And when the Door demanded a whole life, freely given—one entire self, surrendered willingly—he didn't hesitate. He didn't even argue properly. He just stepped forward, and he gave it, every theft and every road and every name, and the last thing anyone said to him was his own name, screamed by a girl who loved him too late to say it better."

The garden was very quiet. The bees had gone still in the lavender. The man sat with his dirty hands loose in his lap, and tears were sliding down his face—silent, unhurried, bewildering him; he touched his cheek and looked at his wet fingertips with genuine puzzlement, as though the grief belonged to someone else and had simply gotten lost on its way home.

"That's a sad story," he said at last, and his voice cracked gently in the middle. "But it's a good one. Is that how it ends? With the girl screaming?"

"No," Sera said. "That's how it *pauses*. Stories do that. They stop in dark places and make you wait." She slid off the wall and knelt in the dirt beside him, close enough now that she could smell the lavender on him, the green smell of crushed stems, the astonishing ordinary warmth of a living man. "Because here's what the story doesn't say yet. It doesn't say that the girl followed him through. It doesn't say that she searched every camp and every road in a new world for six days, calling a name that no longer answered to anyone. And it doesn't say that when she finally found him, he was kneeling in a hundred-year-old garden, planting things, smiling at strangers, happier maybe than he had ever been in the life he gave away—and that she couldn't even hate him for it, because she had been empty once too, and she knew the peace of it, and she knew it was a lie you could live inside if you had someone willing to sit beside you and talk."

"Is someone going to do that?" he asked. "Sit beside me and talk?"

"I am," said Sera. "Every day, if you'll allow it. I'm going to tell you the whole story, start to finish, as many times as it takes. The market in Vell. The burned safehouse. The chapel with the erased books. The ford, and the lanterns, and the battle of borrowed faces. I'll tell you about a fence named Odile who cheated everyone except the people she loved. I'll tell you about a Warden who forgot his mother's face and what it cost him to let two strangers go. I will tell you every single thing, and when I run out of true things I'll invent new ones, and eventually—" her voice steadied, became something like a vow, "—eventually you won't be a man called No One. You'll be a man with a story. And a story, my friend, is just a name you can carry in your pocket instead of your head."

He was quiet a long moment. Then he picked up another seedling, and offered it to her, handle-first, roots cradled in his palm—an invitation, an occupation, a beginning.

"All right," he said. "But it's your turn to dig. Fair's fair."

They planted together as the light went long and gold across the terraces, and she talked, and he listened, and when she reached the part about the rooftop boy in Vell counting coins that weren't his yet, he laughed so hard he had to put down the trowel.

Later, as the first stars came out over the valley and the lamps of the refugee camps glimmered far below like a constellation brought down to earth, he paused with his hand resting on the fresh-turned soil and said, musingly, almost to himself:

"Tamsin."

Sera went absolutely still.

"It keeps surfacing," he said, frowning at the dirt as though the name were written there in some language only gardens could read. "I don't know what it is. A place? A person?" He shrugged, smiled, let it go, the way you let go of a leaf on a stream. "Funny, the things that stick."

"Funny," Sera agreed, and her voice did not break, because she had promised herself it wouldn't, and because she had learned from a thief that the most valuable things are the ones you give away quietly, without demanding they be returned.

She sat beside him in the darkening garden, shoulder almost touching shoulder, and began the story again from the beginning.

And far below, at the edge of the lamplight, at the border of the oldest terrace where the grass grew strange and silver, something small and bright had begun to push up through the soil—a green shoot tipped with ember-colored light, opening, petal by petal, toward a moon that was newly waxing in the east: the first moon of another thirteen.
