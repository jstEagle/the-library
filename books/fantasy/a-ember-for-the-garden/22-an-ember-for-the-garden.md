## 22. An Ember for the Garden

The first snow came in the third month, and Sera learned that Aldermere had never once seen snow.

She stood on the terrace above the terraced gardens—everything here was terraces and gardens, the whole country folded into green steps like a hand cupped to hold light—and watched white fall onto a land that had never felt it. The old citizens came out of their twilight houses in their soft grey robes and stared upward with faces like opened doors. One of them, a man who had been a bridge-builder before the erasure, caught a flake on his palm and wept without sound.

They remembered everything, these people. That was the cruelty and the mercy of Aldermere both: the kingdom had been struck from every map, every ledger, every tongue beyond the wall of forgetting, but inside its own borders nothing had been lost. The last citizens carried four hundred years of songs and harvests and small grievances, held in perfect suspension while the thirteenth moon circled and circled and the Door stayed shut. They remembered rain. They remembered the queen who refused the tax. They remembered each other's names going back eleven generations.

Sera remembered none of it. She had been born here, in one of these stone houses on one of these green steps, and the country knew her and she did not know it back.

But that was changing. Slowly, the way frost changes glass.

* * *

The Door's grace had come to her on the far side of the crossing, in the flood of refugees and soldiers-who-laid-down-their-arms and children blinking at real sunlight. It had not returned her memories as a wave returns a shore. It returned them the way a garden grows: by inches, by season, root before stem before flower.

First she remembered rain—the sensation of it, cold needling her scalp, the smell of wet dust—and she had stood in the courtyard of the keeper's house laughing and crying at once while Tamsin—

While the nameless man—watched her with an expression she could not yet name, because he had no memories left with which to name anything.

That was the arithmetic she lived inside now. He had given away everything so the Door would open. His thefts, his roads, his mother's face, his own name—all of it poured into the lock like water into dry earth, and the Door had drunk him down and swung wide. What remained was a man of perhaps twenty-five years with clear eyes and empty hands, who woke each morning surprised, pleasantly, to find himself alive.

He did not know her. But when she sat beside him, some animal part of him turned toward her warmth like a leaf toward light, and she had decided, in those first raw weeks, that this was enough. That it would have to be enough.

Then the seasons began to pass, and she learned it was not enough, and also—it was more.

* * *

Spring came to Aldermere like a rumor finally confirmed.

The suspended twilight thinned after the Door opened; the keeper said it would, that the country was waking from a long held breath. Real sun crossed the terraces. Things that had been planted a century ago and frozen mid-gesture pushed up through soil gone soft, blooming out of season and out of time, so that the whole valley looked like a memory of spring rather than spring itself—until, day by day, it simply became spring.

The nameless man took to the gardens the way water takes to a channel. No one taught him. He woke early, went out with bare hands into the beds, and worked until dusk, and if you asked him why, he smiled with a small confusion and said, *It feels like something I would do.*

He didn't remember planting things. He planted them anyway. Sera kept a private tally of the difference between those two sentences, and it broke her heart a little less each day.

She lived in the house next to his—there had been no discussion; she had simply stopped leaving. The keeper, an ancient woman named Ilseve who had tended the Door since before Vessaline was born, observed this with the patience of someone who had watched centuries of people orbit each other and said only: *Some seeds don't ask permission of the ground. They fall where they fall.*

In the evenings Sera told him stories. This had become ritual, unspoken but absolute: after supper, on the bench beneath the plum tree that had blossomed impossibly early, she told him about a thief named Tamsin Joubert.

She started with the market city of Vell, with a wedding memory lifted from a fat merchant's mind, a bride's nervous laughter stolen into a thief's pocket. She told him about Thornfell's smuggler passes, the mountains full of echoes. She told him about Warden Corvane, hollowed and wandering now somewhere in the world, carrying a stranger's family in place of his own lost name—she chose not to tell that story sadly. She told it as a story about mercy, which is what it had been, in the end, whatever it cost.

And every night the nameless man listened with his chin on his fist and his eyes half-closed, and sometimes he said, *Tell the part about the ford again,* and sometimes he said, *Was he afraid?*—and once, only once, very quietly: *I think I would have liked him.*

*You would have,* Sera said. *You did.*

* * *

By summer she had recovered most of herself.

Memories came back to her in the garden, oddly enough—she suspected the country itself was feeding them back, soil giving up what it had been entrusted with. She remembered her mother's kitchen smelling of cardamom and ash. She remembered the taste of river water over stones. She remembered being seven years old and hiding under the terrace stairs while crown riders passed on the far side of the border-wall, and her mother pressing a hand over her mouth and whispering, *Aldermere. Say it only inside yourself. Aldermere.* She remembered that the word had nearly dissolved on her tongue even then, the forgetting already leaking through.

And she remembered Tamsin. All of him. The way he counted his stolen memories like coins at night, lips moving. The way he lied badly and stole beautifully. The way he looked standing on top of the Door with his whole life pouring out of him—terrified, resolute, lit from below by the light of the thirteenth moon at its fullest—and the way her own voice had torn apart screaming his name, the last time anyone said it, the last time it existed anywhere in the world.

She had screamed it and the Door had swallowed the sound, and then there were no more Tamsins anywhere. Only this man with dirt under his fingernails who liked her stories.

Here is what she understood that summer, working beside him in the bean rows while he hummed a tune he didn't know was the Aldermere harvest song: you cannot give a man back his life. She had asked Ilseve, plainly, twice, and twice the old woman shook her head. *What is freely surrendered cannot be freely taken back. That is the whole law of doors.* And besides—even if it could be—what would she return to him? A boy beaten in Vell alleys? A young man who sold pieces of other souls to buy his supper? A fugitive who trusted no one, loved no one, slept in burned safehouses?

She would not take his emptiness from him. His emptiness was the bravest thing anyone had ever done.

But emptiness, she thought, watching him turn soil, is not the same as openness. An empty room waits. An empty room can be furnished.

* * *

The memory-seeds grew in the chapel garden.

After the battle—after Vessaline crumbled into a frightened old woman with no idea which of her thousand lives were hers, after the soldiers wept themselves empty and laid down their arms—the Aldermere defenders had gathered the weaponized memories off the field like shrapnel. Grief-bombs and joy-blades, bottled years loosed from the queen's hoard. Most were given back to their original owners or their descendants, homecomings that filled the valley all that autumn with the sound of people meeting their own pasts again.

But some had no owners left. Orphaned memories, unclaimed lives, sealed in glass jars in the ruined chapel where the records of the erased country had been kept. Sera went there often. She told herself it was research. It was grief, mostly, but she let herself believe in the research.

It was there, among the jars, that she found the seeds.

Ilseve showed her, the week of first frost. Not every orphaned memory survived as memory, the keeper explained. Some curdled, some faded—but a few, the ones that had been held longest and loved hardest, condensed. They became something else. Small, warm, dense as a coal. A seed, Ilseve called it, though it looked more like an ember: a knot of pure potential, a life compressed to its glowing essence. Plant one in an open mind—not to restore, never to restore; restoration was theft's twin—and it would grow forward instead of backward. Not remembering. Becoming.

*A life not yet lived,* Ilseve said, turning the ember in her spotted palm. It shed a faint gold light, like a lamp behind skin. *We made three of them, in the early centuries, for children born empty. They grow into whatever the person needs most. They take the shape of the hollow they're planted in.* She closed her hand. *There has only ever been one mind I've seen open enough for this. I wondered, for a while, why the Door chose him.*

Sera held out her hands before she knew she was going to.

* * *

Winter. Snow again, and this year the Aldermere citizens greeted it like an old acquaintance. The valley wore white terraces like a wedding dress. In the house next to his, Sera sat up late with the ember cupped in both palms, feeling its heat against her breastbone, and argued with herself the way she had once argued with him on the top of the Door.

*Let me do it,* she had said then. *I have nothing left to lose.*

*That's exactly why you can't,* he had answered. *Don't you see? You're the only one who gets to start clean. Everyone else is carrying their whole wrecked past up this mountain. You get to walk through carrying nothing. Don't spend that on me.*

He had been wrong about what she'd carry through—her memories had come home to her, by grace—but he had been right about everything that mattered. He had refused to let her vanish. He had chosen, with the last act of his self, to make sure she existed on the other side of the door.

Now it was her turn to refuse. Her turn to choose.

Not to give him back. Never that. But to kneel beside him in the dark of a winter garden, in the country of his sacrifice, and plant one small warm thing in the open ground of him—one ember, one seed, one beginning. Not Tamsin Joubert's life. A new one. Theirs.

She thought of what he'd say if he could say anything. She thought he'd probably make a joke about thieves and planting things that weren't theirs. Then she thought he'd look at her the way he'd looked at her at the ford, when she hauled him out of the water with his whole hoard of other people's years streaming away downstream, and he'd stop joking.

She got up before dawn, when the snow was blue with pre-light, and went next door, and found him already awake—which he often was, mornings, sitting up in bed with the mild wonder of a man re-meeting his own existence. She sat on the edge of his bed. His eyes found her in the dimness without fear; whatever else the Door had taken, it had left his trust in her intact, grown fresh in the empty ground like the first green on a burn.

*I have something for you,* she said. *It isn't your past. I want you to understand that first. Nothing of yours is coming back, and I wouldn't bring it if I could. This is something else.*

*Okay,* he said, and smiled his small morning smile. *You know I like your stories.*

*This one hasn't happened yet. That's the point of it.* She took his face in her hands. His skin was cool; his pulse beat steady under her fingers. *Close your eyes.*

He did. She pressed her forehead to his, the way she had once touched his stored memories to borrow sensation, back when closeness meant consumption and they had both been too hungry to care. But this was the opposite of taking. This was the oldest craft in Aldermere, older than the tax, older than the queen: the planting.

The ember moved from her palms into him like water finding a channel, and she felt it go—a thread of warmth leaving her, entering him, sinking. He made a small sound, not pain. Surprise. The sound of someone feeling sunlight land on them indoors.

Inside him, she knew—Ilseve had told her, and she felt it confirmed through her still-touching hands—the seed would do what seeds do. It would put down roots in the hollow the Door had left. It would grow toward whatever it found there: her voice through the wall, the plum tree, the terraces, the snow, the nightly stories about a thief whose name no one could say. It would grow into a life. Not remembered. Made.

His eyes opened. Wet, though he wasn't crying. He looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him before—something kindled behind the calm, faint and gold, like a lamp lit in a far window of a very large dark house.

*I dreamed something,* he whispered. *Just now. Before I even—there was a garden. Ours, I think. And someone was laughing.* His brow furrowed, reaching. *I couldn't see who.*

*You will,* Sera said. *That's the whole trick of it. That's how it works. It comes true slowly.*

Outside the window, the sky was paling over the eastern terraces. And low on the horizon, thin as a fingernail paring, silver-pale and impossibly young, a new moon was rising—the first moon of a new count, the first turn of another thirteen, and this time no proclamation followed it, no purge, no warden, no door counting down toward shutting. Just the moon, climbing, keeping ordinary time at last over a garden where a nameless man was learning, seed by seed, season by season, to become someone who had never needed stealing to survive.

Sera sat with him and watched it rise, her hand in his, the ember settling warm somewhere behind his ribs.

*Tell me the one about the ford,* he said, leaning into her shoulder. *I always forget—I mean, I want to hear it again.*

*Tomorrow night,* she said. *Tonight I'll tell you a different one. Tonight I'll tell you about the future.*

And far below the terraces, in the frozen garden beds, the first snowdrops of the new year were already pushing up through the dark—small, stubborn, certain—toward a spring that had not happened yet, but was coming, coming, all the same.
