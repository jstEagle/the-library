## 1. The Taster's Portion

The morning of the ninth of Harvestmonth began the way all mornings in the palace kitchens began: with steam, with shouting, with the smell of a hundred breakfasts being made for one woman.

Jude Vane stood at his station beneath the east colonnade, a low stone alcove fitted with a single chair, a single table, and a basin of clean water that had to be changed three times a day whether it needed changing or not. Nine years he had held this post. Nine years of swallowing small deaths on schedule, of keeping his face pleasant while his throat learned to distinguish between arsenic and antimony by aftertaste alone, of writing everything down afterward in a hand so neat that Master Corvel, may he rest unquietly, would have found nothing to correct.

He was thirty-one years old and he could count on one hand the number of people who knew what he did for a living. The Queen was not among them. She had never once looked at him directly, and this was correct. A taster who was seen became a taster who was threatened, and a taster who was threatened became a taster who could be bought.

So Jude sat in his alcove and waited for the first tray.

It came at half past seven, borne by a kitchen boy named Pell who had the unfortunate habit of whistling through the gap where his front teeth should have been. Pell set down the covered dishes with the reverence of an acolyte laying out relics: porridge with honey and poached pear, soft-boiled eggs in little silver cups, brown bread still warm enough to fog the underside of its cloth, and a pot of black tea from the high terraces, bitter as a widow's morning.

"Morning, Master Vane," Pell said. "Cook says the Queen's been restless. Bad dreams again."

"The Queen's dreams are her own affair," Jude said, which was what he always said, and Pell grinned and whistled himself back toward the kitchens, leaving Jude alone with four dishes and the particular solitude of a man about to die a little for someone else's sake.

He began, as he always began, with the tea.

This was discipline, not preference. Tea first because tea was the most easily poisoned—bitter herbs masked bitter things—and if the tea were fouled there was no sense wasting attention on eggs. He poured a measure into the tasting cup, let it cool to the temperature at which poison behaved honestly, and drank.

Nothing. Earth and smoke and the long burn of mountain-grown leaf. He swished, held it against his palate, counted to ten, swallowed. His tongue catalogued it automatically, the way a clerk's eye catalogues figures: *clean, clean, clean.*

The porridge next. Honey, pear, cream, a whisper of nutmeg. He took three spoonfuls and waited, watching the light come up over the inner courtyard, gilding the wet cobbles, catching the wings of pigeons. Somewhere below, a cart of winter squash was being unloaded. The palace woke around him in its usual way, indifferent and enormous, and Jude Vane sat inside it like a splinter lodged in wood—small, unnoticed, essential.

The eggs. The bread. He worked through them methodically, noting each flavor in the ledger he kept behind his eyes until he could transcribe it later. By the time he finished, twenty minutes had passed and nothing had burned, numbed, tightened, or turned sweet in the wrong place. He initialed the slate that traveled with the tray—*V.V., approved*—and rang the small bell that told the footman the Queen might eat.

Routine. All of it routine. And then the second tray arrived.

* * *

It came without a bearer.

That was the first wrong thing. Trays did not simply appear; they arrived carried, announced, logged. But when Jude returned from rinsing his mouth at the basin, there it stood at the edge of his table—a dish he had not heard delivered, on a plate he did not recognize, under a cloth of grey linen that no kitchen in the palace used. The palace linens were white or the Queen's blue. This cloth was the color of old rain.

He stood very still for a moment, the way Master Corvel had taught him to stand when something was wrong. *Stillness first,* the old man used to say, tapping Jude's sternum with one dry finger. *Panic is a flavor too. Never season your own tongue.*

Stillness. Then observation. The dish was small, ceramic, dark-glazed, unmarked by any maker's stamp. It held perhaps two cupfuls of something deep red, almost black, thick enough that it moved slowly when he tilted the plate. Wine, by its look. But no wine service had come up this morning, and the Queen's table took only white wines before noon.

He lifted the cloth.

The smell reached him before the sight did, and it was the smell that stopped his breath.

Not grapes. Not spice, not honey, not oak. What rose from the dark liquid was something older and stranger—the scent of a summer afternoon, cut grass and sun-warmed stone and someone else's childhood. Underneath it ran a thread of woodsmoke and salt, and beneath *that*, faint but unmistakable to anyone who had spent nine years learning the grammar of flavors, was the taste of grief. Grief has a flavor. Jude knew this the way he knew the weight of arsenic on the tongue: bottled sorrow, uncorked, smelled like rain on hot dust and like crying you couldn't remember doing.

He knew what it was. Of course he knew. He had written the entries himself.

Memory-wine. Lot 44, pressed last spring from the vintage of a fisherman's widow in the Saltrow district, decanted from her own recollection of the day her sons came home from the sea alive—one afternoon's joy, drawn off like tapwater, aged in glass, sold through the Hollow Guild to a buyer whose name was recorded only as a sigil. The guild's ledgers described the product precisely: *imparts to the drinker the full sensory weight of another's remembered hour. Side effects include borrowed nostalgia, unexplained weeping, and in cases of prolonged use, displacement of the drinker's own memories.*

Jude had catalogued forty-one lots of it over the years. He had never once tasted it.

His hands, he noticed distantly, were perfectly steady. Nine years of practice will do that. The body learns its composure separately from the mind, the way a soldier's hands learn to load a musket while his thoughts are elsewhere entirely. His body stood at the table. His mind was running.

Who had sent this? Not the kitchens—he would go ask, but he already knew the answer, because no kitchen admitted making a thing it had never seen. Not the Queen's cellars; the cellars kept honest wine and dishonest accounts, but nothing like this. Which left the other possibility, the one that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone:

Someone had sent it to *him*. To the taster. Knowing he would taste it. Knowing what he would know.

Because here was the trouble with being the royal food-taster: you could not refuse the portion. Whatever came onto the tray, whatever appeared at his station, he was required to sample it, log it, clear it or condemn it. That was the whole architecture of his office. A taster who picked and chose was a taster who could be leveraged. Corvel had drilled it into him until refusal felt physically impossible, like trying to blink one eye.

And whoever had placed this dish here knew that too.

Jude looked at the memory-wine for a long time. The light strengthened across the courtyard. A bell rang somewhere for the Queen's rising. His mouth had gone dry, which was inconvenient, since his profession required it to be otherwise.

*You don't have to,* said some small surviving voice in him, the voice of a boy who had once been hungry enough to eat anything and had promised himself, later, that he would never again put anything in his mouth he didn't choose.

But he had chosen this, hadn't he? Nine years ago, starving in the gutter outside the guildhall, he had signed the guild's indenture papers and the crown's commission in the same week, and both documents bound him to exactly this: the portion, whatever it was. Nothing given freely can be owned—that was the guild's founding principle, carved over its door—but nothing freely given is ever really free either, and Jude had understood at nineteen that his freedom had been purchased by his mother before he was born, and purchased things carry debts.

He thought of the ledger entry. Lot 44. *Vintage: one afternoon, coastal, joyous.* He had written those words in his own neat hand, priced the lot at eleven crowns, and sold it to a buyer whose sigil he had copied without understanding.

The sigil had been a closed circle with a vertical line through it. He had seen it before, over the years, on other lots—rare ones, expensive ones, always paid in old coin. He had assumed it was a merchant's mark. Merchants had marks the way men had faces.

Now, holding the widow's stolen afternoon in a dark-glazed dish, Jude wondered, for the first time in nine years of wondering nothing, where those lots went. Who drank other people's joy, and why they wanted it served cold to a food-taster in the Queen's palace.

He lifted the dish.

* * *

He tasted it the way he tasted everything: a measure, a wait, a swallow. Discipline. Stillness first.

The memory-wine moved across his tongue like something alive. It started as fruit—dark cherry, plum skin—and then it opened, the way a door opens, and he was standing on a shingle beach in hard bright sunlight with salt drying white on his forearms, and there were two boys running out of the surf toward him, laughing, and the sound of their laughter was the truest thing he had ever heard, and he knew their names, though he had never known them, and he loved them with a love so complete and so specific that it had edges, like a thing cut to shape—

Then it was gone. Swallowed. Over. He was standing in his alcove in the palace, alone, with wet eyes and a taste in his mouth like the end of summer, and the widow's afternoon sat in his stomach now, warm and foreign, a guest that would not give back its coat.

His hands shook. He let them, briefly, where no one could see. Then he stilled them, because that was the job.

He wrote the entry. That was also the job. *Unidentified dish, dark glaze, grey linen. Contents: memory-wine, lot unknown. Flavor profile consistent with Hollow Guild stock, coastal vintage. No toxicity detected. No kitchen claims authorship.* His pen paused over the last line. There was no column for what he actually wanted to write, which was: *someone knows what I am and what I keep, and they are telling me so.*

He blotted the page. He initialed the slate. He rang the bell, and then, because the dish cleared neither the kitchen nor any cellar he knew, he rang it twice more—the signal, unused in six years, that meant *hold the Queen's tray; query the source.*

The footman came at a run. Jude gave him the message verbatim, watched it travel down the colonnade, and then did what he always did when the world tilted: he went to work. He walked down to the kitchens, past the sweating line cooks and the scullery boys, and asked, casually, who had sent up the second tray.

No one had. Cook swore it on her mother's grave. The under-cooks swore it on theirs. No grey linen in the stores. No dark-glazed ceramics in the inventory. Pell, wide-eyed, swore he had brought only the one tray, and Jude believed him, because Pell was a bad liar and a good boy, and because the truth was already settling over Jude with the cold weight of something he should have expected and somehow never had:

The dish had come from outside the palace. From the city. From the guild's side of the ledger.

From someone who wanted the royal taster to know that the Hollow Guild's wares were in the palace, that they had passed his tongue, and that he had recognized them—which meant he had read the ledgers too closely, or remembered too much, or simply *seen* something he was never meant to see.

Jude climbed back up to his alcove. The morning was fully bright now, ordinary, indifferent. Below, the city went about its business. Above, the sky stretched blue and empty and utterly silent, and Jude caught himself looking at it longer than he meant to—at the pale washed-out stars that no one else seemed to notice were fading, night by night, like breath on glass.

He sat. He opened his personal notebook—the small one, the private one, the one he kept locked in his desk alongside the guild's real ledgers—and on a fresh page, beneath the date, he wrote a single line:

*Lot 44 came home today.*

He looked at the words for a long time. Then, because dread is a discipline too, he turned back through the notebook to the old entries, the rare lots, the ones bearing the closed-circle sigil, and began, for the first time, to trace where they had gone.

There were eleven of them. Eleven lots of impossible things, sold over nine years, paid in old coin, marked with a sign he had copied a hundred times without once asking what it meant.

Tonight, he decided, he would find out. Tonight he would pull the oldest ledger off the shelf—the one with the cracked spine, the one Master Corvel had told him never to open, the one he had never opened—and he would read what was written in the margins.

He had a feeling, cold and certain as arsenic on the tongue, that he was already in there.
