## 2. Ledgers of Impossible Things

The counting room of the Hollow Guild sat beneath the tannery on Vetch Street, which was a joke of geography that only guild members ever laughed at: above them, men worked all day turning death into something soft enough to sell; below them, Jude turned other things—years, griefs, names—into columns and sums.

He liked it down there. That was the truth he never said aloud. He liked the smell of tallow and old paper, the way candlelight made the ink look wet again, as if every entry had been written only moments ago by someone who still believed in what they were doing. He liked the arithmetic most of all. Numbers did not lie, and they did not leave, and they did not die in plague winters. A number was the same at midnight as it was at dawn.

Nine years of tasting had taught him to find comfort where it could actually be found.

He set out his tools the way Master Corvel had taught him, back when Corvel still bothered to teach anything: three candles, trimmed and lit; the sandglass for timing his eyes—he allowed himself one hour before resting them, no more; the blotting sand; the steel rule; and the ledgers themselves, brought down from the shelf in order of age, oldest at his left hand, because debt flowed downhill through time the same way water did, from the high promises of the past into the low valleys of the present, gathering silt.

Tonight's work was reconciliation. The quarterly audit. Every trade recorded in the field books had to be matched against the master ledger, every payment confirmed, every balance struck. It was tedious. It was holy. Jude had long ago stopped distinguishing between the two.

He began, as always, with the newest entries and worked backward.

*Item:* one borrowed year, sold by a cooper of Salt Row to pay his daughter's apprenticeship fee. *Received:* twelve crowns and a promise of first refusal on her marriage dowry, should she ever wish to sell it. *Status:* seven months elapsed, ten years and five months remaining. *Note:* the seller has begun visiting the guild house on feast days, standing across the street, not speaking to anyone. He does not come in. He does not stop coming.

Jude wrote the note in his small, even hand, because notes were part of the discipline. Master Corvel had been merciless about it. *A ledger that records only coin is an abacus,* the old man used to say, tapping the page with one yellowed nail. *A ledger worth keeping records what the coin cost.* Jude had thought, in those early years, that this was sentiment. He had learned better. Sentiment was liability; but detail was armor. The day would come—it always came—when someone stood in front of your desk demanding proof, and the difference between survival and ruin was whether you had written down the color of the ribbon.

*Item:* one bottled grief, decanted from a woman whose husband drowned in the harbor ice. She had carried it for six years like a stone behind her ribs, and at last she had brought it to the guild in a clay jar sealed with wax and prayer, and the guild had paid her four crowns and the jar's weight in bread. The buyer—a merchant's widow from the hill houses, who collected sorrows the way other women collected porcelain—had reported the grief in excellent condition. *Bitterness: moderate. Salt content: high. Weeps readily when warmed.* Jude verified the entry against the warehouse manifest, initialled it, moved on.

This was the work. This was the comfort. Everything in its column. Everything accounted for.

The candles burned down a thumb's width. The sandglass ran out and he turned it over without looking, the way a man turns in his sleep.

*Item:* one laughter, whole and unbroken, sold by a widow of Tanner's Lane.

Jude paused over this one longer than the others, though he could not have said why. Perhaps because it was recent—only eleven days old—and the ink still smelled faintly of the field book's cheap tannin. Perhaps because he had been the one to witness the sale, standing in the doorway of the guild's front room while the widow signed.

Her name was Maren Ostrey. Her son had taken the fever in the last week of winter, the bad fever, the one that filled a child's lungs like floodwater fills a cellar. The healers had come and gone. The priests had come and gone. What remained, when they were gone, was the guild, because the guild remained for everyone eventually, and Maren Ostrey had walked through the door with her shawl pinned wrong and her eyes already somewhere else, and she had asked what a laughter was worth.

Not her son's laughter. Her own. She hadn't laughed since the fever came—she told Jude this plainly, without drama, the way people report weather—and she offered it up entire, the whole instrument of it, thirty-one years of it, from the first time her father held her above his head to whatever joke would have cracked her open next spring.

The appraiser had valued it fairly. Laughter ran cheaper than grief, oddly—grief was rarer in good condition—but hers was exceptional stock, deep-bellied, contagious, the kind other people paid to sit near. Against it the guild posted the price of the working: a fever-breaking, performed within the day, guaranteed.

Jude remembered watching her sign. Her hand had been steady. Steadier than his would have been. And when it was done she had looked at the clerk—not at Jude, at the clerk—and asked, very quietly, *Will I miss it?*

And the clerk had given the standard answer, the one written into the guild's own procedures in Master Corvel's hand two generations ago: *You will know something is gone. You will not know what.*

She had nodded. She had thanked them. She had walked home to a son whose lungs were clearing even as she crossed the threshold, and she had sat by his bed all night, and in the morning he had asked her why she wasn't smiling, and she had looked at him with love and blankness and said, *I am, sweetheart. I'm smiling right now.*

Jude knew this because the follow-up visit was standard procedure, and he had insisted on making it himself, and he had sat in Maren Ostrey's kitchen drinking tea he didn't want while she described, with genuine puzzlement, the strange flatness in her own chest. *It's not sadness,* she'd said. *That's the thing. Sadness I'd understand. It's like a room where the furniture's been removed and you can see the shapes on the floor where it stood.*

He had written all of it down. Cost of the coin. Armor.

Now he matched the entry, initialled it, and reached for the next volume back—the ledger from nine years ago, his first year in the guild's employ, when his handwriting had still been a starving boy's handwriting, all sharp elbows and hunger in the letterforms.

And that was when he saw the margin.

At first he took it for a stain. Old ledgers accumulated stains the way old soldiers accumulated scars, and the vellum of the older volumes was mottled with water-marks and candle-spit and, once memorably, blood—the story of whose blood was itself recorded in the margin beside the stain, in Corvel's hand, with a dryness that had made Jude laugh when he was young enough to laugh at such things.

But this was not a stain. This was writing. Dense, small, pressed hard into the margin beside an entry dated twenty-six years ago, and then scraped away—pumiced and scraped with real violence, so that the surface of the page had thinned to translucence, so that the ghost of the letters showed through from the *other side*, reversed, like faces pressed against a window from a dark room.

Jude held the page up to the candlelight.

He could not read it. The reverse-writing defeated him, letterforms swimming, meaning dissolving just at the edge of comprehension the way a word repeated too many times dissolves into noise. But he could read one thing. One fragment near the top of the scraped passage, where the pumice had missed, where a single word survived intact in the original hand:

*Vane.*

His name. His family's name. In a ledger twenty-six years old, in a margin beside an entry he had not yet read, in ink that someone had tried very hard to destroy.

Jude set the ledger down. His hands were steady—he noticed this with the detached professional interest of a man who had trained his hands to be steady under worse provocations than paper—but his mouth had gone dry in a particular way, a way he recognized. Nine years of tasting gave a man a taxonomy of fear. There was the copper taste of sudden danger, the bitter-almond ghost of dread, the numbness of resignation. This was none of those. This was the taste of the morning after eating something you should not have, waiting to learn what it would do to you.

He read the entry the margin belonged to.

It was dated in the plague winter. The worst one. The winter the old people still wouldn't speak about except to count their dead on their fingers, silently, like penance. The entry was brief, in a hand Jude did not recognize—younger than Corvel's, more hurried, the letters leaning forward as if the writer were being chased:

*Received: one soul, portion thereof, undivided interest, in perpetuity. Sold by: [name withheld per terms]. Price: the child lives. Conditions: collection deferred until such time as the wards fail or the stars go dark, whichever comes last. Note: the seller wept. Note: the child was already dying. Note: it worked.*

Jude read it three times.

The words did not change. That was the terrible thing about writing; it lacked the mercy of memory, which blurred and softened and let you pretend. The words sat on the page in their hurried, leaning script and said what they said.

*A soul, portion thereof.* Whose portion? The entry did not say—or rather, it said, and then someone had scraped the saying away, and the scraping had left only the surname behind, missed by the pumice, stubborn as a scar: *Vane.*

*Sold by: name withheld per terms.* His mother's name. It had to be. Twenty-six years ago. The plague winter. He had been born in the plague winter; his mother had told him that much, laughing—no, not laughing, she had never quite laughed, there had always been something careful in her mouth when she spoke of that year—she had told him he was a winter child, born in the cold months when the bells rang constantly for the dead, and that the midwife had sworn he wouldn't last the night, and that she had sworn otherwise.

*The child was already dying. It worked.*

Jude sat very still in the candlelight and listened to the building above him. The tannery was quiet at this hour; the workers had gone home to their suppers; the beams ticked as they cooled. Somewhere a cat was hunting in the walls. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world that had not changed in the last four minutes.

Except that everything in his chest had rearranged itself, quietly, the way furniture is rearranged in a locked room while the owner sleeps.

He had always known he was bought. That was the bargain every orphan of the capital understood: the guild fed you, housed you, trained you, and in exchange you belonged to it, body and hours. He had signed himself over at eight years old with a handshake and a bowl of stew, and he had never resented it, because the alternative had been the street, and the street in that year had been eating children the way winter ate firewood. Belonging to the guild had been the warmest thing available. He had loved it the way a prisoner loves the wall that keeps out the wolves.

But this was different. This was not the guild owning his labor. This was something older, colder, written in a hand that leaned forward as if running, recording a transaction in which a dying infant's life had been purchased with a piece of the infant himself—and the purchaser was not the Hollow Guild. The guild was merely the bookkeeper. The entry recorded a receipt, not a sale. Someone else had bought. Someone else was holding.

*Collection deferred until such time as the wards fail or the stars go dark, whichever comes last.*

He read the conditions twice more, and each time his eyes snagged on the phrase the way a boot snags on a root. Wards. Stars. These were not guild matters. The guild traded in small things, human things, the intimate currencies of one life weighed against another. Wards and stars were the crown's business, the kingdom's business, the vast slow machinery overhead that ordinary people trusted the way they trusted the sunrise—without thinking, without gratitude, without any suspicion that it might one day simply stop.

Why would a soul-debt be payable against the sky?

Jude closed the ledger. Then opened it again. Then forced himself, with the discipline of nine years' practice, to continue the audit—to work forward through the remaining volumes, matching entries, striking balances, initialling pages—as though the margin were not burning a hole through the whole architecture of the evening. This was how he survived things. He had learned it young: when the world tilts, do the next small task. Taste the spoon. Write the line. Turn the glass. The tilt passes, or it doesn't, but either way the tasks get done, and a man who has done his tasks is harder to kill than a man who has spent the day screaming.

So he worked. The widow's laughter, balanced. The cooper's borrowed year, balanced. Bottled grief, verified against manifest, balanced. Entry after entry, year after year, the guild's whole quiet economy of impossible things flowing through his hands, and all the while the scraped margin waited at his left elbow like a guest who refuses to leave.

Near midnight he came to the last volume of the audit—the current one, nearly full—and here he permitted himself to stop, because the final page held an entry in his own hand, dated this morning, and reading it felt suddenly like reading a stranger's confession.

*Item:* one dish, provenance unknown, served at the Queen's table. Flavor profile consistent with memory-wine, vintage indeterminate, base memory: childhood, coastal, pre-age-ten. Taster's response: recognition without recall. Kitchen denies knowledge. Query logged.

Recognition without recall. He had written those three words this morning with a professional's detachment, and now they came back to him wearing a different face. A coastal memory. From before he was ten. The flavor of a place he had lived, poured into a sauce and set in front of the Queen, in a palace where no kitchen admitted to making it.

Someone was cooking with pieces of him. Or with pieces of whatever his mother had sold. The distinction, he was beginning to understand, might not exist.

Jude blew out two of the three candles and sat in the reduced light, rubbing his eyes until sparks swam. The sandglass lay empty. The audit was done; every column balanced; the guild's books were clean as a chapel. And none of it meant anything anymore, because a book is only as honest as its keeper, and somewhere in these shelves, twenty-six years ago, someone had kept a book that contained him—that had contained him before he could walk, before he could speak, before he was anything at all except a dying child and a mother out of options—and then someone had tried to erase the fact, badly, leaving his name showing through the wound in the page like bone through skin.

He thought of Maren Ostrey in her kitchen. *Like a room where the furniture's been removed and you can see the shapes on the floor where it stood.*

He looked down at his own life and saw, for the first time, the shapes on the floor. The gaps he had always taken for personality—the sea he claimed to remember but could not describe; the father he had never asked about; the reason his mother had gone silent whenever he mentioned the mountain country to the south, going still as a hare, changing the subject with a skill he had mistaken, all his childhood, for indifference. Shapes. Outlines. The negative space where something had been carried out of the room before he was old enough to see it go.

The last candle guttered. Jude watched the flame lean and recover, lean and recover, and understood that he was doing the same thing, and had been doing it for twenty-six years, and that the draft came from a door he had never known was open.

There was one place left to look. Not the ledgers—those recorded transactions, and transactions ended. But Corvel had kept something else, toward the end, in the years when his mind had begun to fail him and his hand had grown uncertain: a private book, part memoir, part confession, part index, in which the old man had written down everything he could not put in the official record. Jude had seen it exactly once, years ago, in Corvel's chambers, and had been ordered out with a vehemence that had frightened him more than any anger ever had. *Some accounts are kept precisely so they can be settled later,* the old man had said, gripping Jude's wrist hard enough to bruise. *When I am dead, boy, if you are clever, you will burn mine unread.*

Corvel had been dead four years. The book, as far as Jude knew, still sat in the locked drawer of the old man's desk in the guildmaster's keeping—Ossen had never been able to bring himself to destroy it, or perhaps had never dared.

Tomorrow, Jude decided. Tomorrow he would ask Ossen for it, or take it, whichever the man's face made necessary. Tomorrow he would learn what his mother had signed, and to whom, and what *portion* meant when the portion was a person.

But tonight—tonight he climbed the stairs out of the counting room into the cold air of Vetch Street, and stood a moment among the tannery stinks and the sleeping city, and looked up out of habit at the sky.

The stars were all there. Every one. Burning in their ancient indifferent constellations, wheeling as they had wheeled over his mother's grave and his cradle and the guild's crooked roof, and Jude Vane stood beneath them feeling, for the first time in his orderly and well-accounted life, like a debt that had not yet fallen due—watched, priced, and patiently awaited by something patient enough to wait twenty-six years, and older than any star he could name.

Behind him, down in the dark of the counting room, the scraped margin waited on its page, and the reversed letters pressed against the back of the vellum like faces at a window, mouths moving, saying his name over and over in a language he almost—almost—remembered.
