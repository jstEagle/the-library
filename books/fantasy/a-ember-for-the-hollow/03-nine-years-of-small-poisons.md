## 3. Nine Years of Small Poisons

The first poison Jude ever swallowed was hemlock water-dropwort, cut with honey so it would go down easy.

He was nine years old and had not eaten in two days when Master Corvel found him trying to pick the lock of the guildhouse kitchen window with a bent spoon. Corvel did not shout. He did not grab the boy's collar or call for the watch. He simply stood in the alley fog with his hands folded behind his back like a man admiring a painting, and said, "You have clever fingers. Clever fingers are worth more than an honest back. Come inside."

Jude had been warned about men who were kind to children in alleys. But he was also starving, and hunger makes a poor philosopher.

Inside there was bread. Real wheat bread, pale and soft, with butter melting into its crumb in a way Jude had only ever imagined while pressing his face to bakery windows. He ate four pieces before he thought to be suspicious, which was exactly the point, though it took him years to understand that.

"You'll work for your meals," Corvel told him, sitting across the table with his own plate untouched, watching the boy eat the way a falconer watches a hawk. "The guild feeds its own. But understand what you are eating first."

He set down a small glass. In it, a liquid the color of weak tea, sweetened, he would later learn, to hide the bitter edge of the dropwort.

"This will make you very sick," Corvel said. "It will not kill you. I have measured it. Drink."

Jude looked at the glass. He looked at the bread, half-eaten still on his plate, which Corvel had made no move to take away. That was the cruelty of it—or the honesty, depending on how you counted. The food was already his. The poison was the price of keeping it.

He drank.

* * *

The sickness lasted three days. Corvel sat with him through the worst of it, or near him—on a stool by the pallet in the cellar room where Jude now slept, reading ledger pages by candlelight, occasionally pressing a cool cloth to the boy's forehead with hands that smelled of ink and copper. He never once said *there, there*, or *you'll be all right*, or any of the soft useless things mothers said. When Jude vomited blood-flecked bile at two in the morning, Corvel simply noted the color, asked about the pain on a scale of one to ten, wrote the answer down, and gave him water.

"You're building something," Corvel explained on the fourth day, when Jude could keep broth down. "Your body is a house, and poisons are thieves. The first thief gets in because no one is guarding the door. But the house remembers. Post a guard once, and the next thief finds the door already barred. We are going to bar every door in you, one by one, until there is nothing left in creation that can take you unaware." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes that might, in another man, have been regret. "Most people do this by living. Living is slow and unreliable. We will do it deliberately."

"Why me?" Jude asked. His voice was a ruin. "Why not someone bigger?"

"Because you were dying anyway," Corvel said, without unkindness, without any expression at all. "A boy with nothing loses nothing. And a boy who has starved understands the value of what I'm offering better than any lord's son would." He closed the ledger. "You will taste for the Crown one day. Queens will put their lives in your mouth. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

No. Not then. Then he only understood bread, and warmth, and that the man with the cold eyes had not turned him out into the frost.

"Yes," Jude lied, because lying well was also apparently part of the curriculum, and Corvel almost smiled.

* * *

The regimen had a rhythm, and the rhythm became the architecture of Jude's childhood.

Mornings were theory: the great families of venom, drawn out in Corvel's precise hand on slate—alkaloids, glycosides, the mineral poisons, the animal ones, the fungal. Afternoons were practice, which meant swallowing things no child should swallow, in doses measured to the grain on a brass scale Corvel kept locked in a drawer lined with wool. Evenings were recovery, and recovery was when the guild's other residents drifted through the cellar—smugglers home between runs, hedge-witches paying their dues in remedies, the fat and cheerful factor named Doss who taught Jude dice and never let him win, because, he said, "a man who's used to winning makes mistakes when the luck turns."

The poisons came in order of gentleness. Nightshade before aconite. Arsenic in grains small enough to balance on an eyelash, building week over week like sand filling a jar. Adder venom diluted past reason, then less diluted. Corvel kept charts—fever curves, pupil response, hours of illness—and Jude learned to read his own body the way a sailor reads weather. The prickling in the gums meant the dose was too high. Numbness in the fingertips meant they'd guessed right, just barely. A night spent shaking and sweating was a door barred; a morning of ordinary weakness was a door left open, and they would try again.

By twelve he could eat foxglove leaves in a salad. By thirteen, a viper bit him on the ankle during a field exercise in the marsh grass, and Corvel's only comment was to check the puncture marks, note that the swelling crested smaller than predicted, and say, "Good. The adder doors are holding. We'll move to the sea-harvest next."

"The sea-harvest" meant shellfish taint, puffer roe smuggled up from southern ports, the strange slow paralysis of certain reef fish whose names changed depending on which smuggler was selling them. These were the worst. Paralytic poisons didn't burn; they *dissolved*. Jude would lie on the cellar pallet unable to move anything but his eyes while the candle burned down, fully conscious, breathing shallowly, waiting for the tide of it to turn. Corvel stayed for those nights. Every time. He never slept in a chair, never fidgeted, never looked afraid. He read his ledgers and turned the pages at perfectly even intervals, and if his presence was not comfort, it was at least a fixed point in a world gone liquid.

Once—only once—Jude woke from a paralytic haze to find Corvel's hand resting on his hair, light as a moth. The moment he stirred, the hand withdrew, and Corvel's voice came as dry as always: "Pulse sixty-two. Better. You'll take broth today."

Jude pretended to still be asleep a moment longer, memorizing the ghost-weight of it. He understood, even then, even at thirteen, that this was contraband. Tenderness, in Master Corvel's economy, was a liability—an unpriced debt that could be called against you at the worst possible moment. The guild traded in impossible things, but affection was the one commodity it refused to stock, and Jude learned to keep his own reserves hidden.

* * *

At fourteen came the year of the antidotes.

"A guard at the door is not enough," Corvel announced over breakfast, sliding a vial of gray syrup next to Jude's porridge. "Guards sleep. Doors fail. You will learn the counter-measures as thoroughly as the agents. Bitterweed for the alkaloids. The white clay suspensions for minerals. If you are poisoned in the field, you may have thirty seconds between knowing and dying. Thirty seconds is enough time to save yourself, if you have practiced the saving until it needs no thought."

So Jude drank antidotes daily whether poisoned or not, until the taste of bitterweed became the taste of his own mouth, until his stomach accepted chalk-and-herb slurry the way other boys' stomachs accepted ale. He learned to induce vomiting with a finger and to stop vomiting with willpower. He learned the smell of cyanide—bitter almonds, always bitter almonds, the cliché that saved lives—and trained himself not to flinch from it, taking Corvel's sealed demonstration jars one at a time and breathing deep until the smell meant nothing more than bread.

That was the year Jude stopped tasting food entirely, in the ordinary way people mean it.

Honey tasted of sweetness and threat. Wine tasted of fruit and of the twelve ways it could be fouled. His tongue had become an instrument, and an instrument cannot also be an audience. Corvel caught him once, pushing an apricot around his plate at supper, and said, "You mourn it," and Jude, fourteen and stupid with sudden anger, said, "Don't you?"

Corvel considered the question longer than any question had a right to be considered.

"I was a taster for eleven years before I was an instructor," he said finally. "I buried three apprentices. Two to carelessness. One because she loved her own tongue more than her own life, and took one untested pleasure too many." He rose from the table, gathering his papers. "Mourn whatever you need to, boy. But mourn it in private, and finish mourning before the Queen's kitchen sends up the soup."

* * *

The first royal tasting happened in Jude's sixteenth year, and it went wrong in the way that taught him everything.

The dish was a quail custard, pale gold, trembling, crowned with candied violets. The palace kitchens had sent it under triple seal. The duty steward watched Jude with the flat patience of a man waiting for a horse to be walked around the paddock. Jude took his measured portion—the taster's third, the fraction Corvel had drilled into him, large enough to catch, small enough to survive—and stood with it on his tongue while the seconds crawled.

Nothing. Sweet cream, thyme, the green dust of the violets. Clean.

He swallowed. He waited out the full quarter hour as protocol demanded, pulse steady, breath easy. The steward nodded and the dish went up to the Queen's table.

Twenty minutes later, a chambermaid came down the service stair at a run, and the word that followed her—"vomiting"—put the entire household into motion. By luck and by the taster's third, the Queen had received only the smallest dose. She sickened for two days and recovered. The confectioner who had sugared the violets was found to have stored them beside a jar of aconite paste, and he was taken to the cells, weeping innocence, and Jude never learned what became of him because he made himself not learn it.

Corvel debriefed him that night in the guildhouse cellar, and Jude expected fury. Instead, Corvel poured two cups of wine—one of which he drank himself, which he had never done in Jude's presence—and said, "Tell me what you missed."

"The aconite was masked. Fat masks alkaloid bitterness. Cream, marrow, egg yolk—it's basic tradecraft, I know the masking profiles, I recited them to you when I was ten—"

"You recited them. You did not *taste* for them." Corvel set down his cup. "You tasted for what should not be there. From tonight you taste for what is hidden inside what should be. The difference is the whole profession." He was quiet a moment. "The girl who died of her pleasures died because she trusted her tongue. Your tongue lied to you today, boy. It said *clean*. It was wrong. What will you do about that?"

Jude stared into his cup for a long time. "Never trust it alone again," he said at last. "Taste with the ledger instead. Everything known, everything cross-referenced. The tongue proposes; the books dispose."

Something in Corvel's shoulders eased—a centimeter of release, invisible to anyone who hadn't spent five years learning to read him. "Yes," he said. "That is precisely it. Numbers don't love you and don't lie to you. Keep your accounts, and your accounts will keep you."

It became scripture. Jude began keeping personal ledgers that same night—flavor profiles, masking compounds, kitchen staff rotations, seasonal risks, the whole vast arithmetic of staying alive in a city where the Queen's enemies would cheerfully murder a boy to get at her. He filled notebook after notebook in his small careful hand. Order was a wall he could stand behind. When the nightmares came—the quail custard, the weeping confectioner, the cell—he did not pray or weep. He balanced columns until dawn, and the numbers held the dark off like lamplight.

* * *

There was one more lesson, the last one, delivered in Jude's seventeenth year, the week he formally took the post and Corvel formally retired his instructor's seal.

"You want to ask me why I never praised you," Corvel said. They stood in the guildhouse yard in the blue hour before dawn, breath steaming, and Corvel looked older than he ever had—suddenly, all at once, like a wall finding its crack. "So I will tell you before you go. Praise is a debt. Every kindness you accept becomes a lien against you, and the court below the mountain"—he said it lightly, a figure of speech, a story grandmothers told—"the courts of this world collect liens, boy. I kept my hand clean of you so that no one could spend you through me. You noticed. Of course you noticed; you notice everything. And you hated me for it a little, and that was correct too."

"Was it worth it?" Jude asked. His voice sounded very young in the cold air. "The doors. All of them barred. Was it worth what it cost?"

Corvel looked at him for a long moment, and for the last time in Jude's life, the old man let something show in his face—not warmth, exactly. Recognition. One survivor acknowledging another.

"You're alive," Corvel said. "Ask me again in thirty years."

Then he went inside, and Jude stood alone in the yard, seventeen years old, immune to nearly everything the world could pour into him, employed by the Crown, fed and housed and armored in chemistry and arithmetic—and so guarded, so thoroughly barred and bolted, that he sometimes wondered whether anything at all could still reach the thing inside the walls that all this protection had supposedly been *for*.

* * *

Nine years later, Jude woke in his narrow bed above the taster's quarters with the taste of memory-wine still ghosting his tongue from yesterday's impossible dish, and reached automatically for the ledger on his nightstand—the current one, volume nineteen, its spine cracked from use.

He wrote the date. He wrote *tongue proposes, books dispose*. He started to write the day's column, and stopped.

Outside the window, the sky over the capital was wrong. Not cloudy—wrong. The grey of pre-dawn should have been thinning toward gold by now, and it wasn't, and the lamp-flame on his desk was burning strangely still, and somewhere out in the streets a dog had begun to howl in a register Jude had never heard from a dog before, low and continuous, like an alarm sounding under the world.

He got up and went to the window, taster's calm already settling over him out of pure habit, and looked up at where the stars should have been fading into morning.

There were no stars. There was no morning. There was only darkness, total and absolute, pressing against the glass like water against a dam—

—and far below, faint but unmistakable, he heard the first screams begin.
