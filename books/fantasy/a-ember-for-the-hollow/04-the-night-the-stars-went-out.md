## 4. The Night the Stars Went Out

Jude Vane had spent nine years learning to sleep lightly, the way a man learns to sleep in a house built over a river—with one ear always listening for the water. So when the darkness came, he did not wake so much as he was *dismissed* from sleep, gently and completely, like a servant told his presence was no longer required.

He opened his eyes to nothing.

Not dimness. Not the grey breath of a clouded night through shutters. Nothing. The kind of dark that had texture, that pressed against the eyes like wool. He lay still in his narrow bed above the guild's counting room and listened to the city beneath him, and the city was making a sound he had never heard it make before.

It was quiet.

Vellum City at any hour had a voice—the watchmen's calls, the argument of gulls over the fish-market scraps, cart wheels on cobbles, the great groaning sigh of the palace hill settling its stones. Even at the dead hour past midnight there was always something, some small animal industry of ten thousand lives. Now there was only a low murmur, rising, like water coming to a boil somewhere far below.

Jude sat up. The darkness didn't shift. He raised his hand before his face and saw—nothing. Not even the suggestion of fingers.

He had tasted forty-one poisons in his life. He knew what the body did when it understood, ahead of the mind, that something was wrong: the scalp tightened, the mouth went metallic, the pulse climbed into the throat and knocked there politely, asking to be let out. His body did all of this now, in its practiced order, and Jude sat in the middle of it and made himself breathe slowly, four counts in, six counts out, the way Master Corvel had taught him with a switch across the knuckles whenever he panicked during immunity lessons. *Fear is a flavor,* Corvel used to say. *Dilute it.*

He found the shutter by touch. When he pulled it open, the darkness poured in unchanged, total, and Jude stood at his window looking out at a sky with nothing in it—not stars, not moon, not the amber stain of Vellum's thousand hearth-fires reflected off the low cloud. The cloud itself was gone, or rather the sky behind it was gone, and the cloud with it, and everything. It was as if someone had reached up and closed a lid.

And underneath the terror, small and cold and professional, the ledger-keeper in him noted: *this is an entry, not an event.* Things that happened had causes, and causes could be priced.

Then the first scream came up from the Street of Kettles, and the boiling murmur broke into full riot, and there was no more time for professionalism.

* * *

He dressed by feel—shirt, coat, boots, and last, out of nine years of habit, the small lead box from under his floorboard that held the antidotes. Charcoal and honey, ipecac and salt-of-orpin, each vial stoppered and labeled in his own cramped hand. A taster who could be poisoned was a taster halfway dead already; Corvel had drilled that into him along with the immunities. He pocketed the box, took his cloak, took the stair down into the guild hall two steps at a time.

The counting room was lit—one of the few places in the city that still was—and the light looked strange now, fragile, like a held breath. Guildmaster Ossen stood at the great table with both palms flat on the ledgers, and around her were gathered perhaps a dozen of the senior traders, and every one of them was looking at the ceiling as if they could see through it.

"Tell me what you felt," Ossen said without turning. She wasn't asking Jude.

"The wards," said Mistress Halloway, whose trade was bottled grief and whose face never moved except at the corners. "All of them. The whole border line. I have standing contracts keyed to the western marches—I'd know if one failed. They didn't fail." Her voice did something very small at the edges. "They were *released.*"

"That's not possible," said Brannic.

Jude had seen Brannic take a smuggler's blade in the shoulder without changing expression. He was watching him now, and Brannic's big hands were shaking against the table's edge.

"Nothing is impossible," Ossen said. "Everything is *priced.* Someone paid to release the border wards. All of them. At once."

"To what end?" Halloway asked. "What does anyone gain—"

The doors banged open and a junior runner came in, a boy of maybe fourteen, white to the lips, holding a lantern that guttered as though the flame itself were frightened.

"The palace," the boy said. "There are riders out. The Queen's sealed herself in the inner keep. They're saying—" He swallowed. "They're saying the sky's been *stolen*, mistress. That it went dark all at once, everywhere, all the way to the coast. Fishermen came upriver screaming about it. And the wards-wards-wards—" He was losing the thread of his own terror. "People say it's a curse. People say it's the Hollow Court come up out of the mountain. People are burning things in the Street of Saints because burning things is *something*—"

"Thank you," Ossen said, with a calm that Jude recognized and admired and, tonight, slightly feared. "Go home. Walk, don't run; running starts stampedes."

The boy fled. For a moment nobody spoke. The candle flames leaned and straightened, leaned and straightened.

"Jude," Ossen said.

"Mistress Guildmaster."

"You taste for the crown. In the morning you'll be wanted at the palace. Whatever they ask you, whatever they tell you—" she turned then, and her eyes in the candlelight were the color of old varnish, patient and unreadable, "—remember that the guild's books must balance. Whatever happens to the sky, the books must balance."

"Yes, mistress," Jude said, which was not agreement, and she knew it was not agreement, and let it stand.

He was almost at the door when Brannic caught his arm. The smuggler's grip was careful, which from Brannic meant urgent.

"Taster." Brannic lowered his voice. "You've read more of the old margins than anyone. Has anything like this ever been written? Ever?"

Jude thought of the redacted line beside his own name, three nights ago, in a ledger older than the guildhall. He thought of the wine that no kitchen claimed, tasting of someone else's remembered summer.

"No," he lied, easily, smoothly, with the same skill he used to swallow hemlock and keep his face pleasant for the Queen. It was the first lie he had ever told the guild, and it slid down like water.

Brannic searched his face, found whatever he found, and let him go.

* * *

Outside, Vellum was coming apart with remarkable good manners, the way a well-fed city comes apart—shutters banging, voices raised, the occasional crash of something dropped and not picked up. Here and there folk had done the obvious thing and lit every lamp and candle they owned, so whole streets glowed like festival nights, but the glow only made it worse, Jude thought, because above each bright street there was nothing, and the eye kept going up expecting the sky and finding instead a wall.

That was the true horror of it, he decided, walking fast with his hood up. It wasn't darkness. Darkness was ordinary; everyone had known darkness. This was a *ceiling*. The world had acquired a lid, low and close and matte-black, and ten thousand people were walking around beneath it realizing, all together, in their separate ways, that they had never once in their lives thought to wonder what held the sky up—until now, when something had visibly stopped holding it.

At the Corner of Saint Ferro a watch patrol stood clustered around their sergeant, and as Jude passed he heard the sergeant saying, flatly, "I've a wife and children. I'm not dying for a palace," and watched, with a sensation like stepping into empty air, as four men of the city watch unclasped their badges and set them down on the fountain's edge in a neat row, and walked away into the dark. The sergeant himself stood a moment longer. Then he set his own badge down last, precisely, squared to the others, and followed them.

Nobody had ever deserted in front of Jude before. He filed it away with everything else, the way he filed everything, and understood even as he did it that the filing was a kind of cowardice—that order was the shape his fear took—and kept walking.

The palace hill was lit like a siege. Every brazier on the approach road burned, and guards lined the terraces in numbers Jude had never seen, pikes pointed outward at their own city. At the gate a young officer with a beautiful terrified face demanded his business.

"Jude Vane. Royal food-taster. Summoned."

"Summoned by whom?"

"I don't know yet," Jude said. "But I'm expected."

This was true in the way that mattered. Tasters were always expected; a court without its taster was a court that had to imagine its own death in every dish, and courts would pay anything, *anything*, not to have to do that. The officer waved him through, and Jude climbed the terraces between walls of torchlight, and everywhere he looked servants were moving with the particular terrible discipline of people doing ordinary work while extraordinary things happen—carrying linens, hauling coals, pretending.

In the Queen's antechamber the chamberlain met him, grey-faced, and did not waste words.

"She hasn't eaten since midnight. She won't sleep. She wants her taster where she can see him."

"Understood."

"And Vane." The chamberlain glanced at the drawn curtains, at the wall of torches beyond them fighting the dark. "Between us—what do you think it is?"

Jude considered lying again, found it strangely easy, and chose instead the truth, because it was stranger.

"I think," he said, "that someone has submitted an enormous invoice. And the rest of us are going to find out what we're billed for."

The chamberlain stared at him. Then, horribly, he laughed—a single cracked note, quickly smothered—and went to announce him.

* * *

The Queen received him standing at her window, or where her window should have been; the shutters were barred and the curtains drawn and she had ordered them drawn anyway, as if the act of not-looking were a form of defiance. She was not yet thirty and she had been Queen since sixteen and she had the stillness of someone who had learned young that a monarch's fear is a currency that spends fast.

"You felt it too," she said, without turning.

"Felt what, Majesty?"

"Don't." But there was no iron in it; it was almost gentle. "You've swallowed poison for me nine years, Jude Vane. You don't get to *don't* me."

Jude came and stood at the window beside her, and looked at the curtain as though it were transparent, and said: "Like a held breath. Like a debt called in."

She was silent a moment. "My grandmother's grandmother," she said, "built the border wards. Two hundred years of queens have maintained them. There are accounts—" she stopped. Started again, differently. "There are accounts I am not permitted to see. Did you know that? A queen, and there are doors in my own archives my seal doesn't open. Tonight I sent for the ward-keys. Tonight I learned those doors don't open for anyone living."

The candles shivered in some draft. Somewhere beyond the curtain, high above the palace, something moved across the sealed black ceiling of the world—a vast slow sliding, like a hand passed over silk—and both of them looked up at nothing and neither of them spoke of it.

"Taste this," the Queen said at last, and gestured.

On the table by her elbow sat a covered dish. Jude had not noticed it arrive. He was fairly certain it had not been there when he entered the room, and he was absolutely certain that no servant had crossed the chamber, and the certainty settled into him with the specific gravity of memory-wine, of summer that belonged to someone else, of the ledger margin with his name redacted.

He lifted the cover.

Beneath it lay a single fig, cut open, and a slip of paper folded small.

"Majesty," he said, keeping his voice level the way he kept it level over arsenic, "did you send for fruit?"

The Queen turned. For the first time she looked afraid—not of the dark outside, Jude understood, but of his face.

"No," she said.

Jude ate the fig. Nine years of discipline put it on his tongue and took it down and held it there, cataloguing: sweetness, first; then the faint resin-bitter green of the skin; then, blooming late at the back of the throat, unmistakable, impossible—

Summer. Someone else's summer. Dust and river-water and a woman's voice humming.

Memory-wine. But not wine.

He unfolded the paper with steady fingers. On it, in a hand he did not know, in ink the color of dried blood, was written a single line:

*Received with thanks. Balance due within thirty days.*

Below the line, where a signature would go, someone had pressed a signet into wax that was still soft. The seal showed a mountain, and beneath the mountain, small and patient, an open door.

Behind him, very quietly, Queen Alissette began to pray.
