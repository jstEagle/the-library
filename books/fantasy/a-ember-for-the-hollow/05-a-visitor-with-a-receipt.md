## 5. A Visitor With a Receipt

The knock came at the hour when the city should have been loudest and was not.

Jude had spent the day since the dark sky the way he spent every strange day: by making things ordinary. He had swept his step. He had counted his tins of tea. He had copied out three pages of the guild's morning ledger in a hand so neat it looked printed, because ink and arithmetic were the two ropes he had always climbed when the world tilted. Outside his window, Vellum Street lay quiet in a way streets were never meant to be quiet at midday—the silence of held breath, of shutters drawn against a heaven that had betrayed its one duty. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, had been barking for an hour, would probably bark until it died of it.

So when the knock came—three raps, precise as coins dropped on a counter—he knew before he opened the door that this was not a neighbor, not a guard, not a beggar. Beggars knocked like apologies. This knock knocked like a claim.

He opened the door.

The woman on his step was dressed in grey so deep it drank the daylight, though there was little daylight left to drink. She stood very straight, hands folded before her like a clerk awaiting dictation, and her face—

Her face was the problem. It was beautiful the way a balanced column is beautiful: correct, symmetrical, bearing no expression whatsoever, and yet somehow implying that all expressions had been considered and found unnecessary. Her skin had the evenness of paper. Her eyes were the color of iron filings, and they did not move the way eyes move; they went where they wished without the courtesy of turning her head.

"Jude Vane," she said. Not a question. A reading aloud.

"You have me," Jude said. Nine years of tasting had taught him to keep his voice level while his stomach catalogued everything wrong with a dish. His stomach was working now. "I'm afraid I don't have you."

"Sable." She said it the way other people said their titles—*magister*, *guildmaster*, *your grace*. "Collector of tithes, Office of the Deep Account, Court Beneath the Mountain." She paused, and something flickered across the perfect face—not a smile, but the place where a smile had once lived and been evicted. "You may have heard less of us than we of you."

"I've heard nothing of you."

"Yes," Sable said. "That was the arrangement."

She produced, from the folds of her grey sleeve, a document.

Jude had handled contracts for nine years. The Hollow Guild's papers were strange things—some written on vellum, some on bark, one memorable instrument inscribed on the inside of a violin—but they shared a grammar, and he read that grammar the way other men read weather. What Sable held was old. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, soft at the edges, and it was folded around a strip of lead stamped with a seal he did not know: a mountain inverted, its peak driven downward into darkness like a fang.

"May I?" he said, and reached for it, and she let him take it, which surprised him more than anything else she had done.

The handwriting hit him before the words did.

He knew that hand. He knew the slight forward lean of it, the way the ascenders hooked left like a person reaching back over her shoulder, the *t*'s crossed with a single impatient stroke. He had seen it on a recipe card for feverfew tisane that still lived in his kitchen drawer. He had seen it on the letter tucked into the bottom of his strongbox, the one that began *My Jude, if you are reading this I am sorry for what I could not tell you.* He had seen it last on a headstone he had paid a stonemason far too much money to carve properly, because some things deserved good lettering even in stone.

His mother's hand. Writing terms.

*In consideration of the preservation of the life of the child Jude Vane through the winter of the Grey Fever, the undersigned conveys unto the Court Beneath the Mountain one-half part of the soul of the said child, payable upon the child's twenty-sixth year or upon the falling of the border wards, whichever shall come first.*

Below it, in the same forward-leaning hand: her signature. *Maren Vane.* And beneath hers, in ink so old it had gone brown, another signature entirely—a long scrawl like a root system, like something that had grown rather than been written.

The street tilted. Jude became aware that he was standing in his own doorway holding his mother's handwriting, and that his hands were perfectly steady, and that this steadiness was the most frightening thing about him.

"Come inside," he heard himself say.

* * *

She sat in his one good chair with her spine not touching the back of it, as if sitting were a task performed rather than a rest taken. He made tea because his hands needed employment and because nine years in a palace had taught him that no negotiation ever went worse for the man holding the teapot. She accepted a cup and did not drink it. She simply held it, warming her paper-colored fingers, and watched him with those filing-iron eyes.

"You've read the instrument," she said.

"I've read it." He sat across from her, deliberately, taking the lower chair, the humbler posture. Let her look down if she wanted to. "I'd like to know why my mother's handwriting is in your possession, and why it's promising half of me to a court I've never heard of, in payment for a debt I never incurred."

"You were four months old," Sable said. "You incur debts the way rivers incur banks. You simply flow within them."

"The plague winter." He said it flatly, testing the words. "The Grey Fever. Half the capital's children died in it. My mother told me—" He stopped. He had been about to say *my mother told me we were lucky*, and even as the sentence formed he heard the hollow in it, the seam, the place where a story had been stitched over a wound. She had told him they were lucky. She had never told him how. He had been a starving infant in a starving city, and he had lived, and he had never once asked the question that any honest accountant would ask first.

*Paid to whom?*

"She came to us in the ninth month of the fever," Sable said, and her voice changed—it did not warm, exactly, but it acquired texture, like silk over stone. "The Court does not solicit. We do not need to. But she found the road down. They sometimes do, the desperate ones. There is a door in the hill country east of here, behind a waterfall that falls upward, and she walked through it carrying you, and she knelt in the accounting hall for six hours while we drafted terms, because she would not put you down, and we are patient."

"Six hours kneeling with an infant."

"She never shifted her weight," Sable said. "I remember it. I remember most things." Something moved behind her eyes again, that ghost of an expression, there and gone like a fish under ice. "It was a clean contract. One-half part, against full preservation of body and mind through the fever and after. No interest accrued. No collateral beyond yourself. Your mother bargained well, for a woman with nothing but a child and her own name to offer."

"And the other half?"

"Remains hers to have given, and she did not give it." Sable set down the untouched cup. "Which is why I am here and not someone less courteous. The wards fell four nights ago, Master Vane. Every ward on the border, simultaneously, like candles pinched by one breath. Do you know what that means in our books?"

"I couldn't say."

"It means the condition has been met." She recited it without inflection, the way he himself read ledger clauses aloud at guild audits: "*Payable upon the child's twenty-sixth year or upon the falling of the border wards, whichever shall come first.* You are twenty-six in the spring. The wards fell on Tuesday. Whichever comes first." Her iron eyes settled on him fully now, and he felt them the way one feels a hand laid flat on one's chest—not painful, just present, and immovable. "The tithe is called. I am come to call it."

Jude looked at his tea. His reflection floated in it, broken by the meniscus, and for a moment he had the vertiginous sense that the reflection was looking back at someone else—that the man in the cup was a stranger wearing his face, a man who had been owned since before he could walk, who had swallowed poison for a crown that had known, perhaps, exactly what it was feeding. Who had spent nine years keeping accounts for a guild whose ledgers he suddenly understood in a new and sickening light. *Every entry has two sides,* Corvel used to say. *Find the other side and you find the truth.*

His mother's side of this entry was six hours on her knees in a hall beneath a mountain, refusing to put him down.

"What does collection entail?" he asked. His voice was steady. He was beginning to hate how steady his voice was. "Do you take it now? Is there a knife? A ritual? Do I sign something?"

"Nothing so dramatic." Sable almost sounded disappointed by his disappointment. "A tithe is collected the way rain collects. It has already begun—you felt it, I think. Four nights ago. Something missing and no name for the missing thing."

He opened his mouth to deny it and closed it again.

Because there *was* something. Since Tuesday night, since the stars had gone out like snuffed lamps, there had been a small absence riding at the edge of every thought, the way the tongue finds the gap of a lost tooth. He had blamed the sleepless nights. He had blamed the dead sky. He had sat up with his ledgers and felt, occasionally, a sensation like a page being turned somewhere in a room he could not enter—and each time he turned to catch it, it was gone, leaving only the certainty that whatever had been on that page was now elsewhere.

"The sea," he said quietly.

Sable inclined her head a fraction. "What of it?"

"I grew up—" He stopped. Started again. "There was water. Near where I was born. Before the capital. I used to be able to—" And here the horror arrived, not with a shout but with the small administrative click of a figure failing to balance. He could see the *fact* of the sea, could state it the way he could state the population of a town he'd never visited: *the sea is large, the sea is salt, I lived near it.* But the thing itself—the sound of it, the particular green-grey of it, the smell that had supposedly soaked into him before memory proper began—was a door painted on a wall. He pressed against it and his hand passed through.

"That is the tithe," Sable said, and for the first time her voice carried something almost gentle, the way a scalpel might be described as delicate. "Half a soul, collected in installments. Memory is where the soul keeps its household goods. We begin with the outer rooms." She rose, smoothing her grey skirts, and the movement was fluid in a way that made him think of ink spreading through water. "You will lose more. Roughly one remembrance per day the sky stays dark, though the schedule varies with the depth of the memory. The sea was shallow—near the surface, barely yours. Deeper rooms take longer to empty."

"And when the house is empty?"

"Then the deed completes, and you belong to the Court entire." She said it the way she might report the price of grain. "I will return in thirty days to witness the final entry. Until then, Master Vane, live as you please. Spend your remaining self as you see fit. Most do one of three things: they run, and running changes nothing, for the road down is older than roads up; they bargain, and the Court enjoys bargaining; or they sit very still and wait to become furniture." She retrieved her document, folded it with a clerk's economy, and vanished it into her sleeve. "Your mother did none of these. For what it is worth—which, in my office, is a great deal—she asked us, at the end, whether the half she kept would be enough to keep you *you*. We told her the truth: that halves are unpredictable. That you might grow up lopsided. That some souls divided young knit strangely, or fiercely, or not at all."

"And?"

"And I have read your file, Jude Vane." The ghost of the expression crossed her face again and this time lingered, and he saw with a cold turn of his stomach that it was not pity and not cruelty but recognition—the look of one professional auditing another's work. "Nine years a taster. Keeper of ledgers for a guild that trades in grief and years and names. A man who swallows poison daily and balances the books nightly and asks no one for help." She moved toward the door, and paused with her hand on the latch. "You knit fiercely. Your mother would have been pleased. Whether it will save you is another entry entirely."

"Sable." He stood. The word came out of him before he had decided to spend it. "The other signature. Under my mother's. The one like roots. Whose is it?"

For a long moment she regarded him, and the silence of Vellum Street pressed against the windows like a tide.

"That," she said, "is the signature of the King Beneath the Mountain. And I will tell you something free of charge, taster, because you gave me tea and asked a sharp question, and the Court respects both. In four hundred years of collection, I have seen that signature affixed to eleven thousand contracts. And in four hundred years, I have never once seen it *dated*." She opened the door. Grey light—or the memory of light, the city's borrowed gloom—fell across her shoulders. "Think about what that means. You're an accountant. You'll want to check the figures."

Then she stepped out, and the street took her, and she was gone between one heartbeat and the next—not walking away, simply *absent*, the way a star had been absent from the sky since Tuesday.

* * *

Jude stood in his doorway for a long time after.

The dog had stopped barking. In the silence he could hear the city breathing—shutters, footsteps, the distant clatter of the palace guard changing a watch no one believed in anymore. Above the rooftops, where stars should have been, there was only a black so complete it seemed less like night than like a ceiling, low and load-bearing, as if the whole sky had been lowered and bolted into place by careful hands.

He went inside. He took down his mother's letter from the strongbox and read it again, all of it, every apologetic line, hunting for the seams. *If you are reading this I am sorry for what I could not tell you. Some prices are paid in coin and some in kind and some in blood, and mine was none of these, and you must never blame me for choosing you.* Never blame her. As if she had known he would try. As if she had known he would stand in a kitchen holding her handwriting and feel the exact shape of the transaction underneath the love.

He got a fresh notebook. He sharpened a pen. And at the top of the first page, in his neatest hand, he wrote:

*Account of Jude Vane, debtor. Assets remaining: unknown. Liabilities: one-half soul, collecting. Creditor: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Terms: thirty days.*

Then, beneath it, because he was his mother's son and an accountant to the marrow, he wrote the only line that mattered, the line Corvel had beaten into him across nine years of cold instruction:

*Every entry has two sides. Find the other side, and you find the truth.*

Outside, the false ceiling of the sky pressed down. Somewhere beneath the world, in a hall where a woman had once knelt for six hours rather than put down her child, a king with an undated signature was waiting for the rest of his payment.

Thirty days.

Jude dipped his pen, and began to make a list of everything he could still remember—starting with the sea, while there was still a sea to start with.
