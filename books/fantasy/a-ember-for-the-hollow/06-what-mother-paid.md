## 6. What Mother Paid

Jude did not sleep.

He sat at his kitchen table with a single candle burning and the contract's copy spread before him — Sable had left it behind as one leaves a calling card, or a knife in a doorframe — and he read his mother's signature until the letters stopped meaning anything. *Maren Vane.* The hand was small and slanted, the letters pressed hard enough into the paper that he could feel the grooves when he dragged his fingertip across them. She had written this the way she wrote everything: like someone afraid the words would be taken back if she didn't hold them down.

He had been four years old during the plague winter. He knew this the way he knew his own hands. And yet when he reached for the winter itself — for its shape, its smell, the particular quality of its dark — his memory came up smooth and blank on one side, like a stone worn by water.

He tried again, methodically, because method was what he had. He had spent nine years tasting death in measured spoonfuls; he could approach his own childhood the same way, a careful portion at a time, waiting to see what burned.

The plague winter had come when he was four. That was fact, written somewhere in him deeper than memory. The coughing sickness, the grey fever, the winter the priests called the Hollowing because the dead were buried so fast the gravediggers stopped saying names over the holes. His mother had kept him alive. That too was fact. Everyone in the tanners' quarter knew the story of Maren Vane, who had carried her son through three weeks of fever to the herb-woman's door and back again, who had traded —

Traded what?

Jude set down the candle and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

There. There it was. Not an absence exactly — absences he knew how to name; he had catalogued enough poisons to know what a missing ingredient felt like in the mouth. This was worse than absence. This was a gap *shaped like something*. A socket where a tooth had been, still tender at the edges. When he thought *my mother saved me*, the sentence completed itself, but the middle of it — the how, the price, the terrible arithmetic of it — closed over like water over a dropped coin.

He got up and fetched paper.

*What I know,* he wrote, because writing things down was the first discipline Master Corvel had ever taught him, and the only one that had never failed him. *The plague winter. I was four. I was sick. I did not die. My mother paid something to someone to keep me alive.*

His pen hovered. Then, smaller:

*I do not remember her face.*

He stared at that line for a long time. He could remember her hands — chapped, quick, smelling of tallow and lye. He could remember being carried, the sway of it, a shoulder against his cheek. He could remember a lullaby with no words in it, just a rising hum like wind through a chimney. But her face was a smudge, a portrait left out in the rain. And until tonight he had never once noticed, which meant the noticing itself was new, which meant —

Which meant the tithe-collector had already begun.

Sable's voice came back to him across the candle flame, patient as a clerk reading terms aloud: *Each day the stars stay dead, another portion comes due. We will collect in the order the contract specifies. You signed nothing, Mr. Vane, but your mother did, and her signature binds what she purchased.*

He had assumed she'd meant his soul in some distant, theological sense — a thing to be collected at the end, after death, the way the church collected its tithes. He had not understood that the Undercourt took payment in installments. That every starless night was a small withdrawal from the account of himself.

And it had started with her face.

* * *

In the morning he went to the Guild house, because there was nowhere else in the capital where a man could ask questions about impossible transactions without being laughed at or arrested.

The Hollow Guild kept its records in a cellar beneath a chandler's shop on Candlewick Row, down stairs that smelled of tallow and old paper and, faintly, of the river. Jude had worked these ledgers for nine years. He knew their weight in his arms, their particular mildew, the way the ink of different decades aged differently — the older entries browning toward rust, the newer ones still sharp and black as fresh wounds. He had always found the cellar comforting. Order lived here. Everything that could not otherwise be accounted for was accounted for here.

Today the order looked like a lie.

"Looking for plague-winter entries," he told Ossen's under-clerk, a nervous boy named Pell who chewed his nails down to the quick. "Tanners' quarter. Anything touching a Maren Vane."

Pell's chewing stopped. "Plague winter's deep archive," he said. "Restricted."

"I balance the restricted books every quarter-day," Jude said. "I've read entries older than the city walls. Hand me the key."

It wasn't quite insubordination. It wasn't quite obedience either. Pell looked at him the way people had begun looking at him lately — since the stars went out, since word moved through the Guild's lower rooms like smoke that the taster Vane had received a visitor, that the visitor had come with papers — and then he fetched the key without another word.

The deep archive was a room within the room, walled off in iron-banded oak, cold enough that Jude's breath showed. He found the plague-winter ledgers by their spines: black leather gone grey, stamped with the year in faded gilt. He carried two volumes to the reading table, lit the lamp, and began.

The Guild recorded everything. That was its pride and its curse. Every trade of an impossible thing — a borrowed year, a bottled grief, a name surrendered, a shadow pawned against a debt — was entered in a fair hand with date, parties, consideration, and witness. Jude had read ten thousand such entries. He knew their cadence the way a priest knows liturgy.

He found the tanners' quarter section. He ran his finger down the column of names.

Vane. Vane. There — *Maren Vane, widow, tanner's daughter.*

And beside it, where the entry should have described the thing given and the thing received, there was nothing. Not a blank — blanks happened, clerks died mid-entry, ink spilled. This was a *redaction*. A rectangle of heavy black, laid down with a brush, so dense it had soaked through the page and ghosted onto the next. Someone had wanted this entry unreadable by anyone who might come digging with a lamp in a cold cellar twenty-two years later.

Someone had known he would come digging.

Jude sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above him, the chandler's shop opened for the day, and ordinary life went on selling candles to people who still believed the sun would rise tomorrow.

He checked the neighboring entries. The dates around his mother's redaction were intact: a cooper who sold six months of his own strength to keep his shop from burning; a midwife who bought a night's worth of borrowed luck. Ordinary trades, if anything done in this cellar could be called ordinary. His mother's entry sat among them like a pulled tooth in a row of healthy ones.

But redactions cost money. He knew that too — the Guild charged a fee to seal an entry, steep enough that only the wealthy or the desperate paid it. Who had sealed his mother's? Not her; a tanner's widow in a plague winter would have had nothing. Which meant someone else had paid to bury the record of what she'd done.

Or someone else had arranged the whole transaction, and buried the evidence of their arrangement.

He copied the ledger reference number into his notebook, along with the date, along with the clerk's initials in the margin — *E.C.*, a hand he didn't recognize, from before Corvel's time. Then he sat back and let himself do the thing he had been avoiding all night: he let himself grieve in advance.

Because here was the shape of it now, undeniable as a bruise. His mother had gone into the deep winter with a dying child and come out with a living one, and the difference between those two states had been paid for out of her son's own self, in a currency he was only now learning to count. And someone had made sure he would never read the receipt.

*You were never free,* Sable had said, and he had heard it as a threat. Sitting in the cold with the lamp guttering, he heard it differently. Not a threat. A diagnosis. He had spent nine years swallowing poison for a crown, keeping books for a guild, being useful and invisible and careful — and all of it, every disciplined day of it, had been built on ground that was never his. Even his survival had been a loan.

Grief came up in him like floodwater, and he let it, because Master Corvel had taught him that too: never fight a poison on its way through. Feel it fully, note its progress, survive it. Grief was just a poison that loved you.

When it passed, what remained was colder and cleaner and far more dangerous.

Purpose.

* * *

The graveyard at Saint Ferren's-in-the-Marsh lay outside the old wall, where the city gave up trying to be a city and became mud and graves and crows. Jude walked there in the late afternoon, under a sky the color of dirty wool. No stars had returned. Three days now. People had stopped looking up; that was the worst of it, the way a wound stops hurting when the flesh around it dies.

His mother's grave was in the paupers' rows, third from the marsh fence, marked by a stone he had paid for out of his first year's wages as taster — a modest slab, because modesty was what fit both his wages and his nature, carved with her name and *She Kept What Was Given Her*. He had chosen the words himself at nineteen, thinking they meant she had guarded him. Now they read differently. Now they sounded like an epitaph written by someone who already knew about the contract.

He came around the fence line with his collar up against the wind — and stopped.

The grave was open.

Not desecrated. Not robbed. *Turned.* The earth lay in a neat dark mound beside the hole, cut with the clean edges of a proper spade, and the slab had been lifted aside and set carefully on planks so it wouldn't crack. Whoever had done it had done it with respect, with method, with — Jude's stomach turned slowly over — with *bookkeeping*. This was not violence. This was an audit.

He stood at the edge of the hole for a long moment, wind pulling at his coat, and then he looked down.

The coffin was still there. Untouched. Its lid sat exactly as it had for twenty-two years, warped with damp, furred with white mold along the seams. Whatever the diggers had wanted, they hadn't taken the body.

They had taken what was *in* the coffin with her.

He knew it the way he'd known the gap in his memory — not by seeing, but by the shape of the space. His mother had been buried with things. He remembered the funeral dimly, through a nineteen-year-old's numbness: the priest, the rain, the neighbors' bowed heads. And a bundle. She had been buried with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tucked against her side, that the priest had pretended not to see because the family of the dead are owed their mysteries. Jude had been too grief-struck to ask what it was. He had assumed letters. Love tokens. A woman's private history.

Now the space where the bundle had lain was empty, and the mold grew in a rectangle where the oilcloth had protected the wood from rot, and the rectangle was long and flat and exactly the size of a ledger.

Jude crouched at the lip of the grave, close enough to smell the turned earth, the sweet rot of twenty-two years, and he laughed — one short, ugly sound that startled a crow off the fence.

Of course. Of course she had kept a copy. She was a tanner's daughter who had grown up counting hides and haggling with factors; she knew that any bargain is only as good as your proof of it. She had signed away her son's soul to the court beneath the mountain, and she had kept the duplicate — the true copy, maybe, the one with the terms, the price, the *name of the buyer* — and she had gone into the ground holding it like a shield over her boy.

And someone had dug her up to take it.

The wind came off the marsh, carrying the smell of brackish water and old reeds, and Jude knelt there in the mud with his palms flat on the frozen grass and let the last soft thing in him finish dying. He thought: *they were here ahead of me.* He thought: *Sable said thirty days, but the collection has been running longer than Sable has been visiting.* He thought: *someone in this city — in the Guild, in the court, in whatever sits beneath the mountain wearing a king's patience — has known about my mother's insurance policy for years, and waited, and watched me taste the Queen's food every morning like a good little instrument, and only now, with the stars dead and the wards fallen, have they come to tidy the accounts.*

He stood. His knees cracked. He looked at the open grave a final time — at the coffin, at the mold-rectangle, at the small slanted signature of his whole life's meaning lying somewhere in a stranger's hands — and he spoke aloud, because there was no one to hear and because some things must be said over the dead or they curdle inside you.

"I'm going to find it," he told her. "Whatever they took. Whatever you paid. I'm going to read every line of it, and then I'm going to make them wish they'd left you sleeping."

The crow on the fence tilted its head. The sky went on being empty.

He filled the grave himself. It took an hour in the freezing light, and he did it badly, the spade clumsy in his scholar's hands, blisters opening on his palms — and he noted, distantly, with the part of his mind that was still a taster taking measurements, that he did not remember learning to dig, that his body seemed to know motions his memory had misplaced, that more of him was going missing by the hour and he was becoming a man assembled from habits alone.

By the time he patted the last of the earth level and dragged the slab back into place, full dark had come. No stars. No moon. Just the city's scattered lamps and, far off toward the mountain, a faint glow beneath the horizon like a banked fire — the Undercourt's stolen sky, burning underground.

Jude wiped the spade and leaned it against the fence, and took out his notebook, and wrote by feel, the letters crooked in the dark:

*Mother kept a second copy. It was buried with her. It is gone. Whoever took it knew the grave, knew the bundle, knew the Guild's sealing fees. Short list. Very short list.*

Then, underneath, the thought he had been circling all day and could no longer avoid:

*Tomorrow I go to Ossen. Tomorrow I stand up in front of the whole Guild and ask who sealed my mother's entry — and watch every face in the room.*

He put the notebook away and started walking back toward a city with no sky, and behind him the marsh wind moved over the settled earth of his mother's grave, gentle as a hand smoothing a blanket, as if apologizing for the company it had been forced to keep.
