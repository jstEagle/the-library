## 7. The Guild Assembles

The summons came at dusk, folded into the beak of a paper crow that tapped against my window until I opened it.

I knew the fold at a glance—triple-creased, wax-sealed with the sigil of the scales-and-key—and knew what it meant before I read a word. The Hollow Guild did not summon its members for routine business. Routine business traveled by ledger-runner and quiet word in the market stalls beneath the Tanners' Bridge. A paper crow meant emergency. A paper crow sent to *me* meant something worse than emergency, because the Guild had spent nine years perfecting the art of pretending I was furniture that happened to count very well.

The message inside held four lines in Ossen's cramped hand:

*All members to the Vault Hall by moonrise. Bring your books. Bring your silence. Failure to attend will be noted.*

*Noted.* In the Guild's vocabulary, that word had teeth. Noted debts were collected. Noted names were sold. I dressed in my second-best coat—the one without poison stains at the cuffs, though every coat I owned had some history soaked into its seams—and went out into a city that had forgotten how night was supposed to look.

* * *

Nine days since the stars died. Nine days of torch-smoke haze where constellations should have been, of sailors refusing to leave harbor, of the Temple of Hours ringing its bells wrong because the bell-ringers calibrated their hours by the sky and the sky had stopped cooperating. The moon still hung up there, but it hung wrong—a coin nailed to black velvet, giving light but no comfort, as if it too were waiting for instructions.

The streets between my lodging and the Guildhall were crowded with people who had no better place to be. Fear does that. It empties the churches and fills the alleys. I passed a woman selling bottled breath—legitimate trade, common as salt before the darkness—and her stall was mobbed. Behind her, a man hawked charms against forgetting, little tin boxes with nothing inside them, and people bought those too. When the sky goes out, the city will purchase anything that promises to hold.

The Guildhall itself crouched at the end of Candlemakers' Row like a toad that had learned architecture: squat, wide, older than the street around it, its windows lit with the particular amber of tallow candles burned down to stubs because no one had time to trim them properly. I gave my name at the door—not my name, precisely; my function. *Jude Vane, taster, keeper of the secondary ledgers.* The door-warden, a broad woman named Hess whose knuckles were scarred from years of cracking smuggled lockboxes, looked at me a moment longer than she needed to.

"You're wanted in the front rows," she said. "Ossen's orders."

That was new. For nine years I had stood at the back of every gathering, close enough to the door that leaving required no one's permission and no one's notice. Front rows were for principals—for traders with inventories, for the men and women whose signatures moved through the books like fish through water. I said, "Is there a reason?"

"There's always a reason." Hess stepped aside. "Whether you're told it is another matter."

* * *

The Vault Hall had been a wine cellar once, back when the building belonged to a merchant family that had traded its own generations into comfortable oblivion. Now it was all iron shelving and locked cabinets rising three stories into the dark, each shelf holding its inventory of impossible things under glass and ward-cloth: jars of borrowed summers, stoppered screams, a wedding vow kept in amber, forty years of someone's patience measured out in thimblefuls. The air tasted of dust and cold iron and, underneath, faintly sweet—the smell of things being preserved past their natural deaths.

By the time I took my assigned seat, the hall held perhaps sixty members. I recognized most of them the way a clerk recognizes handwriting: Master Quillam of the years-trade, his fingers stained silver from handling longevity vials; the sisters Vetch, who dealt in griefs and wore matching mourning they hadn't actually earned; old Tam Broadstairs, who brokered names and hadn't spoken above a whisper in a decade, on principle. They sat in their usual clusters, and the clusters were wrong tonight—pulled tight, shoulders angled inward, conversations conducted behind cupped hands.

Every one of them glanced at me when I sat down. Every one of them looked away too quickly.

I had been a member of this Guild for nine years and a stranger to it for nine years, and I had told myself the arrangement suited me. Furniture is not asked to explain itself. Furniture is not invited into plots. But sitting in the front row, feeling sixty pairs of eyes flick over me like appraisers' thumbs across a coin, I understood for the first time that the arrangement had never been mine to choose. Belonging here had always been conditional, and the condition—the one written nowhere but enforced everywhere—was that I know exactly as much as my ledgers required and not one syllable more.

Ossen came down the central stair at moonrise exactly.

Guildmaster Ossen was seventy if he was a day, built like a rain barrel, with a voice that could have carried orders across a battlefield and a face that suggested he'd tried it once. He carried no book. That alone set the hall murmuring; Ossen without his master-ledger was like the tide without the moon. He mounted the low dais, gripped its edge with both hands, and let the silence ripen before he spoke.

"Three nights ago," he said, "the Ferrier widow came to the exchange window to renew her son's borrowed year. The boy was sick with lungfever; she'd bought him twelve months of a strong man's health, standard terms, renewal due at winter's end. She laid her coin on the counter." He paused. "The vial was empty."

Murmurs. Quillam, whose department that was, went grey.

"Not drunk," Ossen continued. "Not spilled. *Empty*. As if the year inside it had simply—left. Gone home to whoever owned it originally, or gone nowhere at all. And she is not alone. We have eleven open contracts on borrowed years in this city. Seven of them have failed since the stars went dark. The debtors are demanding restitution. Two of them are demanding it loudly, in front of witnesses, using words like *fraud*."

"The wards," someone called from the middle rows. "It's the wards. Whatever broke them took the strength out of everything we—"

"The wards are Crown business," Ossen said, in the tone of a man closing a door. "We do not speculate about Crown business in this hall. What concerns us—" he lifted one thick finger— "is that our inventory is failing. All of it. Across every category."

He nodded toward the shelves, and a junior clerk scurried up a ladder and pulled back the ward-cloth from a display case so the whole hall could see: a row of small blue bottles, each sealed with the Vetch sisters' signature wax.

"Bottled grief," Ossen said. "Stable product. Grief keeps the way honey keeps—we've sold it for two hundred years and never had a bottle crack. There are thirty bottles in that case. Yesterday morning there were thirty bottles in that case, intact. This afternoon—" he gestured, and the clerk tilted the case toward the candlelight, and I saw it, saw everyone see it: hairline weeps of condensation running down the inside of the glass, blue tears leaking through seals that had held for decades, pooling at the bottom of the case in a thin sad shimmer. "They're leaking. Not breaking. *Leaking*. As if whatever's inside has remembered it belongs to somebody."

The sisters Vetch stood up together, an eerie mirrored motion, and the elder one said, "We'll recall the stock. Full refund, re-bottle, re-seal—"

"With what?" Ossen asked, not unkindly. "Grief isn't made anymore, mistress. Grief is *harvested*, and the harvest requires—" he stopped himself, which was unlike him, and I watched him decide how much truth this room could carry. "The harvest requires conditions," he finished. "And the conditions have changed."

* * *

He went down the list then, and the list was long, and each item landed in the hall like a stone dropped into a well whose bottom we could no longer guess at.

A memory-dealer in Saltmarket had opened his strongbox to find his stock of afternoons gone bland—all the flavor boiled out of them, sunshine without warmth, the taste of other people's happiness reduced to description. A contract of protection, paid for in blood by a dockside family, had simply lapsed; the family's enemies had walked through charms that should have blistered them. Tam Broadstairs reported that three names he held in escrow had begun to *answer* when spoken—as if the people who'd sold them were reaching back through the transaction, trying to take them home.

"And last," Ossen said, and his voice changed, and I felt the change in my chest before I understood it, "there is the matter of the tithe."

The hall went very still.

"You will have heard the stories. A collector walking the city. Pale coat. Carries papers. Asks questions at doors—not threats, questions. *Who lives here. How long. What do they owe, and to whom.*" He looked out over the assembled members, and then, deliberately, terribly, his gaze found me in the front row and stayed there. "The Undercourt has not sent a collector above ground in living memory. The treaties forbid it. But the treaties presumed the border wards would hold, and the wards are down, and the stories say the collector has been asking after one particular name."

Sixty people did not turn to look at me. That was the horror of it—they were too well-trained even for that. Sixty people became exquisitely, elaborately interested in their own hands, their own candles, the middle distance, while every line of their attention bent toward my chair like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"Guild policy," Ossen said, "in the event of Undercourt inquiry, is non-engagement. We do not confirm holdings. We do not deny them. We refer all parties to the original instruments and we protect the anonymity of our clients, living or otherwise. That policy stands." He gathered the dais edge again. "But I will say this once, plainly, because half of you are frightened and the other half are greedy and both halves make mistakes when frightened or greedy. If any member of this Guild has knowledge bearing on the collector's inquiry—that member will bring that knowledge to *me*. Tonight. Before the collector brings it to anyone else. Because I promise you, whatever the Undercourt wants, it will not stop at wanting. And when it takes, it takes from the nearest hand first, and the nearest hands—" his eyes swept the hall, and I watched sixty merchants discover simultaneously that they had been standing next to a lit fuse for nine years— "are ours."

* * *

The meeting dissolved into knots of whispered conversation, and I stayed in my chair because standing up felt like admitting the target painted on me was real.

Brannic found me anyway. He always did—he had a smuggler's gift for moving through crowds without disturbing them, appearing at my shoulder with a tankard of small beer and the expression of a man who'd rather be anywhere else and had decided, with great effort, to be here instead.

"You heard the list," he said quietly.

"I wrote half of it," I said. "The failure reports come through the secondary ledgers. Years, griefs, protections—I've been balancing losses for six days. The numbers don't work, Brannic. It's not depreciation. Depreciation follows rules. This is—" I searched for the word and hated the one I found. "*Repossession.* Everything we hold, everywhere, is being called in at once."

"By whom?"

"I don't know. Something older than the contracts, maybe. Or something the contracts were always pointing toward, and we just never read the fine print far enough down." I turned the tankard in my hands. Around us the hall emptied shelf by shelf, members collecting their coats and their courage and slipping out into the starless dark. "There's an entry I can't place. Every failed asset in the last nine days traces back through a clearing-house notation I've never seen before. A mark like a closed eye. It appears in the margins going back further than I've had time to check."

"How far have you checked?"

"Forty years."

Brannic drank. His hands, I noticed, were not steady, and Brannic's hands were the steadiest thing about him; I'd watched them pick a locked strongbox in a rolling river barge during a storm. "My mother's contracts are older than that," he said at last. "She dealt in the plague years. Everything from the plague years went strange."

Everything from the plague years. I thought of my mother, whom I thought of rarely and carefully, the way you touch a bruise to learn its borders. Plague winter. A fever I was never supposed to survive. A grave I visited every year on the same day because ritual was easier than grief.

"Jude." Brannic set down the tankard. He was looking at me with an expression I'd never seen on him—pity, maybe, or its smuggler's cousin, guilt. "The collector. The pale coat. You've seen her."

It wasn't a question. I realized, with a lurch, that it didn't need to be. "How?"

"Because she was asking at the docks yesterday. Asking after ships that ran the mountain road twenty-six years ago. Asking who traveled them, and why, and whether any of them carried child." He held my eyes. "And because Ossen called you to the front row tonight, and Ossen hasn't looked anyone in the eye in fifteen years unless he was measuring them for a coffin."

Across the hall, the last members were leaving. Ossen remained on the dais, talking low with Hess the door-warden, and as I watched, he turned and looked at me again—directly, deliberately, the way a man looks at a debt he has known about for a very long time and hoped, against all his professional instincts, would never come due.

"Go home," Brannic said softly. "Lock your door. Write down everything you remember, starting tonight, starting now. Trust me on that much."

"Why?" I asked. "What happens to memories?"

But Brannic only shook his head, already moving toward the door, already becoming one more shadow among shadows, and behind him Ossen descended from the dais and crossed the emptying hall toward me with the master-ledger finally in his hands, held out before him like something that might need to be surrendered—or defended—

"Vane," he said. "We need to talk about your mother's account."
