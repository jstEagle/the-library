## 8. Terms of Forfeiture

The summons came the way all the worst things in Jude's life had come: on good paper.

It arrived at midmorning, delivered by a palace page who did not meet his eyes—a boy of perhaps twelve, in livery too large for him, holding out a folded card sealed with grey wax. Not the Queen's crimson. Not the Guild's plain brown. Grey, the color of ash that has finished being something else.

"You're to attend," the boy recited, in the flat voice of someone who had been told to say exactly this and nothing more. "The visitor requests confirmation of receipt."

"Confirmation of what?"

"The visitor requests confirmation of receipt," the boy said again, and held out the card as though it might burn him if he kept it a moment longer.

Jude took it. The wax was cold—unnaturally so, cold as a cellar in deep winter, though the morning was mild and the sun lay warm on the flagstones of the service court. He broke the seal. Inside, in handwriting so precise it looked printed:

*Mr. Vane — Re: outstanding balance, account opened 26 years past. Follow-up appointment scheduled for this evening, your residence, after dusk. Please have available any documents pertaining to the account. Light refreshments will not be necessary.*

No signature. No crest. Only, at the bottom, in smaller script, almost an afterthought:

*We appreciate your continued patience during this adjustment period.*

Jude read it three times. *Adjustment period.* As though the falling of the wards, the death of every star over the capital, the screaming in the streets four nights ago—all of it were a delay in service, an inconvenience the Undercourt regretted and would make right in due course.

He thought of the ledgers down in the guild vault, the columns he had balanced for nine years, and understood with a lurch that he knew this tone. He had written letters like this himself. *We regret the delay in delivery of your purchased year. Your grief, bottled per your specification, remains secure.* Bureaucracy was the same everywhere, he realized. It was the language people spoke when they wanted you to feel that what was happening to you was procedure rather than violence.

That was the most frightening thing he had learned all week, and he had learned a great many frightening things.

He spent the day doing what he always did when terror came: he worked. He tasted the Queen's midday meal—the kitchen subdued, the cooks flinching at shadows, everyone pretending the sky was still full—and found nothing but salt and fat and the faint metallic ghost of fear in the broth, which no assay could detect but which he had learned to taste anyway. He walked the long galleries back to his rooms above the guildhouse and counted his steps, because counting was a prayer he still believed in.

And then dusk came, and with it, knocking politely at his door, the tithe-collector.

* * *

Sable had brought her own chair.

Jude noticed this before he noticed anything else—that she stood waiting in the corridor while he unlatched the door, and that behind her, leaning against the wall, was a folding chair of black lacquered wood, slender-legged, absurdly elegant for a servant's stairwell. She picked it up without asking, carried it into his rooms, unfolded it beside his table, and sat, arranging her grey skirts with the care of a woman settling in for a long afternoon of accounts.

"You'll forgive the informality," she said. "Your guild's meeting rooms are under seal. I checked."

"Under seal?"

"Guildmaster Ossen invoked the old protections at moonrise." Her mouth curved—not quite a smile; more the memory of one, worn smooth by centuries of use. "Wards within wards. Very thorough. Also very futile, but I admire the craftsmanship. Shall we begin?"

She produced, from some fold of her clothing that could not logically have held it, a document.

Jude had seen ten thousand contracts in his life. He had drafted them, witnessed them, filed them, watched desperate people sign them with shaking hands and steady eyes. This one was different in only one respect, and the difference was enough to stop his breath: the paper was old. Old the way stone is old. The vellum had gone the color of old teeth, soft at the edges, and yet the ink upon it was black as the night overhead, wet-looking, fresh, as though the words had been written an hour ago by a hand that had been writing for a very long time.

"This is the original," Sable said. "Or rather—it is the original. Copies degrade. Truths do not."

She turned it toward him, and Jude made himself look.

The header read, in a formal script two centuries out of fashion: *Instrument of Tithe, Undercourt of the Hollow Mountain, against the account of one Maren Vane, of the parish of Saltmere, for services rendered and debts forgiven.* Below it, terms in dense, patient clauses. And below those, a signature.

His mother's name. His mother's hand—he knew it, would know it anywhere, the slight leftward slant, the way she pressed too hard on her downstrokes because she had learned to write with a stub of charcoal on slate before she ever touched ink. *Maren Vane,* signed in charcoal-grey fading to brown, dated in the plague winter, twenty-six years ago.

"She couldn't write with ink that winter," Jude said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it in equal measure. "There was no ink to be had in Saltmere. Everything was charcoal. Everything was slate."

"Which is why the instrument records it faithfully." Sable tapped the signature with one pale finger. "We do not falsify our documents, Mr. Vane. Falsification implies doubt. We have never doubted."

"And what does it say?" He already knew. He had known since she first stood in his doorway with her receipt. But knowing a thing and hearing its terms read aloud were different animals, and only one of them could be survived sitting down. He sat.

Sable read. She read well—no theatrics, no relish, just the clear unhurried cadence of a clerk who respected the text. That was somehow worse than gloating would have been.

"*In consideration of the preservation of the life of her issue, to wit: one male child, born the third of Frostmonth, the said Maren Vane conveys unto the Undercourt the whole of the issue's soul, in trust, deliverable upon the issue's twenty-sixth year, or upon the failure of the border wards, whichever shall first occur.*"

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, in the guildhall, a shutter banged in a wind he could not feel up here.

"Whichever shall first occur," Jude repeated.

"The wards failed four nights ago." Sable folded her hands. "You are twenty-six years and some months old. Both conditions, as it happens, are met. The court is not greedy, Mr. Vane. It does not collect twice. Once is sufficient."

"And if I refuse?"

"Refusal is not among the options. It was never among the options. Refusal is a word for people who hold collateral." She said it without cruelty, which was the cruelest way to say it. "However. The court recognizes that transitions of this kind go more smoothly with preparation. So it has extended to you a courtesy."

She drew a second document from the same impossible fold—this one new, crisp, ordinary-looking, and therefore obscene next to its elder. She slid it across the table.

"Thirty days," she said. "From tonight. At the end of thirty days, the tithe completes, and you pass wholly into the court's keeping. During the interval, you remain—technically—your own. Free to settle affairs. Free to say farewells. Free, if you wish, to attempt whatever your guild friends are whispering about behind their sealed doors." Her eyes lifted to his. "The court finds hope useful. Hope keeps debtors lucid. Lucid debtors sign cleaner."

Jude looked down at the new document. The terms were printed in the Guild's own house style—he recognized the typeface, the margins, the clause numbering. Someone had set this page using the Hollow Guild's presses. The thought arrived cold and complete: *they have been inside for years.*

"What is the courtesy?" he asked. "There's always a price on a courtesy. I keep the books. I know how courtesies are priced."

"No price." Sable rose, smoothed her skirts, began folding her ancient chair with small precise movements. "Only a term you should understand, so that you do not mistake it for malice. The stars are dead, Mr. Vane. The sky is a ledger with nothing written in it. Something must hold the light while the accounts are settled." She paused at the door, and for a moment—only a moment—the courtesy dropped from her face like a mask slipping its cord, and what was underneath was tired. Anciently, unbearably tired. "Each night the stars stay dark, the tithe draws interest. Interest on a soul is paid in what the soul holds. You will lose things. One each night, roughly. Small ones first. You will not know which until you reach for them and find the shelf empty."

"That's not a term," Jude said. "That's erosion."

"It's arithmetic," said Sable. "I'm sorry. I was a taster too, once. I know how much you hate a flavor you can't spit out."

The door closed behind her. The chair was gone from the room as though it had never stood there. On the table, the two documents remained—one old as sin, one new as tomorrow—and outside the window, the starless dark pressed against the glass like a face.

* * *

He did not sleep. He told himself he was working.

By candlelight he built, on a fresh sheet of paper, an inventory. If he was going to be robbed nightly, then he would do what he had done his whole life: he would count. He started with the categories the guild used for appraisals—*sensory memories, relational memories, skills, griefs, joys, names*—and beneath each heading he wrote everything he could summon, fast, before the candle burned lower, before the first night's withdrawal could land.

Under *sensory*, the sea. Saltmere was a fishing village; the sea was the first fact of his life. He wrote: *grey-green, cold even in summer, the smell of weed at low tide, gulls arguing over the drying racks, the sound of it through the wall of Mother's room, all night, all night, like breathing.*

Under *relational*: his mother's face. He wrote her name, Maren, and then stopped, pen hovering, and found—to his horror—that the face above the name was already going soft at the edges, like a sketch left out in rain. He could remember her hands. Charcoal-stained. He could remember the sound of her coughing in the plague winter, a dry tearing sound. But the face—

He wrote faster. *Tall. Dark hair kept back with a strip of red cloth. A gap between her front teeth she covered with her lip when she laughed, which was rarely, which was why it mattered.* He wrote until the description ran to half a page, hoarding details like a man carrying water out of a burning house.

Under *skills*: numbers, obviously. Poisons—forty-one by taste alone, sixty-three by symptom, the whole taxonomy Master Corvel had beaten into him dish by dish. Ledgers. The particular trick of reading a lie in a column of figures, the place where a cheat's arithmetic always grew a telltale tremor.

Under *joys*: one entry. One afternoon of sunlight, years ago, on the palace roof, stolen between tastings, doing absolutely nothing, wanting absolutely nothing. He wrote it down in full and felt, writing it, the strange vertigo of a man cataloguing his own treasure for thieves.

The candle guttered. Outside, the true dark of the fourth—or was it fifth?—starless night lay over the capital like a lid.

He fell asleep at the table somewhere past midnight, cheek on the inventory, pen still in hand.

When he woke, the candle was a cold puddle of tallow, the documents were exactly where Sable had left them, and he reached automatically for the memory of the sea—the sound of it through the wall, all night, all night—

And found the shelf empty.

Not damaged. Not dimmed. *Empty*, the way a drawer is empty after a careful thief, everything else undisturbed, the absence itself neat as a signature. He knew he had known it. He could see the words in his own handwriting on the page in front of him—*the sound of it through the wall of Mother's room, all night, all night, like breathing*—and the words described a thing that was no longer in him. He was reading a stranger's elegy for a sense he used to own.

Jude Vane sat in the grey light of a starless morning, holding his inventory, and laughed once—a short, cracked sound with nothing in it—and then he did the only thing he knew how to do.

He sharpened his pen, pulled a fresh sheet toward him, and began to write again. Faster this time. Everything. His mother's hands. Corvel's voice saying *swallow, boy, and count to fifty*. The weight of the guild ledgers. Wren—no, not Wren, he didn't know a Wren yet, but there was a girl at the market who sold apples and argued about the count, and he wrote her down too, in case. Every face. Every afternoon. Every poison and every antidote and every kindness anyone had ever done him, entered in columns, dated, cross-referenced, because if the Undercourt wanted to take him apart piece by piece, then by every god whose name had survived the last accounting, it would have to take him apart *in order*.

On the thirtieth line, his pen slowed.

Because he had caught it—the flaw in his own defense, the tremor in the column. A notebook could hold the words. But the words were not the thing. When he lost the sea, he hadn't lost the sentence; the sentence remained. What he'd lost was the only proof that the sentence was true.

Thirty days. Twenty-nine now. And the dark outside his window, patient as a creditor, waited for tonight's installment.

He dipped his pen and kept writing.
