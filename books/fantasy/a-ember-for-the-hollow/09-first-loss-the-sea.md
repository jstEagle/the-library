## 9. First Loss: The Sea

The morning it happened, Jude Vane was buttering toast.

He had taken to eating in the kitchen now rather than the little room the palace kept for its taster—closer to the ovens, closer to other people, because silence had developed a texture lately, and the texture was Sable's voice reciting terms. The cooks moved around him with the careful courtesy they'd always shown, half deference and half dread. A man who swallowed poison for a living made people polite. A man being slowly unwritten made them quieter still.

The toast was good. The butter was salted. Through the high window the sky sat over the capital like a dropped lid, starless even at dawn, that particular grey-black that had stopped being night and refused to become day. Nine days now. Nine days since the wards fell and the stars went out all at once, like candles pinched by a single wet finger.

He was thinking about none of this. He was thinking about bread, which is why he noticed.

Because for one half-second—one breath between the knife and his mouth—he could not remember where he had learned to love salt.

It passed. It came back. He knew the sea, of course he knew the sea: grey-green, cold, the smell of weed at low tide, gulls like torn paper against the wind. He knew it the way he knew his own hands.

He set down the knife.

*Knew it,* said something small and precise at the back of his skull, in the voice he used for ledgers. *Present tense, Jude. When did you last stand in it?*

*When did you last—*

He reached for the memory the way a man reaches for a stair in the dark and finds the step gone. Not gone, exactly. He could see the shape of where it should be. There was a coastline town—that much held. There was a house with a slate roof furred green with damp. His mother had hung washing on a line that ran crooked between two poles, and the wind off the water filled the sheets like sails, and he had run beneath them—

Yes. That was there. But when he tried to go past the sheets, out the gate, down the path to the shore itself, he found his mind offering him only a smooth blank, the way a tongue finds the socket of a pulled tooth. He knew there was an ocean there. He could not find it.

"Master Vane?" The scullery girl was watching him. "You've gone white as flour."

"The toast," he said, and his voice worked fine, which felt like an obscenity. "It's fine."

He left the bread untouched on the plate.

* * *

He walked home fast, faster than dignity allowed, and locked the door of his rooms, and stood in the middle of them breathing like a man who has run from something invisible.

Sable had told him the terms. She had told him with the pleasant bureaucratic patience of someone reading a shipping manifest: thirty days until the tithe completed. And each starless day, she had said, folding her pale hands, *one memory would come due.* She had not said which. She had not said how he would know. She had smiled—the first expression he'd seen crack her porcelain calm—and said, "Oh, you'll know. Debts are always felt at collection."

Nine days. Which meant eight memories were already gone, walking out of him silently while he slept, ate, tasted, lived, and this was the first one he had caught in the act—or rather, caught after the fact, standing over the empty space where it used to be.

Eight others. Gone without witnesses. He tried to inventory what might be missing and discovered the horror of it immediately: you cannot audit a book when pages are being removed and the removals leave no gap in the numbering. He remembered his mother's face—did he? He closed his eyes and summoned it, and it came, thin-lipped and wind-burned and laughing at something he couldn't recall, and he clutched at it and thought, *is this all there ever was, or is this what's left?*

He remembered his first taste of almond-and-bitter-clover, Master Corvel watching him with those scales-for-eyes, saying *swallow, boy, and count backward from a hundred.* That was there. He remembered the smell of the guildhall's cellar, ink and tallow and something under everything like crushed violets—the scent of bottled grief, which seeped into the stone over decades. There.

The sea was not there.

He sat down hard on the edge of his bed and did the thing he always did when the world turned traitor. He reached for order.

* * *

The first notebook he filled by candlelight before midnight.

He started with facts, because facts were load-bearing. *I was born in Saltwreck, on the Verig coast, in the year the plague came.* He wrote it and stared at it and realized he could not confirm it. Was it called Saltwreck? The name felt right, felt like wet rope in his hand, but names could be sold, changed, forged. He wrote *Saltwreck (?)* and hated himself for the question mark and kept it anyway. Honest accounting. That was the whole discipline. You marked your uncertainties or your books lied.

*I know these things about the sea:* he wrote.

*It is cold. Cold enough to ache.*
*There is a sound it makes that is not one sound but many, waves arriving slightly out of step with each other, so the shore never gets silence.*
*At low tide the rocks stink of weed and iodine, and limpets cling, and if you pry one loose—*
*(why do I know I pried one loose once, and not what happened after?)*
*Gulls. White-grey, loud, thieving.*
*My mother carried me into the water once. I don't know why. I don't know how old I was. I know the shock of it—I think I know the shock of it—but I may have built that feeling afterward out of cold bathwater and stories.*

He stopped. Read that last line back. *I may have built that feeling afterward.*

That was the true wound, he understood then. It wasn't losing the sea. It was that every remaining memory was now suspect, contaminated by proximity to theft. How could he trust the sheets on the line, snapping in the wind? How could he trust Corvel's eyes, the Queen's tired voice saying *thank you, Master Vane*, anything? A ledger with one forged entry is a poisoned dish; you throw out nothing and trust nothing, and you re-taste all of it.

He was trained for exactly this. Nine years of swallowing small doses had taught him the arithmetic of damage: how much harm, how fast, what survives. So he did what the discipline demanded.

He began to inventory himself.

* * *

By the third notebook he had developed a system, because he was who he was.

Page one of each book: the master list, everything he could verify or nearly verify, written in his smallest ledger-hand, columns ruled straight by eye. Name, birthplace (queried), mother's name (he wrote *Maren*—and then, heart slamming, checked chapter and verse of himself for whether that was real or whether he'd read it somewhere, and found it anchored to a memory of gravestone letters going soft with lichen, and wrote *(confirmed: grave, east road)* beside it).

Then, page two onward, the memories themselves, numbered and dated as best he could, each one written twice—once as fact (*age six, fell from the harbour wall, pulled out by a fisherman whose name I cannot—*) and once as sensation, because he had realized somewhere in the second sleepless night that sensations were harder to counterfeit than facts. Facts could be forged. The specific cold of specific water, entering his chest through his clothes all at once like a door slammed open in winter—either that had happened to him or it hadn't, and either way it was *his*, and he wanted it down on paper before the collectors got to it.

He wrote until his hand cramped. He wrote through meals he forgot to eat. He interrogated himself the way Corvel had taught him to interrogate a suspect dish—gently, thoroughly, assuming poison in every portion.

*The afternoon of sunlight.* That one he guarded most jealously and saved for last each night, like dessert. He had found it during the second inventory pass: a single unbroken scene, age perhaps ten or eleven, lying on warm boards—the pier? the roof of the drying shed?—with the light coming through his closed eyelids red-gold, and somewhere nearby his mother talking with a woman about nothing, prices or weather, their voices rising and falling like the water, and he had been so completely safe and so completely bored that the boredom itself had become a kind of sweetness. He remembered wanting the afternoon never to end and knowing it would and being fine with that, both at once, which is a thing only children can hold.

He wrote it out in full. Then, at the bottom, in the red ink reserved in guild practice for irreplaceable holdings: *DO NOT TRADE. NOT MINE TO LOSE.*

Everything else, he supposed, was mine to lose. That was the whole point of the tithe. It didn't take what you'd give up. It took the load-bearing walls.

* * *

On the fifth day of writing, he went looking for the sea deliberately, and failed, and learned something worse than failure.

He sat in his room and did the exercise Master Corvel had once used for training palate-memory: build the thing from components. Salt—yes, he knew salt. Cold—knew it. The sound—could describe it, had described it above, waves out of step. The smell—iodine, weed, tar. He assembled them one by one, carefully, like a cook rebuilding a recipe from a partial card.

And it made *nothing*. It was a list wearing a costume. He could describe the sea to a stranger and had, in fact, been describing it to himself for five days, and there was no ocean anywhere behind the description. Just the words, and the words' shadow.

That was when he understood the true shape of what the Undercourt took. It didn't erase information. Information stayed; he had plenty of information. It took the *thing the information pointed at*. Somewhere in him there had been a door, and behind the door an actual grey-green living coldness that had held him up and once almost drowned him and had been there before he had language and would have been there after, and the door was bricked over now, and he was standing in front of a wall reading out a catalogue of the furniture.

He put his head in his hands and, for the first time since he was seven years old and learning to swallow, wept—not for the sea, because he couldn't properly grieve what he could no longer reach, but for the eight before it, whatever they were, unwept and unmourned, buried without names.

Mourning yourself in advance, he thought. That was the trade nobody should agree to. Every morning now he would wake and walk through his own halls checking locks, finding some doors gone smooth into wallpaper, and he would have to write down what he'd lost by describing the hole, because the contents were already confiscated.

He blew his nose. He opened notebook six.

*What I know about the hole where the sea was:*

*It is roughly childhood-shaped.*
*It is on the Verig coast, probably at a town called Saltwreck.*
*It mattered. I can tell it mattered, the way you can tell a room was important by the quality of its absence.*
*Whoever takes inventory of me next should know: this was not willingly given. Mark it stolen, not sold. There is a difference in the ledgers. There had better still be a difference in the ledgers.*

* * *

Brannic found him on the eighth day, because of course word travels among people who deal in impossible things.

The smuggler let himself in the way smugglers do, with a knock occurring simultaneously with the door opening, and stood filling the frame with his big weathered bulk, looking at Jude's table. The table had disappeared under notebooks. Fourteen of them now, spines labeled in his precise hand: *INVENTORY OF JUDE VANE, VOL. I–XIV.*

"Guildmaster sent me," Brannic said. "Ossen wants you at the hall. Things are getting worse. Borrowed years failing all over the city—" He stopped. He was staring at the books. "What is all this?"

"Myself," Jude said. "As much of it as I can catch."

He explained, briefly, flatly, the way he reported poison results: symptoms, timeline, prognosis. Eight memories gone. The sea among the newly missing. Thirty-day clock, twenty-two days left on it now. Brannic listened the way big men sometimes listen, utterly still, and when Jude finished, the smuggler pulled out the second chair—the chair Jude's mother had never sat in, in rooms Jude had rented for nine years—and sat down heavily.

"Show me one," Brannic said.

Jude slid Volume III across. "Read the entry marked forty-one."

Brannic read. His face, which was built from leather and stubbornness, did something complicated. "This is the sea."

"That's a description of the sea. I have excellent descriptions. What I don't have—" Jude tapped the table, "—is the sea. And here's the part that should worry Ossen more than it apparently does." He leaned forward. "The Undercourt doesn't just collect me, Brannic. It *stores* me. My memories aren't destroyed. They're held. Somewhere under that mountain is a shelf with my ocean in a jar, and every starless day another jar gets added. Twenty-two days, and there won't be enough of me left to want any of it back."

"So we go get them before the shelves fill."

Jude looked up sharply. "We?"

Brannic's jaw worked. Whatever he was going to say, he swallowed it, and Jude—professional recognizance of another professional's concealment—noted it and let it lie. "Roads are broken since the wards fell," Brannic said instead. "Wards lie snapped across half the countryside like fence-posts in a flood. It's ugly travel. But I know the ways down toward the mountain that the tithe-roads don't show. Smuggler's knowledge." He stood, and his shadow covered the notebooks entire. "Pack your ink and your books, taster. If you're going to mourn yourself, do it on the road. We leave at dark—which, these nights, is whenever we feel like it."

Jude looked at the fourteen volumes. At the red-inked line in Volume III: *mark it stolen, not sold.*

"One condition," he said. "We stop at a graveyard on the east road first. My mother's grave." He hesitated, and heard himself say the thing he had not told anyone: "I want to check it's still there. Everything else of hers seems to be turning out freshly touched."

Brannic went very still in a new way, a different stillness entirely.

"Taster," he said quietly, "I've been carrying something twenty years that I'll tell you on the road. But understand this now—there's no version of this trip where we stop at one grave. Not the way things are turning." He pulled the door open onto the starless noon-dark. "Get your coat. The mountain won't wait politely forever, and neither will whoever's been digging."
