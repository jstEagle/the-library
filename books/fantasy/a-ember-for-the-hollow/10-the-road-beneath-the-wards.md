## 10. The Road Beneath the Wards

We left the capital before the fourth bell, when the dark was still total and the gates were guarded by men who had stopped asking questions.

Brannic had chosen the hour. "Deserters go out at night," he said, shouldering a pack that clinked with the particular music of hidden coin. "Smugglers too. Nobody looks twice at a man leaving a dying city in the dark—they only wish they'd had the sense."

I looked twice at him anyway. I couldn't help it. Nine years of tasting had trained me to read what went into my mouth; twenty-six years of living had taught me to read what came out of other people's faces, and Brannic's face was a locked ledger. Broad through the shoulders, gray at the temples, hands like knotted rope. He wore the guild's plainness like armor—no sigil, no ornament, nothing to name him—and yet he moved through the starless streets with the ease of a man walking his own kitchen.

"You knew this was coming," I said as we passed under the Northgate arch. The ward-stones there were old as the kingdom, carved with script that had glowed faintly for two centuries, blue as foxfire. They were dead now. Dead stone, dead letters, and beneath them a guard post abandoned so recently that a bowl of soup still sat on the stool, skinned over with cold.

"Knew something was coming." Brannic didn't look back at the gate. "Ossen's been selling off inventory for a year. Quietly. Small lots, no ledgers. Guildmasters don't liquidate when the sky's healthy, Vane."

"He told you to take me?"

"He told me the mountain road needed walking and I was the man to walk it." A pause, long enough for our boots to fill it. "He didn't tell me why. He didn't have to. You're the taster. The tithe-collector came to your door. Do the arithmetic yourself—you're better at it than I am."

I did the arithmetic. It came out the same way it always did lately: *Jude Vane, half-owned, thirty days remaining, one memory per day of dead stars.* Yesterday I had lost the sea. This morning, testing myself in the grey hour before we left, I had reached for my mother's face and found a shape where it should have been—a warmth, a direction, the way a hand remembers a banister in the dark—but no face. No eyes. I had written down everything I could still salvage: *she sang, she smelled of tallow and thyme, her hands were rough on the inside of the fingers.* It wasn't much. It was less than yesterday.

So I carried three notebooks in my coat, wrapped in oilcloth, and a pencil stub worn to the length of a thumb, and I walked out of the only city I had ever been free in—which is to say, the only city I had ever been owned in—toward the court that held the lien.

* * *

The wards of the kingdom had always been invisible. That was their genius. For two hundred years they had stood along the borders like a second skin over the land, fed by tithe and toll and—now I knew—the slow siphoning of souls, and no farmer had ever seen them, because you do not see your own health until it fails.

Now they failed everywhere, and I saw them all at once.

We were a day out from the capital, following the old drover's road south toward the Thornfell pass, when Brannic stopped and swore softly. Ahead of us, crossing a wheat field gone to seed, ran a line of fallen light—or rather, the husk of light. The ward had come down physically, the way a fence comes down in a storm: great translucent segments lying in the wet grass like shed snakeskin, yards long, still faintly iridescent, humming with a dying charge that made my teeth ache when I leaned close. Where it lay across a hawthorn hedge, the hedge had grown around it in a season of strange, stunted spirals, leaves silvered like coins.

"It fell," I said, stating the obvious because the obvious was all I had.

"They all fell." Brannic crouched, touched a segment, snatched his fingers back. "Same night. Every border, every boundary-ward, every hearth-charm tied into the network. Ossen's maps say the whole kingdom went dark in under a minute." He wiped his hand on his thigh. "You don't build a system that fails everywhere at once unless someone turned it off. Or pulled the plug out of its heart."

"The Undercourt," I said. "They called in the debt. All of it. At once."

"Or the crown stopped paying." He said it flatly, but I heard the weight underneath. Brannic had spent twenty years moving impossible things across impossible lines; he understood systems the way I understood poisons—not academically, but in the body. "Either way, Vane, we're walking through a country with no skin. Anything that wanted in—" he gestured at the horizon, at the starless black above the hills, "—is already in."

We walked on. By afternoon we passed a village where the boundary-ward had been a ring of standing stones, each stone now cracked down the middle like a split loaf, and the villagers stood in their doorways watching us with the particular stillness of people who have stopped believing in weather. An old woman sold us bread and hard cheese and would not take coin.

"Money's paper," she said, pressing the loaf into my hands. Her eyes went up, past me, to the empty sky, and stayed there a moment too long. "Bread's bread. Go on, then. Don't stop after dark."

"What comes after dark?" Brannic asked.

She looked at him as if he'd asked what came after drowning. "Everything that used to be kept out."

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a ruined waystation, a squat toll-house from the old kings' era, its roof fallen in, its hearth big enough to stand in. Brannic built a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter, and we ate the old woman's bread toasted on knife-points, and neither of us spoke for a long time.

It was Brannic who broke the silence, in the way of men who have traveled alone too long and find conversation a muscle gone stiff.

"How many days?"

"Twenty-eight," I said. "The collector gave me thirty. Two are gone."

"And you lose a memory for every day the stars stay dead."

"That's the terms." I turned the bread in my fingers. "Sable was very precise about it. She's precise about everything. It's the most frightening thing about her—that, and the receipt."

"She showed you the contract? Your mother's hand?"

I nodded. I had not described it aloud to anyone, and saying it now felt like opening a wound to check whether it had learned to close. "*In consideration of the preservation of the child Jude Vane through the winter of the gray fever,*" I recited, because I had copied it into my first notebook word for word, guarding it the way you guard a splinter you can't dig out, "*the bearer conveys unto the Undercourt one soul entire, redeemable upon terms to be determined.*" I stopped. "There's more. Clauses. Schedules. My mother signed with an X—she couldn't write—but she pressed her thumb below it, and the ink took the whorls exactly. Sable let me look for as long as I wanted. That's how I knew it was real. Cruelty that hurries is just cruelty. Cruelty that waits is confidence."

Brannic fed a stick to the fire. His face did something complicated and settled on grim.

"My daughter liked stories," he said, abruptly. "When she was small. Before." He didn't finish the sentence, and I didn't ask him to; some doors you can see the shape of without opening. "Point is, in the stories, the debt-collector is always a monster. Horns, teeth, the lot. Yours talks like a clerk."

"She was a taster once," I said. "Before me. She told me that much on the doorstep, in the tone you'd use to tell a man the water's poisoned. As a warning, and as a sentence."

"A taster." He chewed on that. "And now she collects for the thing that owns her."

"Now she collects for the thing that owns her," I agreed. "Which tells you exactly what happens if I fail."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark country, something cried—not a fox, not an owl, something with too much architecture in its voice—and Brannic's hand went to the long knife at his hip and stayed there until the sound moved off westward.

"Sleep," he said finally. "I'll take first watch. And Vane—" I paused, halfway into my bedroll. "Whatever you wrote in those books of yours. The memories. Read them every morning. Out loud if you have to. Memory's like a taste, in my experience. You keep it alive by revisiting it. Let it sit, it goes stale, and then it's gone and you don't even miss it, because missing requires remembering."

I thought of the sea I no longer had. I had written *the sea is enormous and it sounds like breathing* in my notebook before I fully understood I was losing it, and now that sentence was all that remained—an epitaph for a landscape, accurate and useless as a tax record.

"I'll read them," I said.

* * *

The third day brought us into the hill country, and the hills brought the first hope I had felt since the night the sky went out.

It came, as hope often does, disguised as evidence.

We had climbed away from the drover's road onto a shepherd's track, following Brannic's instinct for high ground—he distrusted valleys now, said anything that hunted would hunt where the walking was easy—and near midday we crested a ridge and looked down into a valley where a river ran bright and quick between alders, and on the far slope stood a shrine.

Not a grand one. A roadside shrine, the kind farmers raise to whatever saint governs their particular desperation: a stone alcove, a carved figure worn featureless by rain, offerings of ribbon and bread and copper coins gone green. Ordinary. Except that the alcove was lit.

Not by fire. Not by any lamp I could see. The little shrine simply glowed, a warm gold light spilling from the stone itself, steady as butter, in a world where every ward had died and every star had gone out.

Brannic saw it at the same moment I did. We stood on the ridge together, wind pulling at our collars, and neither of us breathed for a count of ten.

"That shouldn't be," he said.

"No," I agreed. "Show me."

We came down off the ridge fast, and the shrine resolved as we approached: the carved saint, the ribbons, the coins—and threaded through the stone behind them, fine as veins in marble, a lattice of light. Old work. Older than the shrine, maybe older than the kingdom's wards, a different order of making entirely. Not the crown's network, which had fallen like cut puppet strings. Something local. Something rooted.

"Deep wards," Brannic murmured. He circled the alcove with the wariness of a man checking a horse's hooves. "Old style. Laid into the land itself, not hung off the crown's web. The border-wards were built in the last two hundred years—centralized, fed from the capital, fed by—" he glanced at me, and finished differently, "—fed by whatever fed them. But before that, folk warded their own ground. Hearth by hearth, field by field. Slow work. Generational work. Most of it's been paved over or forgotten." He touched the glowing stone, gently this time, and did not flinch. "But forgotten isn't destroyed. Look—it's still burning. Whatever killed the crown's wards, it didn't reach this."

I put my palm against the alcove. The light was warm. Not metaphorically—I mean the stone held heat like a sleeping animal, and something in that warmth went through my hand and up my arm and settled behind my sternum, and I stood there like a fool with my eyes shut, feeling *kept.*

Because here was the arithmetic that mattered, the first sum in weeks that had come out in my favor: if the Undercourt had extinguished the crown's wards by calling in the debts that funded them, then these deep wards—these old, rooted, locally-fed things—had never been part of that ledger. They ran on something else. Tending. Inheritance. The accumulated small payments of people who had never sold anything at all.

"Nothing given freely can be owned," I said aloud.

Brannic looked at me. "What?"

"The guild's founding principle. First entry, first page, every apprentice learns it before they learn to hold a pen." I traced the vein of light in the stone. "I always thought it was a slogan. A bit of theater to make the trading feel clean. But this—" I tapped the shrine, "—this is what it looks like when it's true. This ward was given freely, tended freely, for hundreds of years. There's no contract on it. Nothing to call due. So it's still here, in the middle of the collapse, burning like nothing happened."

Brannic was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—rougher, lower, the voice of a man reading a map he'd carried folded for twenty years.

"If that's true," he said slowly, "then the King's power isn't absolute. It only runs on what's been sold. Everything that was never sold—" he swept a hand at the valley, the river, the glowing shrine, the whole stubborn green world, "—everything that was never sold is still *ours.*"

"Yes," I said. And then, because honesty is a discipline like any other, and nine years of swallowing poison had taught me that the bitter doses matter most: "It also means the road ahead gets worse before it gets better. If the deep wards hold where the crown's failed, then the places where nothing holds—the villages that survived the plague winter by *trading*, the ones that paid in the only coin they had—are going to be the darkest holes in this country. And the mountain road goes straight through them."

Brannic shouldered his pack. Above us the ridge line ran black against a sky with no stars in it, and somewhere south, beyond two ranges and a pass, the Undercourt sat beneath its stolen ceiling, patient as interest.

"Then we walk fast," he said, "and we sleep in the glow where we can find it."

We walked. Late in the day, as the light failed in the ordinary way that light still failed, we came over a final rise and saw the valley below us: a cluster of rooftops around a well, fields lying fallow, woodsmoke rising thin and straight in the windless air. A village, whole and quiet and welcoming.

And yet from this distance, with the last grey light on it, I noticed what I would spend the next day failing to forget: no dogs barked. No children shouted. The smoke rose, the chimneys stood, the doors were closed against the coming dark—and the whole settlement sat as silent as a painting of a village, beautiful and complete and utterly without sound.

Brannic had seen it too. His hand found his knife again.

"Well," he said. "Somebody's home."
