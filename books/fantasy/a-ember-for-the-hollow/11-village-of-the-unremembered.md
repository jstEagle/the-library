## 11. Village of the Unremembered

The road beneath the wards was a road beneath ribs.

That was how Jude thought of it by the third day—the broken wardstones leaning out of the earth at intervals like something vast and picked-clean, and the two of them riding the hollows between. Brannic said little. He drove their mule-cart with the flat patience of a man who had spent twenty years going one direction and did not intend to waste breath describing it.

The stones themselves still hummed, faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has officially ended. Jude felt it in his teeth. Nine years of tasting had taught him to notice what lived in his own mouth—copper, bitterness, the ghost-sweetness of memory-wine—and now the whole country tasted faintly of that wine. The wards had been fed on something for two centuries. He was beginning to suspect what, though he kept the suspicion where he kept all his worst knowledge: folded small, in the pocket behind his breastbone, next to the list of things he could no longer remember.

He had started the list in the capital. *The sea.* Two words, written in his own hand, describing a thing he knew he had known. He read them each night before sleep the way other men said prayers, and each night they meant slightly less, the way a word repeated too often goes strange in the mouth—*sea, sea, sea*—until it was only a shape with salt inside it.

"You're doing the face," Brannic said, as the road bent around a hillside terraced in old apple orchards.

"What face?"

"The one where you've counted something you don't like."

"I'm a taster. I have one face. It's the face that says the soup is fine."

"Then you're wearing it over the other one." Brannic nodded ahead. "Look there. Smoke. Three chimneys, maybe four. And look at the orchards."

Jude looked. The apple trees were heavy. It was early autumn, true, but these were heavy the way a table is heavy—not with fruit alone but with abundance laid on deliberately, bowls filled past usefulness. Branches drooped nearly to the grass. Windfalls lay in rows, unspoiled, unmolested by birds or wasps, arranged almost, along the furrows like offerings no one had come to collect.

"No crows," Jude said.

"There's that face again."

They came down through the orchard as the light went long and gold, and the village received them the way water receives a stone: gently, totally, without disturbance.

* * *

There were perhaps forty houses, whitewashed, thatched, each with a bench by its door and herbs in pots and a swept step. The people came out to meet them before the cart had stopped—came out smiling, all of them, and this was the first wrongness that made Jude's skin tighten: not that they smiled, but that every single one of them was already smiling, as if joy here were not a response to anything but a condition, like height or hair color.

A woman stepped forward, gray-haired, round-faced, her sleeves dusted with flour. "Travelers! Oh, thank the dark for you—we so rarely have anyone new." She took Brannic's hand in both of hers and pressed it. "You'll stay. You must. There's a stew on, there always is. I'm Maren."

"Brannic. This is Jude. We're bound for the mountain."

Something crossed Maren's face then—a flicker, quick as a fish under ice, gone before it could be named. Not fear. Not warning. More like a man hearing his own name spoken in an unfamiliar accent. Then the smile reassembled itself, warm as ever. "The mountain," she echoed pleasantly. "Well. Eat first. Everything is better after eating."

They were walked through the village in a kind of procession, children trailing behind, adults drifting alongside, everyone asking questions with a gentle, unhurried curiosity that never quite sharpened into actual interest. They asked where the travelers had come from and nodded at the answer without appearing to hold it. They asked if the roads were safe and nodded again. When Jude asked in return—about the harvest, about the war-season, about whether the plague had reached them—the answers came smooth and contented and empty as river-worn stones.

*"Plague?"* Maren laughed lightly. "Oh, ages ago. We were very fortunate here."

An old man beside her said, "We were very fortunate," in exactly the same tone, at exactly the same rhythm, and a younger woman on the far side said, "Very fortunate," and Jude felt the sentence go around the group like a coin passed hand to hand, worn smooth by handling.

At the well in the village square, a girl of maybe fifteen was drawing water, singing under her breath. She had a lovely voice and no tune. The melody wandered, doubled back on itself, arrived nowhere; it was music built from the memory of music, and listening to it was like watching someone walk confidently down a corridor that ended three steps ago. She caught Jude watching and beamed at him. "I'll teach it to you, if you like. Everyone knows it here."

"Do you know who wrote it?"

Her smile held perfectly steady. Her eyes, for just a moment, held nothing at all—two clear windows with the lamps out behind them. "It was always ours," she said.

* * *

The supper was magnificent. That was somehow the worst part.

Long tables under a roof-beam hung with dried flowers. Bread with a crust like lacquer. The stew, dark and rich, venison and roots and something herbaceous Jude couldn't name, cooked by hands that had forgotten a hundred years of recipes and somehow remembered everything that mattered about feeding people. Forty villagers ate together and talked and laughed, and the laughter rose and fell in waves, generous and easy, and Jude sat among it with his taster's discipline locked around him like a coat and found himself grieving.

Because it was good. That was the trap of it. The bread was genuinely good. The welcome was genuinely warm—or it performed warmth so flawlessly that the difference had stopped mattering, which might be the same thing, which might be the most terrible thing he'd ever thought. He had spent nine years swallowing poisons in measured doses and calling it a profession, and he knew in his marrow the taste of something that nourishes and kills on the same spoon.

He watched Maren ladle stew for a child, watched her tuck the napkin into the boy's collar with practiced tenderness, and he understood that she loved him. He also understood that if you asked her the boy's name she would say it brightly, and if you asked her where it had come from, whom it belonged to before it belonged to him, what it cost and who had paid—if you asked her what she had traded in the plague winter so that this table, this child, this flour-dusted ordinary evening could exist—she would smile at you with those lampless eyes and say, "We were very fortunate."

He set his spoon down.

Brannic, beside him, ate steadily and spoke pleasantly and missed nothing; Jude could tell by his hands, which never left the vicinity of his knife.

"They're happy," Brannic murmured, when the laughter crested near them.

"Yes."

"That's more than most villages can say. More than the capital can say, half the time."

"Yes," Jude said. "Now ask yourself what happiness is worth when it isn't attached to anything."

Brannic chewed. Swallowed. "Cheaper than bread," he said quietly. "And easier to carry."

* * *

Afterward, the whole village walked them to the guest house—an entire cottage kept ready, linens aired, fire laid, as if hospitality here were a standing order that outlived everyone who issued it. Maren showed them the washbasin, the extra blankets, the shutter that catches the morning light. At the door she paused.

"You're going up to the mountain," she said again. Her voice was still pleasant. But her hand had found Jude's sleeve, and her grip was not pleasant at all; it was the grip of a woman hauling a drowning stranger out of a river. "Don't trade there."

"Maren—" said one of the men behind her, mildly.

"Don't give them anything you'd miss." Her eyes searched Jude's face with a ferocious, desperate attention, as if she were trying to memorize him against a loss she could feel coming and not name. "Do you understand me? Whatever they offer. Whatever it seems to cost nothing. It costs nothing only because you haven't found the price yet."

Then the moment closed over, the way the fish-flicker had closed over, and she was smiling again, patting his arm, telling him the stew would keep till morning if he got hungry. The villagers drifted away down the lane in the dusk, forty people walking home under a starless sky, and not one of them looked up.

Jude stood in the doorway until they were gone.

* * *

He could not sleep. He sat by the banked fire with his notebooks spread on his knees, adding to the list by feel more than memory.

*My mother's voice singing. Gone—I know the shape of where it was.*
*The smell of my first master's shop. Tar? Leather? Gone.*
*The name of the street I slept on, age nine. Gone.*
*The sea.*

He heard Brannic shift on the other pallet. "Ask me," Jude said, without turning. "If I start sounding like them. Ask me something and make me answer slow."

"All right." A pause. "Where did you grow up?"

Jude opened his mouth. Waited. Felt for it the way you feel for a stair in the dark that isn't where your body insists it should be—that lurching absence, the drop into nothing. "South," he said at last. "Coastal. There was a sea." His own voice sounded far away. "I wrote it down. It's true because it's written down. That has to count for something."

"It counts," Brannic said. "Go on sleeping with the list."

"Brannic. Those people in there. Every one of them paid. You saw it—they all traded in the same winter, they all bought the same years. A whole village survived by selling itself piece by piece, and now they tend each other's emptiness like gardeners. They feed the hollow parts and call it fortune." He stared into the coals. "And the horror isn't that they suffer. That I could bear. The horror is that they don't. They're *happy*, Brannic. Something wore their grief away and left the smile, and I sat at their table and nearly wept for a woman who has forgotten she ever had anything to weep for."

"That's tomorrow's problem."

"It's *my* problem. Don't you see? That's the tithe working. That's what Sable collects toward. In"—he did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way he did all arithmetic, instantly, helplessly—"twenty-six days, minus whatever the stars take on the way, I am Maren. I will greet travelers on a swept step with bread I remember how to bake and a life I don't remember living, and I will press their hands and mean the warmth, whatever warmth is when it isn't attached to anything, and some part of me will grip their sleeve for one second and say *don't trade there* and then forget why."

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, faintly, the girl was singing her tuneless song to the empty square.

Brannic was quiet a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice had changed—roughened, lower, as though something long packed away had shifted under its wrapping.

"You want to know the truth of it, taster? What you just described—the smile over the hollow, the grief worn smooth—it's not some strange fate waiting for you up the mountain." Jude heard him turn over, heard the pallet ropes creak, heard him breathe once, twice, the way a man breathes before lifting something very heavy.

"I've been to this village before," Brannic said. "Twenty years ago. I came through on my way down, the same as you, and I stood in that same square, and I watched a woman by the well who didn't recognize the sound of her own daughter's name." His breath went out slowly. "I know exactly what they paid here. Because I sold the same thing. And I've been climbing back toward that well ever since."

Jude turned. In the ember-light, Brannic's face was stripped of twenty years of road-dust patience, and underneath it Jude saw the thing he had glimpsed only in fragments across three days of travel—the guilt shaped like a person, the cargo he had carried since before Jude was a taster.

"My daughter," Brannic said, "is named Wren. Was named Wren. Up in the capital tonight there's a girl walking around who doesn't know it." He met Jude's eyes, and his voice cracked down the middle like wardstone. "And I'm the one who sold it."
