## 12. Brannic's Price

They made camp that night in the shell of a waystation that had once fed travelers on the mountain road—Jude knew this because a sign still hung by one rusted nail, though the letters had been picked away by weather until only *W YST TION* remained, and even that felt like a guess.

The wards here were worse than broken. Broken things at least kept their shape. These had come apart like rope rotted through, and what was left lay across the fields in long gray strands that caught the starless dark and held it, so that walking through them was like wading into shallow water made of night. Brannic had warned him not to touch them. Jude hadn't needed the warning. He could feel them from ten feet off—a pressure against the skin, faint but insistent, like standing too close to someone grieving.

He built the fire small, out of habit. Nine years of tasting taught him that anything worth having was worth having quietly.

Brannic sat across from it, cleaning his boots with a knife he'd also been using to cut bread, which said most of what needed saying about the man. He was fifty if he was a day, broad as a door, with a smuggler's permanent squint and hands like knotted rope. For three days of travel he had spoken perhaps forty words, half of them directions and half of them curses, and Jude had begun to think that was simply all there was to him.

Then, without looking up from his boot: "You wrote in your book again today."

It wasn't a question. Jude had filled eleven pages at the noon halt, hunched over the notebook like a monk at vespers, recording everything he could still hold—the smell of the capital's tanneries, the Queen's left-handedness, the exact weight of Master Corvel's disapproval. Writing it down didn't stop the losses. But it meant that when a memory went, something of it stayed behind, inked and orphaned, waiting for a reader who would never quite be him.

"I write every day," Jude said.

"You write like a man bailing a boat." Brannic sheathed the knife. "I've watched you do it. You don't write what happened. You write what you're afraid to lose next."

Jude turned his hands toward the fire. The right one still trembled sometimes—not from poison anymore, just from the habit of trembling, the body keeping its own ledgers. "And what would you know about it?"

"More than you'd credit." Brannic reached into his coat, into some inner pocket that seemed deeper than the coat itself, and drew out a flat square of oilcloth tied with waxed string. He set it on his knee. He did not untie it. "Twenty years I've carried this. Never opened it past the first look. You want to know why I'm taking you down the mountain? Not for guild coin. Ossen's coin stopped meaning anything to me years ago."

"Then why?"

"Because you're going where I've been trying to go since before you could grow a beard. And because—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, the words coming out like stones pulled from a riverbed, cold and heavy and long submerged. "Because I sold my daughter's name. To them. To the court under the mountain. And I've spent twenty years learning everything there is to learn about how to buy it back, and everything I've learned says it can't be done. So now I'm pinning my hope on a food-taster with holes in his head, because hope's the last thing I've got that isn't nailed down."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the dead wards, and they sang—a thin, glassy note, like a wet finger on a rim.

Jude waited. He had learned patience from Corvel, who used to make him sit for an hour after each dose, watching, waiting to see what the poison did, and Jude had learned that most truths came out the same way poisons did: given time, and no interference.

Brannic untied the string.

Inside the oilcloth was a ledger page. Just a page, torn cleanly at the spine, yellowed to the color of old tallow. Jude recognized the format instantly—the Hollow Guild's own hand, the ruled columns, the trade recorded in that deceptively gentle script the guild had used for two hundred years. But this wasn't a guild page. The ruling was different. The columns ran the wrong direction. And the ink—

The ink was dark. Darker than iron-gall, darker than any ink Jude had ever catalogued. It seemed to drink the firelight rather than reflect it, and when he tilted the page, the letters shifted, not moving exactly, but rearranging their weight, the way a face rearranges itself when you look at it too long.

*Received of Brannic Ashfell,* it read, *one (1) name, being the true and whole name of Wren Ashfell, aged six years, freely surrendered by her father in settlement of debt incurred.*

Below that, in smaller script: *Consideration received: passage of the fever from the named child to the unnamed. Child survives. Debt discharged.*

"The plague winter," Jude said. His voice came out strange. "That was the same winter my mother—"

"Aye. Bad year. Worst year." Brannic's squint had gone somewhere else entirely; his eyes were fixed on the middle distance, on a night twenty years gone. "Wren took the fever when she was six. Gray-lung. You remember gray-lung?"

"I remember people being afraid of it." Even that felt uncertain now, a memory with soft edges, already thinning. He added it silently to the list of things to write down.

"It killed nine of every ten it touched. Children worst. There was no cure. There was nothing." Brannic's hands, those great rope-knuckled hands, closed slowly around each other. "Except there was a rumor. A man came through the village—came through every village, I learned later, working a circuit. Quiet fellow, well-dressed, never sweated. Said he represented certain interests below the mountain. Said debts could be settled in ways the healers couldn't match. All you had to do was sign."

"And you signed."

"My wife was already dead. Dead of the same fever, three days before. I'd buried her in frozen ground with my bare hands because nobody would help me touch her." He said it flatly, without drama, the way men say things they have said to themselves ten thousand times. "And then Wren started coughing blood, and this quiet man sat in my kitchen drinking tea I couldn't afford, and he said, *the child can live, father, it's only her name.* Only her name. Like a name was a coat you could take off. Like she'd still be her underneath."

Jude looked at the page. At the neat, terrible arithmetic of it. *Child survives. Debt discharged.* The Undercourt's hand was the guild's hand, he realized—or rather, the guild's hand was the Undercourt's, learned from it, inherited from it, the way a counterfeit learns from the mint. Two centuries of trades, and the same patient script behind all of them.

"What happened to her?" he asked. "When the name went?"

Brannic was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped to nearly nothing, worn smooth by repetition.

"She lived. That was the bargain, and they keep their bargains—they always keep their bargains, that's the horror of them. The fever passed out of her inside a week. But she woke up and she didn't—" His jaw worked. "A name isn't a word, taster. It's the handle. It's how the world picks you up. Without it, she was—she was *there*, she ate, she slept, she grew, but she was like a house with nobody home and all the doors open. She'd smile at anyone. She'd go home with anyone. She couldn't tell you what she liked because liking needs a self to do the liking, and the self is what they took. She called herself—when people asked, she'd say whatever the last person had called her. Whatever was lying around. She was a room full of other people's furniture."

The fire had burned low. Jude fed it another stick without looking away from the man.

"What did you do?"

"What any coward does. I sent her away. To an aunt in the capital, far from me, because I couldn't stand to look at what I'd done, and I told myself it was for her sake, fresh start, new city, she wouldn't know the difference anyway, wouldn't she?" A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh in some earlier, unbroken version of himself. "She wouldn't know the difference. Gods. Twenty years, and that sentence still fits in my mouth like a knife."

"And the aunt?"

"Died. Four years back. Wren would have been—she'd be twenty-six now. Same age as you, near enough." Brannic finally looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were wet and furious and terribly steady. "She's in the capital somewhere. Alive, last I heard. Working. Living some life stitched together out of scraps, because her father sold the cloth it should have been cut from. And every year on her birthday I come within a day of riding north to find her, and every year I don't, because what would I say? *Hello, daughter, sorry about your soul, I've brought you a torn piece of paper?*"

Wind again. The wards sang their thin note, and this time Jude thought he heard harmonics in it, many voices' worth, and wondered how much of the countryside's grief was soaked into those gray strands, and whether the Undercourt heard it as music or as income.

"Why show me this?" Jude asked. "Why now?"

"Because yesterday you asked me why the guild's books matter. Why we bother, balancing accounts of laughter and years and names, when the whole trade is rotten top to bottom." Brannic folded the page along its old creases, careful as a priest handling relics. "And I didn't answer you. This is the answer. The books are the only place she exists whole. Somewhere in the guild's records there's a copy of this entry—I've seen it, I copied it myself twenty years ago, that's how I got started in the trade, trying to learn the system well enough to beat it. Her name is written down. Which means it's *counted*. And counted things can be reckoned. That's the whole faith of the guild, taster, the one thing Ossen actually believes: what is written can be balanced. What is balanced can be paid. What is paid can be *freed*."

"That's not what the guild believes," Jude said quietly. "That's what the guild *sells*. I've kept the ledgers nine years. Nobody gets freed. Debts get traded, rolled over, resold. The books don't balance, Brannic. They circulate."

"Then you balance them better than the guild ever did." Brannic leaned forward, and the firelight carved his face into something older than smuggling, older than guilt—something closer to purpose, which is what guilt becomes when it finally stops circling and picks a direction. "You're going down there anyway. Your soul's already half theirs; you've got thirty days and a collector counting. You're going to walk into that court and sit across from whatever king keeps his books under a mountain. And when you do, I'm asking you—one smuggler to one thief of a taster—to find one line in their ledger. One entry. *Ashfell, Wren.* And you price it. You reckon it. You make them name the cost of giving back what was never theirs to hold."

Jude was silent. In the coals, a knot of pine hissed and spat its resin.

"You understand what you're asking," he said at last. "Everything down there has a price, and the prices are real. Sable showed me my mother's contract. My whole existence, itemized. If I open negotiations for your daughter's name, I'm not doing it with words. I'll be paying with what's left of me. Memories. Years. The afternoon of sunlight I've been saving. Whatever I have that they value."

"I know."

"She may not even want it back. Twenty years. She's become whoever she became. A name arriving after two decades—it might break her worse than losing it did."

"I know that too." Brannic's voice cracked on it, and he let it crack, didn't try to hide it, and Jude understood that this was the bravest thing the man had done in twenty years: saying plainly that his rescue might be a second wound. "But it's hers. Whatever it costs her, whatever it costs me, it's *hers*, and it's sitting in a vault under a mountain because I was too frightened and too poor and too godsdamned tired to fight for it. I'm not asking you to fix that. Nothing fixes that. I'm asking you to carry it down with yours. Two debts on one road travels lighter than one."

Jude looked at the folded page in the smuggler's hands—at the dark ink, at the small, tidy record of a six-year-old girl reduced to a line item—and felt something in his chest turn over and settle, the way a lock turns when the right key finally finds it.

His mother had sold *him*. Whole, before birth, to buy him breath through a plague winter. Brannic had sold his daughter's name to buy her lungs. Different entries, same winter, same patient script, same court beneath the mountain feeding on the desperate love of parents who had nothing else left to give. He had thought his story was singular, a private horror shaped exactly to him. It wasn't. It was a *column*. There were thousands of lines like his, thousands like Wren's, two hundred years of them, and every parent who signed had believed they were alone at the table.

That was the trick, he saw now. That was always the trick. Isolation was the interest rate.

"Give me the page," Jude said.

Brannic blinked. "What?"

"The original. Their paper, their ink, their terms. If I'm going to argue arithmetic with a king, I want primary sources." He held out his hand. "And tomorrow you start teaching me the smuggling routes. All of them. Every ward-gap, every tollgate, every back way into that mountain. If I'm carrying your debt down with mine, I want to know the ground."

For a moment Brannic just looked at him, and something moved across his ruined face that was almost unbearably like relief—not joy, nothing so clean, but the easing of a weight held so long its absence would feel like falling.

He put the page in Jude's hand. It was colder than the night air, and heavier than paper had any right to be, and through the soles of his boots Jude felt the dead wards humming their many-voiced note, as if the mountain had noticed the transaction and entered it in some vast, patient book.

"One more thing," Brannic said, as Jude wrapped the page back in its oilcloth. "The quiet man who bought her name. Well-dressed, never sweated, worked the villages circuit by circuit."

"Yes?"

"He had a woman with him. Carried the contracts. Never spoke." Brannic stared into the fire. "Gray-skinned. Beautiful, in the way a knife's beautiful. I was half-mad with grief and fear, and even I noticed her. Twenty years I've tried not to think about her face."

Jude's hand went still around the oilcloth.

He had seen that face four days ago, across his own doorstep, holding a contract signed in his mother's hand.

"Sable," he said.

"Aye," said Brannic. "Sable."

Out beyond the firelight, the broken wards sang on in the dark, and far to the south, Jude knew, a tithe-collector was marking another day off his thirty, and somewhere beneath the mountain a king turned a page.

* * *

He did not sleep. Instead he sat by the dying fire with the notebook on his knees and wrote by feel, the light too low for reading, let alone writing. He wrote Wren Ashfell, aged six, gray-lung, name sold for her life. He wrote Brannic buried his wife with his bare hands. He wrote the consideration received column runs the wrong direction, ask me later, I won't remember to ask.

At the bottom of the page, after a long pause, he added one more line, pressing hard enough to score the paper beneath:

There are more of us. Find out how many.

Then he closed the book, and looked east, where the mountain waited under its lid of darkness, and began, methodically, to count what he had left to spend.
