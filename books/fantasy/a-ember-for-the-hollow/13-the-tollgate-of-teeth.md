## 13. The Tollgate of Teeth

The mountain did not look like a mountain anymore.

Jude had seen it from the capital a hundred times—on clear days before the stars died, a blue-grey shoulder on the horizon that children called the Sleeping King, that cartographers marked with the polite fiction of *here be wards*. Now, three days out from the last broken fence-line of the border country, it rose out of the fog like something that had been waiting for them specifically. Its slopes were bare of snow in a world gone cold. Its stone was the colour of old teeth. And set into its face, at the end of a road that no map admitted to drawing, was a gate.

"It's real," Brannic said, in the voice of a man who had spent twenty years hoping he was wrong. "Gods below. It's actually real."

"You've been here before," Jude said.

"I've been to where it isn't." Brannic shifted the pack on his shoulders and did not look at the gate while he said it, the way a man does not look at a wound while describing it. "There's a difference. Smugglers' lore says the mountain has a hundred mouths and none of them open twice for the same man. We're about to test whether the lore is kind or merely thorough."

They came down the last stretch of road as the light failed—not sunset, because there had been no proper sun since the stars went out, only a bruised grey dimness that never quite committed to either day or night—and the gate grew as they approached until it stopped being a thing in the landscape and became the landscape. Two pillars of black stone, each carved—or grown; Jude could not decide which disturbed him more—with rows upon rows of teeth. Not fangs. Teeth. Molars and incisors and canines, human teeth by their size, thousands of them, set in spirals that drew the eye inward and would not let it go. Between the pillars hung a door of nothing at all: an absence shaped like a doorway, faintly warm-looking, like the dark behind a lit window in winter.

The air around it smelled of copper and, beneath the copper, of something older and sweeter—the smell of a room where someone has been weeping for a very long time.

Jude stopped ten paces out. Habit made him catalogue first: the pillars were warm to the eye, if such a thing could be said; the road before the gate was worn smooth in a narrow path, as though many had come this way but few had stood long; there was no dust anywhere, which in a dead country with dead stars was its own kind of testimony. Something kept this place. Something tidy.

"Is there a keeper?" he asked quietly.

"There's a toll," Brannic said. "That's keeper enough."

As if the words were a key, the darkness between the pillars moved. It gathered itself the way breath gathers on cold glass, and became a voice—not heard so much as remembered, arriving already inside the skull, courteous as a clerk and patient as rot.

***Two travellers. One road. The way is open to those who pay.***

Brannic's hand found the knife at his belt and Jude touched his arm, once, and the hand fell away.

"What does it take?" Jude asked the dark.

***What you cannot replace.*** The voice did not hurry. ***Not what you love—that is common currency, and love regrows like grass. Not what you fear—we have no use for fear; it is given away freely every day. What you cannot replace. Bring it forward and the gate will weigh it. If it is light, the gate stays shut.***

"And if we refuse?"

***Then you are welcome to stand here.*** A pause with the texture of a smile. ***Many do. The road is smooth. The view does not change.***

* * *

They made camp within sight of the gate, because there seemed little point making it anywhere else, and because Jude wanted to watch the thing through the grey hours and learn its habits, the way he had once learned the habits of poisons. Brannic built a fire from scrub-pine that burned with a sullen, starless light and cooked the last of the smoked fish without appetite.

"The daughter's name," Jude said, after a while. "If it asks you for something you can't replace—"

"I know what I'll give it."

"Brannic."

"I know." The smuggler turned the fish on its stick with unnecessary care. "Twenty years I've carried her name's absence around like a stone in my boot. You get used to stones. But every step still hurts, and every step reminds you why." He looked up, and in the firelight his face was older than it had been in the capital, older than it had been three days ago; the road was doing its work on him. "You don't talk me out of my stone, taster. I didn't talk you out of yours."

"My mother's contract isn't mine to pay with. Sable holds it."

"No. But you've got something." Brannic nodded at him across the fire. "I've watched you nine years, boy, even when you thought nobody watched. You taste everything twice. You count everything twice. You walk through a room like it might be armed. That's not caution. That's armour. And armour's heavy, and men don't carry heavy things they can put down unless they think they'll need them." He pointed the fish-stick toward the gate. "Whatever you paid to become that—I'd guess the gate knows its weight."

Jude did not answer. He looked at the gate instead, at the spiral of teeth catching the firelight, each one small and white and once part of somebody, and he thought about the notebooks in his pack—four of them now, filled with his own handwriting, the sea described in as much detail as grief could squeeze onto a page, his mother's face rendered and re-rendered from a memory that was already going soft at the edges like fruit. He had written down the lullaby. He had written down the smell of Master Corvel's kitchen, tallow and ash and bitterroot. He had written down, in the margin of the third notebook, in letters smaller than the rest, as though ashamed of the confession: *I do not remember being happy. I remember being safe. These are not the same thing.*

He knew what he could not replace. He had known since the village of the unremembered, since he had watched a woman smile at him with a face that had traded away the memory of her own child's laughter and been glad of the bargain. Because he had looked at her hollow, hospitable eyes and felt—not horror, not first. Recognition. He had spent nine years building himself into a thing that could survive anything, and a thing that could survive anything was a thing that could lose anything, and somewhere along the way he had mistaken that invulnerability for a self.

It was the one possession he owned outright. His mother had bought his life; the Undercourt held his soul; the guild had taught him his trade and the crown had employed his tongue. But the tolerances—those he had built himself, dish by dish, year by year, under Corvel's cold instruction, swallowing ruin in measured doses until ruin grew bored of him. Nine years of small poisons. Every one of them had left a brick in the wall between Jude Vane and death.

And the wall was the only thing standing between him and becoming the smiling woman in the village.

* * *

At what might have been dawn, the gate spoke again without being addressed.

***The tall one dreams of a name. The small one dreams of a ledger.***

Jude sat up. Brannic was already awake; Jude suspected neither of them had truly slept.

"Can it be bargained with?" Jude asked the dark. "Partial payment? Installments?"

***The gate does not lend.*** For the first time the voice carried something almost like warmth, which was worse than its patience. ***Lending is the court's art. The gate only weighs. Bring what you cannot replace, or turn back, or stand. The options are old and they are all of them.***

Jude stood. His knees ached from the road; his mouth tasted of smoke and fear, and underneath both, faintly, of bitterroot—a ghost of flavour that had lived on his tongue for nine years, the aftertaste of the first poison Corvel ever fed him, hemlock cut with honey so a starving boy could keep it down. He had hated that taste for nine years. He had also, in the wordless arithmetic of survival, loved it, because it meant he had kept it down, and keeping things down was the whole of his life's work.

He walked to the gate. Brannic started to rise; Jude raised a hand and the smuggler stayed, jaw tight, fists closed on nothing.

The darkness between the pillars stirred and shaped itself into something like attention.

"I'm Jude Vane," Jude said. "Royal food-taster. Keeper of the Hollow Guild's ledgers. Son of Maren Vane, whose debt your court claims." Saying her name aloud on this road felt like setting a lamp in wind. "I've come to bargain with your king. Open the gate."

***Everything opens,*** the gate said. ***For a price. What do you bring that you cannot replace?***

Jude opened his mouth to speak and found, to his surprise, that the speech he had composed in the night—the careful, itemized offer, the taster's instinct to portion everything into measured doses—would not come. What came instead was the truth, plain as water.

"Nine years ago a man named Corvel fed me poison to see if I'd die," he said. "I didn't. So he fed me more. Different ones, in different doses, on a schedule he kept like scripture. Nightshade and arsenic and bitterroot and seven things whose names I was never told. He called it training. It was—" His voice caught, and he let it. "It was the only tenderness anyone ever showed me, and it wasn't tender at all. By the end I could drink what kills a horse. I could eat the queen's plate with a smile. There is no poison in the world above that my body doesn't greet like an old friend."

He pulled the stopper from the flask at his belt—the one every taster carried, the one that had saved his life four times in nine years—and poured its contents onto the worn road: a measure of broad antidote, bitter and brown, the distillation of Corvel's whole curriculum. The ground drank it without comment.

"That," Jude said. "All of it. Every tolerance. Every immunity. Take the wall down, brick by brick. After today, a cup of wine with hemlock in it will kill me as easily as it would kill any man. A kitchen accident. A rival's ring. A drop on a spoon." His hands were steady, which surprised him; he had expected them to shake. "That's what I can't replace. It took nine years and it nearly killed me eleven times and it is the only thing I own that I built with my own body. And I am giving it to you."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere high on the black slopes, stone ticked and settled, or perhaps the mountain was thinking.

***Why,*** the gate said, and it was not a challenge. It sounded genuinely curious, the way a scale is curious.

"Because I'm going down there to take back something they say is mine," Jude said. "And I can't take it back if I'm still hiding behind the one thing that made me safe. Safety's how they get you. I've read the ledgers. Nobody sells their soul in a moment of desperation—they sell it a piece at a time, always in exchange for safety, always telling themselves the rest of them is still their own." He looked up at the spiral of teeth. "So. Weigh it."

The darkness reached for him.

There was no other word for it. It came off the doorway like a tide and passed through him, and Jude felt it move through his body the way a slow poison moves—cataloguing, weighing, finding each tolerance the way his tongue found adulterants in a sauce. He felt the nightshade leave first, the immunity to it peeling away like a scab, and with it went a memory he hadn't known was attached: Corvel's kitchen, age fourteen, sweating and shivering through a nightshade dose while the old man sat across the table sharpening a knife and saying, *Breathe through it, boy. Fear is just another flavour.* Then arsenic, and the memory of the week he couldn't keep anything down and Corvel fed him rice water and never once said well done. Then bitterroot, and the taste of honey, and the plague winter he'd never lived through because his mother had bought him out of it—

Then all of it, all at once, and Jude Vane dropped to his knees on the road before the Tollgate of Teeth and was, for the first time in nine years, exactly as mortal as everyone else.

* * *

The gate opened.

There was no grinding of stone, no trumpet of revelation. The absence between the pillars simply became a passage, and the passage exhaled air that smelled of deep places—wet rock, cold iron, and beneath it all, impossibly, the green scent of growing things, as though gardens grew down there in the dark, tended by patient hands.

Jude knelt a moment longer. His body felt strangely loud—every heartbeat, every breath, every ache of the road now unbuffered, unmuffled, reported directly to a man with no defences left. He was aware of his own pulse the way he had never been, even as a boy. He was aware that somewhere in the world above there existed a mushroom that could stop his heart, and a root, and a leaf, and half a dozen minerals, and the simple kindness of bacteria in bad meat. He was food now. Everything was poison if you were patient enough.

He found that he was smiling.

"Taster." Brannic crouched beside him, and the smuggler's face was doing something complicated—pity and awe and a flicker of guilt, because his own turn at the gate was coming. "Was it worth it?"

Jude considered the question with the seriousness he gave all questions of price.

"I'll tell you when I'm done using the answer," he said, and got up, and his legs held.

He took the notebooks from his pack—all four—and weighed them in his hands. Then he handed them to Brannic. "If I don't come back up, burn them. Don't read them first." He paused. "No. Read them first. Then burn them. Someone should know what was lost besides the ledger."

Brannic took them like a man accepting a reliquary. "And if it asks me for mine—"

"Pay it," Jude said. "Whatever it is. Some prices you only get offered once."

He stepped between the pillars. The teeth spiralled overhead and around him, thousands of small white surrenders, and as he passed beneath them he understood, finally, what the gate was made of and why it demanded what it demanded: it was not cruelty. It was symmetry. Everyone who entered the deep places left a piece of themselves at the door, so that the mountain would hold a part of them even if they never returned—so that no one who descended was ever entirely lost, only mostly.

His tolerances were in the walls now. Nine years of survival, mortared into black stone. Part of him would stand here forever, immune to everything, feeling nothing.

The dark took him gently, the way sleep takes a child, and the gate closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting.

Above, on the worn road, Brannic Vael stood alone with four notebooks and a fire burning low, and turned to face the teeth, and began to speak his daughter's name aloud so that he would not forget the shape of the silence where it used to be.

Below, far below, in a corridor lit by stolen stars, something ancient turned a page in a ledger older than kingdoms, and noted, in a hand like frost forming on glass:

*One taster, disarmed. He gave up the wall. Interesting. They rarely give up the wall.*

*Send Sable to meet him at the second door. Let us see what he does when he is afraid.*
