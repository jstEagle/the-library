## 14. Descent

The road beneath the wards did not look like a road at all.

Jude had expected something dramatic—the way the ballads described it, a stair of black stone spiraling down into the mountain's root, guarded by things with too many joints. What he found instead was a cart track, rutted and ordinary, running between hedgerows gone wild with neglect, as if the Undercourt had never bothered to dress itself for visitors. The only wrongness was the light. There was no sun to speak of—only a gray luminescence that came from everywhere and nowhere, the color of an overcast sky remembered by someone who had forgotten what overcast meant.

"It's called the Hollow Way," Brannic said, walking beside the mule he'd named Turnip, because, he claimed, "a man who names his mule something grand gets attached, and attachment is expensive out here." He had said little since they left the capital three days ago, and what he said was always practical: where the water was safe, which villages still traded honestly, how far they could go before the broken wards began to press in on a body like deep water.

"And people just... walk this?" Jude asked. "Knowing where it goes?"

"Smugglers walk it. Debtors walk it. People who've sold something they can't live without and changed their minds." Brannic adjusted the rope halter. "The road doesn't care why you're on it. That's the one mercy it offers."

Jude walked with his notebooks strapped against his chest under his coat, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. He had filled four of them now. Every morning he woke and read the last pages first, checking himself against himself, the way Master Corvel had taught him to check a dish against its smell before trusting either. *My name is Jude Vane. I am twenty-six years old. I grew up near the sea.* He had written that last part twice, in two different hands, because the second time he wrote it he could no longer remember writing it the first.

The sea itself was already gone. He knew that the way you know a room has been emptied after years away—you remember the shape of the furniture without being able to picture any of it. He knew there had been gulls. He could not hear them anymore, even in memory. When he tried, he heard instead the sound the tithe-collector's receipt had made when Sable unfolded it: dry paper, patient paper, the sound of something that had been waiting a long time and would wait longer if it had to.

*Day nine,* he wrote that evening, by the light of a lantern Brannic had warded with salt. *Still have my mother's face. Still have her voice. I checked twice.*

He did not write down that he was afraid. Some entries were for the man who would read them later, and some were lies told to keep that man walking.

* * *

**From the ledger of Maren Vane, entry the first, undated:**

*I am writing this on the road, or what passes for one. The ink freezes if I stop moving, so I will write fast and badly and let future-me forgive present-me, who is very cold.*

*They say the mountain takes payment at the gate. They say the gate decides what a thing is worth. I have decided not to believe them, because believing it means admitting what I'm about to do, and I need my hands steady for the walking.*

*The child kicked today. First time. Right when I was deciding whether to turn back. As if to say: well? Are you going to stand in this ditch all winter arguing with yourself, or are you going to get us both somewhere warm?*

*I have never been spoken to so rudely by someone I love so much.*

*If anyone finds this ledger—if you find this, whoever you are, and you are reading the private accounting of a dead woman—know that I did not sell my child. Whatever the court tells you, whatever their collectors say with their smooth voices and their receipts, I did not sell him. I bought him time. There is a difference. I intend to make them write the difference down in their own books, in their own hand, and initial it.*

*The fever took everyone else on our street. Everyone. Old Tam with his forty years. The twins from the bakery, who were six. My sister. My sister's husband. My sister's baby, who was four months old and whose name I cannot say yet without stopping, and I mustn't stop, because the ink.*

*The child lived. The child lived and the plague-winter said, very politely, that everything lives on credit here, and someone would have to hold the note.*

*I volunteered. Of course I volunteered. But volunteering and paying are different rooms in the same house, and I am only now learning how many corridors lie between them.*

*Below tomorrow. If the stories are true, there is a court under the mountain that keeps accounts older than the kingdom. I have seen what their agents can do—a woman in Grayharbor paid away her grief for her drowned son and smiled through the funeral like spring itself. I thought it monstrous then. I think it monstrous now. Monstrous and necessary, which is the worst kind of thing, the kind that survives.*

*The child kicked again. He does not know what his mother is about to become. Good. Let him be born into ignorance and keep it as long as the world allows. Ignorance is the only inheritance I have that isn't mortgaged.*

*—M.V.*

* * *

On the fourth day below the capital, the wards began.

Or rather, the places where the wards had been. Jude had spent his whole life inside them without once thinking about them, the way a fish does not think about water, and now their absence was a presence of its own. Along the roadside stood stones the height of a man, each carved with sigils worn nearly smooth, and around each stone the air was wrong—not dark exactly, but thin, as though reality had been stretched too tightly over the bones of the world and had begun, in places, to show the weave.

Brannic touched none of them. He gave them a wide berth, leading the mule in a careful arc, and when Jude asked why, the smuggler only said, "Wards are promises. These ones got broke. You don't put your hand in a broken promise."

That night they sheltered in the shell of a tollhouse, its roof half-collapsed, its hearth still sound enough to hold a small fire. Brannic slept first—he insisted on taking the first watch asleep and the second awake, an arrangement he refused to explain—and Jude sat with his notebook on his knees, adding to the inventory of himself.

*Things I know about my mother:* he wrote. *Her name was Maren. She sang while she worked, but off-key, deliberately, because she said songs were meant to be ruined a little or they went stale. She smelled of tallow and rosemary. She died when I was nine, in the second bad winter, and I buried her myself because there was no one else, and I was angry for years afterward that no one else came, and then I stopped being angry, and I don't remember choosing to stop.*

He read it back. Then he read it again, slower, and felt the ground shift under the sentence the way snow shifts underfoot before it gives.

*I buried her myself.* He had written those words in chapter after chapter of his own life, and they had always sat there comfortably, load-bearing, unquestioned. But now, with the tithe eating through him day by day, he examined them the way he'd been trained to examine a dish: texture, weight, aftertaste.

He had been nine. Nine-year-olds did not dig graves in frozen ground. Nine-year-olds did not carry bodies. Someone had helped him. Someone had—

And there the memory simply ended, sheared clean, the edges smooth as cut cloth. Not faded. Cut. A gap shaped like a transaction.

"You've gone quiet," Brannic said. Jude hadn't heard him wake.

"I'm trying to remember who helped me bury my mother."

Brannic was silent a moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the thin dark, something made a sound like a bell being rung underwater.

"The tithe take it?" Brannic asked. "Or was it taken before?"

"I don't know. That's the problem. I can't tell which holes are new."

"They'll all be new eventually." Brannic fed a stick to the fire. His face, lit from below, looked older than it had in the capital, the lines around his eyes carved deeper. "You want to know something, taster? Twenty years I've walked pieces of this road, and every person I ever met going down had the same look. Like a man reading his own contract and finding clauses he signed in his sleep."

"Did you?"

Brannic didn't answer directly. He looked at the fire instead, and his silence had the particular weight of a man holding a door shut from the inside.

"Sleep," he said finally. "Tomorrow we reach the place where the road stops pretending to be a road."

* * *

**From the ledger of Maren Vane, entry the fourth:**

*The gate is real. Everything is real. I want that recorded plainly, because some part of me kept hoping the whole thing was a story poor women tell each other to feel less alone in the dark, and it is not, it is stairs, actual stairs, going down farther than my lamp can argue with.*

*But I am getting ahead of myself, and the child is kicking again, and I suspect he will kick his way through every difficult paragraph I ever write, so let me order my thoughts.*

*There is a village at the foot of the descent. Or what used to be a village. The people there smile. They smiled at me when I arrived half-frozen with my hand on my belly and my whole fortune sewn into my coat lining, and they brought me bread and broth and sat with me by their fire, and every single one of them smiled, and not one of them could tell me their own name.*

*They traded, during the plague-winter. All of them. Memories, mostly—memories buy better than grief, the collector told them, memories hold their value. One old man sold the war. A young woman sold her wedding. A boy of maybe ten sold his mother's face, and when she came to the village looking for him he greeted her kindly, as one greets a stranger, and offered her soup.*

*She stayed anyway. That's the part I can't get out of my chest. She stayed, in a village of smiling strangers, and learned to love her son twice—once as the child she'd raised, and once as the sweet empty boy across the table who liked her soup. She told me: it isn't the same love. But it is love. It grows in the space like grass through floorboards.*

*I held my belly and I thought: I will not be the grass. I will be the floor. Whatever they take from me, they will not take the knowing of him.*

*So I write it all down. Every memory, numbered, dated, witnessed. If they take the thing itself, the record remains. A memory in ink may be a corpse of a memory, but a corpse proves something lived.*

*Entry one: the sea at Grayharbor in autumn, iron-colored, loud, the gulls screaming like unpaid debts. Entry two: my wedding, such as it was, a borrowed coat and a wet churchyard and I laughed so hard at his vows I snorted, and he loved me forever for the snort. Entry three: the night this child was conceived, which I will not describe except to say it rained and we were happy and happiness in that year was an act of defiance.*

*I will number them all the way down. And when the collectors come with their scales, I will pay exactly what I owe and not one memory more, and I will get a receipt, and I will keep the receipt, and someday—*

*Someday is doing a great deal of work in these entries. Someday is carrying more than any someday should. But a pregnant woman's someday is a strong beast. It has to be. It carries two.*

*—M.V., the descent, by lamplight, with a kicking passenger*

* * *

The place where the road stopped pretending was a cliff face.

Not a wall of stone, precisely—it moved, when you watched it long enough, the way smoke moves, or the way heat shimmer moves above summer fields. It rose out of the earth without transition, gray becoming grayer, until Jude understood that he was not looking at rock but at a decision the world had made about where it ended.

Beyond it, the mountain. Above it, nothing: no stars, no moon, no sky worth the name. The starless dark pressed down like a lid.

"We camp here," Brannic said. "Last fire. Past this point, flame burns strange. Colors go out of it." He unsaddled Turnip and rubbed the mule down with handfuls of dry grass, and his hands were steady, but he talked more than usual, and Jude had learned enough about smugglers to know that talk was what fear sounded like when it had money.

"How far down?" Jude asked.

"Farther than anyone's come back to report. Except her."

Jude looked up sharply. "Except who?"

Brannic's hands went still on the mule's flank. For a long moment the only sound was Turnip's breathing and the wind making its underwater-bell noise along the cliff face.

"There's a story among the road-people," Brannic said at last, slowly, as if each word cost toll. "About a woman who went down twenty-six years ago, in the plague-winter, big with child. Who paid at the gate and paid again below and walked back up the whole way, alone, carrying a newborn, and lived. Nobody walks back up. She did." He turned and looked at Jude, and his eyes in the firelight were terrible with a question he'd apparently been carrying for days. "Your mother's name, taster. You've never said it."

The wind rang. The fire burned strangely already, its colors draining toward white.

"Maren," Jude said. "Maren Vane."

Brannic closed his eyes. When he opened them, he reached into his coat—an old habit, Jude thought, the reflex of a man who had spent twenty years keeping one hand near the thing that mattered most—and drew out a scrap of leather, cracked and soft with handling, folded and refolded until its creases were worn to threads.

He held it out.

"She gave it to the ferryman at the Grayharbor crossing," Brannic said. "Twenty-six years ago. Paid him to carry word to the capital, someday, to anyone asking after a woman who came down the mountain with a baby. He kept it in his toll-book. He died two winters back, and I bought his book—not for this, I swear it, I bought it for the routes—but her page was in it, and I've carried it since, telling myself I'd find her family someday, knowing full well I was really looking for a reason to go down this road myself."

Jude took the leather. His hands were shaking. Inside, in ink gone brown with age, in a handwriting that hit him like a blow to the sternum because he knew it, he had always known it, it was the handwriting from the recipe cards in the kitchen of his childhood, the firm downstrokes, the little hook she put on the final letter of every line—

*To whoever asks: I went down owing and came up paid. Ask the court for my account. Make them open their books. My son's name is Jude. Tell him the lullaby was mine before it was anyone's, and tell him his mother did not lose.*

At the bottom, in smaller letters, almost hidden:

*They keep ledgers too. Everything they own is written down. Find the founding entry.*

Jude read it three times. The fire burned white and colorless. Far overhead, where stars should have been, the dark sat like a debt unpaid.

"Brannic," he said, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect, "she's telling me how to beat them."

"I know," Brannic said quietly. "I've read it every night for two years." He looked up at the blank sky, then at the cliff face where the world ended, and squared his shoulders like a man stepping up to a counter he'd been avoiding for two decades. "Question is whether we're brave enough to follow a dead woman's arithmetic. Come morning, we find out."
