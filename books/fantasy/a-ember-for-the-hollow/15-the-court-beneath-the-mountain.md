## 15. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The last of the descent was not a road at all.

Jude had expected stone, stairs, the ordinary architecture of going down. What he got instead was a passage that narrowed and then refused to end—a throat of black rock that swallowed the sound of his own footsteps so completely that he could not be sure he was moving at all except that Brannic's lantern swung ahead of him, smaller and smaller, a gold coin sinking in dark water.

"Keep talking," Brannic said without turning around. His voice came back to Jude strangely flattened, as if the mountain were pressing it thin. "Not for company. For proof you're still you."

"What would I say?"

"Anything. Say your ledgers. You know them by heart, don't you? I've seen you recite entries like prayers."

So Jude talked. He gave the mountain the guild's accounts: three measures of borrowed years against one of fever-breaking, the widow's laughter entered as paid in full, the cooper's fear of deep water sold twice by mistake and repurchased at a loss. The numbers rose out of him easily, worn smooth by nine years of handling, and he found that saying them aloud in the dark was like holding a rope. Each figure was a rung. Each entry proved there had been a world with candles in it, and ledgers, and a man who kept them.

He did not say the things he had written in his notebooks. *I cannot remember the sea.* That page stayed folded against his chest where he carried it, along with the others—forty-one pages now, forty-one days of starless sky, each morning waking to find some small room in himself swept empty and bare. Two days ago it had been the smell of rain on hot cobblestones. Yesterday, the name of the street he'd lived on before the palace took him in. This morning, when he woke in the cold waystation below the wards, he had reached for his mother's face and found only the shape of a woman kneeling beside a bed, hands busy, face turned away. He had written that down too. *She knelt. She worked. I loved her. The rest is gone.*

"You've stopped," Brannic said.

"I haven't." Jude started again, faster.

The passage ended the way a held breath ends.

One moment there was rock pressing close enough to touch on both sides; the next, the dark opened beneath him into space, and Jude stood on a ledge of black marble at the top of an impossible stair, looking down into a city that should not exist.

It was lit from above.

That was the first thing—the thing that stopped his heart and then restarted it wrong. Light fell from overhead in cold white points, hundreds of them, thousands, arranged in no pattern any astronomer would recognize and yet unmistakably a sky. Stars. Real stars, or what had been stars, hanging perhaps two hundred feet up in a ceiling of seamless night-stone, burning with the steady patient light of things that had been shining over the capital since before anyone alive was born.

"There it is," Brannic said quietly. "There's our sky."

Jude could not answer. He was doing arithmetic—he always did arithmetic when feeling threatened him—and the sum was unbearable. On the night the border wards collapsed, every star over the kingdom had gone dark at once. He had stood in the palace courtyard with the Queen's household around him, all of them staring up at a blackness so total it felt less like absence than like being watched, and he had thought: *catastrophe*. Wards failing. Some enemy's working. A wound in the world.

No one had thought to look down.

"They were never lost," he said. "They were collected."

"Aye." Brannic set the lantern down between them, and its little flame looked obscene here, a candle dragged into a cathedral. "Twenty-six years I've heard stories. Thought they were stories. But the smugglers' songs all agree on one thing—the Undercourt doesn't make its own light. Why would it? It has ours."

Jude began to descend the stair.

* * *

The steps were wide enough for six abreast and cut from a stone that was black without being dull—it drank the starlight and gave back a faint sheen, like water at night. As they went down, the city resolved itself out of shadow, and Jude's breath went shallow, because he knew it.

He knew it the way you know your own handwriting.

The Undercourt was the royal palace inverted. Not imitated—inverted, the way a reflection is inverted, everything true above made false and everything false made true below. The White Spire, where the Queen held her dawn audiences, stood here as a spire of jet, its windows glowing warm instead of pale. The Long Gallery, where portraits of nine generations of monarchs hung in gilt frames, ran along the eastern wall—but the faces in those frames were wrong, blurred, as if painted by someone who remembered people only as shapes. The great doors of the throne room, oak and iron above, were silver and bone below, and carved across their height was a single line of script in a language Jude did not know and yet could read, the way he could read the guild's oldest ledgers without ever having been taught:

*ALL ACCOUNTS SETTLED BELOW.*

"It mirrors," he said. "Every hall, every court. Why?"

"Because it's older," Brannic said. "That's the part nobody likes. They think the Undercourt copied the palace. It didn't. The palace copied *it*. Every king who laid a stone up top was building a copy of something he'd been shown in a dream, or a bargain, or a mirror. We live in the forgery, taster. This is the original."

Jude touched the balustrade. It was warm. Stone should not be warm, this far under the earth, but it was warm the way a body is warm, and he snatched his hand back and pretended he hadn't.

They passed through streets that were streets and markets that were markets, and the people—

The people were the worst of it, and the best, and Jude could not decide which.

They looked human. That was the horror of it. A woman sold pears from a stall—pears, underground, in a lightless country—and her hands were a fruit-seller's hands, nicked and capable. Two men argued cheerfully over the price of a bolt of blue cloth. Children ran past with a hoop, shrieking, and the sound of their laughter was exactly the sound of children anywhere, which meant it was exactly the sound of children in the capital, which meant—

"They're souls," Jude said. His voice came out very flat. Taster's voice. The voice he used announcing poison. "The tithe. The contracts. Everyone the crown ever sold—they're *here*, living here, shopping, selling pears—"

"Living afterlives," Brannic said. "Or living prices. Depends how you look at it. Down here a soul isn't a person, exactly. It's a holding. An asset. But assets get dressed, taster. Assets go to market. Assets forget, sometimes, that they were ever anything else." He pulled his hood lower. "Don't stare. And don't buy anything."

"I wasn't going to buy anything."

"You were going to look at the pears like a man who misses pears. That's how it starts. Everything down here is priced to be wanted."

Jude made himself look at the cobbles instead, and immediately wished he hadn't, because the cobbles were engraved. Every stone bore a name. Thousands of names, tens of thousands, worn shallow by feet, and he understood with a lurch that these were not decorations. These were the *unredeemed*. The souls whose debts had never come due, or whose debts had come due and been paid by someone else, walking daily on the record of everyone still outstanding.

He found himself reading them. He couldn't stop. *Maren Ostwick. Tam of the Ford. Alys, called Sparrow.* Names the way the guild wrote names, in the old shorthand, with the little marks for condition and provenance—and then, near the edge of the street, half-worn away:

*Vane.*

His own surname. Not his name—there was no Jude, no birthdate, no mother's mark. Just *Vane*, and beneath it a symbol he knew intimately from the guild's oldest books: the sign for *encumbered, pending*.

Brannic caught his arm. "Walk."

"He put my family name in the *street*."

"The whole city's a ledger. You knew that coming down. You just didn't know you'd be able to read it." Brannic steered him left, away from the main thoroughfare, toward a colonnade whose pillars were carved as figures—tall, courteous, faceless, each with its hands open and extended, palms up. Receiving. "Now listen. The audience isn't granted. It's scheduled. There's a clerk of petitions—we find the clerk, we state our business, we wait. Could be hours. Could be days. You do not speak to anyone of rank unless spoken to first, and if the King speaks to you—"

"I know how to behave at court. I've tasted for the Queen for nine years."

"This isn't her court." Brannic stopped, and in the starlight his scarred face looked older than Jude had ever seen it. "Up there, when the Queen looks at you, she sees a servant. Down here, when the King looks at you, he sees a *sum*. Twenty-six years of interest, taster. You're not a man walking into that throne room. You're an investment coming home to be counted. Remember that, whatever he says. However kind he sounds. Especially if he's kind."

* * *

The clerk of petitions kept an office in what would have been, above ground, the chamber where the Master of Coin received the tax rolls. Below, it was a long narrow room lined floor to ceiling with drawers—wooden drawers, brass-handled, stretching away into dimness farther than the eye could follow, and Jude's professional soul flinched because he recognized the filing system. It was the Hollow Guild's system. The same cataloguing scheme, the same marginal notation, the same red-ink conventions for delinquent accounts. Someone had taught the Undercourt to keep books the way the guild kept books—or the guild had learned its trade here, all those centuries ago, and Jude had spent nine years balancing columns in a child's copy of this.

The clerk was small and gray and precise, with ink stains to the second knuckle, and when Jude stated his business—"Jude Vane, of the capital, petitioning audience with His Patient Majesty regarding contract 4-Vane"—the clerk did not blink, did not ask how a mortal had come so far, did not do anything so vulgar as express surprise.

"Contract 4-Vane," the clerk repeated, and opened a drawer somewhere behind his left shoulder without turning around. The drawer slid out into his hand as though it had been waiting. From it he drew a single card, cream-colored, soft-edged with age. He read it. He looked at Jude. He read it again, longer.

"Interesting," the clerk said, in the tone of a man finding a misfiled entry.

"What's interesting?"

"That is not information I provide." The clerk produced a bell—not a service bell but a small hand-bell of greenish metal—and rang it once. The sound did not echo. It *descended*, rolling downward through the floors of the palace like a stone dropped down a well, and somewhere far below, Jude felt rather than heard an answering note. "You will be received. Follow the gray corridor. Do not open any door that is warm."

"Why?"

"Because a door that is warm already knows you're coming, and prefers to choose the moment." The clerk was already writing, dipping his pen, entering Jude into some vast running account. "Wait. One more thing." He glanced up, and his eyes were the color of ledger-paper. "You gave your name as Jude Vane. The contract gives a different name. Shall I enter you under the contract's name?"

The question dropped through Jude like cold water through cloth.

"What name does it give?"

"The one his mother chose for him," the clerk said mildly. "Before. They usually differ. Mothers are sentimental; contracts are accurate. Well?"

And Jude—who had written forty-one pages against forgetting, who had surrendered the sea and the rain-smell and his mother's face one by one to the starless dark, who had walked on his own surname set into the street—heard himself say, quietly, in his best taster's voice:

"Enter me under both. And note the discrepancy. Discrepancies are how errors get found."

Something moved behind the clerk's paper eyes—amusement, or appetite, or recognition, the look of one bookkeeper meeting another across a hostile table. He wrote. The pen scratched like a small animal.

"Noted," he said. "Go down. He's expecting you. He's been expecting you for twenty-six years, and His Majesty is very patient, but even patience appreciates punctuality."

* * *

The gray corridor sloped gently downward for a long time. Brannic had been made to wait at the clerk's office—"Petitioners of secondary standing await in the antechamber," the clerk had said, and Brannic had gripped Jude's forearm once, hard, and said only, "Count everything. Even the kindnesses. *Especially* the kindnesses," and let him go.

Alone, then. Jude walked, and the stars overhead thinned and changed as he descended, and he realized with a slow cold turn of his stomach that the constellations were rearranging themselves around his progress—that the stolen sky was watching him move through it, the way the Queen's court used to watch him taste, all eyes on the man with the spoon, waiting to see whether he lived.

The corridor ended at doors of black wood banded in silver, tall as a ship's masts, and they stood already open.

Beyond them lay the throne room of the Undercourt, and Jude stopped on the threshold because it was beautiful, and he hated it, and he could not make the hating stick.

Above, the throne room of the palace was all gold and banners and the smoke of incense, built to overwhelm. Below, it was built to *hold*. The vaulted ceiling soared up into the artificial night, and the stolen stars hung in it thick as frost on a window, so many and so bright that the hall below lay in pools and rivers of white light, and every surface—the black marble floor, the silver pillars, the long still reflecting pool that ran the length of the nave—caught that light and multiplied it, until Jude seemed to stand inside a sky that had fallen and been taught manners. The air smelled of cold iron and, faintly, incredibly, of plums. His mother had once bought him a single plum, in the plague winter, and had watched him eat it without eating anything herself.

At the far end, where the Queen's throne stood above, there was a chair. It was plain. It was made of some pale wood, unadorned, almost humble, and it sat on a dais of one step, and it was the most frightening object Jude had ever seen, because everything else in this place was magnificent and the King had chosen simplicity, the way a moneylender wears gray.

The pool reflected the stars perfectly. Jude looked into it as he walked the long approach—because there was nothing else to look at, because his legs needed somewhere to go—and saw that the reflections were wrong. Not the stars. The stars reflected true. But among them, in the water, moved shapes that were not in the ceiling: faces, hundreds of faces, drifting up from the depths of the pool like bubbles, pressing against the underside of the reflection, mouths opening without sound.

Looking up at him.

*The unredeemed,* he thought. *The ones in the stones. The ones in the drawers.* He kept walking. His heart was a fist knocking on a door no one would answer.

And then he was at the foot of the single step, and the plain chair was before him, and it was occupied.

He had expected age. He had expected vastness, terror, a presence like a storm. Instead the King of the Undercourt was a lean, courteous figure in undyed wool, with a long face and quiet eyes and hands folded in his lap like a man listening to a recital, and when he saw Jude he smiled—not the smile of a predator, which Jude had been braced for, but the smile of a creditor greeting a debtor he has thought about fondly for a very long time.

"Jude Vane," the King said, and his voice was warm, and reasonable, and it filled the starlit hall the way water fills a cup. "Also called, if we consult the older instruments, another name entirely. Twenty-six years in the making. Do sit down—you must be tired; the stairs are long, and you have been losing pieces of yourself the whole way down. I could tell you which ones. I keep excellent records."

Behind the plain chair, high in the vaulted dark, one of the stolen stars flickered—just once, briefly, like a held breath—and Jude felt something shift inside his skull, some small room in him going suddenly, quietly empty, and he understood that the flicker had been for him.

The King watched him feel it, and smiled more kindly still.

"Now," he said. "Let us discuss what you are worth."
