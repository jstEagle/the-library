## 16. Audience with the Patient King

The doors of the Undercourt were made of antler and iron, and they opened without being touched.

Jude had expected cold. He had prepared himself for cold the way he had once prepared his body for poison—deliberately, in measured doses, imagining the worst until the worst became familiar. But the air beneath the mountain was warm. It was the warmth of a room where a fire has burned for centuries without ever being fed, a warmth that came from nowhere and belonged to no one, and it settled against his skin like a hand he hadn't invited.

Beyond the doors stretched a hall that was, unmistakably, a mirror.

He had seen engravings of the royal palace above—the long gallery with its pillars of white stone, its windows of colored glass, the ceiling painted with the constellations as the cartographers had fixed them three hundred years ago. This was that hall turned inside out. The pillars were black basalt veined with silver. The windows were walls of unlit glass, dark as pond ice. And overhead, where the painted stars should have been—

The stolen sky.

They hung in their hundreds, embedded in the vaulted dark like coins pressed into wax: every star that had gone out over the capital on that first dead night, and thousands more besides, older ones, dimmer ones, stars Jude had never known because they had been taken before his grandfather was born. They did not blaze. They glowed the way embers glow, patient and banked, and their light fell through the hall in long gray-gold shafts that did not quite reach the floor.

*So this is where it went,* Jude thought, and felt something in him tilt, the way a man tilts when the ground proves to be a deck.

Brannic stood at his shoulder, breathing hard. The smuggler's face, seamed and sun-cured above ground, looked wrong down here—too warm a color for all this dark, like a lamp burning the wrong oil. His hand kept moving toward the knife at his belt and then away again, a small futile rhythm.

"Don't," Jude said quietly. "You've seen what they price things at."

"I've seen what they *take* things at." Brannic spat, and the sound was very loud. "Twenty years I've dreamed about this place. Never thought I'd walk in through the front door."

"The front door is the only door that opens for the indebted." The voice came from ahead of them, from the far end of the hall, and it was not loud, but it arrived the way water arrives in a dry room—everywhere at once. "Come. You have walked a long way to be appraised. Let us not keep you standing."

* * *

The throne was not a throne.

It was a scale.

Jude understood this only gradually, as they crossed the hall, because at first it looked like an ordinary seat of state—a high-backed chair of black wood upon a dais of seven steps. But the arms ended in pans, great shallow dishes of hammered silver, and from beneath the seat hung chains that descended into darkness and did not clink when the King shifted, because nothing that hung in this court moved unless it was moved by weight.

The King sat in the middle of his own instrument, and he was smaller than Jude had imagined.

That was the first cruelty of him. Sable had been tall, terrible, her shadow arriving before she did. Jude had built the King out of her proportions and added centuries. But the figure on the dais was slight, almost delicate, robed in gray so fine it seemed woven from the ash of other garments. His face was long and courteous and old—not wrinkled, not aged the way flesh ages, but worn smooth the way a coin wears smooth, all the sharpness rubbed away by handling. His eyes were the color of the light falling from the ceiling: banked gold, ember-dark, patient past all patience.

He rose when Jude entered. That, too, was a courtesy, and courtesies from kings are always invoices.

"Jude Vane," the King said. His voice was dry and kind, paper and honey. "Taster to the Queen. Keeper of ledgers for the Hollow Guild. Son of Maren Vane, who paid well and asked little." He inclined his head, and the gesture was so precisely human that Jude's skin crawled. "Be welcome. You are expected. You have been expected for twenty-six years, which is a long time even for me, and I have learned to expect things the way stones learn to expect rain."

"Your Majesty." Jude bowed the exact depth he would have bowed a foreign ambassador—respectful, noncommittal, the bow of a man who represents someone else's authority. Nine years of court had taught him that much. "I've come about my account."

"You have come about your account." The King smiled. It was a good smile. It was the best smile Jude had ever seen, and he had spent nine years watching faces for the small lies that live around the mouth. "I admire that. Most who come here weep first. Or bargain blindly, offering things they have already lost. You come speaking bookkeeping. Your mother did the same, at the end. It is a family trait, I think—to meet the unbearable with arithmetic."

"My mother met you with her life."

"With a portion of it. The rest she left to grow." The King gestured, and though no servant appeared, two chairs did—drawn up before the dais as if they had always stood there, as if generations of visitors had sat in exactly those spots and sweated in exactly that way. "Sit. This will take some time, and I dislike conducting appraisal on my feet. Weighing is delicate work."

Jude sat. Brannic remained standing, planted like a fence post, until the King's ember eyes moved to him with mild interest, and something in that mildness made the smuggler sit down after all, heavily, like a man taking a blow.

"Now." The King folded his hands. They were beautiful hands, uncalloused, the hands of someone who has never carried anything heavier than a pen. "Let us begin with terms, since you are a man of terms. Do you know what currency is?"

"Something everyone agrees to value."

"Good. Better than good—you have read your guild histories. Currency is agreement, and agreement is debt wearing its public clothes. Above, you trade in coin, which is a promise the crown makes and mostly keeps. Here—" he spread one elegant hand toward the star-hung dark, the empty glass walls, the whole vast mirrored hush of the Undercourt—"we trade in the thing itself. Souls. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. A soul, Jude Vane, is a quantity. It can be divided, weighted, invested, lent at interest, and held in trust. Yours, for instance."

The word landed in Jude's chest like a swallowed stone.

"Your mother came to me in the plague winter," the King went on, conversational now, almost gentle, a physician discussing a chart. "You were seven months along inside her, and you were dying. The fever had decided you were not worth keeping. She had nothing—no coin, no name worth selling, no years to borrow; the guild did not yet serve the poor in those days, or rather it served them only as harvest. So she offered the only thing of value she possessed. Half of you. The half that had not yet finished becoming."

"She didn't understand what she was signing," Jude said. His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later, and ashamed of the pride.

"No," the King agreed pleasantly. "She understood perfectly. That is why the contract grieves her so beautifully. Grief purchased with full knowledge is the finest grief there is—it holds its value forever. If she had signed in ignorance, the sorrow would have curdled into blame within a decade and depreciated accordingly. As it stands—" he sighed, a small appreciative sigh, a collector regarding a well-kept piece— "her regret has matured like wine. I have drawn on it twice. Once to seal the western wards, once to buy a winter that never quite arrived. Excellent vintage, Maren Vane's remorse. You should be proud of her portfolio."

Something hot climbed Jude's throat. He let it climb, and then he swallowed it, deliberately, the way he had swallowed nine years of measured doses, and when he spoke his voice was still level.

"You said my soul has been appreciating."

"It has been *thriving*." For the first time, genuine warmth entered the King's voice, the warmth of a merchant praising stock he loves. "Do you know what makes a soul appreciate, Jude Vane? Scarcity, yes—but also *use*. A soul left idle in a jar loses value, like any asset. A soul that lives, that works, that suffers and endures and builds tolerances and keeps accounts and walks nine hundred miles into the dark to confront its creditor—that soul develops history. Texture. Weight." He leaned forward, and the light from the ceiling-stars caught the worn-smooth planes of his face. "Every year you survived increased you. Every memory you made thickened you. Even your losses—your father, your hunger, your nine years of swallowing death in spoonfuls—even those are deposits. You are, I say this with professional admiration, the finest single holding in my entire court. There are archdukes below whose souls would not fetch a tenth of yours at open auction."

"So appraise me," Jude said. "Give me the number."

"Oh, numbers." The King laughed softly. "You want the number. Of course you do. Numbers make the unbearable bearable; that is their whole gift and their whole treachery. Very well." He raised one hand, and somewhere below the dais, deep in the chains, something began to move—a slow shifting of weight, a groan of balances finding each other. "By current valuation: your soul stands at four thousand, two hundred and six years of stored time, or its equivalent in memory, love, name, or grief. That is the principal. Interest accrues daily at the court's standard rate, which has not changed since the mountain was young. Against it, your mother's payment covers—" the scales ticked, a sound like a knuckle cracking— "eleven percent."

The number sat in the air between them.

Eleven percent. Jude did the arithmetic without wanting to, the way he did everything: twenty-six years of living, of growing, of becoming precisely the man who could sit in this chair without weeping—and it amounted to eleven percent of himself. The rest, eighty-nine parts of every hundred, was owed.

"And if I cannot pay," he said.

"Then the tithe completes, and the difference becomes mine outright. You will continue to exist." The King said it kindly, the way one reassures a child about a move to a new house. "You will exist very comfortably. You will keep my ledgers—I find myself in need of a keeper with your particular gifts, and I pay excellently in everything except departure. You will remember what I permit you to remember. You will be, in every way that matters, yourself."

"And in the ways that don't matter?"

"In those ways, mine." The King smiled again, and this time Jude saw the teeth of the thing under the courtesy: not fangs, nothing so honest. Just patience. Patience with edges. "You misunderstand me if you think I am cruel, taster. Cruelty is wasteful. I do not break what I buy. Ask anyone below—they are content. They are *kept*. Is a kept thing unhappy? Only if it remembers being otherwise, and memory, as you may have noticed lately, is negotiable."

Outside Jude's own skull, faintly, another memory thinned. He felt it go the way one feels a tooth loosen—not gone, but no longer certain. He gripped the chair's arm and kept his face still. Thirty days, Sable had said. Each starless day took one. He counted backward silently, cataloguing what he still owned: the sea, his mother's hands, Corvel's kitchen, the taste of plum wine, the afternoon of sunlight he had been hoarding like a miser. The sea still there? Still there. He checked it the way a man checks a wound for bleeding.

"You're doing it now," the King observed, with interest. "Counting yourself. Inventorying. It won't help, you know. Appraisal is my art, and you cannot out-count the appraiser in his own hall."

"Perhaps not." Jude leaned forward, mirroring the King's posture, and saw the ember eyes flicker—just slightly—with something that might have been surprise, or might have been appetite. "But I've read ledgers since I was nine years old, Your Majesty. Guild ledgers, crown ledgers, smuggling manifests, and once, drunk, a grain merchant's private book of bribes. And I have learned one thing that holds across every book ever kept."

"Which is?"

"No ledger is only what's written in it." Jude let the silence stretch, one heartbeat, two, the trick Corvel had taught him for making a small statement feel enormous. "Every entry points at another entry. Every debt implies a debtor. Every balance was struck by someone, against something, at some price—and prices, Majesty, can be audited."

For the first time, the Patient King was quiet.

It lasted perhaps three seconds. To a being who had waited twenty-six years for one soul, Jude guessed it must have felt like a season. When the King spoke again, his voice had changed—not colder, not warmer, but *closer*, the way a lens comes close.

"Audited," he repeated, tasting the word. "Oh, you are your mother's son. She too sat where you sit and spoke of fairness. Fairness." He shook his head with something like fondness. "Very well, Jude Vane. You wish to see the books behind the books. Every debtor does, eventually; it is the last stage of grief. I will grant it—not as kindness, but as investment. See my accounts. Study them. Understand the magnitude of what you owe and the antiquity of the system that holds the note. Then return to me, and we will discuss terms like professionals." He rose, and the great scales groaned, and the light from the stolen stars seemed to lean toward him. "My steward will conduct you to the Ledger Room. No visitor has walked its aisles in four hundred years. Consider it a courtesy extended to fine stock."

"I'm not stock."

"Not yet," the King agreed pleasantly. "Appraisal takes time. That is why they call me patient."

As Jude rose to follow the gray-robed steward who had materialized at the dais's foot, the King spoke once more, softly, almost tenderly, and the words followed Jude down the hall like a hand between his shoulder blades:

"Give my regards to the entries, taster. Especially the early ones. You'll find a familiar signature."

* * *

Brannic caught up with him at the edge of the hall, under a pillar of black stone, and grabbed his sleeve with fingers that trembled despite everything.

"What did he mean," the smuggler whispered, "by a familiar signature?"

Jude looked back once, at the small worn figure settling onto its throne of scales, at the hundreds of banked stars burning overhead like debts that had learned to shine.

"He meant," Jude said, "that my mother wasn't the first Vane in his books. Come on. Before he changes his mind—or before I lose whatever memory tells me to be afraid."

They followed the steward into the dark, toward the Ledger Room, and overhead the stolen stars watched them go with the patient light of things that had been appraised long ago, and had never once been asked.
