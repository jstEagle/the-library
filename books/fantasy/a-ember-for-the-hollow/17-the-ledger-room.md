## 17. The Ledger Room

The corridor behind the throne hall did not exist on any map of the palace above, and Jude knew the palace above better than he had ever known his own face.

He had slipped through during the changing of a guard that did not breathe—had counted the seconds between the court's slow ritual motions the way he had once counted courses at the Queen's table, waiting for the safe interval, the unwatched gap. The Undercourt kept time like a clock with too much oil in it: everything gliding, everything deliberate, nothing hurried except when it wished to frighten you. He had learned in three days among the mirrored courtiers that fear here was a seasoning, applied with intent. He had decided not to be seasoned.

So he walked. Past alcoves where veiled figures traded whispers weighted like coins. Past a fountain that ran not with water but with something slower and silverier, and which he made himself not look at directly, because he had been a taster for nine years and knew the first rule of unfamiliar substances: never let it see you want it.

The corridor narrowed. Then turned where no turn should have been. Then opened.

And Jude Vane, who had trained his whole life to keep his face still, felt his composure crack down the middle like a dropped plate.

Shelves.

Shelves from floor to a ceiling lost in shadow, curving away in ranks upon ranks, an architecture made entirely of accounts. The room was vast beyond reason—the mountain's own hollow heart, he realized, this was what the court had been carved out of—and every inch of it was ledgers. Spines of cracked leather, spines of pale bark, spines of hammered metal, some bound in vellum so fine it glowed faintly, the way good paper glows when it has absorbed something that was never meant to be written on it.

The air smelled the way the guild's counting room had always smelled on the night before balance-day: candle tallow, dust, iron gall ink, and underneath it all a sweetness like overripe plums. Nine years of his life had smelled like this room. That was the first betrayal. That was the thing that made his hands shake as he stepped inside—that the enemy's house smelled like *home*.

*They keep books,* he thought. Of course they kept books. Sable carried contracts; the tithe was collected against signatures; his mother's name had lived somewhere in ink for twenty-six years. But knowing a thing and standing inside its cathedral were different animals entirely. The Hollow Guild's archive in the capital held perhaps four hundred volumes. This room held four hundred thousand.

He had come looking for a weapon. He had told himself, descending the servants' stair behind the throne dais, that somewhere in the Undercourt there would be a record of his own contract—the original, the true copy, the one document in creation that could tell him exactly what his mother had signed away and exactly how much remained. A ledger-keeper's instinct. Find the entry. Read the terms. Every debt has a shape, and every shape can be argued with.

He had not expected to find the guild already here. Waiting for him. Reflected.

* *

He began, as he always began, with organization.

The nearest shelf held recent volumes—he could tell by the binding, supple and dark, and by the way the ink had not yet fully sunk into the page-souls of them. He pulled one at random and opened it across his palms, and the handwriting nearly stopped his heart.

It was Master Corvel's hand.

Not Corvel himself—Corvel was forty years dead, and Jude had watched the burial from the kitchen doorway with a cup of broth going cold in his hands. But the script was unmistakable: that narrow, upright, merciless cursive, each numeral dressed in its serifs like a servant in livery. Jude had copied that hand for six years as an apprentice, until his own writing bent toward it the way a boy's gait bends toward his father's.

The entry read:

*Received of Elen Marish, of Candlewick Row, one (1) memory of her husband alive, in payment of her son's coughing sickness, now cured. Condition: fair. Appraised at two years' warmth of winter. Entered by: —*

The attribution line was blank. Not redacted. *Blank*, as if whoever had entered it had lifted the pen and simply never known their own name.

Jude stood very still. Elen Marish. He had balanced that exact trade on the guild's side eleven years ago, in Ossen's back room, by the light of two candles because Ossen said three invited eavesdroppers. A widow on Candlewick Row had come to the guild with her husband's death still wet in her, and she had asked—not for money, she'd wanted no coin—but to have one night of grief removed so she could nurse her son through the white fever without weeping where he could see. The guild had granted it. The boy had lived. Jude had recorded the transaction under date and seal, entry number 4,117 in the year's book, and had thought nothing of it since.

And here it was again. Same widow. Same son. Same fever. Different book.

The Undercourt's version listed the same terms but a different appraisal: *two years' warmth of winter* against the guild's valuation of *one winter's grief, moderate*. The numbers were close enough to be a translation and different enough to be a second opinion. As if two merchants in two cities had both appraised the same cargo and each kept a copy of the other's bill of lading.

His mouth had gone dry. He turned pages. Another entry: a fisherman trading three decades of luck at sea for his daughter's safe childbirth. He had entered it himself—*himself*, he could see his own younger hand in the memory, careful, slightly cramped—and here it stood in the Undercourt's book, mirrored, countersigned by no one.

Page after page after page. Trades he had balanced. Trades Corvel had balanced before him. Trades, when he went deeper into the stacks, older than Corvel, older than the guild's charter, written in hands that shaded backward through history like sediment—the same transactions appearing on both sides of the world's only wall, recorded above and below, twin books kept in twin languages of ink.

Someone had been feeding it entries. For generations.

* *

"Curious, is it not."

Jude did not jump. It cost him something not to, and he paid that cost the way he paid all his costs, silently and in full, but he did not jump. He closed the ledger on his finger to mark the place and turned.

Sable stood between two shelves with her hands folded, her receipt-case hanging at her hip like a surgeon's bag. In the starlight that bled from the ceiling-far above—those stolen points of cold fire embedded in the Undercourt's vault like jewels in a black lid—her face was all planes and patience.

"You walk quietly," Jude said. "For someone who wants to be heard."

"I wanted you to have a moment alone with it." She came forward, and her footsteps made no sound at all, which in this room full of rustling paper seemed almost obscene. "Most people need a moment alone with it. The traders who come down. The ones who last long enough to find this room." Her eyes went to the open ledger with something that was not nostalgia—it was older than nostalgia, more worn. "They always think at first that we stole your books."

"Didn't you?"

"No." She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that he could see the grain of her skin, which was very fine and very wrong, like porcelain painted to look like pores. "Read the attributions, taster. Tell me who copies whom."

He looked. He looked for a long time. The blank lines stared back, dozens of them, hundreds, an army of unnamed clerks stretching down the centuries.

"There's no one," he said slowly. "No one signs."

"For four hundred years," Sable said. "In our books and in yours. Your founding charter lists no author. Your guildmaster's succession rolls break off for sixty years in the third century and resume in a hand no one recognizes. You thought that was negligence?" Something moved across her face that on a human being might have been a smile. "Your guild has no founder's grave, Jude Vane, because your guild has no founder. It has a *counterpart*. Two sets of books require two keepers, and neither keeper may be a person, because a person would ask why the columns match."

The plum-sweetness of the air seemed thicker now. Jude became aware of his own heartbeat and disliked how loud it was.

"You're saying the Hollow Guild answers to you. That Ossen reports downward."

"I'm saying," Sable said, "that when a widow on Candlewick Row sells her grief, the grief goes somewhere. When a smuggler sells his daughter's name, the name goes somewhere. Nothing given is destroyed, taster. It is *transferred*. And transfer requires a receiving account." She gestured, unhurried, at the miles of shelves. "You are standing in the receiving account."

"And the guild? The guild that taught me my letters and fed me poison by the half-drop and paid me nine years of wages—"

"The guild is a funnel," she said, gently, and the gentleness was the cruelest thing she had done yet. "A pretty shop-front with kind rules and small mercies, yes—I do not say it is nothing; I bought my own humanity through its doors once, in a manner of speaking. But every mercy it granted above was an entry flowing below. Every impossible thing it sold, it sold because the order came down. You have been keeping our ledgers for nine years, Jude Vane. You simply weren't told whose ink you mixed."

He set the ledger back on the shelf. He did it carefully, aligning the spine with the others, because his hands needed employment or they were going to do something theatrical, and he had not survived nine years of courts and kitchens by being theatrical.

"The wards," he said. "Two hundred years of border wards, funded by—" He had seen it. Three nights ago, in the contract vault, he had found the original instrument: the Crown's covenant, selling subjects' souls by the thousand to hold the borders against whatever pressed outside them. His mother's signature sat in the middle of that ocean of ink, a single wave. "The Crown sold souls to buy safety. Did the guild broker those sales too?"

Sable was quiet for a moment. Above them, impossibly far up, one of the stolen stars pulsed—or seemed to; in this place, seeming was a form of honesty.

"Every generation," she said, "the Crown's steward comes to the guild with a list. Names. The guild prices them, takes its commission, and passes the entries down. The wards are raised. The kingdom sleeps. And the books below grow by a volume." She tilted her head. "You look angry. I want to be accurate about it: are you angry at us, or at the arithmetic?"

"That's a clerk's question."

"I am a clerk. So are you. It is the only honest conversation we will ever have." She moved past him then, trailing one finger along the shelf-edges, and ledgers seemed to lean toward her touch like grass toward wind. "Come. There is something you should see before you go wherever you have decided to go. You were hunting your mother's contract—I know; you searched the vault badly and left the false drawer ajar. But if you truly want to understand what she signed, you must understand what she signed *into*. And that lives here."

She drew a volume from a shelf so deep in the stacks that the starlight gave out entirely, and darkness there was not absence but substance, thick as wool. When she brought it into the dim glow, Jude saw that the binding was not leather. It was skin, old and freckled, and he made himself look at it without flinching because flinching was also theatrical.

"This is the concordance," Sable said. "The master reconciliation. Once a century, someone brings the guild's total forward and sets it against ours." She opened it to a ribbon-marked page. "Do you know what I am going to show you?"

"No," said Jude. And then, because lying to a clerk is a fool's economy: "Yes. The columns don't match."

"They don't," she agreed. "Ours shows a surplus. Yours shows a deficit. Every century, the gap widens." She turned the book toward him. "For two hundred years, the guild above has recorded fewer receipts than the court below has recorded deposits. Do you understand what that means, taster? Somewhere between your world and mine, someone has been skimming souls."

The word landed in the silent room and rang there.

"Skimming," Jude repeated. His voice sounded strange to him, flattened, professional. He recognized the feeling underneath it: it was the feeling from the Queen's table, the day the memory-wine arrived from no kitchen at all. Dread, wearing the mask of procedure. "Souls can be stolen in transit."

"Everything can be stolen in transit," Sable said. "That is what transit is *for*." She closed the concordance with a sound like a door. "The King does not know about the gap. Or—he knows and permits it, which for him is the same posture. But you, Jude Vane, you balance books. Look at these two columns and tell me what a missing soul looks like. Because I will tell you what I have suspected across four centuries of service, and it is why I let you find this room tonight instead of reporting you at the door."

She leaned close. Her breath had no smell at all, which frightened him more than the plum-sweetness.

"A soul that enters the accounts below but was never properly sold above," she whispered, "isn't currency. It's a *forgery*. And there are thousands of them on our shelves. Someone has been counterfeiting the court's money for generations—and the King, who can smell a lie in any language, has never noticed, because the lies were written in his own native tongue."

She straightened. Somewhere far off in the stacks, a page turned itself.

"So keep your hands steady, taster, and your face quiet," said Sable, "because in six days the King means to make you his permanent ledger-keeper—and the last keeper who found this gap before you is bound in the binding of the book you are holding."

* * *

Jude did not remember deciding to walk. He was walking.

The stacks fell away behind him rank by rank, and the plum-sweetness thinned toward the colder stone-smell of the corridors, and still his feet carried him forward while his mind stayed pinned in that room, turning the two columns over and over like a coin he could not stop flipping.

*A deficit.* All his life, order had been the floor under him. Numbers did not lie; entries balanced or they did not; the world was cruel but it was *legible*, and legibility had been the one mercy a starving boy could rely on when nothing else was reliable. Now the mercy itself was rotted through. The guild that had raised him was a funnel. His nine years of careful work had been freight moving down a chute. And beneath even that betrayal lay the newer one, gleaming and terrible: somewhere in the dark between worlds, for two centuries, someone had been doing exactly what he had begun doing three nights ago in his cell—writing debts that did not exist, forging entries, counterfeiting souls.

He was not the first. He was only the latest in a long, hidden tradition of counterfeiters, and the previous holder of that tradition was stitched into a bookbinding ten paces behind him.

His mother's face surfaced in his memory—one of the few survivals, guarded now like flame in cupped hands: sharp-boned, tired, laughing once at something he could no longer retrieve, only the fact of the laugh. She had descended this same road pregnant and desperate. She had signed. She had believed, presumably, that the ledger was honest, because everyone believes the ledger is honest; that is the entire power of a ledger.

*Nothing given freely can be owned.* The guild's founding principle. He had quoted it to the King himself, days ago, in defiance. Now the principle rang differently in him—hollowed out, suspect, its founding voice anonymous. And yet. And yet. If the books were crooked, then the crookedness could be *found*. If souls had been skimmed, there was a skimmer, and a skimmed account is an account that can be audited.

He reached his borrowed cell—a niche off the servitors' gallery with a cot of gray velvet and a mirror he kept covered, having learned quickly what the mirrors here liked to do. He barred the door with a chair. Then he sat on the cot's edge, took out the stub of pencil and the scrap of paper he had hoarded, and wrote, in the smallest possible hand:

*Concordance exists. Columns diverge ~2 centuries. Gap = forged souls, thousands. Prior finder: bound into binding. Six days.*

He read it back. It looked absurdly little to show for a revelation that had rearranged the architecture of his life. Then he looked at the last line again, and the dread finally caught up with him and sat down heavily on his chest.

Six days. And the King, patient and ancient and courteous, had offered him the one post in creation he was uniquely fitted for—the post he had, in a sense, been apprenticed to since the day Corvel put the first half-drop of hemlock on his tongue. Ledger-keeper of the Undercourt. Keeper of the receiving account.

And now he knew something about that account that the King did not know the court knew.

Above the mountain, he thought, the stars were still dying, one per night, each extinguishment taking a memory from someone in the world who could ill afford the loss. Below the mountain, the books were crooked. And somewhere in the miles of shelving between those two facts lay either his destruction or the first real weapon anyone had ever carried into this court.

He covered the mirror. He blew out nothing, because there was no candle; the starlight never quite left this place, another theft repurposed as furniture. He lay back and listened to the Undercourt breathe around him, patient as interest accruing.

Six days. He had entered the enemy's house expecting to find a monster.

Instead he had found a colleague.
