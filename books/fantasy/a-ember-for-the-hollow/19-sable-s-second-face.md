## 19. Sable's Second Face

The contract lay open on the table before me like something dissected.

Two hundred years of entries. Two hundred years of names in the Crown's own hand—chancellors and chamberlains, stewards and secretaries, each of them signing away the souls of subjects who would never know they'd been spent. My mother's name was there, in the ninth column of the forty-first page, written in ink the color of dried blood: *Vane, one soul, whole and accruing, tendered against winter wards, plague year.* Beneath it, a clerk's annotation in a smaller, neater script: *Received in good order.*

Received in good order. As if my mother had been a shipment of grain.

I sat in the Ledger Room with the candle I'd been allowed—one candle, guttering, because the Undercourt did not need light and resented providing it—and I read until my eyes burned, and when I looked up, Sable was standing at the far end of the room.

She had not opened the door. The door was behind me, and I had heard nothing.

"You read quickly," she said. "For a dead man."

"I'm not dead yet."

"Thirty days is not yet." She came forward slowly, her footsteps making no sound on the stone, though every other sound in that room seemed too loud—the tick of cooling wax, the whisper of the candle's flame. "You've found the Crown's ledger."

"I've found the Crown's *theft*." I turned the book toward her, my finger on my mother's line. "Explain this to me. Explain how a kingdom sells its people to pay for its own protection, and calls it governance."

Sable looked down at the entry for a long moment. In the candlelight her face did what faces do when they forget themselves—a flicker, a softening, something almost like grief passing over features that had been built for stillness.

"It was called necessity," she said. "It is always called necessity."

"Not *was*. Is. You're still collecting."

"Yes." She said it without apology, without excuse, the way a river says *downhill*. "And you want to know why I do it. You've been circling the question since the mountain's mouth, Vane. Every time I come to you with papers and deadlines, you look at me the way your guild looks at a rival trader—cataloguing, appraising, waiting for the weakness to show. So." She drew out the chair across from me and sat, which she had never done before; in all our meetings she had remained standing, upright as a blade. "Ask."

I should have asked about the deadline. About the terms of forfeiture, about whether the King could be moved, about the twenty-six years of interest accruing on a soul sold before its owner drew breath. Those were the questions that mattered to my survival, and survival had been my discipline for nine years, and disciplines do not break easily.

But I looked at her hands—folded now on the table, pale and precise, and scarred. Scarred. I had never noticed scars on her before. They were old ones, faded to silver, and they ran across the backs of both hands in patterns I recognized, because I wore their brothers on my own.

Patterns made by testing blades. By pricked fingers. By a life spent putting unknown things into one's mouth so that someone richer would not have to.

"You were a taster," I said.

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"Four hundred years ago," Sable said, "there was a kingdom east of here whose name no longer exists, because names are currency too, and theirs was spent. I was born in the lowest quarter of its capital, in a winter much like your plague winter. I had a mother who loved me and no way to feed me, and the palace had an opening for a child with no future and a strong stomach." Her voice was level, unhurried, the voice of a woman reciting figures. "I took the position. I was very good at it. Better than good—I learned the poisons faster than they could be taught to me. I learned to taste arsenic in almond, hemlock in honey, the mineral ghost of lead in wine. I built myself into a fortress, dish by dish, the way I imagine you did."

"How do you know how I—"

"Because I have watched you for nine years, Jude Vane. Do you think the tithe-collector does not keep accounts? Your Master Corvel reported to us, indirectly, through three intermediaries who never knew whom they served. Your immunity was not only your master's investment. It was ours. A soul appreciating in value must be protected from premature spoilage." She smiled then, and it was the worst thing I had seen in the Undercourt, worse than the mirrored halls, worse than the ceiling of stolen stars—because the smile was warm, and human, and four hundred years out of practice. "You and I drank from the same cup, little brother. Only my cup was deeper."

"What happened?" I asked. "Your collection. What failed?"

For a long moment she did not answer. The candle burned between us, and somewhere far above—or below; direction meant nothing in that place—water dripped in a rhythm like slow counting.

"My king died," she said finally. "The mortal one. Poisoned by his brother, with a compound I had never tasted, because his brother had bought it from the court beneath the mountain—the same court, the same ledgers, the same patient arithmetic. They sold the poison that killed my charge, and then they collected the soul of the taster who failed, because failure, in their books, is also a debt." She unfolded her hands, and I saw that the scars continued up beneath her sleeves. "I was given a choice. It was framed as a choice. Serve as collector, or forfeit immediately. I chose service. I told myself I would find a way out from inside. I told myself many things, in the first century."

"And the second?"

"The second century, I stopped telling myself things." She met my eyes, and hers were the color of coins left too long in the earth. "That is what the court does, Vane. It does not torture. Torture is crude; torture creates resistance, and resistance creates stories, and stories are dangerous. The court simply waits. Debt is patient. Debt knows that everything erodes—hope, hatred, memory, love—all of it wears down like stone under water, until one day you cannot remember why you ever wanted to leave. The King has never lost a negotiation in the history of the world. Do you understand? Not one. Because he does not negotiate. He merely holds the paper and lets time do the winning for him."

I thought of Brannic, somewhere in the dark corridors above or below, carrying twenty years of guilt like ballast. I thought of the village of the unremembered, with its smiling, hollow hosts. I thought of my notebooks back in the capital, filling page by page with everything I was afraid to lose.

"You're telling me this to frighten me."

"I'm telling you this because you looked at my hands." Something moved behind her face—quick, suppressed, like a fish turning under ice. "No one has looked at my hands in three hundred years. They see the collector. The receipt with legs. You saw the scars, and you knew, because you are the first taster they've taken since me who might actually be worth taking." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped to something almost intimate, almost kind. "So hear me, Jude Vane, and hear me once, because I will deny saying it if you repeat it. You cannot beat him at bargaining. Whatever cleverness you're carrying in those guild-trained fingers of yours—whatever forgery, whatever loophole, whatever arithmetic—you are speaking to him in his native tongue, and no one has ever out-spoken a native in his own country. He will let you think you're winning. He will give you ground. He will smile and extend terms and appear, once, briefly, to lose—because a debt that thinks it has escaped accrues more interest than a debt that knows it's caught."

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

"I don't know." And there it was again, that flicker, that terrible human motion under the stillness. "Four hundred years, and I don't know. But I know what doesn't work. Bargains don't work. Cleverness doesn't work. Running doesn't work—the mountain has no outside. If there is a door out of this place, it isn't a door he built, and it isn't a door he's watching, and I have never found it." She rose, smoothing her coat, the collector reassembling herself piece by piece like armor being buckled on. "Your thirty days stand. Twenty-two remain. Spend them however you like. That, at least, is honest: he always lets you choose the manner of your surrender. He says choice improves the flavor."

She turned toward the door.

"Sable."

She paused. Did not turn around.

"When you were human," I said, "before all of this—was there anything you kept? Anything they didn't take?"

Her shoulders went very still. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, a ledger-voice, a voice reading numbers aloud so it wouldn't have to feel them.

"There was a boy," she said. "A cooper's son, with flour in his hair and a laugh like a cart going downhill. I was going to marry him in the spring. I can tell you that he existed. I can tell you his name was Aldous and that he whistled while he worked and that he gave me a carved wooden thrush on the festival of first thaw." She turned her head, just slightly, enough that I caught her profile against the dark. "I can tell you all of it the way you'd read it from a page. But the feeling of it—the weight of his hand, the reason I said yes—that was collected in my first year of service. It was worth a great deal. First loves appreciate beautifully." A pause. "They auctioned it last month. Someone bought it. I hope they enjoy it. I hope it fits them better than it fit me."

The door closed behind her without a sound.

* * *

I sat alone in the Ledger Room for a long time after that, with the candle burning down and the Crown's great book open before me, and I confess that for some portion of it I simply breathed—in, out, the oldest discipline, the one that comes before all others.

Pity is a liability. Master Corvel told me that when I was eleven years old and weeping over a kitchen cat that had gotten into the testing scraps. *Tenderness is a door, boy, and doors admit things.* Nine years of tasting had taught me the truth of it: the taster who pities the dish hesitates, and the hesitation is where the poison gets in.

But pity is also information. That was the other thing Corvel taught, later, when I was older and harder: *Everything you feel tells you something about what's in front of you. Fear means bitterness. Longing means sweetness. Pity—" and here he had tapped my chest with one dry finger—"pity means you've found something broken that still remembers being whole.*

Sable was broken. Four hundred years broken, polished smooth by service, worn into a shape the court found useful. And she had come to me—not with papers, not with deadlines, but with a story. With her hands. With a boy named Aldous and a wooden thrush and the confession that everything soft in her had been sold off and purchased by strangers.

Why?

I turned back to the contract and forced myself to think like a ledger-keeper instead of a man. Facts first.

Fact: she had warned me the King could not be beaten at bargaining. Fact: she had told me this freely, at cost to herself, if indeed she could still incur costs. Fact: she had watched me for nine years—had, by her own admission, helped preserve me, protect my value, keep the merchandise unspoiled. Fact: she had said I was the first taster since her worth taking.

Worth taking. Or—

I pulled the Crown's ledger toward me and began, methodically, to search. Not for my mother's name this time. For older names. For the earliest entries, two hundred years back, when the first chancellor had signed the first ward-contract. And there, in the founding pages, in ink gone brown as old tea, I found the clause I should have been looking for all along.

*All debts herein are assignable,* it read. *Collectors appointed by the Court shall hold lien upon all sums outstanding, together with such interest as accrues to their office.*

Assignable. Lien. Interest accruing *to their office*.

Sable wasn't just the collector. She was collateral. Four hundred years of service, and her own contract—her own soul, her own self—was bound into the machinery she operated. Every debt she collected serviced the debt she owed. She wasn't the King's servant. She was part of his balance sheet, an asset that walked and spoke and carried receipts, and if the King's holdings were ever threatened—if the court's authority were ever challenged at its root—then the first thing to be liquidated would be her.

She hadn't come to warn me out of kindness. Kindness, maybe, was beyond her now, the way the feeling of Aldous's hand was beyond her. She had come because I was the first threat the court had seen in centuries, and a threatened asset will do strange things. An asset that remembers being human, most strange of all.

Or perhaps—my pen hovered over my notebook, and I wrote this down slowly, watching the letters form in the candlelight—perhaps it was both. Perhaps pity and self-interest had grown together so long in her that even she could no longer tell which root fed which. That, too, was a kind of information. The court did not make creatures incapable of mercy. It made creatures whose mercy had been priced, liened, and folded into the accounts, until mercy itself became a transaction they could not perform without the King's implicit signature.

Debt is the court's native tongue. She had said it herself. Which meant that if I wanted to speak to anything human left in Sable, I would have to stop speaking the court's language entirely.

I closed the Crown's ledger and sat in the dark a while, thinking about auctions.

Because that was the next thing. I had counted the days; I had done the arithmetic of my remaining memories; and I knew that words alone would not buy me what I needed now—not audience, not leverage, not time. The Undercourt respected one thing above all others, and that was price. Everything here was valued. Everything here was bid upon. Somewhere in the dark below the Ledger Room, I had been told, there was a market that never closed, where the sold and the salvaged changed hands under lights that never warmed.

If I could not beat the King at his own game, then perhaps I needed to change the game. To walk into his market and put something of mine on the block—not because I wanted to sell it, but because a seller sets the terms, and a seller learns who bids, and a bidder who wants a thing reveals how much the thing is worth.

And perhaps, if I watched carefully enough—at the edges of the crowd, in the pauses between offers—I would see what four hundred years of service had left unsold in Sable. Some small lot, overlooked. Some carved wooden thrush of a memory, gathering dust in a corner of the market, waiting for someone to recognize what it had cost.

Twenty-two days. I snuffed the candle, and in the dark I began, quietly, to count what I had left that anyone would want.
