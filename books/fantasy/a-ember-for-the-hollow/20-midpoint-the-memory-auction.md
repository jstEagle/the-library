## 20. Midpoint: The Memory Auction

The market beneath the mountain opened at no hour Jude could name, because there was no hour to open in. Time down here was a currency like everything else, and the Undercourt spent it sparingly. He knew the market had begun when the sound reached him—a low, wet murmur, like a tide deciding whether to come in—and Sable appeared at his elbow with her lantern of green glass and said, "If you want an audience with the King on anything but his terms, you'll need standing. Standing is bought. And you have nothing to buy it with except yourself."

"I've been told that before," Jude said. "By better salesmen."

Sable's face did not change. Four hundred years had worn her expressions down to a smoothness like river stone, but her eyes flicked—just once—toward the market's mouth, and he understood that this was as close as she came to warning him.

* * *

The market was held in a cavern shaped like the inside of a bell, and it rang. Not with bells—with voices. Hundreds of them, layered, murmuring, some of them speaking languages that predated the kingdom above, and the ceiling was hung with lanterns that were not lanterns at all but bottled sounds, each one glowing faintly with whatever had been said inside it. A last apology. A christening. Someone's dying words, sold by a family who needed the money more than the memory.

Jude walked through it the way he had once walked through plague streets: hands still, face empty, cataloguing.

That was the discipline Master Corvel had beaten into him across nine years of small poisons. *Feel nothing until you know what you're feeling.* So he catalogued. A stall where a woman with moth-winged shoulders sold afternoons by the handful—loose hours, unlabeled, going cheap. A stall where a man bought back his own childhood in installments, weeping with gratitude each time, and Jude did the arithmetic without wanting to: at that rate of purchase, the man would die owing himself. A stall that sold silence, which was apparently rare down here, and expensive.

And everywhere, everywhere, the ledgers. Little books bound in skin he chose not to identify, chained to wrists, inked in silver that moved. The Undercourt kept accounts the way the Hollow Guild did—the same columns, the same careful hands—and Jude had spent three days in the ledger room confirming what he'd already begun to suspect: someone above had been feeding these books for generations. The enemy spoke his language fluently. That was the horror of it. Not that the court was alien, but that it was *familiar*, that its mathematics were clean and its manners were impeccable and every cruelty in it had been entered twice, debited and credited, in a fair hand.

"Lot forty-one," cried a crier whose voice came from three mouths at once. "One summer evening, aged nine, catching fireflies in a jar. Condition: excellent. Seller motivated."

A jar sat on the block containing nothing visible. But when the bidding began, the nearest bidders' faces went soft and far away, and Jude realized they could smell it—that particular amber light, the grass itch, the small triumph of the lid closing over light. He could almost smell it too, and his chest went tight, because lot forty-one might have been his. It might have been anyone's. That was the genius of the place: every memory for sale here was generic until purchased, and then it became yours forever, and the person it had been taken from went on walking around above with a soft spot where the evening used to be.

He had come here to do exactly that to himself.

"You're hesitating," Sable said.

"I'm pricing," Jude said. "There's a difference."

"There isn't. Not here." She turned to face him fully then, and up close her age showed—not wrinkles, but a kind of translucency, as if she had been handled so often she was wearing thin. "Listen to me, taster. I have watched four centuries of sellers walk onto that block, and they all believe they are different. They believe they are selling dross and keeping gold. They are wrong. The court prices fairly. That is its cruelest feature. Whatever you put up will fetch its true worth, and you will have to stand there and watch strangers pay it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you go to the King with empty hands, and he will be courteous, and he will give you tea, and he will own you by winter." She paused. "You want time. An audience costs standing; standing costs coin; and your only mint is in your skull. Auction yourself, buy your hour, and use the hour well. Or don't. I am not permitted to advise you. Only to collect you."

"Not permitted," Jude repeated softly. "Or not willing?"

Something moved behind Sable's eyes, deep down, like a fish under ice. She did not answer, and he filed that away with everything else—in the notebook in his head, the one he'd been keeping since the sea slipped out of him, since he'd woken one morning unable to remember the sound of waves and had to ask Brannic what water sounded like when it was big.

Brannic waited near the market's edge, arms crossed, watching the stalls with the flat hatred of a man reading a price list he'd been on once. Twenty years he'd carried the loss of his daughter's name—had carried it so long he sometimes called her *girl* instead of Wren, because the word snagged on the hole where it used to live. When Jude told him the plan, Brannic had been silent a long moment and then said only: "Watch what they bid. You'll learn what you're worth. Most men never get to know that, and most men are luckier."

Now the auctioneer's triple voice called the lots, and Jude stepped toward the block before his discipline could fail him.

* * *

The auction block was a disc of black stone that floated above the crowd, and when Jude mounted it, the murmuring below quieted into attention. Attention, he would learn, was itself a commodity here. Two hundred faces turned up at him—some human or nearly, some not, some wearing bodies the way other people wore borrowed coats—and among them he spotted the moth-winged woman, the installment-buyer, a child who was either a child or very old.

"The seller offers," announced the triple voice, "memories from a life above. Unverified provenance. The court appraises honestly."

Jude had prepared. He had spent the night in a cell of guest-quarters that was comfortable the way a trap is comfortable, writing lists: what he owned in his head, what it might fetch, what he could bear to lose. His skill with numbers—he'd keep that; it was load-bearing now. His hatred—for the King, for the crown above that had sold thousands of mothers' children to fund its wards; keep that too; hatred was fuel. One afternoon of sunlight, a specific afternoon, the one he'd measured all other happiness against since—he would sell that last, if ever, and hoped he wouldn't have to.

But first, the cheap things. The things he told himself were cheap.

"First lot," he said, and his voice held, because nine years of tasting poison in front of queens teaches a man to hold his voice. "My first kiss."

It rose out of him gently, the way a splinter leaves the flesh when the flesh has softened around it. For a moment he stood inside it again—rain on a awning, a girl named Liss with flour on her jaw from her mother's bakery, the shocking warmth of her mouth tasting of yeast and courage—and then it detached, and he felt it go, and the crowd made a sound like a sigh moving through wheat.

The bidding was fast. Three buyers, then five. A pale gentleman in a coat of grey feathers took it at eleven marks of unmarked silver, and Jude watched the man's face as the memory settled into him—watched Liss's rain-warm mouth become a stranger's treasure—and felt something in his chest go carefully, deliberately numb. *Feel nothing until you know what you're feeling.* He knew what he was feeling. He priced it anyway, because that was the work.

"Second lot," he said. "An ordinary morning. Bread, coffee, sunlight through a kitchen window. Nothing happens in it. Nothing at all."

That one fetched less—four marks—and the buyer, a tired-looking clerk-thing with too many fingers, clutched it like a beggar given bread. Jude understood then, with a lurch, that ordinariness was precious down here precisely because none of the court could make it. They could buy any ecstasy ever felt, but they could not manufacture a Tuesday. The Undercourt was full of beings drunk on other people's mornings.

He sold a storm watched from a doorway. He sold the smell of his mother's hands—bread and lamp-oil and something herbal underneath, feverfew maybe, though the herb-name came to him now with a slight delay, like a word in a language he was forgetting. Each sale left a smooth place. Not pain, exactly. Absence. He was becoming a house with rooms cleared out for new tenants, and the tenants were strangers, and the rent they paid was silver he didn't want.

Then the crier's triple voice said, "The seller offers: a lullaby. Sung by the seller's mother. Provenance: plague winter. Condition: worn, much-loved."

"No," Jude heard himself say.

But the lot was already rising in him, because he had listed it—God help him, he had *listed* it, in his neat guild hand, as a mid-value asset—and the market had accepted his own inventory as consent. The lullaby lifted out of him note by note, and he stood on the black stone hearing it leave: his mother's voice, roughened by the cough that would kill her, singing about a ember that keeps its glow under ash, *keep the ember, hollow one, keep the ember through the dark*—

The bidding exploded.

Not three buyers. Not ten. Half the market surged forward at once, creatures and ex-humans and things that had forgotten their own names pressing against the block's edge, and the bids climbed past anything the kiss had fetched, past anything, and Jude stood in the center of it watching his mother's voice get argued over like a bolt of cloth, and the numbness broke.

"Stop," he said. "Withdraw the lot."

"Withdrawal forfeits the deposit," said the triple voice, pleasant as a knife being oiled. "The seller agreed to terms."

Jude looked down at the crowd—at the moth-winged woman with her hand raised, at the grey-feathered gentleman, at a huge slow thing at the back that bid in a voice like a landslide—and he did the only mathematics available to him. The lullaby would sell regardless now; withdrawal would just cost him the deposit and buy the crowd his desperation. Better to choose the buyer than be chosen by the market.

"One condition," he said loudly. "Whoever buys it—I want to hear where it goes. I want to hear it sung. Once. In my presence."

Silence. Then, from the back, the landslide voice: "Agreed."

The huge thing came forward—a shape like a hill that had learned to walk, mossy, patient, old beyond sense—and paid a sum that made the auctioneer's three mouths go briefly reverent. And then, while the whole market watched, it opened what served it for a mouth and sang.

The lullaby came out wrong. Of course it did. The notes were right but the voice was stone and moss and twenty thousand years of patience, and hearing his mother's song in that throat was like seeing her face on a stranger. But the words survived the crossing. *Keep the ember, hollow one.* And the market listened the way the market never listened to anything—hushed, hungry, grieving for something it couldn't name—and Jude stood on the block with tears running down his face and did not wipe them, because wiping them would have cost him composure, and composure was the last thing he owned outright.

When it ended, the hill-thing inclined its vast head to him. "Your mother," it rumbled, "sang that to someone else once. Before you. Ask your King."

* * *

Afterward, Sable found him sitting on the steps outside the bell-cavern, counting his silver by touch because looking at it made his stomach turn. Eleven lots sold. Enough standing to buy his audience. Enough, perhaps, to survive the King's courtesy.

"You did well," she said.

"Don't." He didn't look up. "Don't tell me I did well. I just watched a rock sing my mother to sleep and half the Undercourt bid on her voice."

"Yes," Sable said. "I know. I sold mine four hundred years ago. My mother's voice, I mean. I still remember the auction." Her translucent face turned toward the dark. "What I don't remember is the song."

Jude looked up at her then, and for one moment the collector and the collected were the same thing, and both of them knew it.

"The hill-thing said my mother sang it to someone else. Before me."

"I know," Sable said. "I was there."

She said it without drama, without mercy, the way one states an entry in a ledger, and then she held out her hand for his silver, because standing must be purchased, and the audience he had bled for was granted for tomorrow—or for whatever the Undercourt used instead of tomorrow—and somewhere above them, under a sky with no stars in it, a girl named Wren was waking up with a name she hadn't had yesterday, and a King who always won was already smiling at the news that the taster had finally begun paying.
