## 21. What I Kept

The cell they gave me after the auction was not a cell at all. That was the first cruelty of it.

It was a room in the Undercourt's guesting wing, if a place that had never hosted a guest could be said to have one—a chamber of grey stone gone soft with age, hung with tapestries that depicted hunts no animal had ever died in. The hounds in them were woven from shadow. The quarry was always just beyond the frame. I lay on a bed that was too comfortable to be kind and listened to the mountain breathe around me, a slow tidal sound like lungs the size of cathedrals, and I took stock of myself the way Master Corvel had taught me to take stock of a dish: what is present, what is absent, what has been added that does not belong.

I had sold eleven memories at the auction. Eleven lots, hammered down to strangers whose faces I had already begun to forget, which was the point of the market and its most elegant obscenity. You could watch your own past walk away on someone else's face and feel nothing but a faint transactional satisfaction, because the part of you that would have grieved went with the lot.

So: inventory. What remained of Jude Vane.

* * *

I sat on the floor with my back against the bedframe and did it properly, as I did everything—by candlelight, in columns, because numbers were still mine. Numbers had never been for sale. There was no market for arithmetic; the Undercourt dealt in feeling, in years, in names, and the dry machinery of calculation bored it. My skill with figures sat inside me like a stone tool, useless to them, indispensable to me.

Item the first: I could count. Not merely count—I could *balance*. Nine years of the Hollow Guild's ledgers had taught me to hold a hundred accounts in my head at once, to feel an error the way a taster feels almond in a cup of wine, a wrongness on the tongue before the mind names it. That sense had not been sold. It could not be, I suspected, because it was not memory at all but something closer to instinct, something that lived below the waterline where the tithe-collector's hooks could not reach.

Item the second: my hatred. I tested it carefully, the way you press a bruise to learn its shape. It was intact. The hatred of Sable's receipt, of the redacted margin where my name should have been, of two centuries of kings selling their subjects by the soul-weight to keep their pretty wards alight—it all came when I called it, whole and hot and entirely mine. I wondered, briefly, whether hatred counted as a memory or something else, and decided it didn't matter. The King could have it when he pried it out of me at auction, and I would drive the price up first.

Item the third—and this one I had to sit with a long while—one afternoon of sunlight.

I don't know why that one survived. It was nothing. It should have been the first thing the market ate. But there it was: a specific afternoon, late summer, I would have been perhaps nine, lying in the dry grass behind the tannery in the town where I was born—the sea-town, though the sea itself was already gone from me, sold in Lot the First to a woman with a moth-winged collar—and the sun lay on me like a hand. Warmth through the shirt. The smell of cut grass and hot wood. A bee investigating my elbow with grave professional interest. Someone was calling a name in the distance, some other child's name, and I remember thinking with enormous contentment that it wasn't mine, that nobody wanted anything from me just then, that the afternoon asked nothing and gave everything.

One afternoon of sunlight. That was the whole estate of my childhood now: a warmth without a face attached, a bee, a name that wasn't mine being called for someone luckier.

I found I was crying, which interested me in a distant way, because I couldn't have said what I was grieving. Grief without its object. The guild bottled such things and sold them by the dram. Down here they'd have fetched a good price.

I wiped my face and got back to work. Corvel's voice in my head, cold as ever: *Sentiment is a liability, boy. Catalogue it and move on.*

* * *

They let me walk the court. That surprised me until I understood it. The Undercourt had no locks because it had no need of them—every corridor ended either in the mirrored dark of the deep galleries or in the throne room, and both were worse than any cell. Besides, I think the King found me interesting. A debt that walked and argued and kept books. He had told me once, in that voice like dust settling in an empty house, that curiosity was the oldest currency of all, and that he hoarded it.

So I walked. And I learned the geography of the ledger room.

It stood at the heart of the court, past the gallery where the stolen stars were mortared into the ceiling like jewels in a crown—our stars, the sky's stars, burning cold white down here while the world above went starless and afraid. The ledger room's doors were black wood carved with a motto I recognized, because a version of it hung over the Hollow Guild's own counting-house:

*ALL THINGS ARE OWED BY SOMEONE.*

Inside, the books went back further than the guild's did. Further than the kingdom's. Shelves receded into darkness in ranks, and each volume breathed faintly when you touched it, the way the guild's ledgers did not, because these were not records *of* souls—they were made *of* them. Vellum from somewhere I preferred not to imagine. Ink that moved when you weren't looking.

The clerks who tended them were not people. They had been people once—I could see the residue, the way you can see a fire's shape in ash—but they had been rendered down into function, into hands and eyes and the patient motion of the pen. They wrote without stopping. They had been writing, I understood, since before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

And here was the thing I had noticed on my second visit, the thing that had kept me awake on the too-comfortable bed, turning it over like a coin suspected of being clipped:

The Undercourt's entries matched the Hollow Guild's.

Not approximately. *Exactly.* Every trade Ossen's traders had recorded above—every widow's laughter sold for her son's fever, every borrowed year, every bottled grief—the same transaction appeared below, entered in the same hand, dated the same day, valued at the same rate. Two sets of books, mirror-perfect, across a hundred miles of stone.

There were only two explanations. Either the guild was feeding the court—every entry copied, every trade reported, a pipeline running from our counting-house down into this dark—or the court was the guild, the deep foundation under the shallow building, and Ossen's operation was nothing but a retail front for a wholesale business older than kingdoms.

Either way, someone above was keeping the court's books current.

And either way, the court trusted its ledger absolutely. That was the lesson of the place, written in the breathing vellum and the tireless clerk-hands: down here, the book *was* the truth. If the ledger said a soul was owed, it was owed. If the ledger said a debt was paid, it was paid, and no argument in any tongue could unpay it. Sable had told me once that debt was the court's native language. She hadn't mentioned that the court was also illiterate in every other—that it could not conceive of a fact existing anywhere except in an entry.

A taster learns to find the flaw in the dish. A ledger-keeper learns to find the flaw in the book. And every book has one, because every book is kept by hands, and hands want things.

Mine wanted my soul back.

* * *

I began small.

That is the whole art of forgery—not the grand stroke but the modest one, the entry so ordinary no eye snags on it. Corvel had taught me poisons the same way: start with doses too small to notice, build the body's tolerance grain by grain until the dose that would kill a king barely warms you. So I started with grains.

My first forged entry was a widow's debt. Some woman in some village above, dead these hundred years, who had supposedly borrowed six months of health from the court in her final winter and died before paying. I invented her out of whole cloth—name, village, ailment, terms—and wrote her into a side-ledger in a clerk-hand I had spent three nights practicing, copying the penmanship of the thing that had once been a person until my wrist ached and my copy was indistinguishable from the original.

Then I assigned her debt a holder. A creditor. And the creditor I chose was a name I dug out of the deepest shelf, from a contract so old its ink had gone brown: a minor functionary of the court, some steward-of-tithes whose own entry showed he had been owed a trifling sum since before the wards were built and had never collected, because the debtor's line had died out.

I transferred the widow's fictional debt to him. Then I marked it paid—in full, with interest, in the only currency the court minted: years. Sixty years, credited to his account.

On paper, that forgotten steward became one of the wealthiest beings in the Undercourt.

Nothing happened. No alarm. No tremor in the breathing shelves. The clerks wrote on. Somewhere in the dark, a functionary who had been poor for two thousand years was suddenly rich and didn't know it yet.

I sat back from the desk I had claimed—a clerk's station abandoned in a far corner, its occupant presumably rendered down elsewhere—and I felt something I had not felt since the stars went out.

Power. Small, poisoned, dangerous power. But mine.

* * *

Over the next days—for time down there was a courtesy the court extended and withdrew as it pleased—I built the structure out.

Forgery, done properly, is not writing lies. Anyone can write lies. Forgery is building a lie so load-bearing that the truth rests on it comfortably, and then walking away before anyone checks the mortar. So I did not simply invent wealth; I invented *history*. A debt implies a transaction; a transaction implies a contract; a contract implies witnesses. I forged backward from my widow to her fictional father, who had allegedly traded a memory of the sea—

I stopped when I reached that word. Sat very still. The sea. My sea, the one I'd sold, the one I could no longer picture except as a blue absence shaped like grief. Somewhere above, a stranger owned it now, wore it, maybe stood on real shores letting my waves break over their ankles.

I wrote the entry anyway. Let the court traffic in seas. I would drown it in counterfeits until no one could tell which debts were real—and when the moment came, when the audit arrived, the whole book would be suspect. Every entry questioned. Every valuation frozen. You cannot collect on a ledger no one trusts.

That was the theory, anyway. The theory of a food-taster who had learned his trade from a cold man in a cold kitchen: don't fight the poison head-on. Dilute it. Make the cup so uncertain that the king himself cannot drink safely.

Entry by entry, night by night, I salted the Undercourt's books. Debts that had never existed, held by creditors who had never lived, paid in currency that had never been minted. I forged remissions—debts forgiven, which the court's theology insisted was impossible, forgiveness being the one transaction with no exchange. I forged reversals: souls marked returned to their owners, lines of entry crossed through in the clerk-hand with a notation of *paid in full, discharged, no further claim*, which if it were ever believed would unravel contracts going back two centuries. Including, buried among forty others so it would not stand alone, one contract signed in my mother's hand.

I worked with my hatred banked low and steady, like a coal. I worked with my one afternoon of sunlight spread out in memory beside the inkpot, warming my hands when they shook. I worked with the numbers, always the numbers, columns and columns of them, and the strange peace of a craft practiced well even in hell.

Once, deep in the third night of it, one of the clerk-things paused at my shoulder. Its pen hovered. Its face was smooth as an egg where features had been, but it turned toward me, and I felt its attention the way you feel weather change.

"You are not entered," it said. Its voice was the sound of a page turning.

"Neither are you," I said. "Not anymore."

It considered this for a long moment. Then it resumed writing, and I told myself the pause had meant nothing, and did not believe myself.

* * *

It was on what I judged to be the ninth day that I made my mistake, and I want it recorded that I knew it was a mistake while I was making it, and made it anyway, because I am my mother's son and she also paid prices she could see coming.

I had grown bold. The forged architecture stood three layers deep now, self-referencing, elegant. And I found myself at the shelf where the crown's own contracts slept—the royal debts, the two-century river of sold subjects that funded the wards above—and I thought: *one more*. One more reversal, one more discharge, entered against the crown's master account. A needle in the haystack of needles. If the audit came, let them find the whole royal treasury of souls marked *paid*.

I wrote it. Crown of the Upper Realm, debtor. Subjects enumerated: four thousand, one hundred and twelve. Status: discharged. Authority cited: an internal instrument I invented on the spot, sealed with a sigil I designed to look older than it was.

The ink dried. And every light in the ledger room dimmed.

Not went out—dimmed, the way a candle gutters when a door opens somewhere in the house. The clerk-things all stopped writing at once, hundreds of pens lifted mid-stroke, and the silence was total and enormous, the silence of a machine holding its breath.

Far off, through miles of stone, I heard the throne room's doors open. I heard footsteps that did not hurry, because nothing in the Undercourt had hurried in ten thousand years, and they were coming this way, unhurried, patient, amused.

I closed the forged ledger with steady hands. Whatever came through those doors, I would meet it with a straight spine and clean cuffs, the way Corvel taught me to meet a dish I already knew was poisoned: taste it calmly, smile at the Queen, and die slowly enough to buy someone else time.

But under the calm, where the tithe-collector's hooks couldn't reach, the arithmetic was already running, cold and clear: *He knows. He has known since the widow. The question is not whether you're caught—it's what he wants from you now.*

The doors of the ledger room opened without being touched.

And the Patient King stepped through, smiling, and said, "Ledger-keeper. We should talk about your arithmetic."
