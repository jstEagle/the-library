## 22. The King Notices

The forgery held for six days.

Jude counted them the way he counted everything now—in entries, in columns, in the small clean arithmetic of what remained. Six days of walking the Undercourt's mirrored corridors with a ledger under his arm, adding debts that had never been incurred, and each day the false numbers sat in the great book like well-behaved children, quiet and plausible, and each night he went back to his cell of a room above the market and slept without dreaming, because dreaming required memories and he was down to a handful.

He had learned the Undercourt's accounting language by then. That was the terrible thing about it—the ease. The Hollow Guild's ledgers and the court's master book were dialects of the same tongue, the way two villages on opposite sides of a mountain might speak words that were almost the same but for the vowels. *Obligation carried forward. Consideration received. Interest accruing at the rate of forgetting.* He had spent nine years balancing books that traded in impossible things, and it turned out all that time he had been practicing for this: a market beneath the world where souls were currency and every price was negotiable except the ones already paid.

The forged entries were simple in concept. A debt is only a story told in numbers—a promise that someone owes someone something. And the Undercourt's whole authority rested on its books being true. If the court could show a soul owed to it, the soul belonged to it. Jude could not steal his own soul out of the King's vault; he had looked; there was no vault, not exactly, only the ledger itself, the entry that said *Vane, Jude—consideration received, balance outstanding.* So he did the next best thing, the thing a taster learns before anything else: he adulterated the supply.

He wrote in debts owed *to* the court by parties who did not exist. A miller in a village that had burned forty years ago, owing three years of his life. A midwife owing a name. Small sums, scattered wide, the kind of entries no one audits because they are too small to matter and too sad to question. Then he used those phantom assets as collateral—offsets, in guild parlance—to reduce his own outstanding balance, entry by entry, night by night, the way water wears a groove in stone.

By the sixth morning, the ledger said he owed the Undercourt almost nothing. By the seventh, it would have said he was owed something himself—a refund, absurdly, of overpayment—and he had planned to present that to Sable and see what her face did.

There was no seventh morning.

* * *

They came for him an hour before the market opened, when the stalls were still shuttered and the vendors—those pale, patient things that had once been people—were unwinding their awnings of grey silk. Two ushers made of candle-smoke and courtesy. They did not touch him. They never touched anyone here; touching implied transfer, and transfer implied value, and value implied consent, and consent was the one coin the court could not mint.

"The King will see you," said the first usher, in a voice like paper turning.

"Now?" Jude said.

"He has been seeing you," said the second, "for some time."

The throne room of the Undercourt was the throne room of the royal palace turned inside out, the way a glove turns inside out when you pull it off in a hurry. Jude had stood in the palace's throne room a thousand mornings, tasting the Queen's breakfast while courtiers watched him swallow and waited to see if he died. That room was tall and cold and full of light from high windows. This one was low and warm and lit entirely from above—the stolen stars set into the ceiling like jewels in a black crown, each one burning with a light that had once belonged to the sky over the capital, over the sea where he'd grown up, over every road he had ever walked.

He did not look up. Looking up meant wanting, and wanting was how the court fed.

The Patient King sat on a throne of stacked contracts—not metaphorically; Jude could see the edges of them, ten thousand documents fused into a seat, the oldest at the bottom gone soft and brown as leaf-mould. He was not large. He was not small. He was the exact size of whatever room he was in, which was somehow worse than either. His face was kind the way a scale is kind: perfectly balanced, indifferent, precise.

"Jude Vane," the King said, and smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. "Sit. No—stand, if you prefer. You've spent your life standing while others eat. I imagine it's comfortable."

"I'm comfortable nowhere," Jude said. "It's a professional condition."

"Good. Honesty. You'd be surprised how rare it is down here." The King gestured, and a table appeared between them—or rather, Jude realized, it had always been there, and the King had simply permitted him to notice it. On it lay the master ledger, open to the page with his name. "Do you know what I admire most about your work? Not the forgeries themselves. Any competent counterfeiter can make a number. It's the *bookkeeping*. You didn't just write false debts—you wrote false debts that *aged correctly*. You gave them provenance. A miller who died in the Ashfall fires, owing three years—that entry has a smell to it. Wear. You understood that a lie must be maintained the way a garden is maintained, daily, or it dies."

"You're not angry," Jude said.

"Angry?" The King laughed, and the stars overhead flickered, all of them at once, a shudder running through the ceiling like wind through wheat. "My dear boy. I have been angry four times in eleven hundred years, and each time cost me a province. No. I am *delighted*. Do you know how long it has been since anyone tried to cheat me? Centuries of debtors coming down here weeping, bargaining, offering their mothers, their children, their eyes. Honest misery. Boring as rain. And then you arrive—a food-taster, a clerk, a man whose entire soul was collateral on a plague winter—and within a month you're forging entries in my own hand-style. Because you studied mine. You learned my handwriting." He leaned forward, and the warmth of the room leaned with him. "That is the most flattering thing anyone has done to me since the wards were built."

"It wasn't flattery," Jude said. "It was theft."

"Yes," the King agreed warmly. "Exactly. You see why I want you."

* * *

The offer came the way Jude had known it would—not as a threat, but as a kindness. That was the court's native grammar. Everything here was phrased as a gift.

"Here is your position," the King said. "Your balance stands at"—he touched the ledger, and the numbers rearranged themselves like insects regrouping—"considerably more than you have managed to hide. Your memory stands at"—another touch—"a lullaby, an afternoon, a hatred, and a talent for sums. In thirty days, less now, the tithe completes, and everything you are comes home to roost. Standard terms. You know them. You've read them often enough."

"I've written them," Jude said.

"Even better. Now consider the alternative." The King folded his hands, and Jude saw that his fingers were very long and very clean, the hands of someone who has never once done work. "Serve as my ledger-keeper. Permanently. The Hollow Guild kept accounts for the surface; you would keep them for everything. Every soul ever sold, every year ever borrowed, every grief ever bottled—the whole architecture of exchange, in your hands. You would be the most powerful clerk in creation."

"And in return?"

"You keep yourself. A curated edition." The King's voice softened into something almost tender, and that tenderness was the most frightening thing in the room, more than the stars, more than the throne of contracts. "I am not a monster, Jude Vane. I don't want your suffering. Suffering is just noise; I want *order*. Choose what you keep. The lullaby, certainly—it's lovely. The afternoon of sunlight, if it matters to you. Your arithmetic, obviously; I insist on it. We would edit out the rest together. The fear. The hunger. The nine years of swallowing poison for people who never learned your name. What remains would be... clean. A self with the rot trimmed away. You would remember being happy. You would forget having reason not to be."

"And everyone else's memories?" Jude asked. "The ones the dead stars are eating? The widow upstairs in the capital who woke last week and couldn't remember her son's face?"

"The market requires liquidity," the King said, with the mild regret of a man citing weather. "Stars go out. Memories flow down. It's not cruelty. It's *circulation*."

"It's theft with good lighting."

"It's *accounting*," the King said, and for one moment the kindness dropped away and Jude saw what was underneath, and underneath was nothing—no rage, no hunger, only vast, patient, structural indifference, the indifference of a bridge or a tax. Then the kindness returned, seamless. "You would have a room above the market. Better than the one you have. A window—I'd have one cut. You'd see the stars from your bed. You'd fall asleep every night under the sky you helped keep."

Jude was silent for a long moment. And here was the thing he would not admit to anyone, later, when he told this part: for one breath—one single, traitorous breath—he wanted it.

He wanted it the way a starving man wants bread, the way a drowning man wants the sweet grey water in his lungs. A curated self. A self with the fear filed off, the hunger amputated, the nine years of small poisons edited out like a bad chapter. He was so tired. He had been tired since he was seven years old and Master Corvel put the first dish in front of him and said, *swallow, boy, and thank me after.* He could stop being tired. All he had to do was agree to become a smaller, tidier man in a warm room under a beautiful lie.

"There it is," the King said softly, watching his face. "There it is. You feel it. Everyone feels it. That's not weakness, Jude. That's simply what a self is—a thing that wants to be comfortable. I've never had to threaten anyone in eleven hundred years. I only ever have to *offer*."

* * *

Jude thought about his mother.

Not the memory—he barely had the memory anymore; the plague winter had been eaten down to its bones, a shape with gaps shaped like transactions. But he thought about the fact of her, the woman who had walked this same road pregnant and desperate and signed a contract in a hand he knew from letters she'd sent to a cousin, the loop of her g's, the way she crossed her t's twice. She had paid with him before he existed to be paid. And somewhere in the mirrors, in the cells of this place, her choice was filed as an entry: *consideration received.*

"No," Jude said.

The King sighed—not disappointed, Jude noticed. *Interested.* "No?"

"No. And not because I'm brave. I want to be clear about that. I want it. I've wanted it since the moment you described it. But I've tasted enough dishes to know when something's been seasoned by someone who wants me to swallow." He met the King's eyes, which was like meeting the eyes of a well. "A curated self isn't a self. It's a dish someone else prepared. And I've spent my whole life being the one who checks the dish. I know what happens to the taster who trusts the kitchen."

The King regarded him for a long moment. The stars overhead burned on, indifferent, beautiful, stolen.

"You understand," the King said at last, pleasantly, "that refusal has a price schedule."

"I assumed."

"Your thirty days continue. Your losses continue. One memory per starless day, as contracted." The King closed the ledger with a sound like a door shutting in another room. "But I'll tell you something for free, since you appreciate honest accounting. Your friend Brannic came to see me yesterday. Alone. While you were asleep." The smile again, the fitted key. "He made an offer. An old man's offer. Years for a name. I found it touching. I find most love touching, right up until it's spent."

Ice went through Jude, slow and total. "What did you tell him?"

"I told him I'd consider it. I'm considering it still." The King rose, and the room rose with him, walls stretching, ceiling receding, until Jude felt like a coin at the bottom of a purse. "You have twenty-three days, ledger-keeper. Spend them well. And do keep forging—it's the finest entertainment I've had in centuries. I've left your entries intact, incidentally. All of them. I find I want to see how far you'll go."

Jude turned to go. At the door—the door that hadn't been there when he entered—the King spoke once more, and the pleasantness in his voice had a blade in it now, thin and bright:

"One more thing. The widow you mentioned. With the son. She lost his face last night, at roughly the hour you were writing your little miller into existence. Every entry has a cost, Jude Vane. Even the false ones. Especially the false ones. Someone always pays the difference."

Jude walked out through corridors of grey silk and candle-smoke, past the shuttered stalls, up toward the long dark of the ascent road, and behind him, faint and far below, he heard the market opening—hundreds of voices, patient and pale, calling out their prices to no one.

Twenty-three days. And somewhere above him, an old smuggler was counting his remaining years into a pile, and a daughter in the capital was waking with a name she didn't recognize, and the stars stayed dead, and stayed dead, and stayed dead.
