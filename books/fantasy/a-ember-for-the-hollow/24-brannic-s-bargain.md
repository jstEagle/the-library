## 24. Brannic's Bargain

Brannic had learned, in twenty years of smuggling, that the hardest doors were the ones that opened willingly.

The King's audience hall had opened for him without a word. He had walked in through a gap in the mirrors that he still could not explain—a seam where his own reflection should have been, a doorway shaped exactly like the absence of him—and now he stood on a floor of black glass beneath a ceiling of dying stars, and the Patient King sat at the far end of the hall as though he had been waiting since before Brannic was born.

Perhaps he had.

"You are not supposed to be here," the King said. His voice was the sound of water finding its way through stone—patient, unhurried, certain of arrival. "The taster's friend. The one who carries him down the dark roads. I know you, Brannic Ostre. I have known your account for a long time."

"I know," Brannic said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. Everything in this place made his throat feel full of ash. "That's why I came."

He had left Jude sleeping in the alcove behind the ledger room, or pretending to sleep, which amounted to the same courtesy between men who had spent nine days on the road together and had run out of pretense. He had told himself he would only look. Only walk the corridor once, learn its shape, see whether the daughter's name was kept in some vault he might someday crack. That was the lie he'd carried down the mountain like a lantern with a guttering wick: *I'll only look.*

But the door had opened for him. And the King was smiling.

"Twenty-six years ago," the King said, "a man came to my tollgate of teeth carrying a newborn wrapped in a fisherman's coat. The plague was in the village. It was in the child. He had nothing to pay for her life except the one thing I wanted." The King's fingers drummed once against the arm of a throne that might have been grown rather than carved—pale wood, root-pale, like something pulled up out of deep water. "Her name. Not the word itself—I gave him back the word, because a word is nothing, and he left believing he had made a clever trade. But the *name*, Vane's predecessor. The true name. The thing that ties a soul to its sound." The smile deepened by a degree. "I keep it here. In the treasury of unclaimed things."

"She goes by Wren," Brannic said. "She's gone by Wren her whole life."

"And how has she been, going by it?" the King asked softly. "Has the name ever fit her? Has she ever once answered to it without a half-second's hesitation, like a woman hearing someone else called in a crowd? Have her lovers never felt they were holding something just out of reach? Has she never stood in front of a mirror and felt the glass was showing her an almost?" He leaned forward, and the light of the failing stars moved across his face like water. "You did not sell her a name, smuggler. You sold her the ability to be anyone at all. She has spent twenty-six years as an unfinished sentence. You know this. It is why you are here."

Brannic's hands were fists at his sides. He could feel his pulse in his palms, in his teeth, in the old scar across his knuckles from a fence wire he'd climbed the night she was born. Nine days he had watched Jude lose pieces of himself—the sea first, then his mother's voice singing, then whole seasons of his boyhood peeling away like paint—and each loss had carved the same question deeper into Brannic: *what would I pay to give back what I took?*

He knew the answer. He had always known it. He had simply spent twenty years hoping the question would go away.

"What do you want for it," he said. "Her true name. Given back whole. Hers forever, no lien, no tithe, no claim of yours touching her or her children or her children's children. What's the price."

The King regarded him for a long moment. Around them, the ceiling ticked and dimmed as stars went out—Jude's memories, Brannic understood now, each dead point of light a day of a man's life being swallowed somewhere above them—and in that slow darkness the throne room felt less like a place than like the inside of a decision.

"You have been in my ledgers a long time," the King said. "Would you like to hear what they say about you?"

"No."

"Humor me. It is the only entertainment I permit myself." The King rose, and rising, he seemed to unfold—he was taller standing than the room should have allowed, and older, and the pale wood grain of his skin caught the starlight the way birch catches a low sun. "Brannic Ostre. Fifty-one years of age by the counting of the sun. Smuggler of wardsalt, unlicensed physic, twice jailed, once hanged and improperly so—the rope broke; you did not consider it an omen, which tells me everything about you." A pause. "Remaining years, per the current actuarial tables of the Hollow Guild: thirty-one. Fewer, given the roads you walk. More, given how hard you are to kill." The King's smile returned, thin as a knife laid flat. "I want them."

Brannic did not ask him to repeat it. He had heard it clearly the first time, and some part of him—the part that had climbed the fence wire, that had survived the rope, that had carried a plague-sick infant through a winter that ate whole villages—had already done the arithmetic before the words were finished.

"All of them," Brannic said.

"All of them. Thirty-one years, surrendered at the rate of one per day, beginning tonight. You will age as they go. Quickly, at the end. You will feel your body remembering every cold night you ever slept through and collecting the debt all at once." The King said it gently, the way a physician describes a treatment. "In exchange: Wren Ostre, born Wren of no name at all, receives her true name back into her own keeping. Irrevocable. Unpriced thereafter—it will be hers in the way that nothing else in this court is hers. Free and clear."

*Free and clear.* The words rang strangely in the black glass hall, like a coin dropped in a temple.

"And when the years run out," Brannic said. "What then?"

"Then you die," the King said, "as everything dies, but sooner, and owing nothing. Which is more than most men here can say." He extended his hand. His fingers were long and pale and slightly translucent at the tips, as though he were lit from within by something that was slowly going out. "Consider, if you like. Consider for a week. I am patient; it is the one thing I have never run short of."

"How long does she wait while I consider?" Brannic asked. "Another twenty years? Another plague?"

The King said nothing. The silence was answer enough.

Brannic thought of her anyway. He could not help it; thinking of her was the oldest habit he had. Wren at three days old, so small in his two hands, burning with fever, her breath a whistle through a throat too narrow for the world. Wren at six, asking why she had no mother and getting the half-truth he'd built for her out of rope and river fog. Wren grown, sharp-eyed, kind in a way he had never managed to teach because he'd never possessed it to teach—kind the way people are kind when something fundamental in them has always been missing and they've decided to fill the space themselves. He had watched her hesitate at her own reflection. He had seen her flinch when strangers spoke to her warmly, as if warmth were addressed to someone else. Twenty-six years of small hesitations, and every one of them was a payment on his debt, collected slowly, in a currency he hadn't known he was spending.

Jude would tell him to bargain harder. Jude, with his ledgers and his poisoned blood and his terrible clear mind, would find the flaw in the contract, the clause hidden in the margin, the way to pay less and keep more. Jude had come down this mountain to prove that the court's arithmetic could be beaten.

But Jude was fighting for his own soul. This wasn't Jude's fight. This was the one transaction in Brannic's life he did not want to win on terms. He was tired of clever trades. He had made his last clever trade on the night his daughter stopped being fully herself, and he had spent every year since learning exactly what it had cost her, itemized, compounding interest.

Love priced honestly, the guild's oldest traders used to say, was the only trade that never soured. He had thought it was sentiment. He understood it now. It meant you paid what it was actually worth. All of it. Without flinching.

"Yes," Brannic said.

He crossed the black glass floor, and his footsteps made no sound at all, and he took the King's hand.

The contract wrote itself as they clasped—not on paper, but along his arm, fine silver script winding up from the joined palms to the elbow, each clause cooling as it settled into his skin like rain into dry ground. He read what he could of it upside down. *Thirty-one years, remitted daily. The name Wren-of-no-name restored entire, absolute, perpetual, unto her and her heirs, free of lien and tithe forever.* At the bottom, where his signature should have been, the script asked him for something else instead.

It asked for his name.

"Not the word," Brannic said, and his mouth was dry. "The true one."

"The word stays with you," the King said. "As it stayed with her. But the tie—" He gestured, and a small door appeared in the mirrored wall, a door Brannic had not seen, plain and iron-banded and utterly ordinary, the most human thing in the room. Beyond it: the treasury of unclaimed things. "Come and pay."

They walked together, king and smuggler, down a corridor lined with shelves that receded farther than the mountain had any right to be deep. On the shelves sat the inventory of twenty centuries: jars of laughter gone cloudy with age, folded griefs pressed flat as linen, names coiled in boxes like sleeping snakes, a single summer afternoon labeled in faded ink, a child's fear of the dark, somebody's talent for whistling. Brannic looked at none of it except to note, with a smuggler's eye for cargo, that the shelves went on past the range of sight.

Here. Third shelf, second row, in a box of river-stone gray, tied with the kind of twine fishermen use.

The King opened it. Inside lay a name. Brannic could not describe it better than that—it was not a paper with a word on it, not a sound hanging in the air. It was a *her-ness*, small and bright and furious even in repose, and the moment his eyes touched it he wept, abruptly and completely, the way he had not wept at the hanging, or at the fever, or at any of the funerals of the years between, because this was not grief. It was recognition. It was seeing, after twenty-six years, the exact shape of what he had taken.

"Take it yourself," the King said. "It must be given freely by the holder to count as yours to carry. That is the rule even I keep."

Brannic lifted the name from the box with both hands, the way he had once lifted his daughter out of a fever-bed, and it weighed nothing and everything, and he said, into the dark of the treasury, in a voice scraped raw:

"Wren. Your name is Wren. It was always Wren. It's yours. Take it home."

And the name leapt from his hands like a spark off a grindstone, upward, out, gone—through rock and root and ward and ward-break, over the broken fences of the kingdom, into the capital where dawn had not yet come, into a narrow room above a chandler's shop where a woman slept badly, as she always slept badly, dreaming of a crowd calling a stranger's name—

And Brannic Ostre stood in the dark holding his empty hands, and the first year left him.

He felt it go the way you feel a wave take sand from under your feet: not pain, precisely, but a sudden lightness, an undermining. Somewhere in his body a year of walking ridgelines in the rain simply ceased to have happened. His knees knew it first. Then his back. Then his eyes, which had read ten thousand river-marks at dusk.

"One," said the King, softly, and there was—for the first time in four hundred years, though no record of it survives—something in his voice that a generous listener might have called respect. "You may go, smuggler. You know the way out. It opens for those who have paid."

* * *

Jude found him at the mouth of the descent passage, sitting on the stone steps with his back against the wall, and for one horrible heartbeat Jude did not recognize him.

That was the thing. He had lost so many of his own memories that he had begun to distrust his recognition of anything—but this was different. This was his eyes working correctly and reporting an impossibility. The man on the steps was eighty. Maybe ninety. White-haired, white-browed, the backs of his hands mapped with veins and spots, his smuggler's coat hanging from shoulders that had shrunk inside it like fruit drying in its skin. Only the eyes were Brannic's—gray, level, and wet.

"Sit down before you fall down," Brannic rasped. "Old men don't stand on mountains."

"Brannic." Jude crouched beside him, and the smell of the man was still right, tobacco and tar under the new smell of great age. "What did you do? What did you *give him?*"

"My years." Brannic smiled, and the smile was terrible and radiant, a young man's smile installed in an ancient face. "All of them. Thirty-one, more or less, minus the one he already took. One a day. I'll be done in a month." He said it lightly, the way he announced weather. "Cheaper than I expected, honestly. I always figured it'd cost more than I had."

"You didn't haggle." Jude's voice shook. He was aware, distantly, that another star had gone out overhead—that today's memory was being taken even now, something warm slipping out of him whose shape he couldn't yet identify—and that in a month this old man would be dead, and that both of these facts were the same fact, the court's arithmetic grinding forward, and that he had brought this man down here. "You had leverage. You had time. I could have found the clause, I could have—"

"Jude." Brannic reached out and gripped his wrist with a hand that was still, barely, strong. "Listen to me. Twenty-six years I watched her not quite be herself. Every mirror. Every hesitation. Every man who loved her and somehow never quite knew who he was loving. I did that. Me. Not the King—the King just held the collateral. And you know what the guild taught us both: a debt delayed isn't a debt forgiven. It accrues." He laughed, a dry whisper of a laugh. "So I paid. Full price. Asking price, no argument, cash on delivery." His grip tightened. "And it's *done*, boy. Do you understand? For the first time in twenty-six years, it's done. She's whole and I'm square and there's not a ledger anywhere under this mountain with my family's name in it."

"Where is it now?" Jude asked quietly. "The name."

"Gone home." Brannic closed his eyes. Tears ran freely down the seamed landscape of his face and he made no move to stop them, and he was smiling. "Should arrive about dawn. She won't know what it is. She'll wake up and the world will fit differently and she won't know why, and she'll go looking, because she's her father's daughter and she can't leave a mystery alone." He opened his eyes, and there was something urgent in them now, something being handed over. "She'll find your notebooks, Jude. You left them in the capital. She'll read them and she'll come looking for the man who wrote his own elegy—and when she comes, she cannot follow you down here. Whatever she learns, whatever she guesses—you keep her out of this mountain. Promise me. I bought her freedom. Spend it wisely."

"I promise," Jude said, and found that he meant it with the whole of what remained of himself, which was less than it had been yesterday and would be less again tomorrow, and which was, nevertheless, entirely sincere.

Brannic nodded, satisfied, and let his head rest back against the stone. Above them the ceiling-stars ticked and dimmed. Below, somewhere in the deep of the court, the Patient King returned to his throne and his patience, unaware—or so Jude would later tell himself, clinging to the hope like a man clinging to a ledge—that the smuggler had shown him something in paying that the King had never priced into his system.

Because Jude had watched the transaction happen. He had felt it pass through the stones, the way you feel thunder through a floor. And he understood now, with his counterfeiter's instinct and his taster's tongue for poison, what Brannic's bargain had proven:

A price paid honestly, in full, by a free man, closes an account forever.

The King's whole court ran on accounts that never closed.

Which meant the King owed something too—and had, Jude suspected, for longer than the stars had hung in this stolen sky.

"Help me up," Brannic said. "One day less means one day shorter the walk. We've work to do, boy, and I mean to see your revolution started before my knees give out entirely."

Jude took the old man's arm—light as a bundle of dried reeds, steady as a keel—and together they began to climb back down toward the court, toward the ledgers, toward the arithmetic that was going to end a kingdom.

Far above them, in a narrow room in the capital, Wren woke at dawn with a name on her lips that she had never been given, and sat up in the gray light, and said it aloud to the empty room—said it *whole*, for the first time, and did not yet know who had paid for the sound.
