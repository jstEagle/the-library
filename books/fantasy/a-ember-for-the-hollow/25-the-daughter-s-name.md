## 25. The Daughter's Name

Wren woke with a word in her mouth.

Not a dream—she had not dreamed, or did not remember dreaming, which for her amounted to the same thing. Just a word, sitting on her tongue like a coin left there while she slept, and she lay still in the grey hour before dawn and turned it over without understanding it. *Wren.* A small brown bird. A name.

She had been called Girl for as long as she could remember, which was not very long at all. That was the shape of her life: a short hallway of memory with a door at the end that would not open no matter how she pressed against it. Before her twentieth winter there was nothing. Not darkness exactly—darkness would have been something, would have had texture and weight—but an absence with edges, smooth as river stone. The healer in Saltrow had called it *the thin place* and told her some minds simply shed their early years like coats in spring. Wren had never believed that. You do not shed your own name. And she had known, with the wordless certainty of a person who has lost a limb and still feels it itching, that whatever was missing from before had been taken, not dropped.

Now there was a word in her mouth, warm as if someone had whispered it there moments ago.

"Wren," she said aloud, to the rented room, to the cracked plaster ceiling, to the grey light seeping through shutters that didn't quite close. Her voice came out rusty; she hadn't spoken since yesterday, when she'd paid a carter three coppers to bring her the last leg of the road into the capital.

The city had frightened her when she arrived. She had grown up—or the part of her that remained had grown up—in villages where the loudest thing was a bell. The capital was a roar, a crush of bodies and cart-wheels and fish-smell and incense, and above all of it, wrongness. She hadn't understood what was wrong until her second day, when she caught herself looking up and up and up into a sky with nothing in it. No stars. None. She remembered stars. She must have remembered stars—the way you remember rain, the way your skin remembers cold—and their absence sat in her chest like a held breath that wouldn't release. People in the streets walked quickly and looked down. Lamplighters worked through what should have been daylight hours because the nights had gone so long and so black that no one could say anymore where one ended and the next began.

Wren dressed, pinned her hair, and put the word in her mouth like a pebble under her tongue. Then she went out to find out who had given it to her.

* * *

She started, as one does, with the obvious: she asked people if they knew her.

It sounds foolish now, but she had no other thread to pull. Twenty years, Brannic had said—no. No one had said Brannic. No one had ever said that name to her. She corrected herself sharply and stood in the middle of Copperlane market feeling the absence open under her feet again. Twenty years of *something* had happened to her before the thin place, and she had no one to ask about it, no kin whose face she knew, no letters, no keepsakes. Only the pull that had brought her east for half a season, village by village, the way a compass needle finds north—not toward anything, exactly, but away from everything else.

At the third stall she stopped—a fruit-seller with hands stained purple to the wrist—Wren asked the question she had asked perhaps forty times since leaving home.

"I'm looking for my family. I lost my memory. I don't know my own name." She had learned to say it flatly, quickly, before pity could pool. "Does Wren mean anything to you? Anyone?"

The woman shook her head, already turning to the next customer, then paused. "There was a man," she said. "Two nights past. Odd fellow, big, weathered, looked like he'd walked a long way. He was asking after the Hollow Guild."

"The Hollow Guild," Wren repeated. The words landed strangely, the way a key lands in a lock you didn't know you had.

"Debt-collectors," the woman said, wrinkling her nose. "Or worse. They trade in things that shouldn't be traded. Years off your life. Your grief, bottled. My sister sold her wedding-day once, needed the money for medicine, and afterward she couldn't tell you what she'd sold, only that something was gone. Like a tooth-socket." She weighed a pear in her palm, not looking at Wren. "They have a hall off Chandler's Wynd. But I wouldn't go asking there, girl. They don't answer questions. They price them."

Wren thanked her, bought the pear because it seemed rude not to, and ate it walking, juice running down her wrist, while the word *hollow* turned over and over in her mind alongside the other word, the one in her mouth.

Chandler's Wynd was easy to find because it stank of tallow even in cold air, and the guildhall was easy to find because it was the only building on the street with no smell at all. That was the first strange thing she noticed about it. Everything in the capital smelled—of smoke, of sewage, of roasting meat, of the river's rot—but the Hollow Guild's hall stood behind a plain black door as scentless as a page. As if the building itself had been traded clean.

The door didn't knock. It had a brass plate with a slot, and beneath the slot a sign in careful script: *State your business and your consideration. Appraisals at the discretion of the house.*

Wren stood before it for a long moment, the pear's stone pit in her cheek, and thought: *I am the business. I am somebody's consideration.* She didn't know how she knew that. She only knew that the door made her feel like an entry in a ledger—something with a number attached, waiting to be written.

She wrote on the back of her hand with a charcoal stub: *Hollow Guild — Chandler's Wynd,* because writing things down was the only discipline she trusted. Memory had proven unreliable. Ink did not have a thin place.

Then, because doors that don't open are best watched rather than battered, she found the corner of a chandler's porch across the wynd, wrapped her coat around her, and settled in to wait for the hall to show her its habits.

* * *

The Hollow Guild kept strange hours.

In the six days Wren watched it, she learned them. Business was done between dusk and the deep of night, when the starless dark made everyone equally blind and equally desperate. Clients arrived hooded, alone, at intervals; each was admitted by a clerk with a lantern shuttered to a knife-slit of light, and each left again with a different walk than they'd come with. Some left lighter. Some left heavier. One old gentleman came out weeping silently, wiping his face with both hands like a man washing, and Wren watched him go and wondered whether he had bought something or sold it, and whether he knew the difference anymore.

But the strangest thing happened at dawn on the fourth morning.

A clerk emerged—young, precise, ink-stained to the second knuckle—and began taking down the hall's sign. Wren leaned forward on her cold perch. The sign came down. Then the brass plate was unscrewed, wrapped in cloth, carried inside. By midmorning the windows were papered over from within, and by evening the whole building stood blank-faced, anonymous, dead as any other empty house in a frightened city.

They were closing. In the middle of everything—in the middle of the dark sky and the failing wards and the rumors that ran the streets now like rats, *plague in the palace, the Queen is dying, the borders are open, the borders are open*—the Hollow Guild was shutting its doors.

That night a woman tried the black door, hammered until her fists bled, screaming that she wanted to sell, she needed to sell, she'd give anything, *anything*, and no one came. Wren sat in the shadows across the wynd and felt the woman's desperation like weather. And she thought, with a clarity that frightened her: *I know why she needs to sell. I know what it is to have a piece of yourself in someone else's till.*

Because that was the conviction she could not shake, the thing that had pulled her east for half a season: somewhere in that building was a line in a book with her name on it. Or her namelessness. Whatever transaction had hollowed out her twenty years, it had passed through hands like these. She could feel the shape of the absence the way you feel the shape of a missing tooth.

On the fifth night, the clerks began carrying out ledgers.

* * *

They burned them in the yard behind the hall.

Wren saw the glow first—an orange flicker where no fire should be—and crossed the wynd soft-footed, keeping to the wall, and found a gap between a cooper's shed and the guildhall's flank where she could watch. Three clerks fed books to a brazier built from an old wash-boiler, and the firelight showed their faces: grim, methodical, frightened. They burned in silence. Page by page, volume by volume, debts going up in sparks.

She should have felt horror. Some part of her understood that those pages were people—that every sheet curling black represented a laughter or a year or a name, that the ash drifting up into the starless sky was somebody's mother's lullaby, somebody's childhood, going wherever such things go when their record is destroyed. But what she felt instead was panic, cold and total. *My name might be in one of those books.*

She was moving before she decided to move—slipping from the gap, crossing the yard's dark corner, snatching at the stack of volumes waiting beside the brazier. The nearest clerk shouted. Another grabbed her arm, and his grip was strong and practiced, and for one heartbeat Wren hung in the firelight, one hand on a ledger bound in grey leather, and the ledger fell open in the scuffle, and she saw—

Not words. Just a margin. A redaction: a thick block of black ink obliterating a single line, and beside the blot, in faded brown-red, a small notation she read in the half-second before the book was torn from her grip:

*Paid in full. Name restored.*

Then hands were on her, the grey book gone, and a clerk—older, grey-bearded, with the tired eyes of a man doing work he hated—held her at arm's length and studied her face in the firelight.

"Who are you?" he demanded. "Who sent you?"

And Wren, whose name had lived in her mouth for six days now like a coal that would not cool, said, "I don't know. That's the whole point. Someone gave me my name back and I need to know who, and I think it's in your books, and you're burning them."

The older clerk's grip loosened. Something moved behind his eyes—recognition, or fear, or arithmetic.

"Name restored," he said slowly, as though reading it off the page again. "Recent?"

"Six days ago. I woke up with it. I've got nothing before that—nothing since I was small. Twenty years, gone, and then a name, like—" her voice cracked, which she hated, "—like a gift arriving without its giver."

The clerk let go of her entirely. He stepped back and looked at the fire, at the books, at his fellows, and the fear on his face rearranged itself into something worse: decision.

"There was a man," he said. "Came here the night before last. Big fellow. Weathered. He'd been to the Undercourt—we could see it on him, the way it wears a person—and he'd paid something enormous, because the restoration came through anyway, and restorations *never* come through. The instruments were dead, the lines were frozen, and his daughter's name came back regardless, like water finding a crack." He rubbed his mouth. "He asked after a taster. Jude Vane. He said Vane was owed, that whatever Vane had done beneath the mountain, the guild was to see it honored." His eyes came up to hers. "Is Vane your father, girl? Is that the connection?"

"No," Wren said. The word came out steady even though nothing in her was. "Brannic. Was his name Brannic?"

Something in the clerk's face confirmed it before his mouth did. And the world tilted—because she had not known that name, had never heard it, and yet it arrived in her like a key turning, like a word finding its definition, and with it came not memories but the *shape* of memories, the negative space where a big weathered man had carried her on his shoulders, had taught her knots, had sung badly, had loved her with the clumsy ferocity of a man who had already lost too much—

"He sold something," Wren whispered. "To get my name back. Didn't he. What did he pay?"

The clerk looked at the fire.

"Years," he said quietly. "All of them remaining. Restorations require equivalence, and a name is—" he hesitated, "—a name is expensive. He paid and walked out of here aged twenty years in a step, and he was crying, and he was laughing, and he went toward the mountain. Toward the Undercourt. Nobody goes toward the mountain."

Behind him the fire collapsed inward in a bloom of sparks, and above the yard the sky hung black and starless, and Wren stood very still while the arithmetic finished itself inside her: a father she had only just learned existed, spending his life to return what someone had stolen, and then walking into the dark to find the thief—or to find the man who had made it possible.

"Jude Vane," she said.

"Aye." The clerk was watching her strangely now. "Guildmaster's boy. Taster to the Queen. He vanished weeks ago, down under the wards, chasing his own soul. Half the city thinks he's dead. The other half thinks he's the reason the sky went out." He picked up another ledger, weighed it in his hands, and did not throw it on the fire. "And this morning the Queen's household posted a notice. Poison in the palace. The Queen is dying, and there's no taster to catch it, because ours went under the mountain." He swallowed. "If Vane comes back up that road, girl—if he comes back at all—he'll find a kingdom coming apart and a guild burning its own books to keep them out of certain hands. There are people who want these ledgers intact. Powerful people. The kind who signed entries in them."

"Then I'll wait for him at the mountain," Wren said.

She heard herself say it as if someone else had said it, and understood immediately that it was true. She had come east on a compass-pull with no name and no reason; now she had a name, a father, a debt, and a direction, and all four pointed the same way. Down the road beneath the broken wards. Toward the mountain where nobody goes.

The grey-bearded clerk studied her a moment longer, then reached into his coat and pressed something into her hand: a slim notebook, cheap paper, and a charcoal pencil worn short.

"If you're going to be near Vane," he said, "write everything down. He was always saying ink doesn't have a thin place." At her look, he almost smiled. "Everyone who worked for him ends up saying his sayings eventually. Go on, girl. And when you see him—tell him Sable sent you. Tell him she remembers."

* * *

Wren walked out of the capital at first grey light, when the gates opened onto a countryside she had only heard rumor of: wards fallen like fences in a flood, mile after mile of broken standing stones leaning every which way, the land beyond them gone strange and quiet. No birds sang. The grass grew tall and pale in the ditches. Somewhere far ahead, under the bruise-colored horizon, the mountain waited with its stolen ceiling of stars.

She had the word in her mouth, and the notebook in her pocket, and on the first page, in careful letters, she had written what she knew so it could not be taken the way the rest had been taken:

*My name is Wren. My father is Brannic, and he paid everything for me. A man named Jude Vane made it possible. I owe a stranger my name and my father his errand, and I am going to collect on both.*

By noon she could see the road beneath the wards, descending toward the dark in long switchbacks, and on its edge, small in the distance, a figure was climbing up—stumbling, burdened, carrying what looked like two bodies over its shoulders, one fading like smoke and one heavy as honest death. Behind it, impossibly, faintly, the sky at its back was beginning to glitter.

Wren dropped her pack and ran.
