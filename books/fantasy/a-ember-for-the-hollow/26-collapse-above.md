## 26. Collapse Above

The stars died in order of brightness.

That was the thing no one had warned her about, Wren thought, watching from the bakery's rooftop on the ninth night after the sky went dark. It wasn't a single extinguishing, one great snuffing like a candle between wet fingers. It was a dying in sequence, as if some vast ledger were being balanced overhead and each debt called in turn. First the bright ones went—those she had known all her life, the Sailor, the Hearth-Keeper, the seven sisters of the Loom—and the city had grieved them properly, with bells and prayers and a kind of festive dread. But now it was the dim ones, the faint salt-grains of light that only sailors and shepherds could name, and their going was worse somehow, quieter, like watching an old man lose his hearing one frequency at a time.

Above her, the last star in the western sky flickered like a moth against glass.

"Come inside," her mother called from below, though her mother had been dead six years, and Wren knew it, and climbed down anyway because the voice had come from the kitchen and the kitchen had lamplight and lamplight was becoming a currency more precious than coin.

Inside, her father sat where he always sat now, in the chair by the oven with his hands open on his knees. He had come back to her gaunt and white-bearded, twenty years older than when he'd walked out her door, and he would not say where he had been or what he had paid. He only held her once, very hard, and said her name—*Wren*—over and over, as if tasting whether it were true, and then he slept for two days, and when he woke he began to fail.

The physicians called it wasting. The neighbors called it grief made flesh. Wren, who had grown up on her father's stories of the Hollow Guild without knowing they were confessions, called it what it looked like: a man who had spent himself down to the wick.

"You should eat," she told him now, setting bread beside him. She had begun adding things to his food—feverfew, willow bark, the bitter greens the herbalist in Salt Row swore by—but nothing took. It was like feeding a fire that had already gone to ash.

"I'm not hungry," he said, and then, in the tone of a man following a thought a long way behind his mouth: "There was a taster. Jude Vane. Did I ever tell you about Jude Vane?"

"You've mentioned him." Nine times, now. She had counted. "He left the city."

"He didn't leave," Brannic said, looking at the dead oven, at nothing. "He went under. There's a difference."

* * *

By day, the capital rotted with remarkable good manners.

That was the horror of it, Wren thought, walking to the market through streets where the lamps burned at noon because there was no telling noon from dusk anymore. The Queen's criers still announced grain prices. The guildhalls still opened. Children still played their games in the alleys, though their rhymes had changed: *starless night, starless night, hold your name and hold it tight.* The city performed its own continuance like an actor who has forgotten his lines and is improvising, magnificently, while somewhere offstage the theater burns.

But there were signs for those who walked slowly enough to see.

The wards failed first in the poor quarters—that was arithmetic, not malice; the ward-stones along the Midden Wall were oldest and cheapest—and so the first plague houses appeared in Cinder Ward, and were chalked with the red cross, and the red crosses spread like a rash written by a trembling hand. Wren passed three of them on her way to buy flour. Behind the door of the third, a woman was singing. It was a lullaby Wren did not know, and she found herself standing in the street listening until the song stopped mid-phrase, unfinished forever, and then she walked on faster than before.

The plague of nine-and-twenty years ago had been called the Gray Winter, and her father had survived it as a young man, and he had told her once—he had been drunk, which was the only time he spoke plainly—that the Gray Winter had ended too neatly, that plagues don't simply *stop*, that someone had negotiated. She had thought it a smuggler's superstition. Now the wards were down and the sickness had returned within days, punctual as a creditor, and Wren understood that whatever bargain had held the Gray Winter at bay for a generation, its term had finally elapsed.

At the apothecary's, the line stretched into the street. The herbalist behind the counter had the gray-rimmed eyes of early infection and kept working anyway, measuring out feverfew with steady hands.

"Take this to your father," she said, wrapping a packet. "And girl—if you find any of his old guild friends, ask them about the tithe."

"The tithe?"

"The old ones whisper it's due again." The woman's voice dropped. "Every few generations, the mountain collects. My grandmother saw the last collection. She said the sky went dark for a season, and when it came back, half the children born that year were... less. Smiling things. Sweet as milk and empty as pails." She pushed the packet across. "Go home. Lock your door. And whatever knocks, don't answer with your whole name."

* * *

On the twelfth day, the Queen began to die.

The criers tried to soften it—the Queen is unwell, the Queen rests, the Queen keeps her council—but Wren heard it from the kitchen maid of a palace steward who bought her bread every morning, and the kitchen maid had it from the under-taster directly: Her Majesty had taken ill at evening meal, and no one could say how.

"No taster caught it," the maid whispered over the warm loaves. "And Vane's gone, and the under-taster swears he tested every dish twice. They've got the poisoner's guild in chains and they can't even charge them, because there's nothing to charge them *with*. Whatever it is doesn't show in the blood, doesn't show in the smell. She's just—" the maid's hands wrung her apron— "*fading*. Like a lamp."

Wren carried the phrase home like a stone in her shoe. *Like a lamp.* That evening she fed her father his broth and watched the light go on diminishing in him and thought: we are all lamps, and something is drawing off the oil.

Brannic slept most of the time now. When he woke, he asked her name.

Not *what* is your name—he knew that, mostly, on his better days. He would take her hand and say, "Tell me your name," and she would say *Wren*, and he would close his eyes as if swallowing something precious, something he needed to keep down. Once he said, "I paid twenty years for four letters. Don't ever let anyone tell you what you're worth before you know it yourself." And once, terribly, he called her *Marta*, and wept without knowing why, and Wren held his papery hands and did not correct him because some ghosts deserve their visits.

It was on one of his waking nights that he gave her the key.

"Under the floor," he said. "Bakery. Where the old proofing box was, before the renovation." His voice was a thread. "If anything happens to me. If anyone comes asking. There's papers. A taster's notebooks. You give them to no one but the taster himself, do you hear? No one but Vane."

"Father, Vane's gone under the mountain, everyone says—"

"*Himself*," Brannic said, with a flash of the smuggler's iron that had crossed borders and dodged tariffs for thirty years. Then the iron guttered and he was only a dying man again. "He'll want them. A man should have his own elegy in his own hand."

* * *

Her father died on the morning of the fifteenth day, in the hour when the sky should have been brightening but only deepened, gray going to darker gray like ink stirred into water.

Wren did what had to be done—there was always something that had to be done; grief was a luxury of the leisured—and washed him and wrapped him and arranged with the carter, who charged her double because grave-digging had become a growth industry. The cemetery on the hill was filling fast, new rows of mounds like furrows, and the gravediggers worked in masks soaked in vinegar.

She buried him beside her mother, whose grave, she noticed with the dull attention of shock, had been freshly turned sometime in the past year, though no one in the family had tended it. She stood a long moment over that small wrongness, unable to think what it meant, and then the carter coughed pointedly and she let it go.

* * *

She found the notebooks that same evening, because the house demanded to be emptied of secrets before she could bear to sleep in it.

The loose flagstone lifted easier than it should have—her father had oiled it recently, recently meaning sometime before whatever journey had aged him two decades—and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay a bundle: guild papers, a few contracts with names scraped or burned away, and seven notebooks in a hand she recognized from the invoices her father sometimes brought home, a small precise script, economical, upright, the writing of a man who distrusted flourish.

Jude Vane's notebooks.

She should have bundled them away unread. Her father's instruction had been delivery, not perusal. But she was alone in a house that still smelled of her father's death, in a city with no sky, and the first page fell open to a line that caught her like a fishhook:

*Nine days without the sea now. I am losing the ocean itself, not just the view of it. This morning I tried to remember the sound and got my own imagination pretending.*

Wren sat down on the cold kitchen floor with the lamp pulled close, and read.

*Day 11. Today I lost my mother's face. I have her hands still—I remember her hands, cracked at the knuckles, always moving—but the face above them is a smudge where features should be. I drew what I remember in the back of this book so that if the drawing survives I can at least know I once knew. It looks like everyone and no one.*

*Day 14. Lost: the taste of plums. Kept: arithmetic. Curious that the court takes the sweet things first. Or perhaps I value sweetness differently now and only imagine the theft is selective.*

*Day 19. Corvel's face today, gone like Mother's. I keep his voice though. Cold as a well-chain. "Tenderness is a liability, boy." I begin to suspect he was wrong, but I built my whole self on his being right, and a man cannot easily refit his foundations in a month.*

*Day 22. Wrote my own name forty times this morning to be sure. Still mine. For now the name holds. Everything else is weather.*

There were entries that were not losses. There were pages of accounts—she recognized the format, having grown up deciphering her father's ledgers hidden inside flour manifests—entries in which Jude Vane had begun recording debts that did not exist, credits never issued, a phantom economy drafted in a careful hand. In the margins he had noted, almost cheerfully:

*A forged entry is only a lie until someone honors it. Then it is a fact. The Undercourt runs on facts of this kind. So does everything else, I suspect.*

And there were the personal pages, rarer, pressed flat between columns of figures like flowers in a book of sermons:

*I remember one afternoon entirely. Summer, I must have been eleven. The wall behind the taster's quarters held the sun until evening and I sat on it and ate a stolen peach and no one wanted anything from me for one hour. I spend that afternoon now the way misers spend coins. I have maybe forty visits left in it before it goes thin.*

*I hate them. I want that recorded in a fair hand: the crown sold me before I was born and called it warding the borders, and I hate them cleanly, without heat, the way I hate poison—a professional hatred, dosed daily. It keeps. Hatred keeps better than grief. Grief spoils.*

*If anyone reads this: I did not go willingly under the mountain. But I went. Note the distinction. Willingness is the only currency they respect, which is their cruelest invention.*

Wren read until the lamp fat was nearly gone, and then she did a strange thing: she read the whole of it again, aloud, in a low voice, because the notebooks were an elegy and elegies are meant to be spoken, and their author was not dead, and might not even be lost, and someone ought to practice the homecoming.

She understood, by the second reading, what her father had known—why he had aged twenty years in a month, why he had said *he'll want them, a man should have his own elegy in his own hand*. These books were Jude Vane assembling himself against dissolution, a self rebuilt out of ink every morning like a city rebuilding its walls. And they were also a map—not of roads, but of debts. The forged entries. The founding principle he kept quoting in the margins: *nothing given freely can be owned.* The arithmetic of a man who had decided, in cold blood and warm ink, to make war upon a court with a pen.

* * *

Near dawn—it was hard to call it dawn; the sky merely became a different species of dark—Wren wrapped the notebooks back in their oilcloth, and then unwrapped them, and kept out the seventh, the newest, because she was not her father; she had never sold anything and did not intend to start with her own curiosity.

She climbed to the rooftop with it, as she had climbed every night, to watch the stars die.

Only two remained now, low in the east, twin points so faint they seemed less like stars than the memory of stars, the way her notebook-author remembered the sea: imagination pretending. As she watched, one of them flickered—and steadied—and flickered again, a moth against glass, and Wren found she was gripping the roof-edge hard enough to hurt her hands.

Behind her, in the street below, a bell began to ring. Not the plague-bell's slow toll but the palace pattern, quick and broken, the rhythm reserved for a death in the royal family. Other bells took it up across the dark city, district by district, the news passing outward in bronze like ripples in a pond: the Queen was dead, or dying, or something in between, and the capital stood orphaned under a sky with perhaps one star left.

And then—one star.

Wren counted. Recounted. The eastern sky held a single point of light, trembling.

In her lap, open to its last written page, the seventh notebook offered its author's final instruction, dated the morning he had left with her father:

*Ledgers balance or they burn. I am going under the mountain to settle the account. If the sky begins to return, it means the payment is clearing. Watch the eastern horizon, and if you see light coming back—any light, however small—know that it was bought dearly, and by whom, and that he intends to pay for everything.*

One star trembled in the east.

Wren closed the book, held it to her chest, and went down to pack.
