## 27. Prison of Mirrors

They did not drag him. That was the worst of it, at first—the courtesy. Two guards of the Undercourt, tall and gray as unlit candles, had simply stood on either side of Jude in the ruined market and waited, and he had understood that running was a thing the architecture would not permit. The corridors bent toward them. The doors opened onto the cell whether he walked or was carried. So he walked, chin up, hands loose at his sides, because Master Corvel had taught him nine years ago that a taster's last defense was the appearance of appetite.

The cell, when it resolved around him, was not stone.

It was glass. Or something older than glass—mirrored on every surface, floor and ceiling and every wall, so that Jude stood in the center of an infinite Jude, receding in all directions like a corridor built of himself. The light came from nowhere and everywhere, the color of old silver, and it did not flicker because there was no flame to flicker.

He turned slowly. His reflections did not all turn with him.

That was the first horror: some of them lagged, half a heartbeat behind, so that for a moment there were two Judes facing each other across no distance at all. The second horror took longer to arrive, and when it did, it arrived quietly, the way poison does—a sweetness first, then the understanding.

The mirrors were not showing him. They were showing *versions*.

In one, he stood in the guildhall wearing Ossen's chain of office, gray at the temples, kind-eyed and compromised. In another he was a boy again, thin as a winter branch, standing in the doorway of a house with a red door, and the door was opening onto warmth he could almost smell—bread, tallow, rain on hot stone—and then the mirror rippled and the boy stepped back into the dark. In another, he wore the Queen's livery and held a cup he did not drink from, and his face was serene, and dead behind the serenity, and Jude knew that version without being told: the taster who had stopped tasting, who swallowed everything and felt nothing, who survived by becoming a vessel instead of a man.

There was one where he had died at seven. He recognized it by the smallness of the hands.

"Stop," he said aloud, and his voice fell flat in the silver room, absorbed before it could echo. "I know what this is."

The mirrors did not stop. Why would they? A prison is not an argument. It does not need your agreement; it needs only your company.

He counted them, because counting was what he had. Forty-one reflections within immediate sight, and beyond those, more, diminishing into haze like stars into the deep sky. He made himself look at each one. This was the discipline Corvel had drilled into him across nine years of measured doses: *name the thing before you swallow it.* The monkshood. The foxglove. The slow arsenic they put in the border-wine to test his tongue. Name it, and it is a problem. Refuse to name it, and it becomes a fear, and fears compound faster than interest.

So he named them. The guildmaster he might have been. The coward who fled the capital the night the stars went out and lived fat and forgotten in some southern town. The father—he flinched at that one, a man with a child on his shoulders in a market square that did not exist, laughing at nothing Jude had ever gotten to laugh at. The corpse in the plague pit. The king's creature. The free man, walking out of the mountain with his soul under his arm like a parcel.

That last one hurt most, and he made himself stand before it longest.

Hours passed, or something shaped like hours. There were no meals here, no bells, no change of light. He sat down in the exact center of the cell, where the reflections met and multiplied him past counting, and he began, deliberately, to remember. It was harder now than it had been a month ago. The starless days above had taken their tithe: the sea was gone, gone, still gone, a blank coast-shaped ache where his childhood should have been. But some things remained. His skill with numbers. His hatred, banked and patient as coals. One afternoon of sunlight, a courtyard, a woman's sleeve the color of apricots, dust turning gold in the air—he kept that one wrapped up tight, visited it only sparingly, the way a starving man rations his last loaf.

He recited ledger entries to himself until his voice went hoarse. *Item: one widow's laughter, sold, proceeds applied against her son's fever. Balance: settled.* He recited the founding principle until it wore smooth as a river stone: *nothing given freely can be owned.* He said his mother's name—not the redacted margin-version, but the name he had recovered below, in the Undercourt's own books, the name she had paid everything to keep off any ledger at all.

And the mirrors listened. That was the trap inside the trap. Every time he remembered something true, a reflection somewhere sharpened—a version of him that had never known that truth grew clearer, more solid, more *available*. The cell was not punishing him. It was *shopping.*

When sleep finally took him, it took him sideways, and even his dreams were mirrored.

* * *

She came without sound.

One moment the silver room held only its infinite Judes; the next, Sable stood among them, and the reflections parted around her like water around a blade. She looked different down here—or rather, she looked *as she was*, and Jude realized with a dull shock that above ground she must have been wearing restraint like a coat. Here, in the court's native light, the four hundred years showed. Not as age. As *weight*. Her skin had the stillness of things long submerged. Her eyes were the eyes of someone who has read every book in a library and hated all of them.

"You're counting," she said. "You've been counting since they put you in. I watched you do it through the glass."

"It passes the time."

"Nothing passes the time here." She lowered herself to sit across from him, folding down with the care of someone whose joints remember centuries. "Time pools. You'll learn to hate the word *presently*."

Jude said nothing. In the mirror behind her, he could see another Sable, younger, in a taster's uniform two hundred years out of fashion, holding a cup with steady hands. He looked away from it. She caught him looking anyway. Of course she did.

"That one," she said quietly, "is real. Or it was. The cell keeps everything we ever refused to be. It thinks inventory is mercy."

"Why are you here?" His voice came out rougher than he intended. "To gloat on the King's behalf? To explain, patiently, why I should have taken the offer?"

"No." She drew her knees up, an oddly human gesture, and for a moment the weight of centuries shifted and there was just a tired woman sitting on a mirrored floor. "I'm here because when I was where you are—four hundred and eleven years ago, in this same cell, though the glass has been replaced twice since—I would have given anything for one visitor who wasn't selling something. So I'm paying that forward. Call it the only honest trade I ever made."

Jude studied her. Above ground, in the capital, she had been the tithe-collector: immaculate, inevitable, a receipt given legs. Below, stripped of the King's authority, she seemed almost—small. Not weak. Worn, the way a stair is worn, by the traffic of her own regrets.

"How long did you sit here?" he asked.

"Nine days. Then the King came, and offered me what he offered you. Serve as the court's ledger-keeper. Keep a curated self. All your best memories, polished and permanent; all your griefs filed away where they can't reach you." She smiled, and it was the saddest expression Jude had ever seen on a face that old. "I was a taster, Jude. Before you, before the one before you. I built my immunity dish by dish under a master colder than your Corvel, and when the wards failed in my century—in my *starless night*, which no one above remembers anymore because the court bought the remembering too—I was brought here, and I sat where you're sitting, and I did the arithmetic. And the arithmetic said yes."

"And?"

"And I have been the court's ledger-keeper for four hundred and eleven years." She spread her hands. The rings on her fingers caught the silver light—rings Jude recognized now, each one a seal of office, each one a shackle. "You've seen my work. Every entry in the Undercourt's books that mirrors your guild's—that's mine. Every soul valued, every memory appraised, every mother's payment recorded in a fair and even hand. I told myself it was stewardship. Someone must keep the accounts, after all. Better me than someone crueler." Her voice thinned. "Do you know what four hundred years of keeping perfect books does to a person? It makes you *accurate*. Nothing else survives. Kindness is imprecise. Love doesn't balance. I have not wept since the reign of a king whose name the histories burned, because weeping is a rounding error, and I was made to be exact."

The mirrors around them had gone quiet. Even the versions of Jude seemed to be listening.

"The sea," Jude said suddenly. "I lost the sea. First thing they took. Do you still—"

"I don't remember water," Sable said. "I remember its *valuation*. Three drops of a drowned sailor's last breath fetch a remarkable price among the deeper courts. That's what's left of the ocean, for me. A price column." She looked at him then, fully, and her eyes were terrible and clear. "That is what acceptance buys. That is the curated self, forty decades downstream. You asked why I'm here. I'm here because you refused, and I needed to see it with my own eyes. I needed to know the refusal was possible. I'd begun to believe it wasn't—that anyone would have signed, in my place. That it was never a choice at all, only a delay."

"It was a choice," Jude said. "A stupid one, maybe. He'll starve the refusal out of me. That's what the cell is for. Every day I hold a true memory, a reflection gets sharper. It's not a prison, it's a *market*. It's waiting for me to sell."

"Yes." She nodded slowly. "And you understand that, which is why you might survive it. I didn't understand. I thought it was punishment, and I resolved to endure it, and endurance was exactly the wrong answer. Endurance is what it wants. A man enduring is a man negotiating with himself about how much he can bear, and every negotiation ends in a signature." She leaned forward. "Listen to me, taster. I have one piece of counsel, purchased at the cost of everything I am, and I will give it freely—which, incidentally, is the only kind of gift the court cannot tax. Don't endure. Don't resist. *Audit.*"

"Audit?"

"This cell shows you what you might have been. Very well. Look closely at the versions. Really look. What do they all have in common, every one of them, the guildmaster and the coward and the father and the corpse?" She waited. When he didn't answer, she answered for him, softly: "They're all *priced*. Each one is a valuation. The cell isn't showing you alternate lives—it's showing you the King's appraisal of you, itemized. He thinks in ledgers, Jude. Everything he builds is a ledger, including this room. Which means—" and here her voice dropped, and something moved behind her eyes that Jude had not seen there before, something that four hundred years of accuracy had not quite managed to kill, "—which means the room can be *reconciled*. Books that balance can be unbalanced. Find the entry that doesn't belong. Find the line item the King himself never paid for."

Jude stared at her. Around them the silver light pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat heard through a wall—as if the cell had understood her, and did not like being understood.

"He watches," Sable murmured, rising. "He always watches. So say nothing true when I'm gone. Sing something. Recite tariffs. I used to recite the salt schedules of the Second Dynasty; it took him three hundred years to stop listening." She paused at the edge of the reflections, where the mirrors would close behind her like water closing over a stone, and she looked back at him with an expression he would carry out of this place if he carried nothing else—not pity, not hope, but *witness*: the rarest thing in the Undercourt, the one commodity never once entered in its books.

"When I was in this cell," she said, "no one came. Four hundred years, and I have wondered every single day whether a visit would have changed what I chose. Now I know it wouldn't have. But it would have meant I wasn't alone while I chose." Her hand rose, hesitated, and touched the air near his cheek without landing—an accuracy of restraint, a rounding error she permitted herself. "You are not alone while you choose, Jude Vane. Remember that. Write it somewhere they can't take."

Then she stepped backward into the mirrors, and the versions closed over her, and Jude sat alone in the silver room with forty-one selves and one sentence, repeating it under his breath like a countersign:

*I am not alone while I choose.*

* * *

He did not sleep after that. Sleep was surrender to the inventory.

Instead he began, in the dark that was not dark, to audit.

He walked the cell's perimeter slowly, mirror by mirror, and this time he did not look away from any of them. The guildmaster-Jude, priced at the cost of a conscience. The father-Jude, priced at the cost of a life he'd never risked. The corpse, priced at nothing, worth exactly what a plague death fetched. He examined each valuation the way he had once examined dishes for the Queen—looking not for the poison, but for the *flavor that didn't belong*, the note too rich, the sauce covering something.

Forty-one versions. He catalogued them. He found thirty-nine accounted for: every one traceable to a choice, a chance, a payment, a price. Cause and effect, entry and balance.

Two did not reconcile.

The first was the boy at the red door. Jude stood before that mirror a long time. The door was opening. It was always opening, and the boy always stepped back, and Jude could not find the transaction that produced this version anywhere in his own history. No plague winter had spared that house. No choice of his mother's had closed that door. It was a life that existed without a debt attached—and that, in the King's ledger, should have been impossible. Unless the King had included it precisely because it was impossible. Unless it was bait. Unless somewhere in the court's books there was an entry for *the life Jude Vane would have lived had no one ever paid for him at all*—and such an entry could only exist if the court had already computed what his unpaid life was worth.

Which meant the court had valued it. Which meant the court had *priced* a thing that had never been sold.

His heart began, very quietly, to hammer.

The second unreconciled mirror was worse, and better. It stood at the far end of the cell, half-lost in haze, and Jude had avoided it for what felt like days because it showed nothing but an empty room—a plain room, whitewashed, with a table, a chair, a window with honest daylight in it. No figure. No Jude at all. He had assumed it was broken, or that it showed some final erasure, the self reduced to absence.

Now, auditing, he understood. Every other mirror showed a version of him *at a price*. This one showed a version of him at *no price whatsoever*—and the court's glass, asked to display a thing with no valuation, could only render the valuation: an empty room. A self the system had no column for. A man outside the books entirely.

Sable's voice, in his memory: *Find the line item the King himself never paid for.*

Jude pressed his palm flat against the empty-room mirror. The glass was cold, and beneath the cold, faint as a pulse under a wrist, something moved. Not an image. An *arithmetic*. Deep in the substance of the mirror, numbers were turning over like soil—numbers he recognized, because he had written their cousins in the guild's margins for nine years. The mathematics of debts that compound past their principal. The mathematics of a debt so old it has eaten its own origin.

The King's own existence, priced somewhere no one had ever dared to check.

The mirror warmed under his hand. Behind him, thirty-nine reflections dimmed, and the boy at the red door turned his head—slowly, deliberately, for the first time—to look at Jude with his own eyes.

And far above, through leagues of mountain, Jude felt rather than heard the court shift its weight, the way a great house shifts when someone has spoken in a room where everyone was presumed asleep.

Somewhere in the dark, a ledger-keeper four hundred years old whispered, *well done,* to no one.

And somewhere closer, patient as interest accruing, the King began to walk toward the mirrors.
