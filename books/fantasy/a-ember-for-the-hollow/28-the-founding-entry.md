## 28. The Founding Entry

The mirrors did not sleep.

That was the first thing Jude learned about his cell, in the long dark hours when the Undercourt's borrowed light dimmed to a coal-red murmur and even the distant music of the market below fell silent. The mirrors watched. They waited. They held their thousand Judes in suspension like flies in amber, and when he closed his eyes he could feel them anyway, the way one feels a stare through the back of the neck—patient, curious, faintly hungry.

He had stopped counting the days. Counting required a unit of time, and time in the cell of mirrors moved like honey poured uphill. Sometimes Sable came. Sometimes she did not. When she came, she sat on the far side of the glass—there was no door he could find, only a seam in the reflections that opened for her and no one else—and she spoke to him in her dry, papery voice, and sometimes he listened and sometimes he turned his face to the wall and let the walls be walls, for once, even if they were made of him.

Tonight he was not sleeping. Tonight he was walking.

He had discovered it by accident, three losses ago or four: if he moved slowly along the curve of the mirror-wall, tracing his fingertips along the cold silver, the reflections did not simply follow. They *changed*. A few steps and the Jude in the glass was taller, hollow-cheeked, wearing the grey apron of a chandler. A few steps more and he was fat and laughing, a Jude who had never tasted poison in his life, a Jude with flour on his hands. Another step and the glass held a Jude in a queen's guard uniform, a Jude in a beggar's rags, a Jude with a wedding band, a Jude with no hands at all. Every life the tithe had not taken. Every self his mother's bargain had spent before he was born.

He had hated it at first. He had pressed his palm flat against the glass and breathed on it until his reflection was a ghost behind fog, and even that small erasure had felt like mercy.

But tonight he was not walking to grieve. Tonight he was walking to work.

Because Sable had said something, the last time she came, that had lodged in him like a fishbone. He had asked her—half in despair, half in spite—*why mirrors? Why keep me in a room made of every man I might have been?* And she had been quiet for a long moment, her four-hundred-year-old face unreadable in the coal-light, and then she had said:

"Because the King likes his prisoners to understand what they cost him. And because the cell was not built for you, taster. It was built for the first one who refused. Everything in this court is older than it looks. Everything is *secondhand*."

*Secondhand.* The word had turned over in him all through the dark like a stone in a streambed, and now, in the dead hours, he went to the glass to dig it out.

* * *

He began where the cell began—at the seam where Sable entered, the one place in the room that was not a reflection of him. It was a narrow panel of mirror, taller than a door, and the Jude in it was strange: not older, not younger, but *simpler*, as if the glass could not quite decide what to show there. He pressed his fingers to it. Cold. Colder than the rest, cold as well-water, cold as the bottom of a thing.

"Show me," he said quietly. He felt foolish. He had felt foolish for days now, talking to mirrors, but foolishness was a luxury he had learned to afford nine years ago in Master Corvel's kitchen, when a starving boy swallowed his first measured drop of nightshade and was told, *dignity is for those who can survive having it.* "You show me everything else. Every man I didn't become. Show me what this room was before."

The mirror did nothing, of course. Mirrors never do anything. That is their whole profession.

But as he turned away, something caught at the edge of his eye—not in the glass, but *behind* it, the way a watermark hides in paper until you tilt it toward the light. He turned back. Tilted his head. Breathed on the silver and wiped it with his sleeve, slow, in an arc, the way he had polished the guild's brass scales a thousand mornings.

And the fog of his breath did not fade evenly. It faded in *lines*.

Jude went very still.

Lines. Ruled lines, faint as veins in marble, running vertical and horizontal behind the mirror's surface. And crossing them, in a hand so old the letters had begun to dream of becoming pictures—*writing*.

He knew that hand. He had spent nine years learning hands, because a ledger is only as honest as its script, and a forger always reveals himself in the downstrokes. He knew Master Corvel's cramped, pinched numerals. He knew Ossen's grand, wasteful flourishes. He knew his own careful, self-effacing print, the hand of a man who had taught himself to take up as little room as possible.

This was none of them. This was a hand from before any of them—a hand that wrote the way people wrote when paper was dear and every word had to earn its ink. The letters were tall and narrow and leaned forward like travelers against a wind.

He could not read it through the glass. But he could read the *shape* of it, the way he could read a ledger's shape through a locked strongroom door—the rhythm of entries, the columns, the small dense block at the top where a title would go, and beneath it, in a space left deliberately empty, a redaction.

A redaction. In a mirror. In the Undercourt.

His heart began to do the slow, heavy work it did when he stood before a dish that might kill him. He wet his sleeve from the thin condensation that wept down the cell's stone frame—there was always water in the Undercourt; water remembered being sky, and it grieved—and he wiped the panel again, and again, working the moisture into the silver until the glass darkened and the lines beneath swam up like fish rising to a fed pond.

It was a ledger page. An entire ledger page, pressed into the mirror the way a leaf is pressed into stone. The Undercourt had not built this cell. It had *bound* something—bound a page of the Hollow Guild's first book into the silver, the way you bind a spirit into a ring, the way you bind a name into a contract. A prison made from the guild's own founding paper.

And at the top, in that tall, wind-leaning hand, a title:

*The Book of What Was Owed.*

* * *

He worked for hours. He had no way to know how many; the cell kept no hours, only his own exhaustion, and he had long ago taught his body to ignore that too. He breathed and wiped, breathed and wiped, coaxing the page up out of the silver a line at a time, and as he worked he whispered the words aloud, because a thing spoken can be carried even when a thing written is taken from you, and he had lost too much already to trust paper.

The entries were small. That was what undid him, at first—their smallness. He had expected the founding of the Hollow Guild to be written in thunder. Instead it was written in the same voice he used every day: a widow's grief weighed against a fever's breaking. A farmer's courage sold to fund a harvest. A child's fear of the dark, purchased by a mother who could not bear to watch him carry it, and resold three years later to a soldier who wanted to feel nothing at the wall.

The first guildmaster had been a bookkeeper of ordinary sorrows. She had discovered, somewhere in a plague winter of her own, that grief could be *moved*—lifted from one shoulder and set on another, or set down entirely, if there was a strongroom deep enough to hold it. And she had discovered that everything moved must be recorded, because a grief that no one remembers owning is a grief that owns *you*.

The ledger was not founded on greed. Jude had assumed greed; he had assumed, in his darker hours, that Ossen and Corvel and all the grey-robed traders before them were simply vultures in respectable robes. But the founding entries read like the work of a woman trying to *save* a drowning city, one sorrow at a time, and running out of hands.

Then he reached the entry that was different.

It sat alone, after a gap in the page—a gap like a held breath. The ink here was different too, darker, pressed harder, as if the writer had been leaning her whole weight into the pen. And the entry was not a trade between two people. It had only one name in it. Her own.

He wiped the glass again and again, and the words came up slow, and he spoke them as they came, and his voice in the cell of mirrors was a hundred Judes speaking in ragged unison:

*"Let it be entered that I, Maren Vos, first keeper of this book, do sell the one thing I have left that cannot be borrowed or bottled or weighed: my death. Not the manner of it. The fact of it. I give my death into keeping, that the ledger may have a foundation-stone, for a debt must rest on something, and I have nothing else to give.*

*"Let it be entered that the price of my death is the keeping of this book, forever, by whoever holds it after me.*

*"Let it be entered that I do not know who holds the other end of this bargain.*

*"Let it be entered that I did not ask."*

The page ended there. Below it, in the same hard-pressed ink, one final line, smaller, almost an afterthought, the kind of line a bookkeeper adds at the bottom of a column when the sum troubles her and she cannot say why:

*"Balance outstanding."*

Jude stood in the coal-red dark with his hand flat against the mirror, and the cold of it climbed his arm and settled in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

*Balance outstanding.*

Four hundred years. Four hundred years of trades—the widow's laughter, the farmer's courage, the child's fear, the borrowed years and bottled griefs, his mother's soul, his own soul, Brannic's daughter's name, Sable's four centuries of servitude—all of it, every entry in every book in every strongroom in the capital, resting on a foundation that had never been paid for. Maren Vos had sold her death to start the ledger, and no one had ever bought it back. No one had ever even *known* to buy it back. It sat in the Undercourt's keeping like a stone at the bottom of a well, and every trade since had been built on top of it, and every trade since had inherited the debt.

The guild's books balanced. He had balanced them himself, nine years of mornings, candle and quill, and they balanced to the last copper. But they balanced the way a house balances on a cliff-edge: perfectly, and only because no one looked down.

*The whole system is unpaid grief.* His mother's grief, priced and pawned. Maren Vos's death, sold and never redeemed. Sable's regret, compounding for four centuries. The Undercourt did not run on souls. Souls were only the coin. It ran on the *outstanding balance*—on the great unpaid weight of it, drawing interest in the dark, and the Patient King was not a king at all. He was a *creditor*. He was what you got when a debt grew so old and so large that it learned to speak, learned to be courteous, learned to wait.

And the guild—the guild that had fed him, sheltered him, taught him that survival was a discipline and tenderness a liability—the guild had been, from its first day, a woman's grief that no one had ever finished paying for.

Jude slid down the mirror until he was sitting on the cold floor, his back against a hundred versions of himself, and for a long time he did not move. The coal-light breathed. Somewhere far below, the market's music had stopped entirely, and the silence had a texture to it, a held-breath quality, like the pause after a sum comes out wrong.

* * *

It was the mirrors that brought him back. Not the strange Jude in the seam-panel—the others. As he sat with the founding entry burning in his memory, the reflections around him began, very slowly, to change. The chandler-Jude, the guard-Jude, the flour-handed laughing Jude—they dimmed, one by one, like lamps being pinched out, and in their places the glass showed him something else.

Not other lives. *Other ledgers.*

Page after page, rising behind the silver like the founding page had risen, and he understood with a lurch that the whole cell was lined with them—that the King had built his prison of mirrors out of the guild's own history, every generation of books bound into the glass, and that he had been sitting for days in a room made entirely of the evidence.

He got to his knees. He wiped. He read.

The second keeper's book. The ninth. The twenty-third. He read the guild's whole life in fragments, breath and sleeve and whisper, and the story it told was the story of a debt compounding. Each keeper inherited the balance and added to it. Each keeper redacted a little more—the founding entry itself was redacted by the third generation, he saw, struck through in a careful official hand, *entry sealed by order of the Guildmaster's Conclave, for the stability of the trade*—and each redaction made the foundation harder to see and the structure above it heavier. They had not hidden the truth out of malice. They had hidden it out of *fear*, which is worse, because fear is a reason that always sounds like a good one at the time.

And in the margins, in a hundred different hands across four hundred years, the same note appearing again and again, small and troubled, like a cough in a quiet congregation:

*The other party to the founding contract has never been identified.*
*Who holds Maren Vos's death?*
*The strongroom reports the foundation-stone cold to the touch. Colder each year.*
*Asked the Guildmaster who we owe. Was told the question is not profitable.*

*Not profitable.* Jude laughed—a short, cracked sound, the first he had made in the cell that was not despair. Not profitable. The guild's one unforgivable sin, the question that would have unraveled them all, and they had buried it under redactions for four hundred years because the answer might have cost them everything.

It *was* going to cost them everything. It was going to cost them everything because a debt unpaid for four hundred years does not sit still. It grows. It *appreciates*—the King's own word, the King's own appetite. And Jude understood, sitting in the dark with the guild's whole history pressed into the walls around him, why the court beneath the mountain had wanted a taster who was also a ledger-keeper. Not for his soul. His soul was pocket money. They wanted the one man alive who had read every book, who knew every entry, who could trace the whole towering structure of trades down to the cracked foundation-stone at the bottom—and who was, by nine years of discipline and one mother's terrible bargain, almost incapable of walking away from an unbalanced column.

They had hired their own auditor. And the audit was nearly complete.

He stood. His knees ached; his eyes ached; his throat was raw from whispering. None of it mattered. In his memory—memory, the one strongroom they had not yet emptied, the one vault the tithe could not seem to reach no matter how many stars went out—he held the founding entry entire. Maren Vos. Her death, sold. Her price, the keeping of the book. Her balance, outstanding.

And one more thing, a thing so small he had almost wiped past it: in the corner of the founding page, where a bookkeeper notes the counterparty to a contract, there was a symbol. Not a name. A sigil, small as a thumbnail—a circle, and within the circle, a set of scales, and beneath the scales, a single vertical line, like a door standing shut.

He had seen that sigil before. He had seen it that very week, in the great ledger room of the Undercourt, stamped in gold on the spine of the King's own master-book—the book in which the court recorded *its* holdings, *its* debts, *its* purchases.

The court had not merely bought Maren Vos's death. The court had been *founded on* it. The King's authority, the court's power to hold and price and collect—the whole inverted palace with its ceiling of stolen stars rested on a single unpaid entry in a bookkeeper's hand.

Which meant the King's power had a sum. And every sum, no matter how old, no matter how patient, no matter how courteous—

Every sum could be *called*.

Jude pressed his palm flat against the mirror, against the faint ghost of Maren Vos's wind-leaning letters, and spoke to her across four hundred years, in the only language they shared.

"Balance outstanding," he said. "I'm going to collect."

Behind the silver, so faint he might have imagined it, the letters seemed to lean a little closer—like travelers against a wind, like a woman listening at a door.

And far above, through a thousand feet of mountain, the first of the ceiling-stars went out, and somewhere in the capital a stranger forgot the smell of rain, and the Patient King turned his ancient head toward a cell of mirrors and began, for the first time in four centuries, to be afraid.
