## 29. Escape by Arithmetic

The mirrors did not sleep.

That was the first thing Jude had learned in the cell — that the court beneath the mountain kept no honest dark. In the palace above, night had been a mercy, a curtain drawn over the day's accounts so a man could pretend the ledger was balanced until morning. Here there was no morning and no curtain. The mirrors burned with a low silver light like moonlight strained through cloth, and they showed him everything he might have been, arranged in tiers around him like a gallery of unpaid debts.

The taster who had said yes to Master Corvel's first dish and wept after, and been dismissed for it.

The taster who had never taken Corvel's coin at all, who had starved honestly through the plague winter and died at eleven in a ditch behind the tannery, nameless, unrecorded.

The son who had known what his mother paid. Who had read the contract at nine years old and run into the wards and broken them himself rather than let them stand on his soul's back.

The king.

That one was new. It had appeared sometime during what Jude thought of as his third night, though time below the mountain moved like water in a stopped clock — it went nowhere, but it was always going. The mirror at the apex of the cell showed him tall and robed in shadow, crowned with a circlet of dead stars, patient beyond all patience. His face in that reflection was his own face aged by centuries, smoothed by power, emptied by it.

"You flatter yourself," Jude told it.

The reflection did not answer. None of them did. They only watched, and waited for him to become one of them.

He sat on the cold floor with his back against the true wall — the only surface in the cell that returned nothing, gave back nothing, lied about nothing — and he did what he had done every waking hour since the King's guards had carried him here: he counted.

It was not comfort. Comfort had been stripped from him along with everything else; Sable had seen to that warning before they took him, her second face grave and old. *He will leave you your mind,* she had said. *He wants you sharp when you break. A broken fool proves nothing. A broken accountant proves the ledger is just.* So they had left him his skill with numbers the way a butcher leaves a carcass its bones, and Jude had decided, in the first hour, in the first cold minute, that if they were going to leave him anything, he would make it a weapon.

He counted because counting was the last honest thing left in this place.

And somewhere in the long silver dark of the fourth night or the fourteenth, in the middle of an inventory he had already done forty times, the numbers turned on him and showed their teeth.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes in his life had begun, with a margin.

He had been reconstructing the Undercourt's books from memory — not the guild's ledgers, which he knew line by line, but the mirrored versions he had seen in the Ledger Room during those few stolen hours before the King's patience ended. Two hundred years of entries. Souls purchased from the crown at a fixed rate. Wards funded. Tithes collected. Interest accrued on every borrowed year, every bottled grief, every name sold by a desperate parent in a plague winter.

He had been checking the King's arithmetic, because checking arithmetic was the only prayer he knew.

And the arithmetic was wrong.

Not sloppy-wrong. Not the error of a tired clerk. Wrong in the specific, elegant, terrible way that a thing is wrong when it has been wrong so long that everyone has forgotten it was ever supposed to be right.

Jude got up off the floor. His body protested — they fed him enough to keep the machinery running and no more, and his ruined immunity-ruined flesh ached in a hundred small ways since the Tollgate of Teeth had taken his tolerances — but he crossed to the nearest mirror anyway, the one showing him as a grey-faced clerk in some countinghouse, and he pressed his finger to the glass.

"Say I'm wrong," he whispered. "Prove me wrong."

The mirror, naturally, said nothing. Mirrors never do. That is their whole profession.

So he proved it himself, aloud, in the ringing silence, the way Master Corvel had taught him to recite poison dosages — slowly, precisely, each figure set down like a stone in a wall.

"Entry one." His breath fogged the silvered glass. "The court purchases the mountain. Not claims it — *purchases* it. I saw the founding entry in the Ledger Room, buried under two centuries of accruals. The Undercourt did not arise here. It was bought. From something older. There is a seller's mark on the deed, and the seller's mark is not the King's."

The mirrors around him shimmered faintly, the way water shivers when a fish passes far below.

"Entry two. The purchase price was never paid in full. It couldn't have been. I ran the figures. The court's entire revenue — two hundred years of souls, tithes, interest, the whole ghastly harvest — compounded against the original debt at the rate written into the founding deed—" his voice caught, steadied "—the rate written into the founding deed exceeds the court's total yield. Every year. Every single year since the beginning. The court has been running a deficit since before the guild existed. Before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath."

He began to pace, three steps, turn, three steps, the geometry of a man thinking hard in a small space.

"That's why the tithe-collectors are so hungry. That's why Sable worked four hundred years without rest. That's why the King takes souls that are already half-spent, why he'd bargain for a food-taster whose soul has been appreciating — appreciation, gods, he used the word *appreciation*, like a merchant talking about wine." Jude laughed, and the sound came out cracked and strange in the mirrored dark. "You don't appreciate an asset you own outright. You appreciate an asset you're leveraged on. He's not collecting because he's greedy. He's collecting because he's *insolvent*."

The word fell into the silence and lay there shining.

Insolvent. The King of the Undercourt, ancient and courteous and terrible, the patient thing in the dark that had outlasted kingdoms — insolvent. A debtor. The greatest debtor in the world, presiding over the greatest engine of debt-collection ever built, feeding everything it gathered into a hole that never filled, paying interest forever on a purchase made before human memory, to a creditor whose name had been redacted even down here.

Jude stopped pacing.

His heart was doing something complicated in his chest — fear and joy in the same irregular rhythm, like a pulse learning a new dance.

"Everything is a debt," he said softly. "That's what Sable told me. Debt is the court's native tongue. And I spent weeks thinking that meant I could never win — that a man made of payments couldn't out-bargain the language itself." He turned in a slow circle, looking up at the tiered mirrors, at all the selves he might have been, at the crowned one at the top wearing his face like a mask worn too long. "But if debt is the native tongue, then the King speaks it too. He speaks nothing else. And a debtor — a true debtor, a drowning debtor — will do anything to keep his creditors from being named."

The mirrors had gone very still. Even the false light seemed to lean inward.

"The founding entry," Jude breathed. "The first guildmaster sold her own death to start the ledger. I found that in the glass. Every trade since rests on a debt never repaid — I thought that was the guild's shame. But the guild learned it from somewhere. The guild is a *copy*. A crude, mortal imitation of the original book." He pressed both palms flat against the cold true wall. "The original book is the deed to this mountain. And somewhere in it, under the redaction, under the seller's seal, there is a name. A creditor older than the court. Something the King owes everything to and has paid nothing."

He closed his eyes.

"And if I can prove that," he whispered, "then every soul in this court is collateral on a debt the King cannot pay. Which means none of us were ever truly *his*. You can't own what you've pledged. We've been held, not owned. Pawned, not purchased." His eyes opened. "Which means the valuations are fraudulent. All of them. Every price ever set on every soul down here was set by a man with no clear title to set prices at all."

Somewhere far above, in the vaulted dark where the stolen stars hung in the ceiling like fruit on a poisoned tree, something shifted.

* * *

He worked for what felt like days.

There was no ink in the cell, no chalk, nothing to write with — the King wanted him whole-minded but empty-handed, a blade kept sharp in a sheath of glass. So Jude wrote in the only medium available: himself. He scratched the figures into memory the way he had once memorized poison dosages, building them in layers, checking each stratum against the last. The founding deed's principal. The interest clause — compounding annually, he was certain, at the rate of one year per year per soul-equivalent, which sounded modest until you understood that the court's *yield* was capped by the number of souls it could harvest, while its *debt* grew geometrically, forever, indifferent to harvests.

A capped income against an uncapped obligation.

He had audited merchants who hanged themselves over less.

By the end — whether the end was the fifth day or the fiftieth, he no longer knew — he stood in the center of the cell with the whole architecture of the proof assembled in his skull, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall, and he spoke it out loud to the mirrors, formally, the way a verdict is read.

"Hear the account," he said. His voice was hoarse from disuse but steady. "In the year the wards were first raised, the Crown of the surface kingdom purchased protection from the court beneath the mountain. To fund that purchase, the court itself borrowed — from a party older than either throne, whose name appears in the founding deed under seal. The court's obligation compounds without limit. The court's revenue is finite. Therefore, at some point in the past — I can give you the decade, given an hour and a straightedge — the court's liabilities exceeded its assets, and the King of the Undercourt became, in law and in ledger, a bankrupt."

The mirrors flickered.

"Therefore," Jude continued, louder, "every valuation issued since that date was issued by an insolvent party and is void. Therefore every soul held in this court is held against fraudulent paper. Therefore the market is frozen — not by revolt, not by refusal, but by *arithmetic*. You cannot collect a debt with money you do not own. You cannot own collateral on a pledge you defaulted. The whole edifice—" and here his voice rose, cracked, rose again, "—the whole beautiful, patient, eternal edifice is a house of receipts, and I am holding the one that burns it down."

The light in the cell changed.

It did not dim — it *trembled*, the way candlelight trembles when a door opens somewhere far away, and Jude understood that the King had heard. Of course the King had heard. This whole prison was probably the King's ear. Jude had spoken the proof aloud in the court's native tongue, in its own language, in the one dialect it could not refuse to parse, and now the words were loose in the walls like water in a foundation.

The crowned mirror at the apex rippled.

For the first time, the reflection moved. The King-that-was-not-him turned its head — Jude's face, ancient and smooth and terribly calm — and looked down at him with something that was not anger. Anger would have been a relief. This was worse. This was the look of a merchant watching a customer find the flaw in the goods.

"Clever," said the mirror, in a voice like dust settling. "Clerks are always clever. It is why I keep them."

"You don't keep me," Jude said. "You can't afford to. That's rather the point."

The silence that followed had weight. It pressed on his shoulders like snow.

"Do you imagine," the King said, and now the voice came from every mirror at once, from the walls themselves, from the floor, "that you are the first to find the flaw? Four hundred years ago a taster stood in this cell and found a discrepancy in the tithe rolls. Six hundred years ago a guildmaster found the same seam in the founding deed. They were clever too. They are part of the ceiling now." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "The debt is real, little accountant. The creditor is real. I have paid what could be paid for longer than your species has kept calendars. Do you think I *enjoy* the harvest?"

"No," Jude said. "I think you need it. That's worse, for you."

Something moved in the silvered dark — a pressure, a gathering, the sense of vast attention narrowing to a point.

"I offer you the same mercy I offered the others. Serve as my ledger-keeper. Curate yourself. Keep the arithmetic safe, keep it *quiet*, and I will keep you comfortable until the sun burns out. This is generosity. This is the last generosity—"

"You want me to guard your insolvency," Jude said. "You want an auditor who cooks the books. I've refused better offers from worse liars."

The mirrors screamed.

There was no other word for it — a soundless scream, a shriek of pressure, every reflection warping at once, the tiers of possible Judes stretching and melting like wax, and the King's voice tore across them: "*Then be unpriced. Be nothing. Be a footnote in a ledger that outlives your name—*"

And Jude, standing in the storm of his own shattered possibilities, did the bravest and simplest thing he had ever done.

He smiled, and he repeated the figures.

Not the offer. Not the plea. The *numbers*. Principal, rate, term, default date — he recited them in a clear carrying voice, again and again, a litany, an audit read into the record, and with every repetition the mirrors warped harder, and with every repetition the words sank deeper into the walls, into the foundations, into whatever ancient listening dark lay beneath the mountain and held the court's original deed.

Because that was the thing the King had never understood about clerks — the thing no king understands.

You can imprison a man. You can starve him, hollow him, sell his memories by the dram. But if you leave him the numbers, the numbers will do the rest. Numbers are patient. Numbers are loyal to no one. And a true figure, once spoken into a room, cannot be unsaid — it propagates. It waits. It gets into the mortar of the world and it *compounds*.

"*Liabilities exceed assets,*" Jude said. "*The valuations are void. The market is frozen by arithmetic.*"

The mirror at the apex cracked.

A single line, hair-fine, running through the crowned reflection of the King's borrowed face — and then another, crossing it, and then a web of them, spreading outward from that mirror to the next and the next, crackle and bloom, the whole gallery of possible Judes fracturing into silver lace, and through the cracks Jude saw not darkness but *light* — thin, white, honest light, the kind that comes from no star at all, the kind that was owed to this place before the first ward was raised.

The cell burst.

Glass went everywhere, a blizzard of silvered shards, and none of it cut him — he would wonder about that later, much later — and Jude Vane stood in the ruins of his own reflections with his fists full of falling light, breathing hard, alive, unpriced, free of the gallery if not yet of the mountain.

Around him, the Undercourt was waking.

He could hear it through the broken wall: a sound like a thousand voices realizing, all at once, in the same instant, the same simple fact. The sound of the sold discovering their paper was worthless. The sound of a market freezing mid-transaction. Somewhere below, a tithe-collector's bell rang once and stopped, as if the hand on it had remembered it belonged to someone else.

Sable's face rose in his memory — her second face, the old one, the human one. *Debt is the court's native tongue.* Yes. And he had just read the court its own confession in front of witnesses.

Jude picked a single shard of mirror out of the rubble — a sliver no bigger than a finger, still holding one last reflection: not a king, not a corpse in a ditch, just a tired man with ink-stained hands and his mother's jaw — and he put it in his pocket like a coin.

Then he turned toward the sound of the waking dead, and started walking.

Behind him, far above, the first of the ceiling-stars went out — not taken this time, not harvested, but *flickering*, uncertain, like a flame that has heard, at last, the exact size of the debt that feeds it.

The revolt of the sold had begun. And it needed a ledger-keeper.
