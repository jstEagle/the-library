## 30. Revolt of the Sold

The arithmetic fit on a single page.

That was the thing about it—the thing that made it dangerous. The King's court had been built on volumes: ledgers stacked to the ceiling of every corridor, contracts bound in vellum and shadow, valuations so dense with notation that only the court's own clerks could read them. But a debt was a debt, and debts obeyed simple laws. Jude had spent nine years balancing books in a back room by candlelight. He knew what happened when the numbers stopped agreeing.

He wrote it out three times before he trusted it. Then he copied it eleven more times, because he did not know how many hands he would need.

*The Undercourt purchased its ground from something older.* He did not name the older thing. He didn't have to; the mirrors had shown him enough, and besides, the proof required no names—only dates. *The purchase price was never paid. Interest has accrued for an age beyond counting. Therefore the court itself is a debt. Therefore everything valued against the court's authority is valued against nothing.*

A currency is only as sound as the vault behind it. And the King's vault was empty, and had been empty since before the first guildmaster sold her death to start the Hollow Guild's ledger, and every trade since—every borrowed year, every bottled grief, every soul signed away in a plague winter by a desperate woman with a child still quick inside her—had been paid out of that emptiness.

Jude folded the pages into his coat and went down into the market.

* * *

The market beneath the mountain did not sleep, but it had rhythms, the way a heart does even in fever. In the long dark hours between the ceiling-stars' slow dimming, the stalls of the sold opened their shutters: memories laid out like fish on ice, years measured into stoppered vials, names chalked on slate boards with prices beside them. The vendors were the goods themselves. That was the genius of the place, and the obscenity. A woman sold the taste of bread from her own mouth and stood afterward smiling at customers who bought it, unable to remember why she had ever liked it. A man sold his wedding day in thirds and attended each sale personally, holding the vial up to the light, praising its clarity.

They had all been told this was fair. They had all been shown their valuations—slips of pale parchment with figures in the court's flowing hand—and they had all accepted them, because acceptance was the only verb the court permitted. You could sell. You could buy. You could not refuse.

Jude walked the aisles with his collar up and his eyes down, the way he had walked through the palace kitchens for nine years: invisible, unremarkable, a man whose whole art was being overlooked. Sable's key hung cold against his chest. Brannic was somewhere above, or below—he had stopped asking which direction meant what—and Wren was a name in a notebook a world away, waking into a gift she couldn't trace. He carried them all with him anyway. He was learning that you carry people whether or not they are present. It was one of the few things the court could not inventory.

The first page he gave to the bread-woman.

She took it the way the sold take everything offered—with the flat courtesy of those who have had their surprise removed. Her eyes moved across the figures. Jude watched her face do something he had not seen in the Undercourt before: it hesitated.

"This says the court owes," she said slowly.

"It says the court has never paid," Jude said. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"Everything here is owed." She said it without heat, the way one recites a catechism. "I owed. I signed."

"You signed against a currency that doesn't exist." He kept his voice low, level, the voice Master Corvel had drilled into him over dishes of increasing lethality: *calm is a discipline, boy, and discipline keeps you breathing.* "Read the dates. Read the interest. If I owe you a coin and I have no coins—not now, not ever, not in any account anywhere—then what did I pay you? What are you holding?"

She looked at the slip again. Around her, the market murmured on: bids called, vials uncorked, grief decanted by the dram. But she had gone quiet in a way that spread, the way a single still figure spreads quiet through a crowd that is watching without admitting it watches.

"I'm holding my daughter's laugh," she said. "I sold it when the fever came. They said it was worth exactly what the medicine cost. Exactly. To the copper." Her thumb moved over the valuation slip, back and forth, back and forth. "It was worth more than that. I always thought—I always felt it was worth more than that, but there was no word for the feeling, so I put it away."

"There's a word now," Jude said. "*Underpaid.*"

Something moved behind her face—behind the hollow where her surprise used to live. Not memory. Memory was gone, sold or spent. But the shape where memory had been remembered its own outline, the way a scar remembers the wound without recalling the knife.

She folded the page once, twice, and put it inside her bodice, against her skin.

"What do I do with it?" she asked.

"Nothing," Jude said. "That's the whole of it. Refuse to accept the number. When they come to value you tomorrow, say the valuation is false. Say it until they can't unhear it. You don't have to fight. You don't have to run. Just—" and here his voice caught, because he thought of his mother, of a woman signing her unborn child away in a plague winter because the alternative was a grave, "—just stop agreeing."

* * *

By the fourth hour of the market's long night, Jude had given away nine pages.

He learned the craft of it as he went, the way he had once learned poison: dose by dose, reaction by reaction. Some of the sold could not read—the court preferred them illiterate, of course it did, literacy was leverage—so he read aloud to them in the shadows behind the stall of a man who sold silence, which meant the man could not object and no one passing could hear. Some wept, though most of them had sold their weeping and could only shake, dry-eyed, like trees in wind. One old soldier heard the arithmetic through and then laughed, a terrible rusty sound, and said, "I gave them forty years. Forty. And they gave me a pension of three. I *knew* it was a bad bargain. I've known it for two hundred years. I just never knew I was allowed to say so."

"That's the trick of it," Jude said. "There was never a rule against saying so. There was only the agreement. And agreements need two."

"And if they punish us?"

"They can't afford to." He tapped the page. "Every punishment is priced. Every price is drawn from the vault. The vault is empty. Watch what happens when they try."

The tenth page went to a child.

That one nearly broke him. She was small and gray-eyed and she sat at the edge of the market selling fragments of a summer she barely recalled—a lake, a dog, someone's hand in hers—and her valuation slip was pinned to her dress like a brooch, like a decoration, like a prize. Jude crouched in front of her and explained the arithmetic in words of one syllable, and she listened with the ferocious attention children give to anything that sounds like a story about unfairness, because children are the last true experts on unfairness.

"So the king is poor," she concluded.

"The king is poor," Jude agreed.

"Then why does everyone act rich?"

Because fear is a currency too, he almost said, and it's the only one the vault never ran out of. But he looked at her gray eyes and said instead: "Because nobody told them the truth yet. Will you tell them?"

She took the page the way children take important things—both hands, solemnly—and pinned it to her dress beside the valuation slip, so the two documents hung together like rivals, like accusers.

* * *

The market froze at dawn, if the Undercourt could be said to have a dawn—which was to say, at the hour when the ceiling-stars brightened to simulate it, casting their stolen light down over stalls and clerks and the long marble tables where valuations were performed.

Jude was watching from the gallery above when it happened.

The valuers came out with their ledgers, as they did every morning, robed in the court's gray, carrying their pens of black bone. They approached the first stall. The bread-woman stood behind it, and Jude saw her set both hands flat on the counter—an ordinary gesture, a merchant's gesture, but he saw the tremor in it, the enormous effort of an ordinary gesture in a place built to make ordinary gestures impossible.

"Your valuation," said the valuer, extending the morning's slip.

"No," said the bread-woman.

The word dropped into the market like a stone into still water.

The valuer blinked. Somewhere in his gray robes, Jude imagined, some ancient mechanism tried to process the input and found no slot for it. "Madam, your valuation—"

"—is false." Her voice shook and held. "You valued my daughter's laugh at the price of fever-medicine. My daughter's laugh. I have carried the wrongness of that number for longer than your court has had a ceiling. I am not accepting it. I am not accepting any of them. Come back when your king pays what he owes."

The valuer turned to the next stall. The man who sold silence pointed silently at the page pinned inside his coat. The next stall: the old soldier, arms crossed, laughing his terrible laugh. The next: a cluster of the sold standing shoulder to shoulder, each with a folded paper visible somewhere on their person, in a pocket, under a sleeve, against the heart.

And the child. The child stood at the edge of the marble tables with her two documents pinned to her dress, and when a valuer came near her she held up the arithmetic page with both hands, high above her head, the way other children held up offerings, and read out—in a clear small voice that carried further than any bell—"*Therefore the court itself is a debt.*"

The market stopped.

Not emptied. Stopped. Bids died half-spoken. Vials stayed uncorked. Buyers and sellers stood facing one another across counters laden with years and griefs and names, and no hand moved toward any of them. The silence that fell was not the silence the man sold—that had been absence, a hole shaped like sound. This was presence. This was a thousand throats closed around a thousand refusals, and the weight of it pressed down on the marble like snowfall.

Jude gripped the gallery rail and watched the thing he had made, and felt not triumph but something stranger: vertigo, the same vertigo of the starless night, the invisible man suddenly visible. For nine years he had kept accounts for people who traded in impossible things and told himself the ledgers were neutral, that ink bore no blame. Now the ink was moving. Now every entry he had ever balanced was standing up in the market below and declining, politely, immovably, to be worth what the books said it was worth.

A valuer's pen snapped in his hand. Another simply sat down on the marble amid his scattered slips, staring at them as though the figures had become foreign script. The court's whole grammar had depended on one unspoken axiom—*that the sold agree*—and the axiom had failed, and with it every sentence written in that language.

And then the ceiling flickered.

All the stars at once, a stutter of stolen light, and a cold rolled through the Undercourt that had nothing to do with temperature. Jude knew that cold. He had felt it in his mother's grave-earth and in the turning of fresh soil. The King had noticed.

The light steadied. Down the great stair from the throne dais came the tithe-collector, Sable, four hundred years of careful composure worn like armor over something that had once been a face, and her eyes found Jude in the gallery with unerring accuracy, the way they always had, the way a collector's eyes find the debtor in any crowd.

She stopped at the foot of the stairs. She looked out over the frozen market—the bread-woman with her hands flat on the counter, the child with her page raised, the old soldier still laughing softly to himself like a man hearing music after centuries deaf—and Jude saw, in the set of her shoulders, that she was not looking at a riot.

She was looking at a door.

"You should run," she said, and her voice carried in the hush, pitched for him alone and reaching everyone. "What comes next is not a punishment. Punishments are priced, and you've taught them all that prices are lies. What comes next is older than pricing." Her hand went to her breast—to the oldest contract in existence, chained where a heart should be. "And I am the only one in this court who knows how to survive it. Or how to end it."

She began to climb the stairs toward him.
