## 31. Sable Chooses

The arithmetic had spread through the Undercourt like water through chalk.

Jude felt it before he saw it—a change in the pressure of the air, the way the mirrored halls seemed to hold their breath. For three days he had walked the corridors of the court beneath the mountain, pressing the proof into every hand that would take it, whispering it into the ears of souls who had forgotten they had ears. *The King is a debt. The court was purchased and never paid off. A debt unpaid can be called in.* Simple words. Ledger words. The only language this place respected.

And now the market had gone quiet.

He stood at the edge of the great trading floor—the inverted bazaar where memories hung in glass like lanterns and years were weighed on brass scales—and listened to the silence. It was not the silence of peace. It was the silence of a held coin, of a scale trembling between weights. The sold souls of the Undercourt stood among their stalls without selling. A woman who had traded her grief four hundred years ago held the little bottle of it now, turning it in her fingers, and did not let go. A boy—there were boys here, there had always been boys here, Jude tried not to think about that—stood before a shelf of borrowed summers and simply looked at them, as if seeing for the first time that they were his summers, or someone's, and that looking cost nothing.

Nothing given freely can be owned. The guild's founding principle, spoken aloud in a place built entirely on the opposite axiom.

The ceiling-stars overhead burned low and sullen. The King had begun extinguishing them after Jude's refusal—one by one, each death taking its memory from the world above—and perhaps a third of them were dark now, ragged holes in the false sky through which nothing fell but a cold that smelled of iron. Jude had counted them. Counting was what he had. Forty-one stars dead since the refusal. Forty-one kinds of forgetting loose in the capital like weather.

"You've made a mess," said a voice behind him, "of my home."

Sable.

She came down the market aisle the way she had come up his stairs in the capital all those weeks ago: unhurried, exact, her gray dress moving without sound, her face wearing the expression of a woman reading a bill she already knew the total of. But something in her was different. Jude had spent nine years learning to read people by what they swallowed and what they hid, and he could see it now—the smallest tremor at the corner of her mouth, the way her hands hung at her sides not folded but *open*, palms turned slightly outward, as if she had set something down and not yet learned to stand without it.

"Your home," Jude said. "You told me once you didn't have one."

"I told you I didn't have anything else." She stopped an arm's length from him. Up close, in the starlight of the stolen sky, he could see what four centuries had written on her: fine lines like ledger rules across her knuckles, a faint silvering at her temples that might have been age or might have been frost. "They're calling it in, your arithmetic. Did you know that? There are souls down here older than kingdoms, and they're standing in their stalls asking each other who purchased the purchaser. One of them asked me." Her voice stayed level. Her eyes did not. "Four hundred years I have answered every question this court has ever asked me correctly. And today a dead woman's son asked me a question I couldn't answer."

"What question?"

"*Whose side am I on.*"

* * *

They walked. It was what one did in the Undercourt when the conversation grew too large for standing still—the corridors absorbed speech, gave it somewhere to go. Jude followed Sable past the Hall of Valuations, past the long gallery where the King's ancestors should have hung but instead hung mirrors, past doors that breathed faintly when they passed, as if the court itself were listening. Perhaps it was. The mountain had been awake for a long time; Jude no longer pretended otherwise.

"The King knows," Sable said. "Of course he knows. He has known since the moment you tore the first forged page, and he has been amused, because amusement is what he does with things that cannot hurt him. Debt is his native tongue, and you spoke it well enough to be understood—but understanding a language is not the same as owning it. He will offer you something again. He will offer everyone something. That is how he has survived eleven thousand years. Not strength. *Generosity.* Every revolt in this court's history ended with the rebels accepting gifts."

"And if we refuse the gifts?"

"Then he waits. He is very good at waiting. You are thirty-one days into a tithe, taster. Your body above ground is running out of days to be alive in. He only needs to outlast your heartbeat." She paused at a junction where three corridors met, and in the low light her face went strange—young, almost, the face beneath the four hundred years, the face of whoever she had been before the court wore her smooth. "Unless someone changes the terms."

"Is that why you came? To change the terms?"

"No." She turned to face him fully, and Jude saw that her hands were shaking—not much, barely, the tremor of a woman holding something heavier than her arms remembered. "I came because I owe you a truth, and I have discovered, to my considerable inconvenience, that I still keep accounts. Old habit. The first thing this court takes is the desire to settle debts honestly; they leave you the ledgers but burn out the conscience that wants to balance them. Mine apparently survived. Four hundred years, and it survived, and I hate it, and I am grateful for it, and both of those things are true, which is the first honest sentence I have spoken in this mountain in three centuries."

"Sable—"

"My name wasn't Sable."

The corridor seemed to lean in. Somewhere far below, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be water.

"I was born Maren Ostrey, in a fishing town on the coast you can no longer remember," she said. "I was the king's taster. Not this king—the king above, two hundred years before your Queen's grandmother's grandmother. I swallowed poison in measured doses and kept my ledgers neat and believed, the way you believed, that precision was a kind of armor." A breath. "Then the wards needed feeding, and the crown needed souls, and the taster—who tasted everything, who was trusted with everything—was asked to carry the contracts. Because who would search the food-taster? Who would suspect the invisible man?" Her mouth curved, and it was the saddest curve Jude had ever seen. "I carried them for six years. Six years of souls in my satchel, wrapped in oilcloth next to the bread. And when the Undercourt finally sent its collector for me—for my own soul, payment for services rendered, because the court pays its agents in the only coin it has—I signed. I told myself it was temporary. I told myself I would buy my way back out. I have been telling myself that for four hundred years, and the interest has been compounding the entire time."

Jude thought of his mother. Of course he did. He thought of a pregnant woman walking this same road in the dark, signing her unborn son's soul against a plague winter, and he understood suddenly that Sable had been describing his mother's future—or his own, the version of him that had said yes to the King's curated mercy.

"Why tell me this now?"

"Because of what I'm about to do." She reached into the gray folds of her dress. "And because you deserve to know that the hand giving it to you is not clean. Nothing in this mountain is clean. But it is *mine*, and for four hundred years nothing here has been mine, and I find—" her voice caught, actually caught, a hairline fracture in four centuries of marble— "I find I want one thing in my possession when the end comes that the King did not give me."

She drew out a scroll.

It was small. That was Jude's first thought—that the oldest contract in the Undercourt, the document that had chained a civilization, should be so small. Blackened at the edges as if it had been carried through fire, soft with handling, tied with a cord that might once have been red. It hummed. Not a sound—a vibration Jude felt in his teeth, in the fillings of his immunity-ruined jaw, in the bones behind his eyes. Power, he realized. Raw, patient, appallingly old power, folded up small enough to hide in a dress.

"This is the master key," Sable said. "The first contract. My contract. Do you understand what that means? Every authority this court exercises flows from it. When the first bargain was struck—the bargain that purchased this court from whatever sleeps beneath it—the King required a mortal signature to anchor the debt in the living world. A co-signatory. Someone human, whose soul would bind the terms to flesh. He chose me because I was convenient. He has regretted it for four hundred years, though he has never once shown it, because showing it would mean admitting the flaw." She pressed the scroll into Jude's hands, and the humming climbed into his wrists like a second pulse. "A contract is only as strong as its oldest clause. And the oldest clause in existence is mine. Which means that the chain around this court's neck—" she lifted her chin toward the ceiling, toward the throne room, toward the patient ancient thing waiting there— "*runs through my hands.*"

Jude stared at the scroll. His professional mind, the part of him that had balanced impossible books by candlelight for nine years, was already reading it, already seeing the architecture: the anchoring clause, the succession-of-liability, the beautiful terrible sentence that made one dead woman's signature the load-bearing wall of an empire. If the co-signatory revoked—

"It will kill me," Sable said, before he could ask. "Revocation requires the signatory's surrender. Not her signature—her *surrender*. The whole of it. Everything the court gave me over four centuries: the years, the unaging, the office, the name. All of it returns to the debt pool at once, and what's left over afterward is whatever I would have been if I'd died on schedule two hundred years before your grandfather was born." She smiled, and for one moment the smile was young, was Maren Ostrey's, was a girl on a coast Jude could not remember. "Which is to say: not much. An old woman with bad teeth and salt in her lungs. I've done the arithmetic, taster. I'm rather good at it. Four hundred years of compound interest, paid all at once—it doesn't leave a person."

"There has to be another way."

"There is always another way. That is the King's gospel, and it is a lie. There is only the price, and whether you pay it lying to yourself or standing up." She closed his fingers over the scroll with both of her hands, and her hands were cold, and steady now, the trembling gone as if the decision itself had weight enough to hold her still. "Brannic taught me that, do you know. Your smuggler. Twenty years searching for a daughter's name, and when the chance came he didn't haggle—he paid. Love priced honestly at last. I watched him walk out of the throne room aged and weeping and *free*, and I stood in the shadows doing the sums I have been avoiding since before your kingdom existed, and the sum came out the same way it always comes out."

"What way is that?"

"*Everything costs. The only choice is what.*"

* * *

They went up.

The throne room of the Undercourt lay at the heart of the mountain, and the road to it was the road of mirrors, and the mirrors had changed. Three days ago they had shown Jude every version of himself he might have been—the taster grown fat and safe, the taster dead in a ditch, the taster crowned, the taster kneeling. Now they showed the court itself: the market frozen mid-trade, the sold souls standing in their aisles with their heads lifting like a field of flowers finding the sun, and everywhere, everywhere, the arithmetic passing hand to hand, whispered, scratched into stall-tops, sung in fragments by voices that had not sung in centuries.

The King sat on his throne of black glass beneath the greatest of the stolen stars, and he was watching the mirrors too.

He did not rise when they entered. He never rose; rising was effort and effort was for debtors. He sat with his hands folded, ancient and courteous, his face a handsome blur that Jude's eyes slid off of no matter how hard he looked, and when he spoke his voice filled the chamber the way cold fills a cellar.

"Maren."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Jude felt Sable flinch beside him—the first flinch he had ever seen from her, four hundred years of composure cracking along a single syllable.

"You have been listening at doors," the King said pleasantly. "Carrying contraband. Feeding the unrest with the little boy's arithmetic. I have watched you do it, and I confess I wondered when you would remember what you are. Not what you were—what you *are*. You are my first instrument. My oldest pen. Every entry in every book below the mountain was written in a hand descended from yours. And you would turn on me now, at the end, over the word of a food-taster with thirty-one days left in his account?" The blur of his face inclined toward Jude, and Jude felt the appraisal like a hand on his sternum. "I could give you her, you know. I could forgive her trespass, restore her youth, make her what she was before she was anything at all. One gift. Freely given. You believe in those, don't you? Things freely given cannot be owned. Take her freedom as a gift, and watch what my generosity does to your principle."

Jude's grip tightened on the scroll until the blackened edges bit his palms. *That is how he wins,* Sable had warned him. *Not strength. Generosity.*

"He's right about one thing," Sable said quietly. "There will be an accounting for what I'm about to do. So let us do it properly, in front of the books." She stepped forward, out of the shadow of Jude's shoulder, into the full light of the dying stars, and drew herself up—not tall, she had never been tall, but upright the way a ledger line is upright, exact and final. "My lord King. Eleven thousand years ago you bought this court from something older, and you never finished paying. Two thousand years ago you began selling the souls of the living to service the debt. Four hundred years ago you made me your anchor, because you needed a mortal hand to hold the chain, and you chose a taster because tasters are invisible." Her voice did not shake. "You chose wrong. Tasters know poison. And we know that the deadliest ones are the doses so small the body learns to crave them."

"Maren," the King said again, softer now, and Jude heard it—the hunger under the courtesy, the creditor's panic dressed in patience. "Consider what you are. Without the court you are a grave and a fishing town that no longer exists. With me you are eternal."

"I am a *clause*," Sable said. "And clauses end."

She turned to Jude, and took his hands, and folded them together over the scroll the way one folds hands over a dying bird. Her eyes were bright and terribly clear.

"Revocation needs my surrender and your witness. Those are the terms—the original terms, the ones he hoped no one would read to the bottom of the page. A signatory may revoke in the presence of a claimant with standing. You have standing, taster. Your soul is the newest entry in his book and the loudest." A breath. "When I say the words, take the cord off the scroll. Unroll it. And read the last line aloud. Don't hesitate. Hesitation is interest, and interest is his."

"Sable—Maren—" Jude's throat was tight. "What's left over. After the arithmetic. Will there be enough of you to—"

"To be an old woman with bad teeth?" The young smile again, brief as a struck match. "I intend to find out. I have four hundred years of curiosity saved up and nowhere to spend it but forward." She squeezed his hands once, hard, and turned back to the throne. "Now. Before I lose my nerve, which I promise you I have been losing by degrees since the reign of your grandmother's grandmother."

The King rose.

It was the first time Jude had seen him rise, and the sight was worse than any violence—the whole chamber seeming to tilt around the motion, the mirrors recoiling, the stolen stars guttering as if in wind. "Maren Ostrey," the King said, and his voice was no longer pleasant, was no longer anything so small as a voice; it was the mountain speaking, the debt itself speaking, eleven thousand years of unpaid balance finding its tongue. "If you do this, you unmake the roof over every soul in this court. You unmake the wards above. You unmake the sky itself, which hangs by the same thread. Everything falls. Is that the price you choose?"

Sable did not look at the ceiling. She looked at Jude.

"Everything falls," she agreed. "And then, taster—everything gets picked up."

She opened her mouth to speak the words of surrender, and the stars above the throne began, one after another, to go out.
