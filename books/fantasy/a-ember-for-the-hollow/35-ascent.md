## 35. Ascent

The mountain did not want to let us go.

I felt it in the stone itself—a reluctance, a slow contraction, as though the whole Undercourt were a fist that had finally unclenched and now could not bear the emptiness of its own open hand. Behind us the great halls were coming down in silence, which was somehow worse than noise. Mirrors fell without shattering. The ceiling of stars went dark one by one, not extinguished but *released*, each light lifting free of its setting like a moth leaving a wall, and drifting upward through rock as though rock were a rumor it had never believed.

Sable weighed almost nothing.

That frightened me more than anything else in the long climb. Four hundred years of collected self, of bargains and titles and the patient machinery of her office—and when the court's currency failed, all of it had drained out of her like water from a cracked cup. She lay across my shoulders with her head against my neck, and she was light the way an empty coat is light, the way a name is light when no one is left to say it. Her breath came in small, irregular pulls. Once she stirred and murmured something I could not make out, and I chose to believe it was a number. Ledger-keepers to the last, both of us, counting our way toward morning.

Brannic I carried on my back, bound with rope we had found in the tollgate's abandoned booth, because his body deserved better than being dragged. He was heavy. He was so heavy, and I loved him for it—the honest weight of a man who had spent his years honestly at the end, who had walked up to the Patient King and laid two decades of his remaining life on the table like a farmer laying down seed grain, and had asked for nothing but his daughter's name. He had gotten it. He had come back to me aged twenty years in a single hour, weeping and laughing, holding the word *Wren* in his mouth like a coal he was afraid would go out.

He had died before the court finished falling. I think he knew. I think he timed it, the stubborn old smuggler—waited until he heard me say *she'll know your face* before he let go, so that he could leave on a full ledger.

The stair wound upward through the mountain's throat. It was not the road we had descended by; that road had been a tollgate and a negotiation, and negotiations were over now. This was older—a rough-cut spiral of steps that might have been carved by the first debtors, or by the thing older than the King from whom the court itself had been purchased and never paid off. The arithmetic still rang in my head, clean as a struck bell. Every system of value rests on a first valuation, and the Undercourt's first valuation had been theft. I had written it out three times in the dust of the mirror-prison before the mirrors broke, and the proof had broken them, and everything after had been only the sound of a very old lie falling down.

My legs shook. My hands, where they gripped Sable's arms, were gray with stone-dust and something finer than dust—the residue of four centuries of accounting, perhaps, sifting down around us as the deep places emptied. The air grew colder, then warmer, then cold again, and then, abruptly, it smelled of pine.

Pine. Snowmelt. Night air with living things in it.

I stopped on a step and breathed it like a drowning man, and above me, far above, I saw a coin of paler darkness: the mouth of the mountain, open to the sky.

The sky. Which for thirty days had been a dead black page with nothing written on it.

"Almost," I said, to Sable, to Brannic, to myself. "Almost."

* * *

The last hundred steps took an hour. I will not pretend otherwise. There were moments when I set my shoulder against the wall and simply stood, letting the mountain hold me up, feeling the tremor in my thighs and wondering distantly whether my body—my immunity-ruined, poison-scarred, borrowed-time body—would consent to carry two people the rest of the way or whether I would have to choose, and how a man chooses between a friend's corpse and the woman who spent four hundred years hunting him and then handed him the key to her own chains.

I did not have to choose. That is the mercy I remember most clearly from those hours, if mercy is the word: the mountain, dying as it was, seemed to want its debts carried out. The steps rose to meet me. Or I hallucinated that they did. Exhaustion does that; hunger for sleep invents kindnesses. But I climbed, and the coin of sky widened, and the smell of pine became the smell of the world.

Then I was through—out onto a shelf of bare granite under the open night—and my knees went, and I went down with them, easing Sable off my shoulder and lowering Brannic beside her with a care that cost me more than the climbing had.

And there I made myself look up.

The stars were coming back.

Not all at once. Not the way they had gone out, all together, in a single night of collective horror. They returned the way debts are paid—one by one, in ones and twos, here a bright point kindling above the eastern ridge, there a fainter one waking near the zenith, each arrival marked by a softness in the air, a pressure change, the way a room feels when someone enters it. I watched a constellation assemble itself overhead, star by star, and I recognized it—the Ploughman, the one my mother used to point out from the doorstep in the plague winter, the one whose handle she said you could hang your worries on—and I put my face in my filthy hands and shook.

Because I remembered her pointing. That was the thing. Thirty days of losses, thirty memories gone into the tithe-collector's ledger, the sea gone first and then the lullaby and then the color of my mother's eyes—and now, as the stars came home, the memories came home with them, flooding back along channels I had thought scoured dry. The sea arrived all at once: salt, gull-cries, the green glass of shallows, my own small feet in cold foam. The lullaby arrived mid-note, humming itself in my throat before I knew I was doing it. And her eyes—brown, ordinary, warm as bread—I got them back standing on a mountainside at midnight, kneeling between a fading ghost and a dead man, and I laughed, actually laughed, a cracked and ugly sound, because grief and gratitude had gotten tangled somewhere in my chest and neither one would let the other finish.

"Jude."

So quiet I almost missed it. Sable's voice, worn down to thread.

I turned. Her eyes were open. In the growing starlight they looked less like the collector's flat silver and more like what they must have been, four hundred years ago, when she was a girl tasting a queen's food and believing she was safe. Human eyes. Tired ones.

"You're fading," I said. Stupidly. As if she didn't know.

"I'm *finishing*," she corrected, and the ghost of her old precision made me want to weep. "There's a difference. A ledger closes; it doesn't fade." Her hand moved, or tried to. I took it. Her fingers were cool and insubstantial, like holding smoke that had decided to be polite. "The key was mine. Giving it away was the last entry in my column. When the books balance—" she paused, and something moved across her face that on a younger woman I would have called fear, "—the account closes."

"Then don't balance," I said. "Stay unbalanced. God knows I've made a profession of it."

She smiled. It transformed her. For one moment she was not the tithe-collector, not the thing that had stood in my doorway with my mother's signature in her hand; she was just a woman on a mountain, looking up at returning stars, and I understood that this was the face she had worn before the court took her, and that she had kept it folded away all this time the way one keeps a letter one cannot bear to reread.

"The others," she said. "The sold ones. Are they—"

"Walking out." I had seen them behind me in the last galleries: generations of souls, bought and appraised and shelved, putting down their valuations like tools at the end of a shift and moving toward the stairs. Some had bodies waiting for them above—bodies long dead, I supposed, though I had stopped pretending to understand the rules. Others were only light now, or memory, or whatever a soul becomes when it stops agreeing to be currency. They were leaving. All of them. The market was frozen, the King was nowhere, and the Undercourt was emptying itself the way a house empties when the last of a family dies and everyone comes home for the funeral. "They're free. Your arithmetic held."

"*Your* arithmetic." Her eyes closed, opened. "I only unlocked the door. You proved the house was stolen." A breath. "Jude. Listen. When I'm done—there will be a moment. Everything I was, all of it at once, four hundred years of it. It will want to go somewhere. Don't let it go into the ground. Don't waste it."

"Waste it on what?"

"That's yours to decide." Her grip tightened, surprising strength in it, the last strength. "You're the counterfeiter. You're the one who wrote debts that didn't exist and made them true. Write one more."

I wanted to argue. I wanted to tell her about healers, about slow care, about the garden I had already half-invented for her in some unguarded corner of my mind—soil and sun and a mortal body learning to be tired in ordinary ways. But her face stopped me. She had chosen. Four hundred years of other people's choices had been taken from her, priced and traded and shelved, and this one—this final closing of her own account—was hers, and I had torn up enough contracts in the last week to know better than to write another one over a living person's signature.

"All right," I said.

She nodded, once, satisfied, and looked past me at the sky, where the Ploughman hung complete now, every star in its place, and beyond it more constellations were assembling, the whole ruined ceiling of the world being mended stitch by stitch.

"It's beautiful," she said, in the voice of a taster who has just been told the dish is safe. "I'd forgotten it was beautiful."

And she began to finish.

* * *

I will not describe it fully. Some things are not for the ledger. But there was a moment—her body brightening rather than dimming, all that hoarded centuries-self rising in her like heat shimmer—and then a rushing, a wind with no source, and light pouring off her in threads that did not fall but *rose*, streaming upward past me into the night, joining the stars. That was where the returning lights were coming from, I realized. Not only from the fallen court. From her. From every soul walking out of the mountain behind me, each one paying itself back into the sky, the whole stolen ceiling of the kingdom being rehung by the very people it had been stolen from.

Her last act was small. Her hand, going translucent, turned palm-up toward Brannic's shrouded body, and I heard her say—not to me, to him, or to no one—*"Paid in full,"* in the tone of a clerk stamping a document, and then she was light, and then she was air, and then she was stars, and I knelt alone on the granite with a dead man and a scattering of brightness settling on my shoulders like snow.

I sat with it. I sat with all of it—the loss and the gift braided together, indistinguishable, which is the only honest way anything ever comes—and above me the sky filled, and filled, and filled.

* * *

Dawn found me still there, or found what was left of me. I had dragged Brannic to the lip of the shelf where the mountain's shoulder met the treeline, because I refused to let him meet the sunrise underground-adjacent, and I had wrapped Sable's—no. There was nothing left to wrap. I had marked the place instead, scratching a crude line in the granite with the edge of a coin from my pocket, the last coin of a dead currency: *here an account closed.*

The east went gray, then pearl, then the particular bruised gold that means the sun is thinking about it. The pines below the shelf stood black against it. Somewhere a bird started up, tentative, as if checking whether birdsong was still permitted, and when nothing forbade it, a dozen more joined in, ragged and joyful.

And that was when I saw the lanterns.

Three of them, maybe four, bobbing up the switchback trail from the valley—small flames in the gray light, almost invisible, carried by figures moving fast. My heart did something complicated. Because I knew that walk. I knew the set of the lead figure's shoulders, the impatient stride of someone who has been climbing all night and has decided that the mountain owes her an apology.

Wren.

Brannic had described her to me once, in the low hours on the road beneath the wards, when guilt had loosened him the way wine loosens other men: *small, but walks like she's tall. Got my temper and none of my sense.* He had sold her name to the Undercourt twenty years ago to pay a smuggling debt, and she had grown up nameless in a city that tolerated the nameless the way a floor tolerates dust, and she had spent those years becoming exactly the kind of person who climbs a haunted mountain alone at night because the ledgers she'd found in an abandoned guildhouse told her someone she'd never heard of had gone up it carrying her father's debt.

She crested the trail below the shelf and stopped. The lantern swung, throwing wild shadows. Even at forty paces I saw her take in the scene: the man kneeling, the wrapped body, the scratched line in the rock, the sky full of impossible returned stars.

"You're Jude Vane," she called. Not a question. Of course not a question; she had read my notebooks, the ones I'd filled against memory-loss, the elegy I'd written for myself in advance. She knew my handwriting before she knew my face.

"Yes."

"Where's the court?"

"Gone. Dissolved. Paid off." I gestured vaguely at the sky. "It's all up there now."

She absorbed that the way she apparently absorbed everything—quickly, suspiciously, filing it. Then her eyes went to the body beside me, and I watched her understand, and I watched the understanding hit her like a wave hits a swimmer who thought she was past the breakers. The lantern sank. She came up the last stretch of trail at a run and dropped to her knees in the frost-bright grass, and her hand hovered over the shroud, not touching, the way you hover over a stove you know is hot.

"He went to get my name back," she said. Her voice was steady in the way that costs everything. "Twenty years. The traders in the capital, they told me stories—smugglers' stories, a ghost story about a man who sold his daughter and couldn't stop looking. I thought it was a cautionary tale. I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "Was he—did he know? At the end? Did he know it worked?"

"He carried your name up out of the court himself," I said. "He said it aloud on the stairs. He waited until I promised you'd learn his face before he let go."

She made a sound then that was not a word, and folded forward, and pressed her forehead to her father's shrouded shoulder, and the lantern guttered and steadied, and I looked away to give her the privacy of it, and looked up instead at the sky, where the last few stars were kindling in the west even as the dawn erased the east—as if the night were determined to finish its accounting before the day took over the books.

We stayed like that a while. Two strangers and a dead man, under a mended sky, while the birds got louder and the gold came up over the ridge and the mountain behind us stood silent, hollowed, its long tenancy ended. Exhaustion sat on me like a second Brannic. I could feel every one of the nine years of small poisons in my joints, every dose, every measured swallow, the whole tax my body had paid for a crown that had been selling its subjects all along. I would need a healer. I would need weeks. I would need, someday, to decide what a man does after he has burned down the concept of price.

But first—

"Vane." Wren had lifted her head. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level. "In your notebooks. The last pages. There are entries that don't add up. Debts listed with no creditors, payments with no payees. I checked them three times on the road up. Either you made errors—which the rest of your work says you don't—or you wrote things that weren't true yet."

I smiled. It hurt my face, which meant I hadn't smiled in a long time.

"A lady I knew taught me that a ledger can close," I said, "but it doesn't have to end. There's work in those entries. Debts the crown owes—thousands of them, two centuries of sold souls, and most of the sold are still owed restitution. Someone has to keep those books. Honestly, this time. Grain and coin and names returned to their owners." I looked at her—at the sharp, unsentimental, grieving, magnificent creature her father's sacrifice had named. "I'm going to need an apprentice who checks my sums."

Below us, the sun cleared the ridge, and the first full daylight in thirty-one days lay across the valley like a payment received, and far to the south, tiny with distance, the spires of the capital caught fire with it—and I felt, rather than saw, the moment the light reached the city, and the moment the city's sleeping thousands began, all together, to remember.

Wren saw my face change.

"What?" she said. "What is it?"

"They're waking up," I said. "Everyone the stars took from. And Wren—the Queen was poisoned. Whatever killed her is still in the palace, and the only taster who knows the flavor of it is sitting on this mountain." I got my knees under me, swaying, and reached for the shroud. "Help me carry him down. We're going to be famous, and I'd rather be quick."

The first star of the evening, when it came, would find us on the road.
