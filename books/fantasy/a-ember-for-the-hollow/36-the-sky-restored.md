## 36. The Sky Restored

The first star came back while Jude was still climbing.

He felt it before he saw it—a warmth behind his sternum, like a coal breathed upon after long dark—and then the mountain's mouth was suddenly rimmed with light that did not come from Wren's lanterns. He turned, Sable's fading weight across his shoulders and Brannic's body slung in his arms, and looked up at a sky that had been empty for thirty-one days.

One point of white fire, low in the east, burning steady.

Then another. Then ten, then a hundred, stars kindling not all at once but in sequence, as though someone were walking through the heavens lighting lamps one by one, patient as a clerk closing out an account. The darkness above the capital peeled back like a bandage coming off a wound.

Jude sat down where he stood, because his legs would no longer be argued with, and watched the sky remember itself.

* * *

It began with the sea.

Not gradually—there was nothing gradual about it. One moment he was a man who knew *of* the ocean the way you know of a country you've never visited, from other people's descriptions, from his own frantic notebooks; the next he was standing ankle-deep in cold green water at six years old, salt drying on his lips, gulls screaming over the fish-market at Greyharbor, his mother's hand rough on the back of his neck. The smell hit him so hard he made a sound—somebody else's voice, a boy's voice, startled out of him after twenty years.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, it *smelled* like that."

He had written in the notebooks: *the sea is loud. The sea is large.* Both true and both useless, the way a description of sunlight is useless to the blind. What he had lost was not information. It was the specific weight of a wave shoving him off his feet, the taste of brine in a cut on his knee, the way the horizon looked at dusk when the fishing boats came home with their lamps lit—little earthbound stars, he'd thought even then, little stars come down to rest.

And beneath the sea, arriving with it like sediment settling, came the cost.

His mother's face.

He had feared this. In the mountain, in the mirror cell, he had understood what restoration meant—not just the sweet entries returning but the whole ledger, balanced, every line item restored including the ones written in grief. Her face arrived whole: thin, wind-chapped, gray going early at the temples, eyes the same unremarkable brown as his own that everyone had always said were remarkable because they missed nothing. She was laughing in the first memory, laughing at something he'd said, some child's nonsense about buying the moon with pocket coppers.

*You can't afford the moon, love,* she'd told him. *Nobody can. That's how they keep it.*

He wept without dignity, sitting in the scree with two bodies beside him and the sky filling overhead, and he let the memories come the way you let a fever break—shaking, grateful, undone.

The lullaby came last of that first wave. It had no words he could find; it was older than words, a hummed thing, three notes rising and falling like breathing, like waves, like the tide his mother had grown up beside and carried inland to a plague city to save his life. He hummed it there on the mountainside, cracked and half-remembered and now fully remembered, and it sounded exactly as terrible as it always had, and he could not stop.

* * *

Wren found him like that.

She had climbed the last stretch alone, lantern held high, calling his name into the dark—and then the dark had stopped being dark, and she had seen him sitting among the rocks with tears on his face looking up at a sky that was filling with light, and she had stopped calling and simply walked over and sat down beside him, because some things do not need asking after.

"You're crying," she said.

"Yes."

"Good things or bad things?"

Jude considered the question honestly, the way he considered all accounts. "Both. All of them. They came back together." He wiped his face with a sleeve that had been ruined for days anyway. "I thought—I spent those weeks thinking I was mourning what I'd lose. But I wasn't mourning it yet. You can't mourn a thing until you hold it. Now I'm holding all of it at once."

Wren looked up at the stars. There were hundreds now, and still more kindling, the great slow procession of them marching westward across the vault of heaven. Below them, far below, the capital would be waking to the same sight—the whole kingdom, every village and road and broken ward-fence, under a sky again.

"People are going to remember," she said. "Everyone who lost anything. It's coming back to all of them, isn't it? Like water finding its level."

"Yes."

"That's going to hurt people."

"It already is." Jude could feel it faintly, the way he had once felt the Undercourt's ledger humming through the stone—a vast settling of accounts across the world, joy and grief arriving in equal measure to every soul who had paid something into the dark. Somewhere a woman was getting back her laughter and remembering, in the same instant, the fevered son she'd traded it for. Somewhere Brannic's twenty years were pouring back into a body that no longer needed them, and he would wake—or he wouldn't—to find himself young again and holding the memory of what that youth had cost. "There's no version of this where they get the sweetness without the salt. I asked the mirrors for one. They don't make it."

Wren was quiet a moment. Then: "My name came back too. Last night, before the stars. Just—arrived. I woke up knowing it, knowing it had been gone, knowing someone had bought it home." She looked at him sideways. "I don't know who. The ledger entry was burned with everything else."

Jude looked at Brannic's body, laid gently against a boulder, his old man's hands folded by Wren's careful arrangement, his face in the new starlight looking younger than it had any right to.

"He knew," Jude said. "What it would cost. He priced it honestly and paid it anyway. That's the only clean trade I ever saw in either court." He did not say more. Some gifts should arrive without their giver; Brannic had believed that, and Jude could honor it a little longer.

* * *

The memories kept coming through the night.

That was the thing no one had warned him about—that restoration was not an event but a tide, that thirty-one days of stolen self would return in waves for hours, each one breaking over him with its freight of sensation. He sat wrapped in his cloak between Wren and the boulder and let them come.

The taste of Master Corvel's training kitchen: chalk dust and copper bowls, the old man's voice saying *again* in a tone that had no cruelty in it and no kindness either, only the flat patience of a whetstone. Nine years of small poisons rising in him all at once—hemlock's numbness blooming at the tongue's edge, the bright vegetable wrongness of foxglove, the slow warm rot of a hundred lesser toxins his blood had learned to shrug off. And beneath them, arriving with terrible clarity, the Tollgate of Teeth: the gate's many mouths opening, the sensation of his immunity being drawn out of him like thread from a spool, every dish, every dose, every disciplined swallow leaving him forever in a single long exhalation.

He was defenseless now. He kept waiting to panic about it and found, instead, a strange quietness. He had spent nine years being a vessel for poison and twenty-six years being a vessel for debt. It was, perhaps, allowed that he simply be a man for a while—a soft-bodied, mortal, easily-killed man watching the stars come home.

Other tides brought smaller things. The exact weight of a guild ledger in the hand—heavy, that was the surprise people never anticipated, the books were *heavy*, dense with vellum and consequence. The sound of Ossen clearing her throat before announcing bad news. The particular way candlelight moved across columns of figures at three in the morning, making the ink shine like wet roads. The afternoon of sunlight he had kept through everything, hoarded like a coin bitten and found true—one golden afternoon, age eleven, lying in a hayfield doing absolutely nothing, which he had guarded through the auction and the mirrors and the King's courteous appetite, and which returned to him now intact, unspent, still warm.

And the redacted margin. His own name in the old guild book, blacked out by his mother's hand, and beneath the blackout—now that the wards of the Undercourt were dissolved, now that no contract anywhere held force—the truth arriving plain as daylight:

*Jude Vane. Sold before birth. Price: one winter survived. Paid in full by Maren Vane, who descended the mountain road pregnant and came back with a son and a debt she never told him of, because she meant to pay it herself, alone, forever.*

She had almost managed it. Nine years of tithe-collectors passing her door. She had died owing nothing but the principal, and the principal had appreciated, and the court had waited, patient as stone, for the interest to mature.

"I forgive her," Jude said aloud, to the stars, to Wren, to nobody. "For not telling me. For carrying it alone. For selling me to keep me." He swallowed. "I think I forgave her in the mountain. But it's different saying it with her face in my head."

Wren put her hand briefly on his shoulder and said nothing, which was the correct accounting.

* * *

Dawn came up gold behind the eastern ridge, and with it the first sounds of the world remembering.

They heard the village before they saw it—a hamlet in the valley below the mountain road, smoke rising from chimneys, and rising with the smoke a sound Jude had not heard in over a month: voices. Not the smiling hollow hospitality of the Unremembered, but the ragged, weeping, arguing, laughing noise of people getting themselves back. A woman was singing badly and loudly and stopping every few bars because she couldn't see through her tears. A man was shouting a name—*Tamsin! TAMSIN!*—and somewhere a child answered. Someone was ringing the chapel bell with no rhythm whatsoever, just ringing it, because there was a bell and a rope and a sky worth ringing about.

"They'll all be like that," Jude said. "Every village on the road. Every soul that paid something." He got to his feet, joints protesting, and looked down at the two forms he had carried out of the dying court. "We should go down. People will have questions. Terrible questions, and they deserve terrible answers."

"And what will you tell them?"

"The truth. That their crown sold them. Two centuries of it, entry by entry, to keep the wards lit—that the wards were never magic at all, only collateral, only the interest payments on a loan the royal family took out before anyone living was born." He flexed his hands, feeling how they shook. "That the Hollow Guild kept the books. That I kept the books. And that it's over—but over isn't the same as fixed. Everything the court held has to be unwound honestly, trade by trade, or we'll just build another one."

Wren studied him. She had her father's way, Jude was beginning to see, of looking at a person until the person's arithmetic showed.

"You're going to burn the ledgers," she said. "The guild's books. That's why you're shaking."

Jude looked at his hands. "Every outstanding debt in those books is void now—the court that enforced them is gone, the currency is ash. But the *records* remain. And records are how systems start. Someone finds the old formats, the old formulas, the elegant little machinery of valuation, and thinks: here is a tool that worked. Here is how you price a grief." He closed his fists. "I built my life on those books. I loved them. Order, balance, everything accounted. And the whole edifice rested on one unpaid debt at the bottom of page one—an old woman's death, sold to start the ledger, never repaid. Grief, compounding, for four hundred years." He opened his eyes. "Yes. I'm going to burn them. Publicly. In the capital square, if they'll let me, with the Queen as witness. Every book. Every copy. And then—" his voice caught, steadied— "and then I'm going to learn to count honest things. Grain. Coin. Roof-beams. Things that stay what they are."

Below them the bell went on ringing, and the sun cleared the ridge, and the last few stars faded into the blue not extinguished but merely outshone, which was, Jude thought, the entire difference between the world above and the world below, stated plainly at last.

* * *

They buried what needed burying before they left.

Brannic went into the ground on the mountainside, facing the valley, with a cairn of grey stones and no marker but that, because—as Wren said, wiping her face with a fierce efficiency that fooled neither of them—he had spent twenty years being a man without a name, and he could rest as a man without a stone. Jude spoke the guild's words over him, the old ones, the ones from before the corruption: *Nothing given freely can be owned. He gave freely. Let him go free.* And then, because the guild's words had never once in his nine years made anyone feel better, he added words of his own: "He paid love's price at love's rate, and the receipt came home. Rest, old smuggler. Your account is closed, and it closed clean."

Sable they did not bury, because Sable was not dead.

She lay in the shade of the boulder, breathing, barely—four hundred years of borrowed existence draining out of her since the court's dissolution, the ancient contract that had chained the court's authority dissolving along with it and taking her unnatural tenure with it. But she was mortal now, truly mortal, with a mortal span ahead of her instead of behind, and when Jude knelt beside her she opened her eyes—human eyes, brown, tired, ordinary—and looked past him at the sky.

"All of them," she whispered. "I counted them, the first night they went out. Four hundred years ago there were more. The court ate a few. Did you know that?" A breath. "They're all back. Every one."

"Every one," Jude confirmed.

Her mouth moved toward a smile and didn't arrive. "I tasted for a king once. Before your king. Before your kingdom, nearly." Her fingers found his wrist, gripped with surprising strength. "You refused the gift. In the end. When he offered it free." Her grip tightened. "Tell me why. Tell me slowly. I have time now—real time—for the first time in four centuries, and I want to spend some of it understanding the only person who ever beat him."

"I'll tell you on the road," Jude said. "It's a long walk to the capital, and you're not walking it alone."

* * *

They came down out of the hills three days later, four travelers on the mountain road—Jude, Wren with her lantern long since unneeded, Sable leaning on a staff Wren had cut for her, and behind them a village's worth of restored people who had taken up the journey out of sheer gravitational pull, because when the sky comes back, people want to walk toward each other.

On the last evening, camped within sight of the capital's walls, the tide brought Jude its final wave.

It was small, as the last waves are small. He was banking the fire when it broke over him: his mother, in the plague winter, in the room with the blanket nailed over the window, holding him—already infected, already burning—singing the wordless lullaby, and above her, on the ceiling, in the cracks of the plaster, the shape of the bargain assembling itself in her mind. He saw her face change as she understood what the mountain would take and what it would give. He saw her choose. Not desperation—choice, clear-eyed, priced honestly, the only clean trade he had ever witnessed besides Brannic's. *You can't afford the moon, love. Nobody can. That's how they keep it.*

But she had afforded it. She had paid the moon's price and gotten back a son, and the son had gotten back the sky, and somewhere in the arithmetic of it—Jude held the equation up to the firelight and examined it from every side, the accountant's habit that would never leave him—somewhere in it, the numbers finally balanced.

Not painlessly. Never painlessly. The restoration included the cost; that was its nature and its honor. He would carry the sea and the drowning-feeling of the tollgate together. The lullaby and the redacted margin. Her laugh and her lie. All of it, held, remembered, *kept*—because a self assembled from other people's payments was still, in the end, his, and he had chosen to remember everything, even what it cost.

Especially what it cost.

Jude fed the fire another branch and looked toward the capital, where tomorrow the guildhall waited with its shelves of heavy books, and the square waited beyond it, and the Queen waited beyond that, and a bonfire's worth of dry vellum waited to become smoke and freedom.

"Wren," he said quietly, across the flames. "Tomorrow, when we reach the city—find me the driest corner of the guild archive. The oldest shelf. There's a book there I need to read one last time before it burns." He stared into the fire. "Page one. The founding entry. Before I end it, I want to say her name aloud—the first guildmaster, the one who started all of this with a debt no one ever paid. Everyone she ruined deserves to hear it. And I think—" his jaw tightened— "I think she's owed a funeral four hundred years late."
