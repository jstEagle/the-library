## 37. Burning the Ledgers

The pyre was built in the guildyard, where the scales used to hang.

Jude had chosen the spot deliberately. For nine years he had walked beneath those brass balances every morning, and they had never once swung for him—never weighed his portion, never measured what the poisons took. The scales had been for the trades, for the weighing of laughter against fever-breaking, of years against coin, of names against survival. Now the scales hung empty, their chains cut, and in their place stood a scaffold of old shelving from the ledger room, stacked with thirty-one years of the Hollow Guild's accounts.

It had taken four days to carry them all out.

Four days of Wren hauling volumes down the narrow stair while Jude sat on the landing with a lantern, reading each spine one last time. Not because he needed to know what was in them—he knew, better than anyone alive—but because some part of him that was still a taster insisted on examining everything before it went into the fire. *Know your portion,* Master Corvel had said, a lifetime ago, over a dish of arsenic-laced honey. *Never swallow what you haven't looked at.* He had swallowed so much. He would look at all of it now.

The city came to watch.

He had expected anger, perhaps. Or worse, indifference—the particular indifference of people who had never been able to afford an impossible thing and felt no grief for the shop that sold them. But the crowd that gathered in the guildyard on the morning of the burning was quiet, and full, and strange. He saw faces he knew from the ledgers without knowing their names: the widow whose laughter had been sold to break her son's fever, older now, her mouth set in a line that might have been gratitude or its opposite. A man who had traded three years of his life for his daughter's dowry, standing with that daughter, both of them looking at the books as if expecting them to speak. An old woman clutching a receipt gone soft as cloth with handling.

They had come to see whether it was true. Whether the debts were really dead.

"Read one," someone called from the back. "Read us one, so we know."

Jude stood on the guild steps with the first volume in his hands. His body ached in the way it always ached now—a deep, structural ache, like a house settling on bad foundations. Nine years of small poisons, and then the tollgate's taking, and then whatever the Undercourt's collapse had done to a soul being reassembled in real time. The healers at the palace had used words like *cumulative damage* and *no quick remedy*, spoken in the gentle voices people use for the dying. He had thanked them and asked for a healer who worked slowly instead.

But first, this.

He opened the volume at random. The paper smelled of candle smoke and something sweeter underneath—bottled grief, perhaps, leaking its faint residue into everything it had touched. He found the entry by feel, because he had always known where things were in these books, the way a man knows where the stones are in his own floor.

"On the third of Frostmonth, in the year the river froze," he read, and his voice carried further than he expected, the guildyard holding sound the way a cupped hand holds water, "a woman named Maren Ostrey sold the memory of her wedding day to the Hollow Guild, in exchange for the mending of her daughter's broken back. The debt is hereby—" and here he stopped, because the next word had been *held*, held against future payment, held against interest, held against the possibility that the daughter's back might fail again someday and require more. That was how the ledgers worked. Nothing was ever fully given. Everything was a loan wearing generosity's clothes.

"The debt is hereby ended," Jude said. "Not held. Not forgiven—forgiven implies it was owed. Ended. It never existed. What she paid, she paid freely, and what she received was hers by right, and no account will ever be kept of it again."

A sound went through the crowd—not cheering, nothing so simple. More like the sigh a house makes when a burden is taken off its roof beam.

* * *

They burned the books through the afternoon and into the dark.

Wren kept the fire fed while Jude read entries aloud, one after another, freeing each debt in turn. It was slow work—there were thousands, tens of thousands, whole generations of small desperate bargains—and by the time the light failed, torches had been brought, and people had begun calling out names from the crowd, asking him to find theirs.

"Ostrey," someone would call. "My grandmother was Ostrey."

And Jude would find it, because he could still find anything in those books, his fingers moving through the pages with the certainty of long habit, and he would read it out and end it, and somewhere in the crowd a person would put their hand over their mouth or take another's arm or simply close their eyes.

Brannic's body lay in state in the guildhall behind them, washed and wrapped in smuggler's canvas, waiting for the morning. Jude had wanted to burn the ledgers before burying him, though he could not have said why, except that Brannic had spent twenty years searching for a way to buy back a name, and it seemed right that he should not have to hear the books still speaking while he waited to be put in the ground. Wren had sat with her father most of the day, one hand resting on the canvas over his chest, saying nothing. Once, passing the doorway, Jude had seen her lips moving—not prayer, he thought. Accounting. Telling her father the sums. What he'd paid, what he'd gotten, what it had cost the rest of them. She was her father's daughter even if she hadn't known it until a month ago.

Sable did not come to the burning.

She was mortal now—truly mortal, her four hundred years of borrowed time collapsing into ordinary human days—and she had chosen to spend the first of them above the sealed mountain, in the ruined village where the unremembered lived, planting things. Jude had seen her off two days ago at the north road. She had looked smaller without the court's authority folded around her like a cloak, and older, and somehow more herself than he had ever seen her, and when he asked what she intended to grow in soil that had forgotten how to be generous, she had said, "Something that doesn't keep accounts. If such a thing exists." Then she had walked north under a sky that was filling back up with stars, one by one, night after night, like a wound closing.

By full dark, the last volume was ash.

Jude stood at the edge of the cooling fire, and the crowd was thinning, and Wren came to stand beside him with soot on her face and her father's name written on a slip of paper in her pocket—the real one, the recovered one, which she had shown him only once and then folded away like something too bright to look at often.

"It's done," she said.

"No," Jude said. "It's *begun*. Done would mean there was nothing left to do." He watched a page-corner glow and curl. "There are other guilds. Other cities. Other courts beneath other mountains, maybe. The arithmetic I used down there—it works anywhere there's a ledger. Anyone can learn it."

"You're going to teach it."

"I'm going to teach honest accounting." He almost laughed; it hurt his ribs. "Grain in, grain out. Coin owed, coin paid. Debts that end. It sounds absurdly small after all this. But small is what I have left, and it turns out small was the point all along."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. In the firelight she had her father's stubborn jaw and none of his guilt, and Jude thought that was the best inheritance a person could receive.

"The palace healer comes tomorrow," she said. "The slow one. Halvard."

"I know."

"You're frightened."

It wasn't a question, so he didn't insult her with a denial. He was frightened. Not of pain—he had a professional relationship with pain going back nine years—but of what the healing would uncover. Poisons don't simply leave. They leave architecture. Channels worn through the body, tolerances built like scar tissue over organs that had never consented to any of it, and now that the tolerance was gone—spent at the tollgate of teeth, surrendered irrevocably—all of that architecture was exposed, raw, a house stripped of its walls in winter. Halvard had examined him once already and grown very quiet, the way Corvel used to go quiet before announcing a new dish.

"Come to the guildhall tonight," Jude said. "Before you go home. There's something I want to show you. And there's ale, if Ossen's cellar survived the looting."

"Did it?"

"Ossen fled with the keys and left the ale. Very like him."

* * *

The guildhall was cold and smelled of dust and old ink. Jude lit lamps along the great room, and the shadows of the empty shelves stretched out like ribs.

In the corner, where the master's desk had stood, he had laid out the things he was keeping. Not many. His mother's contract, torn, flattened under glass because he could not bring himself to throw away even the instrument of his own sale—her signature visible now, unredacted, *Ilse Vane*, in a hand that shook and steadied and shook again. One notebook from the starless months, the earliest, the handwriting deteriorating page by page as the memories left him, ending mid-sentence in a script he barely recognized as his own. And the founding entry—the true one, copied out in his own hand before the mirrors shattered, because some debts must be remembered precisely so they are never repeated: *the first guildmaster sold her own death to begin this book.*

"That's what I wanted to show you," he said, when Wren had read them all twice. "This is what we're rebuilding from. Not the guild. The truth about the guild. Every trade rested on a debt that was never repaid, and everyone who ever signed believed they were the exception, the one bargain that was fair. There are no fair bargains with power. There's only what you refuse."

Wren touched the glass over her mother's—over *Ilse Vane's*—signature, careful, the way you touch a scar. "You're giving me your mother's name?"

"I'm showing it to you. You'll need to know names, if you're going to keep honest books. Names are the whole difference. A ledger that forgets names becomes the Undercourt. A ledger that remembers them stays a ledger."

She nodded slowly, and he saw her filing it away, and thought: *she'll be better at this than I ever was.* Then he poured the ale, and they drank to Brannic, and to Sable somewhere on the north road, and to Ilse Vane, who had paid a terrible price in a plague winter and whose choice, at least, was finally nobody's property at all.

* * *

Halvard came at dawn, as promised.

She was a broad, grey-braided woman with the unhurried hands of someone who had spent forty years coaxing bodies back from the edges of things, and she began, as Jude had known she would, not with medicines but with questions. Where did it hurt. When did it hurt worst. What could he no longer taste—because yes, she knew what he was, everyone knew now, the taster who had burned the ledgers, the story had run ahead of him through the capital like spilled wine through linen.

"Everything tastes like copper," he admitted. "Or nothing. Some days I can't tell which."

"That's the tolerance dying. It'll feel like loss. It isn't. It's feeling returning to ground that was salted." She unpacked her kit: tinctures, poultices, a bundle of needles, a small brass scale—*a scale*, and he flinched, and she noticed, and set it aside without comment. "I won't lie to you, taster. This is a year's work, maybe two. Maybe it never finishes. Your body kept you alive by spending itself, and now the bill has come due, and unlike the ones you burned, this one's real."

"I know about real bills."

"I expect you do." She took his wrist, turned his palm up, pressed points along it with her thumb, watching his face with clinical attention. "We start today. Small doses. Bitter tonics your body will fight, because fighting is what it knows. You'll be sick. You'll be angry. Some mornings you'll wish you'd stayed poisoned, because poison at least let you work." Her grip firmed. "Can you be ordinary for a year? Can you wake up, drink something foul, ache, rest, and do it again? No missions. No courts. No kings asking favors?"

Jude looked out the window of his rented room—at the ordinary street below, a cart driver arguing about a wheel, a child chasing a dog, the baker's boy delivering bread. The sun was coming up gold and clean over rooftops that had nearly fallen, over a city that remembered itself again. Somewhere north, Sable was putting seeds into hard ground. Somewhere in the guildhall, Wren was probably already copying out the founding entry in her own careful hand.

Ordinary. A year of it. Two. A life, maybe, of drinking bitter tonics and learning to taste bread again.

"Yes," Jude Vane said, and meant it, and for the first time in nine years the word cost him nothing at all. "I can be ordinary."

Halvard smiled, and reached for her first vial, and outside the window the baker's boy whistled past, and the day—plain, unpriced, entirely his—began.
