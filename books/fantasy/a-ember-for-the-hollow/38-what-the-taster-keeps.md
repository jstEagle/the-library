## 38. What the Taster Keeps

The counting house on Candlemaker's Row had been a chandler's shop before the war with the sky, and it still smelled faintly of tallow and honeyed smoke, as if the walls had learned sweetness and could not unlearn it. Jude had chosen it for that reason. He had spent nine years in rooms that smelled of nothing at all—the tasting chamber scrubbed of every scent so that no perfume might disguise a poison—and he wanted, for the rest of his life, to work somewhere that smelled like something.

He had hung no sign. Word had done the work instead, the way water finds the low places. A grain merchant first, then a wool factor, then a widow with three barrels of salt herring and a suspicion her factor was skimming her blind. They came because they had heard of the man who had burned the ledgers of the Hollow Guild in the palace square, who had fed page after page of impossible debts into a fire while the whole city watched and the stars came back overhead one by one like lamps being lit down a long corridor.

They came expecting a reformer. What they got was an accountant.

"Again," Jude said.

Wren sat across the scarred oak table from him, sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink to the second knuckle, glaring at the column of figures as though it had insulted her mother. In a way, he supposed, it had. Numbers had been the language of the thing that ate twenty years of her father.

"The barley," she said. "The barley doesn't balance."

"Why not?"

"Because—" She stopped. Breathed. He watched her do what he had taught her to do in the first week: put the fury down beside the ledger like a tool she was allowed to pick up later. "Because Harl Vetch entered the same delivery twice. Once on the ninth and once on the eleventh, but the eleventh entry is in different ink, and the amounts differ by four sacks."

"And?"

"And either he made an honest mistake twice, which is unlikely, or he is stealing from the miller in increments small enough that no one would bother to hang him for them." She looked up. Her eyes were Brannic's eyes—dark, quick, always a half-second ahead of whatever face she wore over them. "Four sacks of barley. It's nothing."

"It's never nothing." Jude turned the ledger toward himself and read the entries again, slowly, letting her watch him read. "That's the first lesson and the last lesson and all the lessons between. The Hollow Guild didn't begin with souls. It began with four sacks of grain. Someone, somewhere, decided that a small theft from someone who couldn't count was harmless, and then discovered that harmlessness compounds." He tapped the margin. "Every soul in the Undercourt was once a rounding error."

Wren wrote that down. She wrote everything down now—had ever since the night she'd found his notebooks in the abandoned guildhouse, forty-one of them, filled with a stranger's careful hand cataloguing his own disappearance. She told him once, quietly, over tea he had tasted out of habit before drinking, that reading those notebooks had been like standing at a grave and hearing the dead man describe the weather. He hadn't known what to say to that. He still didn't. Grief for a self you'd nearly lost was a strange country, and she had walked partway into it without being asked.

"Now the second question," he said. "The important one. What do we do about Harl Vetch?"

"Report him?"

"To whom? The magistrates' rolls are still half ash. Half the clerks are dead or gone." He leaned back. The afternoon light came through the east window in a long amber bar and lay across the table between them like something they were dividing. "Think. What did the old system do with a thief?"

"Owned him. Found the debt he owed and made the debt itself the punishment." She said it flatly, without flinching, and he felt again the particular pride he took in her—that she could speak the old language without believing in its gods anymore. "And then the debt grew interest, and the interest grew teeth, and twenty years later his daughter was paying it off without knowing it existed."

"So?"

"So we knock on his door." She almost smiled. "We show him the arithmetic. We let him see exactly how far he'd already fallen, and how much further he'd have gone, and then we give him a choice: repay the miller in labor, openly, or be named at the market cross. No interest. No teeth. Just the weight of the thing he actually did, which is heavy enough."

Jude nodded and looked out the window at the street below, where a cartload of autumn apples was passing and two children were running alongside it hoping for a bruised one to fall. Ordinary theft. Ordinary consequence. It seemed so small a thing to have fought a mountain for, and yet he knew—it was carved into him now deeper than any memory—that the mountain had only ever been this, scaled up past all mercy.

"That's the trade I keep," he said. "Not souls. Not years. Just this: making the numbers tell the truth, and then making the truth cost what it actually costs. Nothing more. Can you live inside something that small, after everything you've seen?"

Wren considered it with the seriousness she brought to everything, pen still moving, and then she said, "It's not small. It's just honest about its size. There's a difference." And she went back to the barley, and Jude went back to the herring widow's columns, and for a long while the only sounds in the counting house were pens, and the tick of the clock Corvel had left him in his will—a cold man's one gesture of warmth, kept wound out of something Jude refused to examine too closely—and beneath it all, faint and constant, the sound of the city rebuilding itself: hammering from the roofline across the street, a cart's complaint, someone laughing three doors down at something that was probably not funny enough to explain the laughter, except that people laughed easily these days, extravagantly, the way the starving eat.

* * *

Sable's garden lay above the sealed mountain, on the shelf of green terraces where the Undercourt's tollgate had once demanded teeth and gotten them.

She had gone up in the spring, alone, carrying seeds in her pockets and a spade over her shoulder like a soldier's pike, and when the villagers of the nearest town—Harrowmere, rebuilt now, its well no longer whispering names—had asked her what she intended to grow on that bare stone, she had said, "Everything that was taken. It has to go somewhere."

Jude visited twice a year, when the accounting season eased. He climbed the pilgrim road on foot because the mountain had earned his legs' honesty, and each time the garden had grown larger, wilder, more deliberate in its disorder.

This visit was the autumn one. Frost had touched the high country early; the squash vines had gone to gold lace, and the bean poles stood empty as gallows, and Sable was on her knees in the turnip bed with dirt to her elbows and silver hair escaping its braid, looking—for the first time since he'd known her, in all her faces—old.

Not aged. Old. There was a difference, and the difference was peace.

"You're late," she said, without turning around. Four hundred years of listening for footsteps did not wear off in eighteen months.

"The herring widow argues."

"They always argue. That's how you know the numbers matter to them." She sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on her apron, leaving brown streaks like a map of somewhere better. "Sit. The bench by the wall. Mind the frost-bitten basil; it's sulking."

The bench had been built from the tollgate itself. She had told him this the first time he'd come, with a defiance that dared him to object: the gate that had eaten his immunity, broken apart plank by plank and iron fitting by iron fitting, and remade into a place to sit facing the valley. The wood was black and dense as bog oak and would outlast everyone alive. Jude sat on the remains of the thing that had taken his tolerances and found, as always, that it made a very good bench.

Below them the valley opened in long folds of harvested field and woodsmoke, the town a scatter of slate roofs, the road a pale thread stitching it all together. Above them, the sky did what the sky did now, which was simply exist—blue going violet at the edges, the first stars pricking through like needles through cloth.

"How many did you plant this year?" he asked.

"Two hundred and eleven varieties. I've stopped keeping exact count." She smiled at his expression. "Yes. Me. Sable. Telling an accountant I've stopped counting. You may faint if you need to."

"What are they?"

"Names, mostly. Or things that used to be names." She pulled her shawl closer against the coming dark. "When the court dissolved, everything it held had to go somewhere. The stars went home. The memories went home. But there were things in the vaults that had no owners left to return to—names of people whose whole lines ended generations ago, griefs bottled for families extinct, a hundred years of borrowed laughter whose lenders died owing it still. Unclaimed property." She looked out at the valley. "So I claimed it. All of it. And then I planted it."

Jude had seen the garden do what it did. He had watched, on his spring visit, a row of ordinary cabbages stand in the evening light and murmur—not words, not quite, but the shape of words, the rhythm of a name being remembered by no one, over and over, gentle as rain. The bees worked those rows and came away heavy with something sweeter than honey. The villagers called it the Speaking Garden and left offerings of bread and buttons at its gate, and Sable let them, and ate the bread, and gave the buttons to the crows, who collected them in the hollow of the wall and glittered there like a dragon's hoard assembled by idiots.

"You look tired," she said. "Good tired or bad tired?"

"I don't know anymore. Is that bad?"

"It's human. Congratulations. Took you long enough." She studied him with those eyes that had been a taster's eyes once too, four centuries ago, before her collection failed and the King's offer caught her falling. "Your hands shake in the mornings. Don't deny it; I watched you hold your cup with both hands like a child. The poisons are still working their way out. Some of them will take decades. Some will never leave."

"I know."

"Do you regret it? The gate. Paying with the tolerance. You built that immunity dish by dish, year by year, a boy swallowing ruin so he could survive, and you handed it to a door made of teeth for the price of walking through." Her voice was light, but her eyes weren't. She had paid prices too. She knew the arithmetic of them by heart.

Jude considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to Sable had never once worked anyway.

"No," he said. "It kept me alive when staying alive was the only thing I had. But it was also the last piece of the old system I was still carrying—the idea that I had to make myself proof against the world, poison by poison, that survival was a discipline and tenderness was a liability." He flexed his fingers, watching the fine tremor in them without alarm. "Corvel taught me that. The guild taught me that. The mountain taught it best of all. And the moment I gave it up, I discovered I could still walk through the world. I just had to trust it a little. Trust the healer's slow care. Trust Wren checking my cups. Trust that not everything unknown in a dish is a knife."

Sable was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and pressed her dirt-streaked hand briefly over his shaking one, and the touch smelled of turnip greens and frost and, faintly, impossibly, of starlight—the residue of four hundred years sleeping under a ceiling of stolen sky.

"The turnips are good this year," she said, resuming practicality like a coat. "Take some down to the capital. And there's a jar of last summer's honey with the first batch of speaking-bees' comb in it. Eat it on midwinter morning. It tastes like someone's name. A good name. I checked."

* * *

Winter came down on the capital like a lid settling onto a pot, and inside the pot the city simmered with the ordinary business of being alive.

Jude's days had a shape now, and he had learned—slowly, clumsily, the way a man learns a language late in life—to love the shape. Mornings at the counting house with Wren, arguing over barley and herring and the wool factor who was either a genius or a criminal and possibly both. Afternoons walking, because his body needed the movement now that it could no longer burn through what harmed it; he walked the river road and the market streets and, once a week, the long climb to the palace, where the Queen received him not as a servant but as a guest, and the kitchen sent up dishes he tasted out of courtesy and habit and found, every single time, clean.

Evenings he wrote. Not ledgers. Letters, mostly—to the families of the sold, telling them what he'd found in the recovered records, delivering the terrible gifts of knowing. And sometimes, when the letters were done, he wrote in a private book he kept locked in the third drawer, and what he wrote there was memory: the sea at Greywater Bay where he'd been born, the color of it, the sound, the way the gulls argued over fish heads; his mother's face, reconstructed now from the restored sky and still slightly wrong around the mouth, because some gaps even miracles don't fill perfectly; the lullaby, all six verses, which he sang under his breath on the stairs and which Wren had learned without asking and hummed sometimes when she thought he wasn't listening, so that the song now belonged to both of them, which was, he suspected, how songs had always survived.

On the evening the letter came, snow was falling in fat, unhurried flakes, and Wren had gone home to her own fire, and Jude was alone in the counting house with a candle and a cup of cider he had tasted out of nine years of reflex and then drunk out of one year of trust.

The letter bore the royal seal. He broke it expecting a summons, a formality, perhaps a request to audit the reconstruction accounts.

It was an invitation.

*His Majesty and Her Majesty request the pleasure of Jude Vane's company at the Night of Returning Stars, one year to the day since the sky was mended,* it read, in a clerk's beautiful hand. *There will be a feast. Your presence at the tasting is neither required nor requested. Come as a guest. Eat anything. Everything.*

Jude read it three times. Then he set it down on the table, next to the herring widow's balanced columns and Wren's barley arithmetic and the little brass clock ticking its cold man's heartbeat, and he looked at it for a long time.

Come as a guest. Eat anything.

Nine years ago—he could remember all nine now, every day of them, hoarded like coins—he had stood in the tasting chamber at dawn and swallowed death in measured doses so that a woman he'd never spoken to could eat breakfast without fear. He had been furniture. He had been a hinge. He had been a number in a ledger older than the kingdom, signed for before he drew breath, and every meal he had ever made safe had been, underneath, a payment toward a debt he didn't know he owed.

And now they wanted him at the table. Not guarding it. Sitting at it.

His hands, he noticed, were shaking—but it wasn't the poisons this time, or not only. It was the other thing, the thing he had chosen to keep along with everything else: the full weight of remembering what it had cost, all of it, the sea and the lullaby and the gate of teeth and Brannic's body on his shoulder all the long climb into dawn, and Sable's contract burning like a key, and his mother's name finally unredacted at last—Maren Vane, born Maren Coldharbour, who had walked down into the dark pregnant and desperate and had bought her son's life with the only coin she had.

He picked up the invitation again. Beneath the formal text, in different ink—a queen's own hand, quick and slanted—someone had added a line:

*There is one more thing we must discuss. About your mother's contract. We found the original copy.*

Jude stood very still in the warm room while the snow came down past the window, soft as ash, soft as feathers, soft as the first star of evening finding its place in the mended sky.

He reached for his coat.
