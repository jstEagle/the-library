## 39. A Ember for the Hollow

The invitation came on paper the color of cream, sealed in wax the color of blood, and Jude Vane read it three times before he understood that it was not a summons.

*Her Majesty requests the pleasure of your company at the anniversary feast.*

Requests. The word sat strangely on the page, like a coin from a country that no longer existed. For nine years he had entered the palace by the servants' stair, through the door that smelled of tallow and onion, and no one had ever requested his presence anywhere. He had been furniture that swallowed. A hinge. A portion of the machinery that kept the Queen breathing, tested daily, replaced never.

Now his name was written at the top of the card in a clerk's careful hand — *Master Jude Vane* — and beneath it, smaller, almost shy: *as a guest.*

He set the card on the table beside the window, where the morning light could get at it, and looked at it for a long time. Outside, the city was hauling itself toward autumn. Smoke rose straight from a hundred chimneys because there was no wind, and above the smoke the sky went up and up and up, blue fading to a depth that hurt, and scattered through all of it — he counted without meaning to, an old habit, the taster's arithmetic — forty-one stars, pale as salt, patient as debt.

They had come back a year ago tonight.

He still woke some mornings reaching for the dark, the way a man who has carried a heavy load for years wakes reaching for a weight that is no longer on his shoulders, and finds his own arm strange and light.

* * *

He dressed carefully, which was itself a novelty. For nine years he had dressed for function: plain wool, nothing that caught the eye, nothing that said *I matter* to any assassin watching the high table. Today he wore the grey coat Wren had bullied him into buying, and the small silver pin she had made him from a melted-down guild seal — the hollow circle of the Hollow Guild, reforged into something closed, complete, a ring instead of a wound.

Wren arrived at his door at midday to walk him to the palace, because she did not trust him to arrive on time, and because — though neither of them said it — anniversaries were easier in company.

She was twenty now, quick-eyed, ink-stained to the second knuckle, with her father's stubborn jaw and none of his silence. She carried her ledger under one arm the way other people carried children: close, protective, constantly adjusted.

"You're doing the thing," she said, when he opened the door.

"What thing?"

"The counting thing. You're standing in your doorway counting stars at noon, which is impossible, so you must be counting something else." She peered past him into the flat. "How many spoons do you own?"

"Eleven."

"And you've checked them how many times today?"

Jude smiled and reached for his coat. "Twice."

The old habits had not left him. He suspected they never would. But they had changed their employment. Once he had counted to stay ahead of death; now he counted the way a man counts his blessings who does not quite believe in them — touching each one, confirming it is real, putting it back exactly where he found it.

They walked down through the city together, and the city was loud, and Jude let it be loud around him.

A year ago he would have flinched from crowds. A year ago every crowd was a wall of possible poisons, every market-stall a chemistry, every stranger's hands a question. Now he walked through the fish-market with its reek of brine and ice, past the bread ovens exhaling heat, past the fountain where children were shrieking and wetting each other with cupped handfuls, and he simply — breathed it. All of it. The unpoisoned ordinary world, which he had spent nine years tasting in terror and one year tasting in peace.

"Grain ledgers are done for the season," Wren reported, because she always reported, because honest accounting was a religion to her now and he was its only priest. "South granary's full. The miller tried to shave the weights again and I wrote it in the margin where everyone could see, and he turned red as a beet and paid the difference double."

"Good."

"He called me a name I won't repeat."

"Also good. That means it's working."

She laughed, and the sound of it did what it always did — snagged briefly on something in his chest, the ghost of another laugh, lower and older, a woman humming in a plague-winter kitchen. His mother's laugh. He had it back now, along with everything else. The sea. The lullaby. Her face, which was his face aged and softened, her hands, her voice saying *eat, love, eat* over a bowl of thin barley soup while outside the dead were carted and the stars were going out one winter at a time.

He remembered everything now. That had been the choice, in the collapsing dark beneath the mountain, with the court dissolving around him like sugar in water: *all of it, even the cost.* And the cost had come home to roost, as costs do, and he had paid it in sleepless nights and sudden weeping in the grain-ledger office and one terrible afternoon when he had remembered the exact weight of his mother's hand on his hair and had to sit down on the floor among the sacks until it passed.

But it passed. That was the thing no ledger had ever taught him. Grief, honestly accounted, balanced. Not erased — balanced. Carried forward. Part of the sum.

"You're doing the other thing now," Wren said quietly, watching his face.

"I know." He cleared his throat. "Tell me about the harvest festival instead. Distract me."

So she talked, and he listened, and the palace grew ahead of them out of the autumn haze, its white stone gilded by the low sun, and above its highest tower the sky stood open and star-scattered and free.

* * *

They admitted him through the front doors.

That was the detail that undid him, more than the coat or the title or the card. The front doors of the palace were thirty feet of carved oak and he had never once seen them from this side. Nine years of the servants' stair, and now a footman in livery bowed — *bowed* — and said, "Master Vane. Her Majesty is delighted," and the great hall opened before him like a held breath released.

It was dressed for the anniversary. Banners in the royal colors, yes, but also — and this was new, this was the Queen's doing, he learned later, against considerable counsel — lanterns. Hundreds of them, paper and glass, hung from the vaulting on fine chains, and each one painted with a single star. The whole ceiling of the great hall was a sky of small handmade lights, and beneath it the court moved in its glittering shoals, and every face that turned toward Jude Vane turned with something he had never once been given in this building.

Respect. Uncomplicated, unafraid, freely given respect.

He felt his ears go hot. Old habit again: the invisible man suddenly visible. But this time the visibility did not feel like vertigo. It felt like being set down after a long carry.

"Master Vane."

The Queen.

She came through the crowd and the crowd parted for her the way water parts, and she stopped before him, and she was older than she had been a year ago — the poison that killed no taster had nearly killed her anyway, and the healer's slow care had taken its toll — but her eyes were the same: grey, level, and tired in the way of someone who has decided to keep carrying things regardless.

"Your Majesty." He began to bow. Her hand closed on his forearm before he got halfway.

"No." She said it quietly, but the hall heard it anyway; the nearest conversations faltered. "Not tonight. Tonight you are the man who gave the sky back. The bow can wait for the rest of my life, and it will be owed the whole time."

"Your Majesty—"

"Jude." She used his name like a key turning. "I have eaten safely for nine years because of you, and I never once thanked you, because thanking the taster means admitting there is something to taste for. I am done with that arithmetic. Sit at my table. Eat my food. Let me watch you enjoy it. That is the only repayment I ask, and I know—" her mouth curved, dry as her humor had always been, "—that you of all people will appreciate a debt honestly discharged."

He did not trust his voice, so he nodded.

The feast was long and loud and lit by its little painted stars. Jude sat at the high table, to the Queen's right, between Wren and an old general who told interminable stories about border campaigns and was, Jude realized with a start somewhere in the third story, genuinely funny. The food came course upon course, and he tasted each dish the old way — automatically, professionally, the tongue cataloguing, the mind running its silent inventory of bitter notes and wrong sweetnesses —

and found, in every single dish, nothing.

Nothing but saffron and honey and slow-cooked lamb and wine dark as garnet and bread still warm enough to steam. Nothing but flavor, whole and innocent, food that was only food. He ate like a man breaking a fast nine years long, and the Queen watched him do it, and twice he caught her smiling into her cup, and once she raised that cup slightly toward him across the candlelight, and he raised his back.

Near midnight she leaned toward him, under the noise, and asked the question she had clearly been holding all evening.

"Do you miss it? The tasting?"

Jude considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because he had promised himself, coming out of the mountain, that he would never again answer anything dishonestly — not the court, not the guild, not himself.

"I miss being necessary," he said. "I don't miss being afraid. They felt the same, for a long time. That was the trick of it. That was how they kept us all."

"And now?"

"Now I'm necessary for grain ledgers." He glanced down the table at Wren, who was demolishing a honey-cake with the focus of a siege engine and arguing points of municipal accounting with the treasurer, who was losing. "It turns out a kingdom runs on the same principle as a soul. Everything should be counted, and nothing should be owned."

The Queen looked at him for a moment longer. Then she laughed — really laughed, not the court version — and said, "They should put that over the treasury door," and the moment broke gently, like a wave, and the feast rolled on.

* * *

He slipped out near the end, as he had planned to all along. There was one more stop, and it was not a stop the palace could make for him.

The night air was cold and clean and smelled of woodsmoke and fallen leaves. The streets had emptied toward the feast, toward the taverns, toward the hilltops where families had spread blankets to watch the sky — because that was the custom now, one year old and already feeling ancient: on the anniversary of the night the stars returned, you went outside and looked up. Whole cities, faces tilted skyward, keeping the appointment.

Jude walked against the current, out through the western gate, up the winding road to the churchyard on the hill where the grass grew long and the wind came off the fields with the smell of tomorrow's rain.

His mother's grave lay under a rowan tree gone scarlet with the season.

The stone was new — Wren had arranged it, in the spring, with a stonemason who owed her a favor and was paid honestly besides. And cut deep into the granite, where for twenty-six years the records had said only *VANE, J., MOTHER OF — ENTRY WITHHELD*, where the guild's old books had carried a redaction like a bruise, where even his own memory had once had a gap shaped like a transaction:

**MAREN VANE**
*Who paid everything, and owed nothing.*
*Beloved. Remembered.*

Her name. Her whole name, in the open air, under the rowan, under the stars.

Jude knelt in the long grass, joints protesting — the immunity-ruined body, the healer's slow care, all of it still true, all of it still his — and pressed his palm flat against the stone the way you press a hand against a door you have finally been allowed to open.

"Maren," he said aloud. Just to hear it. Just to say it in the open where the sky could hear, where no ledger owned it, where no contract had ever touched it. "Maren Vane. My mother."

The wind moved through the rowan and the leaves rattled like small applause, and he stayed there a long time, telling her things. About the feast. About Wren, who kept the grain accounts and had made a kingdom's merchants afraid of margins. About Brannic, whose body he had carried up out of the dark and buried here beside her, facing east, because Wren said he'd earned a view of the sunrise. About Sable, mortal now, four hundred years old and learning to garden above the sealed mountain, who had written him one letter in the spring — *the beans came up,* it said, and nothing else, and he had read it a dozen times and understood every word.

About the sea. He told her about the sea, which he had gotten back a year ago tonight, which he had visited in the summer and stood in up to his knees, weeping like a child while strangers politely looked away, the cold water real around his ankles, the horizon exactly as far away as memory insisted.

"I remember all of it," he told her. "Even the cost. You'd have hated that, I think. You paid to give me a life, not a ledger. But it turns out they're the same thing, Mother. It turns out a life is just a ledger you write yourself, if you're lucky, and I'm lucky. I'm so lucky it embarrasses me."

Above him, unhurried, the stars burned. Forty-one of them over the hill, and beyond the hill, he knew, hundreds more, thousands, all the stolen lights of two centuries poured back into the dark and kindled — including, though no astronomer had yet identified it and he had never told anyone, one small blue-white star low on the southern horizon that he had privately named, in the silence of his own heart, *Maren.*

He stood at last, stiffly, and brushed the grass from his knees.

"Happy anniversary," he said to the stone. Then, because peace is also a discipline, and survival is finally worth its price, and the road home was long and the night was beautiful: "I'll see you next year."

He walked down the hill with the wind at his back, and below him the capital lay spread out in its rivers of lamplight, and above it the sky stood full — full the way a ledger stands full when every line is honest, every debt discharged, every entry signed in a living hand.

At the bottom of the hill, waiting by the gate with two lanterns and an impatient expression and a bundle of harvest-festival honey-cakes she had absolutely stolen from the high table, stood Wren.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"I was paying a visit."

"I know." Her voice gentled. She lifted her lantern, and the light caught the silver pin at his collar, the closed circle, the ring where the wound had been. "Come on, Master Vane. The accounts are balanced, the stars are out, and tomorrow we start the winter ledgers."

Jude Vane took the lantern she offered him, and together they walked home under the whole of the restored sky — and far to the south, low over the hills, the small blue-white star he had named for his mother burned steady and bright, keeping its own faithful count of the years to come.
