## 40. Chapter 40

The year turned the way a door turns when no one is leaning on it: slowly, and then all at once.

Jude felt it first in his hands. They had been ruined by degrees—the tollgate of teeth had taken his immunity the way a tide takes sand from underfoot, one grain at a time until the whole beach was gone—and now, on cold mornings, they ached in a way that was almost musical. A different ache for every poison he had ever swallowed. Foxglove sang low in his left wrist. Hemlock kept a thin whistle in the bones of his right hand. The healer, Maren, said this was common among those who surrendered their tolerances all at once. She said it the way she said most things, which was to say while not looking at him, because looking at him meant seeing what he had paid, and Maren was a woman who preferred her griefs itemized.

He did not mind the aching. That was the strange arithmetic of it, the last sum of a life spent balancing other people's columns: pain was proof of memory, and memory was proof that he had chosen to keep it. On the night the stars came back, he could have asked for a curated self, a tidy version of Jude Vane with the sharp edges sanded off. He had refused. He remembered everything now—the sea, the lullaby, the smell of his mother's ledger ink, the exact weight of Sable's body as it faded against his shoulder on the long climb out of the dark. All of it, including the cost. Especially the cost.

He was thinking about costs on the morning of the anniversary, because the Queen had sent for him.

Not as taster. Those days were done; Wren had the appointment now, having talked her way into it with the same stubbornness she'd used to talk her way into everything else—into the guild's ruins, into his notebooks, into his life. No, the summons said *guest*, in the Queen's own looping hand, and beneath it, in smaller script: *There is something I owe you an answer to.*

Jude dressed carefully. He still owned very little—a habit of nine years of expecting to be taken—but what he owned was good: a coat of undyed wool, boots Brannic's daughter had insisted on buying him, and around his neck, on a cord, a small square of paper gone soft as cloth from handling. His mother's name, unredacted. *Ilse Vane, who paid.* He touched it once, the way another man might touch a saint's medal, and went out into the morning.

The city had learned to be loud again. That was the thing no chronicle would record, he thought, crossing the market square: not the stars returning, but the noise returning underneath them. For a year after the restoration, the capital had moved like a convalescent, quiet, careful, flinching at dark windows. Now the fishwives shouted. Children ran underfoot with kites shaped like constellations. A man was selling roasted chestnuts from a cart whose awning had been painted with the old wards—not working ones, nobody's were working anymore, and nobody needed them to be—just painted, decorative, the way people paint flowers on things that will never bloom.

"Master Vane." The chestnut seller nodded to him without fear. A year ago, everyone in this square had known what he was: the man who'd burned the ledgers, the man who'd unpriced them. Some had wept at their debts' dissolution. Some had raged—they'd *paid*, hadn't they, fairly, in full, and now where was the value of what they'd bought? Grief does strange bookkeeping. But the rage had cooled into something more ordinary, and ordinary was what Jude had wanted all along. He bought six chestnuts because refusing kindness had nearly ended the world once, and he was practicing receiving it.

The palace gates opened for him without ceremony. That, too, was new.

* * *

The Queen received him in the small morning room, not the great hall—the room with the south-facing windows and the plain bread and salt on the table, which he understood, correctly, as a message. You do not set poison before a guest. You do not even set the possibility of it.

She looked older than she had a year ago. The plague that had swept the capital in the starless months had taken her sister, and grief had carved her down to something leaner and more honest. She wore no crown. She never wore it indoors anymore; Jude suspected it reminded her of what the crown had cost, two centuries of sold souls funding two centuries of wards, and that she was trying to earn the right to wear it again rather than simply inherit it.

"Taster," she said, and then caught herself, and smiled ruefully. "No. Forgive me. Old habits. *Jude.*"

"Your Majesty."

"I've told you. When there is no crown between us, there is no Majesty either." She gestured him to a chair across the table. Between them sat the bread, the salt, and a single covered dish of silver, small enough to hold a fig.

His heart performed an old, practiced maneuver—nine years of discipline rising before thought, the taster's calm settling over him like a second skin—and then he let it go, deliberately, the way he had taught himself to let go of the ledgers. Whatever was under that cover, he was not here to die for it.

"You said there was something you owed me an answer to," he said.

"There is." She looked at the dish. "But first I want to tell you what I have learned this year, because I think you are perhaps the only person in my kingdom who will understand it, and because I have been a coward about saying it aloud for eleven months."

Jude waited. Waiting was the oldest skill he had.

"My family built the wards," the Queen said. "You know this now. Everyone knows it, though we dressed it up in the histories as *sacrifice* and *protection*. Two hundred years of buying safety with other people's souls, and calling it governance." Her hands were flat on the table, steady, but he saw the effort of the steadiness. "When the truth came out, my advisors counseled me to deny it. Then, when denial failed, to apologize broadly and change nothing. Then, when that failed, to find someone to blame—dead chancellors, dead guildmasters, anyone whose grave couldn't argue." She looked up. "I want you to know that I considered every one of those counsels. And I want you to know why I didn't take them."

"Why?"

"Because of you." She said it plainly, without ornament. "Because a food-taster walked into a court older than my dynasty, armed with nothing but arithmetic and a refusal, and ended it. Because if a man with no title and no army could unmake two hundred years of inherited evil, then a queen has no excuse whatsoever." Something moved through her face—grief, or its cousin. "My sister used to say the crown was a debt we could never repay. She was wrong. It's a debt we can only repay by never borrowing that way again. So. The wards stay down. The tithe is abolished by law, with my seal, and the law names your mother. Ilse Vane. By name. In the first line."

Jude's throat tightened. He reached for the practiced calm and found, to his surprise, that he didn't need it. He let his eyes sting in front of the Queen of the realm, and she pretended, graciously, to study the salt.

"She paid to keep me alive through the plague winter," he said, when he could speak. "She thought she was paying a fever-cure. She didn't know what she was really signing."

"No. And the law doesn't care. It says her name because names were the currency of the crime, so names are how it gets answered." The Queen straightened. "Now. The answer I owed you." She lifted the silver cover from the dish.

Inside was a pear. A single winter pear, gold-blushed, perfect, sitting in a shallow pool of its own syrup.

"The night the stars went out," the Queen said, "a dish arrived at my table that no kitchen admitted making. You tasted it. You told me it was memory-wine, guild stock, and that someone in my household was trading with the Hollow Guild behind my back. We never found who sent it. The investigation died when everything else died that year." She slid the dish toward him. "I found the sender eight months ago. It was my uncle. He was trying to buy my grief for the Undercourt before the collapse—he thought the court beneath the mountain would win, and he wanted to be owed something when it did. He is in exile now. That part isn't the answer."

"And the answer?"

"The answer is that I have had this pear grown, in my own orchards, by my own gardeners, for one year, and I am going to ask you to taste it. Not because I doubt it. Because *you* should have this. One dish, served to you, that you eat as a free man, that no one made you swallow, that carries nothing in it but sugar and sun." Her voice frayed, just slightly, on the last word. "Nine years, Jude. Nine years of dying by spoonfuls so that I could live. I signed those orders. I benefited from them every single day. I cannot give you back nine years. But I can give you breakfast."

Jude looked at the pear for a long time.

He thought of Master Corvel's cold voice: *tenderness is a liability, boy; chew and swallow and report.* He thought of the tollgate, and the teeth, and the taste of his own tolerance leaving him like a tide. He thought of Sable, four hundred years a collector, planting lettuces above the sealed mountain with dirt-blackened hands and an expression of permanent astonishment, as though the soil might yet present her with an invoice. He thought of Wren, who had found her father's name in the restored sky's light and wept for a man who had traded everything and gotten exactly what he wanted.

Then he picked up the pear with his scarred, aching, hemlock-whistling hand, and ate it.

It was sweet. It was only sweet. It was the best thing he had ever tasted, and he had tasted everything.

"Well?" the Queen asked, when he had finished, and there was real anxiety under the formality, the anxiety of a woman who had rehearsed this for a year.

"It needs nothing," Jude said. "No correction. No report." He smiled, and felt his face make the expression honestly, which was still novel enough to be startling. "Though if you want my professional opinion, Your Majesty—the orchard keeper deserves a raise."

She laughed, wet-eyed, and for a moment they were just two survivors of the same terrible year, sitting in morning light over an empty dish.

* * *

He walked home through the city as the afternoon went gold.

Home was a narrow house near the old guildhall, three rooms and a leaky roof, chosen because it was walking distance from the school of accounting where he taught twice a week and from Wren's lodgings and from the harbor, because he liked, now, to be able to see the sea whenever he wished—the sea he had lost and been given back, gray and enormous and entirely unpaid for.

Wren was waiting on his step, which was not unusual. What was unusual was her face. Wren had many faces—stubborn, wry, ferociously competent—but the one she wore now was new to him, and it took him a moment to name it. Wonder, held very carefully, the way you hold something that might still fly away.

"You're late," she said. "I've been here an hour."

"The Queen fed me pears. Kingship moves slowly." He unlocked the door. "What is it? You look like you've found an error in the universe's books."

"Worse." She followed him inside and put a package on his table. Brown paper, tied with string, travel-worn, postmarked from a village he didn't recognize in the hill country east of the mountain. "This came for you by carrier. It's been forwarded three times—it started addressed to the Hollow Guild."

"Nothing's been addressed to the Hollow Guild in a year. I burned it."

"I know what you burned, Jude. I helped carry the ashes." She untied the string with fingers that were not quite steady. "Open it."

Inside the paper was a ledger.

Small, hand-bound, the cover stitched from grey leather gone soft with age. Jude knew the binding style instantly—he had catalogued a thousand such books in the guild's vaults—but this one was unlike any of them, because the pages, when he opened it, were not accounts.

They were letters. Or the beginnings of them. Dozens of entries in dozens of hands, some careful, some scrawled, some in ink, some in charcoal, one pressed into the page hard enough to tear it. And every entry began the same way:

*To the one who unpriced us.*

Jude sat down slowly.

"They're coming from everywhere," Wren said quietly. "Villages, cities, other kingdoms—the story traveled farther than we thought. People who were sold. People whose families were sold. People who traded years, names, griefs, and got them back when the court dissolved and the debts dissolved with them." She tapped the page, where a child's uneven letters spelled out *thank you for my grandmother's laugh, we have it back now, she uses it mostly at weddings.* "They can't send them to a guild that doesn't exist. So they're sending them to you. This is the fourth parcel. There's a merchant in the capital collecting them—I don't know how they're finding each other, but they are. There's going to be more."

Jude turned the pages. A widow writing that her son's fever had broken twice now, once by trade and once by grace, and grace was better. A man who had sold his courage and spent a year afraid of stairs, writing that he had climbed the bell tower last week just to feel the wind. An old woman who signed her entry with a name Jude recognized from the ledgers—one of the village of the unremembered, the smiling hollow ones—and who wrote, in a hand growing firmer month by month: *I am learning to be a person again. It is slower than forgetting. It is better.*

At the back of the book, in fresher ink, someone had written a question. Not a thank-you. A question:

*We were taught that everything has a price. You taught us otherwise. What do we build in the space where the price used to be?*

Jude read it three times. Outside, the light was failing, and the first stars were coming out over the rooftops—all of them, every one, burning in their ancient stations, keeping no accounts, owing nothing, shining anyway.

He looked at Wren. "Get your coat," he said. "And bring your pen. I think we're going to need a bigger ledger."

She grinned—the fierce, unpriceable grin of a woman whose father had loved her enough to bankrupt a kingdom's worth of darkness—and went to fetch it.

Jude stood a moment longer at the window, his mother's name soft against his chest, the question heavy and bright in his hands, and began, in his neat taster's script, to write the first honest entry of the rest of his life:

*Received, freely given: one world, mending.*

*Owed in return: everything we have.*

*Terms: open-ended.*

*Interest: none. Some things were never for sale.*

He blew out the candle, because he no longer needed it to see by, and went to answer the letter.
