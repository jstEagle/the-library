## 1. The Smuggler's Toll

The mules knew before Bram did.

He felt it in the way Sorrel's ears swiveled backward, sharp as compass needles, toward the upper slopes of the pass where the morning light came down thin and white through the pines. The lead mule stopped mid-stride, one hoof raised, and would not put it down.

Bram stopped with her, letting the rope slack in his gloved hand, and listened the way he had taught himself to listen — not with his ears but somewhere deeper, in the hollow of his throat where the forbidden thing lived.

*Stone is tired,* Sorrel's body said, in the wordless language of stiff legs and rolled white eyes and a low grumble that vibrated through the lead rope like a plucked string. *Stone on the left. Old stone. Stone about to let go.*

Behind him, eight men and one woman shifted in their saddles, breath steaming, impatient. The caravan stretched back along the switchback trail like a string of dark beads threaded through the frost-killed bracken of Thornfell. Above them the mountain rose in terraces of grey scree and black pine, and above that, the peaks wore their early snow like a shroud drawn up to the chin. Winter was coming early this year. Everyone said so. The ravens had been saying it for weeks, in their scattered, glittering way — *cold-cold-cold, the high passes closing, the king's roads filling up with soldiers* — though no one but Bram heard them say anything at all.

"Hold up," Bram called back, pitching his voice to the flat, bored register the guild expected of him. Guide's voice. Nothing voice. "Lead girl's balking. Give her a moment."

"A moment," muttered Hake, the fat one, from the middle of the line. "We've given her three moments and a mile. Mule's got more opinions than my first wife."

Laughter, brief and thin, steam curling in the cold. Bram smiled the smile that went with it and did not join it, because his whole attention was fixed upward and leftward, on the shoulder of scree two hundred feet above the trail where a slab of granite the size of a granary barn leaned against the mountainside like a drunk against a doorframe. It had leaned there for a hundred years or a thousand. It was finished leaning now. He could feel it the way you feel a sneeze building in someone else's face — the deep mineral groan of it, the slow splitting whisper along a fault line no eye could see, ice wedged in the crack and swelling with the freeze-thaw rhythm of the last ten dawns.

Sorrel pulled against the rope. *Down. Down, down.*

"Yes," Bram breathed, so quietly it was hardly a sound at all, only the shape of agreement made with lips alone. And then, before he could stop himself — before the habit of a lifetime could clamp its hand over his mouth — he spoke.

"Not this way, sweet girl. Up and over the far side. Quick now, quick, tell them, tell them all."

The words came out at a murmur, softer than the wind. No man could have heard them. But Sorrel heard, and turned, and screamed.

It was not a sound mules made, that scream. It was a bugle, high and urgent, and she wheeled on the narrow trail and shouldered into the mule behind her and bit its haunch, and that mule bolted the other way, and the whole line broke apart like a dropped necklace. Hooves clattered on frozen stone. Men swore. Hake's mule slammed him against the rock wall of the cut and kept going. And the animals did not bolt downhill along the trail, which was the sensible thing, the animal thing — they bolted *up*, scrambling off the western lip of the path onto the rough shelf of ground above it, dragging their shrieking, stumbling riders with them, hauling the cargo-lashed train in a scrabbling tangle toward the roots of an ancient pine.

Bram stood alone on the empty trail for exactly one heartbeat, feeling his gift ring in his throat like a struck bell, feeling the shape of what he'd done spread outward through eleven animal minds at once.

Then the mountain shrugged.

The crack he had felt singing for ten dawns let go all at once. The slab came down in a long, grinding roar, taking half the slope with it, and the trail disappeared — the exact stretch of trail, forty yards of it, where twelve mules and nine smugglers had been standing thirty seconds before. Stone smashed into stone. Dust boiled up white and choking. A pine at the trail's edge snapped like a green twig, and the sound of it was very small under the vast indifference of the rockfall.

Then silence, except for the settling hiss of dust, and the panicked breathing of mules, and Bram's own heart slamming in his ears.

* * *

They counted themselves twice and found everyone alive. Hake had a bleeding nose and a broken pride. Young Piet, who was seventeen and lied about being nineteen, had lost his hat and nearly wept over it until Vess looked at him, after which he found other things to occupy his face. The cargo — salt pork, iron nails, and six casks of brandy sealed inside honest-looking barrels of fish oil — had survived, lashed tight, jumbled but intact.

And every single one of them, man and mule alike, was standing on the uphill shelf instead of lying crushed beneath four hundred tons of Thornfell granite.

"Sixteen years I've run this pass," said Dags, spitting dust. His voice had a strange, hollowed-out quality; men often sounded like that after the mountain spoke. "Sixteen years. That's no luck. That slide was sitting there waiting for us like a spider in a web."

"Mule smelled it," said Hake thickly through his ruined nose, one hand resting, oddly tender, on Sorrel's neck. She stood trembling still, ears flat, eyes rolling to find Bram every few breaths as if confirming to herself that he was still there, still within the reach of her enormous attention. "Animals know. My grandmother always said. Animals know when the mountain means to move."

"They know," Bram agreed, and made himself say it lightly, and hated how light his voice sounded in his own skull, like paper over a well. "That's why we use them instead of wagons, isn't it? Four legs and good sense."

"Mm," said Vess.

She had not raised her voice once during the whole of it. That was Vess. Guild master of the Grey Route runners, five foot two of coiled patience, grey-eyed, grey-tempered, with a scar through one eyebrow that she never explained and no one ever asked about twice. While the others had floundered and sworn and counted heads, she had simply stood on the shelf with her arms folded, watching — first the rockfall, then the mules, then the men, in that order, and last of all Bram, longest of all.

She watched him now across the trampled snow-dust of the shelf, and her face was pleasant, and her eyes were arithmetic.

"That mule of yours," she said, coming toward him with her hands loose at her sides, boots crunching. "Sorrel."

"She's a good girl." Too warm. He cooled it. "Beast's dumb as a sack of turnips, but her nose works."

"Her nose." Vess stopped an arm's length away. This close, Bram could smell woodsmoke and leather and, faintly, the brandy they carried. "Funny thing about noses. A nose smells fire before it starts, meat before it's butchered. But a nose doesn't smell a rock coming loose two hundred feet up and on the far side of a wind." She tilted her head. The scarred eyebrow rose a fraction. "Wind was blowing the wrong way, Eastwick. You noticed?"

Bram had noticed. Bram had spent thirty-one years noticing everything, cataloguing every witness, every angle, every ear, building the small silent architecture of a life without exposure. He had noticed that the wind blew downslope that morning, carrying scent *away* from the animals, and he had thought, *good, no one can ask how she knew,* and then he had spoken aloud anyway, because a slab of granite the size of a tithe-barn does not care about your careful life.

"I noticed her stop," he said. "I've been paired with her two years. When she plants a foot, there's a reason. I called a halt and got out of the way and so did everyone else, thanks be." He shrugged, guide-flat. "Old animals are lucky animals, guild master. Or they're dead already and it never got around to them."

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Somewhere behind her, Piet was re-lashing a barrel and telling anyone who'd listen that he'd *seen it start*, that he'd *shouted*, and Dags was letting him have the lie because men need their stories. The mules had begun to graze the frost-crisped bracken with the ruthless pragmatism of their kind, as if the mountain had not just tried to murder all of them. Sorrel alone still watched Bram.

"You saved this run," Vess said at last. "Whatever it was — your eye, her nose, the Virgin of the Small Mercies, I don't much care — you saved it. Nine lives' worth of cargo and nine lives' worth of men." Her mouth curved. It was almost a smile. It was the kind of almost-smile that made hardened freight-runners find reasons to be elsewhere. "I pay my debts, Eastwick. Remember that."

"And I keep my ledgers straight," Bram said. "Guild master."

"See that you do." She turned away, then paused, half-facing him, dust still sifting gold and grey through the light between them. "There's a ford crossing at Cold Sorrow, three days on. Ice'll be forming. You'll take point."

"As always."

"As always." She walked back toward the head of the line, calling orders in that level voice that needed no volume, and Bram stood in the settling dust and felt, with perfect clarity, the exact weight of what had changed between them.

Nothing, to any eye. Everything, to hers. She had asked him a question wrapped in a compliment and he had answered it with a shrug, and one of those two things had been true and one of them had been a lie, and Vess had spent twenty years making her living knowing which was which.

* * *

They camped that night in a fold of the mountain below the treeline, a smuggler's hollow that three generations of Grey Route runners had worn smooth: fire-pit ringed with stones, windbreak of stacked boughs, a cache of dry wood hidden under a flat rock against the day someone came through freezing. The mules were hobbled in a semicircle near the fire, and the men sat close to the flames while the cold came down off the peaks like something poured.

Bram took the first watch, as he usually traded for, because the first watch meant solitude and solitude meant he could unclench the hand that lived closed around his throat.

He climbed to the lip of the hollow and sat with his back against a pine, and Sorrel detached herself from the hobbled line and came and stood near him, near enough that her warmth reached him in the dark. Neither of them spoke. Speaking was dangerous even here, even now, though the nearest man was forty yards off and snoring. Thirty-one years of caution sat on his chest like a second skeleton.

But Sorrel deserved *something*. So he laid his hand flat against her cheek, over the great vein, and let the words travel down his arm instead of out through the air, which was a trick with no name that he had invented himself and told no soul, the pressure of meaning passed skin to skin: *You did well. You saved us all. You are the cleverest heart on this mountain and I would die before I let any harm find you.*

She leaned into his palm, a ton of patient muscle surrendering its whole weight to his hand, and her gratitude poured back up his arm, simple and enormous, without any words at all.

This was the bargain, he thought, watching the firelight waver on the snow beyond the hollow. This was the trade he had made without ever signing anything. He could speak with everything that ran, flew, rooted, or crawled, and so he could belong to the living world in a way no other man ever would — and he must never once be seen doing it, and so he belonged to no company of men entirely, not the guild, not a parish, not a hearth. Safety bought with silence. Every road he'd ever run, he'd run half-alone. Every campfire he'd ever warmed his hands at, he'd warmed them at the edge of, one shoulder always turned to the dark, listening to what the dark was saying.

His mother had taught him the first rule when he was six: *Never where anyone can see, Brammen. Not once. They burned a woman in Harrowgate for less than what you are.* She had died when he was eleven, and the rule was the largest thing she'd left him, larger than her face in memory, larger than her voice. He had kept it through famine winters and guild wars and the warden purges three years back, when they'd strung up a shepherd boy in Lowbridge square on nothing but a milkmaid's hysteria and a crow that had answered him too promptly in the marketplace. Bram had been in Lowbridge that week. He had left the next morning and taken the long southern route for a year.

Below him, the fire settled in a collapse of embers, and a knot of sparks lifted and swirled up past him into the black, and somewhere upslope a fox said one short, cynical sentence into the night and received no reply.

*Cold coming,* the fox had said, which was fox for *the winter will be long and the mice will hide deep.* But underneath it, woven through it the way a darker thread runs through cloth, Bram caught something else drifting down from the high country tonight, faint as a smell of smoke from a fire beyond the hills. Unease. Movement. The beasts of the high passes were all saying the same scattered thing in their different tongues — the ravens most clearly, because ravens loved gossip the way other creatures loved carrion.

*Riders in white. Riders in the king's colors riding the storm-line, and they do not ride toward anything. They flee from something. Flee-flee-flee, down by the Cold Sorrow ford, and one of them bleeds.*

Bram sat very still against the pine, his hand buried in Sorrel's mane, and told himself it was none of his affair. Riders fled from things every winter. Smugglers survived by having no affairs at all.

Three days, Vess had said. The ford at Cold Sorrow.

Sorrel shifted her weight and sighed through her nostrils, a long white plume of breath unraveling in the starlight, and pressed her forehead against Bram's shoulder as if to say: *whatever comes, I will be standing next to you when it arrives.*

"Sleep, old girl," Bram murmured, in the voice he used for no one else in the world, and below him the fire burned down, and above him Thornfell glittered with the early, hungry snow.
