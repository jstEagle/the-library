## 2. A Stranger at the Ford

The river had gone quiet in the way rivers only go quiet when they are pretending to be something they are not.

Bram Eastwick heard it from half a mile off — or rather, he heard the absence of it, a hush beneath the wind like a held breath — and he did not like it. Moving water spoke all night long in its small silver tongue, and beasts along the banks answered it in their own ways: the chitter of voles under the snow, the patient patience of herons standing like folded prayers in the reeds. But the Marrow Ford, where the smugglers' road dropped down through black pines to cross the shallows, was silent as a shut door.

Ice, then. Early ice, weeks ahead of any winter that had a right to exist yet.

"Ford's frozen," he said aloud, because Vess was watching him again and silence made her watch harder.

She rode at the head of the line on her dun mare, a small woman shaped entirely of angles and decisions, and she turned just enough to look back down the file of mules and men strung out behind him like beads on a dark cord. "Frozen's good," she said. "Frozen carries weight."

"Frozen lies." Bram kept his voice flat, bored, the voice of a man who knew ice the way he knew his own hands. "It holds for six mules and fails on the seventh. It holds all day and gives way at dusk. I've pulled drowned men out of this ford who swore the ice was thick enough to dance on."

"And have you?" Vess asked mildly. "Danced on it?"

"I don't dance."

A few of the men laughed. Vess did not. She held his eyes a moment longer than comfort allowed, then clicked her mare forward, and the caravan groaned into motion down the last slope toward the river.

Bram fell in beside the third mule, a gray jenny named — not by him, he refused to name them anymore, naming made the silence cost more — but the one everyone called Sorrel-Ear for the burn scar that had taken her left ear's tip. She pressed her warm nose against his shoulder as she walked, the way she always did, and he let her, and he hated himself a little for how much good it did him.

*Cold,* she said, in the way mules say things, which is to say without words at all but with shapes of feeling that arrived whole in his mind. *Cold below. Thin-thin-thin.*

"I know," he murmured, too low for human ears, pitched under the crunch of boots and hooves so that even a man walking beside him would hear nothing but breath. "We'll test it. We'll go slow."

He should not have said even that. He knew the rules he had made for himself, and rule the first was *never answer them*, and rule the second was *never ask*, and rule the third, the one he broke most often and forgave himself least for, was *never, ever use them*. Because a man who spoke to beasts in Thornfell was not a curiosity. He was kindling. There were villages in these mountains that still kept an old stone post at their edge, moss-eaten now, called the speaker's post, and what had been done there three generations ago was done thoroughly enough that no one liked to speak of it, which meant everyone remembered.

So Bram Eastwick walked through the world as a quiet man with a limp and a talent for reading animals — anyone could see a mule's ears pin back, anyone could tell when horses smelled wolves — and every gift he spent, he spent in fractions, in misdirection, in luck manufactured so carefully it looked like coincidence.

The rockslide yesterday had been too much luck. He knew it. He had felt the mountain's shudder through Sorrel-Ear's hooves ten minutes before the scree let go, had murmured *up, up, away from the face* to her and let panic travel back down the lead-line the way fear does among herd animals, and the caravan had halted in just the right place for just long enough that when the slope came down it took the road and not the road's users. Vess had looked at the buried track, and then at him, and her eyes had been the color of a ledger being opened.

She was watching him now. That was the trouble. Not suspicion yet — suspicion could be starved — but attention, which fed on anything.

The trees broke open and the Marrow ran wide and white before them, maybe forty paces of river locked under a lid of gray ice furred with snow, and beyond it the far bank climbed into more pines and, somewhere above those, the high passes that were the whole reason for this journey and its considerable pay. The sky hung low and the color of old pewter, and the air had that flinty smell of cold deepening toward snow.

Vess raised a fist. The column stopped.

"Well?" she said to Bram. "You're our expert on lying surfaces. Go read it."

There was laughter at that, low and easy. Bram unslung his axe, walked out onto the ice the way he had walked onto thin ice a hundred times, weight low, feet spread, listening with his boots as much as his ears. The surface gave a sound like a bowed string, deep and slow. Good sign, mostly. He went out ten paces, fifteen, tapped with the axe haft. Twenty. The ice was a hand thick near the banks, thinner toward the channel where the current still muttered underneath, and he found the seam of it — a dark vein running diagonally, the river's old fast lane still flowing somewhere two fingers below the roof of ice.

He turned and raised a hand, and called the crossing the way Vess paid him to call it: single file, spread apart, mules on the rope, no talking, keep moving, don't stop, don't bunch. The men came out onto the river with the particular hunched reverence of people crossing a thing that might eat them, and the mules came with their ears flat, and Sorrel-Ear said *thin-thin* under her breath the whole way across, and Bram walked backward ahead of the line like a shepherd of small dangers, and everything was going exactly the way such crossings go, which is to say tediously, until the stranger stood up out of the snow on the far bank.

Bram saw him first, because Bram had been looking at the bank already, reading it the way he read everything — the crow-tracks stitched along the shoreline, the fox scent-mark frozen sharp on a boulder. And then the snow itself moved, or something in it did, and a man unfolded from behind a fallen pine as if the forest had been holding him and had finally let go.

"Company," Bram said, and his voice carried flat and hard across the ice, and every hand on the far side of the river found a weapon with the smooth simultaneity of men who had robbed and been robbed and did not require discussion.

The man took one step and fell down.

That changed things. Bandits do not usually fall down. Vess came out onto the ice at a run, low, blade drawn, and Bram followed because following was his job and also because some animal instinct older than his caution had already begun to pull at him like a current. Up close the stranger resolved out of the snow in pieces: torn royal blue, once fine, now stiff with frost; a breastplate missing its front half, the straps cut, not broken; gloves gone, bare blackened fingers; a face young under the ruin of beard and blood, eyes the startling pale gray of river ice, fixed on Bram with terrible accuracy, as if of everyone sliding toward him through the falling dusk he had known in advance which one he needed.

"Royal livery," Vess breathed. She did not lower her blade. "Torn royal livery, at Marrow Ford, in the middle of nowhere. That's a story wearing a dead man's coat."

"Vess." The man's voice came out in crumbs, each word costing him visibly. "Guild master Vess. They said — you move cargo. Quiet. I need —"

"You need a priest, and I haven't got one. Who's hunting you?"

"Wardens." The word dropped out of him like a stone down a well. Behind her, Bram heard the smugglers shift, a rustle of unease passing down the line. Crown wardens did not chase men into Thornfell in the first snows. Crown wardens did not lose. Whatever this was, it was sized wrong, and everyone on that riverbank felt it.

"They can't have seen us," Vess said. "If we're quick, we were never here. Leave him."

"He's dying," someone said.

"He's dying anyway. Question is whether we die with him." But she hesitated, and Bram understood why. Even outlaws want to be told a story before they leave a man in the snow. Even wolves pause.

The stranger's gray eyes slid past Vess and found Bram again, and this time the man began, with a determination that would have been admirable in a healthy man and was horrifying in this one, to crawl. Across the snow. Toward Bram. One arm dragging, leaving a furrow like something dragged from a grave.

"No," Bram said, without thinking, and knelt, because if he didn't kneel the man would crawl until the crawling killed him. "No, stop, I'm nobody. I'm the wrong man."

"You're—" The stranger laughed, wetly, a terrible sound. "Nobody. Yes. Good. Nobody can carry it."

And then the man's blackened hands were at his own chest, tearing open the ruined coat, and what he brought out was wrapped in oilcloth tied with wire, and he pushed it into Bram's hands with the full stupid weight of a dying man's strength, and Bram, whose entire life was built on never touching anything that belonged to other people's disasters, took it, because his arms simply did.

The bundle was light. A map, he understood at once, rolled tight, and something small and hard at its center like a coin or a seal, and the wax on the wrapping — he felt it through the cloth, a disc of stamped wax bigger than any seal he'd ever seen, bearing a device he half-knew and did not want to know: a crown over a mountain. Greywatch. Whatever this was, it came from Greywatch Keep, and Greywatch Keep was the king's own house in these mountains, and nothing with the king's mark on it had ever brought anyone in Thornfell anything but grief.

"Listen." The stranger grabbed Bram's wrist. His grip should have been weak. It was not; dying men spend their last strength all at once, like a purse upended. "Listen to me. The map doesn't show treasure. It shows the way *down*. Under Greywatch. There's a door older than the keep, older than the crown, and the crown is — the crown is the lock. Do you understand? It was never a symbol. It's a *chain*."

"I don't understand," Bram said honestly.

"Good. Honest man. Better than brave." Blood came into the stranger's mouth and he spat it, dark on snow, and kept talking faster, words tumbling now as if he could feel the darkness coming up behind them like wolves. "They took the king years ago. No one noticed because the thing wears him well, it smiles beautifully, it has learned to smile — but the winters come early now, do you feel it? The ice is on the ford and it isn't even winter. That's it feeding. That's it eating his winters, one by one, and when the long winter finishes falling—"

"Who took him?" Vess demanded. "The wardens?"

"The wardens guard the cage. Most of them don't know. Some do." The stranger coughed, a long awful tearing sound, and sagged, and only his grip on Bram's wrist stayed alive. "I was the king's courier. I was — my name doesn't matter, my name died at Greywatch. Listen. Listen. Find the oath-keepers. There's one left, rotting in the border garrisons — Vance, Sera Vance, they broke her for telling the truth once. She knows the rite. The map shows the way down. The rite needs a voice —" and here the gray eyes did something impossible, they *apologized*, even as they condemned, "— it needs a voice the crown has never heard, and I am sorry, I am so sorry, but you were on the road and I am out of roads."

"The king," Bram said. His mouth had gone dry as old bone. "You're saying the king is not the king."

Something blazed in the stranger's face — relief, ferocious and hopeless, the relief of a man who has carried a secret alone through miles of snow and finally set it down. "The king is not the king," he whispered. "But the king is still alive. Underneath. He's still *alive*, do you understand, and nobody else will say it, and if you burn that map I swear to you by every god I've stopped believing in that the winter will not stop falling until it buries your mountains —"

Then his grip loosened. Then his eyes went past Bram's shoulder, upriver, and widened — not with fear, Bram thought later, but with something almost like greeting — and the man said, very quietly, "Ah. Cold," as if recognizing an acquaintance, and lay back in the snow.

Bram caught him, lowered him down. Checked, though he already knew, throat and wrist. Nothing. Just the wind moving the frost-stiff livery, and above them, out of the pewter sky, the first snow began to fall, fat flakes descending with a strange unhurried calm, settling on the dead man's open eyes.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The river muttered under its lid of ice, the only thing in the world still making any sound at all.

"Bury him," Vess said at last, roughly. "Deep. Now, before the snow fills his tracks and Dorn's trackers find the dig." She turned on Bram, and her blade was still out, and her eyes went from the dead man's face to Bram's hands, to the oilcloth bundle he was still, stupidly, holding. "Give it here. Guild property. Everything found on the road belongs to the guild — that's the toll, Eastwick, you know the law better than anyone."

Bram looked down at the bundle in his hands.

He was aware, with terrible clarity, of many things at once: the weight of the map no heavier than a dead bird; the snow landing on his shoulders; Vess's blade; twelve pairs of eyes; Sorrel-Ear at the ice's edge saying, in her wordless way, *wrong-wrong-wrong, bad weather coming, bad thing in your hands.* And underneath all of it, quieter than all of it, a voice that was his own and had been silent for twenty years, saying: *the king is still alive underneath. Nobody else will say it.*

He thought about the speaker's post in the villages, moss-eaten and unspoken-of. He thought about safety, and what it cost, and how he had been paying for it in installments his whole life, and how the currency had always been silence.

"Nothing," Bram said. "He had nothing. Look at him — the wardens took everything worth taking before he ever reached the ford. He grabbed at me because I knelt. That's all."

It was not even a clever lie. It was a bad lie, told badly, by a man who had never lied to Vess before, and he watched her know it. He watched her weigh it — the snow thickening, the light failing, a dead royal courier at her feet and wardens possibly behind, the practical arithmetic of a guild master who could not afford a mutiny on a frozen river any more than she could afford the king's secrets. He watched her choose, for tonight, to pocket the debt instead of collecting it.

"Load up," Vess said, turning away, and her voice promised, clearly, wordlessly, that the ledger remained open. "Eastwick — you carry the dead man's pack. If he truly had nothing, it'll be light."

The men moved. The mules were turned and roped. Someone dragged snow over the beginnings of a grave, and the snow helped, falling steadily now, erasing them as fast as they worked.

Bram stood alone for one more breath at the river's edge, the bundle inside his coat, against his chest, where it seemed to beat faintly like a second heart he had never wanted. *Unwanted inheritance*, he thought. *The worst kind. The kind that fits your hands anyway.*

Sorrel-Ear nudged him, warm breath against his neck.

*Storm,* she said. *Move.*

"Yes," Bram said aloud, to no one, and followed the caravan up the far bank into the pines, into the falling dark, carrying the map that showed the way down.

Behind them, the crows came down out of the trees to stand along the ice in a silent black row, all facing the same direction, all watching him go. And Bram, who could have asked them what they knew, walked on and did not ask — because he was already afraid of the answer, and afraid, far more, that someday soon he would have no choice but to listen.
