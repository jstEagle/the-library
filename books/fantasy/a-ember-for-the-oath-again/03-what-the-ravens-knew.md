## 3. What the Ravens Knew

The crows of Thornfell kept their parliament in the black pines above the Widow's Stair, where the pass narrowed to a throat of ice and the wind came down off the high crags with a sound like a whetstone drawn across bone. Bram Eastwick had been feeding them for eleven years — scraps of salt pork on the cold mornings, suet in the hungry weeks of late winter, once an entire wheel of cheese he'd stolen from Vess's own saddlebag and never confessed to. Eleven years of small bribes, and what he'd bought with them was not friendship exactly. Crows did not do friendship. What he'd bought was attention.

They knew him the way the mountain knew him: as a fixed thing, a reliable thing, a man who walked the Stair at the same hours and always carried something worth eating.

He climbed to them alone that morning, before the others woke, because he could not ask his questions where human ears might catch the answers.

The snow squeaked under his boots. His breath hung about him in rags. Above, the pines were furred white to the second branch, and among those branches the parliament stirred — a shifting of black shapes, a low commentary of *krah* and *krr*, dozens of bright eyes finding him at once the way water finds a drain.

"Morning," Bram said softly. He held up the salt pork wrapped in cloth, let the smell of it loose on the wind. "I've questions this time. Questions first, then breakfast."

A big female dropped from the pine nearest him and landed on the frozen trunk of a fallen tree, head cocked, one eye fixed on him with the terrible patience of her kind. She was old — he could tell by the ragged edge of her left wing feather, by the gray threading the black at her throat. He'd fed her first three winters ago when she'd been starving after a hard molt, and she had never forgotten, and she had never forgiven him for making her need it.

"The man," Bram said. "Yesterday. At the ford. The one who went through the ice." He unwrapped the pork and tore off a strip, held it out flat on his palm. "You saw him. You see everything on this pass."

The crow took the meat with a delicacy that would have shamed a duchess, swallowed it whole, and then gave him the images.

That was how it worked, when it worked at all — not words, not really, though sometimes words came tangled up inside like flies in amber. Mostly it was pictures, pressed into his mind the way a thumb presses into wax: jumbled, sideways, colored by whatever mattered to crow-sense. Hunger. Shine. The difference between safe ground and ground that kills.

The images came, and Bram stood very still in the snow and let them come.

*A man running.* That was the shape of it, over and over, the way a dream circles its one fear. A man in torn blue-and-silver livery running up the pass in the gray hour before dawn, and behind him — this part the crows found very interesting, this part they turned over and showed from several angles — behind him rode men in dark cloaks with steel caps, six of them, then five, then four, moving with the grim economy of hounds that have scented blood and know the scent will hold.

*Crown wardens,* Bram thought, and his stomach went cold in a way the morning hadn't managed.

The stranger had come up the Widow's Stair two nights past, in the dark, in weather no sane man traveled. The crows had watched him from above — watched him fall twice, watched him claw himself upright, watched him press his hand against the rock of the pass as if the mountain itself might hold him steady. One of the younger crows had swooped low over him, drawn by something, and the man had looked up with a face like a dead man's lantern: hollowed, fever-bright, already half gone.

And in his fist, clutched so tight the knuckles stood out like river stones, a rolled tube of oilcloth.

Bram's hand went, without his willing it, to the inner pocket of his coat, where the map lay against his chest like a second heartbeat. He'd slept with it there. He'd woken twice in the night with his palm flat over it, certain someone had taken it, certain it was burning through the wool.

*The king is not the king.*

The images kept coming. The wardens had lost him in the pines above the ford — snow filling tracks, dusk coming down — and the lead rider, a woman with a voice like a door slamming, had stood in the falling snow and turned a slow circle, reading the mountain the way Bram read it, and had not found what she was looking for. Then they had ridden down toward Lowbridge, and the crows had lost interest, because crown wardens carry no food and throw nothing away worth having.

But the man — the man had gone on. Down to the ford. Onto the ice. And here the oldest crow sent him the last image with something almost like gentleness, which crows can do when they choose, though they rarely choose: *the ice going white and soft beneath him, the black water opening, the man's mouth making a shape that might have been a word or might have been only breath, and then nothing but cold and current and the long dark under the river.*

Bram fed them all the rest of the pork. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, some part of him was screaming in a small, trapped voice: *wardens, wardens, royal livery, wardens* — and another part, quieter and older, was saying what it always said when the gift stirred: *say nothing. Tell no one. Be stone.*

He had been stone for thirty-one years. Stone had kept him alive through his mother's leaving, through the village boys who'd called him demon-spawn when a goat had answered him too plainly in their hearing, through every caravan and every campfire where he'd smiled and nodded and kept his mouth shut around the truth of himself. Stone was safe. Stone was silence bought at the price of everything else.

He looked east, down the pass, toward Lowbridge, where four crown wardens were even now asking questions in taverns with the patience of men paid by the year.

He looked west, deeper into Thornfell, where the smugglers' camp smoked gently against the cliffs, where Vess was awake — Vess was always awake before anyone — and watching.

* * *

Vess Corrigan ran the guild the way a miller runs a mill: everything fed in at the top, everything ground fine, everything weighed. She was fifty-one years old and built like the door of a strongroom, with iron-gray hair cut short for practicality and a scar splitting her left eyebrow that she claimed came from a bear and everyone knew came from a husband. She had pulled Bram out of a snowdrift when he was nineteen and half-dead and asked him exactly one question — *can you walk?* — and had never asked him anything else that mattered, which was why he loved her, and why loving her had always felt like standing on thin ice.

She was waiting outside her tent when he came down from the pines, arms crossed, breath steaming, watching him with eyes the color of slate in rain.

"You were feeding the birds," she said.

"I was feeding the birds."

"At dawn. In this cold. Before the mules are even seen to."

"They don't complain like the mules do."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile; Vess rationed smiles like hardtack. "Mule's sound, by the way. The one you talked quiet to yesterday. Farrier says there's not a bruise on her. Lucky animal."

"Lucky," Bram agreed.

She let the silence sit between them. That was her weapon, silence; she'd broken greener smugglers than Bram with nothing but a raised eyebrow and forty seconds of nothing. He counted the seconds now, matching her, and hated himself a little for how good he'd gotten at this.

"Word from Lowbridge," Vess said at last. "Tam's boy came up yesterday evening, ahead of the storm. Says there's crown wardens in the town. Four of them. Asking after a man in livery, torn, traveling alone. Asking after anyone who helped him." Her eyes didn't move from his face. "Asking after anyone who crossed the ford yesterday."

The wind moved through the camp, rattling the canvas, carrying the smell of woodsmoke and mule and frozen pine. Somewhere a kettle clanked. Bram kept his face the way he kept it always: open, honest, slightly slow. It was the best mask he owned, honesty worn so long it fit like skin.

"Bad luck for whoever that was," he said.

"Mm." Vess unfolded her arms. "Here's my thinking, Eastwick. Wardens on the pass means the guild runs dark until spring thaw, or we pay the toll roads and lose a season's profit, or we find out what the wardens want and sell it to them, whichever pays best. And I'm told—" she took one step closer, and her voice dropped into the register she used for ledgers and funerals— "that the dead man at the ford was carrying something. That the ferryman's daughter saw you kneel by him. Saw him grab your arm. Saw his mouth moving against your ear."

"She was half a mile off—"

"Saw. Him. Grab your arm." Each word set down like a coin on a table. "So either a dying royal courier chose you, of all the people on this mountain, for a final confession, or he handed you something, and either way I want to know which, and either way it belongs to the guild. Anything found on the pass is guild property. That's the law of the company, Bram. You know it. You've lived under it eleven years."

*Say nothing. Be stone.* The old litany rose in him, and for the first time in his life it failed — because the map was warm against his chest, warmer than body heat had any right to make it, and the dying man's fingers were still on his wrist in memory, and the words were still in his ear: *the king is not the king.*

If he gave Vess the map, the guild would sell it. Not out of malice — out of arithmetic. Four wardens in Lowbridge meant a buyer with deep pockets, and Vess had never in her life left gold sitting on a table. And whoever wore the king's face would get back whatever the stranger had died carrying, and the stranger would have died for nothing, and Bram would have helped bury him.

If he lied to Vess — outright, to her face, after eleven years —

"Bram." Vess's voice softened by a degree, which was worse than the hardness. "Eleven years you've run my passes. I've never once caught you in a lie. Don't start on the coldest morning of the year."

And there it was. The moment, laid out clean as a blade on white linen. He understood, with terrible clarity, that he was standing at the point of no return — that whatever he said next would split his life into before and after, the way the ice had split under the stranger, the way the river had opened and taken him down.

He thought of his mother's face, which he still remembered, and of the vow he'd made at nine years old in a hayloft with a trembling sparrow cupped in his hands: *never tell, never show, be stone, be safe.*

He thought of a hollow-eyed man pressing a wax seal into his hands with the last of his strength, trusting — insanely, undeservedly — a stranger on a frozen river.

"There was nothing," Bram said. "No map. No message. He grabbed my sleeve because he was drowning and I was the closest solid thing. He died before he got a word out. I searched the body — you'd have wanted me to — and there was nothing but wet wool and a soldier's rosary. I left it with him. It seemed right."

Every word was calm. Every word was false. He heard himself lie with a fluency that frightened him, heard the honest slowness drop into his voice like a coin into a well, and understood that he had just become a different person and could never go back to being the old one.

Vess studied him for a long, long moment. Snow ticked softly against the canvas of the tents. Behind her, the camp was waking — the clink of harness, a mule complaining, someone swearing cheerfully at a frozen rope.

"That's twice now," she said finally. "The mule, and this. Twice you've been lucky, Eastwick." She turned away, then paused, her back to him, broad and immovable as the cliff itself. "I've known you since you were a half-frozen boy who couldn't lie about where he'd been. Whatever you're carrying, I hope it's worth what it costs you." She glanced back over her shoulder, and her slate eyes were unreadable. "Because it'll cost. It always does. Get the caravan ready. We move within the hour, down-road, before the weather closes. And Bram — if the wardens stop us, you'll let me do the talking."

"Yes," Bram said. "Vess."

She went, and he stood alone in the trampled snow of the camp with the map burning against his chest and the taste of his first true lie still bitter on his tongue, like ash, like iron, like the beginning of a long winter.

Above him, in the black pines, a single crow called out — *krah, krah* — the sound carrying down the pass like a warning, or a blessing, or the tolling of a bell for the man Bram Eastwick had been an hour ago.

He touched the map through his coat, once, and went to harness the mules.

Down-road. Toward Lowbridge. Toward the wardens.

Toward whatever came next.
