## 5. Wardens on the White Road

The White Road earned its name honestly. In the high passes above the ford, the snow never fully melted — not in summer, not in any summer Bram had known — and so the old trade route ran pale as a scar across the mountainside, a ribbon of packed ice and grey gravel where nothing grew and everything could be seen for a mile.

Which was precisely the problem.

Bram crouched behind a shelf of wind-carved stone, breath held shallow, and counted the riders coming up the switchbacks below. Six. No — seven, with one trailing on a lathered bay that kept trying to turn back down-slope. The horses knew something was wrong up here. Horses usually did.

Grey cloaks. Iron-banded staves. The sun-crown badge of the wardens stamped in white enamel on each breastplate, catching the flat winter light like small cold moons.

*Crown wardens,* he thought, and the thought had teeth. *Not guild. Not Vess's people. The crown itself.*

Sera Vance knelt beside him, her broken oath-keeper's blade across her knees, watching the riders with the stillness of a woman who had learned patience in a cell and never quite unlearned it. Her breath fogged and drifted. She didn't ask what they were looking at. She'd seen them ten minutes ago, same as him.

"They've cut the low road," she murmured. "That means someone told them which way we'd come."

"Vess," Bram said. The name came out flat, and it cost him something to say it even now, even after everything. Three days since the garrison at Coldmarch. Three days since Sera had kicked open his chains and her own disgrace in the same motion. He still caught himself reaching for the old habits — checking over his shoulder for the guild master's whistle, half-expecting her voice in the dark telling him where to put his feet.

"Maybe." Sera's eyes narrowed. "Or maybe the dead man at the ford wasn't the only one carrying royal secrets. Either way, they're between us and the pass, and the pass is the only way through before the deep snows come."

Below them, the lead rider raised a fist. The column halted. Even at this distance Bram could see the discipline of it — no shouting, no wasted movement, seven riders fanning out across the width of the road like fingers closing around something small.

The man at their center sat taller than the rest, and though Bram couldn't yet make out her face beneath the fur-trimmed hood, he knew her by posture alone. She rode the way stone sits. Captain Ilse Dorn. He'd heard the name whispered in three border towns in as many days: *the Warden of the White Road, the one who doesn't stop, the one who found the traitor Halven's band in a snowdrift and brought back every head.* Whether those heads had been traitors or merely inconvenient, the stories declined to say.

"She'll have outriders on the high trail too," Sera said quietly. "Dorn doesn't leave doors open behind her."

Bram looked up. The high trail clung to the cliff face two hundred feet above the road, a goat-track really, barely wide enough for one man and his regrets. If there were wardens up there, they were hidden well. But the goats would know.

He became aware, then, of the sound he'd been half-hearing for the last several minutes without letting himself hear it: the thin metallic clatter of bells. Goat bells. Dozens of them, somewhere above and behind, carried on the wind off the glacier.

His stomach dropped.

*No,* he told himself, the way he'd been telling himself for thirty years. *Not that. Anything but that.*

"Bram?" Sera said. "You've gone grey as the road."

"Goats," he said. "There's a herd on the ridge above us. Mountain goats, maybe forty head, moving down toward the lower pasture before the storm." He swallowed. "If they come down this way, they'll pass right over us. And if wardens are on the high trail—"

"—the goats will spook, and the wardens will know exactly where we're hiding." Sera finished it for him, because she was quick that way, quicker than anyone he'd traveled with. Then she frowned. "How do you know there's a herd? I can't hear anything but wind."

"The bells," Bram said. "Listen."

She listened. And he watched her listen, and saw the moment she caught it — faint, far, real — and felt the small relief of having told a truth that was also a lie. The bells were real. Everything else he hadn't said.

Above them, on the ridge, the first goat crested the skyline.

It was a big white billy, horns curled back like frozen smoke, and it stood against the sky for a long moment, sniffing the wind. Bram knew that posture. That was the posture of an animal that smelled strangers on its mountain and didn't like it. Behind the billy came another, and another, the herd strung out along the ridgeline like beads on a grey thread, and somewhere among them — hidden, patient, exactly where Sera had predicted — a warden shifted, and a scrap of grey cloak moved against the rock.

The billy's head came up sharply. The whole herd tensed.

And below, Captain Dorn turned her horse to face the cliff, and Bram saw her face clearly for the first time: broad, weathered, older than her years, with eyes the color of slate under ice. She was looking up at the high trail. She had seen the same flicker of cloak they had.

"Down," she called, and her voice carried up the mountain like a thrown knife. "Two of you, up the scree. Flush whatever's on that trail. The rest hold the road."

Everything happened very fast after that.

Two wardens dismounted and started up the loose slope toward the hidden outriders, boots skidding on stone. The herd on the ridge, feeling the wrongness rise from below like heat off a forge, began to bunch and mill. The billy stamped. A nanny bleated, high and ragged. The bells went mad.

And Bram Eastwick stood up.

He didn't decide to do it. That was the thing he would think about later, lying awake in whatever shelter they found, staring at the dark — that he never decided. His body decided for him, the way a hand pulls back from a hot iron, the way a drowning man kicks. Because he had done the arithmetic already, faster than thought: if the wardens flushed their own outriders, the outriders would point down at the shelf of stone, and Dorn would look, and he and Sera would be two figures on a bare white road with nowhere to run and a day's climb ahead of them, and everything — the map sealed against his ribs, the dying man's last words, the whole terrible inheritance of it — would end here, in the snow, for nothing.

Unless the mountain itself got loud enough that nobody could point at anything.

"Sera," he said, and his voice came out strange, stripped, the voice of a man stepping off a ledge. "Don't watch me. Watch the road."

"What—"

He opened the door he had kept shut for thirty years, and let the gift out.

It was not words, not exactly. It had never been words. It was more like leaning close to another creature's heartbeat and humming until the two beats agreed. He found the billy first — old, proud, half-blind in one eye, a leader who had spent his whole life deciding when to run — and Bram poured into that great horned skull a single simple truth shaped like fear: *down, down, the wolves are below, DOWN.*

The billy screamed.

Not bleated — screamed, a sound like a hinge tearing free, and flung itself off the ridgeline. The herd followed because the herd always follows, forty animals in a thundering white cascade, hooves striking sparks from the frozen scree, bells shrieking, the whole slope coming alive with a roar of stone and wool and terror. The two climbing wardens threw themselves flat. The hidden outriders burst from cover like startled birds, grey cloaks flapping, suddenly visible to everyone including their own captain.

And the herd, funneling by instinct and by Bram's insistent, trembling will, did not go around the road.

They went *across* it.

The stampede hit the White Road like a river bursting a dam. Dorn's horse reared screaming; the bay behind it bolted outright, dumping its rider into a drift. Snow and gravel flew. A warden's stave cracked against a horn and snapped. The column dissolved into a churning white chaos of animals and men, and through it all Bram stood on the shelf of stone with his arms half-raised and tears freezing on his cheeks, holding the fear open like a wound, feeding it, shaping it — *run, run, away from the cliff, away from the men, down-valley, down* — while the herd poured past the wardens and down the western slope in a single boiling flood.

"Eastwick." Sera's hand closed hard on his shoulder. "Let them go. You can let them go now."

He let go.

The silence afterward was enormous. The last bell faded down the valley. Somewhere below, a warden was cursing with impressive fluency; somewhere else, Dorn's voice cut through the wreckage, calm and terrible, calling her people back into order. The herd was gone, scattered safe across the lower pastures, their fear already dissolving into ordinary goatish grievance.

Bram realized he was on his knees in the snow. He didn't remember falling.

He also realized, with a slow cold horror that had nothing to do with the altitude, that Sera Vance was standing directly beside him, and had been the entire time, and had watched every moment of it.

*Don't watch me,* he'd said. *Watch the road.*

She had watched him instead.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Below, the wardens were regrouping; above, the high trail stood briefly empty, its outriders scrambling down to report. The window was closing. Bram knew it and couldn't make his legs care.

"So," Sera said at last.

"Don't," Bram said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to say *what are you*, or *how long have you*, or *that's why the mule stopped on the pass*, and I can't—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, hated how young it made him sound. "I can't hear it right now. Not with them down there. Please."

Sera crouched down in front of him. Up close, her face was all sharp planes and frost-reddened skin, and her eyes — grey, steady, the eyes of a woman who had sworn oaths and broken one and carried both ever since — searched his face with a thoroughness that felt almost physical.

"I was going to say," she said slowly, "that we have about four minutes before Dorn's climbers reach this shelf, and that you should save your strength for the climb. Whatever else there is to say can wait for a fire, and a drink, and a locked door."

Bram stared at her. "That's it?"

"That's it." She stood, and offered him her hand, and after a moment he took it. Her grip was iron wrapped in leather. "Bram. Look at me."

He looked.

"I spent six years in a cell at Coldmarch for refusing to name a friend," she said. "Six years, because I believed some things belong to a person alone, even from the law. Especially from the law." Something moved behind her eyes, old and banked like coals. "Whatever you just did — however you did it — it's yours. Not mine. Not theirs." She nodded down the slope, toward the grey cloaks. "Not anyone's."

The relief hit him sideways, sudden and staggering, like warm water on frostbitten skin. He hadn't known how heavy it was until it lifted — thirty years of flinching, of swallowing words, of watching animals suffer in silence because helping them meant risking the rope. One person knew now. One person had seen the whole strange truth of him and simply *stood there*, unafraid, unhating, offering her hand.

He almost laughed. It came out closer to a sob, and he turned it into a cough, and climbed.

* *

They went up.

The high trail was everything its reputation promised: a ledge of ice-glazed rock stitched to the cliff face, wide enough for one, with two hundred feet of clean air on the left and two hundred feet of clean stone on the right. The wind came off the glacier in long white gusts that tried, patiently and without malice, to peel them off the mountain. Bram went first, testing each handhold, whispering — only whispering, only a little, only what was needed — to the ravens riding the thermals, asking them what lay around each bend.

*Men,* the ravens answered, twice. *Cold men. Waiting.*

Twice they pressed themselves into cracks in the cliff and let the wind hide them while warden pickets passed above. Once, a raven landed on a boulder not three feet from Sera's head and croaked a warning so perfectly timed that she shot Bram a look he pretended not to notice. She said nothing about it. She kept saying nothing about it, and each hour of her saying nothing was its own strange gift, and by the time the sun dropped behind the western peaks Bram had stopped waiting for the other shoe and started, cautiously, unbelievably, to breathe.

They camped that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, a fire no bigger than two cupped hands, sharing dried mutton and the last of the ford-town bread. The stars over Thornfell were brutal and beautiful, more of them than seemed lawful.

"Dorn won't stop," Sera said, poking the fire. "Stampede or no stampede. She lost the road today, not the hunt. By tomorrow she'll have fresh horses from the waystation at Harrow Fell, and she'll read that herd's tracks the way you read weather. She'll know the stampede turned west too neatly. She'll know someone turned it."

"I know." Bram pulled his cloak tighter. "I keep thinking about her face. When the goats hit. She wasn't angry."

"No?"

"She was *interested*. Like a woman doing sums." He stared into the flames. "Sera — the man at the ford. The seal. What exactly does it mean, that you recognized it?"

Sera was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice.

"It's the privy seal," she said finally. "Not the great seal they press on proclamations — the private one. The one the king uses for letters that never officially exist. I saw it once, years ago, on a warrant I was sworn to carry and burned instead." Her jaw tightened. "A dying warden of the household guard, fleeing his own brothers-in-arms, pressing the king's private seal into the hands of a smuggler on a frozen river. There's only one message that adds up to, Bram. Whatever wears the crown in Greywatch Keep isn't sending those letters anymore. Someone else is. Or something."

The wind moaned over the lip of the hollow. Far below and behind them, tiny with distance, a line of torchlight crawled along the White Road like a slow bright worm.

"They're still moving," Bram said. "In the dark. In this cold."

"Dorn moves in the dark. It's what she's for." Sera fed a twig to the fire. "Get some sleep. I'll take first watch."

He lay back against his pack and closed his eyes, and sleep came grudgingly, full of white slopes and ringing bells. Just before it took him, he felt the faintest pressure against his mind — not a word, not a thought, just a presence, familiar as a hand on the shoulder. Something out in the dark, beyond the firelight, out on the snowy flank of the mountain. Something hurt, and hungry, and watching them with more than animal attention.

Bram's eyes snapped open.

At the edge of the firelight, half-glimpsed between two stones, a pair of amber eyes looked back at him. They belonged to a wolf — huge, gaunt, its grey flank crusted with old blood — and it did not flee the firelight as wolves do.

It tilted its head, almost politely.

And in a voice like dry leaves and distant thunder, inside his skull and outside it at once, it said:

*You speak. At last, someone on this cursed mountain speaks. Now listen carefully, smuggler — because the ones hunting you are the least of what's coming down out of this winter.*
