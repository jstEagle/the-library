## 6. The Girl Who Sold Yesterday

Lowbridge smelled of wet wool and frying onions and the particular sourness of a market town in late autumn, when the last of the harvest coin had changed hands and everyone was counting what was left. Nix knew the smell the way other children knew their mothers' voices. She had been born into it, more or less — born into a ditch outside the walls, anyway, and carried in before she could walk, and after that the town had been her whole country.

She worked the bridge end of the square, because that was where the crowds compressed. People coming off the river crossing were always half-distracted, still thinking about the water, about whether they'd remembered to bar their doors, about the cold wind coming down off Thornfell with winter behind it like a debt collector. A distracted pocket was an open door.

Nix was small for eleven, which she used. She wore a patched coat two sizes too big and dragged one sleeve in the mud so people looked at her hands instead of her eyes — no, that wasn't right either. So people looked *away* from her hands, because nobody wanted to watch a grubby child's fingers too closely, and while they were looking away her other hand did its work. It was a simple magic trick, the oldest kind: misdirection.

Except it wasn't only misdirection. That was the part nobody in Lowbridge knew. That was the part Nix kept folded up inside herself like a moth in a matchbox.

She could take memories.

Not big ones. Not whole years or whole faces. Just — edges. Shavings. When her fingertips brushed someone's skin, bare skin, for just a heartbeat, she could pull loose a little curl of what they were feeling, what they'd been thinking, and taste it on her tongue like smoke off a snuffed candle. A baker's pride in his morning loaves. A soldier's dread of the road home. An old woman's grief for a dog dead thirty years, which Nix had tasted once by accident and never quite gotten out of her mouth since.

She didn't do it often. Taking cost something — she'd never been sure what, exactly, but afterward there was always a faint wrongness, like a room where a picture had been removed from the wall and the pale rectangle was still there. And if she took too much from one person, she started to lose pieces of her own self to fill the space. She'd learned that lesson at eight, from a drunk carter whose whole head she'd accidentally fallen into. For three days afterward she hadn't known her own name, and when it came back it came back smelling of hay and cheap gin.

So: edges only. Shavings. Enough to know if a mark was worth the risk.

The man came across the bridge just past midday, and Nix marked him before he'd taken ten steps into the square.

He was tall but walked small, shoulders drawn in like he was trying to take up less of the world, which was a stupid way for a man that size to move and therefore interesting. Mountain boots, good ones, resoled twice. A drover's coat gone soft with age. No sword — or none showing — but he moved like someone who knew where blades lived on a body, checking sightlines without seeming to. Behind him came a woman with a sword worn openly and the bearing of somebody who'd once been somebody, and behind *her* — Nix's breath caught — a wolf.

A wolf. In a market square. On a rope lead that the wolf was clearly tolerating rather than obeying, walking at heel like a well-paid actor playing a dog.

*Well,* thought Nix. *That's either very rich or very doomed, and both kinds carry good purses.*

She drifted into the current of the crowd, letting it carry her alongside him, matching his pace the way she'd matched a hundred paces before. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and pine and horse, and under all of it something cleaner, like snow about to fall. His left hand hung loose at his side, gloveless despite the cold — a scarred hand, knuckles split old and healed rough, the hand of someone who worked animals.

Nix liked marks who worked animals. Animal-people were usually soft in the middle somewhere, even when they looked hard.

She let the crowd press her against him — the oldest trick, never hers to invent, older than any guild or gang: *sorry, sorry, sir, the crush, beg pardon* — and her bare fingers brushed the back of his bare hand.

And she fell into him.

It happened sometimes, when the skin contact was clean and the person was carrying something heavy enough. Most people's surface thoughts were puddles. This man's was a flood.

Nix stood in a market square in Lowbridge, and she also stood on a frozen river in the high passes, snow coming down thick as wool, and a man was dying in front of her —

No. He was dying *now*, in the memory, now, his knees buckling on the ice, torn royal livery soaked red down one side, and his hands — his shaking, blue-lipped hands — pressing something into the tall man's grip. Wax. A seal. A map. And the man's mouth moving, frost on his lips, words coming out in a whisper that Nix felt in her own throat as though she were saying them:

*"The king is not the king."*

Then the ice groaned. Then the river took him. Then —

Nix ripped her hand back so hard she staggered into a fishmonger's stall and knocked over a basket of eels.

The eels went everywhere. The fishmonger went everywhere after them, shouting. Nix stood in the middle of the chaos with her heart slamming and the taste of that memory coating her tongue — snow and blood and terror and, underneath it all, the tall man's own feelings as he'd held the dying stranger: not greed, not even fear first, but *grief*. Grief for a man he'd never met, and dread, and a terrible unwilling tenderness, like a man holding something fragile that had been thrown at him.

And under all of that, threaded through everything like a dark wire: *they'll know I have it, they'll know, they'll know.*

Nix fled. She didn't even take the purse.

* * *

She crouched in the shadow of the tithe barn behind the square, hugging her knees, breathing fog, and made herself think.

Here was what she knew, laid out in her head like stolen goods on a blanket:

One. The tall man carried a map sealed with royal wax, given to him by a dying man in royal livery who had said the king was not the king.

Two. The tall man was frightened of it. Frightened in the deep-down way, the way you're frightened of a thing you've decided to keep anyway.

Three. He traveled with a swordswoman who moved like a broken oath and a wolf on a string, and they were heading — she'd seen the edge of it, the direction his feet meant to go — out the north road. Toward Thornfell. Toward Greywatch, if you followed the north road far enough, and everybody knew you didn't follow the north road far enough.

Four. Nobody in Lowbridge knew any of this except her.

Nix pulled her too-big coat tighter and chewed the inside of her cheek.

She should sell it. That was the sensible thing. There were men in Lowbridge who paid for information — not much, but some — and information about a fugitive with a royal seal was worth real money to the right buyer. Crown wardens passed through every few weeks. She'd seen them, grey-cloaked and humorless, asking their flat questions. If wardens were hunting this man — and men with that look in them were always being hunted — then a girl who could say *he's here, he's going north, he travels with a wolf* might eat for a month.

Might eat for a month, and then what?

Then spring, and then nothing, same as always. Same as the ditch she'd been born in and the streets she'd been raised by and the long grey stretch of years ahead in which she would get bigger and slower and less cute and eventually less useful, and then she would be one of the old women who slept under the tithe barn and nobody touched at all, not even to steal from.

Nobody ever touched Nix. That was the thing she couldn't explain to anyone, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. People touched pockets. People touched purses. People grabbed her wrist when they caught her and cuffed her ear and shoved her along. But nobody touched *her* — the inside her, the her that watched and wanted and got lonely in a way that had no bottom to it. Her gift was the cruelest joke the world had ever played on her: she could touch anyone's inside, anyone at all, and no one could ever touch hers.

Except just now, falling into that man's memory, for one strange half-second, she hadn't felt alone. She'd felt *held* — held inside his grief and his dread, wrapped up in it, part of something. It had been awful and warm at the same time, like standing too close to a fire.

She wanted to do it again.

That frightened her more than the wardens did.

* * *

She watched them for the rest of the afternoon, from rooftops and gaps in stalls and once, boldly, from directly behind them in the queue at the chandler's, because the best place to hide is in plain sight of people who don't expect to be followed.

They bought hard bread, dried apples, tallow candles, a flint. The woman — sharp-faced, grey-eyed, with a scar through one eyebrow — counted coins like she distrusted them personally. The man spoke to the chandler's mule.

That was the moment Nix's skin prickled all over.

He didn't speak *to* the mule the way people talk to animals, the empty fond nonsense of *whoa there, boy.* He leaned close, under the noise of the square, and murmured something low and specific, and the mule — which had been balking at the chandler's doorway, ears flat, the way mules do at shadows only they can see — went quiet. Calm. Turned its great stupid head and looked at the man with an expression Nix had never seen on a mule, an expression like *understood.*

And the man glanced around afterward, quick and guilty, to see if anyone had noticed.

*Nobody notices,* Nix thought, hugging her knees on the chandler's roof ridge, wind pulling at her hair. *Nobody ever notices anything. That's my whole living.*

But she had noticed. And now she had two secrets instead of one, and the second one was stranger than the first, and both of them pointed north like compass needles.

The wolf bothered her most. Wolves didn't come down to towns; wolves had sense. This one walked through Lowbridge on a rope with its ears up and its yellow eyes moving over the crowd, cataloguing, and once — only once — its gaze found Nix on her rooftop and stopped there. Held. The wolf looked at her the way no person ever had: as though it could see the inside of her, the watching her, the lonely her, and was deciding what to make of it.

Nix's mouth went dry. She slid back off the ridgeline before the wolf could finish its decision.

* * *

Dusk came down blue and early, the way it did now, the light thinning like broth stretched too far. The north gate would close at full dark. She watched from the wall above the gatehouse as the three of them joined the last trickle of travelers going out — the woman first, scanning, the man leading the mule, the wolf flowing at his heel like a shadow that had learned manners.

The gatewarden barely looked at them. Why would he? A drover, his wife, his dog. The world was full of such things.

Nix sat on the cold stone with her chin on her knees and told herself all the reasons to stay.

She had a corner under the tithe barn. She had a cup hidden in the wall. She had a system, a route, a life — small as a thimble, but hers, fitted to her like the coat wasn't. Going north in the dark after strangers was how orphan girls became dead girls. Everyone knew that. She knew that better than anyone, because she'd watched it happen to Tam Broadfoot, who'd tried to follow a wool merchant to Millhaven, and Tam had been clever, Tam had been quick, and Tam had still come back in a sack.

But.

But the memory was still in her mouth. Snow and blood and a dying man's whisper: *the king is not the king.* But the warmth underneath it, the terrible warm grief of the tall man holding what he'd been given. But the wolf's yellow eyes seeing her, actually seeing her, the inside her, for one held breath.

But nobody in Lowbridge had ever looked at her like that. Nobody in Lowbridge ever would.

"I'm not following them," she said aloud, to hear a voice say it, even if the voice was hers. "I'm just going for a walk. North. At night. In winter."

The voice said it, and neither of them believed it.

She went down the wall by the ivy, the way she'd gone down it a hundred times, dropped into the lane, and trotted for the north gate. The gatewarden was already swinging the leaves shut; she slipped through the gap on the hinge side, the gap every child in Lowbridge knew, and came out on the north road with the dark coming down and the mountains ahead of her black against a sky the color of iron.

Their fire, when she found it, was a mile out, tucked in the lee of a milestone, small and careful. She lay flat in the frozen bracken forty yards off and watched the sparks climb. The woman took first watch. The man fed the mule and then, thinking himself unwatched, put his bare hand flat against the mule's neck and stood there a long time, and the mule leaned into him like a friend.

The wolf turned its head toward the bracken where Nix lay.

Not searching. Not sniffing. Just turning, deliberate, and looking straight at her across forty yards of dark, and holding there, until Nix's heart hammered so loud she was sure it would give her away.

Then the wolf did the strangest thing she had ever seen a creature do. It dipped its head, once, slow — the way a person nods to another person across a room. *I see you. Come or don't. Your choice.*

Nix lay in the bracken until her arms went numb, and the fire burned down, and the stars wheeled slowly over Thornfell, and the choice sat in front of her like a door standing open.

Behind her: Lowbridge, the tithe barn, the thimble life, the bottomless loneliness, safe and grey and forever.

Ahead of her: strangers with secrets, wardens, wolves, a whisper about a king, and a road going north into the teeth of winter, where clever quick girls came back in sacks.

She thought about the dying man's hand pressing the map into a stranger's hands. About how he'd chosen the tall man — or fate had, or the ice had — out of everyone on that river. About how nobody had ever chosen Nix for anything except to be ignored.

She got up. Her legs were stiff and her nose was running and she was, she realized, smiling, which was ridiculous, there was nothing to smile about, and she smiled wider anyway.

"Coming," she whispered to the wolf, to the fire, to nobody. "Don't tell them yet."

And she began to walk north along the dark road, a small shadow behind a small fire, carrying a secret that was starting to feel less like stolen goods and more like a name she hadn't known she had.

Behind her, far back in Lowbridge, a warden's lantern swung through the gate she'd slipped through — grey cloaks, flat questions, a sketch of a tall man with a mule.

They were a day behind her, and closing.
