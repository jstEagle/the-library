## 7. Vess's Ledger

The ledger smelled of tallow and old iron, the way it always did in winter, when the damp crept up through the floorboards of the Wayhouse at Thorn's Foot and got into everything — the paper, the wine, the bones of the building itself. Vess Marrow sat behind her desk with a stub of candle burning low and counted what she knew.

She was good at counting. It was the first thing the guild had ever taught her, before knives, before routes, before the weight of other people's lives: *count what you know, count what you don't, and never let the second column grow larger than the first.*

What she knew:

Bram Eastwick had been with her eleven years. He was quiet as falling snow, steady as a mule on scree, and he had never once lost cargo, a client, or a life on her roads. He asked for no share of the profits beyond his keep and sent half of even that down-mountain to some purpose he never named. He could read weather in the behavior of birds better than any man she'd employed, and she had told herself for a decade that this was simply a talent, the way some men could taste a counterfeit coin or smell a lie.

What she also knew — what she had been trying not to know since the rockslide on the Kettle Stair — was that Bram Eastwick had spoken to a mule. Not *at* it, not murmured comfort into its ear the way teamsters do. Spoken to it. In words. And the mule had *listened*, ears swiveled forward like a soldier receiving orders, and then it had planted itself across the caravan's path and refused to move until the mountain came down where they would have been standing.

She had watched him do it. She had watched him cover it after, smooth as river stone, laughing at himself, blaming the beast's stubbornness, and she had laughed too because that was what you did. But she had seen the look in his eyes afterward — not fear of the rockslide. Fear of *her*.

Vess turned the page of the ledger. The candle guttered and steadied.

The guild law was written in this book in her own hand, copied from her predecessor's hand, copied from hands older than the guild itself. *No secrets from the guild. No gifts kept back. The road belongs to all who walk it, and all who walk it belong to the road.* She had sworn it at nineteen with a knife laid flat against her tongue, and she had believed every word, because she had been nobody then, a dock-rat's daughter with frostbitten feet and nothing in the world but a talent for counting, and the guild had made her into something.

Bram had broken the law. Whatever else his secret was — and she had spent three sleepless nights turning over what it might be, and every answer frightened her worse than the last — he had kept it from her. From *her*. Eleven years of shared fires and shared danger and the particular intimacy of people who trust each other with their lives on bad ground, and he had held this one thing back the whole time.

That was how she framed it, sitting alone with the candle. That was the shape she gave it so she could hold it without cutting herself.

It wasn't curiosity. It wasn't fear. It was justice.

* * *

Captain Ilse Dorn arrived at the Wayhouse two days later, and Vess knew it before the knock came, because the dogs in the yard went silent instead of loud, which was worse.

Crown wardens didn't come to Thorn's Foot in winter. The passes were closing; the high road would be knee-deep in snow within the week; there was nothing up here worth a warden's time except smugglers, and wardens had a long-standing arrangement with smugglers that consisted of looking elsewhere in exchange for first pick of anything interesting that moved. Dorn herself had taken that arrangement twice before, in Vess's memory — once for a shipment of unmarked steel, once for a man whose name Vess had never learned and whose fate she had carefully never asked about.

But Dorn came up the stairs now without waiting to be announced, pushed open the door of the counting room, and stood filling it the way cold fills a room when the door's been left open. Grey cloak, grey eyes, sword worn plain. Snow in the seams of her gloves.

"Guildmaster Marrow."

"Captain." Vess didn't rise. You didn't rise for wardens; it set a precedent. "You're far from your roads."

"My roads are wherever I say they are." Dorn crossed to the window, looked out at the yard, at the mountains going white above the treeline. "I'm looking for a man."

"There are forty men under this roof."

"One of them walked the Kettle Stair four days ago with a caravan of salt-fish and didn't come home. One of them was seen at Miller's Ford yesterday morning, buying oats he didn't need, asking questions he shouldn't. One of them," Dorn said, still watching the window, "was with a dead man in royal livery before the ice took him. A courier. My courier."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Vess kept her face the way she kept her ledgers: balanced, nothing showing in either column.

"I don't know anything about royal couriers."

"No," Dorn agreed. "You know about everything *except* the things that matter. That's the trick of you, Marrow. I've always admired it." She turned from the window at last. Up close her eyes were the color of slate under ice, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road. "I'll pay for him. Name a figure."

"He's my best guide."

"I said name a figure."

"And I said he's my best guide." Vess laced her fingers on the ledger, an old habit, hands doing the work of appearing calm while the rest of her caught up. "Winter's coming in. I've got three runs scheduled through the Wolf's Teeth and no one else who knows the ice-bridges. You'd be asking me to cut my own throat."

Dorn reached into her cloak and set something on the desk between them. A purse. Heavy enough that it landed with a sound like a dropped stone.

"That's the throat-cutting price. There's a second purse for the thing he's carrying. A map, wax-sealed, about so long." She measured it against her forearm. "I want both. The man I want alive — for now. What happens after is crown business, not yours."

Vess looked at the purse and did not touch it. This was the moment. She knew it was the moment the way you know the exact plank on a rope bridge that's going to give.

Because here was the truth she had been circling for two days, the entry she refused to write in either column: she could sell Dorn the route and the description and call it justice, and it would even *be* justice, by guild law, clean and defensible, and none of it would change the fact that somewhere underneath the arithmetic there was a girl of nineteen with a knife against her tongue who had loved Bram Eastwick's silence the way you love a wall that keeps the wind off — and walls, once sold, could not be bought back.

"Bram Eastwick," she said, and heard her own voice go flat and careful, a merchant's voice, "walks the White Road toward Lowbridge. He travels light, moves at night when he can, keeps off the toll-paths. He'll have gone to ground in the shepherd ruins above the ford if the weather turns — there's a dozen of them, he knows them all. Brown hair going early grey at the temples. Carries a long knife he's never once had to use, because—" and here her mouth almost smiled and she let it, because the smile was true and the truth was armor, "—because things on the mountain tend not to bother him. Beasts leave him alone. Always have. I used to think he smelled wrong to them."

Dorn's eyes narrowed a fraction. "Smelled wrong."

"Warden's guess is as good as mine." Vess slid the purse across the desk, finally, and the weight of it left her hand and did not lighten her. "He's headed for Lowbridge. After that, if he's smart, he goes north through the Teeth. If he's not smart, he goes where the map tells him."

"The map goes to Greywatch," Dorn said quietly, and something moved behind her face — quick, buried, like a fish under ice. Something that was not greed. Vess filed it away, the way she filed everything, and hated herself a little for how easily the filing came.

"Then he's not smart," Vess said.

Dorn took the purse back — payment for payment, the description given, the transaction closed — and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. For a moment she looked less like a warden and more like a woman standing in a doorway deciding whether to say a thing, and Vess recognized the look because she was wearing it herself.

"You've known him a long time," Dorn said.

"Eleven years."

"And you're selling him anyway."

"He stole from the guild," Vess said. "Guild property. Guild law. Ask anyone under this roof what happens to thieves."

Dorn considered her for a long moment, and whatever she saw — the flat voice, the balanced face, the ledger lying open between them with its neat columns of everything Vess claimed to know — she apparently decided it was enough. "Greywatch Keep," she said. "If your thief reaches it alive, no one will ever find his body. Remember that you knew that, Marrow."

Then she was gone, boots on the stairs, and the dogs in the yard started up again all at once like a wound closing, and Vess sat alone with her candle and her ledger and the shape of what she'd done.

* * *

She told herself it was justice while she banked the fire.

She told herself it was justice while she checked the strongbox, though there was nothing in it she hadn't counted an hour ago.

She told herself it was justice while she poured a finger of the blackberry brandy she saved for funerals and drank it standing, looking out the window at the pass where the snow was already coming down thick and sideways, erasing the road, erasing the tracks, erasing everything except the fact that somewhere out in that white dark a man who had trusted her was walking north with a dead king's secret in his coat, and she had drawn him on a piece of paper for a woman with a sword.

*Justice.* The word sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

Because here was the thing about Bram Eastwick that she had never written in any ledger: eleven years ago she'd found him half-frozen under an overturned cart on the Salt Road, a boy of maybe seventeen with frostbite in his fingers and no story he was willing to tell, and she'd taken him in the way the guild takes everyone — for use — and he had repaid her by becoming the only person in her employ she had never once had to watch. Everyone else she watched. That was the trade of command: you bought loyalty and you audited it forever. But Bram she had trusted the way you trust the mountain itself, without thinking, without accounting, and now she understood that the trust had been built on a foundation she'd never inspected, and the inspection had come too late, and the mountain might have been hollow all along.

Or — and this thought arrived uninvited, in the small hours, wearing the voice of the girl she had been — or the mountain had been holding *him* up all these years, and he had hidden his one true thing from her not because he was a thief but because he had learned, somewhere before she found him, what happened to people whose one true thing was discovered.

She didn't know which was true. That was the worst of it. Both columns balanced and neither one told her anything, and she was a woman who lived by knowing.

The candle burned down to a blue nub and died. Vess sat in the dark and did the only arithmetic left to her.

If Bram was a traitor, she had done right. If Bram was innocent — if whatever gift he carried was just a gift, a strange and gentle thing, a boy who talked to mules because the world had taught him that talking to people was dangerous — then she had sold a good man to a woman who had promised, in so many words, that he would die at Greywatch.

And either way, she realized, either way, the guilt was already in her. She could feel it taking root the way frost takes root in a wall — invisible at first, patient, working outward from the crack, and one day the whole stone face of her would come away in sheets and everyone would see what was underneath.

She opened the ledger one more time. By the light of the banked coals she could barely see the columns, and that was a mercy. She dipped her pen anyway and wrote, beneath the day's entries, in her small precise hand:

*Bram Eastwick. Sold.*

Then, after a long moment, because she was honest in her books if nowhere else:

*God forgive the price. It wasn't enough.*

She closed the book. Outside, the storm thickened, and down the White Road, in a shepherd's ruin above the frozen ford, a map lay waiting in lamplight — a map that did not sit still under the eye, a map that read its readers back — and none of them yet knew what it would show them, or what it would cost them to look.

But Vess felt it, the way she felt weather in her knees and lies in a client's voice: something turning over in the world's deep pockets. Something waking.

She snuffed the coals and went up to bed, and did not sleep, and in the morning she gave the order that doubled the watches on the northern paths — officially because of the storm, unofficially because she wanted to know, wanted *badly* to know, whether Bram Eastwick would come south again.

And whether, if he did, she would have the courage to warn him —

or the courage to finish what she'd sold.
