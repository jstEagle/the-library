## 9. Winter Closes the Passes

The snow did not fall so much as arrive.

Bram had seen Thornfell winters all his life — the dry whisper of powder on the high shoulders, the fat lazy flakes that came down the valleys like sheep drifting home. This was neither. This was a wall walking. It came off the northern peaks with a sound like a held breath finally let out, and it erased the world in the space of ten heartbeats: the ridge behind them, the pines ahead, the grey seam of sky between. One moment Sera was twenty paces in front, a dark shape against the white; the next she was a rumor.

"Left!" Bram shouted, and the wind took his voice and shredded it. He put his shoulder into Nix's back and shoved her bodily toward the slope where he remembered — prayed he remembered — a shepherd's ruin marked on Vess's old charts. The girl stumbled, caught him, clung. Her hood had blown back and her hair was already crusted white, her lips gone the color of river ice. She wasn't crying. That frightened him more than tears would have. She was just looking at him with the wide, waiting eyes of someone who had learned young that adults decided whether you lived.

"Not far," he lied, because the truth was he could not see his own boots.

Sera materialized out of the white at his elbow like something summoned, snow-packed, half her face raw red. "There's nothing here," she shouted. "Nothing but rock."

"Rock is what we want." He hauled them both along the slope by feel, one hand fisted in each of their cloaks, an absurd little caravan of three. The wind hit them sideways and for a moment the whole world was noise and cold and the taste of iron where he'd bitten his lip. Then his boot struck stone — not scree, but worked stone, a wall's corner — and he nearly wept with relief.

The ruin was worse than a ruin. Half the shepherd's hut had collapsed into its own cellar years ago; what remained was a lean of roof beams and a crescent of wall, and behind it, cut into the hillside itself, the mouth of a cave where generations of shepherds had penned lambs against late storms. The floor was packed earth and ancient straw gone to dust. It smelled of animal and age and, faintly, of safety.

They fell inside it the way drowning men fall into a boat.

* * *

An hour later, with a fire going — Sera's flint, Nix's shaking hands, Bram feeding splinters of the collapsed roof-beam to the flame with the tenderness of a man feeding a newborn — Bram allowed himself to take stock.

Nix sat closest to the fire, wrapped in both her own blanket and Sera's spare cloak, still shivering in great rolling waves. She hadn't spoken since the pass. She kept flexing her fingers, opening and closing them, as if checking they were still hers.

Sera stood at the cave mouth with her sword drawn, watching the white wall where the entrance had been. "If Dorn is behind us," she said, not turning, "she's lost us. No tracker alive follows that."

"And no road out until it breaks," Bram said. "Could be two days. Could be six."

"We have food for three."

"Then we'll be hungry and alive instead of fed and frozen."

She grunted — the oath-keeper's whole vocabulary of agreement — but she came away from the mouth at last and sat, laying the sword across her knees the way other people laid down sleeping children.

It was Nix who heard it first. Bram saw it in her face before he heard anything himself: the girl went rigid, head cocking like a bird's, her strange memory-thief senses reaching out toward something the rest of them couldn't touch.

"There's something dying," she whispered. "Out there. It's — loud. Being hurt is loud, I didn't know that, I never—" She pressed her palms over her ears, which did nothing, because the sound wasn't coming through her ears. "It's thinking about running and it can't run and it keeps trying anyway."

Bram was already moving.

He told himself it was practicality. A wolf carcass outside the cave would draw other things; a wolf's death-scent would hang in the storm for days. He told himself the wind was too bad, the light too poor, that he'd find nothing and come straight back. He told himself all of it while pulling on his cloak, and none of it was true, and he knew it wasn't true, because beneath the practical reasons there was the older reason, the secret one: something was hurting, and he had spent thirty-one years pretending he couldn't hear it, and he was so very tired of pretending.

"Bram—" Sera started.

"Watch her. Ten minutes."

The storm took him like water takes a dropped coin.

He went by feel and by the pull under his breastbone, that wordless compass his gift gave him — not words from beasts, usually, not most of the time, but pressure and direction, the sense of living things the way a man blindfolded feels a candle's heat. The wolf was fifty yards downslope, wedged in a cleft of rock where the wind had carved a pocket, and it was bleeding out from a leg wound that had frozen black around its edges.

It was a big wolf. Bigger than any he'd seen in Thornfell, with fur the color of charcoal and ash mixed, matted now with blood and ice. Its flank heaved. Its amber eye rolled up at him and it gathered itself to lunge, and got nowhere, and made a sound that was half snarl and half something much older and sadder.

"Easy," Bram said.

And then, because there was no one to see, because the storm had erased even witnesses, because the wolf was dying and lies were useless to the dying — he said it the other way. Not with the flat human sounds that meant nothing to anything with four legs, but underneath them, in the low register of the gift, the place where his voice stopped being a man's voice and became weather and warning and welcome all at once.

*I am not meat. I am not your death. I am the warm thing in the storm.*

The wolf stopped struggling.

Its nostrils flared. Its head came up off the rock, slow, trembling with the effort, and it looked at him — really looked, the way no beast had ever quite looked at him before, as if it were doing arithmetic with his face.

*You speak,* it said.

Not pressure. Not image. Words. Actual words, arriving in his mind with a timbre like wind through a burnt forest, low and cracked and unmistakably amused.

Bram sat down in the snow. He would deny this later. But in the moment, thirty-one years of hiding cracked clean through, and he simply sat down in a blizzard and stared at a wolf.

"You talk," he said aloud, stupidly.

*All of us talk,* the wolf said. *Most of you don't listen. You're listening now because you think I'm dying. Kindness at the end. Very human.* The amber eye narrowed. *I'm not dying.*

"You're freezing and you've bled—"

*I know what I've done.* A pause, and when it spoke again some of the brittle humor had gone out of it, leaving something wearier. *I've been running a long time. Longer than you'd believe. And I am tired, small speaker, and my leg is ruined, and if you are going to drag me somewhere warm, I suggest you do it before I change my mind about being dignified.*

Bram laughed. It burst out of him ragged and shocked, steam curling from his mouth, and the wolf watched him laugh with an expression he could only call skeptical.

"What happened to your leg?" he asked, working his arms under the great warm weight of the animal's chest. It was heavier than it looked, dense as a sack of wet gravel, and together they lurched and slid and swore — well, Bram swore; the wolf offered commentary — up the slope toward the cave.

*A trap,* the wolf said. *Iron teeth, set along the high road by men who smell wrong. Wardens. They hunt something in these mountains, and they salt the passes with iron to catch whatever runs ahead of them.* Its breath hitched as the wound dragged over a stone. *I was not what they were hunting. I was simply what ran.*

Inside the cave, Sera had her sword out again and Nix behind her, and both of them stared as Bram half-carried, half-dragged forty pounds of predator through the entrance trailing bloody snow.

"Ash," Bram gasped, by way of explanation.

"That's a wolf," Sera said.

"It's what I called it. On the way up. It seemed fitting." He lowered the animal by the fire, and its coat steamed, and its amber gaze swept the cave — over Sera's blade without a flicker of fear, over Nix with something gentler — and returned to Bram.

*You haven't told them,* the wolf observed. *About me speaking. Or about yourself.*

"No," Bram said aloud, kneeling, working his knife carefully at the frozen mat around the wound. "I haven't."

*Are you going to?*

"I don't know yet."

The wolf made a sound that was almost a shrug. *I was named once, long ago. By a woman who kept bees and spoke to her hives the way you speak to everything. She called me Ash, because of my color and because she said I was what stayed after the burning.* Its eye closed partway as Bram's hands worked. *Ash will serve. It's shorter than my real name, and my real name hurts.*

Behind him, Bram heard Sera sheathe her sword — slowly, deliberately, a decision being made — and sit back down. He heard Nix creep closer across the straw, drawn the way children are drawn to hurt things, until she was sitting cross-legged an arm's length from the wolf's muzzle, staring.

"Its thoughts are gray," Nix said quietly. "Like smoke. Like a burned house where somebody used to be happy." She looked up at Bram. "What is it?"

"It's Ash," Bram said. "And it's staying."

* * *

The wound was ugly but clean, which was more than Bram had dared hope. He flushed it with melted snow and the last of Sera's spirit-wine, packed it with the yarrow he carried for mules' colic, and bound it with strips of his own shirt, because the wolf had growled — a low, conversational growl, more editorial than threatening — at the idea of Nix sacrificing her scarf.

*The leg will mend crooked,* Ash said while he worked. *I'll limp in spring. I've had worse. I once spent eleven years as a story.*

"I'm not going to ask that yet."

*Sensible. It's a long story and your fire is small.*

The fire was indeed small. Their wood was roof-beam splinters and a bundle of sodden pine boughs that smoked more than burned, and the cave's cold came up through the floor like the mountain itself breathing against them. But it was a fire. It threw a circle of orange maybe eight feet across, and inside that circle the four of them arranged themselves with the unconscious choreography of creatures sharing a den: Nix tucked against Ash's uninjured side, her small hand buried in the deep fur of his ruff; Sera across the flames with her sword in reach and her eyes, for the first time since the ford, actually closed; Bram nearest the mouth, because he was the guide and guides sleep facing the door.

Snow sealed the entrance hour by hour. He could hear it — a vast soft sifting, the mountain putting on weight. Somewhere beyond the white, Captain Ilse Dorn might be digging her wardens into some hollow of their own, or dead in a drift, or following a trail the storm had already eaten. Vess might be selling his name in Lowbridge. The king who was not the king sat in Greywatch wearing his crown of hunger, and winter fell on all of it alike, indifferent, enormous.

And here, in a hole in the mountain, a disgraced liar, a child thief, a talking wolf, and a smuggler who spoke to beasts sat around a fire eating the last of the dried plums and passing a single waterskin, and Bram Eastwick realized he was — impossibly, guiltily, gloriously — warm.

"So," Sera said, eyes still shut. "The wolf talks."

Bram's heart stopped, restarted badly.

"I've been listening to you two for an hour," she continued. "You ask it questions. It answers. Your lips barely move and its jaw doesn't move at all, and the girl hears it too, or thinks she does, because she keeps nodding at nothing." She opened her eyes. They were steady and grey and very tired. "I broke my oath protecting a man who lied to me about something smaller than this. So understand me, Eastwick: I'm not asking what you are. I'm asking why you thought I needed protecting from it."

The fire popped. Snow sifted somewhere above. Nix had gone very still against Ash's side, watching Bram with those wide waiting eyes, and Ash — Ash was watching him too, one amber eye open, saying nothing, offering him the silence like an open hand.

"The guild would kill me," Bram said at last. His voice came out rough. "Vess would call it witchcraft, or worse, she'd call it leverage — a man who can turn a caravan's mules, a patrol's horses, a warden's dogs. There's no price on my head worth what my voice would fetch. So I've been quiet my whole life. Quiet in the toll lines. Quiet when the rockslide came and I saved eleven lives and let Vess believe it was luck." He turned his hands palm-up toward the fire. "Quiet is how I've survived. It's the only thing I've ever been good at."

"That's not true," Nix said.

He looked at her.

"You're good at finding people," she said. "At the ford, you found me before I'd even decided to follow you. And you're good at—" she gestured vaguely at all of it, the cave, the fire, the wolf, the storm, "—at making a den. People don't do that. Wolves do that. You do it like it's easy."

"She's right," Sera said. "You collect strays. It's disgusting, honestly. I was supposed to arrest you at the garrison and instead I'm eating plums in a hole with a wolf."

*We are all strays,* Ash said, and this time he said it loudly enough — Bram felt the words go out through the firelight, felt Nix nod, saw Sera's eyes flick toward the wolf and accept, visibly, deliberately, the impossible thing. *That is what a pack is. Not blood. Choice, repeated daily, in the cold.*

Bram swallowed hard. "Her name is Ash," he managed. "She says she earned it."

"Ash," Sera repeated, tasting it. She inclined her head to the wolf, formal as a court salute. "Oath-keeper Sera Vance. Disgraced. Currently in violation of several laws I could name."

*Nix,* the girl said — to the wolf, absurdly, politely, as though introductions at a fireside were the most natural thing in the world. "I steal memories. Mostly by accident lately." Her chin trembled, just once, and she mastered it. "I don't have a family. I mean — I didn't. Before."

*Now you have a wolf with a bad leg, a liar with a good heart, and a broken sword with a woman attached,* Ash said. *Terrible stock. We'll make do.*

Nix laughed — a startled, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years — and burrowed deeper into the charcoal fur, and Ash let her, and Sera pretended not to smile and failed completely, and Bram sat in the orange circle with his strange, impossible, found people and let the warmth soak into bones that had been cold for thirty-one years.

Outside, the blizzard walked on across the passes, sealing the high road, burying the traps, erasing every track of every hunter and hunted alike. The mountain grew heavy with winter. Somewhere south, Lowbridge. Somewhere east, Greywatch, and the crowned thing that wore a king's face and ate his winters one by one.

But that was tomorrow's country. Tonight there was fire, and fur, and the low rumble of Ash's breathing evening out into sleep, and Nix asleep against his ribs with her fist still knotted in his fur, and Sera's sword finally lying flat on the stone, set aside.

Bram banked the coals and lay back against the warm cave wall, and was nearly under when Ash spoke once more, quietly, drowsily, into the edge of his mind:

*Small speaker. When the storm breaks — the wardens' traps run along the high road for miles. Iron and men who smell wrong. But that isn't why I mention it.* A pause, long enough that Bram drifted. *The thing they hunt. The thing that ran ahead of me through these mountains, the thing the traps were truly for.*

*It isn't a beast.*

*And it knows your voice now. It heard you speak to me through the storm. It has been listening ever since.*

Bram's eyes snapped open. Outside, the snow went on falling, soft and endless, and far down the buried pass — faint as a heartbeat under floorboards — something answered.
