## 10. Dorn's Bargain

The trap closed at dusk, in the shadow of the Hanged Oak, where the White Road narrowed between two walls of black ice and the wind sang through the gap like a widow at a graveside.

Captain Ilse Dorn stood in the lee of the eastern crag and watched her wardens close the net with the quiet efficiency she had drilled into them for eleven years. Torchlight ran along the road like spilled honey. The smugglers' caravan — six mules, four men, one woman — bunched and scattered and bunched again, a bead of water trying to climb a slope of glass.

They had no chance. Dorn had made certain of that. She always did.

"Hold your blades," she said, and her voice carried down the defile without effort; it was a voice built for courtyards and battlefields, for being obeyed before it was finished speaking. "Wardens of the fourth rank and above only. Everyone else, hold the road ends."

The smugglers threw down their knives one by one. The mules shuddered and rolled their eyes, and Dorn noticed — filed away without understanding — that they did not shy from the torches as mules ought to, but stood oddly settled, as if something had already spoken to them about surrender and told them it would not be so bad.

The woman was last. She came forward when the torches found her, unhurried, hands open at her sides, and Dorn saw that she was older than the reports had said, with grey coming into her black hair like frost claiming a field, and a scar through one eyebrow, and the kind of face that had done arithmetic with other people's lives for so long it had forgotten how to weep.

"Guild master Vessarin," Dorn said. "Of the Thornfell Company."

"Just Vess." The woman smiled with half her mouth. "Vessarin died somewhere around the second bad winter. You've been hunting her ghost for years, Captain."

"We have been hunting you for years." Dorn stepped closer. Up close there was something in Vess's eyes that unsettled her — not fear, not defiance, but appraisal. The look of a woman reading a ledger and finding the sums strange. "You know what happens now."

"I imagine you'll offer me amnesty," Vess said. "For someone else's head."

Dorn did not answer. That was answer enough.

Vess laughed — a short, dry sound, like a branch breaking under snow. "You see? That's why I never feared you wardens overmuch. You're honest people doing dishonest work. It leaks out of you eventually."

* * *

They made camp in the ruined toll-house below the oak, because the blizzard coming down off the high passes gave them three hours at most, and Dorn did not intend to spend them in the open. Her wardens bound the smugglers' hands and put them in the old cell, all but Vess, whom Dorn kept apart — chained by the ankle to the toll-house hearthstone, which had not warmed in forty years and would not warm tonight either.

Dorn set her own chair across from her prisoner, poured two cups of the black spiced wine she allowed herself once a day on campaign, and pushed one across the stones toward Vess.

"You're not going to drink that," Vess said.

"No."

"Then it's theater."

"It's courtesy." Dorn drank her own. It scalded going down and did nothing against the cold. Outside, the first gusts of the storm arrived and began working at the toll-house roof with patient fingers. "The man whose head I want. Bram Eastwick. Your route-guide."

"My guide." Vess turned her cup slowly without drinking. "And my friend, if you want the word. Though I expect you don't want that word either."

"He took guild property. A map. He took it the night a man died at Miller's Ford, and he has been running since. He is also, as of nine days ago, wanted for the assault on a crown patrol on the White Road. Goats, Captain. He put a herd of goats through a warden patrol and broke Sergeant Halevy's leg."

Something crossed Vess's face at that — not surprise. Recognition, maybe, arriving late. "Goats," she repeated softly, and looked into her cup as though the wine might explain itself. Then her eyes came up, sharp as a gutting knife. "What do you actually want him for?"

"The law wants him."

"I've dealt with the law before," Vess said. "It's usually wearing somebody's face and wanting something else entirely. So I'll ask once more, and then I'll decide how much of you is worth talking to. What does *Ilse Dorn* want with Bram Eastwick?"

The wind rose outside. Somewhere up in the dark, the Hanged Oak groaned — an old tree, dead these thirty years, named for what the first kings of the valley had used it for. Dorn found she could not look at it through the broken window without hearing, faintly, a creak that might have been rope.

She set down her cup.

"Twelve years ago," she said, "I held the king's stirrup at Greywatch. Not the old king. The young one. Aldric, when he was crowned at nineteen, and before the crowning there was a night — do you know about the night?"

"I know stories."

"So did I. Stories." Dorn stared at the fireless hearth. "I was captain of the door-guard the night before his coronation. Six of us in the vault antechamber below Greywatch, and the oath-keepers came down in their grey cloaks carrying a case I was not permitted to see into, and the king went in alone, and the door shut, and I stood at that door for four hours while the candles inside burned in a wind that was not wind. And when Aldric came out—" Her jaw worked. "He came out smiling. And his eyes were wrong. Not cruel. Wrong the way a house is wrong when everyone in it has died and left the lamps burning."

Vess had gone very still.

"I told myself it was nerves. Coronations are heavy things; crowns are heavy things. I told myself that story for twelve years." Dorn's voice stayed level, which was the discipline of a lifetime, and cost more than shouting ever had. "But I served him. I walked beside his horse in the rain and I watched him greet ambassadors and hold babes at naming-days and laugh at feast-jokes, and every year his smile fit worse, like a man wearing his brother's coat. Every winter he grew thinner. Paler. Like something drinking him slowly through a straw no one could see. And the winters themselves got longer, got crueler, got — *hungry*. And I told my story until I couldn't."

"And now?"

"Now the king's own road-wardens hunt a smuggler guide across half the mountains because a dying courier gave him a map, and I am expected not to ask what's on the map." Dorn leaned forward. "I read the dispatches, Vess. I'm not a fool. Someone at court is terrified. Terrified men burn villages to feel warm. I want to know what my king needs before I hand them another corpse to stand on."

* * *

Snow had begun in earnest, fine and hissing, when the memories got ahead of her.

She let them. There was no one here she needed to hide from except herself, and Vess was watching her with the expression of a woman who had spent a life pricing confessions and found this one unexpectedly dear.

The worst of it was not the coronation night. The worst of it was a morning in the third summer, in the palace orchards, when Aldric — twenty-two then, still quick in the body even as the light drained out of him — had caught her arm as she passed and said, quietly, so no one else heard:

*"Ilse. When I was a boy I could remember my mother's voice. I've been trying all week. Isn't that strange?"*

And he had smiled, because he was still capable of smiling, and squeezed her wrist, and said, *"Never mind. Forget I said it,"* and walked on among the apple trees with his shoulders straight and his stride long, the picture of a king, while Dorn stood among the bees with her heart hammering like a prisoner on a door.

She had requested transfer to the road-wardens within the month. Far from court. Far from orchards. Where a soldier's duties were simple: catch thieves, keep the passes, ask nothing.

She had been asking nothing for nine years.

Until a courier in torn royal livery came down out of Thornfell with wardens behind him, and drowned at Miller's Ford with wax sealed under his tongue, and the dispatch that followed spoke of *sedition* and *a map* and *the smuggler who carries it*, and Dorn read the word *sedition* and thought: *no one seditious sends a courier to drown quietly. Sedition shouts. This whispered. Whispers mean doors, and doors mean rooms, and rooms mean whatever is under Greywatch Keep.*

"Here is my offer," she said aloud, and the words tasted like iron filings. "Full amnesty. Yours, your people's, pardons sealed under my name and my rank. Gold enough to winter anywhere in the south. In exchange: you find Bram Eastwick for me. Or tell me where he's going. The map's destination. That's all I need — a place, not a head."

"And if the place is what I think it is?" Vess said.

"Then I need it more than you can know."

Vess studied her for a long time. The storm outside had thickened to a white roar; snow came in through the broken window and lay unmelting on the toll-house floor, and the chains clinked softly as the guild master shifted.

"You're offering to betray your own warrant," Vess said. "You understand that. If your superiors learn what you've promised me—"

"I have been loyal to a warrant for nine years," Dorn said, "to a thing I stopped believing in somewhere around the fifth, and I did it because loyalty is easier than doubt and doubt is easier than action. I am done in order of difficulty. Yes. I am betraying my warrant. I'd like, just once, before I die, to be loyal to something true instead."

The silence stretched. Snow ticked against the shutters. In the old cell across the yard, she could hear her wardens laughing quietly over their dice, ordinary men keeping ordinary watch, and she envied them with a sudden ache.

Then Vess began, very quietly, to laugh too.

"What?"

"You," Vess said. "Gods help me. Nine years I've run the Thornfell routes and the thing I never planned for was meeting an honest woman at the Hanged Oak." She shook her head. "Captain. I'll give you your destination free of charge, because you'll wish you hadn't asked. Greywatch. The map leads to Greywatch Keep. To the vault under it. To the room you stood beside for four hours while the wind blew underground."

The cold that went through Dorn then had nothing to do with the season.

"Bram isn't running *from* the wardens," Vess went on, softer now, watching her the way one watches a wound. "Not really. He's running *to* something. A dying man gave him that map with his last breath, and whatever's in that vault, boy's decided it's his to deal with. He's got a disgraced oath-keeper with him — Sera Vance, yes, the Vance — a wolf that I'm told answers when spoken to, and a girl. And he has something else, Captain, though he thinks nobody knows. Something he keeps so deep down you'd need a shovel of the soul to find it. My mules have loved him for years. My ravens follow him like he owes them money. Beasts talk to Bram Eastwick, and Bram Eastwick talks back, and if you're right about what's under Greywatch—"

"—then a beast-speaker with a map to the cage is either the greatest threat the crown has," Dorn finished, "or the only hope it has left."

"And your superiors," Vess said, "have sent you to bring back his head. Which tells you exactly which one *they* believe."

* * *

Dorn stood at the broken window for a long time after that, letting the snow strike her face, letting it numb her until thinking hurt less.

Amnesty. She had offered it freely, and meant it, and now the meaning had grown teeth. If she brought Bram Eastwick in dead, she delivered the only key to Greywatch into the fire. If she let him run, she was no longer a warden; she was a fugitive with a good coat. And if she went after him — truly went after him, not to take but to follow, to see — then everything she was would end at the edge of this storm, and something unbaptized would walk out of it.

She thought of the king among the apple trees, forgetting his mother's voice.

She thought of the candles burning in a wind that was not wind.

"Your terms are accepted," she said to Vess, without turning. "You'll ride with me. Your people go free at the crossroads with my seal."

"And Bram?"

"That depends on Bram." She turned. "And on what I find when I open the vault door I've been standing beside for twelve years."

Vess smiled her half-smile, but her eyes were tired. "Fair warning, Captain. The last man who looked under that crown came down out of the mountains drowning."

Outside, the blizzard swallowed the road whole. And far to the north, past the white buried miles, a market town called Lowbridge slept beneath the same sky, its lamps guttering one by one — and Dorn, staring into the storm, felt the shape of the next days close around her like a fist, though she could not yet have named the smoke.

She saddled her horse at midnight anyway.

Whatever waited under Greywatch, she would not arrive as a hunter. She had stopped believing in the hunt. What remained to believe in was smaller and harder: a promise made at the Hanged Oak, in the snow, to be loyal to something true.

Even if the truest thing she found was a boy with beasts for friends, standing between the crown and the dark.
