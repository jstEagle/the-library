## 11. Lowbridge Burns

Nix had learned to read a town the way other children learned to read letters, and Lowbridge told her everything she needed to know before they'd walked a hundred paces into it.

It was a market town built on a river that forgot to freeze — a ribbon of black water threading between crooked houses, steam rising off it like breath. Smoke from a hundred chimneys. The smell of tallow and wet wool and frying onions, sharp enough to make her stomach cramp with want. And underneath all of it, threaded through it like wire through cloth: fear.

She'd been tasting fear since the ford. It came off Bram in slow waves whenever he thought no one was watching — not fear of her, not exactly, but fear *for* her, which was worse, because Nix didn't know what to do with it. Nobody had feared *for* her in as long as she could remember. People feared her fingers, quick as they were. They feared what she could take. But this big quiet smuggler with his mule-soft voice kept looking at her like she was something that might break, and Sera did too, in her stiff oath-keeper way, and Nix had decided somewhere on the White Road that she hated it and would follow them until it stopped.

"Stay close," Bram said now, low, pulling his hood forward. "We buy bread, we buy oil, we're gone before the bells."

"You always say that," Nix said. "And then something happens."

"Something usually happens," Sera agreed grimly. She wore her grey cloak turned inside out, oath-keeper's white showing not at all, and her hand never strayed far from the knife at her hip. "Which is why we hurry."

The market square was full and loud and ordinary, and Nix loved it immediately and completely. Stalls under striped canvas. A man selling eels out of a barrel, live ones writhing over his wrists. Children chasing a dog with half a sausage in its mouth. An old woman arguing about the price of turnips with the fury of someone who had been arguing about turnips her whole life and intended to die mid-sentence.

Nix drifted. That was the trick of it — you didn't sneak, sneaking drew eyes. You *drifted*, small and unremarkable, a stray girl nobody wanted to claim, and your hands did their work while your face did nothing at all.

But she wasn't working today. Today she was just walking beside Bram, close enough that his elbow nearly brushed her hair, listening to him haggle for lamp oil with a merchant who clearly thought he was robbing Bram blind and was wrong. And that was when she felt it.

Cold. Sliding down the back of her neck like a finger.

She turned. Across the square, by the well, stood three men in dark cloaks, and beneath the cloaks — she saw it because she'd learned to see the shape of things people hid — mail. Swords worn low. And one of them wore a badge: a tower stamped in grey wax-grey metal, the crown's own mark.

Wardens.

"Nix." Bram's hand came down on her shoulder, gentle but heavy as a beam. He'd seen them too. Of course he had. "Bread. Now. Don't run."

*Don't run* was the hardest instruction in the world, and Nix followed it with her whole body screaming. They walked. The bread stall was at the square's far edge, and every step between here and there stretched like taffy. Behind them, she heard one of the wardens say something to another, and the second warden laugh, and the laugh had nothing funny in it at all.

They bought the bread. Bram counted coins with hands that didn't shake, and Nix marveled at that even while her own palms went damp. Then the bell began to ring.

Not the market bell. The church bell, fast and ragged, the way bells ring when the ringer is running up the stairs rather than climbing them.

"They've posted watchers," Sera said quietly. "On the roads out. Someone talked."

Nobody said Vess's name. Nobody had to. It sat between them like a stone.

"The bridge," Bram said. "South side. If we can cross—"

The first torch arced over the rooftops and landed in the eel-seller's canvas awning.

Nix would remember the sound forever: not the roar, which came later, but the soft *whump* of oiled canvas catching, almost gentle, like a blanket drawn up over a sleeping child. Then the eels were everywhere, silver and frantic across the cobbles, and men were shouting, and the fire found the hay cart parked against the stable wall, and the stable was full of winter fodder, and Lowbridge was made of timber and thatch and two hundred years of dry summers.

"Bram," Nix whispered.

"I know." His face had gone the color of ash. Not fear for himself — she was learning to read him, and what she saw scared her more than the fire did. It was the look of a man watching something he loved start to die. "The map—"

"It's in my coat," Sera said. "Go."

He went. He didn't hesitate, didn't weigh it, didn't do any of the clever calculating things a smuggler should have done. He shoved the bread into a crying child's arms — Nix would think about that later, the absurdity of it, bread pressed into small hands like an answer to a question nobody had asked — and ran straight toward the burning stable where horses screamed behind a door jammed shut with heat-warped wood.

Sera swore in three languages, two of them dead, and went after him.

Nix stood alone in the smoke.

This was the moment, she understood dimly, when a smart person left. The wardens were busy — two of them were hauling a bucket line, actually fighting the fire they'd started, because even crown dogs didn't burn towns for pleasure, or maybe because a burned town couldn't be searched — but the third was coming around the well with his sword drawn, scanning faces, hunting. Hunting *them*. And Nix was small and quick and knew forty ways out of a burning town that a grown man wouldn't fit through.

She took three steps toward the alley. Then she heard the horse.

It was trapped in the stable, along with whatever else lived there, and the door was buckled, and Bram was hauling at it with his bare hands while the roof breathed fire above him, and the horse was screaming in a voice that Nix felt in her teeth. She'd stolen memories from dying animals before, little bright shards of terror, and they were the worst ones, the ones that kept her up at night. This horse was going to die screaming and Bram was going to die with it because he was a fool, a great soft stupid fool who gave bread to strange children—

The warden reached her.

He was young. That was the thing she noticed, the thing she'd keep noticing afterward, in the dark, when she wished she could forget it. Young, with a farmer's broad hands and a pockmarked chin and eyes that darted from face to face in the crowd the way hers did when she was hunting pockets. He grabbed her arm hard enough to bruise and pulled her around to face him.

"Where are they?" he demanded. "The smuggler and the woman. Grey cloaks, a girl with dark hair. You've seen them. Everyone's seen them."

His grip hurt. His breath smelled of cheap beer and fear — real fear, she realized, tasting the edge of it without meaning to, the way you taste blood from a bitten tongue. He was afraid. He'd been riding for days and he was afraid of his captain and afraid of the mountains and afraid, most of all, of failing, because the last boy who failed Captain Dorn had been flogged at the crossroads outside Thornfell and everyone knew it.

"Let go of me," Nix said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"You're her. You're the girl." His eyes went wide and hungry, and Nix watched the arithmetic happen behind them: a captured thief, a reward, a name carried home to some village that probably hadn't fed him properly since harvest. His fingers tightened. "You're coming with—"

She touched his face.

It wasn't a decision. Her hand simply moved, the way it moved toward unguarded purses, and her fingertips found the skin below his eye, and she *pulled*.

Memory-theft was supposed to be small work. A coin purse of recollection, a face, a name, a door code. You took a thread and left the garment standing. But Nix was frightened, and frightened thieves take too much, and the warden's mind opened to her like a door blown wide in a storm, and she fell in.

She got everything.

She got his mother's kitchen, bread rising under a cloth, the smell of yeast and woodsmoke. She got his brother Tom, laughing, missing two teeth, pushing him into a frozen pond. She got the recruiting sergeant's speech and the lie in it that the boy had been too young to hear. She got the flogging at the crossroads, watched from horseback, the victim's screams going on and on. She got Captain Dorn's face — tired, iron-eyed, older than her years — saying *find them, find them, find them.* She got the road south, frost in his beard, his feet wet for three days. She got the fear, all of it, the whole cold river of it, and at the bottom of the river something else, something she only glimpsed before the current tore her loose: doubt. A doubt shaped like a king's face on a coin, looked at too long by firelight.

Then she was back in the burning square, staggering, and the warden was letting go of her arm, swaying, his eyes empty as washed windows.

Because she hadn't taken a thread. She'd taken the garment, the loom, the weaver. She'd taken *today* — the orders, the hunt, the reason he was holding a sword in a burning market town. And memory didn't come back. What was given to her stayed given.

"No," he said softly. He sounded like a child waking somewhere unfamiliar. "No, I was — I'm looking for — why am I—" He looked at the fire. He looked at his sword as if he'd never seen one before. He looked at her, and there was nothing in his face at all, and that nothing was more terrible than anything she'd ever stolen. "Who am I? Who *am* I?"

The stable door burst open. Bram came through it leading two horses and a mule, soot-blackened, coughing, alive, and behind him Sera with her knife out and blood on her sleeve that wasn't hers. The roof groaned and shed embers like orange snow.

"*Run,*" Sera shouted at her.

Nix ran. But she looked back once — she couldn't help it — and saw the young warden standing in the smoke with his sword hanging forgotten from his hand, turning in a slow circle, asking strangers questions they couldn't answer, and the last thing she tasted of him before the distance closed was not anger.

It was relief. He was free, and freedom felt to him like falling, and he was glad.

She carried that out of Lowbridge with everything else.

* * *

They crossed the bridge with the fire at their backs and the town's screams in their ears, and Nix rode double with Sera on a borrowed horse whose true owner would find it grazing in the hills by morning, if it survived. Bram led the mule. The mule, Nix noticed, kept glancing back at the burning town and making a low worried sound, and Bram kept murmuring to it, too quiet for her to catch the words.

They rode until the light changed, until Lowbridge was a smudge of orange on the southern sky, until the road climbed into pine shadow and the sounds of the town faded to nothing. Then Bram stopped in a hollow where a stream cut through the rocks, and swung down, and stood very still with his back to them.

Nix slid off the horse. Her legs shook. Inside her head, the warden's memories lay where she'd dumped them, still warm, still *his*, and they shifted and stirred like coals. She could feel his mother's kitchen. She could smell the bread. Somewhere in the world there was a woman waiting for a son who no longer remembered her, and Nix had done that. She had done that in one frightened second, without deciding, the way she'd done a hundred small thefts, except this one wasn't small and wouldn't ever be small again.

"He'll wander," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange, scraped. "The one I — the young one. He won't remember his orders. He won't remember me. He might not remember his way home."

Bram turned. In the blue dusk his face was unreadable, and then it wasn't; it was tired and sad and kind, which was worse than anger, which would have been easier to carry.

"Are you hurt?" he asked.

That wasn't what she'd expected. She'd expected *what did you do*, or *how could you*, or the question Sera was visibly holding behind her teeth. Instead: *are you hurt*, asked like it mattered, and Nix found that her eyes were stinging and she was furious about it.

"I took too much," she said. "I know I took too much. I was scared and I took too much and now he's — he's *empty*, Bram, he's standing in that fire asking everyone who he is, and I put it in him, I put the emptiness there, and I can't give it back, I can't ever give it back—"

"Hey." He crossed to her and crouched, so their eyes were level, the way you'd approach a spooked animal. She knew that trick. She used it herself. Knowing it didn't make it work less. "Look at me. You were a child cornered by an armed man. Whatever you did, you did to live."

"That's not — that doesn't—"

"No," he agreed quietly. "It doesn't. Nothing makes it clean. I know." Something moved behind his eyes, some old locked room opening a crack. "But listen to me, Nix. There's a difference between a thing you did to survive and a thing you did because you didn't care. I've watched you since the road. You care so much it's eating you alive. That's not nothing. That's the whole difference there is."

She wanted to throw that back at him. She wanted to say *you don't know what I am, you don't know what I've taken before today.* But his face was open and certain, and the warden's mother's kitchen was warm inside her skull, and instead she did the worst possible thing, which was believe him a little.

Sera crouched nearby, cleaning her knife with a strip of linen, and spoke without looking up. "Dorn will have the town's account by dawn. Three fugitives, one fire, one warden addled past questioning. She'll know our direction within a day."

"Then we ride through the night," Bram said.

"We ride through the night," Sera agreed, "and pray the pass holds another day of clear weather, because if the snow closes while Dorn is behind us—"

Behind them, downstream, a branch cracked.

All three of them went still. Bram's hand rose — not to a weapon, but palm-out, flat, the gesture of a man listening to something no one else could hear. His lips moved, shaping silent words. Nix watched his throat work and understood, suddenly and completely, what he was doing, though she'd never say so, though she'd carry that knowledge carefully too, wrapped in wool, the way he carried his secret.

"She says," Bram translated at last, and his voice had gone odd, "that we shouldn't worry. She's only a fox. But she says the men behind us stopped at the stream mouth, and they have dogs, and the dogs lost us at the water."

"Dogs," Sera repeated flatly.

"Lost us at the water," Bram confirmed. "For now." He looked up at the sky, where the first stars were pricking through over the black spine of Thornfell, and Nix saw him doing sums with the clouds the way she did sums with purses. "Half a day," he said softly. "Lowbridge cost us half a day, maybe more. The map wants us north by the shepherd's road, and Dorn is behind us by half a day, and winter is ahead of us by—" he paused, listening to something, or counting something, "—not enough."

Nix hugged her knees. Inside her, the warden's memories had begun to settle, finding corners, making themselves at home. She could already tell which ones would stay sharp: the bread under the cloth. The brother with the missing teeth. The look on a young man's face when everything he was poured out of him into a stranger's fingers, and he smiled, because he didn't know what he'd lost.

*First price of doing right,* she thought bitterly. *Paid in full.*

Above them, far up the mountain, a wolf began to howl — long, low, and wrong somehow, a sound with pain in it, a sound like a question being asked over and over into the dark.

Bram's head came up sharply.

"That wolf," he said slowly, "is dying. And it knows we're here."

Sera stared at him. Nix stared at him. The fox, wherever she'd gone, said nothing at all.

And high on the mountain, the howl came again, closer now, and this time Nix could have sworn — though she'd deny it later, though she'd swear on anything — that it didn't sound like a wolf at all.

It sounded like it was calling *them*. By name.
