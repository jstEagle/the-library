## 13. Greywatch in the Snow

The dead rode with us into Greywatch, and they were better company than most of the living I'd served beside.

Four of them, wrapped in winding-cloth and lashed to the sledge Bram had bought off a grieving widow for three silver and a lie about honoring her husband's memory. They were plague-dead from a village two valleys east — or so our papers claimed — bound for the keep's burning-pits before the deep snow sealed the passes entirely. It was a good story. Corpse-haulers were the one trade no warden wanted to search too closely, and the one cart no soldier volunteered to walk beside.

I walked beside it anyway. Someone had to hold the rope, and Nix had already tried twice to convince Ash that pulling a sledge was beneath his dignity as a wolf.

"It is beneath my dignity," Ash said, low, for my ears alone as we crested the last ridge before the valley.

"Then consider it penance," I murmured back. "You ate half our bacon on the mountain."

"I was keeping it from spoiling."

"You were keeping it from existing."

His breath fogged out of him in a huff that might have been laughter, if wolves laughed. Three weeks ago I would have sworn on every broken oath I owned that there was no such thing as a talking wolf, and here I was, bickering with one like he was a junior keeper under my command. The world had gone strange since the ford. Stranger still since the map began reading us back. But strange, I was learning, was not the same as wrong.

Below us, Greywatch Keep crouched in its valley like something that had grown there rather than been built — black stone furred with frost, towers shouldering up through the falling snow, walls wide enough that the first oath-keepers must have quarried a mountain to make them. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys and flattened against the low clouds. Banners hung from the gatehouse, heavy with ice, the king's sun-and-spear drooping like drowned flowers.

And beneath it all, beneath the smoke and the banners and the little lights of the town huddled against the keep's outer wall like chicks under a hen's wing, I felt it. Or imagined I felt it. A cold that had nothing to do with the season. A wrongness, old and patient, sitting at the bottom of that valley the way a stone sits at the bottom of a well.

"Sera." Bram had come up beside me, quiet as always, mule-quiet. He was looking down at the keep too, and his face had gone the color of the snow. "Do you feel that?"

"So you feel it too." That was a relief and a terror both. If it was only me, it might be my guilt talking — ten years of guilt makes a loud companion. But Bram had never worn an oath, broken or otherwise. Whatever pressed against us from down there pressed on the innocent same as the guilty.

"What is it?" Nix scrambled up the sledge's shaft to stand between us, small and sharp-eyed, her stolen warden's cloak dragging in the snow behind her. She'd barely spoken since Lowbridge. She'd barely slept. When she did sleep, she woke up screaming another man's death, because she'd carried it home with her out of the fire, and none of us had known how to take it from her. "Is it the crown thing? Is it awake?"

"No," Ash said. His hackles were up, a ridge of fur along his spine like a dark blade. "It is not awake. It is *eating*. Can you not hear it? All this way I hoped I was wrong." He turned his head toward the keep, ears flat. "It is very quiet when it feeds. Like a moth. You only hear the wings after."

I looked at the boy who wasn't a boy, the girl who was too old at eleven, the man who spoke to beasts and thought nobody knew, and I said what I'd been avoiding saying since we'd left the shepherd's ruin.

"Once we're inside those walls, there's no walking out the way we came. The gates close at dusk and don't open again till dawn, and dawn's six hours of dark this time of year. Whatever happens in there—" I stopped. Started again. "I broke an oath once. One oath. The only one I ever swore. And it has been ten years of learning exactly how much a person can carry before the carrying breaks them. So before we go down there, I want each of you to look at that keep and decide what you're willing to lose. Because the crown will ask. It asked the king. It'll ask us."

Nobody answered right away. Snow ticked softly against our cloaks. Down in the valley, a bell rang the hour, thin and sweet and utterly ordinary, and somehow that was worse than any omen.

Nix went first, because Nix always went first. "My name," she said. Flat, quick, like ripping off a bandage. "If it wants my name, it can have it. I've been six different girls already. What's one more?" But her hand had found the hem of Bram's sleeve without her seeming to notice, and I pretended not to see, because mercy is mostly pretending not to see.

Bram looked a long time at the keep. Longer than the question deserved, which meant it was exactly as long as the question deserved. "I've spent my whole life being silent," he said finally. "About the one true thing in me. If it takes my silence, let it. Maybe I'll finally learn what my own voice sounds like."

Ash made a sound low in his chest, almost a growl. "It will not have him," he said. "I have decided this. The crown may argue with me if it likes." And there was something in the way he said it — something vast and old under the wolf-shape — that made the hair rise on my arms, and I remembered that he had called himself Ash, ash, and wondered again what the name cost him.

"And you?" Bram asked me. Gentle. He was always gentle; it was the strangest thing about him, gentler than any smuggler had a right to be.

I looked at the keep. At the gatehouse where, even now, I could see the grey cloaks of the watch walking their rounds, and past them, deeper in, the high tower where the king kept his court through the winter.

"My oath," I said. "What's left of it. Which is nothing but the breaking." I tightened my grip on the sledge rope. "Let's go give it back."

* * *

They waved us through the outer gate with barely a glance, exactly as the map had promised. Plague-dead were bad luck to touch and worse luck to delay; the gate sergeant held his lantern high, saw the winding-cloth, and stepped back so fast he nearly fell over his own boots. "Pits are on the east side, past the smithy," he said through his scarf. "Don't stop between here and there. Don't set the sledge down. And gods' grace on you, hauler, for doing work no one else will."

"Gods' grace on you, sergeant," Bram returned, and touched his brow, and rolled the sledge forward, and I marveled — not for the first time — at how easily lies sat on that honest face. Perhaps that was the trick of it. The best liars are the ones who hate doing it.

Inside the walls, Greywatch was a city pretending to be a fortress. We rolled through streets packed with winter: merchants shuttering their stalls against the early dark, children throwing snow at a chained bear some lord had brought for the solstice games, a priest of the Hearthmother ringing her little bell for alms, soldiers everywhere, everywhere, grey-cloaked wardens moving in pairs with the loose, watchful stride of men hunting something they couldn't name. Their eyes slid over our sledge and away, quick and superstitious. Death was our passport. I'd have laughed, if laughing wouldn't have cracked something in me.

We took the turning past the smithy as instructed, and there were the burning-pits — great stone-ringed trenches where the plague-dead went to ash, tended by masked figures who fed the fires and would not meet anyone's eyes. We drew the sledge up beside the nearest pit, and while the tenders were still waving us off, still shouting about contagion, we cut the four shrouded bodies free and left them, and walked away through the smoke with our empty rope coiled like a sleeping snake.

No one followed. No one ever follows a corpse-cart.

"That," Nix whispered, "was the most disgusting thing I've ever done, and I once crawled through a cess-channel to rob a tax collector."

"You robbed a *tax collector*?" Bram said.

"He wasn't using the money."

"*Nix.*"

"He wasn't! He was using it for a second house!" She grinned up at him, and for a moment — just a moment — she looked like a child again, and my chest ached with it.

But then the bells changed.

I heard it before I understood it: the evening bells shifting their pattern, the plain hour-peal giving way to something longer, older, a sequence I knew in my bones because I had once stood in a hall of white stone and learned every bell-song in the kingdom as part of my training. Oath-keepers know the bells the way priests know psalms. This one meant the king was coming out among his people.

"The king doesn't show himself in winter," I said. "Not in the old days. Winter court was closed court. The roads—"

"The roads are closed," Bram finished quietly. "So where else would he be?"

We should have gone to ground. Every instinct I had, every lesson of ten years' hiding, said find a doorway, pull your hood down, become furniture. But Nix was already moving toward the great square ahead, drawn like iron to a lodestone, and Bram was following her, and Ash flowed at their heels, and my feet — traitor feet, oath-feet, feet that had once knelt on cold marble and sworn — carried me after all of them into the open square of Greywatch Keep, where the crowd was gathering thick and silent under the falling snow.

They cleared a space before the great hall's steps. Torches hissed in brackets. Wardens lined the square's edges, facing outward, faces turned from their king.

And then the doors opened, and the King of the Nine Valleys came out to bless his people's winter.

He was not what I expected. I think I expected a monster — something twisted, something with hunger written on it, something you could point to and say *there, that is the thing that wears the crown*. The stories want monsters to look like monsters. It makes the killing simpler.

He looked like a king. Tall, straight-backed, robed in white fur and cloth-of-gold, the Hollow Crown bright on his brow — and it was beautiful, that was the horror of it, a ring of pale metal worked into shapes like frozen flames, catching the torchlight and holding it, holding it too long, the way ice holds a fly. His face was strong and kind and young. Too young. He could not have been more than five-and-twenty, and I remembered — everyone remembered — that the king had reigned nineteen years.

He raised his hands, and the crowd sank to its knees in the snow, hundreds of them, a field of bowed heads, and I went down too because not going down would have been death, and Bram went down, and Nix went down, and Ash lay flat with his ears pinned and a sound in his throat like a river under ice.

"Blessed people of the winter vale," the king said, and his voice carried clear across the square, warm as hearthsmoke. "Long has the snow endured, and long shall we endure with it. Take heart. Take bread. Take each other's hands. The spring belongs to those who hold together through the cold."

A murmur of love went through the crowd. Actual love. An old woman near me wept openly and whispered *bless him, bless him*, and I understood that these people had seen their king perhaps a dozen times in their lives, always like this, distant and golden and kind, and they loved him the way you love a story.

And I was close enough — third row, kneeling, close enough to see — when the king's gaze swept across his kneeling people and passed over us.

For one heartbeat, one single heartbeat between the torch-flare and the next, the warmth went out of his face.

It didn't twist. It didn't snarl. It simply *emptied*, the way a house empties when everyone leaves it — and behind the emptiness, far back, like someone looking out through the wrong end of a spyglass, like a drowning man seen through three feet of river ice, there was a boy. Young-eyed. Terrified. His lips moved, shaping words with no sound behind them, no breath, no voice, and I read them the way you read a whisper in a dream:

*Help me.*

*Help me help me help me.*

Then the torch flared, and the king smiled again, warm as hearthsmoke, and blessed a child held up to him, and moved on down the line of his people, and the Hollow Crown burned on his brow like a ring of captive fire.

I stayed kneeling long after the procession had gone. Long after the crowd rose and dispersed, chattering, warmed by their glimpse of glory. The snow soaked through my knees and I didn't feel it, because everything in me had gone still and cold and *clear*.

Ten years ago I had stood in the Hall of Oaths in the capital and sworn, hand on the white stone, to guard the king's person with my life, my honor, and my truth. And nine years and eleven months ago I had failed — failed so badly, in so ordinary and unforgivable a way, that I had torn the grey cloak from my own shoulders and thrown it in the mud and walked north and never said his name aloud again. Ten years I had told myself the oath was dead. That oaths, once broken, stay broken. That a person could outlive their own worst moment by simply refusing to look at it.

But an oath isn't a cloak. It isn't a thing you wear and can discard. It's a coal banked in the ashes of you, and it needs only one breath.

*Help me.*

He was in there. Under the crown, under the smile, under nineteen eaten winters — the boy-king, the real king, alive, aware, buried in his own body like a voice shouting down a well. And every soul in that square knelt to his jailer and called it love.

"Sera." Bram's hand on my shoulder. I hadn't heard him approach. "Sera, your face."

I looked up at him. My eyes were wet. I hadn't noticed that either.

"I can hear it," I said. My voice came out rusted. "The oath. It's — Bram, it's *singing*. It's been dead ten years and it's singing."

Behind him, Ash stood watching the doors where the king had vanished, and the wolf's eyes were old and grieved. "Now you understand," he said softly. "Why the map chose smugglers and thieves and failures. The loyal are all asleep. Only the fallen remember how to kneel and mean it."

Above the great hall, in the high tower, a light appeared in the king's window. One candle. It stood there a long moment, small and solitary against the dark — and then, deliberate as a signature, it dipped. Rose. Dipped again.

Three times.

Old signal. Keeper's signal. Distress.

Nix saw it too. Her small cold fingers closed around my wrist. "That's not a candle flicker," she breathed. "Candles don't count."

"No," I said, rising, snow sliding from my knees, and felt — for the first time in ten years — the whole of myself arrive in my own body. "They don't."

Somewhere below us, beneath the keep, beneath the vaults, the thing in the crown went on eating, quiet as a moth. But hope had just walked into Greywatch wearing rags and a corpse-cart's stink, and it was kneeling in the snow, and it was not going to leave without him.

"Get me to that tower," I said. "Tonight."
