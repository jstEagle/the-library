## 15. Nix's Price

The vault door had a face.

That was the first thing Nix understood about it, standing in the cold throat of the corridor beneath Greywatch Keep while Sera held the lantern high and Bram pressed his ear to the stone like he was listening for a heartbeat. The door was iron, older than any iron she'd ever seen, black-green with age, and set into its center at the height of a tall man's brow was a face — not carved, not cast, but *grown*, as if the metal had once been soft and someone had pressed a sleeping man into it and let it harden around his grief.

Its eyes were closed. Its mouth was slightly open, mid-word, forever.

"It's a gatekeeper," Ash said. The wolf sat on his haunches in the shadows, breath steaming, amber eyes reflecting the lantern flame like two small suns. "One of the first oath-keepers. They didn't trust locks, so they trusted themselves. A mind poured into the metal to stand watch until the end of things."

"And how do we get past the end of things?" Sera asked. Her voice was flat, but Nix had learned to hear the fear underneath it, the way you could hear water under ice. Sera had been afraid since the moment they'd seen the king mouthing *help me* through his own smiling face, and she hadn't stopped being afraid since, and she carried it the way she carried everything — straight-backed, silent, like a wound she refused to favor.

"You don't," Ash said. "Not with keys. Not with force. The gatekeeper keeps one thing: what happened here. Every memory that touches this door belongs to him now. That's the toll."

Nix felt it before she understood it — a pull, faint as a fisherman's line gone taut somewhere behind her ribs. She turned her head slowly toward the door, toward the sleeping face, and the pull tightened.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, no.*

* * *

She'd been stealing memories since she was six years old, though back then she hadn't known that's what it was. Back then it had just been the way the world worked: she touched people, and pieces of them came away on her fingers like burrs. A baker's memory of his daughter's wedding. A soldier's memory of the last time he'd seen his mother alive. She'd learned quickly which ones were heavy and which were light, which ones a person would miss and which would go unnoticed, and she'd learned to sell the light ones in the market at Lowbridge for coppers and bread, and later, when she got better, for silver and secrets.

She'd never taken a whole person before.

There was a word for it among her kind — the few of her kind, scattered and hunted and mostly dead, because kings did not like thieves who could walk out of your head with your name in their pocket. The word was *wholesale*. It meant reaching into someone and taking not a shard but the whole shelf. Everything. It meant leaving behind a body that breathed and blinked and knew nothing, not even itself.

It also meant risk. Because the hand that reached in had to come back out, and sometimes — this was the story every memory-thief told every memory-thief child, huddled in doorways, in the dark — sometimes the door closed on the wrist. Sometimes the thief took so much of another self into herself that her own self couldn't hold the shape anymore. It sloshed. It diluted. And when she finally gave the stolen memories back or spent them or lost them, she would reach for her own name and find the shelf empty.

"Absolutely not." Sera's voice cracked off the corridor walls. "Find another way."

"There is no other way." Bram hadn't moved from the door. His palm lay flat against the iron, and Nix saw his fingers tremble — not with cold. "I've asked the rats. I've asked the dust-mice in the walls. The vault doesn't open for beasts, doesn't open for blood, doesn't open for oaths. Sera, your oath would burn you to ash on that threshold. Mine too, if I tried to speak it there. There's exactly one key in the world that fits this lock, and it's her."

"She's a child."

"I'm fourteen," Nix said.

"You're a child," Sera said again, and something in her voice made Nix look at her properly. Sera Vance, who had broken her oath and carried the breaking like a stone in her chest for six years. Sera, who had knelt in the snow at the White Road and sworn a new oath over Bram's shaking hands. Sera, who looked at Nix sometimes with an expression Nix had never been able to name until right now, standing in the dark with a door that ate memories breathing against her skin.

Sera looked at her like something worth keeping.

Nobody had ever looked at Nix like that. Not the fence who'd taught her the trade, who'd called her *investment*. Not the market crowds, who'd called her *girl* or *thief* or nothing at all. Not anyone. She had spent eight years being nobody's anything, light-fingered and forgettable, and now here was a disgraced oath-keeper ready to fight a wolf and a smuggler and an iron god rather than let her do the one thing only she could do.

It would have been easy, Nix thought, to let them protect her. To step back. To let the world keep its king and keep its crown and keep its long winter, and let Nix stay small and safe and unspent.

But she had tasted Bram's memory of the dying stranger, back in Lowbridge, by accident — the frozen river, the torn livery, the words *the king is not the king* going down under the ice with the man who spoke them. She had carried that shard for weeks. And she had learned something about carrying other people's moments: some of them you can put down, and some of them put themselves down inside you and grow roots, and then they're yours whether you wanted them or not.

This was hers now. This door. This hour. This price.

"How long?" she asked Ash.

The wolf's ears flattened. "If it goes well? An hour. Perhaps two. You take his keeping, you open the way, you give it back, and you walk out with everything you came in with."

"And if it goes badly?"

Ash said nothing, which was answer enough.

"Nix." Bram turned from the door at last. In the lantern light his face was all hollows and shadow, and his eyes — those quiet, careful eyes that never asked anything of anyone — were wet. "You don't have to buy our way in. We'll find—"

"You won't," Nix said. "You know you won't. The winter finishes falling in three days. Ash said so. The thing under the crown wakes fully, and then it's not just the king who's lost, it's everyone, everyone asleep under the snow forever, and we didn't come all this way — we didn't lose Lowbridge, we didn't lose half a day saving people from the fire, we didn't cross the pass in a blizzard — to stop at a locked door because the lock is scary." She heard her own voice go high and hated it and kept going anyway. "I'm scared. I'm so scared I want to be sick. But I'm the only one who can do this, and if I don't, then I'm just a girl who steals purses, and I've never — I've never been just anything. Not really."

The silence after that was very long. Somewhere far above them, the keep groaned under the weight of the snow, a sound like a great beast turning in its sleep.

Then Sera unbuckled her sword belt and laid it on the ground, and knelt, and held out both hands to Nix, palms up, the way oath-keepers received a vow.

"Then hear my terms," Sera said. "You go in, and you come out. If you forget us — if you forget your own name — we will remind you. Hour by hour, day by day, year by year if it comes to that. I broke one oath already. I will not break a second. Do you hear me, Nix of nowhere?"

*Nix of nowhere.* It should have hurt. Instead it felt like a hand catching hers in the dark.

"I hear you," Nix said, and took the offered hands, and found they were steady, and was surprised to find her own were too.

* * *

The gatekeeper woke when she touched him.

Not with eyes — the iron face stayed sealed, sleeping, its lids smooth as river stones. He woke somewhere deeper, in the place where Nix lived when she worked, the close warm dark behind her own eyes where other people's memories hung like lanterns waiting to be lit. She felt him turn toward her the way a bear turns toward a sound. Old. Enormous. Patient beyond all patience, patient the way mountains are patient.

*Little thief,* he said, without sound, without words really, only pressure and shape. *What do you bring me?*

"Nothing," Nix said aloud, though her lips barely moved; most of her was already elsewhere. "I'm not here to give. I'm here to take."

*No one takes from me.* The pressure deepened. Around her, in the dark behind her eyes, her own memories flickered like candles in wind. Lowbridge burning. Bram's hand pulling her out of the smoke. Sera's voice saying *you're not a tool, you're a person, and persons get choices.* The taste of stolen bread on a winter morning when she was seven and free and hungry and hers. *I am the keeping. All that passes this door passes through me. Show me what you would spend.*

And Nix, who had bargained with fences and cutpurses and once, memorably, a drunk tax collector, understood that this was a bargain too — the oldest kind, the kind where you put your whole hand on the table.

"Everything," she whispered. "I'll trade you everything I am for everything you keep. One hour. Then I take it back."

*And if you cannot?*

"Then keep it," she said. "Keep me. I'd rather be a door than a grave."

For a moment — a heartbeat, a year — the dark behind her eyes went utterly still. Then the gatekeeper laughed, and it was not a cruel sound; it was the sound of a man who had stood watch alone for a thousand years and forgotten what company felt like.

*Bold,* he said. *Come, then, little boldness. Let us see what you are made of.*

He opened himself to her, and Nix fell in.

* * *

She had never taken wholesale. She had never imagined what it would be like. It was not picking a pocket; it was drowning upward. The gatekeeper's keeping poured into her — a thousand years of it, every face that had ever approached this door, every oath spoken in the vault below, the forging of the crown itself, the first oath-keepers with their hands full of fire and sorrow, the thing beneath wearing the young king's face and learning to smile — and Nix took it all, and held it, and felt herself stretch and thin and strain like a net holding a flood.

And the flood took things in exchange. That was the toll working both ways. She felt them go: her mother's face — she'd never known it anyway, but she felt the *not-knowing* deepen, become permanent, become a wall. The smell of Lowbridge before the fire. The color of the sky over Thornfell on the morning they'd crossed the pass. Small things first, then larger, the gatekeeper weighing her like a merchant weighing coin, taking her measure in the only currency she had.

Her name went last.

She felt it leave gently, almost kindly, like a bird lifting off her shoulder. *Nix.* Just — *Nix.* Four letters and a lifetime. And suddenly she was standing in her own skull looking around at shelves that were nearly bare, holding a thousand years of a dead man's vigil, and she could not remember who had come down this corridor with her, or why, or what her hands were for.

There were people. She understood that distantly, the way you understand weather through a window. Two figures in the lamplight, watching her with faces full of something she no longer had a word for. One tall, grey-cloaked, swordless. One slight, dark-haired, with a scarred hand and eyes like a man who listened to things no one else could hear.

They were crying. That seemed important. She wished she knew why.

"The door," said the tall one, and her body — her clever, trained body, moving on eight years of habit with no self behind it — turned and placed both palms against the iron face of the gatekeeper.

*Welcome home,* said the gatekeeper, through her, with her voice.

The iron groaned. Seams that had slept for a thousand years split wide with a sound like a glacier calving. Beyond the opening, stairs descended into a red-gold glow, and the air that rose from below smelled of ash and honey and something vast turning over in its sleep.

The tall one caught her as she swayed. Held her up. Said her name — said *a* name, *Nix*, and the word slid off her like rain off oil.

"Who's Nix?" she asked.

* * *

The hour was the longest hour of several lives.

Bram counted it in heartbeats, sitting against the corridor wall with Nix's head in his lap, her small body cold as the flagstones. Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of stone, Greywatch rang with alarm — Dorn's doing, Bram hoped, Dorn's men buying them their hour, though he couldn't be sure, and couldn't go up to check, because he was not leaving her.

"She'll come back," Ash said, for the fourth time. The wolf lay pressed along Nix's other side, a wall of warm fur. "The gatekeeper made a bargain. His kind keep their bargains. It's the one thing left of them that's still true."

"And if he doesn't?"

"Then we remind her," Ash said. "Every day. As Sera swore. That's what family is, Bram Eastwick. It's the people who remember you when you can't remember yourself."

Sera stood at the threshold of the open vault, sword recovered, watching the stairs, listening to the deep slow breathing of the thing below. She did not speak. She had not spoken since Nix had asked *who's Nix?* in a flat, polite, empty voice. Bram had watched her swallow that question whole and carry it somewhere deep, the way she carried everything.

Forty minutes. Fifty. Bram talked to Nix the whole time, quietly, the way he talked to frightened animals — low, steady, unhurried. He told her about the mule named Buttercup who had saved the caravan. About the crows in the pass and their gossip. About the blizzard, and the cave, and the night Ash first spoke. He told her the story of them, their strange crooked family, in case any of it was landing anywhere, in case any of it was a rope she could climb.

At fifty-eight minutes, her fingers twitched.

At sixty-one minutes, she frowned in her sleep — a real frown, specific, annoyed, *hers* — and muttered: "—told you the blue coat was stupid—"

Bram's breath went out of him like a blade drawn.

At sixty-three minutes, Nix opened her eyes.

She looked up at Bram. At Ash. At Sera, who had crossed the corridor in three strides and dropped to her knees on the stone without a thought for dignity. And Nix blinked, and frowned again, and said:

"Why are you all looking at me like that? And why does my head feel like somebody moved the furniture?"

And Sera Vance, oath-breaker, oath-keeper, began to laugh — a ragged, wet, disbelieving laugh — and pulled the girl up off the stones and held her hard enough to crack ribs, and Nix, bewildered, patted her back with one small hand and mouthed *help* over her shoulder at Bram, and Bram laughed too, helplessly, the sound cracking in the middle.

Below them, down the long stairs, in the red-gold dark, the thing beneath the crown had felt the door open.

It was already climbing.
