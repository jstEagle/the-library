## 16. Captain Dorn's Choice

The vault corridor smelled of cold iron and old prayers.

Ilse Dorn had walked it once before, thirty years ago, as a girl of nineteen with a new grey cloak and a heart full of oaths. Then it had been lit with tallow candles and the low murmur of the king's own voice, warm as a hearth, saying *this is what we keep, and this is why*. Now the torches were dead in their brackets, frost furred the walls like a sickness, and the only sound was her own boots on stone that had forgotten how to be warm.

She had left her patrol at the mouth of the stair. That was the first treason, though she had not let herself name it so. She had told Sergeant Hale to hold the upper gallery, that she would take the corridor alone — standard procedure for a warden captain entering a sealed royal vault, she had said, and Hale, who had served under her for eleven years and trusted her the way dogs trust the hand that feeds them, had saluted and obeyed.

The second treason was the key in her gauntlet, pressed against her palm until its teeth bit crescents into her skin.

The third was still ahead of her. She could feel it waiting in the dark like weather.

* * *

She found them by their breathing.

Four sets of lungs in the black ahead — one shallow and quick, a child's; one steady, a soldier's even after all these years out of uniform; one rough and animal, too deep for any hound; and the last one ragged, frightened, human. The smuggler. Eastwick. The man whose face she had carried in her dispatch case for two hundred miles, sketched from Vess's description: *young, quiet, walks at the front of every line, watches the animals more than the road.*

Dorn lifted her sword. The steel made no sound coming free of the scabbard — she had spent an hour with whetstone and oil the night before, though she could not have said why, except that some part of her had already known this night would come down to a single clean edge between herself and something else.

"Light," she said.

A flint scraped. A hooded lantern bloomed, and there they were: the smuggler with his hands half-raised, palms out, the way men do when they have decided not to fight; the oath-keeper — *Vance*, gods, it was Sera Vance, older and harder but unmistakable, the woman whose disgrace had been whispered through every garrison from here to the coast; the child with wild hair and eyes too old for her small face; and at their feet a wolf the size of a yearling ox, hackles up, amber eyes fixed on Dorn with an attention that felt less like an animal's and more like a judge's.

"Well," Sera Vance said. "Captain Dorn. You've lost weight."

"You've lost everything else." Dorn's voice came out flat and cold, and she was grateful for it. "Sera Vance. Struck from the rolls. Branded a breaker of oaths. And you—" the sword point drifted to Eastwick— "the thief who stole a dead man's map and ran the white road with crown wardens at his heels. Do you know how many men I've buried since Lowbridge? How many townsfolk pulled out of the ash because you four couldn't stay dead like honest fugitives?"

"We didn't burn Lowbridge," the child said, sharp as a fishhook. "Your wardens did. We pulled people out."

"Nix." Vance's hand settled on the girl's shoulder without looking, the way hands do when they have learned each other's geography. "Let me."

"Let you what? Lie to her?" The child's chin came up. "I can smell what she remembers. She remembers the fire. She remembers carrying a boy out of the tannery row with his coat smoking. She just doesn't know why she remembers it kindly."

Dorn's grip tightened on the sword. "What did you say?"

"I steal memories," Nix said, with the flat pride of a child reciting a shameful family trade. "Little ones. Bits. Yours are all tangled up, Captain. Like wool the cat got into. There's a king in them who isn't your king, and there's a king who is, and you keep trying to hang one on the other and it won't fit."

"Enough." The word cracked out of Dorn harder than she meant it to. The wolf's ears flattened. Somewhere far above, frost ticked and groaned in the joints of the keep, the way ice does when it is deciding whether to hold or to fail.

They stood in the lantern light, four strangers and a sword, and Dorn understood suddenly that she had been walking toward this moment since the ford. Since the dead courier with the royal seal frozen to his chest. Since the night she had knelt in the snow beside him and pried the ice from his open eyes and thought, with a certainty that had never left her: *he was running from us. From me. He died more afraid of the crown than of the cold.*

* * *

She had sworn her oath at seventeen, in the courtyard at Greywatch, before the old king — before, though no one had known it then, the thing had finished settling into him like rot settling into timber.

She remembered it differently than the other recruits remembered theirs. They spoke of the crown, the throne, the realm. She had been standing close enough to see the man himself: Aldric, third of his name, a big weathered northerner with flour still on his sleeve because he had wandered into the kitchens that morning to complain about the bread and stayed to argue politics with the baker's wife. He had leaned down from the dais afterward — broken protocol, scandalized the chamberlain — and gripped her shoulder with a hand like a cart-harness and said, quietly, so only she could hear:

"They'll tell you the oath is to the crown, girl. It isn't. Crowns don't bleed. Crowns don't grieve. When the day comes you have to choose, choose the thing that bleeds."

She had thought it a king's whimsy. A joke for a new recruit, the kind of thing old soldiers said to make the young ones laugh instead of faint.

It had taken her thirty years to understand it was a confession. Or perhaps a warning. Perhaps he had already felt the first cold finger of the thing beneath the crown, already known what was coming for him, already begun leaving instructions in whatever cracks it hadn't yet filled — little seeds planted in the minds of anyone young enough and stubborn enough to remember.

She had remembered. Through the years of the king growing strange — the winters lengthening, the eyes going glassy and young, the smiles arriving a half-second late like thunder after lightning — she had remembered. And she had told herself, the way Vess must have told herself things over her ledger, that duty was duty, that the law was the law, that a warden who started picking at the seams of the realm would unravel it entire.

But the seams were unraveling anyway. She could see them now. Everyone could, if they let themselves look. The unnatural winter. The passes sealed since autumn. The villages going dark one by one along the northern roads, and the reports from Greywatch getting thinner and stranger, and the king's proclamations written in a hand that had changed twice in five years without any explanation.

And now: four fugitives in the vault corridor, and the vault door ahead of them unsealed, and the truth of it all sitting in the dark like a patient animal.

* * *

"The courier," Dorn said.

Eastwick blinked. "What?"

"The man at the ford. I reached him twenty minutes after you'd gone. I held him while the ice took him." Her voice was doing something strange, she realized — quieter than she had spoken in years, almost gentle, which was worse than shouting because she could not stop it. "He had a seal on his chain. The privy seal, the true one, not the signet the king uses now. Do you know what that means?"

"It means he was close to the king," Vance said carefully.

"It means he was close enough to be trusted with the one thing the crown cannot afford to have loose." Dorn turned the sword slowly, letting the lantern light run down the blade. "I've spent three months hunting you. I burned my reputation doing it. I told the council you were traitors and thieves, and I believed it, because believing it was easier than asking the question underneath."

"And what's the question?" the child asked.

Dorn looked at her. Small, ragged, fearless, standing slightly in front of the smuggler the way soldiers stand in front of officers they've decided to die for. Something in Dorn's chest turned over, slow and painful, like a wagon wheel in mud.

"*Who gave him the map,*" she said. "That's the question. Because a loyal courier doesn't flee his own wardens. A loyal courier doesn't die with terror on his face. Someone inside those walls wanted this map out of Greywatch, wanted it in hands the crown doesn't control, and chose a smuggler to carry it. Which means someone inside knows what's happening to the king. Which means—" and here her voice finally failed her, and she stood in the frost-lit dark with her sword arm trembling and finished anyway, because she had crossed the last line and there was nothing behind her now but the crossing— "which means the thing I've been protecting isn't the king at all."

The wolf made a sound. Not a growl. Something lower, sadder, almost a word.

Eastwick lowered his hands. "You've seen him," he said. "Haven't you. The king. Up close."

"I've seen him at three audiences." Dorn's jaw ached. She hadn't known she was clenching it. "The first time, six years ago, he asked after my mother. By name. A captain he'd met once, at a border review, and he remembered my mother's name, because that was the kind of man he was." She swallowed. "The second time, two years ago, he smiled at me for eleven seconds. I counted. It was a beautiful smile. It arrived late and it fit him like a mask fits a wall. And the third time—" 

She stopped.

"The third time," Vance prompted softly.

"He wasn't looking at me. He was looking past me, at the door, and his lips were moving. No sound. Just — moving. Over and over." Dorn closed her eyes. Saying it aloud made it real, made it a weight she could no longer set down and pick up later. "Two words. Over and over. I've told myself for two years that I imagined it. That it was a prayer. That it was anything other than what it was."

"*Help me,*" the child said quietly.

Dorn opened her eyes. Nix was watching her with an expression no child should own — the terrible tenderness of someone who has seen inside another person and found the wound exactly where expected.

"Yes," Dorn said.

* * *

The silence that followed was long, and full of frost ticking, and full of the wolf's steady breath, and full of thirty years of a grey cloak worn in service of a lie so large it had become the shape of the world.

Then Ilse Dorn did the thing she had been riding toward since the ford, since the courier's frozen eyes, since a smiling king mouthing two silent words at a closing door.

She reversed her sword.

She held it out, hilt-first, the way a defeated officer surrenders — and then, before Eastwick or anyone could reach for it, she drove it point-down into the stone floor between them, where it stood quivering, a thin bright line of surrender and defiance both, and she went down on one knee on the frozen stones of the vault corridor in full view of a branded oath-breaker, a smuggler, a child thief, and a wolf.

The armor rang against the stone. The sound echoed up the corridor and away, and did not come back.

"What are you doing," Eastwick said. Not a question. Almost a plea.

"My oath," Dorn said. Her knee was freezing and she found she didn't care; the cold felt honest, felt earned, felt like the first true thing she had touched in years. "I swore it to a man who put flour on my cloak and told me crowns don't bleed. Everything I've done since — the hunt, the fires, the warrants — I did for the crown. For the thing that wears him." She drew the key from her gauntlet. Old iron, heavy as sin, worn smooth at the bow by the thumbs of every keeper who had carried it before her. She held it out on her open palm. "This opens the inner vault. It has been in my keeping for eleven years, and in eleven years I never once asked myself what I was keeping. I'm asking now."

Nobody moved. The lantern flame bent in some small draft from the deep places.

"Take it," Dorn said. "And then get out of my way, because I am going to walk into that vault beside you, and when we find whatever is left of my king, I am going to swear the oath again — properly, this time, to the thing that bleeds."

Eastwick looked at Vance. Vance looked at the wolf. The wolf looked at Dorn for a long, measuring moment — and then dipped its great head, once, the way one soldier acknowledges another across a battlefield.

Nix stepped forward and took the key from Dorn's palm. Her fingers were small and cold and shockingly gentle.

"For what it's worth," the child said, "your memories aren't tangled anymore. They're just sad. Sad is fine. Sad means they're lined up straight."

Dorn laughed. It came out rusty, half a sob, entirely involuntary — the first laugh in longer than she could reckon, wrung out of her by a memory-thief child in a frozen corridor beneath a haunted keep. She rose from her knee. Her joints protested. Her grey cloak hung off her like a shed skin.

Behind them, far up the corridor, boots were beginning to echo — her own patrol, coming down the stair, Hale's voice calling her name with the first thread of worry in it.

Dorn picked her sword out of the stone, sheathed it, and turned to face the sound.

"That," she said, "is the rest of my company. Eleven wardens who trust me more than they trust their own mothers." She rolled her shoulders, and when she looked back at the four of them, the hunter was gone from her face and something older remained — something that might, given time and mercy, grow back into a guardian. "So we have about ninety seconds to decide whether I arrest you very loudly while you escape through the vault, or whether I introduce Sergeant Hale to the truth and pray he bleeds loyalty the way I finally have."

Vance grinned — the first grin Dorn had seen on her, sharp and sudden as sunrise through storm cloud. "Captain," she said, "you're going to fit right in with this family."

The boots grew louder. The vault door waited, ancient and iron-banded, and against its lower edge, impossibly, a thin breath of warmth was seeping out into the frozen corridor — the first warmth Greywatch had exhaled in years, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and spring.

Dorn put her hand on the door.

"Whatever's in there," she said quietly, "it's been waiting for us a long time. Let's not keep it any longer."
