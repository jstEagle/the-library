## 18. Every Voice but His Own

The vault beneath Greywatch Keep smelled of cold iron and old prayers.

Bram knelt on the stone floor with the map spread before him, its ink still shifting like water disturbed by wind. Sera stood at his shoulder, her grey cloak — Dorn's grey cloak now, given freely — hanging from her shoulders, and she was speaking her oath aloud for the first time in eleven years. Her voice filled the vault the way water fills a dry well: slowly at first, then all at once, rising up the walls and into the dark.

"I kept it once," she said, and her voice cracked on the words. "I kept it until keeping it broke me. I swear it again — not to the crown, not to the throne, but to the man beneath it. To every man beneath every mask. I will hold the line. I will speak the truth. I will not look away."

The words settled into the stones like seeds into spring earth. Bram felt them take root somewhere beneath his ribs, felt the vault itself lean in to listen.

Above them, muffled by three floors of granite and snow-packed mortar, the court of Greywatch went on with its feast-day revels. Fiddles. Laughter. The hollow young-eyed king smiling his way through another winter of someone else's life. And beneath all of it, so deep that only Bram could feel it, the thing in the crown turning over in its sleep like a great beast dreaming of hunger.

"It knows we're here," Ash said. The wolf lay across the vault's entrance, ears forward, hackles a silver ridge against the torchlight. "It has known since the girl gave up her name at the gate."

"Then it knows what's coming," Bram said.

"It knows *that* something is coming." Ash's amber eyes caught the light. "It does not know *what*. That is your work, beast-speaker. That is why you were brought here."

Bram looked down at his hands. They were shaking, and he let them shake. There was no more hiding in this place, no more silence bought at the cost of everything he loved. He had spent his whole life folding his gift small, tucking it away like contraband, speaking to mules in whispers and crows in riddles, terrified that one open mouth would cost him everything.

And now everything was exactly what it would cost.

"Sera," he said. "When I start — when I give it the voice — I won't be able to stop. You understand? It isn't like talking. It's like bleeding. Once the vein's open—"

"I understand." She knelt beside him, took his face in both hands. Her palms were rough as bark and warm as bread. "I've watched men spend oaths they couldn't take back. Listen to me, Bram Eastwick. Whatever it shows you — whatever it uses against you — it can only show you what you already carry. It cannot make you love anything new. Hold on to that."

"And if I forget you?" His voice came out small. "If I forget Nix, or Vess, or—"

"Then we'll introduce ourselves." Sera smiled, and it was the saddest, fiercest thing he'd ever seen. "Again. As many times as it takes. That's what family is, smuggler. The people who keep finding you."

* * *

The map had told them, in its shifting ink, what the rite required. Not blood. Not gold. Not the king's name or the crown's true word.

A voice the crown had never heard.

For six hundred years, the thing beneath the crown had listened to the same sounds: the liturgy of oath-keepers, the decrees of kings, the prayers of priests, the weeping of the hollowed. Human voices, human tongues, all of them shaped by the same throat, the same grammar of power. The crown had heard every word ever spoken in a royal hall.

But it had never heard the mules.

It had never heard the ravens gossiping on the battlements, or the stable cats complaining about their lot, or the dying hounds in the kennels below, or the mice in the walls, or the horses stamping out their boredom in the prince's own stables. Six hundred years of beasts living and dying inside Greywatch's walls, and the crown had never once heard them speak — because no one had ever given them a voice that could reach the vault.

No one but Bram.

"That's the trick of it," Ash had said, two nights ago, in the cave where they'd first spoken plainly. "The first oath-keepers built the cage out of human words. Every syllable of the binding is a human syllable. So the unbinding has to be something else entirely. Something the cage was never made to hold."

Now Bram understood, and understanding felt like standing at the edge of a cliff in the dark.

He closed his eyes. He reached out — not with his voice, not yet, but with the listening that came before it, the listening he'd been doing since he was four years old and first heard the carthorse tell him its name.

The keep opened around him like a book.

There were the mules in the outer stable, twelve of them, standing hipshot and patient, chewing through the long winter night. He knew their voices. He'd guided some of them over Thornfell passes himself, back when his life was smaller and quieter. *Old Marta*, who feared bridges. *The dun gelding* who loved the taste of pine bark. They were sleepy and unafraid, and when Bram touched the edge of their minds they stirred, curious, the way animals always were when he came calling.

There were the ravens on the west tower, forty strong, roosting shoulder to shoulder against the cold. Ravens remembered faces and grudges and kindnesses, and Bram had fed them dried fish on his way into the keep disguised as a corpse-hauler, and they remembered him for it. They were already watching him. They had been watching him since the gate.

There were the hounds in the kennel below — six of them, old and sick, the hunting pack of some dead lord, kept on out of habit. They lay in straw that needed changing, and their bodies hurt in the ways old bodies hurt, and they were lonely. Gods, they were lonely. No one had spoken kindly to them in years.

And there were the wolves.

Not just Ash. Out in the white forest beyond the walls, the winter packs had drawn close to Greywatch, drawn by the strange wrongness seeping out of the keep like heat from a wound. They circled the outer woods at night, uneasy, hungry, sensing what the humans could not: that something in the heart of the keep was eating the world one winter at a time.

Bram opened his eyes.

"Nix," he said.

The girl looked up from the corner where she sat sharpening her little knife against a whetstone, her stolen memories tucked around her like a coat too big for her. She'd gotten her name back an hour ago — the gatekeeper's memory had finally loosened its grip, and she'd come back to herself sobbing with relief, saying *Bram* and *Sera* over and over like a woman returned from drowning. She was still pale. Still trembling at the edges.

"You don't have to stay for this," Bram told her.

"Yes I do." She sheathed the knife. "You're going to do something stupid and brave. I want to watch. In case you need remembering later."

He laughed — a short, startled sound — and then, because there was nothing left to hide behind, he reached out and took both her hands in his.

"If I forget you," he said, "remind me that you tried to pick my pocket in Lowbridge and got a face full of my worst day instead. Remind me that you screamed when you touched the map. Remind me that you gave up your name to get us down here, and I never thanked you properly."

"You're thanking me now," Nix said. Her chin wobbled. "That's plenty."

* * *

Bram rose to his feet. The vault seemed to tilt around him, or perhaps he tilted within it, and the crown-thing above turned in its sleep and began, very slowly, to wake.

He did not reach for it. He reached past it.

He reached for the mules.

*Old friends,* he said, in the voice that was not a voice, the voice that lived below language, in the place where meaning met instinct. *Old friends, I need you.*

Twelve mule-minds turned toward him like flowers toward sun. Sleepy, patient, willing. Mules had carried the weight of every kingdom ever built, and no king had ever once asked them anything. Bram asked.

*I need your voices. I need you to say with me: this king is not this crown. This crown is not this king. Let him go.*

The mules did not understand the words. They understood Bram — his fear, his hope, his terrible earnestness — and they gave him what he asked for, which was themselves. Twelve rough, honest, stubborn voices rose up inside the vault, and the stones themselves shivered, because the vault had never heard a mule speak, and did not know how to refuse one.

The crown-thing woke fully then.

It came down at Bram like an avalanche of other people's lives.

His mother's face — young, exhausted, turning away from him in a doorway, saying *you'll be fine, you've always been fine on your own* — and the door closing, and the snow coming down between them—

Bram held on.

*This is not mine,* he said to himself, to the mules, to the dark. *This is memory, not truth. She left because leaving was all she knew. This is not mine.*

He reached for the ravens.

*Brothers. Sisters. You know my face. You know I fed you. Lend me your voices — sharp ones, bright ones, the voices of things that see everything and are owned by nothing.*

Forty ravens answered at once, and the sound was like knives being unsheathed in the dark. The vault rang with it. Somewhere far above, the feast-day fiddles stumbled and stopped.

The crown-thing struck again, faster now, desperate: Vess's laugh, warm and low, the laugh from before the ledger, before the betrayal, the laugh of two children sharing stolen plums on a Thornfell hillside — *and you'll never hear it again, boy, you sold that when you lied to her*—

*Not mine,* Bram gasped. *Memory, not truth. She gets to choose who she becomes. So do I.*

He reached for the hounds.

*Old ones. Sick ones. Lonely ones. No one has asked you for anything but obedience in all your lives. I'm asking. Give me your voices — deep ones, grieving ones, the voices of creatures who have loved humans better than humans deserved.*

Six hounds lifted their graying muzzles in their dirty straw and howled, and the howl came down through three floors of stone and into the vault like a funeral bell, and the crown-thing *screamed*.

It was not a sound. It was an absence of sound, a hole torn in the air, and through the hole poured everything the thing had eaten: winters, names, faces, the king's whole life devoured year by year, and it hurled all of it at Bram like a fistful of knives.

His mother's door again. Vess's laugh again. Sera walking away from him down a corridor, saying *I never knew you, smuggler.* Nix's small hand slipping out of his. Ash turning wolf-wild and forgetting him forever—

*NOT MINE,* Bram roared, in every voice he had, in the mule-voice and the raven-voice and the hound-voice and his own poor human throat, *NONE OF IT IS MINE, AND NONE OF IT IS YOURS EITHER —*

And he reached, last of all, for the wolves.

They were waiting. He had known they would be. The winter packs at the forest's edge, thirty strong, and Ash rising to her feet beside him with her head thrown back, and together — beast-speaker and beast, the line between them dissolved now, burned away, spent gloriously and without regret — they called.

The vault shook. Snow fell from the ceiling in silver curtains. Above them, the whole keep woke screaming — horses screaming, dogs baying, ravens bursting off the towers in a black storm, mules kicking their stalls to splinters — and through it all, through every animal voice in Greywatch and the wild white world beyond it, ran one single sentence, spoken in a tongue older than crowns:

*This king is not this crown. This crown is not this king. LET HIM GO.*

The crown-thing made its last stand in Bram's own memory. It showed him the ford, the dying stranger, the map pressed into his hands — and then it showed him the stranger walking away, alive, the whole thing undone, the inheritance refused, the quiet life continuing, safe and silent and small—

And Bram, bleeding out his gift into the dark like a man pouring out his own heart's blood, looked at that false mercy and said, with the last of his voice, in his own plain human words:

"No. I choose this. I choose them. Take whatever you want of me — but let. Him. Go."

The crown shattered.

The sound was not loud. It was the sound of a held breath released after six hundred years, and it went through the keep like dawn going through a valley, and every creature in Greywatch fell silent at once — mules, ravens, hounds, wolves, the girl, the oath-keeper, the smuggler on his knees in the dark with his gift pouring out of him like light through a cracked jar.

And in the sudden ringing silence, Bram heard footsteps on the stairs above.

Slow. Unsteady. Human.

Coming down.

Sera moved to meet them, sword half-drawn, and Ash's hackles rose, and Nix grabbed Bram's arm — and then the door at the top of the vault stairs swung open, spilling torchlight down into the dark, and a figure stood silhouetted against it.

Young-eyed. Hollow-cheeked. Wearing a nightshirt and no crown at all, his bare head shining in the torchlight like an egg, his face empty of everything except a terrible, dawning, wondering confusion —

—and his lips moving, shaping, over and over, the same two words, the words the crown had never let him say in six hundred years:

*"Help me. Help me. Help me."*

Bram, kneeling in the wreckage of his own spent gift, looked up at the king of the realm and found, to his horror and his wonder, that he had no voice left at all.
