## 20. What Bram Forgot

Nix had learned early that grief had a smell.

Her mother's grief, in the last winter before the fever took her, had smelled of tallow and wet wool and the sour edge of gone-off ale. The grief of the men in the Lowbridge alleyways smelled of smoke and old blood. The grief of the warden she'd robbed on the burning road had smelled of scorched hair and terror, and she still carried it folded up inside her like a knife she couldn't put down.

Bram's grief, three days after the crown shattered, smelled of snowmelt and woodsmoke and something underneath, something faint and green, like the first breath of a thaw. Like spring trying to happen inside a man who didn't know yet what he'd lost.

She smelled it the morning they came down from the vault, and she'd been tracking it ever since.

They were camped in the gatehouse of Greywatch now, in a room with a real hearth and real beds, because the restored king had insisted and Sera had not known how to refuse a king who kept looking at her like she'd hung the stars back in their sockets. The unnatural winter was breaking outside the windows in great groaning slabs, the ice on the battlements cracking and sliding, and the whole keep rang with it like a ship coming apart in a gentle sea. Nix sat on the windowsill with her knees drawn up, watching Bram across the room, and did what she was best at.

She watched. And she counted what was missing.

* * *

It was small things at first, and everyone pretended they weren't.

He'd stood in the great hall the day after, when the king — Aldric, he had a name again, Aldric — had thanked them all with a voice that cracked like river ice, and Vess had come through the doors under a truce-banner, ledger under her arm and her jaw set like a woman walking to her own hanging. And Bram had looked at her, and Nix had seen it happen.

He'd looked at her the way you look at someone you've been told matters.

"You're the guild master," he'd said. Polite. Careful. The voice he used for strangers on the road.

Vess had stopped like she'd walked into a door. Nix had watched her face do three things in the space of a heartbeat — confusion, hurt, and then a terrible understanding, the kind that arrives whole and cannot be argued with. "Bram," she'd said, and her voice had come out strange, "it's Vess."

"I know." He'd smiled, and it had been a kind smile, a good one, and it had been aimed at a stranger. "Sera's told me. What you did. Turning on Dorn's patrol, at the shrine. That was — I'm told you were like a sister to me."

*Like a sister to me.* Nix had felt those words go through the room like an arrow through wet canvas.

Vess had stood there a long moment. Then she'd laughed — one short, broken sound — and said, "Yes. Something like that," and turned and walked back out into the snow, and Nix had followed her, because Nix was a thief and thieves know when something valuable has been dropped.

She'd found Vess in the outer courtyard, standing with her bare hands in the snow, and Vess had said, without turning, "The rite took payment. That's how it works, isn't it. That's what the old stories say."

"I don't know the old stories," Nix had said.

"No," Vess had said. "You wouldn't. You're too young for the old stories and too old for the new ones. That's the worst age to be." She'd looked up at the breaking ice on the walls. "He used to laugh at my ledger. Every year at midwinter I'd balance the guild's books and he'd steal my pen and draw a mule in the margin wearing my hat. Every year. Eleven years." Her voice hadn't wavered at all, which was how Nix knew it was taking everything she had. "He doesn't remember the mule."

Nix hadn't said anything. There was nothing to say that wasn't a confession.

* * *

Here is what Nix had never told any of them, in the weeks since Lowbridge, since the fire, since she'd followed a beast-speaker and a broken oath-keeper out of town because a stolen scrap of memory had tasted like a door she needed to walk through:

She had taken more from Bram than he knew.

It had happened the second night, in the shepherd's ruin, when the map was reading them back and the whole world had gone sideways with horror. He'd slept by the fire like the rest of them, exhausted, and she'd crept close the way she crept close to everyone, because sleeping people leak, and a memory-thief who doesn't eat doesn't last long, and Bram's dreams had smelled like bread.

She'd only meant to take a little. A taste. That was the rule she'd made herself after the warden, after she'd carried his dying home inside her skull for a week: a taste, never a meal. But Bram's dreams had been deep that night, deep and warm and full of a woman's voice singing low in a language Nix didn't know, and Nix had been so hungry, and the shard had come away whole before she'd understood what she was holding.

A memory of his mother.

Not a story about her. Not a description. The thing itself — the actual texture of it, the way her hands had smelled of rosemary and tallow soap, the exact pitch of her humming, the specific gold-brown of her eyes with the firelight caught in them, the weight of being four years old and held. Bram couldn't have described it if he'd tried; nobody can describe the inside of their own earliest memories. But Nix had it. She had carried it for weeks like a coal wrapped in cloth, taking it out in the dark sometimes, just to feel how warm a person could be to someone. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever stolen.

And she had never given anything back. Not once, not ever, not to anyone. That was the other rule, the one underneath all her rules: what's taken is kept, because kept things are the only things that are yours.

She sat on the windowsill and turned the shard over in her mind for the hundredth time, and outside the ice groaned and slid, and down in the hall Bram laughed at something Sera said, and the laugh was wrong. Not the sound of it — the sound was fine, it was a good laugh — but Nix had lived in the man's head for one night and she knew the difference between a laugh that comes from the belly and a laugh that comes from the memory of how laughing goes. He was performing himself. He was a very good performer. He'd had years of practice, hiding a voice.

But Nix knew what was under performances. Nix lived under them.

* * *

She found him at the mews at dusk.

He went there every evening now; she'd tracked the habit the way she tracked everything. The keep's falcons had been half-starved through the enchanted winter, and Bram spent an hour each night feeding them and talking to them — not his gift, not the deep speech, just talking, the way people talk to animals when they've forgotten that animals can't answer. Except they did answer him. They always had. The falcons sidled along their perches and made small sounds at him, and he made small sounds back, and it was the only time his shoulders came down from around his ears.

Nix climbed the ladder and sat on the trapdoor edge, legs dangling into the straw-smelling dark, and watched him feed a gyrfalcon strips of hare.

"Ash says you haven't slept," she said.

Bram didn't startle. He'd stopped startling at her a long time ago. "Ash says a lot of things."

"Ash says you stand at the window at night looking at the mountains." She swung her feet. "Ash says you're trying to remember why they make you sad, and you can't, and that's worse than if you could."

The gyrfalcon took the last strip and Bram wiped his fingers slowly on his coat. "Your wolf," he said, "talks too much."

"She says you say that every time, and every time it means you don't want to talk about it, and every time she lets you not talk about it, and she's getting tired of it."

That got a real almost-laugh out of him, small and surprised. Then it faded, and he looked up at her in the gloom, and his face did the thing it did now when it went careful. "Nix."

"You don't remember your mother," Nix said.

It came out flat, the way she said everything that mattered, because if she put any feeling in it she'd have to feel it. Bram went very still. Somewhere below, a falcon shifted on its perch.

"I remember I had one," he said, after a while. "I remember — everyone has one. I remember she must have..." He stopped. Nix watched him reach for it and watched him come up with nothing, the way you reach for a stair in the dark that isn't there. "There's a shape where it should be. Like a room I know I've been in. I know the door. I can't find the handle."

"The rite," Nix said. "It took it. Sera said it would take something, and it took the biggest thing you had."

"It took what the crown offered to trade," Bram said, and his voice was level, and Nix hated how level it was. "It showed me my mother leaving. That's what it fought with. So when I gave the unbinding my voice — my memory of her went into the scale. Fair trade, Sera says. The crown's oldest stolen memory for mine." He shrugged, a small terrible shrug. "I never knew her anyway. I was four. It's not — it's not like losing something I used."

"You used it every day," Nix said. "You just didn't know that's what it was."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the question move behind his eyes — how would you know — and she made her choice the way she made all her choices, fast, before the careful part of her could catch up and talk her out of it.

She climbed down into the mews.

* * *

The falcons watched. Nix was aware of them the whole time, a row of round bright eyes in the dark, and she thought later that maybe they'd known what she was about to do, and had kept still on purpose, the way beasts keep still around births.

"I have to tell you something," she said, "and you're going to be angry, and I need you to be angry after. Not during."

Bram set down the feeding bowl. "Nix—"

"I steal memories. Not just pockets. Not just coin. I take — pieces of people. The inside of them. It's how I've stayed alive since I was six." The words came out fast and flat, a pickpocket's patter, her hands moving even though they held nothing. "I took from you. The second night, at the ruin. You were asleep and I was hungry and I only meant a taste and I took — I took a whole piece. And I kept it. I kept it for weeks and I never gave anything back, ever, that's not what thieves do, we don't give back, giving back means it was never really yours—"

Her voice cracked. She hated that. She kept going anyway.

"And now you've lost it for real, and it's sitting in me, and I've been sitting on it for three days trying to make myself be the person who keeps it, because keeping is all I know how to do." She pulled the memory forward, up from the deep place where she kept her hoard, and held it out — not with her hands, with whatever it was inside her that did the taking, offered in the only direction she'd never offered anything. "So here. Take it back. It was always yours. I was just — I was just holding it warm for you."

Bram had gone white. "Nix. You don't have to—"

"I know I don't have to." And here was the thing, the thing that surprised her most, the thing she'd carry away from this dusk in the mews for the rest of her life: she wanted to. The hoard in her chest, the hoard she'd guarded since she was six, felt this one piece like a splinter. Not because it wasn't beautiful. Because it had never been hers, and she was so tired, so bone-tired, of owning things that had been taken instead of given. "I want to. That's the whole — that's the whole horrible miracle of it. I want to."

She gave it back.

She didn't know how to describe what it was like, the giving. Taking was easy; taking was a door opening and a thing sliding out. Giving was like being the door. It hurt in a way that wasn't damage, exactly — more like a bone setting, the pain of something going back where it belonged. She felt the shard leave her, felt the shape of its absence bloom and then, strangely, start to heal over, and she heard herself make a small sound, and Bram caught her arm as she swayed.

And then she watched it land in him.

* * *

She would remember his face for the rest of her life. She was a memory-thief; she knew the worth of what she kept.

He made a sound like a man surfacing from deep water. His knees went and he sat down hard in the straw, and the gyrfalcon flared its wings and resettled, and Bram Eastwick, the man who had unbound a king with every voice in Greywatch but his own, put his face in his hands and wept.

"Rosemary," he said, into his hands. "Her hands smelled of rosemary. And she — there was a song, she used to sing, it wasn't words, it was just — " He hummed three notes, low and wavering, and the falcons all went silent, every one of them, heads cocked, and Nix realized they were hearing it too, the ghost of a dead woman's humming carried in a living man's throat. "She held me. I was — the fire was low and it was cold outside and she held me and I wasn't afraid of anything. I wasn't afraid of *anything*, Nix. I'd forgotten you could be like that."

"I know," Nix said. Her own eyes were burning. "I've had it in my head for weeks. I know exactly what it's like."

He looked up at her then, face wet, straw in his hair, and he laughed — a real one, from the belly, the first one she'd heard from him since the vault — and it broke in the middle into another sob, and he reached out and pulled her in, and she let him, stiff and astonished, because nobody held Nix, nobody had held Nix since the winter her mother died, and it turned out that being held was exactly like the memory she'd carried. Warm. The fire low and the cold outside and nothing to be afraid of, just for a moment, just for as long as it lasted.

"I should be angry," Bram said into her hair.

"You can be angry after," Nix said, her face mashed against his shoulder. "I told you the rules."

"I'm not going to be angry." He pulled back and held her at arm's length, and his eyes were still wet and fierce. "You kept her safe. All these years she's been gone, some piece of her was — you kept her warm. You said that. You kept her warm for me." His voice roughened. "That's not thieving, Nix. That's the opposite."

"It's the only thing I've ever given back," Nix said. "Don't make it a big thing. I'll die of embarrassment and then you'll have two ghosts."

But it was a big thing, and they both knew it, and the falcons knew it, rustling on their perches with soft murmuring sounds, and when they came down from the mews the sky over Greywatch was doing something it hadn't done in a year — going gold at the edges, the last of the broken winter burning off like mist.

* * *

There was one more thing, and Nix almost didn't do it, and then she did, because the evening had already broken every rule she had and it seemed a shame to stop at eleven.

She found Vess in the courtyard where the truce-banner still hung, and she stood in front of the guild master with her hands in her pockets, and she said: "He can't remember your laugh. Or the mule in the ledger. The rite took it. I watched you figure it out, and I'm sorry, and I can't give that back, because I never stole it. I only steal the beautiful things."

Vess looked down at her for a long moment. The ice on the battlements groaned and let go in a long silver sheet, and the evening light came through it gold.

"You're a strange child," Vess said at last.

"I'm a thief," Nix said. "It's the same thing."

And Vess laughed — one short sound, the broken kind — and then, to Nix's astonishment, she crouched down, so their eyes were level, and said quietly: "Then listen to me, thief. Tell him the mule wore my hat. Tell him I laughed so hard I couldn't finish the accounts, and I made him do them instead, and he was terrible at it, and that's why I never let him near the guild's books again. Tell him he owes me eleven years of midwinter ledgers." Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not. "Tell him I'm keeping count."

"I'll tell him," Nix said.

"And Nix." Vess stood, and pulled her cloak around her, and looked out at the gold-edged sky where spring was winning. "The beautiful things you steal. Take fewer. Give more. You'll live longer, and it'll hurt worse, and that's how you'll know you're doing it right."

Nix thought about that all the way back across the courtyard, and all the way up the stairs, and she was still thinking it when she pushed open the door to the hall and found it full of firelight and noise — Sera and Dorn bent over a table covered in oath-keeper sigils, Ash sprawled enormous and warm across the hearthstones, and Bram in the middle of it, holding a cup of something hot, laughing at something the wolf had said.

He looked up and saw her, and his face did the new thing, the whole thing, the face of a man with his mother's song back in his throat.

"There she is," he said. "Come here. Ash is telling the story of the goats again, and she's lying about how many there were."

"There were forty goats," Ash said with dignity, "and I have never lied in my life."

"There were nine goats," Nix said, dropping onto the bench beside Bram, into the warmth, into the strange new weight of being someone who gives things back. "I was there. I steal memories, wolf, not goats."

And Bram laughed, and outside the last ice came down from the towers of Greywatch like a bell being unbound, and far to the south, past the passes, past the thawing Thornfell, the road went on — and Nix, who had spent her whole life taking, fell asleep that night with empty hands and a full chest, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of nothing she had stolen.

Only of gold light on snow, and a door standing open, and someone — tomorrow, maybe, or the day after — walking through it toward them with news from the king.
