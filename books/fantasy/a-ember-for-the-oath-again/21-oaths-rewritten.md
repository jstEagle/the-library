## 21. Oaths Rewritten

The great hall of Greywatch Keep had never, in Sera Vance's memory of it — and she had no memory of it, only the stories oath-keepers told over dying fires — been warm. It was built to be cold. Cold kept men honest, the first masters said; cold reminded them that oaths were made against the dark, not in spite of it.

But on the third morning after the crown shattered, someone had lit every hearth in the hall, and the old stones drank the heat like thirsty ground drinking snowmelt, and Sera stood beneath the high arched windows watching light fall through ice-feathered glass onto a king who could not stop touching his own face.

He was thinner than the coin-portraits. Younger, too — the thing beneath the crown had worn him down to a scaffolding of bone and duty, and now that it was ash on the wind, what remained looked startled to be alive. He kept pressing his palm to his cheek as if checking whether the face were still his own. Sometimes he laughed, short and broken, at nothing anyone else could see. A servant had told her, hushed, that His Majesty had wept for an hour over a bowl of plums, because he could not remember the last time he had tasted anything at all.

Sera knew that hunger. She had spent six years in a border garrison cell learning exactly how much of a person could be eaten by an institution before the institution noticed anything missing.

"You're staring, Keeper."

Dorn stood beside her, arms crossed, grey cloak gone from her shoulders since dawn — surrendered formally, folded on the king's table like a shed skin. Without it she looked broader somehow, more herself, a woman built of stubbornness and old grief rather than rank.

"I'm not a keeper," Sera said. "I'm a disgraced one. There's a difference. The difference is a cell."

"The cell's empty." Dorn nodded toward the far end of the hall, where Bram Eastwick sat with his strange found family: the wolf Ash sprawled enormous across three flagstones like a rug that had opinions, Nix cross-legged on the wolf's back with a plate of honeyed bread she had absolutely stolen from somewhere, and Vess — Vess, of all people, arrived under truce two days past with forty smugglers camped politely outside the walls — standing over Bram with her arms folded and her jaw tight, mid-argument about something that was clearly twenty years old.

"They're going to pardon him," Dorn said. "All of them. Every name in the guild ledgers east of Thornfell."

"Kings don't pardon smugglers."

"This one will. This one owes a debt no treasury can hold." Dorn's voice went quiet. "I served his father. I served *him*, before—" Her hand rose halfway to her throat, where the grey cloak's clasp used to sit, and dropped again. "Before I understood what I was serving. When he knelt in that corridor and gave me his key, Sera, do you know what he said? Not *spare me*. Not *I am your king*. He said, *I remember my mother's hands*. That's all. That's what the thing took last, and that's what came back first."

Sera watched the king laugh again at nothing, and press his hand to his cheek.

"And you?" she asked. "What happens to Captain Ilse Dorn, who hunted us across half the White Road?"

"That's being decided." Dorn's mouth twisted. "By me. Which is the strangest part. No tribunal. No warden-marshal. Just a man with a new face asking me what I want." She exhaled slowly, breath fogging even in the warmed hall. "I've been law for nineteen years, Vance. Law is a wall. You stand on one side or the other. This morning there's no side. There's just the question."

*What do you want.* Sera turned the phrase over and found it rusted with disuse. What did she want? For six years she had wanted her oath back, the way a starving woman wants bread — simply, totally, without imagination. Now the king's herald had come to her cell — her *old* cell, they'd given her a room and she'd slept on the floor anyway — and said His Majesty requested the presence of the oath-keeper Vance at his convenience, and the word *keeper* had landed on her like a mantle thrown from a great height.

She hadn't earned it back. She'd broken her oath to save a seal and a stranger, and then she'd broken half the laws of the realm besides, and none of it had been clean. But maybe that was the lesson of the whole rotten winter: clean wasn't real. Clean was what institutions promised so you wouldn't notice they'd been hollow for years, wearing a crown that ate their king one winter at a time.

Trumpets sounded at the hall doors. Badly. Someone — she suspected Nix had taught them — had shown the trumpeter how to make the note bend upward at the end like a question.

* * *

The king held court standing, which no king had done in living memory, because kings held court seated so that everyone else's knees would ache before theirs did. He stood at the head of the hall in plain wool, no circlet, no crown — the shards of the Hollow Crown lay in a lead-lined box in the vault below, and Sera had heard him order them melted for nails, *nails*, as if divinity were carpentry — and he spoke in a voice that cracked on the second sentence and steadied by the fifth.

"I have lost eleven years," he said. "I am told this. I do not remember them. I remember my mother's hands, and I remember falling asleep, and I remember waking three days ago with ash in my hair and strangers holding me up." His gaze found Bram, and held. "Strangers who broke laws I am told are good laws, in service of a truth my own wardens were forbidden to seek. So today the laws and I are going to have a conversation. And the conversation begins with pardons."

He named them one by one. Bram Eastwick, beast-speaker, whose gift was declared no crime but a *trust*, to be registered with — Sera nearly laughed — whatever office she ended up building. Nix of nowhere, memory-thief, whose crimes were "commuted to restitution," at which the girl on the wolf's back shouted "*Restitution of WHAT*" and had to be shushed by a wolf. Ilse Dorn, whose hunting of them was ruled "zeal in error, honorably abandoned," and whose grey cloak was returned to her folded, by the king's own hands, with the clasp turned outward — the old sign, Sera realized with a jolt, of a warden serving the man and not the mask. The first oath-keepers had worn it that way. Somewhere in eleven years of stolen winters, the meaning had been burned out of the custom, and here was a king restoring it without knowing he was doing it, the way you restore a word you've never been taught by saying it correctly once.

And then the king said, "Sera Vance," and the hall went quiet in a way that pressed on her eardrums.

"You broke your oath," he said. "I have read the record. You broke it to protect a royal seal you believed was being buried, and you were right, and the men who ordered it buried were wrong, and some of them are in my dungeons now and the rest are running." He paused. "An oath that cannot survive being right is not an oath. It's a leash. I am told the first keepers swore to the realm's *people*, and that somewhere along the way the words got changed to the realm's *crown*, and no one noticed, because the crown was eating whoever might have noticed." He stepped down from the dais, one step, two, until he stood level with her, close enough that she could see the faint ash-grey still shadowing his hairline, a stain that might never wash out. "I am asking you to rebuild them. The keepers. With the old words. I'm told you know a woman who remembers them."

"She remembers everything," Sera said. Her voice came out rough. "That's rather her whole tragedy."

"Then between the two of you, rebuild it. Take whom you trust. Take Dorn, if she'll come — she's already asked, though she'll deny it." A flicker of something almost young crossed the king's face. "I can't wear a crown again. Not yet, perhaps not ever. Someone has to be the thing that stands between the realm and the next thing that wants to eat it. I'd rather it be people who've been outside the walls. People who know where the walls don't reach."

Sera knelt. She hadn't decided to; her knees simply went, six years of wanting collapsing into one motion, and the stone was cold through her trousers and it was the best cold she had ever felt.

"Don't swear yet," the king said, and there was laughter in it, tired and real. "Swear when the words are right. That's rather the point."

* * *

They wrote the oath in the library that afternoon, Sera and Dorn and the memory-thief woman called Wren, who had come down from the northern houses with white in her hair and a mind like a cathedral of open doors. Wren recited fragments; Sera argued; Dorn sat sharpening a knife she no longer needed to sharpen and occasionally said one devastating sentence that ended an hour of debate.

"*To the realm's people,*" Wren said, "in their hearths and on their roads—"

"Not roads," Sera said. "Hearths and *hiding places*. If we only guard the lawful, we guard no one. The smugglers saved the king. Write that into the bones of it."

Dorn, without looking up from the knife: "Put in the clause about disobeying orders. The one they cut. I found it in a pre-fall text in the garrison library, misfiled under agriculture." She slid the whetstone along the blade, a long soft sound like wind under a door. "*If the command and the oath conflict, the oath stands alone.* They cut that in the fourth year of the regency. I checked."

Wren wrote it in. Sera watched the letters take shape — ink on vellum, ordinary ink, nothing shifting or reading her back, thank every god — and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched so long she'd mistaken it for her skeleton.

Outside the library window, snow was melting off the eaves in bright ropes. The unnatural winter had broken the night the crown shattered; the thaw had come down off Thornfell like a released breath, and already the low fields showed black earth through the white, and the river under the walls ran loud and arrogant with meltwater. Men who had spent eleven years believing the deep cold was simply the world now walked around blinking in the sun like moles.

Nix appeared at the window — appeared, in the way she did, as if she'd been assembled from available shadows — and rapped the glass.

"Sera. You're wanted. Vess is doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where she pretends she isn't crying." Nix grinned, gap-toothed, and vanished.

* * *

They gathered in the outer yard, where the guild's forty had camped with a discipline that embarrassed the garrison soldiers watching from the walls. Vess stood before a fire pit, and in her hands was the ledger — the actual ledger, the one Sera had seen Bram flinch at the mention of for a hundred miles of road, the book with every route and every price and every name the crown could hang a smuggler with.

Bram stood across the fire from her. Ash sat at his hip like a second shadow. Nix perched on a cart wheel, eating something. The king watched from a balcony, having refused, firmly, to watch from anywhere more official.

"I sold you," Vess said. Her voice carried flat and clear across the yard, the voice of a woman who had decided that if she was going to bleed in public she would do it neatly. "Your route. Your face. Your description, to Captain Dorn, for gold and for — I told myself it was justice. You stole guild property. I told myself a lot of things." She looked down at the ledger. "Twenty-two years I kept this book. Every toll, every betrayal, every favor owed. It's the truest history of the eastern passes that exists, and it's a history of buying and selling people, and I am its author, and I have read it every night this winter like a prayer book, and I am done."

She fed it to the fire.

It caught slowly — wax and vellum resist — and then all at once, pages curling into black butterflies that rose on the heat and came apart into flakes of ash that drifted, briefly, unmistakably, the same grey as the ash that had poured off a shattered crown three nights ago. Nobody spoke. The fire crackled. Somewhere above, a raven said something rude.

When the book was cinders, Vess reached into her coat and drew out a rolled bundle of oilcloth, tied with cord, and held it out across the dead fire to Bram.

"Your routes," she said. "Copies. My hand, not the guild's scribe's — these never existed in any ledger. The passes, the fords, the warden rotations, the caves that flood in spring. Twenty-two years of knowing where the mountains keep their doors." Her jaw worked. "The guild's done, Bram. Or it's yours, if you want it. Either way, the knowledge shouldn't die with my pride. There are people up in those valleys who get medicine and letters and news because someone knows the ways through. That work doesn't stop because the crown stopped lying."

Bram took the bundle. His hands — Sera had watched those hands speak to mules and goats and a dying wolf, quiet hands, careful hands — shook slightly as he untied the cord and looked at the first page. Whatever he saw there made him close his eyes for a moment.

"Vess," he said. "You were my sister before you were my master."

"I was your sister the whole time," Vess said, and her voice finally cracked, one clean break, quickly repaired. "That's what made selling you possible. You only betray family. Strangers you just rob." She scrubbed a hand across her face, turned to the assembled smugglers, and raised her voice into full guild-master bellow: "Truce holds! Anyone steals so much as an apple inside these walls answers to me, and I've got nothing left to lose but my reputation, which is currently *ash*, so try me!"

Laughter went around the yard — soldiers too, on the walls, the first laughter Sera had heard in Greywatch that didn't sound startled at itself.

Bram rolled the routes closed and held them a moment against his chest, and Sera saw him look up at the balcony, at the king, and saw the king incline his head — one man to another, no throne in it — and saw Bram mouth something that might have been *your majesty* and might have been *thank you* and might, given the man, have been addressed to the ravens.

Ash lifted her head and spoke, that low gravel voice that still made new guards drop things. *"The mountain paths will need a speaker again come spring. The goats have complaints. Many complaints. They have been saving them up all winter."*

"Tell them to form a queue," Bram said.

*"They refuse. Goats do not queue. This is their entire culture."*

More laughter. Nix slid off the cart wheel and attached herself to Bram's side, chattering about restitution and whether the crown owed her back wages, and Vess stood alone by her burning ledger's ashes with her arms folded, watching her life's work drift upward, and if her eyes were wet, the wind coming off the thawing peaks was strong enough that no one could say so.

* * *

Evening found Sera on the north battlement with the draft of the oath in her hands, reading it aloud to herself in the old cadences, testing each line like a rope bridge. Dorn leaned on the parapet beside her, grey cloak back on her shoulders, clasp turned outward, catching the last copper light.

"'If the command and the oath conflict, the oath stands alone,'" Sera read. "It's going to get keepers killed."

"It's going to get keepers *right*," Dorn said. "Sometimes those are the same day." She was quiet a moment. Below them the campfires of the guild dotted the field like a spilled constellation, and beyond them the Thornfell passes stood open for the first time in months, black rock and white snow and, between, the brown thread of the high road going south. "Wren says the old order numbered nine hundred at its height. Nine hundred pairs of eyes the crown couldn't blind. We'll start with twelve. Maybe fifteen, if the garrison cooperates."

"The garrison will cooperate. Their captain took a knee in a vault corridor and lived to be promoted for it."

"Hmph." But Dorn's mouth curved. Then she straightened, and Sera followed her gaze down the south road, where — impossibly, absurdly, at dusk, in mud season — a single rider was climbing the causeway, leading a second horse heavy with saddlebags, moving with the particular exhausted determination of someone who has ridden three days on a rumor.

The gate challenged the rider; torches flared; and Sera heard, drifting up the wall, a voice she knew from a border garrison lifetime ago, thin and cracked and utterly unchanged:

"—message for the oath-keeper Vance, if she lives, and if she doesn't, for whoever's fool enough to be rebuilding the order! From Mother Aldessa at the Fenmarch houses! She says—" the rider paused, gulping air, and the words came out in a rush, "—she says the old vaults under the Fenmarch chapel aren't empty, she says there are *others* like the crown, she says the first keepers bound more than one, and she says two of the seals have *already broken*—"

The wind came off the mountains, smelling of meltwater and stone and, very faintly, of ash.

Sera looked at Dorn. Dorn looked at Sera. On the road below, the rider swayed in the saddle and began, determinedly, to dismount.

"Well," Dorn said. "So much for spring."
