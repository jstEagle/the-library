## 22. An Ember for the Road

The thaw came to Thornfell the way forgiveness comes — slowly at first, in small betrayals of ice, and then all at once.

Bram heard it before he saw it. He heard everything now. That was the price and the gift both: his voice had been spent in the vault beneath Greywatch, poured out like water into a cracked bowl, and what had come back was thinner, quieter, but threaded through with every creature that had answered him that night. When he woke on the first morning after, a sparrow had been sitting on the windowsill of the infirmary, saying *sun, sun, sun* over and over, and Bram had understood it as plainly as his own name.

Now, three weeks later, he stood at the mouth of the high road where it climbed out of Lowbridge's smoke and into the shoulders of the mountains, listening to Thornfell let go of winter.

Snowmelt ran in a hundred bright threads down the black rock. Underneath it all, deep in the bones of the pass, he could feel the mountain itself muttering — a slow, mineral groan of stone remembering how to move. The mule trains would come back soon. The smugglers' paths would open like veins. Vess's ledger, burned and rewritten in her own hand, would fill with spring routes and spring prices, and somewhere in those pages there was still a line reserved for a quiet man who spoke too softly to beasts.

*You could come home,* she'd said at the gate, not quite meeting his eyes, her scarred knuckles white on her horse's rein. *The roads will need a guide who can hear an avalanche before it decides to happen.*

*Maybe I'll guide from the far end of them,* he'd answered, and she had almost smiled — the almost being more than she'd given anyone in ten years.

He wasn't ready to go back yet. Some doors needed to stay shut a while longer so you could learn what you were without standing behind them.

Ash pressed warm against his hip, grey fur smelling of woodsmoke and crushed pine needles, and said, *You're doing the thing again.*

"What thing?"

*Standing still and listening to everything instead of walking toward one thing. Wolves call it prey-sickness. You'd call it thinking too much.*

"I don't have a word for it," Bram admitted. "I used to. I gave some words away."

The wolf huffed, which was Ash's version of laughter, and leaned harder against his leg. The wolf's shoulder reached Bram's ribs now; whatever Bram had coaxed back from death in that cave had kept growing, kept fattening on elk and spring air and, Ash insisted, on being spoken to. *Everything grows when it is addressed,* the wolf liked to say, in that dry rumbling voice that still startled Bram some mornings, waking to find a conversation already in progress beside his bedroll.

Behind them, boots crunched on the slush-crusted road, quick and arrhythmic, and Nix came pelting up the hill with her coat flapping and her hands full of somebody else's apples.

"Four of them," she announced, breathless, holding up her plunder. "The orchard man on the corner. He won't miss them, Bram, he has a whole *tree*, that's practically theft-proof by definition—"

"You could pay him."

"With what? Gratitude?" She distributed the apples anyway — one to Bram, one lobbed high for Ash, who caught it out of the air with a snap of jaws and a look of profound dignity. "Besides, I'm reforming. Slowly. You have to let reformation happen at its own pace or it scars."

Sera came up behind her at a walk, unhurried as ever, her grey oath-keeper's cloak freshly dyed and her new badge of office riding awkwardly on her breast, like a stone she hadn't decided whether to keep in her shoe. The restored king had asked her to rebuild the order from nothing. She'd said yes the way mountains say yes to weather — because there was no sense arguing, and someone had to stand there and take it.

"He's right, you know," Sera said mildly, biting into her apple. "About paying."

"He's *always* right," Nix said darkly. "That's the worst part of traveling with him. It's like walking beside weather."

They climbed. The high road unspooled ahead of them through the greening pass, and Bram felt the day open like a hand.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shepherd's ruin where they had once decoded the map — the same broken ring of stone, the same view down the valley, but transformed now. Where ice had glazed the fallen lintel, moss grew. Where Nix had screamed at the shifting ink, a wren had built a nest in the wall's hollow, and she sat near it for an hour without stealing anything at all, just listening to the small brown bird say *mine, mine, mine* about four twigs and an egg.

Bram built the fire with the care of ritual. Because it was one.

From the inner pocket of his coat, wrapped in oilcloth and then wool and then more oilcloth, he drew out the iron lantern — plain, dented, bought in Lowbridge before it burned, carried since. Inside it, resting on a bed of ash, lay the ember.

It did not look like much. A shard of char no bigger than his thumbnail, gone cool and dull over the long weeks, the way embers do when starved. But it was not dead. He had known that from the moment the crown shattered and the thing of ash and hunger dissolved into drifting grey — known it the way you know a seed is sleeping and not rotten. The crown had been forged by the first oath-keepers to cage something terrible, but fire remembers both of its parents: the burning and the hearth. What remained when the hunger burned away was only warmth. Only light, waiting to be asked.

He fed the fire until it stood up bright, then held the lantern over it and opened the little door.

The ember caught.

It flushed rose-gold along its cracks, and heat bloomed in the lantern's heart, and around the fire every animal within earshot went briefly, utterly silent — and then, one by one, began to speak. Not words. Just acknowledgment. The mules in the inn yard far below shifted in their stalls and murmured. Somewhere up the slope a fox said a single syllable that meant *witnessed*. The wren in the wall sang straight through it, unimpressed, which made Nix laugh until she had to lie down.

"It always does that?" she asked.

"Every time I light it." Bram closed the lantern's shutter partway, letting a blade of gold fall across the stones. "It's not really mine. None of this was, truly. The map found me. The wolf chose me. The girl picked my pocket—" he looked at Nix, "—and never entirely gave it back."

She had the grace to look only slightly guilty. In Greywatch, after, she had pressed into his hand the stolen shard of memory she'd kept — a fragment of his mother's face, saved from the rite's great swallowing — and it had been the first thing in her life she'd ever returned rather than sold. But not everything had come home. He still could not find the sound of Vess's laugh anywhere in himself. He knew it existed. He knew it had mattered. It was like a room in a house he owned but couldn't enter, and sometimes at night he stood outside that door in his mind and listened, and heard nothing, and grieved it quietly.

But grief braided with grace was still grace. He had learned to carry both strands.

"What will you do with it?" Sera asked, nodding at the lantern. "Truly. The king would give you a keep for it. He'd give you a *province*. You ended a hundred-year winter, Bram Eastwick. People are already telling stories, and stories grow teeth."

"That's why I'm taking it on the road instead." Bram set the lantern on the flat stone between them, where its light pooled like something living. "A crown taught me what happens when you lock a fire in one place and tell everyone it's holy. This—" he touched the iron, warm as a sleeping bird, "—this goes where it's needed. To whoever needs it. No throne. No altar. Just a light, passed along."

"The oath-keepers started that way once," Sera said softly. "Lamplighters, my grandmother called us. Before we built walls around ourselves and forgot the doors." Her hand rested briefly on the new badge. "Maybe that's the order worth rebuilding. One that carries embers instead of keeping them."

*Or one that carries wolves,* Ash said from where he'd arranged himself across the ruin's threshold like a moat with opinions. *Every good order needs a wolf. It keeps the meetings short.*

Nix grinned and threw him another apple she had absolutely not stolen, because it had come from their own pack, which everyone agreed barely counted.

* * *

They walked the passes for six days, and Bram learned his new country — the one where every hedgerow and hollow spoke.

His voice was quieter now. He felt the cost most in the mornings, when he woke and reached for a memory the way you reach for a blanket and find only cool linen. But quiet, he discovered, traveled farther than shouting ever had. A whisper meant for a mule arrived in the mule's ears like scripture. The goats of the high summer pastures descended halfway down the mountain just to stand near him while he talked, the way folk gather around a fire that doesn't spit.

On the third day he spoke a landslide out of happening — a slope above the White Road groaning under meltwater, ready to shear — and by evening the story had run ahead of him down the valley faster than any horse: *the beast-speaker walks the high road again, and the mountains listen.* Old women left bread on their doorsteps for the wolf. A boy with a stutter followed them for a full morning before working up the courage to ask if animals ever laughed at people, and seemed enormously comforted when Bram said, truthfully, "Constantly. Especially at me."

On the fifth day they passed the shrine where Vess had once held a knife to his throat, and found someone had left wildflowers in the offering niche — crocuses and snowdrops, the year's first stubborn brave things. Nix declared it sentimental. Sera declared it correct. Ash ate the flowers, which settled the theological question.

And on the sixth day, at the top of the world where the pass split — east to the sea roads, south to the capital, west into the deep Thornfell where maps gave up — they stopped.

Below them the whole valley lay open, running silver with meltwater, patched with the last dirty snow going soft and honest into earth. Smoke rose thin and domestic from Lowbridge, rebuilt in its own ashes, its chimneys like slow exhalations. Beyond it, farther than sight, everything else: every ford and ferry, every garrison and market town, every locked room in the world that might be keeping something it shouldn't.

Bram lifted the lantern and watched the ember breathe.

*Where first?* Ash asked.

Nix was already spinning, finger raised, playing the oldest game on the road — point and declare. South, she argued, for the capital's gossip and its criminal underclass, whom she described as *colleagues she intended to reform aggressively*. West, Sera countered quietly, because the deep passes had gone silent in the reports, and silence was what she'd been sworn to go looking inside of.

Bram didn't answer right away. He listened instead — to the wind combing the high grass, to the river talking to its stones, to the thousand small voices of a world coming back to life after a hundred years of stolen winter. And underneath them all, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, he heard something else.

Far away. Far west. A sound like breathing that wasn't wind, and wasn't beast, and wasn't human.

Something old, under the deep passes, turning over in its sleep.

The ember flared, once, bright against his ribs — as if it heard it too.

"Well," Bram said softly, to the wolf, the girl, the oath-keeper, and the small gold fire he carried for whoever needed it next. "West, then."

Ash's tail swept once across the snow-wet stone. Nix whooped and was already running downhill. And the high road took them, three figures and a lantern, into the open green throat of the mountains — carrying light toward the dark, the way embers have always traveled best: in the hands of people still learning what they're for.
