## 4. Five Years of Borrowed Sky

The bells were wrong. Silas had lived in enough cities to know the difference between a feast and a fire, between celebration and the sound a city makes when it remembers it has teeth.

"Run first," he said, catching Wren's sleeve. "Explanations later. Preferably explanations in a cellar."

"That's not — I have questions—"

"*Run.*"

They ran. Vellamere at night was all wet cobblestones and gaslight gone soft in the rain, and Wren ran like someone who had spent her whole life sprinting only toward bookshelves — elbows in, breath ragged, dignity intact through sheer stubbornness. Silas matched her pace and hated how easily his body remembered this: the turn of a corner taken at speed, the flat calm of a heart that had once outrun thunder.

Behind them, boots on stone. Not many. Four, maybe five. The crown's wardens wore iron-shod heels so you could hear them coming; some clerk a century ago had decided terror was cheaper than secrecy.

Silas pulled her down an alley that smelled of fish and old rain, then through a courtyard where laundry lines hung like the rigging of a drowned ship, then up — because down was where pursuers expected you to go.

"Where," Wren gasped, "are we *going*?"

"Away."

"You're a terrible navigator."

"I'm an excellent one. I'm just navigating away from things, which is a different discipline entirely." He hauled her over a low wall into the yard of a shuttered dyeworks, where vats stood open to the sky and caught the rain in shallow copper mouths. "In here. They'll pass us by."

They pressed against the wall beneath a sagging awning. The boots clattered past the mouth of the alley, paused, argued in low voices, moved on.

Wren slid down the wall until she was sitting in a puddle, and did not appear to care. Her hair had come loose from its coil. She was breathing like a bellows and glaring at him like he'd personally invented being chased.

"All right," she said. "Explanations. Now. In this cellar-adjacent location."

* * *

Silas sat down across from her, rain drumming the awning above them both, and found that the words didn't want to come. Five years of practice at not saying them, and now they jammed in his throat like a swallowed key.

"You saw my name in the ledger," he said finally. "You read what came after it."

"*Storm-caller, royal house, oath-broken.*" She recited it the way she recited everything, precisely, as though the words might be examined later for accuracy. "I assumed it was a title. A job description."

"It was both." He turned his hands over. Under the skin, faint as lightning behind cloud, something coiled and shifted. He let her see it — just a flicker, blue-white at the edge of his knuckles, there and gone. Wren's breath caught, but she didn't flinch. He'd have liked her less if she'd flinched, and he suspected he'd have liked her more if she'd run.

"There used to be magic in Vellamere," he said. "Real magic. Storms you could call by name, dragons in the deep places, kings who kept oaths instead of eating them. Then the crown passed a law, and then another, and then there weren't laws so much as erasures. Magic wasn't banned here, Wren. It was *deleted*. From records, from memory, from the mouths of anyone who remembered it."

"And you're saying you were part of that deleted world."

"I was the royal storm-caller." The words tasted like copper coins. "Last of nine. When I took the oath, they bound the storm into me — literally. It lives under my skin. It's why your charms work when they shouldn't, why your luck-charm clerk walked home dry through a downpour. I leak."

"You *leak*." Wren laughed, one startled note, and pressed her hand over her mouth. "I sold — no, *you* sold a woman a luck-charm last month that made her bread rise for a week. Mrs. Tallow thinks she's been blessed by the harvest gods."

"She has. Sort of. By me. Accidentally."

"This is the least reassuring confession in history."

"I'm getting to the bad part."

Her laugh died. Rain filled the silence, patient, indifferent.

"The entity wearing the king," Silas said. "Whatever hollowed Aldous out — it doesn't eat bodies. It eats *memory*. That's its food, its currency, its leash. Five years ago I stood in the throne room and saw what I saw tonight, and I made the mistake of saying so out loud." His hands curled shut around the flickering light. "So it took my payment. Everything before that night. My mother's face. My teacher's voice. The oath itself — the words, the reason, all of it. It left me the storm, because the storm was useful, and left me the exile, because the storm needed somewhere to be."

He hadn't meant to say the next part. It arrived anyway, quiet, like weather.

"They marched me out of the palace at dawn. And I wept the whole way and didn't know what I was weeping about. Grief with no name attached. Do you know what that's like? Mourning a person who is yourself?"

Wren was silent a long moment. Rain ticked on the copper vats. Somewhere far off, the alarm bells had given up or been silenced.

"Yes," she said.

He looked up.

"Not the same thing," she said quickly. "Nothing like the same thing. But — grief with no shape to it. I spent two years after my mother died unable to remember her voice, and I hated myself for it, and it took me another year to understand that remembering isn't a duty, it's a — a craft. You rebuild. Badly, at first. With wrong materials. But you build." She pushed wet hair out of her face and fixed him with the look she probably gave misfiled manuscripts. "You've been mourning yourself for five years without permission to grieve. That's not exile, Silas. That's cruelty with paperwork."

Something cracked open in his chest, gently, the way ice does when spring gets under it. He realized he was smiling and couldn't remember deciding to.

"You're very strange," he said.

"I'm an archivist. We're all strange. It's in the hiring requirements."

* * *

They should keep moving. Wren knew this the way she knew the archive's closing hours — automatically, without urgency, while her mind went elsewhere. But her legs had opinions and her lungs had filed a formal complaint, so she let them sit a moment longer in the dripping dark of the dyeworks, and she thought.

She thought the way she'd been trained to think: outward from the evidence.

A ledger written a century ago. Two names beside tonight's date — *Silas Fairweather, storm-caller, oath-broken* and *Wren Halloway, keeper of the sealed vault*. Lines beneath that had pulled them bodily through a locked door, lines that described a rite, a hollowing, a dragon, a final line still unread. An entity that ate memories. A man whose memories had already been eaten, and who had survived anyway.

Survived. That word kept snagging.

"If it devoured your past," she said slowly, "why are you still you? Why do you have a sense of humor? A personality? Opinions about navigation?" She paused. "*Skills?*"

Silas shrugged. "It took what was written down in me. The deep records. What's left is—" he waved a hand vaguely at himself, at the rain, at everything— "margins."

"Margins," Wren repeated, and felt the idea land in her chest like a struck bell. "No. Listen. A devourer of memory would want a clean meal. But it left you functional. It left you *charming brass trinkets in a market square*, which — forgive me — seems like an odd thing to leave a threat."

"Perhaps it enjoys irony."

"Or perhaps it *couldn't* take everything." She leaned forward. "Because something in you was anchored. Bound. The storm isn't just living under your skin, is it? It's holding what's left of you together. The oath they bound into you — it's the one record of you it can't erase, because it isn't yours. It's older than you. It's in the ledger too, Silas. *Oath-broken* — but broken oaths still exist. You can't delete a promise. You can only fail it."

He stared at her. In the dim light his face did something complicated, hope and horror sharing the same small space.

"So it needs me alive."

"It needs you *whole*. Or near enough." Wren was standing now, pacing the puddles, hands moving — she couldn't help it, ideas always made her pace. "And it needs me, because I'm named too. Keeper of the sealed vault. Which means the prophecy isn't a warning we stumbled into. It's a structure. A sequence. Both of us, alive, until the final line. Whatever the entity does tonight, whatever it burns or hunts or hollows — it cannot simply kill us. It has to *unwrite* us first, and unwriting takes time, and time is the one thing a prophecy never gives its villain generously."

"You say that very cheerfully for someone describing our mutual doom."

"I say it cheerfully because it means we're not prey, Silas. We're load-bearing." She stopped pacing. The humor drained out of her voice, leaving something steadier underneath. "Which means every step we take matters. Including the steps we take *toward* whatever that final line says. It's not dragging us. It's counting on us."

The rain eased. Through a gap in the dyeworks roof, a slice of sky showed — black cloud tearing along one edge, the faintest bruise of moonlight behind it.

Silas got to his feet. When he spoke again, the flippancy was gone, and what remained was quieter and much more frightening: a man deciding something.

"Then we go where the ledger points," he said. "Before it comes for the pages. Before it figures out that killing the story might be easier than killing us."

"You know where it points."

"The marginalia. The drawings in the margins I told you about." He looked at her, and she watched him weigh the words, and choose honesty anyway — a small surrender, offered like a gift. "Chains. Beneath the palace. Something old, drawn sleeping, with fire coming off it in long ribbons. Wren — I think there's a dragon under Vellamere. I think there always has been."

Wren opened her mouth to say *that's impossible*, because it was impossible, because everything she'd believed about her city for thirty years said so.

What came out instead was: "Then we'll need better shoes."

Silas laughed — really laughed, the first unguarded sound she'd heard from him, bright and startled as the light under his skin. And somewhere across the drowned city, in the palace on its hill, something vast turned its attention toward the sound, and began, patiently, to plan.

But neither of them heard that. They only heard each other, and the rain stopping, and their own footsteps setting off together into the dark — toward the chains, and the fire, and the line still waiting to be read.

* * *

Three streets away, a man in a captain's coat stood beneath a broken lamp and watched the pair slip through the gap in the market gates. He had silver in his beard and guilt in his eyes and a letter in his pocket bearing the crown's seal, and he did not move to follow.

"Forgive me, boy," Captain Ives murmured to the empty street. "I'm buying you time the only way I have left."

He touched the letter once, as if checking it were real, and turned toward the palace, and the dark took him the way it takes all traitors — gently, and without comment.
