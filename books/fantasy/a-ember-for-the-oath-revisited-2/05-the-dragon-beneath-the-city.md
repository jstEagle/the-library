## 5. The Dragon Beneath the City

Wren Halloway had spent eleven years in the Vellamere archives and had developed a professional theory: every secret in the city was kept in a basement. The archives had four of them. The palace, she now suspected, had considerably more.

The marginalia had led them here — a scrawl in faded brown ink tucked into the ledger's gutter, written by a hand that had clearly been shaking:

*Beneath the cisterns, where the old kings kept their sin. Follow the warmth.*

"Follow the warmth," Silas muttered, for the fourth time since they'd slipped through the maintenance gate behind the palace laundry. "That's either prophecy or advice from someone's grandmother."

"It's both, probably. Most wisdom is." Wren held her lantern low and tried not to think about how far above them the streets of Vellamere were carrying on their ordinary evening — carts rattling, bells ringing the ninth hour, some poor soul arguing with a fishmonger about the freshness of his herring. Down here there was only the drip of water and the groan of stone that had been holding its breath for a century.

They had been running for two days. Hunted, rather. Captain Ives's face haunted her more than the crown's soldiers did — the way he'd looked at Silas in that last terrible moment before the alarm bells, like a man swallowing something that fought him on the way down. But Ives had also pressed a key into Wren's palm, and the key had opened this gate, and so she carried her gratitude and her anger in the same pocket and tried not to let them touch.

The tunnel sloped downward. And then—

"Wren." Silas had stopped. He was standing very still, the way he did when the storm under his skin stirred, which she'd learned to read the way sailors read weather. "Do you feel that?"

She did. It was absurd to call it warmth; it was more like standing at the mouth of an oven, except the heat came with a sound beneath hearing, a vast slow pulse, like a heart the size of a cathedral deciding whether to keep beating.

"Oh," Wren said, very quietly. "Oh, it's *real*."

"You thought it wasn't?"

"I thought—" She laughed, a small hysterical thing. "Silas, I'm an archivist. My entire life is cataloguing things other people insist are real while privately hoping no one asks me to prove it. Dragons are in the *mythology* section. Third floor. Between sea serpents and the folkloric history of luck-charms."

"Which you once told me were brass and bone."

"Which I once *suspected* were brass and bone, and I have since apologized."

He grinned at her — tired, rain-damp, still grinning — and Wren felt the familiar treacherous warmth in her chest that had nothing to do with dragons. She sternly filed it away for later examination. There would be a later. The prophecy needed them alive until the final line, which meant, technically, that hope was on their side.

* * *

The cistern opened without warning into a cavern that should not have existed beneath a city that taxed its citizens for chimney smoke.

Wren's lantern seemed to shrink in shame. The space was enormous — a hollowed vault of black rock ribbed with veins of something that glowed a deep, banked orange, like coals seen through closed eyelids. And in the center, wrapped in chains thick as oak trees, lay the dragon.

Emberveil.

Wren had imagined dragons, of course. Everyone did. She had imagined them vast and winged and terrible, all teeth and conquest. What she had not imagined was the *sadness*. Emberveil lay curled around herself like a woman grown old waiting, her scales the color of embers going grey at the edges, her wings folded tight against a body too large for any chain but bound anyway — bound in iron that hummed with script, hundreds of lines of it, glowing faintly gold. Binding-script. Wren recognized the letterforms from the oldest folios. She had catalogued them as decorative.

Her eyes were open. They found Wren first, then Silas, and they were ancient and amber and unbearably *tired*.

"So," the dragon said. Her voice filled the cavern the way thunder fills a valley — not loud so much as everywhere. "The ledger finally grew legs."

Wren's knees nearly went. She gripped Silas's sleeve. "You talk."

"I do many things, little archivist. Talking is the only one left that doesn't hurt." A plume of smoke curled from Emberveil's nostrils, smelling of woodsmoke and, strangely, of libraries — of old paper and warm dust. "You've been reading my fire. Every page of that ledger is written in it. Did you know? Your prophets didn't dream their prophecies. They borrowed my light to see by."

Silas stepped forward, and Wren watched him fight the instinct to kneel and lose. "Then you know why we're here. The thing in the crown—"

"The Hollow King's tenant." Emberveil's great head turned, chains singing a low mournful note. "Yes. I know it better than anyone alive, because I am its anchor. Its leash. Its *meal ticket*, if we're being crude about it." One amber eye fixed on Silas. "It cannot hold itself to this world, storm-caller. Memory is its food, and memory is thin air unless it's *anchored*. So a hundred years ago, your clever kings bound it to my fire. As long as I burn, it stays. As long as I stay, it feeds. On the king. On the archives. On exiles who got too close."

"On me," Silas said quietly.

"On you," Emberveil agreed, without apology. "I felt it take you. Five years ago. It was like feeling someone steal soup from my own pot."

Despite everything — the chains, the dark, the two-day chase sleeping in her bones — Wren felt a laugh escape her. The dragon's eye crinkled, which she hadn't known eyes could do at that scale.

"And now," Emberveil continued, "you've come following ink to ask me how to kill it. Or free the king. Or save yourselves. In roughly that order of sincerity."

"How do you—" Silas began.

"The ledger has your names beside tonight's date. I *wrote* the light those words are drawn in. I know what's coming the way you know a storm is coming: I can smell it." Her voice dropped, and the warmth in the cavern seemed to gather close, listening. "So hear the bargain, storm-caller, because it is terrible, and I will not dress it up. The entity is anchored to my fire. To unbind your king, the anchor must break. But my fire is not merely a chain — it is the last dragon-fire in the world. When it gutters out, nothing of my kind burns again. Ever. Unless—"

"Unless," Wren whispered.

"Unless the fire passes to a vessel strong enough to carry it. A storm-caller. One who owes this world a debt and knows exactly what payment feels like." The eye did not leave Silas. "Take my fire into yourself, break the anchor, end the entity. And spend whatever memories remain to you as fuel. Fire needs feeding, boy. Yours will do nicely."

The silence afterward had weight. Wren heard the drip of water somewhere far off, counting seconds like a patient clerk.

"That's not a bargain," Silas said at last. His voice was steady in the way Wren had learned meant the opposite. "That's just the same theft with extra steps. It took my past. You're asking me to hand over what's left."

"I am asking," Emberveil said, "for a volunteer. There is a difference. It is the whole difference." She lowered her great head until one amber eye was level with him, near enough that Wren could see her own reflection trembling in it. "But know this, and know it truly: refuse, and the entity keeps feeding. Not just the king. The city. The archives. Your archivist's eleven years of careful cataloguing, gone in an afternoon. It grows bolder by the week. I am old, and my fire is low, and when I gutter out on my own — and I *will*, within the season — it goes unchained with no vessel ready to catch what I leave behind."

Wren's stomach turned over. She looked at Silas — at the man who sold luck-charms to nervous clerks and hid lightning under his skin and made jokes about grandmothers because the alternative was screaming — and she saw the arithmetic happening behind his eyes, and she hated it.

"No," she said.

Both of them looked at her. Even the dragon.

"No," Wren said again, stepping forward, because apparently her mouth had decided to lead this expedition without consulting the rest of her. "You don't get to put that on him alone. The ledger has *my* name in it too. Whatever the final line costs, it costs us both — that's what the prophecy says, that's why it pulled us in together, that's—" She was shaking. She kept talking anyway. "And besides, I'm the one who can read the binding-script. If there's a way to break the anchor that doesn't require Silas to become a candle, I will find it. Give me time. Give me the script. That's my price for even *considering* your bargain."

For a long moment the dragon said nothing. Then a sound rolled through the cavern that might have been laughter, or might have been the mountain settling, or might have been both.

"A thousand years," Emberveil rumbled, "and the ones who surprise me are always the librarians." Smoke curled upward, thoughtful. "Very well, little archivist. Read my chains. But read quickly. Because tonight—"

She stopped. Her head snapped toward the tunnel entrance, nostrils flaring, and the banked glow along the cavern walls flickered like a candle in a draft.

"What?" Silas asked. "What is it?"

The dragon's voice came out flat and cold as iron.

"Soldiers. Above. Many. And something *else* walking with them — something that smells of nothing at all." Her chains began to sing. "It has found my door, children. Decide fast."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, Wren heard the palace bells begin to ring — not the hour, not the quarter, but the alarm pattern, over and over, urgent as a heartbeat.

And in her satchel, against all reason, against every law of paper and ink she had ever trusted, the ledger grew warm.
