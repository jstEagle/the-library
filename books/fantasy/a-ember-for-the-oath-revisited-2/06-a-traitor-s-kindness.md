## 6. A Traitor's Kindness

Captain Edric Ives had betrayed exactly three people in his fifty-one years, and he could name every one of them the way a man names his scars.

The first was a boy called Tam Wicker, whom he'd left holding a collapsing barricade during the Saltmarsh Riots because someone had to hold it and Ives had been needed elsewhere. Tam had survived. Ives had bought him a drink every year on the anniversary for two decades until Tam moved inland to grow apples, and then Ives had sent him a bottle instead, and they had never once spoken of the barricade.

The second was his wife, Marta, though he'd only ever told her that once, drunk, on their thirtieth anniversary — "I married you under false colors, love, you thought you were getting an honest man" — and she'd laughed and said she'd known since the wedding and married him anyway.

The third was Silas Fairweather, and there would be no annual drink for that one. There would be no anything, if it went wrong. If it went right, there would be a Silas who hated him, which Ives supposed was at least a Silas.

He stood now at the window of his office above the Third Ward garrison, watching the rain come down on Vellamere like the city owed it money, and turned a brass key over in his fingers until the teeth printed themselves into his palm.

*You're doing the right thing,* he told himself, the way he'd been telling himself for six hours, with diminishing success.

The trouble with doing the right thing, in Ives's long experience, was that from the inside it felt identical to the wrong thing. Same cold hands. Same taste of pennies at the back of the throat. The only difference was what you could say afterward, and even that depended on surviving to say it.

A knock. Two beats, pause, one beat. His own signal, returned.

"Come in, Corporal."

Corporal Denna Voss slipped in, shut the door, and stood at something just short of attention — the posture of a soldier who respected her captain but not necessarily this conversation. She was twenty-six, sharp as a tack with a bad edge, and the best listener in the Third Ward, which was why he'd chosen her. Also she owed him for the business with the falsified patrol logs last spring, and Ives believed in collecting debts at useful moments.

"You look like a man rehearsing an apology," she said.

"I'm rehearsing several. I haven't picked a favorite." He set the brass key on the desk between them. "This goes to the archivist. Wren Halloway."

Voss looked at the key and did not touch it. "That's the woman from the wanted circulars. The one who walked into the sealed vault and out again with Fairweather."

"The very same."

"And you're giving her a key."

"A key," Ives said, "to the Old Archive annex. The sub-levels."

Voss was quiet for a moment, working it through, and he watched her arrive at the same arithmetic he had: the sub-levels held forty years of records the crown had ordered purged twice and failed to purge twice, because the clerks kept mislaying the orders, because clerks were human and some part of them balked. Records of tribute payments that weren't tributes. Of the king's rite schedule changing eleven years ago, all at once, in handwriting that wasn't the king's. Of a storm-caller's discharge papers signed by a man who had never existed.

"They're going to burn them," Voss said. Not a question. Everyone in the garrison above the rank of sergeant had heard the whisper by now: *the palace wants the old paper gone before the Coronation Renewal.* Nobody asked why. Asking why had stopped being healthy around the time the king started smiling with his whole face.

"Tomorrow night, most likely," Ives said. "Officially, I'm to secure the annex tonight and hand it over at dawn. Unofficially—" he tapped the key, "—there is a window between midnight and the changing of the watch where the annex will be tragically unguarded, and if a certain fugitive archivist were to happen upon it, well. Tragedies happen."

"Captain." Voss folded her arms. "Say the rest. You always make me say the rest."

He almost smiled. That was the trouble with training people well; they trained back. "The rest is that I sold Fairweather to the crown this morning. Location, description, the archive connection, all of it."

Voss's eyebrows climbed. "And?"

"And the crown moves faster when it thinks it knows where the prey is. They'll send men to the Ropewalk safehouse within the hour. Men who might otherwise have been sent to burn paper." Ives turned back to the window. Down in the yard, a pair of recruits were failing cheerfully to saddle a horse that had clearly decided on a career in civil resistance. He watched them a moment longer than he needed to. "Fairweather gets taken or runs. Either way, the annex lives another day, and the archivist gets the key, and maybe — *maybe* — whatever's rotting the king from behind his eyes finds itself short one anchor."

"You believe that. About the eyes."

"I've seen it, Denny." And there it was, the thing he hadn't said aloud to anyone, the confession he'd been carrying for eleven years like a stone in his boot. "Eleven years ago I stood post at the Renewal rite, close enough to see the king blink. And he blinked wrong. Both lids together, slow, like a shutter. Like something behind them checking its work." He rubbed the bridge of his nose. "I told myself I imagined it. Told myself for a decade. Then three nights ago I saw the same blink in the eyes of a palace clerk, and the clerk didn't know my name, and I've been buying his mother's bread from her stall for nine years."

Voss said nothing for a while. Rain ticked against the glass.

"So we betray Fairweather," she said finally, "in order to save the paperwork that proves what took his memory in the first place."

"When you say it like that—"

"It sounds mad."

"It sounds," Ives corrected, "like the least mad option available, which in this city is the highest praise a plan can earn."

* * *

He made the delivery himself, because he trusted Voss but not enough for this, and because some cowardly corner of him wanted to see Wren Halloway's face when she understood what he'd done.

She received him in the back room of a chandler's shop off Candlewick Lane — fitting, Ives thought, that everything in this affair now smelled of tallow and smoke. She sat behind a table stacked with ledger pages, ink-stained to the second knuckle, hair escaping its braid in gray-shot wisps. Behind her, half-shadowed among the candle molds, Silas Fairweather stood with his arms crossed, looking like a man who had already done his own arithmetic about why the captain was here.

Wren had not. He watched her take in his uniform, his face, his open hands, and arrive at trust first. That was the kind of person she was. It would get her killed eventually, but it had gotten a great deal of truth found first, and Ives couldn't decide if that was a fair trade. He suspected she'd already decided it was.

"Captain Ives," she said. "Silas told me about you. He said you taught him everything he knows about storms and nothing worth knowing about staying out of trouble."

"He flatters me. I also taught him cards, which is where the real trouble started." Ives set the brass key on the table between them, next to a page covered in Wren's cramped, urgent handwriting. "Old Archive annex, sub-levels. Iron door, east stair, past the map room. It opens what the crown intends to burn tomorrow night."

Wren's breath caught. Her hand hovered over the key without taking it — careful, careful, this one — and then she looked up at him with those clear, terrible, trusting eyes. "Why are you giving us this?"

"Because someone has to remember what's true after everyone clever has finished being safe."

"That's not an answer, Captain."

"No," Ives agreed. "It isn't."

From the shadows, Silas spoke, voice flat as slate. "Tell her the other half, sir. Since you came all this way."

Ah. So he *had* done the arithmetic. Ives met his former student's eyes and found no storm in them, which was somehow worse than lightning; he found only the weary patience of a man waiting for the bill to arrive.

"I reported your position to the palace this morning," Ives said. "The Ropewalk safehouse. They'll have men there within the hour."

Wren went very still. Beside her, a candle flame leaned hard sideways, though there was no draft, and Ives felt the fine hairs of his arms lift — the old, familiar pressure of weather gathering somewhere it shouldn't. Silas closed his fist and the flame straightened, sulking.

"Why?" Wren asked, and her voice didn't shake, and Ives admired her for it the way he admired masonry in a flood.

"To buy time. For you, for the annex, for—" he gestured vaguely upward, at the sky, at the idea of a kingdom, "—all of it. A crown that knows where you are stops hunting blind. A hunt that ends stops needing new bloodhounds. Every soldier they send to the Ropewalk is a soldier not tearing apart the sub-levels looking for loose pages." He exhaled. "I sold you cheap, Fairweather. One safehouse, one night's grace. It was the smallest coin I could pay and still move them."

Silas laughed — a short, bitter sound with no humor in it whatsoever, but underneath it, faintly, something else. Something that remembered a younger man laughing on a rooftop in the rain while an older one shouted about proper grounding technique.

"You always did love a gambit," Silas said. "Even the ones that cost your people the board."

"Yes, well." Ives's throat tightened. "You were the best of them, lad. That's rather the point. If they take you, they'll want you talking, not dead — the entity doesn't waste vessels that can call storms. Which means you'll be alive. Which means the prophecy — whatever it needs you both breathing for — stays possible. I read the ledger's surviving margins. I'm old, not stupid."

"You're both," Silas said, but softer.

"I'm a traitor," Ives said simply. "Third one I've ever made. I keep a list." He straightened, and let the captain come back into his spine. "The watch changes at midnight. You'll have perhaps ninety minutes in the annex. Take the tribute ledgers, the rite schedules, the discharge file marked *Fairweather, S. — voided*. And take the thin black book on the top shelf of the map case. Don't read it down there. Read it in daylight, with salt in your pockets, and don't read it alone."

"What's in it?" Wren asked, already pocketing the key.

Ives looked at her — at the ink stains, the steady hands, the stubborn architecture of her — and felt the guilt and the love in his chest finally stop pretending they were separate things. They never had been. That was the whole rotten trick of caring about people.

"Proof," he said. "Of what he was. Of what was taken. And a page or two," he added quietly, "of what the entity fears."

At the door, he paused, rain drumming on the street beyond, and didn't turn around.

"Midnight, archivist. Ninety minutes. And if you see my name in any of those files—" his voice caught, only slightly, "—remember I watered it down wherever I could."

Then he was out into the wet, whistling for his escort, walking at a traitor's pace toward a king's palace — and behind him, in a chandler's back room, he heard Wren Halloway begin to laugh, low and disbelieving, and Silas Fairweather say:

"He gave us *homework*. Eleven years, and the man's still assigning homework."

Ives smiled all the way to the palace gates, where the doors opened too easily, and the smiling clerks inside blinked — slowly, both lids together — as they waved him through.
