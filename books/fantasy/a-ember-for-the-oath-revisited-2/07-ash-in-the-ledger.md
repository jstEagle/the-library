## 7. Ash in the Ledger

Wren Halloway had catalogued eleven thousand four hundred and six books in her career, and she had never once watched one burn on purpose.

She watched this one burn by accident, which was somehow worse.

It happened in the space of a single breath. She and Silas had made it back to the archive through the servant tunnels — Silas gray-faced and shaking, the ledger clutched to his chest like an infant — and she had barely gotten the lamp lit when the air in the reading room *changed*. It thickened. It went sweet, the way a sickroom goes sweet, and every candle in the archive guttered sideways though no door had opened.

"Down," Silas said, and there was such command in his ruined voice that she dropped behind the reading table without question.

The ledger on the table did not drop. It rose.

Page by page, it peeled itself open, leaves fluttering as if caught in a draft that existed nowhere else in the room. And then the pages began to char — not from any flame Wren could see, but from the edges inward, brown to black to nothing, a slow patient consumption that left ash drifting down like terrible snow.

"No," Wren heard herself say. "No, no, you don't —"

"Wren." Silas's hand closed on her wrist. "You can't stop it."

"It's the only copy."

"Then it was always going to come for the only copy."

She wrenched free of him anyway. She threw herself across the table, arms spread over the book like a mother over a child, and felt the heat of the unseen burning against her cheek — a dry, hungry warmth with no source, no mercy, and no interest whatsoever in her sacrifice. The pages kept going. The prophecy of the Oath Revisited, one hundred years of careful ink, unwriting itself beneath her ribs.

*And at the last, when the crown is unmade—*

That line went up in smoke before she finished reading it.

She did not know what came after. Nobody knew what came after. The whole point of a sealed vault was that nobody knew what came after, and now the entity wearing the king's face had reached into her archive from across the city and burned the answer rather than live to meet it.

Then the fire stopped. The ledger lay open to a page of nothing, blank and warm, and the sweetness drained out of the air, and Wren was left sprawled across the table with soot on her hands and her heart hammering out some kind of furious argument against her chest wall.

"It didn't finish," Silas said quietly.

"What?"

"It stopped before the end." He came to stand beside her, and she saw he was looking not at the book but at her. At her, specifically, with an expression she couldn't name. "Wren. It stopped because it ran out of pages to burn. But I read the ledger in the vault. There were more lines after *when the crown is unmade*. Half a page more, at least."

"Then why—"

"You read them aloud," he said. "In the vault. Both of us did. You have a memory like a catalogue index; you told me so yourself, twice, with the smugness of a woman describing a superpower."

"I do not have a *superpower*, I have a *professionally trained recall*, which is entirely different and considerably less dramatic—"

"Wren." His hand found her shoulder. His voice gentled into something that made her want to both lean into it and run from the room. "You're the only copy now."

* * *

They spent an hour in the wreckage of the reading room, Wren reciting and Silas writing, reconstructing the final lines of the prophecy on the backs of requisition forms because it seemed fitting and because all the proper paper had ash on it. Her voice shook at first. Then something strange began to happen.

Every line she spoke aloud landed in the room like a stone dropped in still water.

Not metaphorically. The lamp flame stood up straighter. The frost on the window — frost, in high summer, since they'd entered the vault — crawled into new patterns, deliberate ones, spirals and runes she almost recognized from the oldest folios. When she recited *the storm-caller's debt repaid in kind*, the hair on Silas's arms lifted and a low roll of thunder crossed a cloudless sky.

"Stop," Silas said hoarsely, after the third time the temperature dropped. "Just — stop for a moment. Please."

"I'm not doing anything."

"Yes you are. You're doing exactly what the entity does, except backwards. It eats memory. You're..." He struggled. Silas, who could call lightning, who had faced down a hollowed king without flinching, looked genuinely frightened of her. "You're *feeding* it back. Every word you remember is a word it can't take out of the world."

"That's absurd. I'm an archivist. We file things. We don't *manifest* them."

"Wren, you just made it snow indoors."

"...I made it *frost* indoors," she corrected, with dignity, and then looked down at her own hands and saw that they were faintly glowing, a pale gold shimmer along the knuckles like sunlight trapped under skin, and sat down very suddenly on a filing cabinet.

The glow faded after a moment. The cold did not.

"The outlawry," she said slowly, working it through the way she worked through anything: aloud, methodically, terrifying herself with each step. "When Vellamere banned magic, ninety years ago, the histories say the magic didn't leave. It was *bound*. Suppressed. Everyone always assumed that meant gone dormant. But suppression isn't deletion, Silas, it's pressure, and pressure has to go somewhere, and if the entity is a thing that consumes memory and the prophecy is a thing that preserves it—"

"Then reciting the prophecy might be loosening the knot."

"Might be tearing the seam," she said. "On a bag full of a century of everything Vellamere tried to bury."

Silence. Somewhere far above them, muffled by floors and stone, the bells of the palace rang the hour — and rang wrong, sour, the way bells ring when a bird lands on them mid-peal, except there were no birds in Vellamere anymore. There hadn't been birds in Vellamere since the outlawry.

"Oh," said Wren softly. "Oh, they're coming back, aren't they? The small things first."

* * *

By midnight, the archive was full of light.

Not lamplight. The books themselves had begun to glow, faintly, the old ones — the ones printed before the ban, the grimoires and herbals and star-charts that Wren had dutifully catalogued as *historical curiosities, non-functional* and shelved in the west stacks. Their spines pulsed like slow heartbeats. Frost spiraled up the windows in patterns that were unmistakably, deliberately language. And through the floor, from the depths of the building where the oldest sealed collections lived, came a sound like a great held breath finally being released.

Wren walked the aisles with her hand trailing along the shelves, feeling warmth bloom under her fingertips wherever she touched. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. It was also the single most illegal thing happening in Vellamere tonight, and it was happening everywhere at once, in her archive, which meant the crown would come here, and—

"We have to move the reconstruction," she said. "If the guards find us with rewritten prophecy pages while the entire city is visibly breaking its own laws—"

"They won't find the pages." Silas was at the window, looking out at streets that had begun, impossibly, to fill with people. Not fleeing. Gathering. Standing in the rain — when had it started raining? — with their faces turned upward, ordinary merchants and clerks and washerwomen, watching green light flicker inside the palace like summer lightning under a blanket. "Look at them. They're not afraid."

"People are rarely afraid until the soldiers arrive. Then they're extremely afraid, historically speaking, and I've read quite a lot of history."

He laughed — a real laugh, short and rough, the first one she'd gotten out of him since the vault. "Gods, you're steady. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else I've ever—" He stopped. A shadow crossed his face: the place where a memory used to be, worn smooth as a river stone. "Everyone I can remember knowing."

Wren wanted to ask. She wanted, badly, to pull that thread. But the ledger's reconstruction lay on the table between them, nine requisition forms covered in her handwriting, and the last lines still gapped and empty, and she understood — with the cold clarity of a woman whose entire life was organization — what those gaps meant.

The prophecy ended with the crown unmade. She remembered saying those words in the vault. She did not remember what followed, because there had been no time to read it properly before the vault spat them out, and now the only being who knew the ending was the entity itself, and it had chosen fire.

"Silas." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "We don't know how it ends. We know the beginning, we know most of the middle, but the ending — the part about the price — I never read it. It's gone. Truly gone. Unless—"

"Unless the dragon knows," Silas finished quietly.

Emberveil. Beneath the palace. Chained for a century above the anchor-point of the thing that wore the king, keeping the crown bound with the last dragon-fire in the world — and, if the marginalia in the ledger's earlier chapters were right, present at its writing. Old enough to remember words that had never been written down anywhere else.

Old enough to know what *repaid in kind* meant, and whether it was survivable.

Outside, the green light beneath the palace doors flared brighter, and the crowd gasped as one, and the rain fell harder, tasting — Wren tipped her head back at the open window, testing, archiving even now — of ozone and old blood. Exactly as the vault had tasted. As though the whole city had become the inside of the prophecy, closing around them page by page.

Somewhere across the rooftops, a bell that hadn't rung in ninety years began to ring itself.

"Whatever she asks," Silas said, pulling his coat on, not looking at Wren, looking at the palace, "whatever she wants in trade for the ending — don't agree to it before I do. Promise me."

"Silas—"

"*Promise me.* Because I've met things that bargain before, and I know how I answer them. And you're the one the prophecy needs alive until the final line. Not me."

Wren opened her mouth to argue — to point out, reasonably, that she was the one with the professional training in not making deals with primary sources — and then the door of the archive burst open, splinters and lamplight, and Captain Ives stood in the doorway with rain streaming off him and blood on his collar and three royal guardsmen fanning out behind him with drawn steel.

His eyes found Silas first. Then Wren. Something moved behind them — guilt, or grief, or their identical twin — and his mouth formed two silent words, aimed at her alone, before he gave the order to seize them:

*Burn it.*

And behind her, in her hands, the reconstructed pages began — gently, terribly, of their own accord — to catch fire.
