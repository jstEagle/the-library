## 8. The Hollow King Speaks

They did not drag Silas to the throne room. That was the first insult.

They *escorted* him — two guards on either side, gloved hands clamped on his arms, marching him up the palace's marble spine with the courtesy of men showing a guest to dinner. His wrists were bound with iron-cored cord that hummed faintly against his skin, drinking down the storm he'd spent five years pretending he didn't have. His coat was gone. His satchel of charms was gone. Somewhere behind him, Wren was being taken a different way, and the not-knowing where sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

"Nice palace," Silas said to the guard on his left, because silence had never been one of his talents. "Very echoey. You could charge admission."

The guard said nothing. The guard on the right said nothing. Silas decided they were both fired from any future career in hospitality.

* * *

The throne room was worse than echoey. It was *hungry*.

Silas felt it the moment the doors opened — a pressure like the air before lightning, but wrong, inverted, a silence that pulled instead of pushed. The great hall stretched away in columns of pale stone, banners of the Aldous line hanging motionless despite the draft, and at the far end, on a throne of black iron that looked like it had been grown rather than forged, sat King Aldous Vellamere.

The king was not what Silas expected. He was small. That was the horror of it — a narrow old man in a crown too heavy for him, hands folded in his lap like a father waiting up past a child's curfew. His face was kind, or had been built kind once, the way a puppet is built with a pleasant expression so children won't cry.

But his eyes.

Silas had seen the entity only once before, at the rite in Coronation Square — a glimpse, a wrongness behind the royal gaze, like seeing the shape of a fish through murky water. Now there was no murky water. Now the thing looked *out*, and it looked at Silas with the particular patience of something that had been waiting a very long time and had never once doubted it would get what it wanted.

"Leave us," said the king's mouth. The voice was the king's — dry, papery, tired. The *cadence* was not. It rolled like water finding cracks in a foundation.

The guards withdrew. The doors closed with a sound like a held breath.

"Silas Fairweather," the entity said through the king's throat, and then, with something almost like delight, "Storm-caller. Do you know, I have missed the taste of that name. I ate most of it, you know. But names are gristly. They catch in the teeth."

"You're welcome," Silas said. His voice came out steadier than his knees. "I'd offer you the rest, but I'm saving it."

The king's head tilted. The motion was too smooth, the joints of a man operated by something with a better understanding of men than men have. "You are frightened. Good. Fear is honest. I have had five hundred years of courtiers who flattered me — *him* — and not one of them honest from the neck down." The king unfolded one hand and gestured at the empty hall. "Do you know what a king is, Silas Fairweather? A king is a vessel. A crown is a lid. And underneath—" the hand turned, palm up, an offering, "—there is so much *room*."

"I've seen the room," Silas said. "I've seen what's left of him. I watched him bless a fishmonger's stall for eleven minutes because he couldn't remember how blessings end."

"He was a mediocre king," the entity said, with the mildness of a man discussing weather. "He is a better door. But you — ah, *you* were never mediocre, were you? Even hollowed, even exiled, even selling brass and bone to nervous clerks—" Silas flinched, and the entity's borrowed mouth curved. "Yes. I have watched you. Watching is most of what I do. It is dreadfully dull. I would recommend against a career in it."

* * *

"You want something," Silas said. "Entities that just want to watch don't arrange arrests. They don't burn archives." He thought of Wren, somewhere in this palace, and made himself keep talking. "You burned the ledger because it says you lose. So here's my question: what does a thing that eats memories offer a man with none?"

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against Silas's ears like deep water.

"Ask me properly," the entity said.

"No."

"Ask me *properly*," the entity repeated, and the pressure in the room doubled, tripled, the banners straining against their rods, and for one vertiginous moment Silas felt the thing's attention the way he once felt storms — vast, ancient, and utterly without malice, which was somehow worse. Malice you could hate. This was simply appetite, patient as geology.

"Fine." Silas lifted his chin. "What are you offering?"

The entity smiled with the king's kind face, and it was the cruelest thing Silas had ever seen, because the smile fit. It fit perfectly.

"Everything," it said.

* * *

It did not hand him memories the way a merchant hands over goods. It opened him.

One moment Silas was standing in the throne room, bound and cold. The next he was eight years old, standing in a palace courtyard in the rain, holding a kite string, and the kite was *alive* — it rode a spiral of wind he had made, his first storm, small and bright and entirely his, and a woman was laughing from a doorway. His mother. He knew her face. He had not known he'd lost it until it came back, and the coming-back was like a bone setting — relief and agony in the same white-hot instant.

*You were happy here,* the entity said, from everywhere. *Before. You were happy, and you were loved, and you were* strong.

The courtyard dissolved into a training ground. Silas at fifteen, calling thunder down on command while Captain Ives shouted corrections — "Left! Your storms drift left, boy, like your politics!" — and Silas laughing, soaked to the skin, powerful, *belonging*. The oath ceremony. His hand on the storm-glass. The king himself, younger, warmer, pinning the silver clasp to Silas's collar and saying, *Vellamere has not heard true weather in a generation. Show us the sky, Fairweather.*

Silas made a sound he was not proud of.

*It can all be yours again,* the entity murmured. *Every year. Every name. Your mother's voice. The taste of the rain you called yourself. I keep nothing badly — ask anyone I have eaten. They are not gone. They are* kept. *I will pour them back into you, and you will walk out of this room whole, and the archivist and the lizard and all the small desperate machinery of prophecy can burn without you.*

"And in exchange," Silas managed. His eyes were wet. He let them be. "I walk away."

"You do nothing so dramatic," the entity said. "You simply stop. The ledger's last line requires three names and a fire, and you will not be one of the names. The archivist will fail. The dragon will gutter. I will remain, fed and patient, in the only vessel in this city large enough to hold me, and you—" the king's head inclined, gracious, "—you will have your life back. All of it. Even the parts you never knew were missing."

* * *

Here is the thing about temptation that the ballads get wrong, Silas thought. The ballads make it a devil with a contract and a fountain pen. Nobody sings about temptation that looks like your mother's doorway. Nobody warns you that the trap is not that the offer is false. The trap is that the offer is *true*.

He could feel the memories waiting, just behind the entity's attention, the way you feel a gift behind someone's back. Five years he had lived as a man with a hole in the middle, selling luck to people who needed it, sleeping in rooms he couldn't make himself call home, flinching every time it rained because the rain remembered him when he couldn't remember himself. And all of it — the whole missing man — was one word away.

*Say yes,* said the entity, gently now, almost kind. *You have already lost so much. Why lose the rest for people you have known a fortnight?*

And Silas, standing in the hollow king's hall with his wrists bound and his heart in ruins, did the bravest and most difficult thing he had ever done.

He looked at the memory of his mother laughing in the doorway — really looked, drank it in, let it hurt — and said, "She wouldn't have liked you."

The entity went still.

"That woman in the courtyard," Silas said, and his voice cracked and he let it, "she raised a boy who called storms for the *joy* of it. Not for crowns. Not for kings. She'd take one look at whatever you are and put you over her knee." He swallowed. "And Wren Halloway — I've known her eleven days, and she reads marginalia like other people pray, and she hasn't once looked at me like I'm a hole where a man used to be. You're offering me my past. She gave me a *future*. Those aren't the same thing, and I finally—" he laughed, wet and unsteady, "—I finally understand why that matters."

The entity's expression did not change. The king's face held its kind, patient smile.

But the room got colder.

"You refuse," it said.

"I refuse."

"You will die refusing. The dragon's fire is nearly spent. The ledger is ash. The archivist is in my cells, and I have begun, gently, at the edges, to *read* her." Something moved behind the king's eyes then, and for the first time Silas saw the thing's true shape — not the patient courtier, not the geology, but hunger, vast and sudden and *afraid*. "You are a man with no past defending a future that does not exist yet. It is the worst bargain in five hundred years of bargains, Silas Fairweather."

"Then we're well matched," Silas said. "Because you're a five-hundred-year-old appetite hiding in a grandfather, and you're *scared of a book*."

The pressure in the room snapped like a wire.

The king rose from the throne — rose *wrong*, joints unfolding in an order joints don't use, the crown settling down over his brow like a lid on a pot about to boil — and when the entity spoke again, it did not bother with the king's voice at all. It spoke in a chorus of every voice it had ever eaten, and the throne room shook with it.

"Then you will watch the last line fail."

The doors burst open. Guards poured in — a dozen, two dozen — and behind them, dragged between two soldiers with her wrists bound and her lip split and her eyes absolutely *furious*, came Wren Halloway.

She looked at Silas. Silas looked at her.

"Nice of you to join me," he said. "The company's terrible."

"Silas," Wren said, and there was something in her voice — not fear, not despair. Urgency. She was trying to tell him something with her eyes, her bound hands, the angle of her chin, and it took him three precious seconds to understand.

She wasn't signaling *help*.

She was signaling *now*.

Above the palace, through the storm-glass windows, the sky over Vellamere — the sky that had been grey and tame for five long years — began, very quietly, to boil.

The entity heard it too. Silas watched it hear: the king's head snapping upward, the chorus of eaten voices faltering into one word, one small, horrified human word in the king's own voice —

"*Storm.*"
