## 9. Storm Over the Iron Throne

The throne room doors opened the way doors open in nightmares: slowly, and with an audience.

Silas came through them with his wrists bound in iron that drank at his skin like a leech, flanked by four guards who had the particular blankness of people who had stopped asking questions years ago. Ahead of him, the throne room of Vellamere stretched out in marble and banners, all of it lit by a thousand candles that guttered in a wind that wasn't there.

King Aldous sat upon the iron throne, and he was smiling.

It was a good smile, in the way a glove is a good hand. It sat where a smile should sit and did none of the work.

"Silas Fairweather," said the thing behind the king's eyes, and the king's mouth moved around the words like a man reciting someone else's letters. "Storm-caller. Oath-breaker. *Empty book.* Do you know how long I have waited for you to be brought to me?"

"I get that a lot," Silas said. "People find me very collectible."

The guards flinched. The king did not.

"You were mine once," the entity said. "Did you know that? Before the exile. Before I ate the years out of you like marrow from a bone. You knelt in this room and swore an oath, and when you tried to unsay it, I took your payment myself." The smile widened by a fraction, a dial turned. "I have kept them all this time. Your memories. Wrapped in silk, as it were. Every one."

Silas's heart did something complicated and painful.

Because he could feel them. That was the cruelty of it — the moment the entity said *wrapped in silk*, something behind the wall of his emptiness stirred, like a cat hearing its name. Not images. Not yet. Just the *weight* of them, the shape of a life pressing against the inside of his absence, warm as a body under a blanket.

Five years he had grieved a person he couldn't remember being. And the grief had been the only proof the person existed.

"They're real," he said quietly. "You weren't lying about that part."

"I never lie," said the entity, which was the most dishonest thing it had said all evening. "I *edit*. Here is my offer, storm-caller, and I will make it only once. The archivist and the dragon are beneath us even now, and they will fail, because they were always going to fail — prophecy is merely the universe's first draft. But you. You I have use for. Give me the storm you are carrying — give it to me freely, as you once gave your oath — and I will return to you every stolen year. Your mother's kitchen. Your first lightning. Your name as your father spoke it." The king's head tilted. "You could be a whole man again by morning. All it costs you is everyone you've met since."

And there it was. The arithmetic, laid out neat and terrible on the marble.

Five years of Wren Halloway — her ink-stained fingers, her laugh that arrived a half-second after everyone else's, the way she said *oh* when she understood something, which was constantly, which was the most beautiful sound in Vellamare. Five years, weighed against a lifetime he had been mourning in his bones.

"You're hesitating," the entity observed, and it sounded almost gentle. "That's honest of you."

"I'm not hesitating," Silas said. "I'm doing math. I'm bad at math. Give me a moment."

*Wren,* he thought, hard, the way you press your thumb into a wound to remember it's yours. *Wren, Emberveil, the vault, the ledger, the rain, the terrible tea, the* —

Above the throne room, through the high windows, the sky went the color of a bruise.

* * *

He had sworn off storms the way some men swear off drink: completely, publicly, and with a white-knuckle grip on the memory — no, on the *idea* — of what it had cost him. For five years the storm had lived coiled under his ribs like a second heart, and he had fed it nothing, and it had grown anyway, patient as a debt.

Now he unclenched.

It began, as it always began, with a smell: rain on hot stone. Then the iron cuffs on his wrists turned to frost and split like bread. The guards went down — not dead, he was careful, even now, *especially* now — simply asleep, their dreams suddenly full of thunder. Silas walked toward the throne through a room that was learning, candle by candle, to be afraid of him.

"You swore," the entity said, and for the first time the king's voice cracked around the edges of the word, showing something underneath that did not wear a crown. "You *swore* you would never —"

"I've been told," Silas said, "that I have a history of breaking oaths. It's apparently my best quality."

The first bolt took the banners off the east wall. The second took the windows out entirely, and Vellamere's night poured in, rain and wind and the smell of a city waking up to the fact that magic had never actually left it. The candles died. The throne room lit itself instead, blue-white and stuttering, every flash a photograph of the king's face — and beneath the king's face, for one stolen frame each time, the thing that wore him: vast, gray, *hungry*, its attention narrowing on Silas like a noose.

"You cannot kill me with weather," it said.

"No," Silas agreed. Thunder rolled his bones like dice. "But I can keep you busy."

Because that was the plan — the whole mad plan, hatched in a dragon's den with a bleeding archivist and a lizard the size of a cathedral. The entity was anchored to the world by two chains: the dragon-fire that fed it, and the king it inhabited. Wren had the fire. Silas had the king. And Silas had one other thing, the thing the entity had made the mistake of showing him, wrapped in silk.

His memories. Still *in* the entity. Still part of it, the way a swallowed stone is part of the swallow.

"You feel it, don't you," Silas said, walking forward through his own storm, hair standing like a saint in a painting, rain streaming down his face and steaming off his skin. "Every step I take toward you, they pull. You kept them too close. You kept them *warm*."

"Stop."

"They're a door," Silas said. "You built it yourself."

"**Stop.**"

The entity came out of the king then, all of it at once, the glove turned inside out — a gray vastness filling the throne room like smoke fills a bottle, and Silas, who had spent five years being an empty book, stood in the middle of it and was not afraid.

Because an empty book, Wren had told him once, ink on her fingers, gentle as a surgeon: *an empty book is just waiting for a hand.*

The entity poured into him, into the vacancy it had made, into the space it knew so well, and for one endless heartbeat Silas Fairweather was full — full of his mother's kitchen, full of his first lightning, full of his name in his father's mouth, full of the oath and the breaking of it, full of every year the thing had eaten —

— and he *held the door shut behind it.*

* * *

Far below, in the dark where the dragon was, Wren Halloway was doing her half. He didn't know that. He trusted it the way you trust the tide.

The dragon-fire came up through the palace's bones like dawn through a keyhole. It found the entity where it coiled inside him, and the entity *screamed*, and Silas, holding the door, holding all of it, understood the final line of the ledger at last — the one Wren had recited with blood on her lip while the pages burned:

*The storm must give what the crown has taken.*

Not fight it. Not banish it. *Feed it to the fire.*

His memories. The recovered years, the whole stolen lifetime, himself entire — the only bait big enough, the only chain strong enough, the only door the entity would walk through and never walk out of. Dragon-fire didn't destroy memory. It *unbound* it, set it loose into the air the way Emberveil's fire had once been loose in the sky. But to feed them, he had to let go of the door. To end the thing, he had to end the having.

The entity thrashed inside him, and its thrashing was *him*, his whole life convulsing, and somewhere very far away a king was gasping on marble as the glove slipped off his hands at last.

*Do it,* said a voice that was not the entity. It was low and vast and old, and it smelled of warm stone and cinnamon, and it was the dragon, speaking through the fire itself, through the chain. *Storm-caller. It is a good bargain. I would know. I made the bad one.*

"I know," Silas said aloud, to no one, to everyone, to the rain.

He thought of Wren. He thought — deliberately, thoroughly, the way you pack a suitcase — of her laugh arriving late, of her saying *oh*, of her hand finding his in the dark under the city, of her writing his name in a ledger that had known it a hundred years before he did.

And then, because grief was a thing you could carry or a thing you could spend, and he had always been better at spending —

He opened the door and walked his life into the fire.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the strange part. It felt like the moment at the end of a long day when you finally put down the thing you'd been carrying and your arms float up on their own, bewildered by lightness.

The entity went with them, unraveling, shrieking down to a whisper, down to a thread, down to nothing at all — a gray smoke burned clean, an edit reversed, a debt forgiven by the only court that had jurisdiction.

The storm spent itself against the walls, gentle now, the way a dog settles after barking. The rain through the broken windows fell soft as an apology.

Silas Fairweather stood in the ruins of the throne room, in the blue after-silence, holding nothing.

King Aldous was on his knees on the marble, weeping, hollow, *free* — a man waking from a century of someone else's dream, with nothing behind his eyes but the ordinary human fog of a man who cannot remember why he is crying. Alive. Emptied, but his own.

Silas looked at him and felt — he searched for it, patiently, the way you search your pockets — and found nothing where his past should be. No mother's kitchen. No first lightning. No name in his father's mouth. Just a warm ash where a life had been, and, impossibly, sitting right in the middle of the ash, one small coal that refused to go out:

The feeling of having chosen rightly.

He laughed. It came out cracked and wet and surprised him.

"Fairweather," he said, testing the name. It fit like a borrowed coat. It would do.

The doors at the far end of the ruined hall banged open, and there she was — Wren Halloway, soot to the elbows, a dragon-scale burn scarred pink across one palm, limping, filthy, *triumphant*, with a ledger clutched to her chest like a child. She stopped when she saw him. Her face did something complicated.

"You don't remember me," she said. Not a question.

"No," Silas said. "But I have the distinct impression I was about to be very glad to see you."

Wren Halloway looked at him for a long moment. Then she opened the ledger to a fresh page, took out her pen, and held it over the paper with the air of a woman beginning the most important work of her life.

"Silas Fairweather," she said. "Age unknown. Profession: charm-seller, allegedly." The pen touched down. "Chapter one."
