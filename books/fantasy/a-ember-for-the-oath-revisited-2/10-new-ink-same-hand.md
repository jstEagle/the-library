## 10. New Ink, Same Hand

Wren Halloway had catalogued ten thousand books in her life, and she had never once been afraid of a blank page. She was afraid of this one.

It lay open on the reading table of the archive's east wing — the wing that smelled now of smoke and rain instead of dust and old leather — and it was bound in the same grey calfskin as the ledger that had started everything. She had not chosen it. It had simply been there that morning, sitting on the shelf where the old prophecy book used to live, as if the archive itself had decided the story wasn't finished.

The first page was blank. The second. The third.

She dipped her pen anyway.

Outside the tall windows, Vellamere was doing something Wren had never seen it do: it was being *loud*. Not the ordinary clatter of carts and clerks, but cheering — actual cheering, ragged and off-key, drifting up from the square where three days ago a king had stood on his balcony with someone else's hunger behind his eyes. Bunting had appeared overnight. Someone had strung it between the archive's own columns, which was technically a violation of six preservation codes, and Wren found she could not bring herself to care.

The king had woken at dawn, they said. Hollow-eyed, quiet, asking for a list of his own ministers' names because he could not remember them — but free. Free in the way a house is free after the fire is out: scorched, standing, and smelling faintly of something terrible that no longer lives there. He had wept when they told him what he'd done under the crown's influence, and then, remarkably, he had asked for paper. He was writing letters of apology, they said. There were going to be a great many of them.

And above the palace, above all of it, Emberveil had risen.

Wren had watched from these same windows, her knuckles white on the sill, as the last dragon in the world climbed out of the pit beneath the throne room like dawn deciding to happen early. Her chains had fallen away mid-air — not broken, not struck, simply *released*, dropping into the clouds below her like shed winter coats. She had circled the city once, low enough that her shadow crossed every street, and the people of Vellamere — a city that had outlawed magic for a hundred years, a city built on the proud belief that wonder was a tax fraud waiting to be committed — had come out of their houses and looked up and *cheered*.

Then she had gone east, toward the mountains, trailing fire like a banner, and Wren had cried into a handkerchief she would later deny owning.

* * *

Silas found her at noon.

She heard him before she saw him — the particular careful tread of a man navigating a world he no longer recognized, pausing at the archive door as though it might have opinions about him. When she looked up, he was standing just inside, dripping slightly from the drizzle, holding two meat pies wrapped in paper like they were evidence of a crime.

"Hello," he said. "I'm told I know you."

Her chest did something complicated. "You're told correctly."

"The baker gave me these." He set them down on the table with exaggerated care, as if pies might detonate. "He said, 'Tell Wren you owe her for the storm,' and then he refused payment and also refused to explain. This has happened eleven times today. A woman kissed my cheek outside the chandlery. I don't know who she is. I'm fairly sure I've never sold anyone a luck-charm in my life, and yet an entire fish market appears to disagree."

"You did," Wren said. "They were brass and bone and they worked, which annoyed everyone including you."

He pulled out the chair across from her and sat, slowly, studying her face the way he'd probably studied every face today — searching for himself in other people's expressions. "They keep telling me things. That I called a storm over the palace. That I fed my memories to a dragon." His voice stayed level, but his hands didn't; he folded them on the table to make them stop. "That I chose it. Willingly."

"Yes."

"And everyone says it like I should be sad." He glanced up at her, and there it was — the thing she'd first seen in him across a market stall, the weariness cracked open by curiosity, except now the weariness was gone and only the curiosity remained, bright and unburdened. "But here's the strange part, Wren. I can't miss what I can't remember. And underneath everything — under all the nothing where a life should be — there's just this..." He pressed a fist lightly against his sternum. "*Feeling*. Like having paid a debt. Like walking out of a building you're glad to leave. Is that grief? It doesn't feel like grief."

Wren thought of the throne room. Of Silas standing in the wreckage with lightning running off him like water, feeding five years of stolen sky into dragon-fire, his face not anguished but *settled* — the face of a man setting down something heavy at last.

"No," she said. "I think it's relief. Grief is for later, maybe. Or maybe never. You earned the right to skip it."

"Hm." He considered this, then reached for a pie and bit into it, and made a small startled sound of pleasure. "Oh, that's good. Do I like these? Did I like these?"

"You complained about them constantly and bought one every single day."

His grin was sudden and crooked and entirely new-old. "Then some things survive erasure. Excellent. Tell me more. Start at the beginning. Apparently I have a beginning."

* * *

So she told him.

She told him about the charm stall and the nervous clerk and the way he'd hidden the storm coiled under his skin like a man hiding a cough in church. She told him about the ledger with his name inked beside tonight's date a century before either of them was born, and how reading it aloud had pulled them bodily through the floor. She told him about the vault that tasted of ozone and old blood, about the king's eyes, about the chase through the rain-slick streets while he confessed a past he'd already lost once. She told him about Emberveil chained beneath the palace, about Captain Ives and the key slipped like a confession into Wren's pocket, about pages burning and a recitation delivered through bloodied lips while the city's outlawed magic woke beneath the cobbles like spring.

He listened without interrupting, which she suspected was not in his nature, and twice he laughed — once at the clerk ("Did he ever get promoted?" "He runs the treasury now." "Good for him"), and once, helplessly, at the image of Captain Ives attempting to look casual while committing treason.

At the end he was quiet a long moment.

"So the memories it offered me back," he said. "In the throne room. Before I chose the fire instead."

"It showed you the boy you were. You told me so, after. You said—" She stopped.

"Said what?"

"That seeing him was the hardest part. But that you were glad he existed, even if he couldn't come home."

Silas absorbed this. Then, softly: "He sounds like someone worth having been." He turned his hands over, examining the palms as though the old life might be written there. "I keep waiting to feel robbed. Everyone watches me like I'm a widow at a funeral. But I feel — Wren, I feel *light*. Whatever I burned, I burned it carrying it too long. Five years of borrowed sky, someone called it. Sounds about right. Sky's heavy."

"Very," Wren agreed solemnly. "Notorious for it."

He laughed again, and she thought: *there*. That was the sound worth a hundred ledgers.

* * *

Evening came down soft over Vellamere, gold where the smoke had cleared. Wren turned to a fresh page in the grey book, and Silas leaned over her shoulder, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him, watching the blankness with frank suspicion.

"What are you writing?"

"A new ledger. The old one ended." She hesitated. "Prophecy books are strange things. They don't record the future so much as argue with it. The entity burned its own defeat and lost anyway, because defeat isn't a line of text — it's a choice. So I thought... perhaps a book that records choices instead. Starting small."

"With what?"

"With names." She set the nib to paper. "Starting with yours."

She wrote:

*Silas Fairweather. Storm-caller. Arrived in this story by way of a luck-charm and left it by way of a storm. Remembers none of it. Chose all of it.*

She read it aloud, and he was silent, and when she glanced up his eyes were wet, which seemed to surprise him more than anything else that day.

"I don't know why that's making me cry," he said, baffled. "It's just words."

"They're your words. Same hand, new ink." She passed him the pen. "Your turn. Write yourself a line. Anything. It's how the book decides you exist."

He took the pen like it might bite, hovered, and then wrote slowly, tongue between his teeth:

*Silas Fairweather likes meat pies, apparently, and is going to find out what else he likes, starting tomorrow, starting with the archivist, if she'll let him.*

Wren read it twice. Her ears went warm. "That's not proper ledger format."

"Is there a format?"

"There's now."

"Then I'm establishing precedent." He set down the pen and looked at her — really looked, with nothing behind his eyes but the man he was choosing to be. "Tomorrow, then. And the day after. I'll need someone to tell me who I was until I've decided who I am. You seem qualified."

"I charge by the hour."

"I'll pay in pies."

Below the windows, the city glowed, and far to the east, high over the mountains, something vast and bright crossed the face of the rising moon — one slow, deliberate circle, as if checking on them — before it flew on toward whatever skies were still unborrowed.

Wren turned to a new page, and began.
