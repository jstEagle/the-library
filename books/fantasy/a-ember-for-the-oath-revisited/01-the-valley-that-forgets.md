## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with Hollowmere, Zephyrine Winterbourne had decided over thirty years of careful observation, was that it was almost perfect.

Not quite perfect—perfection invited attention, and attention was the one thing the valley could not afford. No, Hollowmere was perfect the way a well-made chair is perfect: solid, useful, unremarkable, and never once in its existence likely to make anyone look twice. The mountains cupped it like two weathered hands. The river ran clear and slightly dull, the way water does when nothing interesting lives in it. The pines on the ridgeline stood in rows so orderly they might have been planted by a committee of very boring men.

And it was quiet. Not peaceful-quiet—the hush of a chapel or a snowfall—but quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Zephyrine felt it most mornings as she stepped out to feed the goats: a pressure behind her ears, faint as a headache deciding whether to become one. The valley sat under that silence the way a table sits under a cloth. Everything underneath was still there. Nothing was permitted to move.

She had lived with it so long she sometimes forgot it was there at all. Mostly.

"Good morning," she told the goats, because someone ought to be told good morning, even if no one answered. "Yes, I know. You're starving. You're always starving. It's a miracle any of you survived before I came."

Bramble, her oldest—a grey-muzzled doe with one crooked horn and the permanent expression of a woman reading disappointing correspondence—regarded her with yellow eyes and said nothing. Bramble never said anything. None of them did. In thirty years, not one goat had bleated a single word worth hearing, because in Hollowmere there were no words worth hearing anywhere. The animals here made sounds the way furniture makes creaks: mechanically, meaninglessly, with nothing behind the noise.

Zephyrine had learned, early and thoroughly, to find this comforting.

* * *

Her cottage sat at the valley's western edge where the meadow gave up and the birch scrub began, close enough to the village for market days and far enough that no one visited without purpose. She'd chosen the spot herself, or rather she had let the spot choose her, arriving one grey October with a pack, a limp she had exaggerated for sympathy, and a story about a widowed herb-wife looking for quiet. The story was true in every detail except the ones that mattered.

Thirty years. Long enough that the girl who had crept over the border in the dark was a story even to herself. Long enough that the villagers of Lower Hollowmere thought of her the way they thought of the mill or the church bell or the bad stretch of road past Tanner's field—as a fixture, unremarkable and unexamined. *Old Widow Winterbourne.* Well. Not old-old. Forty-nine, if she was honest, though she dressed older on principle and walked slower than she needed to when anyone watched. A woman who seemed sixty got asked fewer questions than a woman who seemed forty-nine.

She kept goats because a herb-wife without livestock was a curiosity, and curiosities got looked at. She sold simples and salves, feverfew and comfrey, a willow-bark tincture the miller swore by and the reeve's wife bought by the bottle for her headaches, which everyone politely agreed were caused by the miller. Twice a week she walked down to the village square with a basket and traded, and twice a week she performed the small theater of ordinariness that kept her invisible: complaining about rain, admiring babies, losing gracefully at the butcher's endless argument about whether yarrow or plantain drew a splinter better. (Plantain. Obviously. But she lost anyway.)

"You'll want the comfrey for the knee," she told Goodwife Ansel at the square, wrapping the leaves in brown paper. "And you'll want to stop kneeling in the wet garden pulling weeds at your age, but we both know you won't listen, so the comfrey it is."

"My mother used comfrey," Goodwife Ansel said, taking it. "You're the only one who still grows it proper."

"Someone has to keep the old ways." Zephyrine smiled the smile that went with the sentence—warm, a little rueful, entirely empty. *The old ways.* If the woman knew what old ways lived under this valley's silence, she'd have run for the lowlands screaming.

That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement. Magic was hunted in the world beyond the mountains; the Oathwardens in their grey cloaks saw to it with fire and ledger-book, and the law they served had teeth enough that whole bloodlines had been scoured from the maps. So the valley of Hollowmere had been made magic-dead generations ago—warded, smothered, sealed—and anything wild that wandered in went dumb as a stone. And Zephyrine Winterbourne, who had been born able to speak with beasts, who had spent her first nineteen years chattering with hawks and foxes and once, gloriously, an ill-tempered river-horse who'd wanted her opinion on mud, lived out her life among animals that could not answer her.

It was, she reminded herself while she counted coins into her basket, a good trade. Her mother had thought so. Safety for silence. A long gray life instead of a short bright one.

Some mornings she even believed it.

* * *

Tuesdays were bread days, and bread day meant Tobias Grell, which meant vigilance.

Grell kept the counting-house and considered himself the valley's second-most-important resident after the reeve, a ranking he maintained through sheer force of noticing things. He noticed everything. He noticed that Zephyrine bought lamp oil in quantities he found suspiciously steady for a woman who claimed to sleep at dusk. He noticed, three years ago, that her chimney smoked differently on windy days, and had stood in the lane squinting at it for so long she'd started burning damp wood for a month just to give him something boring to conclude. He was not cruel. He was not clever, exactly, either—he was *thorough*, which was worse, because cleverness tires and thoroughness does not.

"Winterbourne." He tipped his hat the exact degree politeness required. "Heard your dog barking all last night."

"I haven't a dog."

"Mm." His eyes did their slow inventory: her basket, her boots, her face. "Could have sworn."

"Perhaps it was a fox."

"Foxes don't bark all night."

"They do when it's courting season."

Something flickered behind his eyes—the frustration of a man offered a perfectly reasonable explanation he couldn't disprove. "Courting season," he repeated, writing something down. She was nearly certain he wrote everything down. There was a ledger in that counting-house, she suspected, with her name at the top of a page, and beneath it thirty years of harmless entries: *bark heard. Fox? Courting season?* It should have frightened her more than it did. Instead it felt almost companionable. Somewhere in Hollowmere, at least one person paid attention to whether she existed.

"Enjoy your bread," she said pleasantly, and walked home doing sums about foxes.

* * *

Autumn came late to the valley and left early, and by the third week of Falling Leaf the birches along her fence line had gone the color of struck matches. Zephyrine spent the bright cold hours putting the garden to bed—cutting back the comfrey, banking the beds with straw, muttering complaints at the frost the way gardeners do, half prayer and half insult.

It was on the fourth evening, with the light going amber and thin, that the crow came.

She heard the wingbeats first—that dry leather rustle—and looked up from the straw to see it drop out of the sky and land on her fence rail, six feet away, bold as a tax collector.

Crows came to Hollowmere often enough. They came for the compost, mostly, and they behaved exactly as valley birds behaved: greedy, silent, empty-eyed. This one was different. It was larger than the local birds, its black shot through with an oily blue sheen she hadn't seen on a valley crow in thirty years, and it landed the way travelers land—like something that had come from elsewhere, from over the ridge, from the world where wings still meant something.

Zephyrine went very still, straw in hand, heart performing a slow complicated maneuver against her ribs.

Because she knew what crows were supposed to say. Every child of the wild places grew up knowing it. Crows were gossips, jesters, carriers of news and insults; a crow's greeting ran to *shiny-things, shiny-things, dead-man's-bread*, and their commentary on human farming practices was unprintable. Thirty years of silence had not erased the knowledge of what she was missing. It had only pressed it flat, like flowers in a book.

The crow tilted its head. One eye, black and round and lit with something almost like intelligence, fixed on her.

*Say it,* she thought, absurdly, the way one thinks at a door one is afraid to knock on. *Go on. Say something rude. Tell me my fence is crooked. Tell me there's a dead elk by the river. Say anything.*

The crow opened its beak.

"Caw," it said.

Just that. Just the sound, hollow and mechanical, a bell with no ringer. It hopped once along the rail, opened its beak again—she saw the effort of it, saw the throat work, saw the eye go briefly, terribly frustrated—and produced another bare *caw*. Then a third. Like a man speaking through clenched teeth. Like a word being strangled somewhere on its way out.

Then it stopped trying. It looked at her a moment longer with an expression she would spend the rest of the night failing to name—apology, maybe, or warning, or simply the blank courtesy of a messenger whose letter has been confiscated—and lifted off the fence and flew west, toward the border ridge, quick and straight, as if late for something.

Zephyrine stood in her dying garden until the light went out of the sky.

A crow that tried to speak. A crow that *wanted* to speak. In thirty years she had never once seen a valley animal strain against the quiet like a man straining against locked doors. The silence of Hollowmere was total; it had always been total; it was the foundation her whole careful life was built on. Either she had imagined the whole thing—

She had not imagined it. She was fifty years old and she had lied to kings' men and out-waited wardens and buried her own mother, and she did not imagine things.

—or else something under the valley was changing.

"Nonsense," she said aloud, to the dark, to the goats, to herself. Her voice sounded strange in the empty yard, the way voices always did here, too loud and then instantly gone, swallowed by the quiet like a coin dropped in deep water. "One crow. Birds are birds. Go to bed, Zephyrine Winterbourne, and stop inventing ghosts."

She banked the fire, checked the latch, lay down in the narrow bed under the eaves. Above the rafters the stars wheeled, indifferent. Below the floor, below the roots, below the deep stone of the valley itself, something vast and old held its breath around her, the way it had every night of her exile.

Except tonight, she thought, drifting, it had sounded—for just a moment, in the space where the crow's word should have been—except tonight it had sounded less like a held breath.

It had sounded like a breath running out.

* * *

In the morning there was frost on the inside corners of the windows, which was unusual, and the goats were standing at the far end of their paddock facing west, all five of them in a row, silent and attentive, like an audience waiting for a play to begin.

"Bramble," Zephyrine called, bucket in hand. "Breakfast. Whatever you're looking at, it hasn't got breakfast."

Bramble turned. The old doe crossed the frozen grass slowly, deliberately, and stopped a hand's length away, and looked up into Zephyrine's face with those flat yellow eyes, and did something she had never done in thirty years.

She shook her head. Slowly. Left, right, left. The way a beast says *no* when it understands the question.

Zephyrine stood very still in the white cold of the morning, the bucket forgotten in her hand, listening to the enormous silence of the valley press down around them both.

"That's impossible," she whispered. "You can't mean anything. Nothing here means anything."

Bramble held her gaze a moment longer—patient, grave, unmistakable—and then turned back to the western fence, where the first snowflakes of the season were beginning to fall, out of a sky that had no clouds.
