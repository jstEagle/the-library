## 2. What the Goats Won't Say

The trick to living thirty years without a voice was learning when to use one.

Zephyrine had worked it out early, the way you work out which floorboards creak: speak in the market, and only about weather; hum at church, but never the old melodies; laugh in company exactly twice per gathering, no more, because a woman who laughed too easily was a woman paying attention, and a woman paying attention might be asked what she found so funny, and the honest answer — *the magpie just told me your hat looks like a startled hedge* — was not an answer that let a person grow old in peace.

So by day she spent her voice like a miser counting coins. But at night —

At night she went out to the goat shed.

It was a ritual worn smooth as a river stone now, so familiar she barely thought of it as magic anymore. It was simply *the other half of her day*, the half that didn't count, the half nobody saw. She'd light no lamp — lamps drew eyes — and she'd settle onto the milking stool with her shawl around her shoulders and her hands folded in her lap, and for a moment she would only breathe. The goats would shuffle and stamp and arrange themselves, because they knew. Thirty years of goats, generation after generation, and every one of them had known within a week that something different happened after dark.

Tonight it was snowing again, fat unhurried flakes that ticked against the shed's roof like fingers drumming on a table. Zephyrine closed her eyes, found the place behind her sternum where the gift lived — it had always felt like that, a warm pocket, a coin of heat sewn into her chest — and opened it, carefully, the way you'd open a door into a room you weren't supposed to be in.

*Good evening,* she said, in the way that wasn't words. The way that was smell and posture and pressure and something older than all of them.

The answering chorus came at once, the way it always did, a wash of small simple minds like pebbles tumbling down a hillside.

*Evening-evening-evening-warm-hay-good*, from the young ones, who thought in bundles rather than sentences.

*Cold*, said Marigold, who complained professionally and took great pride in it.

*Snow is falling on my horns and I have chosen to be at peace with this*, said Clover, who was either very wise or very dim; after twelve years Zephyrine still hadn't decided.

And then, from the corner stall, dry as a page turning:

*You're late.*

Bramble. Twenty-two years old if she was a day, grey-muzzled, yellow-eyed, with the temperament of a retired schoolmistress who had seen every trick a student could pull and graded them all harshly. Zephyrine had raised her from a kid back in the years when raising anything felt like the only proof she existed.

*I'm not late,* Zephyrine answered. *I'm precisely when I always am.*

*Precisely when you always are,* Bramble agreed, *is late. You used to come at dusk. Now you come when the stars have moved. You're rationing yourself even out here, girl. Even from us.*

"I don't—" The human words slipped out before she could catch them, a whisper, and she pressed her lips shut. Old habit. Even here. Even alone, some sentry in her skull stood watch over her mouth.

Bramble's ears swiveled. In the beast-speech, her attention felt like a hand laid flat on a table — patient, immovable. *Say it properly. You know I hate receiving through a keyhole.*

So Zephyrine breathed out, unclenched the last inward fist, and said it properly. *I said: I don't ration myself from you.*

*No?* Bramble chewed, unhurried. The sound of it filled the little shed, companionable as a hearth. *What do you call this, then? A quarter hour of speech, stolen at midnight, from animals, in a locked shed, while you watch the window more than you watch us. If that isn't rationing, it's a very good imitation of it.*

Zephyrine laughed — really laughed, low and unguarded, the sound strange and rusty even to her own ears. That was the cruelty and the kindness of the goats both: they were the only creatures alive who ever heard her laugh.

*Mock me all you like,* she said. *You'll still stand there tomorrow with your hoof in the bucket.*

*That,* said Bramble, with dignity, *was one time, and the bucket was asking for it.*

The young ones tittered — a ripple through the herd-mind like wind through barley — and settled, and for a while the ritual ran along its comfortable grooves. This was the shape of it, most nights. Not conversation so much as company. Zephyrine told them about the market, translating the day into smells and pictures: the new flour, the argument between the miller and his brother, the first snow's particular hush. The goats gave her back their own news — Clover had found a weakness in the fence line and wished it reported; Marigold had opinions about the quality of this year's hay that she wished reported loudly — and Zephyrine listened, and answered, and was, for three-quarters of an hour, entirely herself.

It should have been enough. Some nights she could almost convince herself it was.

But tonight, sitting in the straw-smelling dark with snow ticking overhead, she felt the ache of it the way you feel a tooth you've been ignoring — suddenly, and all at once, and deep. A gift with no witness. That was the phrase she'd never dared think until recently, and now it came nightly, patient as frost. Because that was the truth under the coziness of these evenings: the goats loved her, but they did not understand her. They received her warmth and her pictures and returned their small bright simplicities, and it was lovely, it kept her alive, and it was also like being embraced by someone through a wall. Thirty years of speaking into a wall that leaned affectionately back.

She wanted — and here was the thought she usually caught and drowned before it finished being born — she wanted one creature who could answer in kind. Who could say something back that she hadn't already thought.

*You're doing it again,* Bramble observed.

*Doing what?*

*Sitting in the middle of a full room and starving.*

Zephyrine scratched the old goat's ears, feeling the fine tremble of pleasure run through her, and said nothing, which Bramble permitted, briefly, out of respect for the scratching.

Then Bramble pulled away, turned her head, and fixed Zephyrine with one yellow eye. And Zephyrine felt the temperature of the shed change — felt the herd go quiet around her, the way birdsong stops when a hawk crosses the sun.

*Girl,* Bramble said. *Listen to me now, properly, because I've been chewing on this for three nights and I'm done chewing.*

*You're frightening me.*

*Good. Fear sharpens the ear.* The old goat stepped closer. Her mind, in the speech, had always felt like a well-kept ledger, everything neat and dated — and now the ledger lay open, and Zephyrine read the entries and did not like them. *The quiet is thinning.*

Zephyrine sat back on the stool. *What does that mean?*

*Exactly what it says. The quiet. Your quiet — the deadness, the hush, the thing you hide inside. It's thinning. Like ice toward spring.* Bramble's breath steamed. *I am twenty-two years old, which makes me older than every fence post in this valley and most of its opinions, and I have lived my whole life inside this hush, and I am telling you that lately, at night, I can feel the edges of it. Like a blanket with holes. Cold coming through in places there was no cold before.*

*That's not possible,* Zephyrine said, and heard immediately how much she sounded like everyone else in Hollowmere, saying that exact thing about everything, always. *The ward doesn't thin. It doesn't fray. It's a wall, not a—*

*A wall?* Something moved through the herd then, unease passed nose to nose, flank to flank, the collective shiver of prey animals. Bramble lowered her head. *Child. Every winter for thirty years I have dreamed the same dreamless sleep, because that is what the ward allows — nothing reaches us here. No far voices. No wild speech. Nothing. Two weeks ago I woke at moonset with the taste of pine in my mouth. I have never eaten pine in my life. Three days ago, the crow on your fence —*

*Was silent,* Zephyrine finished, and a cold finger walked up her spine. *I saw. I've been telling myself it meant nothing.*

*Crows are never silent,* Bramble said. *Crows are chatterers, thieves, gossips, boasters. A crow that holds its tongue is a crow holding its breath. And things that hold their breath, girl, are waiting for something to pass — or waiting for something to arrive.*

The snow ticked on the roof. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved down the valley, and Zephyrine realized she was listening to it the way she'd learned to listen to everything — straining past the ordinary sounds for the extraordinary ones, for the voices that were not supposed to exist here, hoping and dreading in the same breath.

*You mistake me if you think I want the old world back,* Bramble went on, gentler now. *I remember nothing of it, thank whatever watches over goats. But you remember. And you've gone pale as milk, and your heart is going like a rabbit's, and you keep looking at the window. So I'll say the rest plainly: something is coming. Or something has already come. The quiet is thinning, and things thin for two reasons — because they're ending, or because something on the other side is wearing through.*

*Stop,* Zephyrine said. Not angrily. Wearily. She stood, and the stool scraped, and the young ones started at the sound. *Please stop. Bramble. Do you know what happens to me if the ward fails? There's a law older than the kingdom. Wardens in grey. They take speakers, and they don't take them kindly. My whole life is built on this valley's silence like a house is built on a foundation. You're telling me the foundation is rotting, and you expect me to do — what? What would you have me do?*

The old goat considered her for a long moment. Outside, the wind rose a little, sighed against the shed, and fell.

*Nothing tonight,* Bramble said at last. *Tonight, scratch my ears and tell me about the miller's brother, because I enjoy pettiness and you tell it well.*

Zephyrine huffed out something that wanted to be a laugh and couldn't quite afford to be. *He accused him of shorting the grain sacks by a thumb's width.*

*A thumb's width!*

*A thumb's width. There were words. There may be litigation.*

*There is no justice in this world for a wronged sack,* said Bramble gravely, and presented her ears for scratching, and the shed's fear dissolved back into its usual warmth — the small safe rituals, the complaints about hay, the comfort of being understood up to a line and no further.

But Zephyrine stayed later than she should have. And when she finally rose to go, Bramble spoke once more, quietly, so the herd wouldn't carry it.

*I dismissed it too, once,* the goat said. *A bad taste on the wind. A silent crow. Little things. But little things are how big things knock, girl. They always knock small first.*

*Go to sleep, you gloomy old matriarch,* Zephyrine said fondly, and doused the last ember of her gift like pinching out a candle, and the world went mute around her, familiar and total, a cellar door closing.

She crossed the yard with snow gathering on her shoulders. Above the valley the clouds had torn open, and the stars burned hard and close, and Hollowmere lay beneath them silvered and utterly still — no owl, no fox-scream, not even the creak of a distant barn. Thirty years, and she had called that stillness safety. Peace. Home.

Standing in her own dooryard, listening to a silence that suddenly felt less like a blanket and more like held breath, she found she could no longer say which it was.

Inside, she banked the fire and lay down and did not sleep. And near dawn — though she'd spend the whole next day persuading herself otherwise — she heard it, faint and far off, at the very ragged edge of the ward's reach: a sound that was not wind, not bird, not any animal she knew.

Something small. Out in the storm.

Coming down the mountain.
