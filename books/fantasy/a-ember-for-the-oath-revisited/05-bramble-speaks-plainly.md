## 5. Bramble Speaks Plainly

To understand what Bramble said that night, you must first understand how a goat says anything at all.

A goat does not speak in sentences, the way humans do, laying their words out like spoons on a table, each one facing the same direction. A goat speaks in weather. A goat speaks in the memory of grass, in the slope of hills that no longer exist, in the smell of rain three days before it falls. When Zephyrine Winterbourne finally knelt in the straw of her own barn, took the old nanny's face in her cold hands, and opened the door she had kept locked for thirty years—*speak to me, Bramble, and speak plainly, because I am frightened*—what came pouring out was not so much language as landscape.

What follows is Zephyrine's translation. She did her best. She was weeping at the time, which does not improve a translation.

---

Bramble had been waiting years for this conversation, and she resented having it in the middle of the night while standing in damp straw with one hoof asleep.

*You,* she began, which was as good a place as any. *You with your hands and your locked door. You come to me now. After all these years of you talking over my head like I'm furniture. Like I'm the fence.*

"I'm sorry," Zephyrine whispered, and meant it, and Bramble could taste the meaning of it the way you taste iron in spring water. That was the cruelty and the mercy of true speech: there was no lying in it. Thirty years of small kindnesses—the extra apple slices, the scratching between the horns when flies were bad, the way Zephyrine hummed off-key at milking time thinking no one heard—all of it stood up at once inside the open channel, all of it evidence.

*Yes, you're sorry,* Bramble said. *You are very sorry. You have been sorry for thirty years, sorry as a stone is heavy, and you carried it around and never once set it down where someone could help you lift it. Stubborn. Goats are stubborn and you are worse than a goat, and I have known some champion goats.*

The child sat in the straw nearby, wrapped in Zephyrine's second-best blanket, watching them both with those eyes that caught the lantern light the way a deer's eyes do—not reflecting it so much as keeping a little of it, holding it back, the way a careful person holds back a coin. The child did not understand goat-speech in words. But the child understood the *openness* of it, the channel being unstoppered after decades, and kept making a small sound in their throat like a kettle deciding whether to sing.

Bramble addressed the child first, because manners mattered, and because she wanted Zephyrine to have to wait for the important part. Waiting was good for humans. Almost nobody got enough practice at it anymore.

*Little one,* she said. *Who sent you?*

The child couldn't answer in kind—not yet, not in any shape Bramble's ears could catch—but the child reached out and laid one small bare foot against Bramble's foreleg. And through that touch, brief as a struck spark, something passed.

Later, Zephyrine would struggle to describe what she received in the translation, because the child's answer did not arrive as information. It arrived as an *age*. It arrived as the feeling of standing in front of something older than hills, and the hills knowing it, and bowing anyway. There was a word folded into the feeling, and the word was *sent*, and beneath the word was snow falling on nine places at once, and beneath the snow was a purpose with teeth.

Zephyrine's hands went cold on Bramble's face.

"What is this child?" she asked, and her voice cracked down the middle like ice on a trough.

And Bramble, who had been carrying the knowledge alone for three nights since the child arrived—who had chewed on it and ruminated on it, because that is what goats do, they take a thing in and bring it back up and look at it from every side until it softens or makes sense, usually neither—heaved the great sigh of the very old who have outlived everyone they were supposed to be able to share burdens with.

*Sit down,* she said. *You'll want to sit. And put the lantern closer. And stop wringing your hands, you'll wear holes in yourself before your time.*

---

*There were nine,* Bramble said. *Once. Nine stones set in nine wild places, and on each stone, blood and breath and word, three kinds of promise mixed together like braided rope. Your grandmothers' grandmothers did it, back before kings, back when the border between the human field and the deep wood wasn't a wall but a handshake.*

"The covenant." Zephyrine said it the way you'd say the name of a dead relative—carefully, testing whether speaking it hurt.

*You know the word. Good. I was beginning to think your mother hadn't even given you that much.* A pause, and then, with less iron in it: *She gave you more than you know. We'll get to that. Don't rush me. I've got maybe ten winters left in these knees and I intend to tell this properly, with pauses, the way stories deserve. You humans gallop through everything. No wonder you choke.*

The child had fallen asleep sitting up, the way young things do when they feel safe, chin dropping, hands loose. Bramble watched them sleep with an expression Zephyrine had never seen on a goat before and would not have believed possible: tenderness, worn smooth by long use, like a river stone.

*Each stone needed a keeper,* Bramble went on. *Not a guard—guards stand outside a thing. A keeper lives *inside* the promise. Flesh of it. The old peoples gave children. Children born half in one world and half in the other, born already knowing both languages, born as living seals so the oath would have a heartbeat. Nine stones. Nine seal-children. The promise walking around in a body, warm, growing up alongside the world it held together.*

"That's—" Zephyrine stopped. Started again. "That's a terrible weight to put on a child."

*Yes,* said Bramble simply. *It was. The old ones never pretended otherwise. They said: better a terrible weight shared by nine than a world gone deaf on both sides. Maybe they were wrong. Maybe they were desperate. Desperate people build heavy doors.* She shifted her weight; the bad hoof complained, and she let it. *But here is what matters tonight, girl, so put your listening ears on, the ones you use for birdsong: the stones are failing. Four are broken already—shattered, dead, cold. Five still sleep, and sleeping is not the same as safe. And the ward over this valley—*

She stopped.

For a long moment the only sound was the wind working at the barn door's bottom edge, and the child's breath going in and out, slow and even, and somewhere far off the crows settling into their roost trees.

"The ward," Zephyrine prompted, barely above a whisper.

*Is dying,* Bramble said. *You've felt it. Don't shake your head at me, I can smell fear on you from across the paddock, I've been able to do it since you were twenty and brought home your first heartbreak disguised as a book of poetry. The quiet is thinning. My grass tastes wrong at the edges, like snow-water where it should be sweet. The crows are remembering words—I've heard them trying, up on the fence line, saying syllables over and over like old women rehearsing insults. The ward was built to press everything flat, and the pressing hand is getting tired.*

"Then the child came here because—"

*Because when the wall comes down, whatever's pressed against it comes through first,* Bramble said. *The last seal-child didn't wander to Hollowmere by accident, girl. This child was *pushed*. Sent through the thinnest gap, to the one person inside the dying ward who could hear them—the one person whose gift was buried alive here instead of killed outright. Somebody knew. Somebody counted on the exact shape of your silence.*

Zephyrine sat back on her heels. The straw rustled. Somewhere in her chest, under thirty years of practiced calm, something turned over like a plowed stone coming up.

"Somebody," she repeated. "My mother."

*I didn't say that.*

"You thought it very loudly."

*I'm an old goat,* said Bramble with dignity. *I think loudly. It's one of my few remaining pleasures.*

---

Here is what else came through the channel that night, in pieces, the way heavy things arrive when the carrier keeps setting them down to rest:

Bramble had not always been Bramble of Hollowmere, one goat among six, owned by the quiet woman on the south slope. She had been born on the far side of the mountains, in a herd that ran with the old speech still intact, before the Oathwardens' sweeps grew thorough. She had come over the pass in a trader's cart, sold as a kid, and had wailed her whole first winter in the dead air of the valley until her voice wore down to what it was now—until speaking felt like shouting underwater, until she mostly stopped.

So she understood Zephyrine. That was the root of all their years together. Two prisoners in adjacent cells, tapping the wall.

And she had known, from the moment the child walked out of the early snow with bare feet and unafraid eyes, what stood in Zephyrine's kitchen eating porridge with both hands. You do not forget the smell of a covenant. It smells like green things under ice. It smells like the first day of the world.

*Nine stones,* she said again, near the end, because goats believe in repetition the way rivers believe in downhill. *Four broken. Five sleeping. And if all nine go dark—if the last seal fails before midwinter—then the long winter comes, and it isn't snow, girl. Snow melts. The long winter is forgetting. The wild realm will lose its memory of you, and yours will lose its memory of the wild, and everything the oath ever held together will drift apart into two silences that don't even know they were once one sound.*

"How long?" Zephyrine asked. Her voice was very steady. Bramble noted this, filed it away as either courage or the numbness that comes before courage, and decided it didn't matter which.

*The ward cracks will tell you. Already the frost is eating at the root of things—it's under your well, if you'd ever look, which you won't, because looking down wells is for braver women than you, which is a low bar indeed.* A pause, and then, gentler than Bramble ever allowed herself in daylight: *Midwinter. Perhaps a little past. Time moves strangely around a dying oath. But count the weeks, girl. Count them on your fingers. You don't have many.*

---

The lantern guttered. Zephyrine fed it oil with hands that shook only slightly, and Bramble pretended not to notice, because pretending not to notice was the greatest kindness available to either of them.

"Why didn't you tell me sooner?" Zephyrine asked. "About the child. About any of it. Three nights, Bramble."

The old goat looked at her for a long time. In the lantern light her eyes were amber coins, ancient and mildly offended.

*Because telling you a thing has never once been the hard part,* she said. *Getting you to hear it—that's the labor of saints, and I am a goat. If I'd told you on the first night you'd have spent three days explaining to yourself why it couldn't be true. I let you sit next to the child instead. I let the child's little bare feet do my arguing. Humans trust feet more than words. It's why you invented shoes, probably—to give yourselves somewhere to hide.*

"That's not wisdom, that's just insult."

*Same thing, most days.*

The child stirred in their sleep and made a sound—a small, rising note, almost a question—and both Zephyrine and Bramble went still until the note settled back down into breathing. When Zephyrine spoke again, her voice had changed. Bramble heard it change and felt something in her old chest turn over, because she had waited thirty years to hear that particular shift: the sound of a closed door becoming an open one.

"Bramble," Zephyrine said slowly, "if the ward is dying, then it isn't just failing to suppress magic anymore. It's failing to protect anyone inside it. From the Wardens. From whatever's coming through the cracks. From—" She looked at the sleeping child. "From all of it."

*Now you're listening.*

"And the ward doesn't just fail quietly, does it? Wards like this—the kind my mother would have known how to raise—they don't fade. They break."

Bramble chewed on this for a moment, though there was nothing left in her mouth. Outside, high on the fence post, a crow said a single syllable—half a word, a broken thing, *rrk-kah*, the ghost of *look*—and fell silent, embarrassed by itself.

*No,* Bramble said. *They break. And when this one breaks, every Oathwarden ear within three kingdoms is going to hear Hollowmere pop like a cork. Every buried gift in this valley, surfacing at once. Yours. Mine. Whatever sleeps in the neighbors. And a seal-child standing in the middle of it, plain as a bell in an empty field, with men in grey cloaks already sniffing along the ridgeline asking questions about irregularities in the silence.*

The barn was very quiet. The child slept on, one small hand curled in the straw, holding nothing, holding everything.

"So I don't have weeks," Zephyrine said. "I have until the ward decides it's done."

*Less,* said Bramble. *Wards die faster once they start. And girl—one more thing, and then you'll carry me out an apple in the morning or I'll eat your coat.*

"Anything."

*Your mother didn't bury your gift here to punish you,* Bramble said, and her voice, rough as it was, went soft as underbelly fur. *Whatever you've decided these thirty years about why she did it—you've been reading it wrong. She wasn't hiding you *from* something. She was hiding you *for* something. For this. Ask the well. The well remembers her.*

Zephyrine's breath caught audibly.

"The well?"

*The dry one,* said Bramble, with the satisfaction of an old creature who has saved the worst and best news for last. *Behind the barn. Where you've drawn water beside your mother's mother's mother without once looking down. Go tomorrow. Take the lantern. Take the child if you must, though I'd leave the child sleeping.*

She turned her face toward the dark doorway, toward the snow coming down soft and relentless over the valley, and added:

*And take a long rope, girl. The stone at the bottom has cracked, and I don't think you're going to like what's leaking out of it.*
