## 7. Riders on the Ridgeline

The snow had stopped by morning, and Zephyrine woke to a silence that was wrong.

She lay still in her narrow bed and listened the way other people looked—carefully, with her whole body. Hollowmere's quiet had a texture she knew better than her own hands. It was thick. It pressed against the windows like wet wool. It muffled Bramble's morning complaints before they could become sound at all, so that the old goat's opinions arrived only as vibrations through the floorboards, a grumble felt rather than heard.

This morning the quiet had a hole in it.

Not a loudness—nothing so obvious. A thinness, the way a blanket wears through at the elbow. Somewhere out past the goat pen, past the frost-stiffened herb rows, something was making a small noise that should not have been able to exist inside the ward, and the ward was letting it.

Zephyrine got up, dressed in the dark, and stood a long moment at the door of the little back room where Wren slept.

The child had been with her four days now. Four days of ash spirals and bird-patterns and meals eaten in a language no human mouth was meant to shape. Four days of sleeping curled like a fox, breathing slow and even, utterly untroubled by cold or dark or the fact that they had appeared barefoot in the first snowfall as if delivered by it.

Zephyrine had begun, against every instinct she owned, to be fond of them. Fondness was dangerous. Fondness was a door left open in winter. But there it was anyway, sitting in her chest like a coal: the way Wren reached for her sleeve when startled, the way they had learned to say *bread* in beast-speech with an air of tremendous satisfaction, the way their deer-bright eyes followed her around the kitchen as though she were the most interesting thing in a valley full of interesting things.

She eased the door shut and went out into the grey morning to find out what the quiet had lost.

* * *

It took her until midmorning to find it, because it kept moving.

A bird. A small brown thing, a hedge-wren ironically enough, sitting on her fence post singing its heart out—a real song, three notes rising and falling, repeated with the stubborn joy of a creature that has never once considered whether anyone is listening.

Inside the ward, where birds did not sing. Where birds had not sung for thirty years.

Zephyrine stood in the dooryard with a pail of goat feed going cold in her hand and watched it, and felt the hair rise along her arms. The crow from last autumn had unsettled her because it said nothing. This wren unsettled her far more, because it said everything, cheerfully, ignorantly, and the sky did not fall.

Unless the sky was already falling. Unless this was what falling looked like—not thunder, but a small brown bird forgetting the rules.

"Bramble," she said quietly, and the old goat lifted her head from the feed trough with the expression of one who has been waiting all morning to be consulted.

*You see it,* Bramble said. Her voice in Zephyrine's mind was dry as winter hay, patient as stone. *I saw it at dawn. I was going to mention it after my breakfast.*

"You were going to mention it."

*I prioritize.*

"Has anything else—" Zephyrine stopped. Listened. Far off, toward the valley's eastern edge where the ridgeline ran ragged against the sky, she heard—or didn't hear, which was worse—the particular quality of absence that meant many hooves moving together. "Bramble. How many horses does it take to make the ground feel busy?"

The goat chewed thoughtfully. *More than Tobias Grell owns. Fewer than an army. Enough.*

Enough. The word sat in Zephyrine's stomach like a swallowed stone.

She had spent thirty years learning the exact weight of the law's attention, the way a fish learns the exact weight of the water above it. Oathwardens did not come to valleys like Hollowmere. That was the entire point of Hollowmere. Her mother had paid whatever terrible price she'd paid precisely so that no grey cloak would ever ride down this road. The ward suppressed magic; the suppression suppressed interest; the interest stayed on the far side of the ridgeline, chasing brighter fires.

And yet.

"Get everyone in the pen," Zephyrine said. "All of them. And stay close to the barn today."

*You're frightened,* Bramble observed, without judgment, the way she might have noted rain coming.

"I'm being careful. There's a difference."

*There is,* Bramble agreed. *But your hands are shaking, so I'll do both.*

* * *

They came down off the ridgeline just past noon, and Zephyrine watched them from behind the frosted kitchen window with Wren pressed against her side, peeking through the gap in the shutter slats.

Six riders. Grey cloaks, grey hoods, horses the color of river fog. They moved down the valley road at an unhurried walk, the pace of men who had never once needed to hurry, and stopped at the Hollis farm first, because the Hollis farm was nearest the road and Farmer Hollis was the sort of man who answered questions the way a bucket answers a kick—promptly, completely, and with a certain amount of spillage.

Even through the ward's muffling, Zephyrine could read the scene like a picture book. One rider dismounted. Two more fanned out along the fence line with the loose competence of men who had done this a hundred times. The leader—taller than the others, stiller—stood in the farmyard while Hollis came out wiping his hands on his apron, bowing a little, smiling the smile farmers keep for tax collectors and weather.

Then the leader raised one gloved hand, and even at this distance, even muffled, Zephyrine felt it.

A pulse. Like a finger tapped against a drumhead. The whole valley's silence shivered, and beneath the shiver—for half a heartbeat—she heard everything the silence was holding down. Wind in the high pines. Water under ice. A dog barking three farms over. And threaded through it all, faint as smoke, the wild speech itself: the low continuous murmur of growing things talking in their sleep, the sound she had not heard clearly since childhood, the sound of the world's own voice.

Then the pulse faded and the wool came back down, and the tall rider lowered his hand as if he'd knocked on a door and listened at it.

"They're testing the ward," Zephyrine whispered. Her breath fogged the window glass. "That's what the bird was. That's what the thinness is. They're listening for holes."

Wren made a small sound against her ribs—not a word, but a question-shaped sound, the kind the child made constantly now, having discovered that Zephyrine understood questions. Their eyes came up, deer-wide, reflecting the grey window light.

"Hunters," Zephyrine said, because lying to a child who spoke truth-speech seemed both pointless and unkind. "Men who stop voices." She hesitated, then added, because Wren's hand had found her sleeve and gripped it: "They won't find you. I promise you that. I'm very good at hiding. I've had practice."

Thirty years of practice. She had never once thought of it as a skill. It turned out every life contained skills; some of them you only recognized when they were finally useful.

* * *

By late afternoon the riders had worked their way down the valley, farm to farm, and the pattern of their questioning had reached Zephyrine through the valley's usual nervous telegraphy: Hollis told his wife, his wife told the miller's boy, the miller's boy told everyone within shouting distance, which in Hollowmere meant everyone.

*An irregularity in the silence.* That was the phrase, passed along with the bread and the gossip. An irregularity in the silence. Had anyone heard singing? Birdsong? Any animal behaving strangely? Anyone new come to the valley? Anyone at all?

Farmer Hollis, according to his wife, had offered up the entire valley's population for inspection, including a detailed account of Tobias Grell's recent purchase of double food portions from the trading post, which Tobias himself had apparently reported first, unprompted, with the helpful precision of a man reading from a ledger.

Zephyrine closed her eyes when she heard that part.

Tobias Grell counted things. He counted his fences, his coins, his neighbor's chimney smoke, the days between visitors to any house but his own. He wasn't cruel. He was worse than cruel—he was thorough. And somewhere in one of his little books, in his small neat handwriting, there was now an entry about Zephyrine Winterbourne buying twice the bread a woman alone could eat.

She had known this day would come eventually. She had simply imagined it arriving differently—imagined herself older, or the danger smaller, or herself braver. Instead here it was, grey-cloaked and patient, walking horse by horse down the one road out of the valley, asking her neighbors about irregularities, while upstairs—no, not upstairs, down—in the root cellar beneath her kitchen floor, a seal-child slept in a nest of blankets beside a barrel of pickled onions, dreaming in a language that frosted glass.

Because of course she had hidden them there. The moment the riders crested the ridgeline she had knelt on the kitchen floor, pulled up the trapdoor, and carried Wren down into the earth among the turnips and the salt pork, and Wren had gone without fear, without question, looking up at her from the dark with those shining eyes and signing one clear sentence in the old speech:

*You have done this before.*

And Zephyrine had signed back, before she could stop herself, honestly: *Yes.*

* * *

They came to her gate at dusk.

Of course they did. She had watched them work down the valley all day, methodical as frost, and she had known the order of things, and her cottage sat near the end of the road the way her life sat near the end of everyone's attention. She had spent the intervening hours doing the most ordinary things she could invent—splitting wood, mending a hinge, scolding Bramble publicly about eating the hedge—and all the while her heart had beaten like a trapped bird, and all the while the trapdoor had waited under the braided rug like a held breath.

Three of them dismounted at her gate. The tall one—the leader—did not. He remained in the saddle, hood shadowing his face, watching her cottage the way a man watches water he suspects has fish in it.

The two who came up her path were younger. One had a kind, tired face, the face of a man who asked questions all day and went home to children who asked better ones. The other had a ledger-look about him, ink-stained fingers, a notebook already open.

"Good evening, mistress," said the kind-faced one. His voice, even muffled by the ward, was pleasant. "Forgive the intrusion. We're riding for the Crown's Office of the Oath. Routine inquiry."

"Evening," Zephyrine said. She kept her hands folded, her shoulders rounded, her gaze modestly fixed slightly left of his face. Thirty years of practice. Invisible woman, invisible life. "Routine, is it? We don't get much of anything routine out here. Mostly weather."

"That's rather what we're here about." He smiled. "There's been an irregularity reported in the valley's silence. Perhaps you've noticed anything unusual. Animals acting strange. Sounds where there shouldn't be sounds."

"Sounds," Zephyrine repeated, and laughed—a short, dry, self-deprecating laugh she had practiced in front of no mirror at all for thirty years. "Master, the only sound on this farm is my goat complaining, and she complains in perfect goat, I assure you. No words. I check."

The ink-stained one wrote something down. Zephyrine's stomach tightened—until she saw what he wrote, or rather how briefly: a single line, a tick, a nothing. Boring. Moving on. She fed him boring the way you feed a fire damp leaves, patiently, smotheringly.

"Anyone new in the valley?" the kind-faced one asked. "Travelers? Kin visiting?"

"Not since spring, and that was the tinker, and he sold me a kettle that leaks, so if you're hunting him I'll draw you a map."

The kind-faced one laughed. Even the ledger man's pen paused. And then—

The tall rider swung down from his horse.

He crossed her dooryard slowly, and up close Zephyrine saw he was older than she'd guessed, silver at his temples, with a face like a cliff that had decided long ago what weather it approved of. He stopped an arm's length away and looked at her—not at her cottage, not at her ledger entry, but at her, directly, the way nobody in Hollowmere had looked at her in thirty years.

"Mistress Winterbourne," he said.

Her blood went to ice water. "You have me at a disadvantage, sir."

"Serah Vane." A slight inclination of the head. Not courtesy exactly—accounting. "I'm told you gather herbs. Yarrow, valerian, feverfew. You trade them at the crossroads market."

"When people are foolish enough to be ill, yes."

"And you've lived alone thirty years."

"Give or take. Time moves oddly in a valley."

Vane's eyes—pale, unhurried, terribly awake—moved past her shoulder to the shuttered window, then down, briefly, to the dooryard. To the rug visible through the open door. To the floor beneath the rug.

Zephyrine did not move. Did not breathe faster. Inside her chest, beneath thirty years of practiced stillness, something screamed like a hawk.

"The silence in this valley," Vane said quietly, "is very well maintained. Old work. Older than the Office. Someone anchored it carefully, and someone has kept it." Her gaze came back up. "Kept places don't usually break on their own, mistress. Something bends them. A crack. A leak. A—" she paused, choosing the word with care, "—a guest."

"A guest," Zephyrine echoed. "In Hollowmere? Sir, we haven't had excitement since the mill burned, and that was mostly the mill's own fault."

For a long moment Vane said nothing. The wind moved the grey cloaks of the riders at the gate. Somewhere behind the wall of the world, faint as memory, a hedge-wren sang three notes.

Then Vane stepped back.

"Thank you for your time, mistress." She turned to her men. "We ride on. There's a shrine at the valley's north end I want to see before full dark." And then, pausing, not quite turning back, she added: "Keep your floors swept, mistress Winterbourne. Dust gathers quickly in old houses. It tells stories."

They mounted. They rode. The hoofbeats faded northward, and the valley's wool came down again, thick and total, and Zephyrine stood in her dooryard until the last grey shape vanished, and then a while longer, because her legs had opinions of their own.

* * *

She opened the trapdoor and descended into the root cellar with a candle, and found Wren sitting up in the blanket nest, wide awake, having heard everything through the floor in the way that children hear everything through floors.

*The tall one knows,* Wren signed. Not a question.

"She suspects," Zephyrine said. "Knowing and suspecting live in different houses. We'll keep them apart as long as we can." She sat down on the cold earthen step, suddenly too tired to stand, and Wren crawled into the space beside her without asking, the way they always did, fitting against her side like something that had always belonged there.

Above them, muffled by floorboards, Bramble stamped once in the barn—her version of a sentry's report: *all quiet, all wrong, all watched.*

Zephyrine stared at the candle flame and did arithmetic she hated. Vane was riding north, to the shrine—but shrines could wait, and suspicion traveled faster than horses, and somewhere in Ferrow's Cross or beyond, papers were already being written. *Irregularity in the silence. Woman living alone. Double provisions. Swept floors.* Sooner or later the numbers would finish adding themselves.

"We can't stay," she said aloud, to the candle, to the child, to the cellar walls that smelled of turnips and thirty years of safety. "Wren—we can't stay."

Wren's hand found hers in the dark. Small fingers, cold as the earth, steady as stone. And the child signed one word, gentle and absolute, the word Zephyrine had been dreading and waiting for since the night the snow delivered them:

*When?*

Zephyrine looked at the trapdoor overhead, at the thin line of lamplight from her kitchen—the last light of the only home she had ever chosen—and heard herself answer.

"Soon," she said. "Before the next snow. Pack light."
