## 8. The Neighbor Who Counts Too Much

Tobias Grell had three great talents in this life, and he would have been the first to admit all of them were unlovely ones.

The first was arithmetic. Not the grand sort that filled ledgers in Ferrow's Cross or made merchants rich — no, Tobias's arithmetic was small and patient and relentless, the kind that lived behind his eyes like a spider behind a loose shutter. He could tell you, without consulting anything but memory, how many eggs Hesta Marlowe had traded at market last frostday (eleven, down from thirteen, which meant her brown hen was off the lay again), how many cords of wood Aldous Pike had stacked before the first snow (four and a half, which was one cord short of prudence, and Aldous would come begging by Thawmoon, mark it), and exactly how many years it had been since Zephyrine Winterbourne had bought so much as a second wheel of cheese from anyone in Hollowmere.

Thirty-one. Thirty-one years of buying for one.

Which was why the second talent — noticing — had been working overtime these past two weeks, humming along like a well-oiled hinge nobody remembered oiling.

The third talent was silence. Tobias could hold a thing behind his teeth for years without it going sour. Some men chewed on gossip like gristle and spat it out half-digested at the tavern. Not Tobias. Tobias wrote things down.

He kept his observations in a leather-bound book that had once been an inventory ledger for his late father's apple press, back when the Grell family had ambitions. The ambitions had died with the father; the book remained, its first forty pages full of cider arithmetic, its remaining pages given over to what Tobias privately called *the true accounts of Hollowmere*. Debts owed and unpaid. Fences mended and not. Who watered their milk. Who let their pigs into the commons and pretended otherwise. And now, in a careful hand at the back, under a heading he'd ruled off with a straightedge:

*Winterbourne, Z. — irregularities.*

*Frostday last: 2 loaves, 1 cheese (double her usual), 3 lbs salted pork. Bought for one?*

*Second visit, same week: snow-melt salts, 4 sacks. She has never bought more than 1 in any winter I have known her. Her walk is short. Her roof does not leak. Whose paths does she mean to clear?*

*Also: lamp oil, doubled. A woman who has kept one lamp lit of an evening for thirty years does not suddenly need two.*

Tobias sat in his kitchen on the evening of the second heavy snowfall, boots drying by the stove, and read over his own entries with the satisfaction of a man reading scripture he himself had authored. Outside, the white world hushed against the windows. Inside, a pot of barley soup breathed on the hob, and his cat, a vast grey tyrant named Duchess, occupied the only chair worth sitting in.

"Move," Tobias told her.

Duchess did not move. Duchess never moved. This was, in its way, another entry for the book: *Duchess — incorrigible.* Though he supposed there was no point writing down things everyone already knew.

He ate standing up, which his mother had declared vulgar and which he had therefore practiced faithfully for forty years out of a stubbornness he mistook for principle. Through the window over the sink, if he leaned just so and the lamplight didn't reflect too badly, he could see the lane that ran past his croft and, farther on, the bend where the Winterbourne track split off toward the old stone house under the eastern slope.

No light there tonight. Or — he squinted, wiped the fog from the pane with his sleeve, leaned further — yes. There. A thin yellow seam under what would be the shutters of the front room. Two lamps' worth of glow, maybe. Hard to say at this distance.

Hard to say, but not impossible to wonder about.

* * *

Here is what the people of Hollowmere believed about Tobias Grell, when they thought of him at all, which was rarely and usually with mild distaste: that he was a miser, a bachelor, a man who counted other people's firewood because he had no fire worth counting of his own.

There was truth in it. Tobias did not dispute the portrait. A man who argued with his own reputation spent his whole life arguing, and Tobias had better uses for his breath — namely, keeping it.

But here is what the people of Hollowmere did not know, because Tobias had never told them and never would: that the book was not cruelty. It had never been cruelty. It had begun, thirty-odd years ago, as grief.

His father had died owing money to half the valley and being owed by the other half, and none of it written down anywhere except in the fading ink of a dying man's memory. Tobias, nineteen and hollowed out, had watched neighbors he'd known all his life shrug and shuffle and forget what they owed, and something in him had quietly resolved that forgetting was a kind of theft, and theft was a kind of lie, and he would not lie. Not even by accident. Not even kindly.

So he wrote everything down. Every debt, every favor, every small unevenness between what people said and what they did. It was, if you looked at it rightly, a kind of devotion. The valley was his congregation and the ledger was his psalm, and if the psalm was mostly about who hadn't returned a borrowed scythe, well — the Lord was said to number the hairs on your head, and nobody called *that* meddling.

Zephyrine Winterbourne had always been one of the cleanest pages in the book. That was the thing that had unsettled him lately, though he'd have bitten off his own tongue before admitting it. Thirty-one years, and she owed nothing, took nothing, asked nothing. She came to market, sold her herbs and her goat cheese, bought exactly what a woman living alone needed, and went home up her track like a coin returning to a purse. Perfectly balanced. Perfectly dull.

And then, eleven days ago, the balance had tipped.

* * *

It had started, as most things in Hollowmere did, at the market shed on frostday morning.

Tobias had arrived early — he always arrived early; lateness was disorder and disorder was a debt against the day — and had taken up his customary position near the grain scales, ostensibly inspecting the quality of Hesta Marlowe's oats, actually observing. Zephyrine had come in with her hood up and her basket empty, which was normal. What was not normal was the manner of her buying.

She'd taken two loaves from the baker's rack. Then she'd paused — and Tobias had seen the pause, that little hitch, a woman catching herself — and taken a third.

Three loaves. For a woman with one mouth.

Then the cheesemonger's stall, where she'd bought a whole wheel instead of her usual wedge, and the salt-pork barrel, where she'd bought enough to feed a family through Candlemas, and — this was the detail that had lodged in Tobias like a splinter — she'd bought honey. A full crock of honey. Zephyrine Winterbourne, who in thirty-one years had never once purchased a sweet thing, as far as his records showed. He'd checked. Twice. His records showed nothing, because there was nothing to show.

People changed, of course. People got lonely and got a cat, or got a lodger, or got religion. But they said something. They mentioned it at market, half-bragging, half-complaining, because that was what people did with changes — they wore them out loud until the change wore smooth. Zephyrine had said nothing. Zephyrine had pulled her hood lower and bought her honey like a woman smuggling contraband, and had gone up her track fast, faster than usual, and had not come down again for six days.

That was when Tobias had begun to worry.

Not suspicious — he'd have denied suspicion under oath. Worried. There was a difference, and the difference mattered to him, though he couldn't have explained why without opening drawers in himself that had been shut since he was nineteen. It was simply this: in thirty-one years of watching — and he was honest enough, alone in his kitchen, to call it watching — Zephyrine Winterbourne had never once done anything interesting. And in his experience, people who had spent decades being perfectly uninteresting only became interesting for two reasons. Either something wonderful had happened to them.

Or something terrible had found them.

* * *

The salts were what decided him.

Snow-melt salts were cheap and heavy and everyone bought them, but everyone bought them in ones. One sack lasted a careful household a winter if you scattered it thin on the steps and the front path. Tobias himself used one and a half, because his lane was long and he was getting old and he refused to break a hip on his own property — that would be disorder of the highest order.

Four sacks. In one purchase. From a woman whose path from gate to door was perhaps twenty paces.

Unless the path wasn't for her.

He turned it over all through the following week, the way he turned everything over, patiently, from all sides, like a stone looking for what lived underneath. A guest, then. A relative come to winter over. But relatives announced themselves. Relatives were dragged to market and shown off and complained about within a fortnight. A secret guest was a different animal entirely, and animals that hid were either shy or wild, and both kinds bit.

On the ninth day after the salts, walking home past the Winterbourne track with a sack of meal on his shoulder, Tobias had seen the smoke.

Not the ordinary smoke — the thin grey thread from the chimney that had risen from that house, steady as a psalm, for thirty-one years. This smoke was thicker, whiter, and it came from two places: the chimney, and the low brick flue of the summer kitchen at the side of the house, the one that hadn't been used since before Tobias could remember. Someone was cooking in quantity. Someone was heating water in quantity. Someone was doing laundry, or bathing, or —

Or keeping something warm.

He had stood there in the falling snow, meal sack on his shoulder, longer than was sensible. Long enough that his shoulders whitened. Long enough that Duchess, waiting at home, would have opinions.

And in all that standing, one image had surfaced in him, unbidden and unwelcome: a memory of Zephyrine at market, some years ago — five? seven? — when a traveling tinker's cart had come through with a caged songbird, some gaudy southern thing, and the bird had burst out singing right there in the market shed, a flood of noise, and every head had turned, and Tobias had happened to be looking at Zephyrine when they did.

She had gone white. Not startled-white. Frightened-white. The white of a woman hearing a sound she had taught herself never to hear. And then she had finished her trading with her back very straight and her eyes very still, and she had left early, and Tobias had written in the book, that night, in a younger hand:

*Winterbourne, Z. — fears birdsong. Reason unknown.*

He had never followed it up. There had seemed no need. The valley was quiet, and quiet suited everyone, and a woman's fear was her own business unless it spilled onto the lane.

But now there were three loaves, and four sacks of salt, and double lamp oil, and smoke from a dead flue, and six days of silence, and somewhere under all of it, faint as a vein of ore, the glinting certainty that the quiet around Zephyrine Winterbourne had never been natural quiet at all. It had been *kept*. The way you kept a fire banked. The way you kept a door shut on a room you didn't want looked into.

And doors that are kept shut, Tobias knew from long experience with other people's doors, are kept shut for one of two reasons: to keep something out.

Or to keep something in.

* * *

That night, he added the final entries by lamplight while Duchess supervised from her chair with the air of a magistrate enduring testimony.

*Ninth day: smoke from summer-kitchen flue (disused 30+ yrs). Path to woodshed newly cleared — wider than needed for one. Tracks in snow beside gate: small. Barefoot? Impossible. Child-sized, regardless. Bootless child in this cold means either neglect or something stranger. Neither sits easy.*

He sat back, pen hovering.

The trouble with writing things down, he reflected, was that eventually the page expected something of you. Ink was a commitment. Every entry in the book was a small promise that the truth mattered, and promises compounded, like debts. He had spent his whole life collecting other people's small unevennesses, and now here was the largest one in the valley, sitting at the bottom of his own page, and it was looking up at him and asking, in its neat ruled way: *and what will you do?*

What would he do?

Nothing, probably. Nothing was the traditional answer. He was a counting man, not a doing man. He would watch, and record, and wait for the pattern to explain itself, because patterns always explained themselves eventually — that was the whole comfort of arithmetic, that sums came out in the end.

Except.

Except that today, coming home along the lane in the blue hour, he had heard — or almost heard — something from the direction of the Winterbourne house. Carried on the wind, thin and high and wrong for human throats: a sound like singing, if singing had been invented by something with feathers. And beneath it, unmistakable even at distance, a woman's voice answering it. Not words. Not quite. Something older than words, rising and falling like wind through a reed bed.

Tobias had stood in the snow with his heart going oddly and told himself: *wind. Wind through the bare oaks. Wind and a goat bleating.* Goats bleated. Everyone knew goats bleated.

But he had also written, in the end, one line more, pressing hard enough that the nib nearly tore the page:

*Winterbourne, Z. — the quiet around her house is not empty. It is guarded. Watch closer. Midwinter approaches, and whatever she is feeding, feeding things makes them grow.*

He blotted the page, closed the book, and set it on the shelf where it lived — spine out, between the almanac and his father's old cider manual, hiding in plain sight the way the best secrets always did.

Outside, the snow went on falling, softening the lanes, smoothing the tracks, erasing the small barefoot prints at the Winterbourne gate as though the valley itself were complicit in the concealment. Tobias banked the stove, trimmed his single lamp — he had never needed two — and went to bed.

He slept poorly, which was unusual, and dreamed of a crow sitting on his fence saying nothing at all, over and over, saying nothing so loudly that he woke before dawn with the certain, unreasonable conviction that the silence in Hollowmere was a ledger too.

And someone, somewhere, had begun to cook the books.
