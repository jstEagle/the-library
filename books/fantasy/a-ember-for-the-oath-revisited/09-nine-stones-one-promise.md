## 9. Nine Stones, One Promise

The snow kept falling through the night, soft and patient, as though the sky had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Zephyrine sat on her kitchen floor with her back against the woodstove, a blanket around her shoulders, and the child—Wren, though she hadn't said the name aloud yet—asleep against her side, breathing in that deep, untroubled rhythm of someone who had never once worried about anything so foolish as cold.

Bramble stood in the doorway with snow on his horns and an expression Zephyrine could only describe as judicial.

"You're going to tell me," she said. "Aren't you. That's why you came inside, which you have done precisely twice in fifteen years, and both times involved a broken fence and my fault."

*Let the record show,* Bramble said, *that I came inside because your fence is inadequate and your judgment is worse.*

"Let the record show," Zephyrine murmured, "that I am talking to a goat."

She hadn't spoken beast-speech indoors since she was nine years old. It felt like opening a window in a house that had been sealed for a funeral—air moving through rooms that had forgotten what air was for. The words came easier than she expected. They always did. That was the cruel joke of it: the gift had never once been difficult. Only the hiding was.

Wren stirred, opened those deer-bright eyes, and looked at Bramble with an expression of perfect recognition.

*Ah,* said Bramble. *Good. The little one's awake. Then everyone can hear me be right.*

* * *

What follows, I will set down as faithfully as I can, though translation is a strange alchemy and goats do not think in human shapes. Bramble spoke in scents and pressures, in the grammar of herd-memory that has no word for *I* without also meaning *we*. What I heard—and what Wren confirmed, nodding along with the solemnity of a child reciting scripture they'd known before they were born—went something like this:

*You think the quiet of this valley is a blanket,* Bramble began, chewing thoughtfully on the edge of Zephyrine's spare shawl, which she allowed because interrupting him meant another hour. *It is not a blanket. It is a bandage. And the wound underneath it is very old, and very large, and it is beginning to weep.*

He told her of the covenant—not the way humans told it, in chapels and carved verses, but the way the wild remembered it, which was less a story than a scar.

Before the first kingdom, before names were written down and hoarded, the two realms—the realm of humankind and the realm of root-and-wing, of antler and river—had made a promise to each other. Not out of love, Bramble was careful to say. The wild does not love as humans love; it keeps faith, which is sturdier. Humans would take from the forest, and the forest would take from humans, and both would survive the taking, because each side *witnessed* the other. That was the whole of the law. To see and be seen. To speak and be answered.

*Your kind forgot how to hear us first,* Bramble said. *Ours forgot how to forgive you for it. The covenant was what stood in the gap.*

And the covenant had been sealed—not with signatures, which mean nothing to a fox, and not with blood, which means everything and is therefore wasted—but with nine stones. Nine oath-stones, set in the wild places where the veil between realms wore thinnest: under mountains, in flooded shrines, on sea-cliffs where the wind had never once held its tongue. Each stone was not merely a marker. Each stone was *alive*, in the slow mineral way, and each one slept around a seal—a living pledge that the promise still had a heartbeat. Seal-children, the old herds called them. Children born half in one world and half in the other, who could speak the true tongue without being taught it, the way a river speaks falling.

Zephyrine's hands had gone very still in her lap.

*There were nine seals,* Bramble said, *once. One for each stone. When a seal died of age or accident, the stone slept deeper, and another was found. That is the old arrangement. That is how it worked for longer than your kingdom has existed, which your kingdom will be pleased to learn.*

*And now?*

Bramble stopped chewing.

*Now there is one,* he said. *And it is asleep on your floor.*

* * *

Wren had drifted off again, which Bramble considered rude but forgave on the grounds that seal-children apparently dreamed the state of the stones while they slept, which was exhausting work, and Zephyrine could hardly blame anyone for napping through an apocalypse if it came with dreams attached.

So Bramble counted, and Zephyrine translated into numbers, because that was the only way either of them could hold it.

Nine stones. Four already destroyed—shattered, deliberately, by hands that knew what they were breaking. Bramble did not know whose hands. He knew only what the news had cost: the marsh stone went first, decades ago, and the fens around it forgot how to filter water. Then the stone of the southern weald, and the oaks there began dying top-down, as if remembering gravity before leaves. The third had been under a hill that men quarried for road-metal. The fourth—

The fourth, Bramble said, had been the stone of Hollowmere itself.

Zephyrine looked up sharply. *This valley had a stone?*

*Had,* Bramble agreed. *When it broke, your mother's generation did what wounded things do. They hid. Your valley's ward—the quiet you have spent thirty years mistaking for peace—is a tourniquet tied around the stump. It suppresses magic because magic leaking through a broken covenant burns. It was mercy, of a kind. It was also amputation, of a kind. And tourniquets, speaker, are not meant to be worn for thirty years.*

Zephyrine thought of the crow on her fence, saying nothing. Saying nothing so loudly.

*Five stones remain,* Bramble went on. *Five sleeping. And here is the arithmetic you will not like. The stones sustain one another. Nine was enough. Five is a fire shared among too few hearths—they burn themselves faster to keep the whole warm. Every year they sleep, they spend more of themselves. And every year, the winter that waits behind the world steps closer.*

*What winter?*

*The long winter.* Bramble said it the way old animals say *flood* or *fire*—not as prophecy but as memory that hasn't happened yet. *When the last stone fails, the covenant dies entire. And when the covenant dies, the two realms stop witnessing each other. Do you understand what that means, Zephyrine Winterbourne? It does not mean war. War requires noticing. No. The realms simply... turn their backs. The wild forgets the shape of human hearts, and humans forget the wild was ever anything but scenery. Rivers forget how to run to the sea. Forests lose the names of their own trees. Birds migrate by instinct alone and arrive nowhere, because *arrival* is a promise, and promises will be over. Everything continues. Nothing remembers why. That is the long winter. Not ice. Forgetting.*

The stove ticked. Snow pressed against the dark window like a hand testing the glass.

*Midwinter,* Bramble said. *That is the deadline no one chose and everyone must keep. Midwinter is when the covenant renews itself, as it has every year since the beginning—or fails to. If five stones still sleep when the solstice comes, they will fail together, like lamps running out of oil in the same hour. If even one can be woken—if the seals can be gathered and the oath spoken again—the chain holds. But the waking must happen before midwinter. After midwinter, there is nothing left to wake.*

Zephyrine sat with that for a long moment. Then she asked the question she already knew the answer to, the way one asks a doctor to repeat the worst part.

*How many days?*

Bramble calculated, which for a goat involved staring fixedly at a wall.

*Nineteen,* he said. *Give or take a blizzard.*

* * *

*Nineteen days,* Zephyrine repeated, when Wren woke and Bramble had gone back out into the snow with the self-satisfaction of a creature who has delivered difficult news and eaten half a shawl in the process. *Nineteen days to find five sleeping stones scattered across gods-know-how-many miles of country I have never set foot in, wake them with a child who doesn't speak any language I grew up with, while staying ahead of the Oathwardens—who, I remind you, exist specifically to kill exactly what we're trying to save.*

Wren watched her pace the length of the kitchen. Back. Forth. Back.

*I'm thirty-seven years old,* Zephyrine said. *I have crossed that ridgeline four times in my life, and twice was to bury people. I own one pair of boots. I don't know where these stones are. I don't know how to wake them. I don't know how to fight. I know goats, Wren. I know which herbs settle a fever and which ones start one. That is the complete inventory of my usefulness.*

Wren slid off the bench, padded across the floor, and took hold of the hem of her shawl—not pulling, just holding, the way you'd hold a rope in wind. Then they pointed one small finger at the ash bucket beside the hearth, and at the smooth swept stone of the hearth-front, and raised their eyebrows with terrible patience until Zephyrine dipped two fingers in the cool ash.

The map Wren drew was crude and complete at once: a coastline, a mountain range drawn in three strokes, rivers like veins. And five spirals, spaced across the wild places, each spiral different—one coiled tight, one open like a fern, one double-wound, one with a line struck through it that made Wren flinch even drawing it. When Zephyrine touched the fifth spiral, far to the north past the mountains, at the very edge of the ash and the page of the world, Wren covered it gently with their whole small hand. Covering it, she understood, the way you cover a candle flame.

*They know where they are,* Zephyrine said slowly. *All of them. They've always known. That's what a seal is—it's not a key, it's a compass.*

Wren nodded. Then pointed at the spiral nearest to them—a day's walk east, in the drowned shrine country—and then pointed at Zephyrine, and then, with devastating simplicity, at the door.

"No," Zephyrine said. "Absolutely not. You are eight years old. You are barefoot. It is snowing."

Wren looked down at their feet, which were bare, considered this objection seriously, and produced from the woodbox—for reasons Zephyrine would never fully reconstruct—a single dry pinecone, which they offered up with the gravity of a knight presenting a sword.

"That's not boots."

Wren did not withdraw the pinecone.

"I'm not taking a pinecone instead of boots."

Wren waited. Somewhere outside, Bramble made a sound that in any other animal Zephyrine would have called laughing.

And that was when she understood, standing in her kitchen with ash on her fingers and nineteen days on the clock, that the argument was already lost—that it had been lost the moment a silent child knocked on her door, or perhaps thirty years earlier, the night her mother carried her across the border into the quiet. She had spent her whole life learning how to stay. Nobody had taught her how to leave. But somebody, it seemed, had been sent to teach her anyway.

"Boots first," she said sternly. "Then pinecone. We negotiate from here."

Wren smiled, and it was the first smile Zephyrine had seen on them—quick and crooked and entirely ordinary, a child's smile, and it went through her like warmth through frost-cracked ground.

Outside, the snow kept falling. Nineteen days. Five stones. One promise, older than kingdoms, waiting in the dark places of the world to be spoken again by whatever fool was willing to say it.

Zephyrine went to find her mother's old traveling cloak.

She had a feeling it was going to fit.
