## 10. Leaving Is a Kind of Dying

The candle had burned down to a thumb's width when Zephyrine finally admitted to herself that she was not going to sleep.

She sat at her kitchen table with Bramble's words still hanging in the air like woodsmoke that wouldn't rise: *the seal-child must go where the stones sleep, and you must go with her, and the valley will not hold you after midwinter whether you leave or not.*

Zephyrine had lived thirty years in Hollowmere. Thirty years of the same chair, the same table, the same draft under the same door that she'd sworn every autumn to fix and never once had. She knew which floorboard sang in the third step from the top—she'd learned its pitch so well she could avoid it in the dark without thinking, the way you avoid a neighbor you don't want to talk to. She knew the goats by their coughs. She knew exactly how many spoonfuls of honey the tea wanted on a night like this, when the wind came down off the ridge carrying snow that fell too early and fell wrong, in long slow spirals, as if the sky itself were hesitating.

She put three spoons in anyway. It was that kind of night.

Across the room, on the truckle bed she'd made up from old quilts and a straw pallet, Wren slept the way children sleep in stories—with total commitment, one arm flung out, mouth open, utterly untroubled by the fact that they were the last living piece of an ancient covenant and that grey-cloaked men were asking questions at the far end of the valley. Zephyrine envied that. She also found it, absurdly, comforting. Whatever was coming, it would have to get past a child who slept like the world had already ended once and turned out fine.

*Wren,* she thought. She'd been calling the child that for four days now, ever since she'd watched them stand in the yard at dawn with their head tipped back, watching a wren work the fence line, with an expression of such fierce recognition that Zephyrine had said it before she could stop herself—"You look like him." The child had turned, pointed at the bird, then at themselves, then made a sound that was almost laughter. And that had been that. Names were like that sometimes. They arrived before you were ready for them and refused to be sent back.

*Wren,* she thought again, testing it. *We have to go.*

And the thought of going hurt in a way she hadn't expected, because it wasn't the leaving of danger that hurt. It was the leaving of the fence post with the notch worn smooth where she rested her elbow every evening. The leaving of the crooked shelf Tobias Grell's father had built for her mother, back when anyone built anything for anyone in this valley. The leaving of the well, which held a broken god under its dry throat and had been, for thirty years, the closest thing to a confessor she had.

Hollowmere had never known her. That was the whole point of Hollowmere. Her mother had carried her inside the ward-line as a baby precisely so that no one here would know her—so that the beast-speech in her blood would lie quiet under the ward's blanket, so that the Oathwardens would ride past without slowing. The valley had kept her the way a cellar keeps apples: in the dark, in the cold, unspoiled by anything.

But a cellar is not a home. And she was only now, packing a bag by candlelight, letting herself say so.

* * *

Packing, it turned out, was its own kind of grief, and it came in small stupid packages.

She started practically, because practicality was armor. Dried herbs: yarrow, comfrey, willow bark, the bitter moss that stopped bleeding if you prayed over it, though the prayer was mostly habit. A needle and thread. Flint and steel. The good knife, and the other knife, because one knife was optimism. Rope. The oilskin her mother had owned, still smelling faintly of the cedar chest.

Then the impractical things crept in, the way mice do.

Her mother's shawl. It was thin and full of holes and worth nothing to anyone alive except her, and she folded it into the bottom of the pack and told herself it was for warmth, which was a lie, and let the lie stand because some lies are load-bearing.

A jar of honey. Because Wren had tried honey for the first time three days ago and had made a sound—a real sound, a human-adjacent sound of pure astonishment—and Zephyrine had thought, *if we are walking into the end of everything, this child deserves to be astonished more than once.* Two jars, actually. She took two jars. She was not a sentimental woman. She was a woman taking provisions.

Bramble watched all of this from her spot by the stove with the air of a landlady supervising a tenant's departure.

"You're bringing the honey," Bramble observed. "Both jars."

"Provisions."

"For the child."

"For morale," Zephyrine said, and rolled a pair of wool socks into a shape that suggested discipline rather than desperation.

Bramble chewed on something that wasn't there. Goats did that when they were choosing between kindness and honesty; it was the beast-speech equivalent of clearing your throat. "You've been practicing what you'll say to the valley," she said finally. "I can smell it on you. All day you've been saying goodbye to fences."

"I have not."

"You touched the gate latch twice this morning and apologized to it."

Zephyrine opened her mouth. Closed it. The truth was that she had apologized to the gate latch, quietly, because it had served her faithfully for thirty years and she had oiled it perhaps six times, and there was a difference between a life and a tenancy, and she was only now working out which hers had been.

"It's just a valley," she said, which was the most dishonest sentence she'd produced since telling Tobias Grell the extra salt was "for the walkways."

"Mm," said Bramble, who had been present for the honey conversation and recognized a lie by its weight.

* * *

Near midnight, she did the thing she'd been avoiding. She banked the fire, wrapped herself in her coat, and went out into the falling snow to say goodbye to the valley properly—not to the fences, whatever Bramble thought, but to the whole of it, the way you'd sit with a dying friend even if neither of you had the words.

The snow had thickened. It came down in the strange slow spirals again, and the whole valley was hushed in that particular way that wasn't silence so much as listening. Under her feet the ground was beginning to stiffen. Above her, past the ragged dark shapes of the ridgeline, the sky had cleared in patches, and stars stood in the gaps like witnesses called unexpectedly to court.

She walked the boundaries of her own small world. The goat shed first, where she checked the latch out of pure ritual and stood a while breathing the warm animal smell of straw and milk and dung—the honest smell, the only smell she'd ever trusted. The garden next, asleep under its first white sheet, the rosemary sticking up through the snow like a hand refusing to be buried. The fence line, where the crow usually sat. No crow tonight. Even the crows had somewhere better to be, or worse, or at least elsewhere.

At the gate she stopped and looked back at the house, her house, lit by one candle in the window behind which Wren slept, and she understood suddenly why leaving felt like dying. Not because she loved the valley—it hadn't earned love, not really, it had only ever tolerated her—but because the person she had been here, careful Zephyrine, invisible Zephyrine, herb-wife who kept her eyes down and her voice locked and her heart in a jar like the honey—that person had nowhere else to exist. Leaving Hollowmere didn't mean carrying that woman with her. It meant burying her here, in the frozen garden, under the rosemary, and walking away lighter and stranger and unrecognizable even to herself.

Grief for a life built on hiding, she thought, is a strange grief, because you can't mourn it openly. There was no grave to visit. There was no one to tell. If she stood in the market square tomorrow and announced *I am grieving thirty years of silence*, the neighbors would nod politely and decide the winter had gotten to her, and they wouldn't be entirely wrong.

So she grieved it privately, standing at her own gate in the falling snow, and the valley received her grief the way it had received everything else about her: silently, without comment, keeping her secret right up until the moment it couldn't anymore.

"You could have been kinder," she told it. Not angry. Just honest, finally, to a valley instead of a well.

The wind moved through the bare trees, and if it meant anything, it meant nothing she could translate, and for once that felt less like loneliness and more like a door closing gently on a room she no longer needed.

* * *

Inside, she found Wren awake.

Not crying, not frightened—awake in the watchful way, sitting up among the quilts with their knees drawn up, eyes catching the candlelight with that deer-bright gleam that had frightened her the first night and now simply looked like Wren. The child had heard her come in. Of course they had. Wren heard everything, in whatever language it arrived.

"Nightmare?" Zephyrine asked, though she knew the signs of Wren's nightmares now—the frosted windows, the screaming in the old tongue—and none of those signs were present.

Wren shook their head. Then they slid off the bed, padded across the cold boards on bare feet—bare feet, always bare feet, the cold never seemed to reach them, another item on the list of things about this child Zephyrine had decided not to think about until after midwinter—and stood beside the packed bag, looking at it. Looking at her. Asking, in the wordless fluent way they had, the question adults take entire evenings to fail to ask.

"Yes," Zephyrine said. "We're going. Tomorrow, before light. Before anyone's stirring, before Grell can count his chickens and find mine missing." She crouched down to the child's height, which her knees protested, and which she ignored. "There are stones that need waking. Old ones. You're... part of how they wake. And I'm part of getting you there safely, and after that—" She faltered. She had rehearsed this speech to the valley and to the well and to the gate latch, but never to the actual person it was for. "After that, we'll see. I'm not going to promise you things I don't understand yet. But I'm not leaving you anywhere. That much I know the way I know my own hands."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then they reached out and took her hand.

No hesitation. No asking. Small fingers, warm as bread, closing around hers with the plain certainty of someone picking up the last thing that belonged to them. And Zephyrine—who had spent thirty years being untouchable, whose hand had shaken maybe five human hands in three decades and each time like a transaction—felt her throat close up entirely.

"That's settled, then," she managed.

Wren nodded once, businesslike, then pointed at the pack, then at themselves, then made the spiraling gesture that meant, as near as Zephyrine could translate it, *the road goes where it goes and we go with it*, or possibly *I want the honey now*. With Wren it was often both.

"Not the honey tonight. The honey is provisions."

The look Wren gave her was so precisely Bramble's look that Zephyrine laughed—a real laugh, rusty, startling them both—and surrendered one spoonful, rationed, ceremonial, from the second jar. The first jar was sacred. Even Wren seemed to grasp this.

They ate their honey by the banked fire, and the child leaned against her side, heavy with sleep again, trusting as a stone rolling downhill, and Zephyrine sat very still and thought: *this is what it costs. Everything I built to stay safe, and it costs one small hand holding mine, and it's cheap. It's unbearably cheap. I paid thirty years for something I could have had for nothing, if I'd been brave enough to open the door.*

Outside, the snow kept falling in its slow uncertain spirals, and somewhere beyond the ridge the ward hummed its failing note, thinner than yesterday, quieter than tomorrow.

* * *

They left in the blue hour before dawn, when the valley was all shadow and breath.

Zephyrine wore her mother's shawl under the coat, for warmth, she told herself. She carried the pack and the second-best staff and led Bramble on a rope halter, because the goat had informed her at milking time, in tones that permitted no appeal, that she was coming along, that leaving her behind was "an insult with a rope on it," and that she was the only member of this expedition who had read the covenant's history firsthand.

"I read it while it was happening," Bramble had said. "Which is better than reading it."

Zephyrine had argued for approximately nine seconds before recognizing the futility. Arguing with Bramble was like arguing with weather. Besides—someone had to carry the cheese.

At the gate she paused one last time. Habit, she told herself. Checking the latch. But she let her palm rest on the cold iron a moment longer than checking required, and she said nothing at all, because there was nothing left to say to a valley that had never known her, and because some goodbyes are better spoken to the road ahead.

Behind her, the house stood dark and ordinary, already forgetting her. Ahead, the track climbed toward the tree line and the border of quiet, toward a world she hadn't heard in thirty years—a world of wind and birdsong and her own loud heartbeat, waiting on the other side of a line in the snow.

Wren slipped their hand into hers without being asked. Bramble tugged the rope impatiently. And Zephyrine Winterbourne stepped off her own doorstep for the last time, feeling the boards sing their third-step note behind her—one final time, unrehearsed, unavoided—and walked north into the falling snow, toward the place where the silence ended.

She did not look back again. But she counted her steps, the way you do when you're leaving something behind, and at two hundred she stopped counting, because the trees had closed around them, and the dark ahead was full of sounds she had almost forgotten existed.

One of them, low and distant, was not a sound any valley should make.

It sounded, Zephyrine thought with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold, like something vast remembering how to breathe.
