## 12. First Road, First Hunters

The King's road was wider than any path Zephyrine had ever walked, and it embarrassed her how much that frightened her.

Thirty years in Hollowmere had taught her the grammar of narrow things: goat trails, deer tracks, the single-lane track to Tobias Grell's fence line. She knew how to be invisible in small places. But a road built for carts and soldiers and the ordinary traffic of an empire—*that* was a different language entirely, and she did not speak it.

"Keep to the verge," she murmured to Wren, who was walking with the particular bounce of a child who had discovered that the world outside the ward was *loud* and had decided, on balance, to approve of it. "Walk like we're going somewhere boring."

Wren looked up at her with those deer-bright eyes and gave her a look that said, as clearly as any speech, *you are the most boring person I have ever met.*

"I mean it," Zephyrine said. "Boring. We're a herb-wife and her niece going to market. Herb-wives are never interesting. That's the whole point of us."

Wren considered this, then deliberately skipped.

Zephyrine sighed and let them skip. There were arguments worth having and arguments that were simply weather, and she was learning—which is to say relearning after thirty years—to tell the difference.

The road ran south out of the hills, following a river that had not yet forgotten its own name. It was early enough in the day that the frost still clung to the fence posts and the hedgerows glittered like someone had strung them with tiny dishonest jewels. Carts passed them now and then—a tinker with a squeaking axle, a farmer hauling turnips, a wool merchant who nodded at Zephyrine with the flat courtesy one gives to strangers one hopes never to see again. Each time, Wren's hand crept into hers, and each time, Zephyrine squeezed back and tried to breathe like a woman with nothing in her pockets but dried yarrow.

They made good distance by midday. Too good, maybe. Zephyrine kept catching herself scanning the ridgelines the way she used to scan the sky for storms before shearing day, and every time she caught herself doing it, she told herself: *the wardens patrol the borders of Hollowmere. They don't ride the King's road looking for herb-wives.*

She believed this the way you believe a floor will hold you. Right up until it doesn't.

* * *

It was Wren who heard them first—or felt them, rather. The child stopped mid-skip, went very still, and turned their face toward the south like a compass needle finding something it wished it hadn't.

Zephyrine listened. At first there was only wind in the hedges and the river muttering over its stones. Then, beneath that, hooves. Many hooves. And under the hooves, something worse: the particular quality of silence that armed men carry with them, a silence like held breath.

"Hedge," she said quietly. "Now."

They got off the road and into the bare-branched tangle at its edge just as the patrol came around the bend—and Zephyrine's stomach dropped through the frozen ground, because these were not the grey-cloaked riders she'd glimpsed on the ridgeline above Hollowmere. These were *Oathwardens*, proper ones, in their slate-and-silver livery, six of them riding in formation with the white oath-knots stitched at their collars. And at their head rode a woman in a plain dark coat with no ornament at all except the sword, which somehow looked more official than any badge would have.

She sat her horse like the horse was furniture. Her eyes moved across the road, the hedges, the sky, cataloguing everything and being satisfied by nothing. Zephyrine had seen that exact expression once before, thirty years ago, on the face of a warden who had come to the valley asking questions about a woman who'd died keeping secrets. The expression had not aged well. Neither, apparently, had the job.

The patrol drew level with their hiding place. Zephyrine pressed Wren against the hedge's cold heart and put her mouth close to the child's ear.

"Don't move. Don't blink if you can help it."

One of the wardens—the youngest, a boy whose armor hadn't learned his shape yet—reined in suddenly. "Commander." He was staring at the hedge. "There's—"

"There's a hedge," said the commander.

Her voice was dry as old paper. Zephyrine filed it away the way she'd file a dangerous plant: *knows what it does, uses it anyway.*

"It sounded like—"

"It sounded like a hedge. Hedges are full of things that rustle, Aldous. If we stopped for every rustle between here and Ferrow's Cross we'd arrive next spring, and the irregularity would have had all winter to become a catastrophe." She turned her horse with two fingers on the rein. "We make the shrine ruin by dusk. Move."

But she didn't move. Not immediately. She sat there, half-turned, and her gaze swept the hedgerow one more time—slowly, thoroughly, the way a hand passes over a shelf checking for dust. Zephyrine held her breath and thought about turnips. Dried yarrow. The price of lamp oil. She thought the most boring thoughts available to a human mind, and she wrapped Wren in them like a blanket.

The commander's eyes passed over the hedge.

Paused.

Moved on.

"Commander?" said the young one.

"Nothing," Serah Vane said, and rode.

* * *

They waited until the hoofbeats faded to memory, and then they waited another hour besides, because Zephyrine had learned patience from goats, and goats are the world's great philosophers of *wait and see*.

"That was them," Wren signed against her palm. The child had begun teaching Zephyrine a small vocabulary of hand-shapes on the road—crude, urgent, wonderful. "The quiet-keepers. They smell wrong. Like iron and forgetting."

"They're looking for something," Zephyrine said. "And 'irregularity' is a word I don't love."

"The hawk," Wren signed. "They'll find the hawk."

Zephyrine frowned. "What hawk?"

Wren pointed at the sky.

There, circling high above the road with the lazy menace of a creature that has never once paid for its dinner, rode a hawk. Zephyrine knew it—not personally, but by reputation; it hunted the river valley and had, in its time, relieved her hen-yard of two birds and zero remorse. It was circling now because circling was what hawks did when the world below them was interesting.

And Zephyrine, standing in a hedge with a seal-child's hand in hers, understood three things at once, the way you understand a fall only once you're falling.

First: the wardens were hunting an irregularity in the silence, and anything wild and awake would draw them like blood draws sharks.

Second: the hawk was wild and awake, and directly above the road where the wardens would camp tonight.

Third: she could talk to it.

"Oh no," she said aloud.

Wren tilted their head.

"No," Zephyrine repeated, mostly to herself. "Because here's what happens. I call down a hawk, I ask it to do me a favor, the favor works, and then—" she gestured helplessly at the empty air where the patrol had been, "—then there's a *pattern*. A woman on the road, and a hawk behaving strangely, and a warden with paper eyes writing it all down. Power used well still leaves fingerprints, Wren. My mother taught me that. Well. She taught me nothing, because she died, but Bramble taught me that, quoting her, which amounts to the same thing."

Wren took her hand and pressed it flat against their own small chest, then pointed south, toward the shrine ruins where the wardens would sleep tonight, and then at the hawk, and then made a shape with both hands that Zephyrine had learned only yesterday and already hated: *danger comes either way.*

The child was right. That was the worst part. The wardens were between them and Ferrow's Cross, and Ferrow's Cross was between them and everything that mattered. Sooner or later she would have to pass those slate-and-silver backs, and she would rather do it while their camp was in chaos than while it was orderly.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. But if this goes badly, you're explaining it to Bramble."

* * *

Climbing was harder than it used to be. Thirty years of valley living had kept her strong in the way that hauling water and chasing goats keeps you strong, but the scree slope above the road argued with her knees the whole way up, and Wren scampered ahead like gravity was a rumor. From the ridge, they could see the whole river valley laid out below, the road a pale ribbon, and—there, in the lee of the ruined shrine, the wardens' camp: six small fires in a careful row, horses picketed, men moving with the economy of people who had done this ten thousand times.

At the center of it, the commander sat apart, writing. Even from here Zephyrine could see the scratch of her pen in the firelight. *Paper eyes,* she thought. *Writing down the world.*

The hawk was roosting on a dead pine twenty feet away, regarding her with one furious amber eye.

Zephyrine crouched, gathered herself, and did the thing she had done perhaps a hundred times in her life and always, always alone: she opened the door in her chest and let the beast-speech out.

It came easier than it ever had in Hollowmere. That frightened her more than the wardens did. Under the open sky, past the ward-line, the gift didn't feel like a shameful secret anymore—it felt like a muscle remembering itself, warm and enormous, and she had to hold it carefully, like carrying water in her hands.

*Sister-of-the-sharp-eye,* she called. *Wind-rider. Mouse-thief. Hen-murderer.*

The hawk's head swiveled. Its eye fixed on her with the affronted intensity of aristocracy interrupted at dinner.

*I know your name among the pines,* Zephyrine said. *I know you took my hens and regretted nothing. I ask a debt of you anyway, and I know what that costs, so hear the whole of it: riders sleep below you. Riders who cage the wild speech and burn what sings. I ask you to fly through their camp screaming like the end of the world. Nothing more. Scream, and wheel, and scream again, and then be gone and free of me forever.*

The hawk was silent for a long moment. Wind moved its feathers the way wind moves grass.

*Hens,* it said at last, in a voice like tearing silk, *were owed. This is not owed.*

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "This is begged."

Another silence. Then, with the terrible grace of a creature agreeing to a favor it will bring up at every opportunity for the rest of its life:

*Once. For the small loud one.* Its eye flicked to Wren. *The last word smells like morning. I remember morning.*

It launched off the pine without waiting for thanks, which was just as well, because Zephyrine wasn't sure she had any left.

* * *

From the ridge, they watched it happen, and it was—insofar as terror permits aesthetic judgment—magnificent.

The hawk came off the dark like a thrown knife, hit the camp at head height, and *screamed*. Not a hunting cry. A war cry, a death cry, the sound a hawk makes when it means to unmake the nerves of everything within earshot, and it wheeled through the firelight once, twice, three times, close enough to clip hats, screaming all the while, and the camp detonated. Horses screamed and broke their picket lines. Men fell over cookfires. One warden drew his sword and fought a bedroll. The young one, Aldous, spent several seconds defending himself heroically against a kettle.

And through all of it, the commander did not run about. Did not shout. Serah Vane stood up from her writing, set down her pen with exaggerated care, and watched the hawk with her head slightly tilted, the way a scholar watches a sentence that has made a mistake.

Then her gaze lifted. Past the hawk. Past the fires. Up the black shoulder of the ridge, to where two figures stood silhouetted against the last grey light.

Zephyrine pulled Wren down so fast her knees cracked on the stone.

"She saw," Wren signed, trembling. Or maybe Zephyrine was the one trembling; their hands were tangled together and it was hard to say whose fear was whose.

"Maybe. Maybe she saw two travelers watching a hawk spook some horses. Travelers watch things. It's practically the law." Zephyrine heard her own voice doing the thing it did when it lied to itself, bright and brisk and thin as ice over a well. "Come on. Down the far side. We put the whole valley between us and that camp tonight."

They descended in the dark, sliding and catching each other, and made three miles along a shepherd's track before Zephyrine let them stop in the shelter of a fallen beech. Wren was asleep almost before they'd settled, curled against her side, breathing the deep breaths of a child whose conscience was unburdened by kettles.

Zephyrine stayed awake.

She kept seeing it: the commander's face tilted upward, the pen set down so carefully, the way she had paused at the hedge that morning—*there's a hedge*—and the way she'd swept the road behind her before riding on. Not careless. Never careless. A woman who noticed the absence of things. A woman who would lie awake tonight assembling a puzzle out of pieces Zephyrine had handed her one by one: a hedge that rustled wrong, a hawk that screamed like a soldier, two watchers on a ridge at exactly the wrong moment.

Power used well still leaves fingerprints. She had known that since she was small enough to be carried across a border she couldn't remember. And yet the first time she'd used her gift in thirty years—truly used it, in the open world, for a good and necessary reason—and what had it bought her? One night's advantage, traded for a name in a ledger she would never see.

*An irregularity in the silence,* the wardens had said on the ridgeline above Hollowmere. Well. The silence had an irregularity now, and it was her, and it was walking south with a seal-child asleep against its ribs.

Far off, through the bare trees, she could see the campfires of the shrine ruin, regrouped and reordered. Six fires again. Order restored. And beside the largest one, a single figure sitting apart with a board across her knees, writing by firelight, unhurried, thorough, patient as frost.

Zephyrine watched until the figure bent low over the page, and she thought: *she's writing about me. She just doesn't know my name yet.*

Then she closed her eyes, held the sleeping child closer against the cold, and began, silently, to rehearse the most boring story ever told about a herb-wife and her niece traveling to market—for she had a feeling she was going to need it sooner than she'd like.

South, toward Ferrow's Cross. Toward rumors, and stones, and the first word Wren would ever speak in a human tongue.

Toward the ledger, whether she liked it or not.
