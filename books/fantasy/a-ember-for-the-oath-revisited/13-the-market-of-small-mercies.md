## 13. The Market of Small Mercies

Ferrow's Cross smelled like wet wool, woodsmoke, and onions left too long in a pot. Zephyrine had forgotten that towns had smells at all. Hollowmere had only ever smelled of pine and cold stone and goat, a narrow honest vocabulary, whereas this place spoke in paragraphs.

She kept her hood up and Wren's hand in hers and told herself, for the hundredth time since the border, that markets were the safest places to hide. A crowd is a kind of forest, her mother used to say. Nothing sees you if everything is looking at something else. Her mother had said a great many useful things before the well took her, and Zephyrine had spent thirty years deciding which of them were wisdom and which were just instructions for disappearing.

The market square was cobbled and crooked, ringed with stalls whose awnings sagged under the weight of yesterday's snow. A man was selling eels from a barrel, holding each one up like a small furious trophy. Two women argued over the price of turnips with the practiced venom of sisters. Children wove between legs, and somewhere a fiddler played badly enough that people paid him to stop, then started again out of spite.

Wren stopped dead in the middle of it all.

Zephyrine felt the small hand tighten around hers—not in fear, exactly. In *volume*. The child's eyes, those deer-bright eyes that caught lantern light even at noon, went wide and moved everywhere at once: the eels, the turnips, the fiddler, a dog stealing bread, a pigeon disputing territory with another pigeon. Thirty years of quiet had made Zephyrine forget what abundance looked like to someone who had never seen it. To Wren, she realized, this must be like walking into a river.

"It's all right," she murmured, and then, because she could not help herself, said it again in the old speech, low as breath: *all of it is loud but none of it is hungry.*

Wren looked up at her. And there—that flicker. That almost-smile. The child had been thawing by degrees since the ford, since the injury, since Maren's salves and Maren's terrible singing, and every degree of it did something to Zephyrine's chest that she refused to name in public.

*Hungry,* Wren signed back—one hand to belly, an old gesture from the road—and then pointed, with devastating precision, at a stall piled with honey buns.

"You've been fed twice today."

*Twice is not forever.*

"That," said Zephyrine, "is the most human thing you've ever said."

They had agreed on rules. Maren had drilled them the night before in the rented room above the tannery, tapping the table like a schoolmistress: *You are a widow from the hill country selling herbs. The child is your sister's orphan, slow of tongue, God rest the mother. You do not make eye contact with soldiers. You do not look at the cathedral.* She had said that last one twice, and looked at Zephyrine over her spectacles in a way that suggested she knew exactly how tempting cathedrals were to people like them.

Zephyrine found the herb-sellers' row along the square's east side, where the ground was softest with spilled lavender and the shouting was marginally less competitive. She set out her bundles on a borrowed corner of a trestle table—yarrow, comfrey, dried nettle, the wintergreen she'd gathered on the pass—and waited to be noticed, which was a skill she had refined over three decades into something close to an art form.

The first customer was a grandmother buying yarrow for a grandson's nosebleeds. The second was a cook who haggled over nettle with the intensity of a woman negotiating a hostage release. The third was nobody; the third time, Zephyrine simply stood and watched the square, and let herself do the thing she never let herself do in Hollowmere.

She listened.

Not with her gift—with her ears, the ordinary ones, the ones that had gone rusty inside the ward until crossing the border had flooded them back to life like a dried creek in spring. Towns talked constantly. Towns talked about grain prices and marriages and whose pig had gotten into whose parsnips. But underneath all that, today, ran a different current, and once she heard it she couldn't stop hearing it.

"—swear on my mother's grave, the whole herd came down through the high passes, deer and elk both, running side by side like they'd signed some treaty—"

"—the shrine up at Coldwell, my cousin walked past it Tuesday, says frost on the stones in the shape of *tears*, actual tears, frozen down the face of it—"

"—my grandfather always said the forest had names for its trees. Called them all fools for laughing. Well, who's laughing now, with the pines going grey up past the ridge—"

"Superstition," said a turnip woman comfortably. "Hard winter makes people strange."

"Hard winter makes *animals* strange," said the first voice. "My brother-in-law swears his dogs won't bark anymore. Just sit at the treeline at dusk, watching. Like they're waiting for somebody."

Zephyrine's hands went still over her herbs.

Beasts fleeing the high forest. Shrines weeping frost. Trees losing their names. She had heard these things from wolves, in the old speech, delivered with the flat grief of creatures reporting weather. But hearing them here—in market cadence, in gossip, sandwiched between complaints about turnip prices—was different. It meant the long winter wasn't a secret the wild was keeping. It was leaking. It was becoming the sort of thing ordinary people mentioned while buying onions, right before deciding it was nothing.

That was how endings came, she thought. Not as thunder. As rumor, repeated until it wore smooth, and then forgotten.

"You're the herb-wife."

She looked up. A broad man in a butcher's apron stood at her table, wiping his hands on it as though it were his own. He nodded at her bundles. "Yarrow? Real yarrow, off a mountain, not the swamp stuff?"

"Off a mountain," she confirmed. "Above the snowline."

He bought four bundles without haggling, which marked him immediately as either very rich or very worried. "My wife's lungs," he said, quieter. "The cold's got into them and won't leave. Doctor's given up. I don't hold with giving up."

"Steep it hot," Zephyrine said. "Honey if you have it. And keep her feet warm—it sounds foolish, but the body fights better when it isn't spending itself on the cold." She hesitated, then added, "And talk to her. Lungs heal faster in a house with talking in it. I don't know why. They just do."

The butcher looked at her oddly for a moment—looked at her the way people sometimes had, all her life, without knowing why—and then he nodded slowly. "Talk to her," he repeated. "Aye. All right." He tucked the bundles away like treasure and went, and Zephyrine watched him go and felt the familiar ache, the one shaped like *I could have done more*, and put it away where she kept all the others.

It was midday when the rumor arrived that changed the temperature of the square.

She heard it first as a change in the crowd's sound—a ripple, the way water ripples when something moves beneath it. Then a boy came pelting across the cobbles from the direction of the north gate, breathless, triumphant, delivering news the way boys deliver news: loudly, and slightly wrong.

"They're coming down! Out of the high forest—the *wolves*—a whole pack, walking the old drover's road like they own it, and old Hesk at the gate swears the leader stopped and looked right at him, right in the eye, and *bowed*. Bowed! Like a courtier!"

"Bowed," said the turnip woman flatly.

"Bowed! And then they went on toward the western hills, and Hesk says—" the boy dropped his voice to the thrilling register reserved for doom, "—he says their eyes were *sorrowful*."

The square laughed, mostly. But not entirely. Zephyrine counted the faces that didn't laugh—the shepherd with the grey beard, the young woman mending a net, the innkeeper standing very still in his doorway—and thought: *there you are.* The ones who had heard something already. The ones whose grandmothers had told them things. The covenant's embers, banked low in human hearths too, still glowing after all.

Her pack-leader, walking the drover's road with sorrowful eyes. She knew why. The pack had parted from them two days back with a promise to spread word among the wild places, and clearly the word was spreading—and being received by humans as spectacle, as omen, as a story to tell children. For now. She thought of Vane, wherever she was, collecting the same reports and reading them very differently.

*Fragile,* she thought. *All of this is fragile.* Hope in an indifferent crowd was a candle in a barn full of hay.

"Wren?" she said softly.

No answer.

Her stomach dropped before her head even turned, the way it does for mothers and goat-keepers and anyone who has ever loved something small in a large world. She spun. The trestle table. The empty space beside it where Wren had been standing.

One heartbeat of pure ice. Two.

Then she saw the crowd parting, gently, puzzledly, around a small figure standing in the open center of the square—and her fear changed shape but did not lessen.

Wren had walked to the middle of the market. Alone. Barefoot still—they'd never managed shoes that the child would tolerate—and utterly unbothered by the cold or the crowd, head tipped back, turning slowly, slowly, like a compass needle finding north. People were stopping to stare. A fishwife had frozen mid-haggle. Even the fiddler had faltered.

And Wren was listening—to something no one else could hear—and Zephyrine, crossing the square at a pace carefully calibrated to look like a mother retrieving a wandering child rather than a fugitive retrieving a seal, felt it too, faintly, through the soles of her feet.

Deep. Far below the cobbles. Below the cellars and the graves and the cathedral's drowned foundations.

A hum. Old and patient and half-asleep.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, no. Oh, yes.*

The third oath-stone. Under Ferrow's Cross. Under the cathedral Maren had told her not to look at. It wasn't sleeping quietly. It was *calling*—and the last living seal had heard it across half a town, the way a bell hears a bell.

She reached Wren and knelt, taking the small shoulders, putting herself between the child and the staring crowd. "Wren. Look at me. Look at me."

Deer-bright eyes came down from whatever height they'd been fixed on and focused on her face. And Wren's mouth opened, worked, struggled—Zephyrine saw the effort of it, the enormous mechanical difficulty of shaping a human word, a skill the child had shown no interest in and no aptitude for through all the weeks of the road—

"—stones," Wren said.

Clear as a bell. First human word, spoken aloud, in the middle of a market square, loud enough that the fishwife heard it, loud enough that the boy with the news heard it, loud enough that a passing pair of grey-cloaked men by the north gate—she would swear this later, would replay it a hundred times—paused, and turned, and looked.

"*Stones*," Wren said again, pointing straight down, delighted, as though Zephyrine might not have understood the first time.

The crowd murmured. Someone chuckled. The turnip woman said, "Children say the strangest things," and the moment began, mercifully, to dissolve back into commerce. But Zephyrine rose with Wren bundled against her hip, heart hammering, and met the eyes of the two grey cloaks across the square.

Oathwardens. Not hunting—patrolling, bored, one eating a meat pie. But one of them was still looking at her. Looking at the barefoot child. Looking with the particular patience of a man whose job was noticing things, and who had just noticed one.

Zephyrine did what thirty years of practice told her to do. She smiled the dull smile of a tired aunt, shifted Wren's weight, and turned toward the herb stalls as though she had forgotten something—as though she were nobody, as though the word *stones* had been nothing, as though the ground beneath the whole singing town weren't humming a name only she and a child could hear.

But as she gathered her bundles with steady hands, Wren leaned against her ear and whispered it once more, softer, in the old speech this time, and the whisper was worse than the shout:

*It knows we're here. It has been waiting so long.*

Behind them, across the square, the bored Oathwarden with the meat pie had stopped chewing. He was writing something down.
