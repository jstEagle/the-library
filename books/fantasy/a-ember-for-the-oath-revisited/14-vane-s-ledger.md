## 14. Vane's Ledger

The trouble with a perfect silence, Serah Vane had learned over nineteen years of service, was that it made people lazy.

She stood at the window of the waystation at Cold Harrow and watched her junior wardens drill in the yard below. Fourteen of them, grey-cloaked, moving through the forms with the crisp mechanical precision of men who had never once needed them. Down here in the low country, the Oathwardens were mostly tax collectors with better boots. They checked seals on traveling merchants, confiscated charms from grandmothers who knotted protective knots without knowing why, and spent their evenings complaining about the food. The wild things that the covenant had once bound—the speaking beasts, the singing stones, the old green hungers of the forest—had been quiet for so long that half her own command believed they had been quiet forever.

Vane knew better. She had been eight years old when they were not.

"Serah?" Corporal Denny Ashfoot stood in the doorway of her quarters, holding a slate like it might bite him. He was twenty-three, earnest, and possessed of the singular talent of looking perpetually surprised by his own limbs. "The hawk report. From the King's Road patrol. You wanted it copied fair."

"I wanted it *read* to me," Vane said, not turning around. "You've read it three times and each time you've made the hawk less important."

Ashfoot blinked. "Less—"

"You keep calling it an incident." She turned now, and took the slate from his hands, and watched him straighten as though he'd been caught slouching in chapel. "A hawk screaming through a warden camp at dusk, scattering four horses and putting a man's eye out on a bramble. That's not an incident, Corporal. That's a signature."

"A... large hawk, serah?"

"*Speaking through* a hawk," Vane said. "Or riding it. Or wearing its throat like a glove. There are perhaps six ways in all the world to make a bird do what that bird did, and every one of them requires a tongue the law says should not exist." She set the slate down on her desk—a heavy thing of black oak she'd carried from posting to posting, its surface scarred with candle-wax and ink—and opened the ledger beneath it.

The ledger was hers alone. Not an official document; the Order would have frowned on a commander keeping private notes outside proper channels. But Serah Vane had learned long ago that proper channels moved at the speed of committees, and evil—if you believed in evil, and she did, with the simple unshakable faith other people reserved for saints—did not wait for committees.

She dipped her pen and wrote, in her small merciless hand:

*Ninth day before midwinter. King's Road, east mile-marker thirty-one. Hawk incident. No beast-speech recorded directly, but the bird's behavior consistent with compulsion. Patrol reports a woman seen on the road two hours prior: tall, grey-haired, leading a pack-goat, accompanied by a child of perhaps eight. The child did not cry when the hawk came screaming. The child looked up.*

*The child looked up,* she wrote again, underlined it twice, and sat back.

Everyone else in that camp had ducked, flailed, sworn. Soldiers did that. Animals' handlers did that. But a child who watches a screaming hawk with interest rather than terror is a child who has heard hawks scream before—not with fear, but with meaning.

"You think it's real," Ashfoot said quietly. He'd come around to look at the ledger despite himself, the way people always did. "The speeching. I thought—I mean, we're taught it died out. Mostly. Except for—"

"Except for stories," Vane said. "And stories are how it survives. A grandmother hums to her bees. A drover whistles his dogs into shapes no whistle should make. Little inheritances, little leaks, generation after generation, each one smaller than the last." She closed the ledger. "That is why the Order endures, Corporal. Not because we hunt monsters. Because we sweep floors. Every day, a little dust. You cannot kill the wild tongue. You can only outlast it."

"Yes, serah." He hesitated. "And if this one isn't small? If this one is—" He gestured vaguely at the window, at the dark treeline beyond the yard, at everything the cloaks were nominally for. "Real-sized?"

Vane almost smiled. It was a good question. It was, in fact, the question, and she was pleased—genuinely, warmly pleased—that the boy had found it on his own. There was hope for him. There had to be hope for someone, or the whole gray machinery of the world was pointless.

"Then we thank the founders," she said, "that we were standing watch when it stuck its head above ground. Fetch me the road-warden's description again. Exact words this time, not your summary. And send for the ferryman at Wether's Ford—I want to know everyone who crossed east in the last ten days."

* * *

The ferryman's name was Odd Halloway, which explained a great deal about him, and he arrived at Cold Harrow smelling of fish and grievance.

"Ten days?" he said, scratching under his hat. "Serah, I cross folk every day. Old women. Sheep. A fella who paid me in turnips, and I want it noted I accepted those turnips under protest—"

"Noted," Vane said, and made a show of writing it down, which delighted him so much he nearly forgot to be difficult. This was another lesson of her years: most people didn't want money or mercy. They wanted to be *counted*. They wanted their turnips entered into the great book where turnips mattered.

"Now." She turned the ledger toward him, open to a fresh page. "A woman. Grey hair, tall, travel-worn. With a goat and a child. Think."

Halloway squinted at the middle distance, lips moving. Then his face cleared like weather breaking. "Aye! Aye, her. Crossed at first light, day after the frost-moon. Paid proper silver, which put her above half my custom already. Quiet woman. Polite. Bought nothing off me, which was wise, my stock being what it is." He chuckled. "But the little'un. That's the thing I told the road-patrol lad and he didn't hardly listen. The little'un stood at the rail the whole crossing, staring down at the water."

"Children stare at water," Vane said.

"This one *listened* to it," Halloway said. "Like it was talking. Like it was telling jokes." He shrugged, a big easy shrug that said he'd carried stranger cargo than a strange child across stranger rivers. "Made a sound at it, too, near midstream. Not words. Sort of a—clicking? Churring? River-bird noise, my missus called it, and she'd know, she's got a tame starling that won't shut its fool head. And I'll tell you what else, serah, since you're counting." He leaned in, dropping his voice into the register men used for gossip that might be worth something. "The trout come up. Whole shoal of 'em, right under the boat, rising to the surface like churchgoers. Never seen the like. My missus says the river liked her."

"The river liked her," Vane repeated.

"Well. Liked the little'un." Halloway beamed, having delivered his testimony, and clearly expected either a coin or a commendation.

He got neither. Vane thanked him with grave courtesy, saw him to the gate, and then stood for a long moment in the cold with her breath steaming, feeling the shape of it settle into place. Woman. Child. Goat. Beast-speech, witnessed twice now—bird and fish, sky and water, the two oldest choirs. Moving east, away from the deep country, toward—

Toward what? Toward Ferrow's Cross, if she followed the road. Market town, cathedral town, fat and safe and full of people who hadn't thought about the covenant since their grandparents' funerals. Or past it, into the high forest, into the old places, the sleeping places.

The stones, said a voice in her memory, in her mother's voice, in the voice of every frightened villager of Marrowdale: *when the stones wake, the wild comes back.*

"Corporal Ashfoot," she called, and the boy appeared as if summoned, because he had been hovering, because he was twenty-three and she was his whole education. "Ride to the provincial office at Dunmark. Request warrant papers—full authority, irregular gift suspected, category three."

Ashfoot's eyes went wide. Category three hadn't been invoked in the province in eleven years. "Full authority means requisition, serah. Means men. Means—"

"Means that if I am wrong, I have inconvenienced a market town," Vane said. "And if I am right, Corporal, I have saved it. Go. And Ashfoot—" he had one foot in the stirrup already, bless his eagerness— "*ride well.* Whatever is coming down that road, I would prefer to meet it prepared rather than punctual."

He went, hooves hammering the frozen mud, and Vane went back inside to her ledger.

* * *

That night she built up the fire and did what she always did before a hunt, the ritual she had never spoken aloud to any living soul, because it was not doctrine and the Order would call it superstition and let them. She opened the ledger to its very first page—faded, brown-spotted, written in a younger hand, a harder hand, her own at nineteen—and she read the names.

Not victims' names. The Order kept rolls of the lost, and she consulted those often enough. These were different names. Small ones. Ordinary ones.

*Marta Ellery, hedgewife, fined four marks, knots confiscated.*

*The Tam brothers, drovers, warned twice, whistles broken.*

*Aldous Pymm, shepherd, one month's imprisonment for singing to his flock in the old cadence.*

Page after page. Year after year. Nineteen years of small names, small crimes, small silences, each entry in her neat hand like a stitch in a garment she was sewing against the cold. Other commanders laughed at her ledger—she knew they did; Commander Brase at Dunmark had called it her *prayer book*, not unkindly, and she had not corrected him, because he was more right than he knew.

Because here was the truth that Vane held the way other people held faith, warm against the chest, unexamined because examination could only bruise it: the flood does not begin with a wave. It begins with drips. A knot here. A whistle there. A grandmother humming to bees, harmless, harmless, until the granddaughter hums something older, and the great-granddaughter speaks, and then one morning there are horns in the high forest and children walking into the trees and a village named Marrowdale with forty graves and no explanation anyone would accept.

She had been eight. She remembered the snow, which had fallen grey. She remembered the mothers at the well saying *the stones have woken, the stones have woken* the way people say a name they can't believe. She remembered her brother Tammy, seven years old, standing at the treeline with his head tipped back, listening to something in the dark between the pines, listening with such perfect happiness—and she remembered that he had turned around once, smiled at her, and stepped in, and that no search ever found so much as a bootprint.

The wild had taken him gently. That was the horror of it. Everyone expected the wilderness to devour; instead it *invited*, and invitation was worse, because you could not guard a child against being loved.

So: drips. Stitches. Sweep the floor daily. Outlast it.

She blew out her candle and lay in the dark listening to the wind move down from the high country, and permitted herself—for exactly the length of time it took the fire to settle—one moment of the old grief, Tammy's smile in the treeline, before folding it away with the rest.

*Somewhere east of me,* she thought, *a woman is teaching a child to speak to fish.*

It was not rage she felt, lying there. Her enemies, had they known her, would have imagined rage—wardens were supposed to burn with zealot fire, all brimstone and banner. But what Serah Vane felt was closer to a surgeon's calm. Somewhere east of her, the drip had become a stream, and streams became rivers, and she intended to find the source and stop it with both hands, patiently, thoroughly, and without hatred, because hatred was a luxury and the work was work enough.

* * *

Warrant papers came back in nine days, sealed with the dunmark signet, and with them a letter from Commander Brase that managed, in three paragraphs, to congratulate her, doubt her, and wash the Order's hands all at once. *Category three is a serious instrument, Serah. Trusting your judgment. Trusting also that whatever walks your roads proves worth the walking.*

"They always say that," muttered Sergeant Quill, her second, reading over her shoulder. Quill was fifty, leathery, and had survived three commands by the simple method of agreeing with whoever currently fed him. "Right up until you catch something real, and then it's *why weren't you faster*."

"Then we shall be fast anyway," Vane said. She folded the warrant into its oilskin case and buckled it inside her cloak, against her heart, where documents of that weight belonged. "Saddle up. Twelve riders, light kit. We ride for the shrine road."

Quill's eyebrows climbed. "Shrine road? That's wild country, serah. Flooded valley, dead villages, nothing worth—"

"Exactly," Vane said. "Nothing worth anything except to someone who values what everyone else abandoned. Our herb-wife with her goat has left the King's Road. The toll-keeper at Millbeck saw her take the fork two days ago. Nobody takes the shrine road, Sergeant. Unless they're going home to something."

She paused at the door of the waystation, looking east, where the morning fog was peeling off the fields and the high forest stood blue and patient on the horizon, waiting the way old things wait.

In her saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth beside the warrant, rode her private ledger, all nineteen years of small names. She brought it on hunts the way other officers brought relics. A reminder of what the stitches were for.

"Twelve riders," she said. "Ride clean. Ride quiet. And if any of you hears a bird say something it shouldn't—" she swung up into the saddle, and for just a moment the surgeon's calm showed the steel underneath it— "you will tell me before you tell God."

They rode east, twelve grey cloaks on the white road, and behind them the waystation bell rang the hour, small and clear and utterly silent of anything else, as bells in a tamed country ought to be.

Ahead of them, past the flooded shrine and the weeping stones and a woman carrying a secret older than kingdoms, midwinter waited—and so, Vane thought grimly, did she.
