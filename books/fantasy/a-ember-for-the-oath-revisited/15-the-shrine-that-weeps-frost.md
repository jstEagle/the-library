## 15. The Shrine That Weeps Frost

The shrine found them before they found it, which was the first strange thing, and by now Zephyrine had learned to take strange things as a kind of weather.

They had been walking two days out of Ferrow's Cross, following the frost. That was Maren's instruction, given over a cup of willow-bark tea while Wren slept off the worst of the fever: *You'll know the stone by its weeping. The old shrines weep frost in summer and weep frost in winter, and the frost runs uphill, which no honest frost should do.* Zephyrine had thought this was healer's poetry. Healers, in her experience, were half poet and half vulture, and it was best not to ask which half was which.

But there it was, on the third morning, when the road gave out and the forest took over: a line of frost running up a hillside like a silver vein in dark stone. It climbed through the bracken, over a fallen birch, past a boulder wearing its lichen like an old man's beard, and vanished over the crest.

"Uphill," Zephyrine said to Wren, who was riding on her back with their chin hooked over her shoulder, watching the world with those deer-bright eyes. "Frost, going uphill. I want you to remember that you saw it too, so that later, when I doubt my own mind, you can confirm it."

Wren patted her cheek, which was their way of saying many things, most of which she chose to find comforting.

The shrine sat in a hollow on the far side of the hill, sunk into the earth like a tooth into a gum. It had been a round building once—she could see the curve of the foundation stones under a century of moss—but the roof had fallen in so long ago that the fallen roof had become a garden, and the garden had become a small wild kingdom of fern and rowan saplings. Water filled the hollow to the depth of a man's knee, dark and perfectly still, so that the whole ruin lay doubled beneath it, shrine and shadow-shrine, and it was impossible to say which one was the more ruined.

And the frost. It wept from the shrine's inner walls in long silver runnels, sliding down the stones, spreading across the black water in feathered sheets—running, Zephyrine noted with a lurch, *uphill* where the walls sloped, pooling at the top of arches, hanging from the underside of lintels. It was as if the cold had forgotten which way was down, or as if the shrine had forgotten, and the cold was simply being polite about it.

"Well," Zephyrine said. "Maren wasn't lying. I owe her an apology. I'll deliver it in the form of complaining about her tea."

She set Wren down at the water's edge and stood a moment, doing what she always did before doing something foolish, which was to catalogue all the reasons not to do it. The water was cold. The water was deep. The water was the exact color of the well in Hollowmere, the one with the cracked ward-stone at the bottom, and she had not enjoyed that descent either, though at least that one had been dry. There was no telling what slept in flooded places. In her experience, flooded places were where the world kept the things it had decided not to think about.

But the frost ran uphill. And Wren had gone still in that particular way—not the stillness of a child being quiet, but the stillness of a tuning fork that has found its note.

They stepped into the water without hesitation, barefoot as always—Zephyrine had given up, somewhere outside Ferrow's Cross, trying to keep shoes on them; the shoes appeared each morning at the edge of their bedroll, neatly paired, like small abandoned boats—and waded toward the shrine's heart, and the frost parted before their feet.

Not melted. *Parted.* It drew back from their ankles the way a crowd draws back from someone important, and Zephyrine, following, saw that it did the same for her, though more slowly, more grudgingly, the way a crowd draws back from someone it isn't sure it likes.

The water reached her knees, then her thighs. At the center of the shrine, where the roof's great ring had once been, a single pillar still stood whole, waist-high above the water, and on its top lay a stone the size of a loaf of bread, round and grey and utterly ordinary, except that it was weeping frost in a steady silver thread, and except that the water around it was the only water in the shrine that had not frozen at all. It steamed, faintly. It was the warmest thing in the hollow, and everything around it was cold, and Zephyrine thought: *of course. It's the hearth. The whole shrine was built around this the way a house is built around a hearth, and when the fire went low, the house grew cold.*

Wren reached the pillar first. They put both hands on the stone.

And they sang.

Zephyrine had heard Wren make sounds before—the word *stones*, offered in the market at Ferrow's Cross like a coin too precious to spend; the wordless humming they did at night, sometimes, that made the frost on the bedroll fern into patterns; the scream, that one terrible morning after the nightmare, in the language that frosted windows. But she had never heard them *sing*. It was not a melody, exactly. It was more like the shape of a melody, the way a footprint is the shape of a foot. It rose and it fell and it repeated, and each repetition was slightly different, the way a river repeats and is never the same river, and Zephyrine felt the hairs on her arms rise and her throat tighten, because she recognized it—not the song, but the *grammar* of it. It was the grammar of migration. It was the pattern the geese wrote across the sky in autumn, the pattern the starlings wrote at dusk, the pattern she had spent thirty years not being able to read and two weeks learning to read, and here was a child standing in a flooded ruin, singing it into a stone.

The stone woke.

Not all the way. That was the heartbreak of it. Zephyrine saw it happen—saw the grey surface flush with a light like dawn seen through a closed door, saw the frost on the pillar flare silver, saw the whole hollow shiver as if a great animal had turned over in its sleep—and then the light guttered, and steadied, and stayed there, half-lit, like a fire that has caught but cannot grow because there is not enough of something. Not wood. Zephyrine understood, with a certainty that settled into her bones like cold water, that what it lacked was not fuel.

*It lacked the rest of the song.*

Wren's hands fell away from the stone and they swayed, and Zephyrine caught them under the arms, and the child's weight against her was frighteningly slight, the weight of a bird, the weight of a bundle of sticks. Their eyes were closed. Frost had crept up their bare legs to the knee, and where it touched their skin it did not melt.

"Wren." She lifted them out of the water, up onto the pillar's shoulder, out of the cold. "Wren, look at me."

Their eyes opened. They smiled—the same untroubled smile they'd worn on her doorstep in the first snow, barefoot and unafraid—and pressed one small hand flat against her sternum, right over her heart, the way they did when they wanted her to understand something that words were too small for.

And the stone took her.

* * *

There was no sensation of falling, which was the strangest part. One moment she was kneeling in freezing water with a child's hand on her chest, and the next she was standing in the same hollow on a night with a full moon, and the water was gone, and the roof was whole, and the shrine was lit with nine small flames that burned without flickering, and the air smelled of juniper and cold iron.

And her mother was there.

Zephyrine had not seen her mother's face in thirty years, and she had forgotten—she had *chosen* to forget, the way you choose not to look at a scar—how young she had been. Not the grey, stooped, herb-crushed woman of the valley's last years, who had died quietly in a chair by a window with her hands folded as if waiting for a lesson to begin. This woman was perhaps five-and-twenty, mud to the knees, hair escaping its braid in wild dark threads, and she was *running*.

She was running through the shrine with a bundle in her arms, and the bundle was screaming, and the nine flames bent toward her as she passed like wheat bending toward wind.

Zephyrine stood at the edge of the memory and could not move. She knew, in the way one knows things in dreams, that she could not be seen, could not be heard, could not change one hair of what was about to happen. She was a ghost at her own christening.

Her mother reached the pillar. The stone on it—this same stone, but awake, blazing softly, alive—hummed as she approached, and the humming had words in it, or the shape of words, and Zephyrine's blood went to ice water because she could *read* it now. Two weeks of Wren, two weeks of the road, two weeks of the grammar of migration written across the sky, and she could read it.

*Who brings the child?*

"A mother who has run out of roads," her mother said, in the human tongue, breathless, and her voice—Zephyrine's whole body flinched at her voice, that voice she had not heard since she was small enough to be carried—her voice cracked on the last word.

*The child is marked,* the stone sang, gentle and vast. *The child will be hunted. There is no place in the wild realms where the Oathwardens' silence does not reach, and no place in the human realms where her gift is not a death sentence.*

"Then I'll make a place." Her mother's jaw set, and Zephyrine saw her own stubbornness there, her own chin, her own refusal, worn by a younger face. "There's a valley. Hollowmere. The old ward—the one from before, the one they built to smother the covenant's voice. It still holds there. It's dying, but it holds. If I put her inside it—"

*You would bury her gift,* the stone said. *You would bury your own with it, as anchor. The ward must be fed. It takes a speaker to hold a silence. You know this.*

"I know this." Her mother's voice did not waver, and that was the worst of it, that steadiness, that terrible competence. "I'll stay. I'll be the anchor. She grows up in the quiet, and she never knows what she is, and the wardens never come, and she lives."

*She will be alone.*

"She'll be *alive*." And then, softer, and Zephyrine watched her mother's face crumple and reassemble itself the way a soldier's does, in the space of a breath: "Alone is a thing you survive. Dead is not. I've done the arithmetic. I've done it every night since she was born and the crows started saying her name over the chimney."

The stone was silent a long moment. The nine flames leaned. And then it said, in that vast gentle grammar:

*Then let it be witnessed. A bargain unwitnessed is only a wish. Bring her close.*

Her mother stepped forward and unwrapped the bundle, and Zephyrine saw herself—a red-faced, furious infant, screaming with the whole force of a voice that had not yet learned to be afraid—and her mother held her up to the stone, and the stone's light washed over them both, and the stone sang something long and low and sorrowful, and Zephyrine caught only the last of it, the way you catch only the last of a wave:

*—and when the quiet fails, as all quiet fails, she will forgive you or she will not, but she will understand. That is the gift I can give her in place of a childhood. Understanding, late.*

Her mother's arms closed around her. "Late is fine," she whispered. "I'll take late."

* * *

The hollow came back all at once: the cold, the water, the smell of wet stone and frost, Wren's hand still pressed over her heart. Zephyrine was kneeling in the flood and she was crying, which was an inconvenience she registered distantly, the way one registers rain.

She had spent thirty years building a story about her mother. It had been a good story, load-bearing, the kind of story a life can stand on. *She was a coward. She was afraid of what I was, and she hid me the way you hide a broken thing, and she never told me because she was ashamed.* She had carried that story out of the valley in her heart the way Tobias Grell carried his ledger, consulting it daily, adding entries.

And the truth was that her mother had done the arithmetic. Every night. Since the crows started saying her name.

"She traded her whole life for mine," Zephyrine said aloud, to no one, to the frost, to the half-lit stone. "And I spent thirty years calling it cowardice because it was easier than calling it love."

Wren's hand stayed where it was. They looked up at her with those deer-bright eyes, and they did not smile this time, and somehow that was better. They understood grief. They carried, she now knew, the memory of eight dead children and eight shattered stones, and they had come anyway. Barefoot. Unafraid. The way her mother had come to this shrine, mud to the knees, running out of roads.

"You knew," Zephyrine said. "That's why the stone showed me. You asked it to." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, which did very little. "You strange, wonderful creature."

Wren patted her cheek again, and then pointed—at the stone, and then at themselves, and then made the small cupping gesture that Zephyrine had learned meant *not finished*, *not whole*, *more needed*.

"I know." Zephyrine looked at the stone, glowing its stubborn half-light, and the anger she expected to feel at it—for accepting her mother's bargain, for letting a woman bury herself alive in a valley's silence—did not come. What came instead was a kind of terrible tenderness. The stone had given her mother the only mercy on offer: a child alive, and understanding, late. "We'll finish waking you. All of you. I don't know how yet, but we will, and I'll do it with my eyes open, which is more than anyone in my family has managed so far."

She lifted Wren onto her back. The frost on the child's legs had melted now, and their weight against her was still too slight, but their arms came around her neck with the old unhesitating trust, and Zephyrine waded for the shore.

Behind them, the stone's light pulsed once—twice—like a heartbeat remembering its rhythm.

And then, as they reached the water's edge, the frost on the shrine's inner walls did something new. It stopped weeping. All at once, every silver runnel went still, and the whole hollow held its breath, and Zephyrine felt the change in the air the way animals feel a storm before it comes.

From the ridge above the shrine—uphill, where no honest frost should go, where nothing should be standing at all—came the sound of a horn. One long note, thin and clear, the kind of note that is meant to be answered by other horns.

Wren's arms tightened around her neck.

Zephyrine did not look up at the ridge. She looked down at the frost at her feet, where the weeping had stopped, and understood what it meant: the stone had felt its siblings wake, or stir, or *die*, somewhere far to the north, and it had gone quiet to listen.

"Run first," she murmured, turning into the trees. "Understand later. It's the family motto."

The horn sounded again, closer.
