## 16. Her Mother's Bargain

They ran until the horn gave up on them.

Zephyrine chose the path by instinct more than sense—downhill first, then along a streambed where their footprints would drown, then up again through a stand of birches so pale and close together they looked like the ribs of some enormous patient animal. Wren rode her back the whole way, light as a bundle of kindling, breath steady against her neck in that unnerving child-way of theirs: no panting, no fear-sweat. Just watching. Just listening with those deer-bright eyes for whatever came next.

When the trees finally opened onto a hollow—a shepherd's bothy long abandoned, its turf roof sagging like an old hat—Zephyrine set the child down and stood with her hands on her knees, breathing like a bellows.

"Well," she said, when she could. "That went *brilliantly*."

Wren looked up at her with mild reproach, the way Bramble did when Zephyrine blamed the goat for a broken fence the goat had, admittedly, broken, but only because the fence had insulted it first.

"Yes, all right," Zephyrine conceded. "We woke a sleeping stone inside a flooded shrine while wardens sounded horns on the ridge above us. I'm hearing it."

The bothy smelled of old smoke and mouse, which was to say it smelled like safety. There was a hearth, a stool missing one leg, a pile of bracken in the corner soft enough to pass for a bed if one were generous or exhausted. Zephyrine was both. She got a small fire going—small being the operative word; she had spent thirty years learning exactly how much smoke a fire could make before it became a message—and shared out the last of the bread from Ferrow's Cross.

It was only when Wren was fed and curled against her side, eyes drifting shut, that Zephyrine let herself think about what the stone had shown her.

Her mother.

Not the mother she remembered—the woman bent over the herb-drying racks, hands smelling of yarrow, humming tunelessly because she couldn't carry a note in a bucket—but a younger version, striding through snow with a baby wrapped against her chest, crossing a border Zephyrine knew better than her own heartbeat.

*The ward-line*, she thought. *She carried me across the ward-line.*

And the frost-figures had kept coming after that, image after image, unspooling behind her eyes even now, even here, even with Wren's weight warm against her ribs.

* * *

She had thought it was over when they left the shrine. It was not over.

Sleep took her the moment she stopped fighting it, and the stone came with her—not as dream, exactly. Dreams have doors you can leave through. This was more like being *held*. Like standing in the shrine's flooded dark again, frost blooming under her boots, except now there was no cold at all, only the strange dry warmth of something very old paying attention to her.

*Watch*, said the stone, in the way stones speak, which is not words but weight.

And she watched.

Snow. Her mother's boots breaking crust with each stride, deliberate, unhurried, the walk of a woman who had rehearsed this. The border of Hollowmere lay ahead—a line you couldn't see, only feel, a flattening of the world the way a pressed flower is flattened. Behind them, far back down the valley of the world, torchlight. Voices. The particular cadence of men reading from a list.

*"Speaker-born,"* one voice said, and it was not a voice so much as a verdict. *"By law, surrendered to the Crown by its seventh year. By grace, spared the wilding. You know the mercy we offer."*

Her mother had not answered. In the memory, Zephyrine watched her own infant face turn toward the torchlight with interest—the way babies watch fire—and felt, with a lurch, the small bright thing inside that baby reaching toward the voices. Reaching out in beast-speech, wordless, curious, a hello offered to horses and hawks and anything with ears.

The horses screamed. One reared. And the man with the list wrote something down, and his face did the thing that faces do when they decide a child is a problem to be solved.

*"Seventh year,"* he repeated. *"Sooner, if it worsens."*

Her mother walked on. She did not run. Running, Zephyrine understood suddenly, would have been a confession. She walked the way women walk to market, the way women walk past things they must not look at, and the torches fell behind, and the snow thickened, and the world began to go quiet in that specific way Hollowmere was quiet—not silent, but *muted*, as if the whole valley lay under a blanket folded with great care.

At the ward-line itself, her mother stopped.

Here the memory did something memories should not do. It slowed. It leaned in close, the way Wren leaned close to frost-figures, wanting every detail. And Zephyrine saw what her mother did at the line, saw it plainly, unmistakably:

She knelt. She pressed one bare hand flat against the frozen ground—to the stone beneath the ground, to the deep buried root of the ward—and she spoke.

Not beast-speech. Not quite human speech either. Something between, the way a lock is between a door and a key. And Zephyrine felt the shape of the words without hearing them, because she *knew* this bargain, knew it in her bones before she knew it in her mind:

*A life for a life. An anchor for an anchor. My daughter goes free inside your quiet, and I hold the quiet closed around her, every hour of every day, until I cannot.*

The ward accepted. The way wards accept anything: silently, permanently, without sympathy.

Her mother stood, tucked the blanket closer around her daughter's face, and stepped across the line into the valley that forgot.

*Why?* Zephyrine cried into the memory—into the stone, into the dark. *Why not run north? Why not take me beyond any law's reach? Why bury us both in silence?*

And the stone, patient as geology, showed her why.

It showed her maps of the world as it had been thirty years ago. It showed her the Oathwardens' reach—not a kingdom's borders but a web, roads and rivers and mountain passes all stitched with grey cloaks. North: warded ports, ships searched for children who flinched at birdsong. East: the high forest burning in patches, "quarantine fires," the lists called them. West: sea cliffs, and below the sea cliffs, the ninth stone, and around the ninth stone, already, an altar garrisoned.

There had been nowhere. That was the truth of it, plain and ugly. Her mother had not chosen silence over freedom. She had chosen the only silence in the world deep enough to hide in, and she had bought it with the only coin she had.

Herself.

* * *

Zephyrine woke with her cheek pressed to the bothy floor and Wren's small hand fisted in her hair, and for a long moment she did not move at all.

Anger is a familiar garment. She had worn it for years, in the early ones—anger at the valley for its deafness, at the crows for their empty beaks, at the whole mute arrangement of her life. Later, when anger wore thin, she had patched it with philosophy: *the ward protects me; the silence keeps me safe; safety is enough.* She had built a life the way you build a shelter in a storm, low and tight and defensible, and told herself the low ceiling was a choice.

But underneath, all along, in the cellar of her heart where she kept the things she didn't count, there had been a colder question, and now it came out into the firelight wearing its true face:

*She put me in a cage. She locked the cage with her own body. And she never told me.*

Thirty years. Thirty years of herb-trading and goat-tending and practicing forbidden words to a audience of one unimpressed goat, and her mother had watched her do it—had *watched*—and said nothing. Had hummed her tuneless hum and dried her yarrow and let her daughter believe the silence was simply weather, simply the way the world was, rather than a wall her own blood was holding up with her spine.

"She could have told me," Zephyrine said aloud, and her voice cracked on it. "One time. Once. *Once.*"

Wren stirred but did not wake.

The fire ticked. Outside, real wind moved through the birches, and somewhere far off an owl asked its one question of the dark and, for once, Zephyrine envied it—envied it its single question, its single answer, its uncomplicated relationship with the night.

Because here was the other thing the stone had shown her, the thing she was trying very hard not to look at directly, the way you don't look directly at a candle you're trying to blow out:

Her mother had died holding the ward.

Not peacefully, not in her bed with her hands folded. Zephyrine remembered the ending differently—remembered a winter illness, a slow fading, a woman who grew quieter and quieter until the quiet swallowed her. She had assumed grief, or age, or the ordinary treachery of bodies.

But the stone showed her the ledger. Every day of anchoring was a withdrawal. Holding a ward shut around a speaker-child, smothering a living gift for thirty years, was like holding a door closed against a flood—except the flood was made of love, and the door was made of yourself. Her mother hadn't faded. She had been *spent*. Poured out, hour by hour, into the ground beneath a dry well, so that her daughter could grow up alive, unmolested, unlisted, free to be lonely.

Free to be lonely. That was the inheritance. That was the whole estate.

"Oh, Mama," Zephyrine said, and it came out small, the way it hadn't come out since she was very young indeed.

The anger didn't vanish. Anger at the dead rarely does; it just changes jobs. But standing there in the ruined bothy with frost feathering the window-glass in patterns that were almost—*almost*—letters, Zephyrine felt it soften the way a knot softens in warm water. Not undone. Loosening. Making room for the other thing underneath it, which was this:

Her mother had been given an impossible choice and had made it without hesitation, and had then lived inside the cost of it for three decades without once asking for credit, thanks, or rescue. She had done quietly, invisibly, thanklessly, precisely the sort of thing Zephyrine had spent her whole life believing herself brave for merely enduring.

*I stayed because I was afraid,* whispered a thought from somewhere deeper than thought. *She stayed because I needed her to.*

And the difference between those two sentences was going to take more than one night by a small fire to understand.

* * *

Near dawn, Wren woke, looked at her, and frowned.

The child crawled over and pressed one palm flat against Zephyrine's sternum, the way they'd pressed it to the oath-stone in the flooded shrine, and cocked their head. Listening. Whatever Wren heard there made the frown deepen into something older than eight years old.

Then the child did something new. They took Zephyrine's hand, opened it, and traced in her palm—not spirals this time, not migration-song, but a figure Zephyrine recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water:

A woman kneeling. One hand flat against the ground.

"You saw it too," Zephyrine whispered. "In the shrine. Before I did. You've known since—"

Wren nodded once. Then, with great care, they drew another figure beside the first: the kneeling woman again, but this time with lines radiating out from her pressed hand, down into the earth, and the lines did not stop. They ran under the snow, under the hills, south and east and west, faint but unbroken, connecting to—

To other figures. Small marks. Stones.

And to one mark drawn larger than all the rest, at the center of everything, wrapped around like a nest around an egg.

Around a valley. Around Hollowmere.

"No," Zephyrine said. Her mouth had gone dry. "No, the anchor died with her. I stood at the well. I *saw* the crack—"

Wren tapped the large mark. Tapped Zephyrine's chest. Back to the mark. Back to the chest.

"That's not how it works," Zephyrine said, and heard her own voice climb. "Anchors are bound. Bound is bound. You don't inherit a ward like a—" Like a coat. Like a debt. Like a gift with no witness, passed mother to daughter down a line of women who paid for their daughters in silence. "*Like a curse.*"

But even as she said it, she was remembering things. Filing them, helplessly, in the drawer that never closed: how the valley's quiet had always felt to her less like absence than like attention. How animals near her went calm in ways they didn't near others, even muted. How the ward-stone under the well had cracked the same season she'd begun, for the first time in her life, to think about leaving.

How the crows had started remembering words the moment she packed her bag.

Outside, the sky was going the grey of old pewter. The wind had shifted in the night, and now it came down from the north carrying a smell Zephyrine knew from her herb-craft and liked least of all her professional acquaintances: the clean, metallic scent of deep cold. Not weather-cold. *Advancing* cold. The long winter, walking.

And threaded through that wind, faint, at the very edge of hearing—at the edge of whatever her hearing was becoming—came something else.

A voice. Low, rough, harmonic, many-throated. Not human. Not bird. Deeper than either, older than either, moving through the trees below the bothy with the particular deliberation of creatures that are not hunting and want you to know it.

Wren's head came up. Their eyes caught the firelight and held it, and for the first time since the shrine, the child looked afraid—not of the voice, Zephyrine realized. For it.

Wren tugged her sleeve and signed, quick and urgent: *last word. they ask. last word?*

Zephyrine rose, pulled back the bothy's rotten shutter, and looked out.

Between the white trunks of the birches, grey shapes stood in the snow. A dozen of them. More. Still as carvings, breath steaming, eyes reflecting the dying fire like a scatter of green coins—and at their head, a she-wolf with a scarred muzzle and frost in her ruff, watching the bothy with an expression Zephyrine had seen before, long ago, on her mother's face at the market: the look of someone who has come a very long way to ask a question they dread the answer to.

The pack-leader lifted her head, and the old speech rolled out of her, vast and wintered, and Zephyrine understood every word of it as though it were her mother tongue—which, she supposed, it was.

"*Little speaker,*" the wolf said. "*We have crossed two dead rivers to find you. Answer carefully, for our pups are hungry and our patience has been eaten by the winter. Is the child the last word? And will you speak for it?*"

Wren's hand found hers and gripped hard.

Zephyrine stepped out into the snow, unarmed, unbargained, and very much alone—and discovered that for once in her life, hiding did not even occur to her.
