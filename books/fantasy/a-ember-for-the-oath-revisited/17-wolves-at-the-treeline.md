## 17. Wolves at the Treeline

The wolves arrived on the second morning out of Ashmoor's foothills, and they announced themselves in the politest possible way: by not announcing themselves at all.

Zephyrine only noticed because Wren stopped mid-step on the frozen track, tilted their head the way a weathervane tilts when the wind changes, and said—still halting, still wondrous over every human word—"Smell."

"Good morning to you too," Zephyrine said. "Yes, I bathed in the creek. Once. It was November. I stand by my choices."

Wren shook their head with the patient exasperation of a teacher whose student has failed a very simple lesson, and pointed back the way they'd come, toward the dark line of pines that had been keeping pace with them on the eastern slope like a hedge that had learned to walk.

Zephyrine looked. She saw nothing, which was itself an answer. In Hollowmere she had spent thirty years learning what absence looked like—the shape of a thing holding its breath—and the treeline was holding six or seven large breaths at once.

"Wolves," she said.

"Pack," Wren corrected, which was somehow worse.

* * *

They traveled all that day under escort, though neither of them spoke of it as escorting. The grey shapes flickered between the trunks at the corner of vision: never closer than forty paces, never farther than sixty, maintaining their distance with the discipline of soldiers who have been told not to touch the merchandise. Once, when Zephyrine paused to retie her bootlace, the entire treeline paused with her. When she started walking again, it did too.

"It's flattering, really," she said to Wren, pitching her voice light, because Wren had gone quiet in the way that meant frightened. "I've never had an audience this attentive. Bramble used to just walk away halfway through my sentences."

"Bramble is wise," Wren said solemnly.

"Everyone's a critic."

She kept her eyes forward and her hands loose and her mind closed, which was the hardest part. Beast-speech was not exactly speech—it was more a door left ajar, a willingness to be smelled and heard and known—and every instinct she had was screaming to open that door and ask the shadows what they wanted. But you didn't knock on a wolf's door uninvited. You let the wolf do the knocking, or you spent a very long time being something a wolf had once decided about.

So she walked, and the pack walked, and the afternoon came down silver and cold, and by the time they made camp in the lee of a fallen boulder, her shoulders had knotted themselves into a shape she suspected a physician would charge money to describe.

* * *

The second day, the wolves grew bored with subtlety.

They began appearing in ones and twos on ridgelines above the track, silhouetted against the pale sky, watching openly now. A big female sat herself down not twenty paces off while Wren ate their noon bread, close enough that Zephyrine could see the frost on her ruff and the yellow patience of her eyes. Wren, instead of being afraid, waved. Actually waved, the whole hand, fingers spread, like a child greeting a neighbor across a lane.

"Do not wave at the wolf," Zephyrine hissed.

The wolf tipped her head, considering the wave.

"She says hello," Wren reported.

"I know what she says. I can feel her saying it. That's rather the problem." Zephyrine pulled her cloak tighter. "Wren, listen to me. Out here, past the ward, everything that speaks the old speech also speaks to everything else. If I open so much as a crack, that pack hears it, and every pack within ten miles hears it, and then we're not a woman and a child on a road anymore. We're a story."

Wren considered this. Then they signed something quick and fluid—not beast-speech proper but the gesture-language they fell back on sometimes, hands speaking faster than their new human mouth could learn.

*Then be a good story,* they signed.

Zephyrine laughed despite herself, a short white puff in the cold air, and the watching female's ears pricked forward at the sound as if filing it away for later.

"That," Zephyrine told her, pointing a gloved finger, "is exactly the kind of encouragement I don't need."

* * *

That night the wolves sang.

It began after full dark, when their little fire had burned down to a red fist and Wren was asleep against Zephyrine's side, breathing in the slow deep rhythm of a child who trusted the world again, at least in a twelve-foot radius. The first voice rose from the north—a long, low note that seemed to come out of the ground itself—and then another answered from the east, and then the whole night opened its throat.

Zephyrine had not heard wolves sing in thirty years. Under the ward, sound had been flat, muffled, wrapped in wool; even memory had sounded muffled. Here, the song went through her like water through a net, catching on everything, taking nothing. And woven through the howling, unmistakable to anyone with her ears, ran words.

Old words. Old speech. Not random music at all, but a conversation, a roll call, a counting of the living against the dying year.

Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her knees and made herself breathe, and beside her Wren slept on, and the fire spat, and somewhere out in the dark, seven wolves were singing about them.

Because they were about them. She could hear it. The song kept circling back to one phrase, passed from throat to throat like an ember, and the phrase was a question:

*Is it true? Is it true? Is the last word walking?*

* * *

They came into the camp at dawn.

Not charging, not slinking—walking. Seven of them, out of the greying light, moving with the unhurried confidence of creatures who have decided something and are merely going through the forms. The big female led. Behind her came a scarred male with one ear, two younger wolves who kept bumping each other's shoulders like siblings, and three more hanging back at the edge of the firelight, all ribs and curiosity.

Wren woke instantly, the way they always did—no groggy climbing up out of sleep for the seal-child, just open eyes—and sat up, and was not afraid. Zephyrine felt their calm arrive through the place where their shoulders touched her arm, a warmth like a stone from the hearth, and she was ashamed to find how much she needed it.

The pack-leader stopped at the fire's edge. Up close she was larger than Zephyrine had judged, grey going white at the muzzle, with eyes the color of old amber and an old amber stillness behind them. Frost beaded her whiskers. She looked at Wren first, long and thorough, the way a scholar reads a difficult page. Then she looked at Zephyrine, and Zephyrine felt the question before it was asked, felt it press against the closed door of her mind like a nose against a gate.

There was no help for it now. There never had been, probably, since the moment Wren had appeared barefoot in the snow.

Zephyrine opened the door.

The old speech rushed in—not sound, exactly, but sense, the way meaning arrives before the words that carry it. Thirty years of disuse, and it fit her like her own hands. She hadn't realized until this moment how much of her had been sitting in a dark room waiting.

*You sing questions about us,* she said to the pack-leader. *I will answer them, if your pack permits.*

A ripple went through the younger wolves—excitement, quickly hushed. The scarred male's lip lifted a fraction off his teeth, not quite a snarl, not quite a smile. The leader silenced them all without looking, simply by continuing to exist with authority.

*A speaker,* the leader said, and her mental voice was dry as autumn grass, worn smooth by use. *In the low country. We thought the low country had eaten its last.* Her nostrils flared. *You smell of goats and hiding. You smell of a valley where nothing answers.*

"That valley raised me," Zephyrine said aloud, forgetting herself, and the absurdity of speaking English at a wolf made the youngest wolf's ears swivel in frank bewilderment.

*Your voice works either way,* the leader observed. *Convenient. Now. The question we sang, woman-of-goats-and-hiding, and mind you answer plainly, because my pack has run two days beside you on the strength of a rumor, and rumors have teeth that bite the rumor-monger.* She stepped forward into the full firelight, and her amber gaze swung to Wren, and every scrap of dryness in her went still and grave.

*The child. Is it the last word?*

Zephyrine understood what was being asked—or she understood the weight of it, which may be the same thing at these depths. Somewhere beyond the firelight, beyond the hills, the covenant was failing stone by stone, and the wild places had sent out their question like a lantern into fog, and here, impossibly, the fog had answered back with eight small feet and a taste for bread and honey.

She could feel Wren beside her, waiting, trusting her to say whatever came next. She could feel the whole pack listening, seven hearts beating in the dark like a drum she was standing on.

And underneath all of it, older than both, was fear—her own, familiar as a hand-me-down coat. Because vouching was a kind of vow. Because the last time she had spoken for someone in the old speech, she had been nine years old and her mother's face had been the last thing she saw through a closing door. Because everything she had ever vouched for had been taken into the silence afterward, and she had learned, deep and wordless, that her voice was a thing that got people buried.

The fire cracked. A log settled. Somewhere far off, ice shifted on the river with a sound like a sigh.

*Yes,* Zephyrine said.

Not aloud. In the old speech, plain and full, with the door of her thrown wide for the first time in thirty years—every wolf on the hillside hearing her, every hawk asleep in the pines, the river, if rivers listened, and she was no longer certain they didn't.

*Yes. This is Wren, and Wren is the last word. I have carried them from the dead valley, and I will carry them to the stones. I vouch for them with my voice, and my voice has been silent a long time, so it is heavy, and I am spending it all at once.*

Silence followed—real silence, the held-breath kind, and Zephyrine sat in it feeling flayed and lighter than she had in decades.

Then the pack-leader did something Zephyrine had read about in her mother's stories and never once believed: she lowered her head, all the way down, until her frost-grey brow nearly touched the frozen ground, and held it there. One heartbeat. Two. Behind her, one by one, six wolves did the same, the young ones clumsy and the scarred male last and slowest, as if the gesture cost him something he was nonetheless willing to pay.

*Rise, then,* the leader said, lifting her head, and there was something almost wry beneath the gravity now. *Both of you. The last word should not be sleeping on stones with nothing but a small fire and a woman who smells of guilt and dried mint.*

"The mint is medicinal," Zephyrine said.

*Of course it is.*

The pack arranged itself around the camp without asking permission—two at the dark side where the trees began, the big female settling between Wren and the open ground like a wall that breathed. Wren, delighted beyond all decorum, reached out and very gently touched the scarred male's paw. He looked at the small hand on his leg with the expression of an uncle enduring an affectionate niece, and did not move away.

Zephyrine sat back against the boulder and let out a breath she suspected she'd been holding since the well in Hollowmere.

Sleep came for her fast and strange. But just before it took her, the pack-leader's voice arrived once more at the edge of her mind, quieter now, almost private—the tone of someone about to stop being a sentinel and start being a witness.

*You vouched well, speaker. Tomorrow we will show you why you were right to. Tomorrow we will show you what the winter is doing to the world—and then you will understand what walks behind you, and why my pack is afraid.*

And then, as the fire died and the wolves' warmth closed around them like a second cloak, she added the part that followed Zephyrine down into sleep and waited for her there:

*We are not the only ones following, woman-of-goats-and-hiding. Something else picked up your trail two nights ago. It does not sing.*
