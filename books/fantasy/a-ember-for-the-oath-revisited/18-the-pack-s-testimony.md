## 18. The Pack's Testimony

*What follows is not my telling. It is Old Sorrow's — the grey she-wolf who leads the pack that walked us out of the high forest — and I have set it down as faithfully as my tongue allows, which is to say imperfectly, because wolves do not think in words and I have had to pour their meaning into human vessels that crack a little at the seams. Where I have added anything of my own, Wren has corrected me. The child sits with her ear against the wolf's ribs when she listens, as if the truth lives better close to the heart.*

* * *

I am called Sorrow by your kind, though the name is older than sorrow and means something closer to *the one who walks behind*, because in a pack someone must guard the rear, and I have done it for nine winters, and before me there was another, and before her another, back to the first winter of the first forgetting.

You want to know what is happening to the wild places. You want it told plainly, the way you tell each other things, one mouth speaking while many ears wait. We do not do this. We have never done this. A wolf learns what a wolf learns by smelling it, and the knowledge goes into the blood, and the blood tells the pups. But the blood is failing now too, and so I will do the shameful thing. I will speak like a woman at a well. I will tell you everything, in order, even the parts that frighten you.

Sit down. Not on the stone — the stone is listening, and it has forgotten how to be flattered.

We scented you two days before you saw us. This should not surprise you; you walk like a wounded deer and smell like goat. Your goat especially. That animal smells like a barn that has been argued with.

The child laughed at this. Good. Laughing is also a kind of remembering.

Here is what we know, from the north, where the winter does not end even in summer now.

There is a river we call the Quick Mother — you would have another name for it, some name with a saint in it, or a king. She runs out of the high ice and down through the birch country, and every living thing within a day's walk drinks from her. Two summers ago, she began to forget.

Understand what forgetting means, when it happens to a river. It is not that she dries. Drying would be honest. It is that she forgets *how*. Water still comes down from the ice, but it moves wrong — pooling where it should run, running where it should pool, doubling back on itself in loops that go nowhere. Fish swim up her length and arrive where they began, again and again, until they stop trying and simply float, facing downstream, waiting for an answer that used to live in the current. Herons stand in the shallows for hours without striking, because the fish no longer make the small mistake every hunter depends on. Everything in her has become patient, and patience, in a river, is death wearing a calm face.

My sister's mate drank from the Quick Mother last spring. Wolves can drink from any water; we are made stubborn that way. But he came back changed. Not sick. *Simplified.* He could still hunt, still run, still sing. But he had lost his grudge against the ravens — and a wolf who loses his grudge against the ravens has lost something structural, if you understand me. He was kinder afterward. He died in autumn, gently, in his sleep, and none of us could remember at the graveside why we had loved him specifically rather than generally, and that frightened us more than his dying did.

That is what the long winter does. It does not kill. It *subtracts*. It takes the specific out of the world and leaves the general, and the general cannot hold a territory or raise a litter or mourn properly. The general just persists, grayly, forever, which is the same as forever being dead but quieter about it.

The forests are worse. Do not look at me like that, herb-woman; I know you love trees. That is exactly why I am telling you this part slowly.

A forest knows its own name. Every tree in it shares one long slow thought, and the thought is the forest's name, and the name is what holds the roots to the soil and the branches to the sky and the moss to the north side of everything. The birchwood north of the Quick Mother has begun losing its name. The trees still stand. They photosynthesize. By your measures they are healthy. But walk into that wood and listen — really listen, the way the child listens — and you will hear nothing coming back. A named wood hums. It answers footsteps. It remembers every fire and every storm and every wolf that ever pissed against its trunks, and it gossips about all of it, endlessly, smugly, to itself.

This wood has gone quiet. And last month, the trees began to lean. All of them, very slightly, toward the south, like a crowd turning to watch something leave. Because something *is* leaving. The name is going somewhere, and the trees feel it go, and they cannot follow it, because roots, and so they lean after it the way old dogs lean after masters who have decided not to come back.

The child put her hand flat on the ground when I said this. She does that. She says the ground still remembers most things, longer than air or water. She says the ground is where names go to hide. I hope she is right. I have stopped hoping in directions I cannot verify.

Now the part you actually asked about, though you were too polite to ask it directly, the way humans circle a wound.

The seal-children. There were nine. You know this already — the goat told you, and goats are terrible gossips but accurate bookkeepers. What the goat did not tell you, because the goat does not know, is what happened to the other eight.

We found the first. In the ash-country east of here, where a shrine stood — you would call it a shrine; we called it a Smelling Place, because the stones there held the scent of every oath ever sworn, layered like sediment, and young wolves went there to learn history through the nose. The stone was shattered. Not broken — shattered, deliberately, with iron and hammers, by men in grey. And in the rubble, small bones. A child's bones, folded neatly, the way a sleeping child folds. The bones had been there a long time. The snow had kept them.

Eight shrines. Eight stones. Eight children, sent out across the years by whatever old power still remembered its duty, each one carrying a piece of the covenant in their body the way a lantern carries fire. Each one arriving at their stone to find it destroyed, and sitting down beside the ruin, and staying. Because that is what a seal does. A seal seals what remains, even if what remains is nothing. Even if what remains is only themselves.

They did not die of cold or hunger. We smelled the truth of it in the bones' surroundings. They died of *completion*. When the last fragment of a covenant's stone fails, the seal-child pours themselves into the gap — all of them, their whole small self, their name and their memory and their particular way of laughing — to hold the door shut a little longer. It buys time. It bought eight small eternities of time, scattered across the wild places, and each eternity is ending now, one by one, like lamps going out along a road.

Your child is the ninth. The last lamp. And she is walking toward the final stone instead of away from it, which is either great courage or great foolishness, and in my experience those two smells are identical.

Wren tugged my sleeve here and made me add something. She says: *they weren't sad at the end. The dream isn't sad. They were glad to be useful.* I am reporting this under protest, because I believe in preparing people for grief, and the child believes in correcting the record. Both instincts are correct. Hold them together; that is what an oath is.

One more thing, and then I will let you sleep, because you smell exhausted and your hands shake when you think I am not watching.

The Quick Mother is not the worst of it. West of the birchwood there is a marsh that has forgotten it was ever a marsh. It lies flat and brown and still, and nothing lives in it, and here is the detail that keeps the whole pack awake on bad nights: it has forgotten it was ever *anything*. Not marsh, not meadow, not lake. Just surface. The land there is waiting to be told what it is, and no one is left to tell it, and so it waits, and the waiting spreads outward a little more each year, the way frost spreads through a wall.

That is the long winter. Not cold. Not dark. *Waiting.* A world holding its breath until midwinter, and if no one speaks the word that tells it what it is —

Well. You are carrying the word. Walk faster.

* * *

Old Sorrow stopped there, and lay down with her chin on her paws, and looked at the fire with the expression wolves wear when they have said more than is comfortable and would prefer never to discuss it again.

I sat a long while with the translation settling in me like silt in shaken water. Wren had fallen asleep against the she-wolf's flank, one hand still buried in the grey fur, breathing in the rhythm of the pack around us — six wolves, arranged in a loose ring, all of them pretending to sleep and none of them fooling anyone.

Bramble limped over from the edge of the camp, where he had spent the evening pointedly grazing, as though associating too closely with wolves might reflect poorly on him.

"You heard," I said quietly, in the beast-speech.

"I hear everything. It's my curse." The old goat chewed thoughtfully. "The she-wolf left one thing out."

"Which was?"

"Which was that the grey men aren't following the stones." Bramble nodded toward the dark southern horizon, where the ridgeline cut black against the stars. "They're following *you*. Have been since the hawk. A clever hunter stops chasing the trail and starts chasing the walker." He turned his yellow eyes on me. "And there's a man-shaped smell on the wind from the valley, herb-woman. Faint. Familiar. Someone small and careful and counting."

"Tobias Grell," I said.

"If it's the one who counts, then yes." Bramble settled himself onto the cold ground with the groan of the elderly. "Sleep fast, Zephyrine. The wolves say the land is forgetting. In my experience, men like that never forget anything — and they always sell what they remember."

Out in the dark, far to the south, something moved along the ridgeline. Small. Patient. Counting.
