## 19. Grell Follows

Tobias Grell had always believed himself a careful man. It was one of the few beliefs he'd managed to keep polished and unchipped through forty-odd years of farming the stingiest soil in Hollowmere. Careful men counted their seed twice before planting. Careful men watered their fence posts so the wood swelled tight against pry-bars. Careful men noticed things.

And Tobias had noticed.

He'd noticed, first, that Zephyrine Winterbourne—the herb-woman up past the old mill, the one who'd lived in the valley longer than anyone could rightly remember her arriving—had bought double portions at market. Flour enough for two mouths. Salted pork for two. A bundle of wool socks in a size that fit no adult he knew of. And snow-melt salts, a whole crate of them, when she kept no road and cleared no path.

He'd noticed that she paid without haggling, which was so unlike her that it was nearly frightening, like watching the sun rise in the west out of sheer contrariness.

Mostly, though, he'd noticed the child. Or rather—he'd noticed *not* seeing the child. Zephyrine's chimney smoked warm on cold mornings, and smoke like that meant a fire tended all night, and a fire tended all night meant someone to keep warm, and Zephyrine Winterbourne had been alone as long as the valley had been forgetting itself. Tobias did sums the way other men said prayers: quietly, constantly, and with a firm conviction that they added up to something true.

So he wrote it down. In his ledger, in the back pages where he tracked debts, he wrote: *W. buys for two. Small boots? Watch.*

Tobias told himself this was prudence. Neighbors watched out for neighbors. That was all.

It was not all, and he knew it wasn't all, because there was also the matter of the goats. Zephyrine sold cheese—good cheese, honestly good—and Tobias had long suspected there was more than milk in those hills. Old stories said the Winterbourne woman knew things. Where lost lambs went. Which ewes would twin. Once, years ago, his brother Alder had sworn blind that a crow had told him the price of barley would fall, and it had fallen, and Alder had laughed about it for a season until the Oathwardens came through asking their quiet questions and Alder had gone pale as whey and never mentioned crows again.

But if there was something *in* those hills—if the silence up around Zephyrine's place had a crack in it, the way a dam has a crack—then knowing where the crack was might be worth more than any harvest Tobias would ever grow. Not to sell, necessarily. Just to know. Just to have it, folded small, like a banknote kept in a boot.

That was how he explained it to himself, anyway, on the grey morning when he strapped on his good boots, told his wife Marta he'd be checking the high pasture fences, and set off along Zephyrine's trail with a day's bread, a skin of cider, and his ledger in his coat pocket like a talisman.

Marta had looked at him a long moment before he left. Marta missed very little herself.

"Fences," she'd said.

"High pasture fences."

"In this weather." It hadn't quite been a question. It had been the shape of a question with the curiosity scooped out of it, which was how Marta expressed disapproval: you were left to fill in the rest yourself, and filling it in yourself always stung worse.

"I'll be careful," Tobias had said, which was, at least, honest as far as it went.

* * *

The trail was easy to follow, which should have worried him more than it did. Fresh snow two nights past, then a thaw, then a hard freeze—perfect track-catching weather, and Zephyrine traveled like a woman who had stopped caring who saw her prints. That alone unsettled him. Thirty years of being nobody, and suddenly nobody was leaving footprints big enough to read from horseback.

He found the cold camp by the second afternoon. Ash raked flat—but not well, not with a traveler's care, just a farmer's quick smear. A patch of pressed grass where two had slept. Small boot-marks beside large ones, and the small ones odd somehow, too far apart in the toes, like the walker didn't quite trust the ground.

Tobias crouched over the little prints for a long time, chewing the inside of his cheek.

"Child," he murmured. "She's got herself a child, and she's running."

Running where? The valley only had the one way out worth calling a way, east along the river cut, and everyone knew what waited beyond the ward-line. Everyone knew, in the vague and unexamined way that people know the sun will come up and taxes will come due: the world past Hollowmere was loud, and loud places had Wardens, and Wardens asked questions that ended careers and sometimes lives.

A sensible man would have turned around here. Tobias stood there a while, weighing sensible against the other thing, the thing in him that had spent forty years counting other men's luck from the outside of a fence.

Then he kept walking.

"Just to see," he told the empty hillside, and the hillside, tactfully, said nothing.

* * *

He caught up to them on the third morning, or near enough—close enough to see without being seen, which for Tobias was the natural order of the universe anyway.

They were in the treeline above the river meadows, and he nearly walked into the scene before he understood what he was walking into. He froze behind a deadfall, breath held, heart doing something undignified.

Zephyrine stood in a clearing with the child—yes, a child, eight years old maybe, wrapped in an oversized coat, hair the color of wet bark—and around them, at the edge of the trees, stood wolves.

Five of them. Big ones, winter-thick, standing calm as cattle at a trough.

Tobias's first thought was that he should run. His second thought was that running from wolves was famously the wrong move, though every instinct he owned was filing objections. His third thought arrived a moment later, and it stopped everything else dead:

Zephyrine was talking to them.

Not shooing. Not shouting. Talking—a low, rolling speech full of clicks and rising notes and sounds that weren't sounds a human throat ought to make, and the wolves were *answering*. The big one in front, scarred across the muzzle, tipped its head back and loosed a call that wasn't quite a howl, and Zephyrine answered *it*, and the two of them went back and forth like neighbors leaning on a gate, and Tobias Grell, careful man, careful keeper of ledgers, felt the whole careful architecture of his understanding tilt sideways and pour out through some crack he hadn't known was there.

Because it wasn't tricks. He knew tricks; he'd seen peddlers, charlatans, a man at Ferrow's Cross once who made a mule count to six with hand-signals. This was none of that. This was conversation. You couldn't fake the way the wolf listened—not performing listening, not waiting-for-the-treat listening, but real attention, ears working, eyes soft. You couldn't fake the way the child reached out and touched the scarred one's nose like they were old friends at midsummer fair.

The wolf spoke again, longer this time, a low urgent thread of sound, and Zephyrine's face changed. Tobias watched her go still, watched her look down at the child, watched something pass over her expression that he recognized despite the strangeness of everything else—because it was worry. Plain, human, neighborly worry, the kind Marta wore when the frost came early.

Then the scarred wolf lifted its head and looked directly at the deadfall where Tobias was hiding.

Tobias would swear to his grave, and later nearly did swear it aloud to people who didn't want to hear it, that the animal looked at him *knowingly*. One golden eye. A slow blink. Almost, he thought afterward in the bad hours, almost courteous. Like a landlord finding a squatter.

Zephyrine followed the wolf's gaze.

For one heartbeat—one long, ringing heartbeat—Tobias looked at Zephyrine Winterbourne across thirty years of never-having-really-looked-at-her, and she looked back, and there was nothing hidden in her face anymore. No careful blankness. No valley-polite nod. She looked tired, and fierce, and sad for him specifically, the way you're sad for a man standing in a burning barn who won't drop the sack he's carrying.

"Tobias Grell," she said, in ordinary words, in a voice pitched to carry. "Go home."

And that—that was the worst part, he realized later. She'd known his name all along. Thirty years of trading cheese and herbs, of nods at market, of "morning, Mistress Winterbourne," and she'd known exactly who he was, and he had thought of her as scenery.

The wolf took one step toward him. Not threatening. Just one step, weight settling forward, the way weather settles before it breaks.

Tobias Grell dropped his bread, dropped his dignity, and ran.

* * *

He ran badly. He knew that even as he did it—crashing downslope instead of contouring, snagging his coat, losing a boot briefly to a frozen rut—but fear does not consult craft. He ran until the woods spat him out onto the old drover's track along the river, and then he ran along that, gasping, cider-skin slapping his hip, ledger clutched against his ribs like a shield against arrows.

He ran for what felt like the length of a psalm and the width of a parish.

Which is why he did not, at first, register the horses.

There were four of them, grey-cloaked riders drawn up across the track ahead, and the lead rider sat her mount with the particular stillness of someone who has been waiting. Tobias skidded, slipped, sat down hard in the frozen mud of the track, and looked up into the face of a woman with iron-grey hair pulled back severe and eyes like the bottom of a well.

Serah Vane. He knew her by reputation the way everyone in three valleys knew her by reputation. The Warden commander who had cleared the trouble out of Marlow's Ford. Who asked questions softly. Who got answers completely.

"Well," said Serah Vane, in a voice mild as milk. "A local man."

Tobias opened his mouth. What emerged was: "I was checking fences."

Vane tilted her head slightly. Behind her, one of the riders dismounted and began, unhurried, to walk a circle around Tobias, looking at the ground, at his tracks coming down from the treeline—from the *wrong side* of the treeline, from country no fence-checker had business in.

"Fences," Vane repeated.

"High pasture," said Tobias, whose mouth had apparently decided to handle this situation without consulting him further.

Vane let the silence sit. She was good at silence, Tobias realized dimly, from the mud. She wore it the way Zephyrine must have worn hers all these years—except Vane's silence wasn't hiding anything. It was a net, and she was holding it open, and she was perfectly content to wait for him to swim into it.

He swam into it.

"There's—" His voice cracked, and he hated it, and kept going anyway, because the alternative was whatever happened to people who stayed silent around Serah Vane. "There's a woman. Winterbourne. From Hollowmere valley. She's traveling with a child, and she talks to animals. To *wolves*. I saw it. Wolves, ma'am, a whole—there were five, and they answered her, actual words, not—"

He heard himself and shut his eyes. Every word was a stone off his chest and a rope around his neck at the same time, and both feelings arrived together, married, inseparable, forever.

When he opened his eyes again, Vane had produced a small book of her own. Her handwriting, when she wrote, was neat and unhurried.

"Winterbourne," she said. "Of Hollowmere."

"Yes."

"The valley ward is failing," she said—not asking, just placing the fact on the track between them like a coin. "We've been sent to find the cause. An irregularity in the silence." She looked up. "You've given me a direction, Master Grell. Directions are useful. Descriptions are more useful. The child."

"A girl. Or—I don't know. Eight, maybe. Barefoot in snow, when she came, Marta said—no, I said, I saw the boots, small boots, she bought small boots at market—" He was babbling now and could not stop, the words coming up like floodwater. "Eyes that catch the light strange. Deer-like. She doesn't speak. Not our tongue, anyhow."

Vane wrote. The quill scratched, small and precise.

"And you followed them," she said. "Why?"

And there it was. The question underneath the questions, the one Tobias had been outrunning for three days and outrunning all his life. Why did a careful man leave his fences and follow a neighbor into the wilds with a ledger in his pocket?

"Greed," said Tobias Grell, because lying to Serah Vane seemed suddenly, catastrophically like the one mistake worse than all the others. "Curiosity. I thought—whatever she had, I wanted to know where it was. I wanted it written down. In case it was worth something." He swallowed. "In case I was worth something, having it."

Something moved behind Vane's well-bottom eyes. Not warmth. Recognition, maybe—the flat acknowledgment of a species she'd catalogued before.

"You'll come with us," she said. "You'll show us the clearing where you saw this. And when we've finished with the woman and the child, Master Grell, you'll be free to go home to your fences."

It was not a promise. Tobias understood that. It was shaped like a promise the way a trap is shaped like shelter.

Still, he climbed onto the remount they offered him, because the alternative was standing alone in the middle of a frozen track with wolves somewhere behind him and no story at all in front of him. He clutched his ledger. One of the riders glanced at it and he hugged it tighter, absurdly, as though it were a child of his own.

As the patrol wheeled east, toward the treeline, toward the clearing, toward the woman who had said *go home* in a voice full of a sadness he still hadn't finished unpacking, Tobias looked back once, the way cowards do—over his shoulder, at the valley he'd traded.

The wind had picked up. Far off along the ridgeline, snow was moving against the sky's grain, wrong-ways, deliberate, and beneath the wind—faint, unmistakable, impossible—came a sound like many voices singing under the ice.

Tobias Grell decided he had imagined it.

He was a careful man. He was very good at deciding things.

Behind them, the land began, quietly, to remember.
