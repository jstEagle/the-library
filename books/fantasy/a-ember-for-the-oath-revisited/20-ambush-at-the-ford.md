## 20. Ambush at the Ford

The river they called the Liss, according to a crooked signpost that had lost an argument with the weather, ran fast and cold with snowmelt, and it was the color of a wolf's eye—pale grey-green with something watching underneath. Zephyrine studied it the way she studied anything new: with suspicion first and wonder second. Thirty years in Hollowmere had taught her that wonder was safe only after suspicion had done its rounds.

"Too deep to walk," she said. "We'll have to find the ford."

Wren, riding double behind her on the shaggy borrowed pony, pointed downstream without hesitation. The child didn't point like other children pointed—at whatever was nearest and shiniest. Wren pointed the way a compass settles. North of them, through a stand of birches gone bone-white in the winter light, the water flattened and widened over gravel. A crossing. Of course Wren knew. Half the time Zephyrine suspected the child could hear rivers the way Zephyrine heard goats: not as noise but as talk.

*The river says come,* Wren signed, hands quick under their shared cloak. They had developed a language of gestures between them over the weeks of road, half invented, half remembered from some older grammar neither of them could name. *It is not angry today. Only loud.*

"Rivers are always loud," Zephyrine said. "That's how they get away with everything."

Wren laughed—a sound like a pebble dropped into still water, small and surprising—and Zephyrine felt the familiar ache-and-warmth combination that had become her constant companion since the night the child appeared barefoot at her door. She was getting used to being needed. That frightened her more than the wardens did, if she was honest, which she tried very hard not to be.

The pony picked its way down the bank. Ice laced the edges of the shallows like lace someone's grandmother had left out on a windowsill. On the far side, the land climbed into pine dark, and beyond that, two days' ride away, slept the second oath-stone, in a shrine Maren had described as "flooded, cursed, and full of eels, but otherwise perfectly pleasant."

Zephyrine dismounted at the water's edge and let the pony drink. Her breath hung around her in clouds. She was forty-nine years old, she realized suddenly, absurdly, standing in a strange river valley far from home; she had spent thirty of those years invisible, and now here she was, wanted on two warrants she'd never read, carrying a child who was the last living word of a dying covenant, and all she could think about was that she'd forgotten to pack any of the good willow bark tea.

"You're smiling," Wren observed, signing one-handed while stealing a strip of dried apple from her saddlebag with the other.

"I'm panicking."

*Same thing,* Wren signed, and ate the apple.

That was when Bramble—the goat, who had insisted on coming, and whom no force in creation could have stopped from coming—lifted her head from where she'd been pretending to graze, and said, in the old speech that came through Zephyrine's mind like a voice heard through a wall:

*Horse-meat and iron on the wind. Behind us. Many.*

Zephyrine went still. The cold seemed to rearrange itself around her spine.

"How many?"

*More than you have fingers. Less than the stars. Coming slow, because they believe you are stupid.*

"Charming," Zephyrine murmured. She turned, scanning the birch stand behind them, the ridge above it. For a moment she saw nothing but white trunks and shadow. Then—a flicker. Grey cloak against grey bark. The particular stillness of a person trying very hard to be scenery.

"Wren." She kept her voice level, the way she'd once kept her voice level talking to tax collectors. "Behind us. Don't turn around. Get on the pony."

Wren turned around, of course. They were eight. Then they were on the pony, small hands knotted in its mane, eyes wide and reflecting the winter light like a deer's, like the first day.

*They have found the crossing,* Bramble said, unbothered, chewing. *They are waiting for you to be halfway through the water, because that is when everyone is worst at everything.*

"That's tactical thinking I don't enjoy."

*It is good thinking. I would do the same.* A pause. *To a fox.*

Zephyrine stood knee-deep in indecision at the edge of the shallows. Back the way they'd come lay open ground and riders. Ahead lay the ford and whatever waited in the pines on the far side. Above, the ridge. Below—

Below was the river itself, and the river was talking. It had been talking the whole time, she realized, in that pre-verbal rush all water made, but now that she listened—truly listened, with the gift she'd spent three decades strangling—she heard something else tangled in the current. Something bright. Layered. Thousands of voices braided together into a single shining rope.

Birdsong. Remembered birdsong. Every warble and whistle and evening chorus the Liss had ever carried past its banks, sunk into the water the way rings sink into old wood. And threaded through it, fainter, older: the leaked magic of Hollowmere, the ward's slow bleed, the quiet-thinning that Bramble had warned her about a lifetime ago. It had followed her. Or she had carried it. Either way, it pooled in this river like light in a cup.

*Zephyrine,* Bramble said, and there was something in the old goat's tone Zephyrine had never heard before. Caution, yes. But also—wonder. *The water knows your name.*

"That's ominous."

*That is an invitation,* said Bramble. *Learn the difference. It will save your life.*

She didn't decide, exactly. Decisions took time and she had perhaps thirty heartbeats. She simply stopped holding it back—the listening, the answering, all of it. She opened the door she'd kept barred since childhood, and everything she'd locked outside came in at once.

The river's memory flooded her. Skylarks spiraling over June meadows. A wren—her Wren's namesake, or maybe the child had been named for the bird, she'd never asked herself properly—scolding from a hedgerow. Geese arguing across autumn skies in vast ragged formations. The dawn chorus of some spring decades dead, every thrush and blackbird and chiffchaff stacked note upon note until the sound was less music than weather. All of it hers now. All of it answerable.

Zephyrine raised her hands—she didn't know why; it felt right, the way kneeling felt right in church even if you believed nothing—and she sang back.

Not with her throat. With everything else.

What came out of her was the flood itself: thirty years of swallowed speech poured into the river's remembered choir and given back to the morning tenfold. The air above the ford filled with birdsong so dense and so loud it stopped being sound and became a presence, a wall, a cathedral built of noise in the space of a heartbeat. Every bird that had ever sung along this water sang again at once, and the song had teeth.

The horses screamed first.

Zephyrine saw it all with a terrible clarity, as though she stood outside her own body: the grey-cloaked wardens breaking cover along the ridge and in the birches, six of them, eight, twelve, and their mounts rearing and twisting beneath them, white-eyed, deafened by a chorus that no living throat was making. One rider went down hard in the shallows. Another was dragged by his panicking horse back up the bank and into the trees. Somewhere a horn blew twice and died mid-note, swallowed by skylarks. Men shouted orders at animals who had stopped believing in men.

*Run,* said Bramble, already splashing into the ford. *Now, while they are busy remembering what birds are.*

Zephyrine seized the pony's bridle and hauled. The water was waist-deep and viciously cold, dragging at her skirts, and Wren clung flat to the pony's back with their cheek pressed to its mane, and the birdsong roared around them like a storm made entirely of joy, and for eleven steps it was working. It was actually working.

Then she saw the twelfth warden.

He had not panicked. He stood hip-deep in the shallows off to their left, apart from the others, and he had drawn not a sword but a crossbow, and he had not been aiming at her at all. He had been aiming, patiently, calmly, at the smallest figure on the pony's back.

Later—many times later, in the long nights that followed—Zephyrine would take that moment apart and examine it, looking for the version of events where she was faster. There wasn't one. He loosed. The bolt crossed the fifteen feet between him and the child faster than thought, faster than guilt, faster than any magic she would ever learn.

And Wren moved.

The child twisted—not away from the bolt, Zephyrine understood that much instantly and would understand it forever, but *into* its path, throwing their small body between the iron and Zephyrine's chest, one hand flung out, palm open, the way you might catch rain.

The bolt struck light.

There was no better word for it. The seal-child's whole body flared—for one instant Wren looked like a figure cut from paper held before a bonfire, all edges and glow—and the bolt shattered into sparks and slivers that hissed out in the water. The birdsong choked off. Silence slammed down like a lid.

Wren slid from the pony's back into the river without a sound.

Zephyrine caught them before the current did. She registered, distantly, that the warden with the crossbow was staring at his own weapon as though it had betrayed him; that he then turned and fled upstream, scrambling, dropping his bow in the shallows. She registered that the rest of the patrol was scattered somewhere behind her in the birches, unhorsed and humiliated and alive. She registered none of it as mattering. The world had contracted to the weight of the child in her arms and the terrible, wrong looseness of them.

"Wren. Wren, look at me. Look at me."

The child's eyes opened halfway. Deer-eyes, catching light that wasn't there anymore. Their lips moved. No sound. Not even their usual silence, which had always been full of gesture and meaning—this was a different silence, an emptied one, and it reached into Zephyrine's chest and closed its hand around everything living there.

She hauled them to the far bank. Bramble was already there, standing over a hollow of dry bracken like a general securing a position. Zephyrine laid Wren down and tore open their coat with hands that would not stop shaking, and found—

Blood. Not a great deal of it. A shallow gouge along the ribs, angry red, where the bolt's fragments had scored the flesh even through the flare of light. And beneath the blood, when she pushed the cloth aside, something worse: the skin around the wound had gone pale as frost, and cold radiated off it like a stone pulled from deep winter ground.

*The seal spent itself,* Bramble said quietly. *The child threw their own fire in front of iron. That is not free.*

"It should have been me." The words came out of Zephyrine cracked down the middle. "Bramble, it should have been me. They were aiming at me first—I saw it—they changed the aim because I—because I was too busy singing to—"

*Yes,* said Bramble, because Bramble had never once in her life lied to be kind. *And the child chose. You know the difference between those things. Say them both or say neither.*

Zephyrine said neither. She bent over Wren instead and did the only work her hands knew: herbs from her satchel, moss, clean linen, the little field-craft of a woman who had spent a lifetime doctoring goats and neighbors who pretended not to notice her skill. The wound was clean enough. The cold in it was not. She warmed water over a fire she built with fingers she ordered not to tremble, and they mostly obeyed, and she soaked cloth and pressed it to the frost-pale skin and whispered—not beast-speech, not the true tongue, just a woman's low human murmur, nonsense and endearments, *there now, I have you, stubborn creature, absolutely not, you are not allowed*—and slowly, slowly, the pallor retreated like a tide going out.

Toward dusk, Wren's eyes opened fully.

*You're leaking,* the child signed weakly. A tear had fallen from Zephyrine's chin onto their cheek.

"I am not leaking. Rivers leak. I am merely—situated near water."

Wren's mouth curved. Then, with effort: *Don't stop. What you did. At the ford. Don't ever stop doing that.*

"Wren—"

*It sounded like the stones. When they sing. You sounded like the covenant sounds, in my dreams.* The small hand found hers and gripped it, fierce despite the weakness. *You sounded like the oath.*

Zephyrine looked at the child who had thrown themselves in front of death with an open palm, and understood—with a settling sensation, like a keel finding water—that guilt was a luxury she was going to have to spend carefully. Her fight had casualties now. Her fight had always had casualties; she simply hadn't been present to count them before. Her mother. Maren on the road behind them. A warden sitting somewhere tonight, staring at a broken crossbow, learning that the world was larger than his ledger.

She could carry that. She would carry it. But she would not put it down and stop.

"The shrine," she said aloud, to Wren, to Bramble, to the darkening pines. "Two days north. We rest tomorrow—one day, no more—and then we move. Slow stages. You'll ride and not argue."

*I never argue,* Wren signed with dignity, and fell asleep mid-gesture.

Zephyrine sat by the small fire long after, feeding it sticks, listening to the Liss talk to itself in the dark. The birdsong was gone from the water now—or rather, folded back into it, sleeping. But she could feel it there, patient, and she understood that she had crossed some border more permanent than the ward-line. There was no folding this part of her away again. Whatever came next—wardens, winter, the cost the stones exacted—she would meet it speaking.

Bramble settled beside her, warm as a stove.

*Ashmoor,* the goat said, after a while. *If the cold in the child worsens. There is a healer there. Exiled. The wolves speak well of her, which for wolves is nearly a love letter.*

"Ashmoor," Zephyrine repeated, and filed it away with all the other hopes she couldn't yet afford.

Above the pines, the first stars came out, indifferent and beautiful, and somewhere south of the river, a woman with a ledger was writing her name.
