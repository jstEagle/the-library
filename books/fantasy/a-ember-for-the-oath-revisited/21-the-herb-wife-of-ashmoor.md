## 21. The Herb-Wife of Ashmoor

The village of Ashmoor was not on any map Zephyrine had ever seen, and she suspected that was precisely how its one remaining resident preferred it.

They found it by accident—or rather, Wren found it, tugging her hand off the road and down a sheep track that wound between frost-bitten hedges until the trees opened onto a crooked cluster of cottages, most of them roofless, their stones going soft with moss. Only one still had smoke rising from its chimney, a thin grey thread against the pale afternoon.

"You have an eye for chimneys," Zephyrine said. "I'll give you that."

Wren looked up at her with those deer-bright eyes and did not answer, because Wren had not spoken since the ford—had not spoken since the moment they'd thrown up their small arms to shield her from the Oathwardens' counter-spell, and the magic had broken over them like a wave over a gull. The healers' hands had done what they could on the road: splints of green hazel, bindings of linen torn from her own shift, willow bark chewed into paste. But the child's left arm was badly hurt, the skin beneath the bandages angry and weeping, and Zephyrine had spent two days pretending she wasn't terrified.

She was very good at pretending. Thirty years of practice.

The cottage at the end of the lane had a garden that stopped her in her tracks. In a season when everything else had surrendered to frost, this plot was riotously, defiantly alive—feverfew and valerian, comfrey broad as dinner plates, a row of sage so old it had gone woody and silver. And along the fence, trained on strings like prisoners being marched somewhere, grew plants Zephyrine knew only from her mother's whispered lists: moonwort, wolf's-bane's gentler cousin, a trailing vine with blue-black berries that made her breath catch.

*Emberleaf,* her mother had called it. *For when the fire inside goes out.*

"Nobody grows emberleaf," Zephyrine murmured. "Nobody *can* grow it outside a ward."

The door opened before she knocked.

The woman standing in it was small and round as a teapot, wrapped in three shawls of clashing colors, with iron-grey hair escaping its braid in every direction and spectacles perched on a nose that had clearly been broken at least once. She looked at Zephyrine. She looked at Wren. Her gaze dropped to the child's bandaged arm, and something in her face went from suspicion to recognition to a kind of grim resignation, all in the space of a heartbeat.

"Well," she said. "I wondered when the road would finally send me something interesting. Come in before the frost does. Mind the cat."

There was no cat. There was, however, a goose standing just inside the doorway, regarding them with the flat yellow-eyed contempt of its kind.

"That's the cat," the woman said, deadpan. "Don't argue with her. I lost that argument years ago."

* * *

Her name was Maren, and she had been the herb-wife of Ashmoor for longer than Ashmoor had been empty.

"They left after the last covenant failure," she said, ladling something fragrant out of an enormous black pot. It smelled of barley and thyme and, underneath, something wilder—a green growing smell, like a forest floor in spring. "Forty years back now. The winter came early, the wells went bitter, and folk decided the land itself had turned against them. So they packed their carts and rolled downhill to Ferrow's Cross, where there were walls and priests and someone else to blame." She handed Zephyrine a bowl, then a second smaller one to Wren. "I stayed. Somebody had to mind the graves."

"And the garden," Zephyrine said carefully. "That garden shouldn't exist. Not here. Not without—"

"Without magic? Yes, well." Maren lowered herself into a chair by the fire with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. "Ashmoor sits on a thin place. Always has. The old shrine up the hill leaks a little warmth even now, half-asleep as it is. Enough to keep a stubborn old woman's herbs alive." She pushed her spectacles up. "You'd know about thin places, I expect. You've got the look of Hollowmere about you."

Zephyrine's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because you flinched when the goose honked." Maren's eyes, magnified behind the spectacles, were sharp as awls. "Thirty years in a silence-ward teaches a body to startle at sound. I've seen it before. I treated a woman once, years ago, who carried that same look—the way she moved like the world might shout at her any moment." Something flickered across her face, quickly smoothed away. "She came through here heading north with a baby, right around midwinter. Never said where she was going. Never said her name."

The fire popped. The goose honked once, softly, as if to say *well?*

"My mother," Zephyrine said quietly.

"Mm." Maren sipped her broth. "Small world. Smaller valleys."

Wren, meanwhile, had eaten their soup with the focused intensity of someone who had been hungry for a very long time, and was now slumped against Zephyrine's side, eyelids drooping. The fever had come up during the afternoon walk; Zephyrine could feel it radiating off the small body like a stone kept near a fire. When she touched the bandage, Wren whimpered—not waking, but close to the surface of sleep, swimming up toward pain.

Maren set down her bowl. "Let me see the arm."

"I've bound it. Willow bark for the fever, comfrey under the—"

"I can see what you've done, girl, and it was well done for someone working alone on a cold road with no proper kit." She was already unwinding the bandage with practiced fingers. "But you've been treating a hurt child. I've spent forty years treating what this land does to people. There's a difference, and you're about to see it."

What lay beneath the bandage made Zephyrine's stomach drop. The burn wasn't healing the way burns healed. The edges had gone silver-white, and in the center of the wound the skin had taken on a faint tracery of lines—delicate, branching, almost like—

"Like frost-fern," Zephyrine whispered. "Like the patterns on the ward-stone."

"Like featherwork," Maren corrected. She bent closer, spectacles nearly touching the wound. "Look again. Those aren't cracks. They're quills. The spell that hit the child is trying to write itself into them, same as it would write into any living seal. Counter-magic always wants a surface." She sat back and rubbed her eyes. "Ordinary salves won't touch it. What this needs is something that remembers what the child is."

"And what is the child?" Zephyrine asked, though her heart already knew, had known since Bramble spoke plainly beside the dry well. "Tell me what you see."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Then she rose, groaning again, and went to a chest in the corner—a great oak thing banded with iron, older than anything else in the room. From it she drew a book. Not a printed book; a hand-copied one, its leather cover worn soft as cloth, its pages the color of weak tea.

"My grandmother copied this from her grandmother, who copied it from a speaker who died in the purges," Maren said. "It's mostly recipes and birthing-charms and nonsense about weather. But there are chapters in it nobody else would want." She turned pages with a tenderness she showed nothing else in the house, not even the goose. "Here. The Covenant of Nine Stones. Sealed at the world's edge before the first king drew his first border. Nine living seals, one for each stone—children given to the wild realms as surety, flesh made promise. As long as a seal lived and slept at its stone, the two realms remembered each other. Spoke each other's names. Kept the seasons honest."

She turned the page, and Zephyrine felt the floor tilt gently beneath her.

The illustration was crude, ink faded to brown, but unmistakable: a small figure standing before a standing stone, arms raised, and around the figure's head a ring of marks—the same spirals Wren had drawn in the ash of her hearth. The same spirals the migration birds sang.

"The seals weren't prisoners," Maren read aloud, tracing the lines. "'They are the word made walking. They do not guard the stones; they *are* the stones, wearing skin.' And here—" her finger stopped, and her voice changed, went quieter, "—here it says the seals were nine children of the speaker-lines, chosen because only a speaker's blood could hold both tongues at once. Human words and true words. Both at once, all their lives, without going mad from it."

Zephyrine's throat was tight. "And the others?"

"The other eight?" Maren closed the book gently. "Girl, I'm an old woman in an empty village, not a historian of atrocities. But I'll tell you what I know. Over the centuries, the kingdom hunted the speakers, and when the speakers ran out, they hunted the seals. Each stone that lost its seal cracked a little more. Each crack let a little forgetting in. By the time your Hollowmere ward went up—what, thirty years ago?—only a few seals were left hiding, and the Wardens called it mercy." Her mouth twisted. "*Mercy.* Taking children from the wild places so the wild places would forget us first. That way, when the last stone failed, nothing would come looking for revenge. Nothing would remember us at all."

The fire crackled. Wren stirred against Zephyrine's shoulder, murmuring something in a language that was not human and yet somehow was—the true tongue, leaking out of dreams.

Maren went very still.

Then she set the book down, crossed the room, and knelt beside the sleeping child with a slowness that had nothing to do with her knees. She listened. Her lips moved, shaping syllables she clearly didn't understand but recognized, the way a woman who has never sailed still knows the sound of the sea.

"Blessed frost and buried light," she breathed. "It's speaking the old covenant-forms. I haven't heard those since my grandmother died." She looked up at Zephyrine, and her eyes behind the spectacles were wet. "Do you know what you're carrying, woman? Do you have any idea?"

"A seal-child," Zephyrine said. "The last one."

"The last one," Maren repeated. She laughed—a short, disbelieving sound—and pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. "Forty years I've sat in this village minding graves and growing herbs, telling myself the old days were done, that the best I could do was die useful. And then the road sends me the last living word of the covenant, feverish, with Warden-spell writing itself into its skin." She stood, decisively, shawls flying. "Right. Goose, watch the door. We're going to work."

* * *

The work took most of the night.

Maren's method was part medicine, part something older. She brewed a decoction of her impossible garden—emberleaf and moonwort and three things she refused to name—"because if I name them you'll worry, and worrying makes wounds worse, that's not superstition, that's observation"—and while it steeped she sang.

Not beast-speech. Not the true tongue. Just songs—old ones, harvest songs and lullabies and one long rambling ballad about a miller's daughter that seemed to have forty verses and no ending. She sang them in a low, unlovely, entirely sincere voice, and as she sang, Zephyrine watched the frost-fern lines in Wren's wound begin to slow their creeping.

"It's not the words," Maren explained, grinding poultice in a mortar. "It's the remembering. A seal feeds on witness—that's what the texts mean when they say 'the word made walking.' Every song sung over it, every story told in its hearing, every person who looks at it and sees something worth keeping—that's food. That's medicine." She glanced up. "Which tells me something about you, too, doesn't it? Thirty years with no one to sing over you. No wonder you flinch."

Zephyrine didn't answer. She held Wren's good hand and watched the fire and let the miller's daughter wash over her, verse after pointless verse, and found, to her surprise, that her eyes were stinging.

Near midnight, they changed the dressing. The wound beneath had changed: the silver quill-lines had retreated to a faint ghosting, and the angry flesh had gone pink and quiet. Wren slept deeply, breathing easy, one small fist curled around Zephyrine's finger.

"There," Maren said, sitting back heavily. "That'll hold. The spell's not gone—it's patient, Warden-work always is—but it's not writing anymore. It's waiting. Which means we've bought time, not victory." She began gathering her tools, then paused. "You'll need to keep the child witnessed. Talk to them. Sing, if you sing. Tell them stories, even foolish ones. Especially foolish ones."

"I tell goat stories," Zephyrine offered. "Bramble has strong opinions about fences."

"Good. Fences are underrated." Maren washed her hands in a basin, frowning at the water. Then, without looking up: "Where are the stones, then? The sleeping ones. You're going after them, or you wouldn't be on this road, and you wouldn't have let me treat a fugitive in my kitchen."

"How do you know I'm a fugitive?"

"Because you checked the window twice while I was singing, and you sit facing the door, and you put yourself between the child and me every time I moved too fast." She wrung out the cloth. "None of which I object to. I'd think less of you if you didn't. Now—the stones."

Zephyrine hesitated. Everything in her, thirty years deep, said *tell him nothing, trust no one, stay small.* But Wren's hand was curled around her finger, and Maren had knelt on a hard floor at midnight for a stranger's child, and some doors, once opened, do not politely close again.

"We've woken one," she said. "The flooded shrine, north of the ford. Half-woken. Wren sang it awake but it wouldn't finish—it wanted something more than the child could give alone. And there's another..." She described the glacier mountain Bramble had spoken of, far to the east, where the third stone slept in ice.

"Mm. The flooded shrine would be Ellowmere's old bath-house—sacred ground before the kingdom was a rumor." Maren dried her hands slowly. "And the glacier is the Sleeping Grandmother. My grandmother's text speaks of both." She retrieved the book and turned to a page near the end, covered in her own cramped annotations layered over older handwriting like sediment. "But you're thinking too small, girl. There are five stones left, not counting the one you woke. Here—" she tapped the map drawn in the margin, a coastline and mountains rendered in faded ink, "—the Grandmother in the east ice. The Drowned One, south, under the mere at Cael Harrow—nobody goes there, the water doesn't freeze even in the worst winters. The Root-Stone, west, in the deep forest the wolves hold. And the last two..."

Her finger stopped. She was quiet a moment.

"The last two are the hard ones. One lies under Ferrow's Cross itself—under the cathedral, if the old notes are right. They built the church on top of it deliberately. Smother it with hymns, bury it under doctrine." Her mouth went dry-looking. "And the ninth is at the world's edge, on the sea-cliff where the first oath was sworn. Where it all began, and where it will end, one way or the other."

Zephyrine stared at the little map. Five stones. A cathedral. An army of Wardens somewhere behind them, and Serah Vane's cold eyes surely already turning in their direction. Midwinter coming on like a debt collector.

"It's impossible," she said.

"Oh, absolutely," Maren agreed cheerfully. "Now. Are you going to sit there stating the obvious, or are you going to help me pack? Because I've been waiting forty years for a reason to leave this village, and I am *not* missing my chance to be impossible with company."

Zephyrine blinked. "You're coming with us?"

"I'm coming with you." Maren was already pulling a second, larger satchel from under her bed, stuffing it with terrifying efficiency—bandages, jars, a knife, the book, a bag of salt, a bundle of dried emberleaf wrapped in oilcloth. "Someone has to keep that wound clean. Someone has to read the old texts when the stones get cryptic—they do get cryptic, it's practically their hobby. And someone has to argue with you when you decide, out of thirty years of bad habit, that you ought to do this alone to keep everyone safe." She straightened up and fixed Zephyrine with a look over her spectacles. "I raised four children and buried a husband and outlived an entire village. You will find, Zephyrine-of-Hollowmere, that I am extremely difficult to leave behind."

"...How do you know my—"

"You talk in your sleep, dear. You said it twice while I was changing the dressing. Also you flinched at the goose again." She hefted the satchel. "Goose! We're leaving. Try not to eat the garden before the frost does."

The goose honked once, imperiously, and waddled out the door ahead of them like a general leading a retreat.

* * *

They left Ashmoor at first light, three figures and a goose on the frozen lane: a woman who had spent thirty years learning to be invisible, a wounded child who was the last word of a dying covenant, and a round old herb-wife who hummed miller's ballads and carried forty years of exile in a canvas satchel.

Wren walked between them, holding a hand of each, and though the child still hadn't spoken since the ford, when Maren stumbled on an icy rut and swore with tremendous creativity, Wren laughed—a small, startled, wonderful sound, the first since the river.

Maren looked down at the child, and then at Zephyrine, and her eyes were bright above her spectacles.

"There," she said. "That's the medicine working."

Behind them, the smoke of Ashmoor's last chimney thinned and vanished into the pale sky. Ahead, the road ran east toward ice and mountains and a grandmother made of glacier, and somewhere far behind, gaining or not gaining, the Wardens rode.

Zephyrine squeezed the small hand in hers and walked on.
