## 22. Wren's Nightmare

The dream does not begin. It is simply already happening, the way weather is already happening when you step outside into it.

Wren is nine places at once.

This is how it always is in the deep part of sleep, past the shallow water where Zephyrine's heartbeat sounds through the wall of the little house, past the warm animal dark where Bramble chews her cud two rooms away and the mice in the thatch argue about seeds. Past all of that, down where the stones still hold a thread of Wren's name, there are nine of her, and eight of them are cold.

*First,* says the dream, and Wren is a boy on an island, standing on a black stone while the sea talks to him in the old way, and he is laughing, because the sea has just told him a joke about a crab who wanted to be a bird—

—and then men come over the dunes with hammers wrapped in iron, and the sea forgets the joke mid-sentence, and the boy learns what it is to be unwritten. Not killed. Unwritten. There is a difference, and the difference is worse. A killed thing leaves a body. An unwritten thing leaves nothing at all, not even the shape of where it used to be. The island keeps its stone but loses its meaning, the way a word keeps its letters after you forget what it said.

*Second,* says the dream, and Wren is a girl in the mountains, and her stone sings under snowfall, and she teaches it a new verse every morning—

—and they do not even bring hammers for her. They bring a priest with a kind face, who explains, gently, patiently, that what she is carrying is a sickness, that her people were deceived, that the wild things she loves never loved her back, that the covenant was a leash and she was the collar. He is so kind. That is the terrible part. He holds her hand while the soldiers work, and he tells her she will feel so much lighter after, and the worst thing, the very worst thing, the thing Wren will carry longest, is that for one moment—one small, traitorous moment—the girl believes him, and feels lighter, and hates herself for it as the song goes out of her like breath from a wound.

*Third,* says the dream, and Wren is twins, two seal-children born the same night to the same mother beneath the same standing stone, which had never happened before and never happened again—

—and the soldiers cannot decide which twin to take first, and argue about it in front of them, and the twins hold hands the whole time, and afterward the stone remembers their hands holding hands longer than it remembers anything else, and that memory is the only reason any of the nine stones remember anything at all anymore.

Wren tries to wake up. This is a skill she learned long ago, or something older than Wren learned it and left it behind like a coat in a closet: *when the dream turns to the unwriting, climb the ladder of your own name.* But the ladder is missing rungs now. Eight of them, to be exact. Each time a seal-child died, a rung went out of the ladder, and Wren has been climbing a thinner and thinner ladder her whole life without knowing why some nights the climbing got hard.

*Fourth,* says the dream, and this one is almost gentle, because the fourth child was old—not old the way people are old, but old the way rivers are old—and when they came for her stone she sat down on it and refused to move, and they waited three days, and she told them stories the whole time, real ones, true ones, about the years before the silence, and by the third day half the soldiers were weeping and would not lift their hammers, and so the captain lifted his own, and the fourth child looked at him with such pity that he dreamed about her every night for the rest of his life, and died old, and on his deathbed said her name, though no one around him knew whose name it was.

That one, Wren doesn't mind so much. That one is almost a victory. The dream keeps it near the front of the drawer, worn smooth from handling.

*Fifth.*

Fifth is the one with the dogs.

The fifth child lived among dogs, ran with a pack the way Wren runs with goats, and when the wardens came, the pack stood between the child and the soldiers, forty strong, snarling, and the wardens did not fight them—they simply spoke a word, a human word, a command-word, the kind of word that only works because somewhere long ago dogs agreed to hear it, and the pack sat down. All forty of them. Sat down, trembling, fighting it, tails flat, eyes begging forgiveness—and the child watched the oldest friend her species had ever made obey a stranger's voice instead of her heart, and the hammer fell, and the dogs howled for a week, and then, one by one, they stopped howling, because grief is heavy and dogs are practical, and that was the fifth rung gone.

In the dream, Wren understands something she has never let herself understand awake: the covenant did not break. It was not weathered away, not eroded, not forgotten by accident. It was *executed*. Nine times. Deliberately, thoroughly, with paperwork. Somewhere there were ledgers. Somewhere someone checked off names.

*Sixth,* says the dream, and Wren is so tired—

The sixth child hid. For eleven years the sixth child hid, moving from hollow to hollow, and the stone slept safe, and Wren's dreaming self wants to shout at her, *keep hiding, keep hiding, you're doing it right,* but hunger is patient and children are small, and in the twelfth year a farmer found her stealing turnips and turned her in for the reward, which was twelve silver pieces, and Wren lies awake inside the dream doing the arithmetic she can never stop doing: *my life was worth twelve silver pieces.* And then, because the dream is cruel in the specific way dreams are cruel, it shows her the farmer's face, and the farmer is tired, and hungry too, and afraid of the wardens, and he wept when they took her, and gave the silver to his daughter for bread.

No one in the whole story is a monster. That is the horror of it. It was done by tired, frightened, ordinary hands, each one doing a small job, each one saying *I only held the ledger, I only drove the cart, I only gave the order, I only sat down when the word was spoken.*

*Seventh,* says the dream, and the seventh child doesn't even get a story, just a flash—iron, snow, a stone splitting like ice on a pond—and Wren realizes the dream itself has lost the seventh child's details, because the seventh child was unwritten so completely that even the shared memory cannot hold them, and Wren screams inside the dream at the unfairness of it, at the theft of it, *at least let me remember you, let me be your ledger, let me carry your name if nowhere else—*

But she doesn't know the name. She never knew the names. That was the design. Kill the seals, kill the memory, and the memory of the killing dies last of all.

*Eighth.*

The eighth child is the one Wren knew.

Not knew—*was*, briefly, before Wren was fully Wren. In the first year, before the sending, before the long crawl through the border country to the valley that forgets, the eight remaining children had been gathered one last time, secretly, by the wolves and the crows and whatever else still remembered, in a hollow place under roots, and they had slept in a pile the way puppies sleep, nine hearts' worth of warmth minus one, and the eighth child had hummed the covenant-song to the others as a lullaby, getting it wrong in places, and the others had corrected her, giggling, and Wren can still feel that pile, that warmth, that wrong-note hummed with love.

Then the wardens found the hollow.

Wren was the smallest. The wolves put her deepest, at the bottom, under everyone, and carried her out through the water when the screaming started, and the eighth child's humming stopped mid-verse, and Wren has been trying to finish that verse her whole life without knowing what she was humming toward.

Eight rungs gone. One left.

And the dream, having shown her everything, finally shows her the truth it has been circling all night, the way a moth circles a lamp:

*You are the last rung, little one. And they are coming for the ladder.*

*

She wakes the way a dam breaks.

There is no gentle surfacing. There is the dream, and then there is sound—her own sound—coming out of her in a language that has no human letters, that predates human letters, that goes through the walls of Maren's cottage in Ashmoor like water through cloth. Frost blooms across the window glass in spirals. The fire in the hearth gutters and turns briefly blue. Outside, every dog in the village begins to howl at once, and then stops, all together, as if a word had been spoken that only they could hear.

Zephyrine is there before Wren finishes the first scream. Zephyrine is always there before the first scream finishes; Wren has stopped wondering how. Arms come around her, smelling of woodsmoke and goat and the particular herb Maren boils for tea, and Wren is talking—pouring it all out, the island boy and the kind priest and the twins and the twelve silver pieces and the eighth child's unfinished verse—in the old speech, fast and broken, and she knows Zephyrine can't catch more than fragments, and she pours anyway, because that is what the covenant was, before it was stones and oaths and law: it was *someone listening.*

Maren appears in the doorway with a candle, grey braid loose, face creased from sleep. She takes one look at the frosted windows—at the spirals, which are not random, which are the migration-patterns, which are the shapes birds sing—and sets the candle down very carefully, the way people set things down when their hands want to shake.

"Oh, child," Maren says softly. "Oh, you poor little ember."

Bramble is in the room too. Wren has no memory of the door opening for her. Goats have their own arrangements with doors. Bramble settles herself against the bed's foot like a sandbag against a flood, chewing slowly, watching Wren with horizontal-pupiled calm, and says, in the plain speech Zephyrine translates with her hands resting on the goat's neck:

"Tell her the dream is not a dream. Tell her the stones talk to her in her sleep because she is what's left of the conversation. And tell her—" Bramble pauses, chews, chooses. "Tell her the eighth child's verse ends with her. That's why she can't finish it. It isn't finished yet."

Wren makes a sound that is half sob and half laugh, because it is exactly the sort of thing Bramble would say, and exactly the sort of thing that helps, and she presses her face into Zephyrine's shoulder and lets the frost on the windows slowly, slowly melt.

Zephyrine rocks her and speaks into her hair in human words Wren has learned to read by tone alone, and then, carefully, quietly, in the old speech—the accent still crooked, the grammar still limping, but improving every week, and every improvement lands in Wren's chest like a coal:

"*Not alone. Not anymore. Say it back.*"

"*Not alone,*" Wren whispers.

"*Say the other part.*"

Wren hesitates. Then: "*Not anymore.*"

It is the first promise anyone has made her that she believes.

*

Later, when the fire is rebuilt and Wren is wrapped in blankets by the hearth with sweetened milk going cold in her hands, the adults think she is asleep. Adults always think that. Children learn early that the most useful thing you can be is invisible, and Wren was taught invisibility by masters—by wolves, by necessity, by thirty years of a valley's silence pressing down on the woman who now guards her sleep.

Maren stands at the frost-spiraled window, tracing one spiral with a fingertip without quite touching it.

"The windows," Maren says. "I've read about this. In the old texts—the ones the cathedral burned, mostly, but texts survive the way weeds survive. When a seal-child wakes from the death-dreams, the frost writes what the stones remember." Her finger follows the curve. "This isn't grief-madness, Zephyrine. It's testimony. The child is a witness, and witnesses dream in evidence."

"And the howling?" Zephyrine asks. "Every dog in Ashmoor, all at once, then silent?"

Maren is quiet a moment. "That," she says, "was the part I'd hoped not to confirm. Because there's a passage—I thought it was metaphor—about what happens when the last seal begins to fail. It says the beasts will hear it first. It says the land will start *calling roll.*" She turns from the window. "Checking whether anyone answers."

By the hearth, wrapped in wool, Wren keeps her breathing slow and even and listens with her whole body, and files the words away where she files everything: *the land is calling roll. Someone must answer.*

And far to the south, though no one in the warm kitchen knows it yet, Serah Vane stands at a map table by candlelight, drawing a line with a compass point from Ashmoor toward the mountains, and says to her lieutenant, in a voice like a closing ledger:

"She's heading for the glacier stone. Wake the vanguard. We ride at first light."

Outside, the first snow of the season begins to fall—early, too early, falling grey instead of white, falling like ash.

The long winter has noticed them back.
