## 23. Why Vane Hates the Wild

The warrant papers came back from the Chantry of the Silence on the third morning after the crypt incident, sealed in grey wax that smelled faintly of cold iron. Serah Vane broke the seal at her field desk, read the single line—*Full authority granted. Resolve as you see fit.*—and felt something in her chest settle into place like a key turning a lock she had been carrying for thirty years.

She should have felt triumphant. She had spent eleven years climbing the Oathwardens' ranks toward exactly this: authority without oversight, resources without accounting, the right to march under open banners instead of skulking through hedgerows asking farmers leading questions. Every junior warden who had ever smirked at her obsession with "an irregularity in the silence" would now salute the obsession.

Instead she sat very still for a long moment and thought about a girl named Pell.

Then she called for ink, and paper, and her second-in-command, and did not think about Pell again for the rest of the day.

* * *

It was Dory who finally made her talk about it, which was typical of Dory. Corporal Theodora Ash, nineteen years old, freckled across the nose like a spattered apron, possessed of the unshakable conviction that silence between superior officers was a problem to be solved rather than respected.

"You knew," Dory said that evening, perched on the supply wagon's tailgate while Vane cleaned her sword by lamplight. "Before any of this started. Before Hollowmere. You already hated them."

"I don't hate anyone, Corporal. Hate is inefficient."

"That's not a no."

Vane ran the whetstone along the blade and let the sound fill the space where an answer should have gone. Around them the camp settled into its night rhythms—horses stamping, sentries coughing, someone's terrible attempt at a marching song dying quickly under a sergeant's glare. A proper camp. Orderly. Human sounds, human voices, nothing whistling out of the treeline that a person hadn't put there.

"Pell was my brother," Vane said at last.

Dory went still. In two years of service she had never once heard Vane mention family. Most wardens hadn't; the Silence took people young and kept their histories in locked drawers.

"He was nine when it happened. I was twelve." The whetstone moved. "There's a village in the north fells called Harrow's End. You won't find it on maps anymore. That's not sentiment—it's policy. Villages that lose their charter to covenant failure get struck from the rolls."

"Covenant failure," Dory repeated slowly. "You mean the wild took it."

"The wild doesn't take, Corporal. That's the first thing they teach wrong in the training halls, all those soft stories about the old friendship between man and beast. The wild doesn't take. It *forgets to leave things alone.* There's a difference, and if you'd seen it, you'd understand why we build wards instead of friendships." She set the whetstone down. "Do you know what actually happens when an oath-stone fails? Not what the doctrine scrolls say. What happens."

"No, Commander."

"Neither did we. We found out over the course of one spring."

* * *

Harrow's End, in memory, always began the same way: with the bees.

That was the thing nobody warned you about a failing covenant. It didn't arrive like an army. It arrived like weather. The bees stopped coming back to the hives, and when her father climbed up to look—and Vane had followed him up the ladder, small and quick, because she followed Pell everywhere—he found the honeycombs full but empty, every cell capped and perfect and holding nothing. Not dead bees. No bodies at all. Just absence, shaped exactly like abundance.

"They've gone strange," her father said, which was a farmer's word for it, and the village said *strange* right back at him, because it was easier than the other word, the word the old grandmother Maret used, sitting on her step with her shawl pulled tight in April: *unbinding.*

Pell thought it was marvelous. That was the cruelty of being nine. He collected the strange things the way other boys collected river stones. The cow that lowed and had her lowing come out as birdsong. The well water that reflected stars during daylight. The morning the blackberries in the hedge ripened overnight in a season when blackberries were still months away, fat and dark and humming faintly, and Pell ate a fistful of them before anyone could stop him and laughed for an hour afterward—a laugh that came out doubled, his own voice underneath and something older and greener laughing just above it, harmonizing.

Vane had told their mother. She wanted to be clear about this later, in the years when she told the story to herself at night: *I told her.* She stood in the kitchen and said, "Pell is wrong. Something's wrong with Pell," and her mother had looked at the blackberry stains on his mouth and said the most dangerous sentence Vane would ever hear:

"It's only the old magic coming back. Maybe it's a blessing."

Maybe it's a blessing. She could still feel how the words had gone into her like a splinter, working deeper with every year. Her mother was not a foolish woman. Her mother could stretch a winter larder across a famine spring and mend a scythe with wire and prayer. But she had grown up on the soft stories too—the ones where the wild realm loved humankind like an eccentric aunt, forgetful and fond, and any strangeness was only a gift arriving early. Everyone in Harrow's End had grown up on those stories. They were a valley people, and valleys make you soft about edges. You start believing the edge is far away because it has always been far away from *you.*

By May, the edge had arrived, and it did not look like teeth or claws or anything a story had prepared them for.

It looked like the children forgetting.

* * *

Not all at once. That was what made it unbearable—the gentleness of it. Little Anthe Corr, four years old, stopped answering to her name, and when her mother shook her gently by the shoulders, Anthe tilted her head with perfect love and made the sound a wren makes, a bright rattling trill, and patted her mother's cheek as though to say *there, there,* the way you'd soothe a large confused animal.

The village healer called it a sickness. The priest called it possession. Grandmother Maret, who had been born before the last warding, called it what it was: the children's names were being eaten. The covenant fraying meant the boundary between *named* and *unnamed* was wearing thin, and children—newly named, their names barely stitched on—came loose first. Like buttons off a coat.

They didn't die. That was the horror everyone outside the fells never understood, the part the doctrine scrolls got wrong in the kind direction. The children stayed healthy, rosy, well-fed. They simply became *other.* Anthe lived in the hedge row after that, and was happy—visibly, terribly happy—eating berries and sleeping in the bracken, and when her mother sat at the hedge's edge every evening calling her name, Anthe would watch her with dark bird eyes full of affection and no recognition whatsoever, and answer in wren.

Sixteen children, that spring. Sixteen families. And the village did what villages do: it argued. Half of them wanted to send for the Oathwardens, and half said the wardens would burn the valley to cleanse it, and both halves were right, which is the worst kind of argument, the kind where nobody wins. Meanwhile the boundary kept thinning. The millpond froze in June and showed the reflection of a forest that did not exist around it. The pastor's dog stood on the church steps one Sunday and preached—in no language, in pure meaning—that they should all go home, and half the congregation obeyed it, because the meaning came through clean as glass and it seemed rude not to.

Pell lasted longest. Whatever the blackberries had done, it had bound him tighter than the others, knitted him to the green side more thoroughly, so that he faded in slow degrees rather than all at once. Vane watched it happen the way you watch a candle burn down, marking each stage. First he stopped wearing shoes. Then he stopped sleeping indoors, though he still came to breakfast. Then he stopped using words for things, pointing instead, then stopped pointing because the things he wanted weren't things anymore—they were directions, winds, times of day. The last time he spoke to her, really spoke, they were sitting on the pasture fence in the blue hour, and he said:

"Serry. When I forget you, don't take it personal."

He was nine. Nine years old, and he had worked out the whole shape of it himself, and he chose the word *personal*, because he'd heard it somewhere and liked it, and she could tell he'd been saving it.

"Forget me how?"

"Like a dream." He was watching the treeline, and his eyes had gone the deep amber-green of new leaves held up to sun. "You wake up and you know there was something. You know it mattered. You just can't get back to it." He swung his feet. "But I'll probably still like you. I like everything now. That's the trouble with it, Serry. It doesn't hate us. Nobody ever tells you that part. It just loves you until there's none of you left."

Three days later he answered to "Pell" the way a fox answers to a whistle—alert, curious, unowned.

* * *

"So the wardens came," Dory said quietly, when Vane's voice had stopped.

"The wardens came. Late, as wardens were late to Harrow's End, and heavy-handed, as heavy-handed as fear makes men. They built a ward-line around what remained of the village and sang the silence back into it, and the silence—" She paused. This was the part she never told anyone, the part she had carried so long it had worn smooth, like a stone in a pocket. "The silence gave them back. All sixteen. The moment the ward closed, the wild lost its grip on the names, and the names came home to the children like birds to a roost."

Dory blinked. "They were saved? Then—but you speak of it like a graveyard."

"Anthe Corr came back to herself standing in a hedge at dusk, eight feet from her mother, and did not remember a single hour of the spring. Do you understand? She reached for her mother with her own mind and found her mother's face wet with months of grief that Anthe had no share of. Pell woke in a hay barn, barefoot in November, nine years old inside, and could not account for a year of his life except as a dream of flying." Vane looked at the fire. "They were saved, yes. The wardens saved them. And every child saved learned within the month what the whole village had learned already: that the wild does not hate us. It loves us until there's none of us left. Anthe Corr never slept with her window open again. Pell—" her voice did something she disliked and she shut it down, "—Pell flinched at birdsong until the day he enlisted in the Silence at fourteen. Youngest volunteer the northern chapter ever recorded. He's a warden now. Senior, like me. We don't write, because neither of us can bear to hear the other's voice do anything but stay flat."

The campfire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, past the pickets, a nightjar called—one ordinary bird making one ordinary sound—and Vane noted without surprise that three separate sentries turned toward it, hands near weapons. Good. They were learning.

"So that's why," Dory said. Not a question.

"That's why I don't find the old stories charming, Corporal. That's why when a woman teaches a hawk to scream on command and a child walks out of a dying ward with deer-light in its eyes, I don't wonder whether they mean well. I'm sure they mean well. The wild means well. That has never once been the question." Vane sheathed her sword. "The question is whether meaning well matters when the result is sixteen children who love their mothers the way you love sunshine. From a great distance. Without knowing your own name."

"And the woman? Winterbourne? She was a child once too, wasn't she? Hidden in some ward somewhere, if what Grell says is true."

"Grell says a great many things for a man who wet himself at the sight of wolves." Vane rose, brushing straw from her cloak, and permitted herself the smallest dry note. "He also sells information to both sides of any conflict, so weigh him accordingly."

"But if she was hidden—if someone hid her from the Silence the way those children needed saving from the wild—"

"Then she has spent thirty years being protected from a choice," Vane said, "and I am about to spend midwinter taking that protection away. Yes." She looked north, toward the high passes, where the reports said the long winter's edge had stalled—stalled, since the crypt, since the light poured up through the cathedral nave in front of three hundred witnesses. Stalled, and behind the stall, impossibly, the land had begun to *green.*

That frightened her more than the winter had. Winter was honest. Greening was a lure. She had seen what the wild's tenderness did.

"Get some sleep, Corporal. We march at dawn for the pass. And Dory—" she paused at her tent flap, "—if you ever wonder whether I enjoy this: I don't. But somebody in this army has to remember Harrow's End, and maps are forbidden to hold it. So it has to be people. It has to be me."

"Yes, Commander," Dory said, and for once, blessedly, asked nothing further.

Alone in her tent, Vane laid out the campaign map and weighted its corners. Five stones remained asleep by her best intelligence—no, four, now, curse the crypt. Four stones between here and the sea-cliffs, and one woman, one strange child, one turncoat herb-healer, and apparently a wolf escort, racing her to them.

Her finger traced the route and stopped, unexpectedly, at Ferrow's Cross. The city where it had happened. Where three hundred souls had watched a stone burn light through a church and called it a miracle, some of them—wept, the reports said. *Wept with joy.*

"Foolishness," she said aloud, to the tent, to the dark, to the girl she had been. "It isn't mercy. You'll see."

Outside, far to the north, something in the treeline sang—three notes, sweet and deliberate, in a pattern no ordinary bird used twice.

Vane listened. Then, deliberately, she snuffed her lamp, and in the darkness wrote tomorrow's orders in her head: *double the pace.*

Whatever the woman and the child intended, they would not reach the next stone first. She would make certain of it—not out of hate, she told herself, and almost believed it—but out of memory.

Somebody had to remember what the wild's love cost.

Somebody had to keep the count.
