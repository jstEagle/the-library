## 24. The Stone Under the City

The cathedral of Ferrow's Cross smelled of candle wax and cold stone and, faintly, of lilies that had been fresh sometime around the founding of the town. Zephyrine stood in the square outside it with her hands jammed in her pockets and her heart doing something unhelpful against her ribs.

"Well," said Maren, squinting up at the twin spires. "Someone built a very large hat on top of it."

"That's your professional observation? A hat?"

"I'm an herb-wife, not an architect. But I know what I know." Maren shifted her basket to her other hip. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth and disguised as dried nettle bundles, lay three of her sharpest knives and a fistful of charms that would have gotten her burned in two of the four provinces she'd lived in. "That church isn't built *near* the stone, girl. It's built *on* it. Look at the foundations—see how they flare out at the corners? That's not for weight. That's for pinning."

Zephyrine looked. She had spent thirty years living above a ward-stone without knowing it was there; she had developed, she supposed, a certain humility about what could hide under her feet. But now that Maren had named it, she couldn't unsee it. The cathedral didn't sit on its foundations so much as *press* down on them, and the whole eastern wall leaned inward by a hand's width, like a man hunching his shoulders over something he wanted to keep warm—or keep quiet.

Wren tugged her sleeve. The child had been quiet all morning, which was not unusual, but the quality of the quiet had changed. It was the quiet of someone listening very hard.

"What is it?" Zephyrine asked, low, in the old speech.

"Down," Wren said. One word, pressed flat. "Down and down and it's dreaming and the dream is heavy."

*Heavy*. From a child who had carried the memory of eight murdered seal-siblings in their small body without complaint, *heavy* was a word to take seriously.

Around them, Ferrow's Cross went about its morning. Fishwives argued over the price of eels. Two apprentices chased each other around the fountain with more enthusiasm than skill. A bell somewhere above them rang the hour, and the sound rolled down through the flagstones and—and there. Zephyrine felt it, or thought she did: a flicker under the bell-tone, like a second voice humming a half-step flat. Most people would call it echo. Zephyrine had spent thirty years listening to what most people called echo.

"It's singing," she said quietly. "Underneath all that stone. They buried it and it just—kept singing."

"Stones are stubborn," Maren said. "It's why I like them better than people." She nodded across the square, toward the cathedral's side door, where a bored-looking verger was sweeping the step with the energy of a man being paid by the hour rather than by the results. "Now. Options. We can go in through the crypt—I'm told the old ossuary connects to the undercrofts, and the undercrofts predate the church by centuries. We can go in through the well-house on the north side; every old shrine has a well-house, and this one's been bricked over, which is as good as a signpost. Or we can wait for the midnight service, when the place is empty except for the watchman, who is old and half-blind and owes me money from a card game in Ashmoor."

Zephyrine considered all three options, and found, to her surprise, that she disliked every one of them—not because they were impractical, but because they were clever. Cleverness had kept her alive for thirty years. Cleverness had also kept her alone in a valley that forgot her name every time she left a room.

She thought about Vane, marching somewhere behind them with a warrant and an army. She thought about the light pouring out of the shrine at Hollowmere's border, and how it had felt to stop hiding. She thought about Bramble, who had once told her—in the blunt, unsentimental way of goats—that a sheep who hides from the dog under the same bramble every day should not be surprised when the bramble stops working.

"No," she said.

Maren raised an eyebrow. "No to which?"

"No to sneaking. All of it." Zephyrine turned to face them both, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Every stone we've woken so far, we've woken in secret, and every time, we've gotten a few days ahead before they understood what happened. This one's different. It's under their cathedral. If we wake it in the dark and slip away, they'll tear the city apart looking for whoever did it, and they'll find us anyway—Vane's already traced us here, you know she has—but worse, they'll find *it*, and a stone found in the dark looks like a crime scene. A stone woken in front of four hundred witnesses during morning mass..." She shrugged. "Looks like a miracle."

Maren stared at her for a long moment. Then she began, very slowly, to grin—a gap-toothed, thoroughly disreputable grin that made her look twenty years younger and roughly forty percent more likely to be arrested.

"Oh, I *like* it," she said. "It's mad, mind. Completely, gorgeously mad. But I like it."

"It might be mad," Zephyrine admitted. "But we've been clever for three stones. Let's see if faith works better than cunning for once. They've certainly got enough of it in that building."

Wren was looking up at the spires again. And then, softly, with the particular seriousness the child reserved for true things: "It wants to be sung."

* * *

Getting into morning mass required no trickery whatsoever, which Zephyrine found darkly funny. They stood in line with fishwives and farmers and one enormous family whose youngest child spent the entire wait attempting to eat a pinecone. Inside, the cathedral was vast and dim and smelled of ten thousand prayers soaked into wood. The congregation murmured. Candles guttered. Somewhere ahead, a priest's voice rose and fell in the comfortable cadences of a man reciting words he no longer bothered to weigh.

And beneath it all—beneath the floor, beneath the crypt, beneath the bones of whatever worthy dead they'd stacked down there—the third oath-stone sang its half-remembered song, muffled by six feet of consecrated masonry.

Zephyrine had expected to feel like an intruder. Instead she felt something closer to grief. They had built all this *on top of it*. Generations of the devout had knelt here asking their god for mercy, never knowing that directly under their knees lay the oldest promise their ancestors ever made—the promise that humans and the wild world would remember each other. They hadn't just forgotten the covenant. They'd paved over it, and then built a bigger forgetting on top, with nice stained glass.

"They didn't know," Maren murmured, reading her face. "The ordinary folk, I mean. The ones who come here to grieve and give thanks. Someone way back knew—that's why the church is here—but everyone since has just been praying in the dark."

"I know," Zephyrine said. "That's what makes me angry. Not the ones who buried it. The ones who've been kneeling on it ever since, hungry for something they can't name, while it sings underneath them."

Wren had gone very still beside her, head cocked, eyes reflecting candleflame like two small hearths. When Zephyrine touched the child's shoulder, Wren pointed—not toward the altar, but toward a low arched door off the north transept, half-hidden behind a funeral banner of some deceased alderman.

"Down," Wren said again. And then, with a small frown of effort: "*Soon.*"

The service ended the way services do, in a shuffling tide of coats and coughs and one lost mitten. The verger began locking the side doors. And Zephyrine made her choice the way she had learned to make choices lately: quickly, terrifiedly, and without a single clever escape route planned.

She walked to the funeral banner, stepped around it, and knocked on the arched door—three slow raps, the rhythm of the old speech, the pattern that meant *I hear you; answer.*

The door was oak, iron-banded, and locked. It did not open.

But the stone *under* the city heard. Zephyrine felt it turn over in its sleep the way a sleeper turns toward a window when someone calls their name. The candles along the north transept—all forty of them—bent their flames sideways at once, toward the door, as if a wind had risen from below. Several late-staying worshippers gasped. The verger dropped his keys.

"Oh good," Maren said cheerfully. "We're doing it *now.*"

The lock on the door clicked, all by itself, and swung inward onto darkness that breathed upward, cold and mineral and green, green, impossibly green—the smell of deep places where things have slept a long time and are not finished sleeping yet.

Zephyrine took Wren's hand. "Stay close. Stay loud if anyone grabs you—louder than you've ever been."

Wren nodded solemnly, and produced, as demonstration, a sound like a kettle being murdered.

"That'll do," said Maren, and followed them down.

* * *

The stairs wound below the crypt, below the ossuary with its tidy skulls, below even the oldest foundation stones, into rock that no human had quarried. The walls changed as they descended—first dressed masonry, then rough vaulting, then natural stone veined with something pale that caught their little lantern-light and held it, glowing faintly after they passed. Roots had found their way down here somehow, threading through cracks in the ceiling, and they were *wrong* roots: silver-grey, leafless, but unmistakably alive, pulsing very slowly, like veins.

"The shrine at Hollowmere had these," Zephyrine whispered. "In the flooded part."

"Roots of the World-Ash," Maren said, in the tone of a scholar who has read too much and believed too little until recently. "Or the Under-Linden, depending on your province and your heresies. The nine stones are supposed to share one root-system. Wake one anywhere, the texts say, and all nine dream of it." She paused. "Which means the church knows exactly where the others are, incidentally, if they ever wondered why they kept building shrines over wells."

The passage opened at last into a round chamber, and Zephyrine stopped breathing.

The oath-stone rose from the chamber floor like the stump of some colossal tree—wide as a millwheel, taller than a man, its surface carved with spirals within spirals within spirals, the same pattern Wren had drawn in ash the first night, the pattern migration birds stitch across autumn skies. It should have been grey. Every stone they'd found so far had been frost-eaten and dim. But this one, sealed away from sky and season under tons of church, had kept something the others had lost: color. Faint, deep, like embers remembered rather than seen—red-gold light moving slowly through the spiral channels, dimming and brightening with the cadence of its buried song.

"It's *warm,*" Maren breathed, holding her palm near it without touching. "Thirty feet under a frozen city and it's warm as a loaf coming out of the oven."

"Dreaming hard," Wren said. The child walked right up to the stone and laid both small hands flat against it. The ember-light flared. "It dreamed of the others waking. It felt them. Three sisters singing and five still sleeping and—" Wren's face crumpled slightly. "Four gone quiet forever."

Zephyrine knelt beside the child. "Can you wake it?"

"Yes." No hesitation. Then, quieter, with that terrible gravity Wren sometimes carried: "But it will be *loud.* Louder than the shrine. Louder than the ford. The stone is strong because it was never allowed to sing. When it sings, everything above us will know. The bells will ring by themselves. The windows—" Wren made a gesture suggesting windows were not long for this world.

Zephyrine looked around the chamber. Warmth, light, roots, song. Everything she had spent thirty years burying in herself, gathered here under a cathedral, waiting.

She thought about running. There was probably a way—Maren's well-house, the watchman's card debts, some clever thread through the city's bones. She could wake it quietly, maybe, dampen it somehow, and they could be miles gone before anyone understood.

And the stone would spend another century smothered. Another century of fishwives kneeling above it, hungry and unnamed. Another century of Oathwardens burning speakers in the dark, unwatched, unquestioned. Another winter creeping closer while everyone agreed, politely, officially, that nothing was happening.

Cleverness had kept her hidden. Hidden had kept her hollow.

"No," she said again, aloud this time, to the stone as much as to herself. "You've been quiet long enough. So have I."

She took off her gloves. She put one hand on the warm stone, beside Wren's small ones, and reached for her voice—not the careful human voice she'd worn like a disguise for thirty years, but the whole of it, beast-speech and bird-cadence and the low ground-hum her mother must have heard the day she carried a baby across the border of silence.

"Wake up," Zephyrine sang. "They're kneeling right above you. Come up and meet them."

Wren began to sing.

The stone answered.

Light poured up through the spiral channels and out—through the roots, through the rock, through six feet of consecrated masonry—and far above them, Zephyrine felt rather than heard the cathedral shudder on its ancient pins. Dust sifted down. Somewhere overhead, glass cracked in its leading. The great bell, silent for the hour, swung once against its own weight and tolled—a deep, wrong, wonderful note that no rope had pulled.

And through the floor, rising like water finding its level, came the sound of four hundred voices in the nave above—crying out, shouting, praying, and beneath all of it, unmistakably, *wondering*.

Maren started laughing helplessly in the ember-light. "Congratulations," she called over the rising song. "You've just committed heresy, trespass, vandalism, and possibly treason, in alphabetical order by witness count."

"Not heresy," Zephyrine said. Her hand was burning gently where it touched the stone, and she found she didn't mind. "Testimony. There's a difference."

"There will be when Vane writes the charges!"

Above them, the bell tolled again, and again, gathering speed, ringing the whole city awake—and down in the warm gold dark, the third oath-stone opened its eyes at last, and began, in a voice like a hearth the size of the horizon, to tell everyone in Ferrow's Cross exactly what they had been kneeling on.

Zephyrine closed her eyes and let her own voice pour into it, witnessed and witnessing, hiding from no one.

Which was, of course, precisely when the crypt door at the top of the stairs banged open, and boots came down—many boots, in step, armored, unhurried, already hers.

Vane had not needed to hunt them at all. Zephyrine had rung the bell herself.
