## 25. Sung Out Loud

The crypt beneath the Cathedral of Saint Ferrow smelled of candle tallow, cold stone, and—faintly, unmistakably—the sea, though Ferrow's Cross sat a hundred miles from any coast. Zephyrine noticed that last smell and understood it at once for what it was: memory. The stone remembered water. Stone remembered everything. That was rather the whole problem, and the whole point.

"Stay close," she whispered to Wren.

Wren, naturally, did not stay close. The child slipped ahead between the pillars like a fish released from a bucket, bare feet silent on flags worn smooth by nine centuries of mourners' knees. Zephyrine had long ago stopped being surprised by this. She had also long ago stopped being surprised by her own fear, which rose now in the familiar gray wave and which she set aside the way one sets aside a heavy pack before crawling under a fence: reluctantly, knowing she would have to pick it up again on the other side.

Above them, separated by one vaulted ceiling and several tons of deliberate forgetting, the evening service went on. Zephyrine could hear it as a mumble, a tide of voices rising and falling in practiced rhythm. Hundreds of people. She had counted the shoes at the doors—she could not help counting; thirty years of avoiding attention taught a person to count exits and shoes—and there had been enough shoes for three hundred souls, at least.

Three hundred witnesses, if things went well.

Three hundred accusers, if they didn't.

"There." Wren's hands flashed the sign they had made together, *here-here-here*, fingers spiraling. In the gloom at the crypt's far end, beneath a window of blue glass gone nearly black with night, stood something that was not a tomb.

Every other monument down here announced itself. Bishops slept under marble slabs carved with their own pious faces; knights lay atop stone horses frozen mid-charge; one wealthy merchant had commissioned an effigy of himself so smug that Zephyrine felt an immediate urge to deface it. But the ninth alcove—no, the *third* alcove, Maren had corrected her twice, and Zephyrine kept miscounting out of some superstitious reluctance to be right—held nothing but a plain slab of unremarkable granite, mortared into the wall as if someone had built a wall over a door and hoped everyone would forget there had ever been a door.

Someone had. Someone had done exactly that.

Mortar, though, was no match for frost that bled upward from below. A fine lace of ice had grown through the joints, glittering faintly, pushing the stones apart by the width of a knife blade, then two. It was the same weeping frost Maren had described at the old shrines: the land trying to cough up something swallowed.

Wren pressed both palms flat against the granite and looked back at Zephyrine with those deer-bright eyes, and the look in them was not eagerness. Zephyrine had learned to read the child's face the way she read weather, and what she saw there was the same thing Zephyrine herself felt: the particular dread of standing before something you cannot take back.

"I know," Zephyrine said softly. "Me too."

* * *

They had argued about it that morning, in the rented room above the dye-shop, in the low urgent voices of people who have learned that walls are hired informants.

"We don't have to do it like this," Zephyrine had said. "We could wait for the service to empty. We could come at dawn. We could—" She heard herself listing clevernesses and stopped. Thirty years of cleverness. Thirty years of entering valleys through gaps in hedges and trading herbs through intermediaries and never once letting her shadow fall where it might be measured.

Maren, folding her herbs into her satchel with the brisk tenderness of a woman packing for other people's emergencies, had not even looked up. "You could come at dawn," she agreed. "And wake it quiet, and slip away, and then what? Four stones burning and the whole world still asleep to it. Vane finds the fifth before you do and shatters it while you're hiding in some hedgerow composing your next apology." She tied off the satchel. "Child, I spent eleven years in exile for brewing fever-tea that worked *too* well. You know what I learned? Hiding doesn't keep you safe. Hiding just means that when they come for you, nobody knows your name to shout in protest."

"That's very grim for a healer."

"I'm very grim for an exile. It's in the job description."

And Wren—Wren had simply taken Zephyrine's hand and put it flat against their own small chest, over the heart, where the seal-light pulsed these days like a second pulse, dimmer than it had been in Ashmoor, dimmer every week. *Now*, the gesture said. Not tonight, not at dawn, not when convenient. The child was burning. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it, the way nobody says *the roof is on fire* while deciding whose boots to put on first.

So: now. Evening service. Three hundred pairs of shoes.

"The choir's good, at least," Maren had said at the door, which was her way of saying *go, and I'll be watching from the back, and if it goes wrong I will cause such a diversion as saints will sing about.* "Though in my professional opinion their bass is flat."

* * *

The crypt air grew colder as they approached the slab, cold in the old way, the way of the high forest and the glacier cave and every sleeping place where something vast waited with more patience than urgency. Zephyrine's breath fogged. Frost feathered across her sleeves.

She knelt. Her knees found the grooves worn by other kneeling pilgrims, centuries of them, though whatever they had come to pray to had been walled up and renamed and prayed-over-by-proxy. Perhaps they had been praying correctly all along without knowing it. That thought comforted her unexpectedly. People were better than their walls. Most of them, most days.

Wren knelt beside her and began to sing.

There is no use describing the true tongue to ears that haven't heard it. Zephyrine had spent her whole life inside it and still couldn't say whether it resembled birdsong or river-speech or the sound of snow settling, only that it was the sound of something *meaning to be heard*. Wren's voice was thin—they had been singing too much, spending themselves too fast—but the stone didn't care about volume. The stone cared about truth.

Light came up through the granite like dawn seen from underwater.

It came slowly at first, veins of gold spreading through the dark slab the way lamplight spreads through a shuttered room when someone finally lifts the bar. Zephyrine put her hands against the stone and felt it breathe—a vast, slow inhalation, nine hundred years held and now released.

Then the mortar cracked. All of it. At once.

The sound was not loud, exactly, but it carried, the way a snapped branch carries in winter woods. Above them the service stumbled. The mumble of voices faltered mid-line, and in the sudden hush Zephyrine heard the other thing: hundreds of individual silences, each person separately deciding whether they had imagined it.

Wren sang louder. The slab slid. And light—

Oh, the light.

It did not leak. It *poured*, up through the fissure, up through the floor of the nave itself, gold-white and green-shot like sun through new leaves, filling the cathedral above them the way water fills a bowl. Even down here in the crypt, Zephyrine saw the glow climb the pillars, saw dust ignite into drifting sparks, heard—for the first time in the open, unhidden, at full strength—the sound of an oath-stone remembering itself.

It sang back to Wren. That was the part that undid her. The child had been singing alone, always alone, the last voice of a murdered covenant, and now the stone answered in harmony, deep and slow, a mountain joining a wren, and Wren's face turned up into the light with an expression Zephyrine had never seen on them before and recognized anyway because she'd been wearing its twin inwardly for thirty years:

Joy at being answered.

Zephyrine opened her mouth and joined them.

She hadn't planned to. But her voice went before her courage did, the old speech pouring out of her rough and rusty and then—there was no other word—*glad*, and the stone took her voice and Wren's voice and braided them, and the light surged, and somewhere above, three hundred people began to scream, or pray, or possibly both.

* * *

"They're coming down," Maren called from the stair, and there was no alarm in it, only information, the way you'd announce rain.

Boots on stone. Many boots. Voices tangling—fear, awe, the specific panic of clergy discovering their foundations have opinions. Zephyrine could have run. There was a servant's passage behind the third alcove—Maren had found it, because Maren found everything—and in the confusion and the light and the general collapse of ordinary reality, two figures and one herb-wife could plausibly have melted away, and tomorrow they'd be rumors, and rumors could be survived. Rumors were survivable. She'd survived on less for decades.

Wren's hand found hers. The child looked up, light washing their face gold, and asked nothing. That was the terrible thing about Wren. The child never asked. They only stood at doors and let you see which ones mattered.

Zephyrine thought of her mother, carrying a baby across the border at dusk, choosing silence, paying for it with a life spent underground as an anchor, dying nameless in a valley designed to forget. She thought of Bramble saying, in that maddening placid goat-way, *the quiet is thinning,* and how Zephyrine had dismissed her, and how the goat had been righter than any book in any library. She thought of Tobias Grell's ledger, of Serah Vane's righteous ink, of wolves asking politely whether a child was *the last word.*

She thought: *I am so tired of surviving.*

The first clerics came around the pillar with candles held up like weapons, and stopped as if they'd walked into glass. Light everywhere. Frost blooming up the walls in fern-patterns. An old woman kneeling in the ruins of a wall with her hands full of gold fire, a strange bright child beside her, both of them singing.

Zephyrine rose. Her legs shook. She let them.

She stopped singing—there was no need anymore; the stone had it now, would carry the note through the night and the century—and faced the crowd pressing down the stairs. Priests. Sacristans. A bishop in half-fastened vestments who had clearly run the whole way, and behind him, spilling down the steps and crowding the arches, ordinary people: the baker with flour still on his forearms, the dyer whose rooms they'd rented, a girl holding somebody's forgotten hymnal like a shield.

They stared at her. She stared back.

Her whole body screamed the old instructions: *down, small, silent, gone.* Thirty years of instructions, written into her shoulders and her step and the way she held her chin. She stood in the middle of all that screaming habit and did none of it. She stayed standing. She let herself be tall, which for Zephyrine meant approximately nothing in inches and everything otherwise.

"Good evening," she said, in the common tongue, because it seemed important to be ordinary about it.

Nobody answered. Somewhere near the back a woman fainted, and was caught, and propped up, and fainted again with slightly more commitment.

"You've been praying to this for nine hundred years," Zephyrine said, gesturing at the blazing stone. "I thought someone should introduce you properly."

The bishop found his voice. It came out thinner than his vestments. "What—what *is* it?"

"It's an oath," Zephyrine said. "The oldest one. Your ancestors helped make it, and then their grandchildren got embarrassed about it and bricked it up." She looked around at the faces, hundreds of them now, packed along the crypt arches, lit gold from below like a crowd of lanterns. Her heart was going like a rabbit's, and she discovered, with genuine surprise, that she was smiling. "It keeps the wild and the human worlds bound to each other. Memory for memory. Voice for voice. When it sleeps, you forget. When it wakes—" she spread her hands, taking in the frost, the light, the terrified holiness of the scene, "—you're looking at it."

"And you?" someone called. "What are *you*?"

And there it was. The question she had spent her whole life dreading, asked by a stranger with flour on his arms, in a crypt, under a cathedral, with nowhere left to hide and no reason left to want to.

"My name is Zephyrine Winterbourne," she said, and heard her own voice crack on the last syllable of it, the first time in thirty years anyone had heard her full name spoken aloud, including her. "I speak beast-speech. I was born with it. My mother hid me in a valley that forgets, and I hid myself there longer than she asked, because I was afraid. And this child—" she put her hand on Wren's shoulder, and Wren leaned into it, glowing, "—is the last living seal of the covenant. We are waking the oath-stones before midwinter. This is the third. There are five more sleeping, four already destroyed, and there are people coming to destroy the rest."

"Why are you telling us?" The bishop again, hoarse. "You could have fled."

"Yes," Zephyrine agreed. "I'm extremely good at fleeing. Decades of practice. But a thing hidden can be shattered, and a thing witnessed—" she looked at their faces, all of them, memorizing them the way the stone memorized everything, "—a thing witnessed has to be reckoned with. So. Witness us."

Silence, of the enormous kind.

Then the girl with the hymnal—she couldn't have been more than twelve—said, in the ringing confident tones of someone solving a problem that has stumped adults, "Well, obviously we can't let the soldiers break it. It's *in our church.*"

A murmur of agreement, startled and growing, rolling up the stairs toward the nave. Zephyrine watched it move through the crowd and understood she was seeing something rarer than magic: people deciding, quickly and together, that the story they had just been handed was theirs now too.

Maren appeared at her elbow, smelling of rosemary and satisfaction. "Told you," she murmured. "Names get shouted."

* * *

They left the cathedral by the front doors, which seemed only correct. The crowd parted for them, touching Wren's hair as they passed—not grabbing, just touching, fingertips to light, the way you touch a reliquary. Wren walked through the gauntlet of hands with their chin up, drinking it in, and Zephyrine realized the child had never once in their life been touched gently by a stranger in numbers. The seal-child had been hunted, hidden, translated, guarded. Never *welcomed.*

Above the rooftops, the sky was clear and hard and the stars stood out like nailheads. Midwinter was eleven days away. Somewhere north, Serah Vane was sharpening her authority into something with edges, and somewhere behind them, Tobias Grell's account was already in a courier's saddlebag, and the fourth stone waited in a glacier that tested honesty, and the fifth, and the sixth—

But tonight, in the square outside the cathedral, three hundred people stood in the falling snow arguing loudly about oaths, and the baker was insisting he'd known something like this would happen since the frost got into his spring well, and the bishop had given up on dignity entirely and was asking Wren questions through Maren's translations with tears running freely into his beard.

Zephyrine stood at the edge of it, half-in, half-out of the torchlight, and let herself feel it: not triumph, nothing so clean. Just the strange, vertiginous lightness of a woman who has spent thirty years being invisible, standing in a pool of light, in full view, with her name attached to her.

It fit badly, like shoes bought for a foot that had grown.

She'd grow into it. She'd have to. Because down at the far end of the square, where the lamplight thinned, a grey-cloaked rider sat motionless on a grey mare, watching. He had been there awhile. He had seen everything.

He turned his horse, unhurried, and rode north, and Zephyrine watched him go and understood that the age of whispers was over for everyone—hers, and Vane's alike.

"Wren," she said quietly. "Get your coat. I think we've just become official."
