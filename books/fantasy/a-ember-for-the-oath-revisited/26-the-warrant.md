## 26. The Warrant

The paper arrived at dawn, and Serah Vane read it three times before she allowed herself to feel anything at all.

That was discipline. Her father had taught her that much before the wild took everything else: *feel it after, girl. Feel it after you've done what needs doing.* He had been a tanner with hands like cracked leather and no patience for weeping, and she had loved him in the way one loves a wall — not warmly, but leaning against it.

The warrant was written in the high hand of the Chancery at Ferrow's Cross, all flourishes and wax, and it said, in language so careful it practically tiptoed:

*Whereas an irregularity of silence has been observed in the district of Hollowmere and its approaches; whereas evidence has been presented of unlicensed speech between persons and beasts; whereas a public manifestation of pre-Covenant phenomena occurred within the cathedral precincts of Ferrow's Cross on the eve of Saint Aldric's Mass; be it known that the matter is hereby declared heretic-adjacent, and full authority of suppression is granted to Warden-Commander Serah Vane, with power of levy, requisition, and judgment.*

Heretic-adjacent. She almost laughed. Somewhere in the Chancery, some ink-stained clerk had spent an entire morning deciding how frightened to sound without frightening anyone, and had arrived at *heretic-adjacent* the way a man might describe a wolf as *dog-adjacent.*

But it was enough. More than enough.

Full authority. Power of levy. Judgment.

She set the paper down on the campaign table, weighted its corners with four smooth stones she kept for exactly this purpose — plain river stones, nothing more, though her lieutenants had long ago developed a superstition about them that she saw no reason to correct — and looked out the window of the requisitioned house that served as her headquarters.

Ferrow's Cross lay below in the grey light, its rooftops smoking gently, its cathedral spire still bearing, if one knew where to look, a faint scorch-bloom of light-stain across the northern face of the tower. The masons had been at it for two days with ladders and vinegar and would be at it for a month. Light did not scrub out. Whatever had poured up through the nave that night had left its signature in the very stone, the way lightning leaves a fern on a wall.

The whole town was talking about it. That was the trouble with miracles. They were terribly bad at keeping quiet.

"Commander?" Lieutenant Hask stood in the doorway with his helmet under his arm and his usual expression of a man who has been asked to carry something heavy and is still negotiating with himself about whether to complain. "The priests are asking again whether they may resume services in the nave."

"Tell them they may resume services in the nave," Vane said, "the moment my sappers confirm there is nothing under the nave but dead men and older stones."

"They won't like that."

"Their congregation sang hymns over a sleeping oath-stone for two hundred years," Vane said. "They can survive two more weeks of inconvenience. Gratitude is not a virtue I expect from the Church, but patience used to be."

Hask lingered. He was good at lingering; it was half his function, like a dog that nudges your elbow when the stew is burning. "There's also the matter of the woman," he said. "The herb-seller. From the market day."

"Maren." Vane did not look up from the map she was unrolling. "Ashmoor. Exiled hedge-healer. Yes."

"She was seen on the north road yesterday. With soldiers' tracks behind her — ours. And she wasn't running."

Vane's quill paused above the map.

Maren of Ashmoor. She had a file on the woman going back eleven years: unlicensed physicking, unlicensed gathering, unlicensed *knowing*, which was the crime the Chancery found hardest to name and easiest to hang. A quiet woman. Careful. The sort who survived exile by being useful to everyone and memorable to no one.

And now she had let herself be seen on a road, walking toward a patrol, not away from it.

"That," Vane said slowly, "is either surrender or strategy."

"Which do you think?"

"I think," Vane said, marking the bridge at Callow Ford with a small, neat X, "that healers make poor martyrs and excellent delays. Put a rider on the ford road. If my men have taken her, I want her alive, comfortable, and talked to. If they haven't taken her yet—" she tapped the X, "—they will find her standing on that bridge looking harmless, and they should ask themselves why a clever woman would choose the one place an army must slow down."

Hask wrote it down. He always wrote things down; it was why she tolerated his lingering.

"And the woman herself?" he asked. "The speaker. With the child."

Vane finally looked up.

She had been hunting Zephyrine Winterbourne — though the name had only come to her three days ago, prised out of Tobias Grell, a man whose soul appeared to be priced by the pound and sold accordingly — since the hawk incident on the King's road. Weeks now. She had tracked her through market rumors and frost-bleeding shrines and a stretch of forest where her own horses had refused, flatly and unanimously, to go further. She had built a portrait of the woman piece by piece the way she built every case: the herb-trades, the double rations, the neighbor's ledger, the goat.

The goat had been Grell's contribution, delivered with the smugness of a man who believes he has sold gold and does not realize he has sold a map to his own grave. *She talks to animals,* Grell had whispered, delighted with himself. *I heard her. Out past the treeline. Talking to wolves like they were neighbors complaining about a fence.*

Wolves. Vane had sent scouts to the treeline. The scouts reported wolf sign everywhere and wolves nowhere, which was either very good luck or very good manners, and Vane did not believe in either.

"She's heading for the remaining stones," Vane said. "Four are awake now — we have reports of blooming snow in the high country, rivers making noise, farmers swearing their cattle answered back. Which means five sleep. Or fewer." She straightened. "She woke the one under the cathedral because she had no choice; it was under our noses and she needed it. But the rest are in wild places. Glacier. Sea-cliff. Places where an army cannot follow quickly."

"So we get there first."

"We get there first," Vane agreed, "and we do not wait for her."

* * *

The levying took six days, and it was the strangest work of her career, because for once nobody argued.

When Vane had hunted quietly — a dozen wardens in grey cloaks, questions asked softly at farm gates — she had been an expense, a rumor, a thing the districts paid for grudgingly the way one pays for rat-catching. Now she rode through villages with a warrant sealed in red and a column of two hundred behind her, and the same farmers who had once given her stale bread and shorter answers brought out their sons, their sledges, their winter stores, and their very best bread, still warm.

It unsettled her more than resistance would have.

"They think we're protecting them," Hask observed, watching a miller's daughter press a charm — a perfectly ordinary knot-charm, wheat-colored, harmless — into the hand of one of his troopers as if blessing him off to war.

"They think we're protecting them," Vane confirmed.

"Aren't we?"

Vane considered the question honestly, because she tried to consider all questions honestly; lies were for people who couldn't afford the truth, and she had never been able to afford anything else.

"Thirty-one years ago," she said, "in the village of Thornhollow, the covenant failed. Not broke — *failed*. Stretched too thin, worn through, whatever pretty word the scholars prefer. The wild came through the gap." She folded the warrant along its old creases, open and shut, open and shut. "I was nine. I watched the mist come down off the fells, and it wasn't weather. It was *looking* at us. Three children walked into it that night — walked, Hask, smiling — and were never found. My sister was one of them."

Hask had heard the story before. Everyone in the wardens had; it was told to recruits the way other regiments told stories of famous battles. But he had never heard it in her voice, which usually kept such things in a locked room, and he had the sense now to say nothing.

"The silence saved us," Vane said. "The Oathwardens, the wards, the law of quiet — it saved everyone who was left. And everyone who was left decided, being human, that the saving had been their idea, and got on with forgetting. So yes. I believe we protect them. From something they've made themselves too comfortable to remember."

"And the child?" Hask asked carefully. "The seal-child. Some of the men are saying — well. There's talk. That it's just a child. That it looks at you like—"

"Like a child," Vane finished. "Yes. I know."

She knew better than anyone. She had seen the child exactly once, at distance, through a spyglass at the ford ambush — a small figure throwing itself in front of a taller one, arms spread, the way children shield things they love. The image had visited her at inconvenient hours since. She had filed it under *enemy propaganda, self-generated*, which was where she filed most things that kept her awake.

"It is a child," she said. "It is also the last living hinge of a door I intend to nail shut forever. Both things are true, and I am not required to enjoy either of them." She stood, and began buckling on her sword belt with the crisp economy of thirty years' practice. "Sound the assembly. We march for the glacier within the hour."

"The glacier? In this season?"

"The fourth stone sleeps in the ice, and the woman is already climbing toward it. Every day we deliberate, she wakes another." Vane pulled on her gloves. "Besides — I want to see this mountain that remembers. I want to see whether it flinches."

* * *

It did not flinch.

That was the report that reached her eight days later, carried by a scout so frostbitten he had to dictate his account through lips that moved like a puppet's. The army had reached the glacier's foot to find the cave mouth open, the mountain's ancient voice still rumbling faintly in the stone like a bell that had been struck days ago — and the woman and the child already descending the far side, a fourth light burning behind them in the ice like a lamp in a green bottle.

And the snow around the cave, the scout insisted, crossing himself with a mittened hand, had been full of flowers.

"Flowers," Vane repeated.

"Through the snow, Commander. Blooming. Little blue ones. The men wouldn't camp near them."

"Because they're flowers."

"Because they're *wrong*, Commander."

Vane dismissed him to the surgeons and stood a long while at the mouth of her command tent, looking up at the glacier. It caught the late sun and held it, blue-white and patient, and somewhere inside it a stone was awake now, and a woman who should have been a footnote in a warden's ledger was becoming, report by report, something else entirely.

Four stones. The land remembering. Rivers singing — actual singing, attested by three separate garrison commanders, one of whom had requested transfer on grounds of spiritual distress.

And each waking cost the child. Maren — before her capture, in the interrogations Vane had conducted personally, gently, over tea the prisoner had complimented with genuine surprise — had let slip that much, wrapped in doctrine and deflection. *A seal burns, Commander. That is what seals are for. Wax takes the impression by being pressed. Ask yourself what presses on a living seal, and how much of it remains.*

Vane had told herself the woman was using the child. It was a comfortable story. It fit the warrant.

But Maren had also said — in the flat, tired voice of a woman past caring what her captor believed — *"Go and watch them together sometime. Watch the woman carry her when she tires. Then tell me who is using whom."*

Vane had ordered the interrogation log sealed. She told herself that was procedure.

* * *

On the last night before the march to the coast, she sat alone with the map and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding.

Five stones had slept. Four were awake. One remained — the ninth, the first, the altar on the sea-cliff where, according to every text Maren could recite and every fragment the Chancery archives surrendered, the original covenant had been sworn by nine seal-children and a king who had since become the kind of story nurses tell to make children behave.

One stone. One child. One woman who had gone from fugitive to legend in a single season, and an army — *her* army — growing larger and slower and more frightened with every mile, because soldiers will march through hell for pay but they balk noticeably at flowers.

And midwinter was nine days away.

Vane spread her hands flat on the map, on the little painted coastline, on the mark she had made weeks ago at World's Edge without quite knowing why it mattered, and admitted to herself, in the privacy of her own skull, the thing she had been circling for a month:

The woman was going to win.

Not might. Was. Unless Vane stopped being a hunter and became something else — something with weight, with mass, with the full terrible momentum of the law behind it. Unless she stopped sending riders and started moving the whole apparatus, warrant and levy and judgment, and put it all between one exhausted woman, one failing child, and one cliff at the edge of the world.

She thought of her sister walking into the mist, smiling. She thought of a child spreading its arms in front of someone it loved.

Both things are true, she reminded herself. You are not required to enjoy either of them.

Vane rolled up the map, snuffed the lamp, and stepped out into the frozen dark where two hundred men were breaking camp by torchlight, breath steaming, harness jingling, an army assembling itself around her conviction the way iron filings assemble around a magnet.

"Hask," she called. "We ride for the coast. Full column. And send a fast courier ahead to World's Edge — I want the cliff road watched, provisioned, and *cleared* of pilgrims. Anyone found traveling toward the altar is to be detained gently and fed well." She pulled her cloak tight. "If we're going to end a covenant that's older than the kingdom, we'll do it in daylight, by lawful order, with no starving civilians in the way when it breaks."

"And if the woman gets there first?"

Vane looked east, where beyond the dark hills the sky had begun, very faintly, to glow — not dawn, dawn was hours off, but something rising over the far mountains like heat-shimmer in reverse, like the world remembering color.

"She won't," Vane said. "She's carrying the child now. I've read the reports. She carries her up every hill."

She swung into the saddle.

"But order the courier to ride as if the kingdom depends on it," she added quietly, to no one in particular. "Because for once in my life, Hask, I suspect it actually might."
