## 27. Maren's Sacrifice

The bridge over the River Sennet was older than the road it carried, older than the kingdom that had named the road, and — according to Maren — considerably more sensible than either.

"Stone doesn't care who's king," she said, thumping her boot on the parapet. "That's why I trust bridges and distrust governments."

Zephyrine leaned against the cold rail and watched the water run brown and quick beneath them, carrying slush and the first honest light of morning. Behind them, three days east, the mountain waited with its fourth stone. Ahead of them, downriver and closing fast, came Serah Vane's vanguard — forty riders, maybe fifty, moving hard since dawn.

Wren sat cross-legged in the middle of the bridge, feeding crumbs to a moorhen that had waddled up out of the reeds as if it had been expecting them. The child's color had come back a little since Ashmoor, but not all of it. Zephyrine had stopped pretending she didn't notice. Every stone they woke took something. Wren never complained; Wren simply grew quieter, the way a candle grows shorter.

"You're doing the face," Maren said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've decided something terrible and you're hoping I won't notice." Maren folded her arms. She was a small woman built like a knot in old timber, grey-haired, sharp-tongued, with hands that smelled permanently of yarrow and woodsmoke. Three days of travel had not softened her. Zephyrine suspected nothing would, short of burial, and even then she'd probably complain about the soil quality. "So. Say it out loud, then. It's always worse unsaid."

Zephyrine looked downriver. The mist there was the wrong kind of thick for the hour, and under it, faint but unmistakable to her ears now — ears that heard everything, whether she wanted them to or not — came the sound of hooves keeping formation. Not scattered. Deliberate. A fist of men and horses, punching up the valley road toward the pass.

"They'll reach the bridge by midmorning," she said. "We can't outrun them to the mountain. Not with Wren tired. Not with the snow deepening past the treeline."

"So we don't outrun them," Maren said. "We make them *not arrive*."

"Maren—"

"I'm not finished." She held up one finger, the same finger she used when dosing out bitter medicine to patients who wanted sweet. "There's a ford two miles south where the river braids through the marsh. If someone were to, say, encourage the water to be a bit less cooperative — raise the level, soften the banks, give the horses somewhere unpleasant to put their feet — a column of fifty riders might lose half a day untangling itself. Might lose more than that if the person encouraging the water was any good at hedge-work."

"You'd be alone with them."

"I'd be *hidden* with them," Maren corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is a willow thicket and thirty years of knowing how not to be seen. I hid from wardens before you were born, girl. I'm very good at being nobody."

Zephyrine turned to face her fully. "And if they find you?"

Maren shrugged, which was its own kind of answer, and Zephyrine hated it.

"No," she said. "No. We go together. We find another way around—"

"There is no other way around. I've been looking at this map since midnight, and I've had sixty years to look at this country besides. There is this bridge, that ford, and the pass. That's the whole menu." Maren's voice gentled, which was somehow worse than the sharpness. "Zeph. Listen to me. You are carrying something the world has needed for thirty years and didn't know to ask for. I am carrying a satchel of dried herbs and opinions. One of us is replaceable."

"That's not—" Zephyrine's throat tightened. "You pulled an arrowhead out of Wren's shoulder with a fishhook and a prayer. You walked us through the marsh fever. You sang to me the night after the ambush when I couldn't stop shaking, and you pretended afterward it hadn't happened, because you knew I needed it to have not happened. That is not a list of replaceable things."

For a moment Maren said nothing. The river ran. The moorhen squabbled with Wren over the last crumb and lost.

"Well," Maren said finally, gruffly, "someone had to do those things, and everyone else available was busy being prophesied at." She reached out and gripped Zephyrine's wrist, her hand dry and strong as bark. "Here is what will happen. I'll work the ford from the willows. When the riders bog down, I'll slip north along the riverbank, cut across the sheep paths, and meet you at the shepherd's hut below the glacier mouth. Two days. Three if the weather turns spiteful. I've survived spiteful weather my whole life; it's people I've never gotten the hang of."

"And if it goes wrong?"

"Then it goes wrong, and you keep walking, and you do not — do you hear me — *do not* turn back. Not for me. Not for anyone. The stones don't wait, and neither does midwinter." Her grip tightened. "Swear it."

"I don't want to swear it."

"Nobody ever does. That's what makes it worth something." 

* * *

They argued for another hour, which changed nothing, because Maren had done her arguing while Zephyrine slept and had already made her decision; everything since had merely been ceremony. Hedge-witches, Zephyrine was learning, were like rivers: they appeared gentle right up until you tried to redirect them.

They parted at the fork below the bridge. Maren pressed a packet into Zephyrine's hand — feverfew, willow bark, and a twist of dried lavender "for the nights your own head gets too loud" — and then, unexpectedly, hugged her. Briefly, fiercely, like slamming a door.

"Mind the child," she said into Zephyrine's ear. "Mind yourself. And mind that goat of yours — Bramble knows more than she says, and she says plenty."

Then she was gone, down the southern path with her satchel and her stick, a small grey figure growing smaller against the grey morning, until the mist took her and Zephyrine had to stand there a long moment convincing her feet that forward was still a direction.

"She'll be fine," Zephyrine told Wren.

Wren looked up at her with those deer-bright eyes, and did not say anything, because Wren rarely wasted words on things that weren't true. Then the child took her hand, and they climbed.

* * *

The shepherd's hut sat in a fold of the hillside like a stone someone had dropped and forgotten, and by dusk Zephyrine had built a fire small enough to hide in a hearth and large enough to matter. Bramble, who had refused every attempt to be left behind and had walked out of Hollowmere like a creditor attending a debtor's funeral, stood in the doorway chewing thoughtfully and watching the dark.

Below them, far off, the river country lay under cloud. Occasionally the wind brought sounds up the slope: a dog barking, once; the creak of some distant cart; and twice — twice — a sound Zephyrine couldn't name, a low thrumming hum, like a struck bell underwater.

"That's her," she murmured. "That's the water working."

Bramble chewed. *Hedge-magic,* she observed, in the tone of a craftsman appraising another's joinery. *Small stitches. But a hundred small stitches hold better than one grand seam.*

"I know. I know that." Zephyrine fed the fire a stick. "Knowing doesn't help."

*It never does,* Bramble agreed. *That's what makes it knowledge instead of comfort.*

Wren had fallen asleep sitting up, the way the child often did lately, sleep taking them mid-thought like a debt collector. Zephyrine eased them down onto the straw pallet, tucked the blanket to their chin, and sat back down by the fire to do the thing she was worst at in all the world, worse even than lying: waiting.

She counted the hours by the fire's appetite. Somewhere past midnight the thrumming from the valley stopped. She told herself that meant the work was done. She told herself a great many things, and the fire ate them all without comment.

Near dawn, hoofbeats.

Not many — four, five, moving slow along the ridge road, torches swinging. Zephyrine killed the fire with a sweep of ash and pulled Wren behind the woodpile, heart hammering against her ribs like a bird in a sack. The riders passed close enough that she could smell the leather and the iron. One of them laughed at something. Ordinary laughter. That was the worst part — how ordinary evil sounded when it wasn't aimed at you yet.

Then they were gone down the slope, toward the river, toward the ford, and Zephyrine lay awake in the ash-smelling dark and understood exactly what direction they were going, and swore at herself silently for every promise she'd made.

*Do not turn back.* 

She lay there until the sky went the color of dishwater, and then she got up, and packed, and did not turn back, and hated every step of it.

* * *

They gained the high path by midmorning, Wren riding on Zephyrine's back in a sling of blankets, lighter than a child should be. The world up here was white and enormous and silent in the old way — the true silence of altitude, not the manufactured hush of Hollowmere — and Zephyrine found, to her surprise, that she no longer flinched from silence. She'd spent thirty years marinating in it. This much, at least, her exile had taught her: how to move quietly, how to listen, how to be patient as stone.

She was listening when the wind shifted and brought her the sound.

Shouting. Far off, down in the river country, thin as thread — human voices, many of them, and under them a crackle that wasn't quite thunder. And threaded through it all, unmistakable, a voice raised not in fear but in *defiance*, pitched high and cracked and absolutely furious, saying words the wind shredded before they arrived but whose shape Zephyrine knew the way you know a friend's silhouette.

*Maren.*

Zephyrine stopped so abruptly that Bramble walked into her haunches and complained.

*Don't,* the goat said quietly.

"I'm not—"

*Your feet stopped. Your heart didn't. Don't.*

Down in the valley, the shouting rose, peaked, and broke apart into the particular ugly rhythm of a crowd doing something to someone. Zephyrine stood on the high path with her fists clenched and her whole body straining southward like a compass needle, and Wren, feeling her tremble through the sling, wrapped small arms around her neck and held on.

*Listen to me,* Bramble said, and came around to stand in front of her, amber eyes level. *If you go down there, you die, the child dies, and Maren's day was bought for nothing. That is the arithmetic. I know it's cruel. Arithmetic usually is.*

"I promised her," Zephyrine whispered.

*Yes. And promises are the only currency she deals in. Honor it.* The goat's voice gentled, as much as a goat's voice can. *Grieve while you walk. Walking and grieving fit together well. Standing still and grieving fit together badly.*

Zephyrine stood still a moment longer anyway. Down in the valley, the noise had faded to nothing, or the wind had turned, or both. Then she turned north, toward the glacier, and walked, and grieved, and the two fit together about as well as Bramble had promised, which was to say: terribly, but endurably.

* * *

They made the shepherd's second hut — a ruin, really, half-swallowed by the moraine — as the light failed. Zephyrine got Wren fed and asleep, then sat in the doorway with her knees drawn up, watching the glacier glow faintly blue in the starlight like something alive holding its breath.

Tomorrow, the mountain. Tomorrow, whatever test it meant to set her, the reliving, the ice and the truth. Tonight there was only this: a satchel of herbs with no owner, a lavender twist she couldn't bring herself to open, and the memory of a voice raised in fury on a bridge she hadn't stayed to defend.

"She knew," Zephyrine said aloud, to no one. "She decided before I woke up. She let me argue so I'd feel like I'd had a choice. That manipulative, stubborn, magnificent old woman."

Behind her, Wren stirred in sleep and murmured something — not the frost-language, not the pre-kingdom tongue, but a small, broken human word, the kind children say in nightmares in every language under the sun.

Zephyrine went inside and lay down beside the child and did not sleep, and outside, high on the glacier, the mountain waited with its long memory, and somewhere south, in a warden camp, an old woman sat bound between soldiers with her chin up and her tongue sharpened, buying time even now, even there — though Zephyrine would not learn that until later, and could not know it tonight.

Tonight she only knew the cost. She laid it out in the dark like herbs on a drying rack, each loss a stem, each stem a name, and understood that the road behind her was paved with them, and the road ahead would want more.

She closed her eyes. In the morning, the mountain.
