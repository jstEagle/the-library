## 28. The Mountain That Remembers

The mountain did not look like a door. That was the first problem.

Zephyrine had imagined—somewhere between the last village and the frozen pass, in the warm hours by the fire when Wren slept against her side—that the fourth oath-stone would be housed in something grander than a wall of blue ice. A shrine, perhaps, like the flooded one where she had first seen her mother's face. A ruin, at least. Some architecture of intention.

Instead there was only the glacier's snout, curling out of the mountain's flank like a tongue of glass, and set into it a cave mouth that the wolves refused to approach.

*Not refuse,* the pack-leader had said, ears flat, breath steaming. *Not-permitted. The mountain is old-talk now. We are too quick for it.*

Wolves, Zephyrine was learning, had an instinct for scale that humans lacked. They knew when a thing was too large for them the way she knew when bread was done—without needing to be told.

She stood at the cave mouth now, Wren's small hand in hers, and listened.

There was a sound coming from inside. Not wind. Not the groan and tick of settling ice, though those were there too, woven through it like thread through cloth. It was deeper—a rhythm so slow she might have called it silence if she hadn't spent thirty years learning what silence actually sounded like. This was not absence. This was patience made audible.

One breath of the mountain took, as far as she could judge, about as long as it takes snow to decide which way it will fall.

"It's awake," Wren said. They had human words now, a handful of them, gathered one by one like dropped coins. *Awake* had been among the first, right after *stones* and *stay*. "It's been awake a long time."

"Yes."

"It wants you."

Zephyrine looked down. In the grey-blue light off the ice, the child's eyes did their deer-thing, catching light that wasn't there. Four stones woken. Five had slept. One of the five was destroyed beyond saving—they had seen its crater from the ridge, a wound in the forest where nothing grew and nothing remembered—and this one, the mountain's stone, was next. Maren's maps said so. The wolves' old stories said so. And the child, apparently, simply knew.

"All right," Zephyrine said, and was surprised to find her voice steady. "Then let's not keep it waiting. Mountains hate that."

"That's not true," Wren said seriously. "Mountains don't hate anything. It takes too long."

* * *

Inside, the cave breathed cold that was somehow not hostile—the cold of a cellar, of a church, of places built to hold things carefully. The walls were ribbed ice over black stone, and far back, past the reach of any lantern, something glowed. Not gold, like the other stones. A deep winter-green, the color of the light under thick ice on a lake, the color Zephyrine had stared into as a girl and thought: *there is a whole world down there and none of it knows I exist.*

They walked. Their footsteps came back to them wrong—too slow, stretched, as if the mountain returned each sound only after considering it.

Halfway in, the glow resolved into the stone.

It stood taller than she did, a seam of green fire running through it like a vein in marble, and around its base the ice had melted into a pool of water so clear it seemed proud of itself. No frost here. No forgetting. The air near the stone tasted of growing things—loam, sap, rain on stone—which was absurd at this altitude and true anyway.

Wren let go of her hand and knelt by the pool, the way they always did near a sleeping stone, folding up small and humming their half-song, the one that woke things halfway.

The stone's vein of light pulsed once.

And then everything changed key.

*Ah,* said the mountain.

It did not say it aloud. There was no sound at all. The word arrived the way a change in pressure arrives—in the joints, in the teeth, in the fluid of the eyes. Zephyrine felt it in the soles of her feet through the ice and understood that she was not hearing a voice; she was being *considered*, and the consideration had weight.

"You woke me early," the mountain said, and each word seemed to arrive a long time after it was meant, like lightning delayed by distance. "I was going to sleep another age. But the seal-child hums, and hums are for waking, and so. Here we are. Hello."

"Hello," Zephyrine said. Her voice came out thin. Beside her, Wren kept humming, patient as water.

"The last four stones woke for the child's song." The pressure of its attention shifted, settled on her fully, and it was like standing under a waterfall made entirely of noticing. "This one is different. This one does not wake for songs. This one wakes for truth. Songs can be sung by anyone. Truth—" the mountain paused, gathering itself, taking another of its geological breaths, "—truth has to be carried to the door."

"I've come a long way," Zephyrine said. "I've carried quite a lot."

"Yes. I know. I have been watching you walk for weeks. You walk like someone apologizing to the ground." A pause the length of a season. "We will begin now."

"Begin wh—"

The years took her.

* * *

The first was easy, and that was the trap.

Year one: she was twenty-three again, newly inside the ward, standing in the cottage that would become hers, saying aloud—to no one, because there was no one—"Well. Here we are." And the words fell out of her mouth and died before they reached the far wall, smothered by the ward's great soft weight, and she laughed at the strangeness of it, and even her laugh came out flattened, like a coin worn smooth.

The memory played through her whole body. She smelled the new plaster. She felt the ache in her feet from walking the valley's circumference three times, looking for a gap, a seam, any flaw in the silence.

Then year two. Year five. Year nine.

They did not rush. That was the terrible courtesy of the thing. The mountain gave her every year at its full length—not the summary version she told herself at night, but the whole slow grain of it. The spring she stopped speaking to the goats because it hurt more than not speaking. The winter she caught herself mouthing words at the market and trained the habit out of her hands. The year Bramble first nudged her at dusk and said, in the small muffled way beast-speech survived even inside the ward, *you're doing it again, living like a held breath*—and she had laughed and scratched his ears and gone on doing exactly that.

Somewhere in the middle years the memories stopped being scenes and became weather. A decade passed through her like fog. She watched herself learn to be invisible so thoroughly that invisibility stopped being a skill and became a personality. She watched herself turn down Tobias Grell's stammering invitation to the harvest dance with such polite blankness that he never asked anyone anything again. She watched herself trade herbs with women whose names she knew perfectly well and pretend, year after year, not to know them.

"Why," she managed, somewhere around year nineteen, her voice echoing strangely off ribs of ice. "Why are you showing me this?"

"To see if you'll lie to me," the mountain said placidly. "Most do. They watch their own lives go by and they tell me it was fine. They tell me they chose it. Choice is such a popular word." Another long pause. Snow decided which way to fall, somewhere above them. "Keep watching."

Year twenty-three: the night she'd sat by the fire practicing beast-speech into the empty dark—she could feel that memory approaching from a long way off, the way you feel a sneeze—and Bramble had said, gravely, *the quiet is thinning*, and she had dismissed him. She watched herself dismiss him. She watched the particular flinch she'd made, the small turning-away of the shoulders, and understood for the first time that it was not the flinch of a woman ignoring a foolish goat.

It was the flinch of a woman who already knew he was right and could not afford to know it.

Year twenty-nine. Year thirty. The crow on the fence, silent. The knock at dusk. Barefoot feet in the snow. Ash spirals on the hearthstone, and the moment—she relived it at full, unbearable brightness—the moment she answered in beast-speech and a strange child burst into tears of relief, and Zephyrine had felt, for one blazing second, *witnessed*, and had immediately been terrified of how much she wanted it.

"There," the mountain said softly. "That one. Tell me about that one."

The years released her.

She was on her knees on the cave floor and did not remember getting there. Wren had stopped humming and knelt beside her, one small hand resting on her shoulder blade, light as a leaf that had decided to stay.

"Tell me," the mountain said, and the words came faster now, almost eager, if a thing that measures time in millennia can be said to be eager. "You watched your life just now. Thirty years, give or take. I showed you all of it honestly. Now you tell it back to me honestly. That is the toll. The stone wakes for truth carried to the door. So carry it."

Zephyrine knelt in the cold clear water-light and found that she did not know where to start, because she had never started. She had rehearsed versions, of course. Everyone who hides rehearses. *My mother hid me here. The law hunted my gift. I had no choice.* All true. All ready.

She opened her mouth to say them.

"My mother put me inside the ward," she said, and it came out steady enough. "To save me from the Oathwardens. She anchored the ward with her own life until it killed her. I was hidden. I was stolen from. The choice wasn't mine."

The mountain said nothing.

The silence went on so long that Zephyrine counted her own heartbeats, then lost count, then heard—truly heard—the difference between the mountain's silence and every other silence she had ever endured. Other silences waited for her to fill them. This one was not waiting. This one simply declined to accept the answer, the way stone declines to accept rain, without anger, without argument, by being fundamentally elsewhere on the question.

Her eyes burned.

"But," she said.

*But,* agreed the mountain, in the tone of someone who has been holding a door open for a very long time.

"She hid me," Zephyrine said. "For one year. Maybe two. The wardens searched Hollowmere in that first winter and found nothing, and moved on, and the search ended. She could have sent for me after. She could have smuggled me out to some other place, some place with sky in it. Or she could have come in, lived beside me, taught me—something, anything, how to be what I am without drowning in it. She had time. She had years." Her voice cracked and she let it, because the mountain had asked for truth and a cracked voice is at least an honest shape. "She stayed outside. And I stayed inside. For thirty years."

"Do you know why you stayed?" the mountain asked. Gently, for a thing the size of a country.

"Because it was safe—"

"No," said the mountain. "That is the reason people give. Try again."

The green light pulsed. In the pool, the reflection of the stone shivered, and in it—for just a heartbeat—Zephyrine saw not ice and rock but a younger woman sitting alone at a hearth, mouth pressed shut, hands folded, guarding her silence the way other people guard treasure.

And she saw the truth of it, and it was so simple it was obscene.

"Because inside the ward I couldn't hear the beasts," she whispered. "And they couldn't hear me. And if no one could hear me, then I never had to find out—" The words came up from somewhere older than the hiding, older than the ward. "If I spoke, out there, with my whole voice—if I was witnessed—and the world still turned me away, still feared me, still hunted me—then it would mean the fault was mine. That I was the thing they said. But as long as I stayed where my voice couldn't exist, I never had to test it. I told myself I was protecting my gift. I was protecting my *excuse*. My mother stole one year of my life and I used it to steal the other twenty-nine from myself."

The cave was very quiet. Even the ice had stopped ticking.

"There," the mountain said. "That is the toll. Paid in full."

The stone's vein of light swelled—from winter-green to a green like the first impossible leaf of thaw—and warmth rolled out of it across the cave floor, and Zephyrine felt the oath-stone turning over in its long sleep, feeling for the surface, beginning to rise toward waking.

But it did not wake.

The light steadied at half, hovering, the exact halfway point where Wren's songs had always left the others—and held there, waiting.

"Why has it stopped?" Wren asked, hushed.

"Because it is honest too," the mountain rumbled. "It knows it is not finished with her. Stone wakes for truth, little seal, but this truth has one more face to show itself, and she has not spoken it yet. She has confessed what she did with her silence." The attention swung back to her, vast and almost kind. "Now tell me what you intend to do about the rest of it. There is a stone yet ahead—the ninth, at world's edge—and it does not want songs and it does not want confessions. It wants a life given. The seal-child burns to feed it, and cannot burn much longer. Unless a speaker binds her own voice into the oath instead. Voice forever. Human words never again." A pause, brief for it, endless for her. "So, Zephyrine Winterbourne. You who chose silence once and called it safety. The mountain asks: would you choose it again? Would you give your voice away—this time knowing exactly what silence costs?"

Behind her, Wren's small hand tightened on her shoulder.

Outside, far below the glacier, along the treeline, the wolves began to howl—not in alarm, Zephyrine realized, but in warning: something was moving up the pass behind them. Something with lanterns, and ledgers, and a warrant.

The mountain waited, patient as only the oldest living thing can be, for her answer.
