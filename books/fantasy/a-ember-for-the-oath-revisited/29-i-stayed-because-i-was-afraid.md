## 29. I Stayed Because I Was Afraid

The mountain did not speak the way Bramble spoke, or the wolves, or even the crows who had begun remembering words back in Hollowmere. The mountain spoke the way glaciers calve—rarely, enormously, and with no particular interest in whether anyone was listening.

Zephyrine heard it as they descended into the blue heart of the ice: a voice so slow that a single word took the length of a spoken sentence, and a full thought could outlast a fire burning down to embers.

*You carry the seal-child.*

"I do," Zephyrine said. Her breath hung in the air like small ghosts. "She's tired. She's given more than anyone should ask of her."

Wren stood beside her at the mouth of the glacier cave, small and pale in the strange blue light, holding Maren's walking staff with both hands the way an old woman holds a fencepost in a gale. Four days on the mountain's flanks had taught Wren the trick of walking on ice; nothing had taught her the trick of being eight years old and burning down to keep the world warm.

*Then you will climb the last stair alone,* the mountain said. *The stone will not wake for me. It has not woken for me in three hundred years. It will only consider a speaker.*

"And if it doesn't consider me?"

*Then you will have climbed a very long way,* the mountain observed, *for nothing.*

There was something almost like humor in it, buried deep under the geological patience. Zephyrine found, to her surprise, that she was not afraid of the mountain. She had been afraid of many things in thirty years—the Oathwardens' grey cloaks, the sound of hoofbeats on the valley road, Tobias Grell's eyes following her through the market—but mountains had never been among them. Mountains were honest. They simply stood there, being what they were, and let the weather say what it liked about them.

"Wait here with Bramble," she told Wren.

Wren's grip on her sleeve tightened. In the old speech, wordless and urgent, the child asked: *If it takes you, how do I call you back?*

"It won't take me," Zephyrine said, and then, because lying to a seal-child seemed like a poor way to enter a place that tested honesty: "I don't know. But I'll come back anyway. That's the whole trick of it."

Bramble butted Zephyrine's hip, which in goat meant approximately *don't be stupid*, and settled herself beside Wren like a woolly sentinel. Zephyrine took one candle stub from her pack—more for the comfort of carrying fire than for light, since the ice glowed faintly all around—and went down.

* * *

The passage narrowed and deepened. The cold changed character as she descended; it stopped being weather and became presence, the cold of a place where snow had fallen before humans had names. The walls of the crevasse were ribbed like the inside of a shell, and embedded in them, far down, she began to see shapes: branches of trees that had frozen mid-growth, a bird's nest with its eggs still whole, and once—a thing that made her stop dead on the ice—a fox, curled as if sleeping, its red coat undimmed by three centuries of winter.

*We keep things,* the mountain said. *It is what we are for.*

"You keep them too well," Zephyrine murmured.

*Everything kept is also lost,* the mountain agreed, and the words fell past her like stones down a well. *That is the question you have brought me. Come. The stair remembers feet.*

There was, impossibly, a stair. Cut steps in the ancient ice, worn smooth, descending in a spiral that followed the crevasse down and down, and each step gave slightly beneath her boot with a creak of compressed centuries. She counted two hundred before she lost the will to count. The candle flame burned straighter the deeper she went, as if the air itself had been waiting undisturbed for someone to breathe it.

And as she walked, the mountain showed her things.

Not visions, exactly—it wasn't the oath-stone's gift-making of memory. It was simpler and crueller than that. The ice around her lit, section by section, and inside each lit panel of frost was a year of her life.

The first panel: Zephyrine at seven years old, standing at her mother's knee while her mother sang a hedge-charm over the beehives, and little Zephie singing it back, note-perfect, and her mother's face going white—not with fear of the girl, Zephyrine understood now, but with fear for her.

*I hid my daughter once,* the memory said, in her mother's voice. *I can hide her again. Hold still, love. This will feel like forgetting.*

The second panel: the crossing of the ward-line, her mother's arms around her, and the moment when the ward closed behind them like water closing over a dropped coin, and the beast-speech drained out of Zephyrine's ears so suddenly she screamed. She remembered screaming. She had thought she would never hear anything true again.

The third panel: Hollowmere. Goats. Herbs. The slow accumulation of a life built entirely of small, careful, forgettable acts. Trading yarrow for salt. Nodding to neighbors. Learning to smile the way people smile who do not wish to be asked questions.

Panel after panel after panel. Thirty years of them, and the mountain played them all without commentary, without mercy, the way a mirror shows you your face when you'd rather it didn't.

*Here,* the mountain said, somewhere around the fifteenth year, *is the year you heard the crow and did not answer.*

She remembered it. Of course she remembered it. A crow on her fence post, tilting its head, opening its beak—and saying nothing, because inside the ward nothing could speak, and she had looked at it and felt the ache rise in her chest and had gone inside and shut the door and baked bread about it.

*Here,* the mountain said, around the twenty-second year, *is the night Bramble first tried to warn you, and you put a pillow over your ears.*

*Bramble speaks so rarely,* young-Zephyrine had mumbled into the pillow. *It's easier not to know.*

*Here is the year the ward first cracked, and you noticed, and told yourself it was settlement-shifting.*

*Here is the year you saw the Oathwardens ride the ridgeline and thanked the dark that you were nobody.*

On and on. And Zephyrine understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what the test was. Not her mother's choice—that had been made for her, made over her, made with love and paid for with her mother's life as the ward's anchor. She had spent the whole road angry about that, and then understanding it, and she had made her peace with it somewhere on the flooded shrine's steps, weeping into the frost-water while the first stone hummed half-awake.

No. The test was the rest of it. The thirty years after her mother died and left her free.

Free to leave. Free to cross the border any day she liked. Free to find the stones, find the wild realm, find whoever else still carried the old gifts—and instead she had stayed. Year after year. Tending goats. Drying herbs. Being invisible, and calling it safety, and calling safety wisdom, until the lie wore smooth as these stairs.

The panels of frost went dark, one by one, and she stood at the bottom of the world with her candle, and the mountain waited.

*Speak,* it said.

* * *

"I came to tell you a heroic story," Zephyrine said. Her voice sounded very small in the blue dark. "I've been composing it the whole way down, actually. You know the kind. *I was stolen from my birthright. I endured. Now I return to claim what's mine.* It's a good story. I've told pieces of it to myself for thirty years to get to sleep."

The mountain said nothing, which was somehow encouraging. Mountains, at least, did not hurry you.

"But it isn't true," she said. "My mother hid me. That part's true, and it broke her doing it, and I've forgiven her—I think I have, mostly, on good days. But she hid me. Nobody hid the woman I became. The woman I became hid herself."

The candle guttered. Or maybe it was just her breath shaking.

"I stayed because I was afraid," she said, and the words came out of her like a tooth coming loose—all at once, with blood, with enormous relief. "Not because the valley needed tending, though it did, and not because the goats would have starved, though some of them absolutely would have. I stayed because every single morning I woke up and considered leaving and every single morning I decided the silence was survivable. And it was. It was survivable. That was the horror of it. I made a whole life that fit inside a wound, and I furnished it nicely, and I called it contentment."

She laughed—a wet, cracked sound that echoed strangely off the ice.

"Do you know what I was afraid of? Not the Wardens. Well—yes, the Wardens, obviously, they're terrifying, have you met Serah Vane. But underneath. Underneath I was afraid that if I crossed the line and the magic came back and nobody out there wanted to hear me—" Her voice failed and restarted. "That the silence would follow me. That the quiet wasn't the ward's. It was mine. That I was unhearable. That I'd spent thirty years becoming a person the world simply couldn't hear, and crossing the border wouldn't change that, it would only prove it."

The ice around her was utterly still. Even the deep groan of the glacier had paused, the way a room goes quiet when someone finally says the true thing.

"And here's the joke," she went on, wiping her face with a mittened hand, "the stupid, ridiculous joke. Out there, right now, sitting on the ice above us, there's an eight-year-old child who thinks I hang the moon. Who learned my language—the language everyone else in creation calls forbidden—as fast as she could drink it in, because I was the first person in her whole short life who answered her. She hears me fine. Wolves vouch for me. Hawks owe me favors. A healer I've known nine days calls me sister. Turns out I was hearable the entire time. Turns out the door was unlocked. I just—never pulled it."

She looked up at the dark above the stair, where her daughter-of-the-road and her stubborn goat waited in the cold.

"So that's my testimony," she said. "My mother chose for me once, and I've stopped blaming her, because she bought me thirty years with her life and that purchase was made in love. But the thirty years after—I own those. Every pillow over the ears. Every door shut on a silent crow. I was afraid, and I stayed, and staying nearly cost two realms their memory of each other. If you want heroism, mountain, I'm fresh out. What I have is the truth, badly delivered, in a freezing hole in the ground, by a woman talking to a glacier. Take it or leave it."

Silence.

Long enough that she began, absurdly, to rehearse apologies. Long enough that the candle burned down past halfway, and her knees ached, and she thought: *well. At least I'll get a funny story out of—*

*Thirty years,* the mountain said, *and you have finally said something worth keeping.*

The ice lit.

Not the frost-panels this time—the whole crevasse, floor to unseen ceiling, filling with blue-white radiance that poured upward through the glacier like dawn happening in the wrong direction. And in the center of the light, rising out of the ice floor as gently as a bubble rising through honey, came the fourth oath-stone.

It was smaller than she expected—no bigger than a loaf of bread, a rough grey oval seamed with lines of gold, and it was *cold* in a way that was different from the glacier's cold. Older. Hungrier. It hung in the air before her, and she felt it take her measure, and this time she did not flinch from being measured.

*The others who came,* the mountain rumbled, deep beneath her, a voice moving continents. *They brought courage. Courage is loud and it lies. One brought rage—he shouted his grievances at the stone until the stone went back to sleep out of boredom. One brought piety. One brought an army, once, long ago, and we ate their siege engines into the ice.* A pause like a landslide settling. *You bring an account. Honest, itemized, unpaid. The stone has not heard an honest account in three hundred years.*

"I'll take that as a compliment."

*Do not. It is merely accurate.* And there it was again, buried under ten thousand tons of ice: humor. *Speaker. Your name.*

"Zephyrine Winterbourne."

*Your mother's name.*

"Sabine Winterbourne," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "Who carried me across the ward-line in a basket in April. Who anchored a dying ward with her own life so her freakish daughter could grow up safe. Who never once, in all the years I knew her, told me she was dying of it. Who I let die quietly because asking questions might have led somewhere I feared to go." She swallowed. "She should be here for this. She'd have done it better."

*No,* the mountain said, with terrible gentleness. *She did what she could bear. You are doing what you could not. That is how it goes, between mothers and daughters. Each generation finishes the sentence the last one started.*

The stone drifted toward her and stopped, a hand's breadth from her chest, gold seams beginning to pulse.

*Wake it,* the mountain said. *Sing. Not the covenant—that is for the child and the ninth altar. Sing only what you are. The stone will hold it, as we hold the fox, and the nests, and everything else that trusted us to remember.*

So Zephyrine Winterbourne, forty-one years old, former invisible woman of Hollowmere, stood at the bottom of a glacier and sang.

She sang the goat-song first, the silly one she'd made up at fifteen for Bramble's kids, all nonsense syllables and affection. Then the herb-chants, the real ones, the ones with power folded in like yeast in dough. Then her mother's bee-charm, note-perfect across thirty years. Then the beast-speech itself—her birthright, her crime, her one true tongue—poured out without shame for the first time in her life, echoing up the spiral stair, reverberating through three hundred years of patient ice.

And the stone drank it. The gold seams brightened seam by seam, and warmth bloomed outward from it in a sphere that turned the cave's breath mild, and somewhere far overhead she heard Wren cry out—not in pain, but in the sharp glad way children cry out when something wonderful happens—and heard Bramble bleat, and heard, faintly, distantly, the glacier itself begin to *hum*.

The stone settled into her cupped hands, warm as a loaf from the oven, light as bread too.

*Four,* the mountain said. *Four stones burning. Go up, Zephyrine Winterbourne, daughter of Sabine. And take better care of the child than the world took of you.*

She climbed the two hundred stairs with the stone against her heart, and every step felt lighter than the last, and by the top she was almost running.

Wren hit her at knee height, arms clamped around her legs, and Zephyrine gathered her up and held her hard, the oath-stone warm between them, and above the glacier's mouth the night sky was doing something impossible: snow was falling upward, caught in the stone's waking, glittering like the whole world had decided to celebrate.

"Mama Zeph," Wren said—her human words were still few, hoarded like sweets—"you're *shining.*"

"That," Zephyrine said, laughing and crying at once, "is a long story, and I'm going to tell you all of it someday. Every embarrassing year of it."

But even as she said it, she looked down at the child in her arms and felt the joy snag, sharp, on a fact she could no longer ignore. Wren's hair—always pale—had gone nearly white at the temples. There were new lines of exhaustion carved under the deer-bright eyes, and when the child smiled, Zephyrine could see the faint golden glow of the four stones pulsing beneath her skin, one two three four, like a heartbeat that wasn't entirely hers.

Four stones burning. And Wren burning with them.

"We rest tonight," Zephyrine said, setting her down and taking her hand firmly in her own. "Tomorrow we go downhill, fast, toward the sea-cliff. And Wren—when we get there, whatever happens at that ninth stone, I need you to promise me you'll let me speak first. Will you do that?"

Wren tilted her head, reading her face the way she read everything, with that unnerving animal attention. Then she nodded slowly, and answered in the old speech, solemn as the mountain:

*You will want to. But the stone asks what it asks.*

Far below them, the glacier hummed its new note into the bones of the world, and away to the south, beyond the passes, beyond Vane's marching army, the long winter shuddered—and advanced.
