## 30. Four Stones Burning

The first flower came up through the snow like a rumor.

Zephyrine saw it on the morning after they left the glacier, a small blue thing pushing its head through two feet of white as if snow were nothing more than an opinion it had decided to disagree with. She stopped so abruptly that Wren walked into her back.

"Look," she said, and then felt foolish, because Wren had already seen it. The child crouched beside the bloom with the reverence of someone greeting an old friend at a funeral — joy and grief in the same posture.

*It remembers,* Wren signed, and their hands trembled doing it.

The mountain's stone had woken three days before, singing up through the ice until the whole glacier groaned like a ship changing course. That made four. Four stones burning, four points of light kindled across a valley-lands map that Maren kept inked on a scrap of leather she consulted the way other people consulted scripture.

And the land was waking with them.

By noon they had passed a stream that was no longer content to murmur. It *sang* — an actual melody, rising off the water in threads of sound that Zephyrine could feel against her skin like sun-warmed silk. The wolves, who had been pacing them at a respectful distance since the pass, broke formation to drink from it, and when they lifted their heads their ears swiveled toward the water with expressions Zephyrine could only describe as delighted.

"Rivers used to sing all the time," Maren said, walking beside her. The old healer moved with a stick now, her hip still bad from the bridge where she'd held Vane's vanguard for a day and a night. "Before the silencing. My grandmother said you couldn't sleep near a river for the noise of it. Everyone thought she was mad." She smiled down at the water. "Turns out she just had good ears."

"It's beautiful," Zephyrine said.

Maren looked at her. Maren had a way of looking at people that made them feel inventory being taken — not unkindly, but thoroughly, the way she checked a wound for what lay beneath the surface.

"Yes," Maren said. "It is. And it isn't free."

Zephyrine didn't answer, because she had already seen it too.

---

She had first noticed it at the second stone, the one in the flooded shrine, though she'd told herself it was exhaustion. Wren had sung that stone awake and afterward slept for nearly two days, and children sleep, that was normal, that was fine. At the third stone, under Ferrow's Cross, Wren had slept again, and this time there had been a grayness around their mouth that Zephyrine had chosen, quite deliberately, not to name.

At the fourth stone, in the glacier cave, Wren had finished the song and turned to smile at her — and Zephyrine had seen that the child's eyes, which had once reflected light like a deer's, now reflected it more faintly. Dimmer. Like a lamp running low on oil.

And Wren had grown. Not taller, exactly. Older. The softness of childhood was thinning from their face the way frost thins from a window in the morning sun. They were perhaps nine years old now instead of eight. Zephyrine had done the arithmetic in the dark of the tent and hated every number.

Four stones. Five remained, counting the ninth at world's edge. And each waking cost the child something that did not come back.

"You're going to have to say it out loud eventually," Maren said that evening, as they made camp in the lee of a hillside where the snow had retreated into patches, surrendering ground to moss and the small green spears of things neither of them could name yet. "Or I'm going to say it, and I'll be less gentle about it than you'd be."

"Gentleness has never been your failing."

"No," Maren agreed comfortably. "It hasn't." She fed sticks to the fire and watched Wren, who sat at the edge of the light talking with the wolf-pack leader in the old speech — low sounds, half-song, the child's hands moving in signs to fill the gaps. "How much do you know about how the covenant actually worked? Not the stories. The mechanics."

"The stones hold the promise. The promise holds the border between forgetting and remembering. Nine seals, nine stones." Zephyrine wrapped her hands around her cup. "That's what Bramble said. That's what the shrine showed me."

"Stories," Maren said again, but without contempt. "Here is what my order knew, back when we were allowed to know things. A stone doesn't wake on its own. It can't. It's a lock with no hand on the key. It needs a seal — living flesh of the old covenant — to give it the word. And here is the part the stories leave out." She looked up, firelight catching the deep lines of her face. "The word costs. Every covenant ever sworn was paid for. Your mother paid. The first speakers paid. And a seal-child—" she nodded toward Wren, "—pays in the only coin they have. Themselves."

"They're burning as fuel," Zephyrine said quietly. "Aren't they."

"Aye." Maren didn't dress it up. Zephyrine was grateful for that, in a bruised sort of way. "Each stone takes more. The first might have cost them a season. The last will cost them everything, unless something changes."

Zephyrine watched Wren laugh at something the wolf-leader said — a real laugh, bright and startling, the laugh of a child who had spent their whole short life carrying the memory of seven dead siblings and had somehow, impossibly, kept the ability to laugh anyway.

"There has to be another way," she said.

"There's always another way," Maren said. "The question is whether anyone's willing to pay its price. Usually the other way costs more, which is why nobody takes it." She poked the fire. "Usually."

---

That night Zephyrine dreamed of Hollowmere.

Not the valley as it was — quiet, herb-scented, her goats drowsing in the paddock — but as it was becoming. In the dream the ward was gone entirely, dissolved like sugar in warm water, and the valley was filling up with remembering. Flowers through the snow on Tobias Grell's roof. The well singing. Crows on every fence post, talking all at once, saying her name in forty different voices.

She woke before dawn with her heart hammering and found Wren awake too, sitting cross-legged at the tent flap, watching the sky go from black to iron to the color of a peach left too long in the sun.

"Can't sleep?" Zephyrine asked, settling beside them. Their shoulders touched. Wren leaned into her, a weight she had come to measure her days by.

*The stones are loud,* Wren signed. *Now that four are awake, I can hear them all the time. Like bells ringing far away.* A pause. Their hands hesitated, which was unusual; Wren's signing was usually as sure as birdsong. *One of them is scared.*

"Scared?"

*The fifth. The one near the sea-marsh. It's been asleep so long it's forgotten it's a stone. It thinks it's just rock.* Wren's mouth quirked. *I'll have to remind it.*

"Wren." Zephyrine took the small cold hands in hers, stilled them. She had promised herself she would not do this — would not interrogate a child about their own diminishing — but promises to oneself were the easiest kind to break. "When you wake a stone. What does it take from you?"

Wren considered her with those dimming deer-bright eyes, and Zephyrine saw the exact moment the child understood the question behind the question. Children always did. That was the cruelty of children: they saw everything and were kind about it, mostly.

*Warmth,* Wren signed finally. *Like blowing on your hands in winter. You don't miss the breath. But it goes somewhere else and does work.* Then, because Wren had never once let Zephyrine hide inside a comfortable lie: *You're asking if it hurts. No. It's more like... getting smaller. Each time. There's less of me after, and the less-of-me is older.*

"How much less, for the last one?"

Wren was quiet for a long time. Somewhere downslope, the river sang its new song, and a nightbird answered it — actually answered, call and response, a conversation that hadn't happened in this country for three hundred years. The world was waking up all around them, gorgeous and greedy.

*Enough,* Wren signed at last. *But not today. Today there's still enough of me for porridge.* And they wriggled free of her grip and went to help Maren with the cookpot, leaving Zephyrine alone with a dread that had teeth.

---

They traveled south and east that day, following Maren's leather map toward the marsh country where the fifth stone slept and forgot itself. The land kept waking around them, and it was impossible not to rejoice in it, and impossible not to grieve, and Zephyrine learned that day that the human heart could do both at once, could carry them together the way a pack animal carries loads on both sides.

Snowdrops by the thousand in a hollow that should have been white until thaw. A stand of birches that had lost their names — grey, silent, anonymous trees, the long winter's fingerprints — suddenly shivering back into birchhood, leaves budding out of season, their bark flushing white with recognition. A fox that trotted alongside them for a mile and told Zephyrine, in the old speech, a long rambling story about a henhouse in 1841 that Zephyrine suspected was apocryphal but enjoyed enormously.

"Everything's coming back," she said to Maren at the midday halt, watching the fox trot off with its tail high. "The flowers, the rivers, the names. It's working. We're winning."

"Mm." Maren was watching Wren, who had climbed onto a fallen log and was teaching three wolf pups a clapping game involving signs, to the visible confusion of the pups and the audible amusement of their mother. "And what's it costing, Zephyrine? Everything comes from somewhere. That's the oldest law there is. Older than the covenant. Older than the stones."

"I know."

"Do you?" Maren's voice wasn't sharp. It was worse than sharp — it was gentle, the gentleness of a woman who had set broken bones for forty years and knew exactly how much pressure the truth could bear. "Because I've watched you look at that child for six days now, and half the time you look like a mother, and half the time you look like a woman doing sums she doesn't want to finish."

Zephyrine said nothing. On the log, Wren collapsed in giggles as a pup got the sequence wrong and clapped itself in the ear.

"When we get to the ninth stone," Maren said, "there will be a choice. There's always a choice at the heart of a covenant — that's what makes it a covenant and not a spell. Spells are one-sided. Covenants are signed by everyone who pays." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and shouldered her pack. "Start thinking about what you'd sign. You've got maybe ten days."

---

They made the marsh country's edge by evening of the third day, and camped within sight of the sea for the first time since the journey began — a grey gleam beyond the dunes, breathing slow and enormous, like something sleeping that was not a stone.

Wren fell asleep early, genuinely early, mid-sentence almost, sitting up with a piece of flatbread in their hand. Zephyrine caught them before they toppled into the fire and carried them to the tent, and the child weighed less than they had a week ago. She was certain of it now. Certain the way you are certain of a wound.

Bramble would have known what to say. Bramble always knew what to say, in her blunt, cud-chewing way. But Bramble was a hundred miles north in Hollowmere, guarding a homestead that no longer needed guarding, and Zephyrine had only the stars, the sea-sound, and Maren sharpening her knife by the coals.

"She talked in her sleep," Maren said, without looking up. "Did you hear it?"

"No."

"Names. Seven names, over and over. Ash. Sorrel. Fen. Lark. Teasel. Moss. And one more I couldn't catch." Maren tested the blade's edge with her thumb. "Those would be the others, I expect. The seal-children who came before her. The ones whose stones got shattered."

"Yes." Zephyrine's voice came out rough. "She dreams them. All of them. She carries what they carried."

"Then she knows better than any of us what's at stake." Maren sheathed the knife and looked at Zephyrine across the fire, and her eyes were very steady. "Which means when the choice comes, girl, you'd best make sure it's a choice and not a theft. There's a difference between a sacrifice and a spending, and the difference is whether the one who pays gets a say." She jerked her chin toward the tent. "That one will offer herself gladly. Gladly and completely, and thank you for the chance. That's what seals are *for*, in the old understanding. So the question you'll have to live with — whichever way it breaks — is whether you let her."

Zephyrine looked at the tent flap, stirring faintly in the sea wind, and felt the future open beneath her like a crevasse.

Beyond the dunes, the sea breathed in, and breathed out, and somewhere inland — faint, far, patient — a bell began to ring that no one else seemed to hear.

The fifth stone, calling. Or warning.

Zephyrine sat up all night, listening to both.
