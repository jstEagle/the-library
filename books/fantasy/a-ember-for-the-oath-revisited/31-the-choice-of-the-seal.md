## 31. The Choice of the Seal

The mountain path did not so much climb as insist. It went up in switchbacks that seemed designed by someone with a grudge against knees, and Zephyrine climbed it with Wren in her arms and Bramble trotting at her heel like a small, judgmental shadow.

Wren had grown lighter. That was the thing Zephyrine could not stop noticing, the way a tongue returns again and again to a broken tooth. A child of eight should not weigh less than a bundle of winter herbs. But each day since the glacier cave, since the fourth stone had opened its blue eye beneath the ice and drunk deep, Wren had grown thinner—not sickly, exactly, but *translucent*, as though the world were beginning to see through them to something older underneath.

"Put me down," Wren signed, for their voice had grown too faint for human words. "I can walk."

"You can walk when we stop," Zephyrine said. "Until then you're cargo."

"I am not *cargo*."

"You're precious cargo. There's a difference. Cargo doesn't get the last of the honey."

That earned her a flicker of a smile, which was worth more than the honey. She had been rationing smiles too, without quite admitting it.

Bramble snorted—a wet, dismissive sound that needed no translation—and butted the back of Zephyrine's knee to keep her moving. The old goat's beard was frosted white now, though whether from age or altitude or sheer theatrical habit, Zephyrine could no longer tell.

They made camp early, in the lee of a granite shoulder where the wind gave up trying to reach them. Zephyrine got a fire going with the last dry wood, fed Wren broth, wrapped them in both blankets, and then sat with her back against Bramble's warm flank and let herself, for one whole minute, do nothing at all.

It was Bramble who broke the minute.

"You have to ask me," the goat said.

Zephyrine opened her eyes. Above them the sky was doing its high-mountain trick, all hard stars and no mercy. "Ask you what?"

"What you've been not-asking me since the fourth stone." Bramble chewed thoughtfully on a twig of dwarf pine, which Zephyrine knew tasted terrible, because she'd tried one once out of solidarity. "You've been carrying me around in your head like a question you're afraid to open. I can hear it rattling. It's very loud. You humans think your skulls are private. They are not. They are *drums*."

Zephyrine looked at the fire. "I don't know how to ask it."

"Liar," said Bramble, with affection. "You know exactly how. You just know what the answer will be."

* * *

Here is what must be understood about beast-speech, and about Bramble in particular: it was never a language of sentences. When Zephyrine translated, she was not repeating words. She was standing in a river of scent and posture and memory and meaning, and pulling out what could survive the crossing into human air. Some things crossed easily. Hunger crossed easily. Fear crossed easily.

What Bramble carried now crossed slowly, heavily, like a stone rolled uphill by an animal far too small for the work—and Zephyrine felt every ounce of it as she gave it shape.

"The seal-child is burning," she said quietly, so Wren—half-asleep against her side—would not hear the whole of it. "I've known since the shrine. I told myself the first stone only took a little. Then the second. Then the mountain." Her hands had closed into fists without her permission. "Say it plainly, Bramble. Please. For once in your contrary life, say it plainly."

Bramble was quiet a long moment. Somewhere below them, a river ran under ice, singing to itself in the dark.

"When the covenant was first sworn," Zephyrine translated, her voice low and steady the way a rope is steady, "there were nine stones and nine children. Living seals. Each stone slept inside a child, and each child slept inside the land, and the land kept its promises because it could feel them—literally feel them—the way you feel a hand holding yours. That was the whole architecture of the peace. Not law. Not wardens. *Holding hands.*"

"And the Oathwardens broke it," Zephyrine said. "Killed the children, shattered four stones—"

"They didn't understand what they were breaking," Bramble said, and there was something in the old goat's tone that Zephyrine had rarely heard: pity, aimed upward, at humans. "You cannot shatter a covenant and keep the peace. You can only postpone the forgetting. The long winter isn't a punishment. It's what happens when two realms stop remembering each other. Like neighbors who stop speaking over a fence until one day neither remembers the other's name, and then the fence comes down and something worse comes through."

Zephyrine waited. She had learned, over thirty years of one-sided conversations, that Bramble circled the hard truths the way goats circle a wolf—showing it their flank, letting it know they'd seen it, refusing to turn their back.

"The last stone is different," she said at last. "The ninth. The altar-stone at the sea-cliff, where the first oath was spoken. It was never meant to sleep inside a child alone. It was meant to be renewed. Every few generations, a speaker would come and give their voice to it—bind their speech into the oath, permanently, so the covenant always had a living tongue. That was the original design. The seal-children were the *backup*. The emergency measure. Nine little lanterns lit against a night no one expected to fall."

"But the speakers stopped coming."

"The wardens made sure of it. Who volunteers to bind their voice forever when binding it means the wardens come for your village?" Bramble shifted, resettling his weight against her back, and Zephyrine felt the truth arrive before the words did, the way you feel rain before it lands. "So. Two doors, Zephyrine Winterbourne. Door one: the final stone takes the seal-child fully given. All of them. Everything Wren is, poured into the stone, and the covenant holds for another age—but Wren will not walk back down that mountain. Door two: a speaker binds their own living voice into the oath beside the child. Shares the weight. Splits the flame between two wicks instead of letting one burn to nothing."

Zephyrine's breath came out slow and white.

"And door two costs—" she began.

"Your voice," Bramble said. "Not your beast-speech. Your *speech*. All of it. Human tongue, wild tongue, every word you will ever want to say to anyone, given over to the stone, forever, to become part of the oath itself. You would still speak. But everything you say would be the covenant speaking through you. You would never again say anything that was only yours."

* * *

The fire cracked. Wren stirred against her side and murmured something in the pre-kingdom tongue—a soft, spiraling phrase that Zephyrine now understood well enough to catch its meaning: *warm, here, staying.*

She looked down at the child and felt the dread in her chest stop being fog and become a thing with edges.

Thirty years ago, her mother had carried her across the border of Hollowmere and paid for it with her whole life, anchored to a dying ward until the ward took the last of her. Zephyrine had spent three decades telling herself a story about that: that her mother had chosen for both of them, that Zephyrine had been robbed of a decision. And it was true. And it was also true—she had admitted this to a mountain, of all confessor—that she had stayed. Year after year after year. Safe, silent, invisible, alive.

Now the choice stood in front of her wearing its true face, and it was not a choice between safety and sacrifice. It was a choice between two kinds of giving, and one of them was already happening, had been happening since the flooded shrine, and she had watched it happen stone by stone because hope was easier to carry than arithmetic.

"How long does Wren have?" she asked. "If I do nothing."

Bramble was silent.

"Bramble."

"If you do nothing," Zephyrine translated, and her voice came out flat because flat was the only way it could come out, "the fifth stone wakes at the solstice, and the seal-child goes with it. Fully given. Whether anyone consents or not. The stones do not ask, Zeph. They were built in an age when no one imagined needing to."

"So it's not door one or door two." She stared into the fire. "Door one is already open. I'm just deciding whether I stand in it."

"Now you're asking the right question," Bramble said gently. "Only about thirty years late. Typical human."

Despite everything—despite the cold and the weight in her arms and the arithmetic of her own cowardice—Zephyrine laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out. "You're a goat. You've eaten my curtains. Twice. You have no moral authority whatsoever."

"I have excellent moral authority. I'm the only one here who has never once lied to you." Bramble rested his chin on her knee, which was as close as a goat ever came to an embrace. "And I will tell you one more thing, and then I'll shut up, which will pain me greatly."

"Go on."

"You're thinking of it wrong. You keep thinking: *my voice, gone, my words, taken.* But I have listened to you talk to animals for thirty years, Zephyrine Winterbourne, and I will tell you what I heard. I heard a woman who never once used her gift to take anything. Not once. Do you know how rare that is? Wolves can't manage it. Crows certainly can't. Humans with power—" he made a sound of profound ovine disgust. "But you. You only ever *witnessed*. You listened, and you answered, and you kept what you heard like it was holy. If anyone's voice belongs inside an oath, it's one that was already an oath. You've been swearing it every day of your hidden little life. The stone wouldn't be taking anything from you. It would finally be *catching up* to what you already were."

Zephyrine did not answer. There was nothing in her throat but a knot, and knots do not translate.

* * *

She woke before dawn, as she had every morning of her life, and found Wren already awake, sitting up in the blankets, watching her with those deer-bright eyes that caught even the grey light and gave it back doubled.

The child signed slowly, deliberately, the way people sign when they have rehearsed.

*I know what you talked about.*

Of course they knew. Children always knew; the whole world simply pretended otherwise, and adults called the pretense kindness.

*I heard the goats talking in my dreams,* Wren signed. *All eight of them. My brothers and sisters. They were afraid at the end. They didn't want to go into the stones.* A pause. Small hands trembling slightly in the cold. *But they were glad, after. Inside. They said it wasn't like dying. It was like being remembered by everything at once.*

"That's not the same as wanting it," Zephyrine said hoarsely.

Wren shook their head. Then they reached out and pressed one small palm flat against her sternum, right over the heartbeat, the way they had done the first night at her door, barefoot in the snow.

*You,* they signed. *I choose you.*

And there it was. The thing no stone could ask and no warden could forbid: consent, offered freely, by a child who had watched eight siblings vanish into rock and light and had come looking for her anyway, across a whole valley of silence, because somewhere in the old blood-memory of the seals, her mother's name was written next to the word *safe.*

Zephyrine gathered the child in and held on, and above them the stars began to fade, and below them the world went on forgetting, tree by tree, river by river, name by name.

"All right," she whispered into Wren's hair. "All right. Both of us, then. We'll split the flame."

Behind her, Bramble made a sound that might have been a sob, if goats sobbed, which they absolutely did not and would deny to the end of days.

It was then that the crow arrived.

It dropped out of the pale dawn sky onto a boulder at the edge of camp, feathers ruffled, eyes bright with urgency, and it spoke in a rush of wing-beats and image that Zephyrine translated almost before she knew she was doing it:

*"The herb-wife is taken. The iron woman marches for the cliff-altar. Midwinter, they mean to break the ninth stone themselves, on the solstice, before the seal can wake—and they have brought a cage."*

A cage. For a child.

Zephyrine rose, and lifted Wren onto her hip, and looked up the mountain toward the pass that led, at the end of all things, to the sea.

"Then we stop walking slowly," she said.
