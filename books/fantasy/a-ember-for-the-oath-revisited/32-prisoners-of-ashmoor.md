## 32. Prisoners of Ashmoor

Maren had been a prisoner for exactly one day and four hours, and she had already reorganized her cell twice.

The cell was a converted grain store in the Ashmoor garrison—stone floor, straw pallet, one barred window set high enough that she could see only sky and the top of the bell tower, which she had decided to think of as a clock rather than a taunt. The pallet had been moved to the far wall first thing, because drafts came from the door side and Maren was sixty-one years old with knees that announced weather like town criers. Then she'd folded her blanket into a proper rectangle. Then she'd swept the straw into a corner with the side of her boot, considered it, and swept it back.

Order was a kind of medicine. Her mother had taught her that. When the world went wrong, you made your small square of it right, and you kept your hands busy, and you did not let them see you count the hours.

Not that counting was hard. She was very good at counting. She'd counted the guards' rotations within the first six hours: two on the door, changing at dawn, midday, dusk, and midnight, with a lazy overlap of perhaps ten minutes when both men stood together and complained about their boots. She'd counted the steps from her window to the bell tower's base by pacing shadows. She'd counted, with some professional disapproval, the number of medicinal herbs visible in the commander's own supply cart through the yard—eleven, and three of them stored damp.

Amateurs, all of them. It was almost comforting.

* * *

Serah Vane came at midday on the second day, which told Maren something. Important prisoners got visited early or not at all; midday meant Vane wanted her to know the visit was a courtesy, not a necessity. A woman managing her own pride. Interesting.

Vane dismissed the guards, pulled the cell's single stool inside—she carried it herself, Maren noted, another calculated courtesy—and sat with her back straight as a fence post, her grey cloak folded over her knee.

"You're the herb-wife of Ashmoor," Vane said.

"I'm the herb-wife formerly of Ashmoor," Maren corrected. "Ashmoor exiled me eleven years ago for treating a fever with willow bark instead of prayer. The fever survived. So did I. We've both been getting along fine since."

Something flickered at the corner of Vane's mouth. Suppressed, but there. Good. A woman who could be made almost-smile was a woman who could be talked to.

"You treated the seal-child," Vane said. "The wound at the ford."

"I treated a child," Maren said. "What you call it is your business. You people do love your labels. Seal-child. Irregularity. Heretic-adjacent." She ticked them off on her fingers. "Half your vocabulary exists so you never have to say *child*."

"The child is not human."

"The child bled red at the ford and cried for its mother in a language older than your order. I've bandaged a great many humans who managed less." Maren settled back against the wall, arranging her hands in her lap like a woman settling in for a long sermon. "But you didn't come here to discuss my bedside manner. You came because your interrogators have been at me for a day and gotten nothing but recipes, and you thought you'd try the direct approach. Sit down yourself. Talk woman to woman. Very sensible. I respect it."

Vane's jaw tightened fractionally. "You were seen traveling with Zephyrine Winterbourne. You delayed my vanguard at Tarn Bridge with hedge-magic. Under the Statutes of Quiet, both are capital offenses."

"Under the Statutes of Quiet, so is whistling on a Sunday in some parishes. Your statutes are less a law than a net, Commander, and nets are for catching things you're afraid of."

"And what would an exile know of law?"

"Everything," Maren said pleasantly. "That's what exile teaches. The law looked at me across a table once, just like this, and told me that saving a life wrongly was worse than losing one rightly. I've had eleven years to turn that sentence over. I still can't make it stand up. Would you like to try? You seem cleverer than the magistrates were."

For a moment Vane simply looked at her—the flat, assessing look of a woman used to being the only person in a room doing arithmetic. Then, unexpectedly, she leaned forward.

"Why?" she asked. "You're no speaker. No wild blood in you—I've read the Ashmoor records; they exiled you for stubbornness, nothing more. You had a practice, a cottage, a life. Why throw it away for a stranger and a—a creature?"

And there it was: the real question, under all the doctrine. Maren had spent thirty years reading faces over sickbeds, and Serah Vane's face was asking something it didn't know it was asking. Not *why did you break the law*. But *why would anyone*.

Maren softened her voice, just slightly. Just enough.

"Because I watched a child take a crossbow bolt meant for a woman who has spent thirty years being nobody, and the child did it without hesitating, and then—" she paused, letting it land, "—and then apologized. In bird-language. For bleeding on her cloak. Commander, I have buried husbands and delivered babies and held the hands of the dying in four parishes, and I have never once seen a soul so entirely without meanness. Whatever your books say that child is, I say I've met worse things wearing crowns and vestments. So. That's why."

Silence. Somewhere above them, the bell tower marked the half hour, and Maren counted the strokes out of habit—six—and watched Vane count them too, without meaning to. A woman who counted. That could be worked with.

"You'll be transferred to Ferrow's Cross within the week," Vane said at last, rising. "There will be a formal inquiry."

"Inquiries," Maren said, "have witnesses."

"Not this one."

* * *

They took her to Ferrow's Cross after all—but not to any inquiry. They put her in a proper cell beneath the garrison, stone-floored and cold, and left her alone for three days, which Maren recognized as a technique and declined to be affected by. She passed the time composing, in her head, a comprehensive critique of the garrison surgeon's inventory, and by the third day had worked herself into such a state of professional outrage that when the door finally opened she nearly thanked him for the interruption.

It was not the surgeon. It was a young warden, barely twenty, with ink-stained fingers and the apologetic posture of a clerk pressed into soldiering.

"Beg pardon, mistress," he said, setting down a tray. "Commander's orders. You're to be fed proper. There's bread, and broth, and—" he glanced at the tray, lowered his voice, "—and willow bark tea. For your joints. I heard you mention them."

Maren looked at the boy. The boy looked at his boots.

"That was thoughtful," she said. "Who told you about my joints?"

"No one, mistress. Only—you were talking to the Commander through the wall, the first night. Everyone on watch heard. About the fever surviving." He shifted. "My sister had a fever once. Willow bark was all that helped. Before the Order posted here, I mean. After, we weren't allowed to—" He stopped himself, visibly, the way young men stop themselves when they've said one sentence too many.

"After, you weren't allowed to," Maren finished gently. She took the cup of tea and held it a moment before drinking, watching him. "What's your name, lad?"

"Perrin, mistress."

"Well, Perrin. Tell whoever brews this that it's steeped too short and strained too fine—the bark wants a full quarter hour and the sediment does half the work. And tell them I said so freely, as a gift, expecting nothing." She smiled at him, warm as a hearth. "Gifts are how you tell friends from jailers, Perrin. Remember that. It may matter someday."

He fled. But the next day, the tea was steeped properly, and there was honey in it, and Maren drank it sitting on her cot with the satisfaction of a general watching a supply line open.

* * *

Vane returned on the fifth day, and this time she brought papers.

She spread them on the cell floor between them—deliberately, Maren noted, making the prisoner look down at them, keeping the geometry of authority—and Maren looked anyway, because looking down at things was how you learned what people wanted hidden.

Maps. Ferrow's Cross, the river country, the high pass. And drawn in fresh ink, circled twice, the sea-cliff altar at the world's edge, with a date written beside it in Vane's own hand.

Midwinter.

"Your friends woke the stone under the cathedral," Vane said. "In front of three hundred witnesses. Do you understand what that cost me? What it *earned* them?"

"It earned them a full warrant and an army," Maren said. "I'd guessed. Congratulations, by the way. Most hunters need years to justify a escalation like that. You managed it in a fortnight. There's ambition in you, Commander. Wasted on this work."

"This work is the only reason there's a *work* to waste ambition on." Vane crouched, tapping the map. "Thirty years ago the covenant failed in the north. Wild magic came down out of the fells like a flood. I saw what it did. So don't lecture me about ambition, herb-wife. I'm not hunting these stones for glory. I'm ending them, permanently, before the land remembers how to eat us."

"Mm," said Maren. "And the child?"

Vane's hand stilled on the map.

"What about the child?"

"You've circled the altar. You've dated it midwinter. You've mustered two hundred men. All of that I understand—it's siege logic, plain enough. But you haven't asked me a single question about where Winterbourne is, or which route she'll take, and you've had me five days. A commander planning an interception asks those questions. So either you already know the answers—" Maren tilted her head, "—or you don't need them. Because you're not planning to catch the child at the altar at all. Are you."

The silence stretched. Outside, high and thin, a gull cried off the distant coast, and Maren thought, absurdly, of Wren's face the first time she'd heard one—wondering, delighted, hungry for every sound the world offered.

"You intend to take the child before midwinter," Maren said quietly. "And execute them publicly. Here. In Ferrow's Cross. On the solstice itself."

Vane rose. She gathered her maps with unhurried precision, and when she spoke, her voice was level as a frozen pond.

"A covenant is a promise, herb-wife. And a promise lives in its keeper. Kill the keeper before the oath completes, and the whole edifice dies—not with a battle, but with a silence. Clean. Legal. Witnessed." She rolled the maps. "No army of wolves. No flowering snow. No magic pouring back into a world that begged to be rid of it. One execution, and the long winter comes gently instead of violently, and humanity keeps what the last failure taught us: quiet is survival."

"You've thought it through," Maren said. "I'll grant you that. You've built a whole theology around one bad winter."

"It wasn't one winter. It was my childhood." For a heartbeat—just one—something moved behind Vane's eyes, old and cold and drowning. Then it was gone, shuttered, and the commander stood in its place. "You'll be held until it's done. Afterward, if you've behaved, you'll be released. The Order doesn't punish healers. Only speakers."

"How generous," Maren said. "One question, Commander, and then I'll let you go."

Vane paused at the door.

"When you were a girl," Maren said, "in the north. During the failure. Did anyone speak for you? Anyone at all? Or did everyone just get very quiet, and stay quiet, and call it safety?"

Vane's knuckles whitened on the door frame.

Then she left, and the lock turned, and Maren sat alone with the answer, which had been the silence itself.

* * *

That night, Maren began to work.

She had, by her count, nineteen days until midwinter. She had a guard named Perrin who brought honeyed tea and a guilty conscience. She had a cell with a window facing the bell tower, a lifetime of herbal knowledge, and a tongue that had been annoying authority figures since before Serah Vane was born.

Nineteen days was not much. But Maren had once kept a man alive for six weeks on willow bark, bone broth, and sheer refusal, and she had learned the same lesson every healer learns eventually: deadlines are not walls. Deadlines are doors with dates written on them. You simply have to be standing on the other side when they open.

She counted the guard rotations again, slower this time. She noted that Perrin drew the midnight-to-dawn watch three nights in seven, and that on those nights he lingered at her door a little longer than regulation allowed, listening to her hum.

On the fourth night, she hummed something new—an old walking song, the kind herb-wives sang on the roads before the Statutes, the kind with verses that changed depending on who was listening. She felt him stop outside the bars. Felt him listen.

"Sing the next verse tomorrow," she murmured, not opening her eyes, "and I'll teach you the one about the king's own hounds. It's a good song. It has a moral."

She heard him swallow. She heard his boots hesitate, and then move on.

Nineteen days. Nineteen nights. A boy with ink-stained fingers and a sister who'd once had a fever. A commander with a drowned childhood and a date circled twice.

Maren lay back on her cot, folded her blanket into a perfect rectangle, and smiled at the ceiling in the dark.

Hold fast, you stubborn goat-woman, she thought, toward the mountains, toward whatever pass Zephyrine was even now dragging herself over. Hold fast, little wren. Help is coming, and it smells of willow bark, and it is extremely annoyed.

Outside, the first flakes of the season's true snow began to fall—soft, patient, and early.

Very early.

Maren watched them past the bars, and her smile thinned, and she started counting again.
