## 33. March Through the Falling Dark

The pass had a name once. Zephyrine was almost certain of it.

She could feel the shape of it in her mind—a word her mother had used, maybe, or one she'd read on a faded survey map pinned to the wall of Ferrow's Cross trading post—*something-pass, something-fell*. But when she reached for it, the name slipped away like a fish in cold water, and what remained was just *the pass*, and the wind that lived in it, and the grey light falling through a sky the color of old pewter.

That was how the long winter worked. It didn't take things all at once. It took their names first.

"Keep walking," she said to Wren, and then, because the child's steps had gone strange and drifting, she took their hand.

Wren's fingers were cold. Not ordinary cold—the cold of a hearth swept out at dawn, of a room where the fire has been carried elsewhere. Zephyrine had been telling herself for three days that it was only the altitude, only the wind off the ice fields, but she was running out of ways to lie to herself with any conviction. She'd never been a gifted liar. Thirty years of hiding had taught her concealment, not falsehood, and there was a difference: concealment required you to know exactly where the truth was so you could stand in front of it.

The truth was that every stone they woke cost Wren something permanent. And they had four stones burning behind them, and one left ahead, and a child who weighed less each morning than they had the night before.

Behind them—far below now, down where the pines began—the forest was going out like lamps.

Zephyrine stopped once, against all sense, and looked back. She couldn't help it. You don't watch a world end every day, and some part of her insisted that bearing witness was a kind of duty, even when witnessing hurt.

The treeline below was still green at its edges, stubborn dark spires holding their color against the snow. But in the middle distances, whole slopes had turned the grey of ash and fog, and as she watched, a patch of grey spread outward from a river valley like ink dropped in water—not fast, not dramatic, just inevitable. The trees didn't fall. They didn't die, exactly. They simply became *trees*, generic and unnamed, the way furniture is wood without being oak or ash. The rivers beneath them ran flat and silent, forgetting their own music.

A wolf stood beside her, also looking back.

Fellhand was his name—or rather, it was the closest human mouth could come to his name, which was less a word than a memory of wind over a ridge. He was enormous, scarred across the muzzle, and he had appointed himself to Zephyrine's left flank two days ago with the unbothered authority of someone who had decided a thing and saw no reason to revisit the decision.

*You look too long,* he said. His voice in her mind was low and dry, like stones settling in a streambed. *Looking backward makes the legs forget forward.*

"I'm memorizing," Zephyrine said. "Someone should remember what it looked like."

*The mountain remembers. The sea remembers. When the winter breaks—and it will break, speaker, or we would not be climbing this hill for nothing—the names come home.* He glanced at her with yellow eyes. *They always come home. They are only waiting to be called.*

"And if we're too late?"

*Then we will have been too late,* Fellhand said, with the maddening simplicity of his kind, *and the calling will be done by whoever comes after us. Walk.*

So they walked.

* * *

The pack had found them on the second morning out of Ashmoor, materializing along a ridgeline like a thought arriving fully formed—eight wolves, then eleven, then fourteen, spaced along the heights above and below them in a loose escort formation that no commander could have improved upon. Zephyrine had known immediately that this was no coincidence. Wolves did not do coincidences. Wolves did intention, territory, and grievance, and occasionally mercy, but never coincidence.

The pack-leader—an old female named in Zephyrine's mind as Grey-Who-Waits—had spoken to her plainly, in front of Wren, which told Zephyrine everything about how seriously the wild places were taking this march.

*We smelled the stones waking,* Grey-Who-Waits had said. *Four fires lit in a dark country. Every ear in the high forest turned toward them like grass toward sun. Then we smelled the child thinning, and we came.* Her muzzle had wrinkled, not quite a snarl, not quite grief. *We lost our river last month. It forgot how to run downhill. It stands in its bed like a dead thing, and the fish in it have forgotten they were ever fish. We do not intend to lose anything else.*

Now the wolves moved with them through the pass in shifts—some ranging ahead, some flanking, two always near Wren, close enough that the child walked in a moving island of fur and warmth. Wren had taken to leaning against the nearest wolf while they walked, one small hand buried in ruff, and the wolves bore it with the patient dignity of elders permitting a grandchild to climb on them.

It was, Zephyrine thought, the strangest kindness she had ever witnessed: an entire species rearranging itself around one exhausted child.

At midday they stopped in the lee of a cairn, and Zephyrine got food into Wren by main force and persuasion. This had become a battle twice daily. Wren ate—but absently, the way you breathe, without attention, as though eating were a habit inherited from a body rather than a need of one. Their eyes had begun to do a thing Zephyrine refused yet to name: catching the grey light and giving back a faint silver shimmer, like frost forming on glass.

"You'll eat the rest," Zephyrine said, "or I'll sing the goat-song about Bramble's opinion of stubborn children. All nine verses. In public. With gestures."

Wren's mouth twitched. It was a small victory, but Zephyrine had learned to bank small victories the way a miser banks coins.

Bramble, of course, had opinions about all of this, delivered nightly around whatever fire they dared—small fires now, screened by stones, because Vane's army was somewhere behind them and Vane's scouts would see smoke the way hawks see mice.

*The child burns because the covenant was built crooked,* Bramble said that evening, chewing thoughtfully on a frozen root she had dug from under the snow with a precision that Zephyrine envied. *Nine seals, and eight of them murdered before their time. What did they expect? That the ninth would carry the whole roof alone? A roof needs rafters, girl. Always needed rafters.*

"I know what you're going to say."

*Then I won't say it.* Bramble chewed. *I'll hum it instead. Hmm-hmm, Zephyrine, hmm-hmm, your voice, hmm-hmm, the choice you've already made and are pretending you haven't.*

"There's no pretending. There's just—" Zephyrine fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "There's a difference between knowing a thing and saying it. Saying it makes it real. I've spent thirty years learning how heavy words get once they're said aloud."

*And I've spent twelve years learning that goats cannot carry burdens for their people, however much we might wish to.* Bramble leaned her warm weight against Zephyrine's shoulder, briefly, the way she had done since Zephyrine was a child hiding in the barn. *Say it on the mountain, then. Or at the cliff. But say it before midwinter, or the saying will be done for you, and I promise you the mountain's version will be less gentle than mine.*

Above them, the first stars came out, hard and white, and the wind changed direction—from behind them now, carrying up the pass a smell that made every wolf lift its head at once.

Ash. Not woodsmoke. Something older and colder, like the smell of a fire that went out centuries ago and had only just been noticed.

The long winter's edge, breathing up the mountain after them.

* * *

They crossed the spine of the pass in the blue hour before dawn, because Fellhand insisted. *The ice fields glare by day,* he said. *By night we are shadows among shadows, and the thing behind us hunts by sight of what is bright. Move in the dark. Rest in the light. It is backwards, and backwards is safe.*

So they climbed through darkness, Wren riding now on the back of the biggest wolf, a broad-chested male the pack called Stonebreaker, because Wren's legs had finally, quietly failed them sometime in the night and the child had not complained, which frightened Zephyrine more than wailing would have. Children who stop complaining have started conserving. Conserving is what candles do.

The stars were magnificent and merciless. Zephyrine's breath hung around her in clouds. Somewhere far below and behind, she knew, Serah Vane was marching with a warrant and an army and the absolute certainty of a woman who believed silence had saved her life—because it had; Zephyrine no longer doubted the shape of Vane's story, only what should be built on top of it. Behind Vane, Maren sat in a cage or a cell, stalling with doctrine and riddles, buying days with her sharp tongue, and Zephyrine sent a silent promise east with every step: *I will not waste what you bought.*

The crest of the pass came all at once, the way crests do—one moment climbing, the next standing on the top of the world with the sky enormous overhead and the land falling away on both sides.

And there, ahead, below, impossibly far and impossibly near: the sea.

Zephyrine had seen the sea twice in her life, both times before Hollowmere, both times as a child, and she had forgotten the size of it. It lay under the pre-dawn grey like hammered lead, running to the edge of everything, and on its horizon the sky was just beginning to consider the idea of light. Wind came up the cliff-side air rich with salt and kelp and the vast, slow, mineral patience of deep water.

Wren stirred on Stonebreaker's back. Turned their head. Looked at the sea.

"*Home,*" Wren said.

Not in the old tongue. In human speech—their third word ever, after *stones* and Zephyrine's own name, which they had offered shyly outside Ashmoor like a gift wrapped in leaves. Zephyrine felt the word go through her chest like a bell struck.

"Yes," she managed. "Almost. One more day."

Grey-Who-Waits came up beside her, snow dusting her shoulders, and stood looking at the sea with an expression Zephyrine could only call longing.

*My grandmother's grandmother saw the ninth stone sworn,* the pack-leader said. *It is in the long-memory, passed mouth to mouth down eleven generations: the cliff, the altar, the nine speakers, the voice that went into the ground like a seed.* She looked at Zephyrine. *They swore it with joy, the long-memory says. Did you know that? The stories your kind tells make it sound grim, all law and punishment. But the long-memory says there was singing. Feasting. Wolves lay down with the speakers' children and let them pull ears. It was not a chain, Zephyrine Winterbourne. It was a marriage. It has only become a chain because half the family died and the survivors forgot they were family.*

Zephyrine looked at Wren—at the silver shimmer in the child's eyes, at the small hands gripping fur—and felt the truth she'd been carrying since the glacier settle into its final weight.

"One day," she said again. To Wren. To the wolves. To herself. "One day, and then we find out what kind of story this is."

* * *

They descended through the morning, and the world got worse and better in equal measure.

Worse, because the winter's edge was visible now even ahead of them—in the high corries where snowfields lay that should have melted generations ago, in a tarn they passed whose surface was frozen solid and utterly silent, no crackle, no tick of expanding ice, just a white eye staring at the sky. Better, because the land was fighting. Where four stones burned, the land remembered, and the remembering showed in small rebellions: a stand of rowan trees still red-berried in defiance of the season, a stream that ran loud and bright for fifty yards before the grey took it, a single patch of earth where crocuses—*crocuses*, in deep winter—had pushed up through snow in a riot of purple, blooming madly, gloriously wrong.

Wren knelt by the crocuses and touched them, and the flowers turned toward the child like faces toward sun.

"They know you," Zephyrine said softly.

Wren nodded. Then, in the old tongue, in a voice grown thin as paper: *They are glad. They think I am spring.* A pause. *I am not spring. I am only the door.*

Zephyrine crouched beside them. "Doors don't open themselves, little wren. Someone has to walk through."

Wren looked up at her, and for a moment the silver in their eyes flickered with something older and warmer—the child underneath the seal, the person underneath the burden. *Will it hurt?* they asked. Not *will it hurt me.* Zephyrine noticed that. The question was general. Philosophical. *Will it hurt*—the way you'd ask about weather.

"Some," Zephyrine said, because she had promised herself, on the glacier, never to lie to this child. "But not alone. Whatever happens at that cliff, neither of us does it alone. That's the whole point. That's what the oath was always supposed to be."

Wren considered this with the gravity of an eight-year-old and the gravity of a dying covenant, which in Wren were the same gravity. Then they picked one crocus, carefully, roots and all, and tucked it into the fold of Zephyrine's cloak.

"For the door," they said.

* * *

They made camp that final night in a hollow above the coast road, and the wolves sang.

Zephyrine woke to it—low at first, a thread of sound rising from the dark ridgelines, wolf after wolf joining until the whole night was woven with it. It wasn't a hunting song. It wasn't grief. It was the old escort-song, Grey-Who-Waits explained sleepily, the one packs sang in the years when the covenant was strong, when wolves walked speakers to the shrines and the shrines answered. Eleven generations silent. Sung now, raggedly, imperfectly, voices rusty with disuse, but sung.

Beside her, Wren slept—truly slept, deeply, for the first time in days, curled against Bramble's flank with a wolf on either side. Even sleeping, the child shimmered faintly, like moonlight caught in water.

Zephyrine sat up and listened to the singing and let herself feel, for exactly one minute, the thing she had forbidden herself all march: fear. Not of Vane, or the army, or the winter. Fear of the choice, which she had made on the glacier and confirmed every day since and still had not said aloud in plain words, even to herself. *I bind my voice. I give my speech into the stone. I become the second seal, beside the child, so the child doesn't burn alone.*

Her voice. Thirty years of careful silence, hoarded like coins, and she was going to spend all of it in one gesture. And what would be left? Fellhand had told her once, casually, terribly: *the true tongue, speaker. The tongue before tongues. Everything that lives will understand you, and you will never again speak only to humans.* She had lain awake half that night imagining it—a voice that could never whisper privately, never mutter, never keep a secret, a voice that was in its very nature a public thing.

Well. She had spent thirty years being private. Perhaps that account was full.

The minute ended. She put the fear away—it didn't leave, it never left, it just went back in its drawer—and looked east, where the sky over the sea had gone the grey of pearl, and beyond it, miles off along the coast, she saw what she had been dreading and expecting in equal measure.

Smoke. Many fires' worth. Organized. A camp the size of a town, strung along the cliffs south of the altar road, between them and the ninth stone.

Vane had arrived first.

Of course she had. Roads, wagons, a warrant, an army—Vane traveled the way law travels, which is faster than hope. Zephyrine counted fires until counting seemed pointless. Fifty? A hundred? Enough.

Fellhand appeared at her shoulder, following her gaze. He was quiet a long moment.

*They are many,* he said at last.

"Yes."

*We are not.* He considered. *But we are awake, and they are only armed. There is a difference, though I grant you armies have historically failed to appreciate it.*

Despite everything, Zephyrine laughed—a short, cracked, genuine sound that startled a nearby wolf into lifting its head.

Below them, the coast road wound down toward the cliffs, and beyond the cliffs the sea waited, and beyond the sea the dawn was coming whether anyone swore any oaths or not. One day's walk. Maybe less. At the end of it, an altar, an army, and a choice she had already made, waiting for her to say it out loud at last.

Zephyrine touched the crocus in her cloak, woke Wren gently, and started down.
