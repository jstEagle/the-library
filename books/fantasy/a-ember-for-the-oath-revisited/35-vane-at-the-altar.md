## 35. Vane at the Altar

The ninth stone stood at the edge of the world like a tooth that had refused to fall out.

Zephyrine had imagined it a hundred ways on the climb—buried, hidden, guarded by riddles or roots—but it was none of those things. It simply stood, a pillar of grey granite taller than three men, on a shelf of wind-scoured rock above the sea. The cliff fell away beyond it into a grey-green nothing that breathed. Gulls wheeled and cried. The salt wind pulled at her cloak with the impatient hands of a hostess who has been waiting all day.

Carved into the stone's face, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather, was a spiral. The same spiral Wren had drawn in ash on her hearth a lifetime ago, in a valley that now felt like a story someone had told her.

And below the altar, spread across the headland like spilled ink, was the army.

Fires. Horses. The grey cloaks of the Oathwardens, hundreds of them, ranked in quiet squares. Banners with the crowned quill—the sigil of the Silence, the law that had eaten her whole life. At their center, on a white mare that stamped and rolled its eyes at the wind, sat a woman in darker grey with a ledger strapped to her saddle.

Serah Vane had stopped hunting quietly, just as Maren had warned.

Zephyrine stood at the altar with Wren's hand in hers. The child was light. That was the thing she could not stop noticing, the thing that sat in her chest like a stone of its own: Wren had grown *lighter* with every stone they woke, as if the covenant were spending them the way a candle spends itself. Four stones burned now across the wild places—she could feel them the way you feel warmth from a hearth in the next room. Flowers bloomed through snow in the high passes. Rivers sang. And the child who had made it possible weighed less than her cloak.

"They came fast," Zephyrine said. Her voice, in the true tongue, was a strange instrument—she could feel it landing in the gulls, in the grass, in the stone itself, understood by all of it. She had not yet learned to whisper in it. She suspected she never would.

"They were always coming," Wren answered. The child's voice had grown thinner too, but the eyes were the same—deer-bright, unafraid. "This is where the first word was spoken. They would not let it end anywhere else."

Below, a horn sounded. Not a war-horn. Something older and flatter, a single sustained note that made the gulls scatter and the horses scream. A single rider detached from the ranks and began the climb to the altar.

Zephyrine knew the horse before she knew the rider. She had heard it scream in her memory a hundred times—the white mare, the ford, the flood of birdsong she had loosed to save them. Serah Vane climbed the last stretch of the cliff path at a walk, unhurried, reins loose, the way a woman climbs who owns the ground she stands on.

She stopped ten paces from the stone. Close enough to talk. Far enough, Zephyrine noted, to draw and loose before Zephyrine could cross the distance.

"Zephyrine Winterbourne." Vane's voice was dry and even, a ledger read aloud. "Of Hollowmere. Thirty years unregistered. Unlicensed gift, class three, beast-speech. Accessory to the waking of four sealed stones. Harborer of an unregistered—" her eyes flicked to Wren, and for just a moment the ledger-voice caught, like a cart wheel hitting a rut, "—child."

"You've been reading my file," Zephyrine said. "I'd hoped it would be longer. I've been very busy."

Something twitched at the corner of Vane's mouth. Not a smile. The ghost of one, maybe, visiting from a younger woman.

"I'm not here to fight you," Vane said.

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "You brought four hundred men to *not* fight me. I noticed. Very peaceful, the way they've ringed the cliff."

"They're here for what comes after. If you refuse." Vane dismounted—slowly, Zephyrine saw, and with a stiffness in her left side that had not been there at the ford. The flood of remembered birdsong had scattered her horses and her men, but it had not left her untouched either. "I'm here to offer you the last mercy the law allows."

"Go on."

"Surrender the seal-child." Vane said it plainly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Come down the hill with me, quietly. Testify. Cooperate. In return, the child is taken into wardship instead of executed publicly. You will be confined, not broken. You will live."

Zephyrine looked at her for a long moment. The wind filled the silence between them, and in the true tongue, silence was never empty—she could hear the sea listening, the stone listening, the four hundred men below straining to hear a conversation too far away to follow.

"You know," Zephyrine said at last, "that's the second time someone has offered to take a child from a woman in my family at this border. The first time, the woman said yes."

Vane's brow creased. "What?"

"Thirty-one years ago. A young woman crossed the ward-line into Hollowmere carrying a baby. She'd made a bargain with the ward itself—her gift, her voice, her whole life, in exchange for the child's silence. The ward took her as its anchor. It held her there until it had wrung her dry, and then it let her die in a house full of goats, alone, while her daughter cooked her meals and never once heard her mother say a true word." Zephyrine's voice did not shake. She had spent thirty years learning not to shake, and it turned out the training held. "Her name was Ede. You won't find her in any ledger. That was rather the point of her."

"I don't see what—"

"You asked for the child. I'm telling you what it costs when women like me say yes to women like you." She turned, deliberately, so that she faced not Vane but the cliff's edge—and beyond it, the army. The wind carried her voice down the slope the way it carries gull-cry, into every ear, into every listening thing. "So no. I won't surrender her. But I'll tell you what I *will* do, Serah Vane. I'll do the one thing the Silence was built to prevent. I'm going to talk."

* * *

Serah Vane had heard heretics sing, scream, curse, and weep. She had never heard one *begin a speech* at her own execution, and it unsettled her more than she let show.

She had come up this cliff with a simple script. Offer the mercy. Record the refusal. Give the order. Clean, documented, lawful. The woman was supposed to be frightened. They were all frightened, in the end—the hawk-witch at the ford, the herb-wife on the bridge who had held her vanguard for a day with nothing but hedge-sparks and a stubborn smile before going down under nets, the wolves that watched from the ridgelines with their yellow patience. Fear was the mortar of order. Vane had built her whole life on it.

But Zephyrine Winterbourne stood at the altar with a dying child's hand in hers, and she did not look frightened. She looked like a woman setting a table.

"Soldiers of the Oath!" the witch called, and her voice went out over the headland impossibly clear, the way a bell is clear, reaching every man without seeming to raise itself. "I know most of you by now. I've been running from you for a season, and running gives a person time to look. You have good boots. You share your rations with your horses before you eat yourselves. Half of you have letters from home in your breast pockets that you read by firelight and don't show each other." A ripple went through the ranks—embarrassment, recognition. "I know because the crows told me. And the crows, I'll remind you, are on the list of things your law says cannot speak."

"Enough," Vane said quietly. It was not an order yet. It was a warning to herself.

"Four hundred of you," the witch went on. "So let me tell you what your silence has cost, and you can each decide on the climb down whether it was worth your boots."

She told them about the valley. Not Hollowmere by name—she was careful, Vane noticed, careful the way a surgeon is careful—but *a valley*, any valley, a valley like the ones most of them had been born in. She told them about a woman who had lived thirty years among neighbors who never learned her name, who traded herbs for eggs and counted her words like coins because a woman who talks too much gets reported. She told them about the night the woman's mother died, and how the daughter had knelt by the bed and spoken to her in the beast-speech because it was the only language left in the house where a true word could be said, and how the dying woman had understood every syllable, because she had paid for that gift with her life and had never once regretted it.

Vane's hand tightened on the ledger strap. *Stop her,* said the part of her that had written doctrine for fifteen years. *This is sedition. This is the thing itself.*

She did not move. She wanted to hear the end of the sentence about the mother. She hated herself for it. She listened anyway.

The witch told them about Maren—Maren of Ashmoor, who had mended broken bones and birthed babies for twenty years until the wardens came, and who two weeks ago had stood on a bridge in the snow and held back an armed column with sparks and stubbornness so that a child could climb a mountain. "She is in your chains right now," the witch said. "Ask her what she regrets. She'll tell you: the bridge was too short."

Someone in the ranks, near the front, made a sound. Vane knew the men of her vanguard. She was almost certain she knew whose voice it was.

She told them about the wolves. This was the part that made the horses stamp and the youngest soldiers look at the treelines. She told them about rivers forgetting how to flow, forests losing the names of their own trees, a long winter coming that would not kill the world but *empty* it—every wild thing and every human thing forgetting the other existed, until the forests were just wood and the animals were just meat and the old shrines were just stones, and no one wept at any of it because no one remembered there had ever been anything to weep for.

"You think the Silence protects you from the wild," she said. "But the Silence doesn't distinguish. It doesn't take the wolf's voice and leave yours. It takes *the conversation*. And a world that has stopped talking to itself forgets, Serah Vane. It forgets on both sides of the fence."

"You watched the wild take children," Vane said.

Her own voice surprised her. It came out of the fifteen-year-old ledger, out of the burning village, out of the girl who had crouched in a root cellar while the hedgerows walked and the river stood up and the village's children were *called*, singing, out of their beds, and never came back. She had not meant to say it here. She had never said it anywhere.

Zephyrine Winterbourne turned from the cliff and looked at her—looked at her the way no one had looked at her in thirty years, not as a warrant or a wall but as a wound.

"Yes," the witch said gently. "I know. The stone under the glacier showed me. It shows everyone their own truth, in the end; that's how it tests you. I saw yours, Serah Vane. I saw a girl in a cellar listening to the hedgerow sing." She paused. "I'm not going to tell you it wasn't real. It was real. The last covenant failed, and when it fails, the wild goes mad with grief, and grief doesn't check whose children it takes. That is exactly why the covenant must be remade—not shattered. A broken oath doesn't end the wild. It just leaves it unanswerable."

"The law—"

"Is a wall built over a door," Zephyrine said. "And you have spent your life guarding the wall so hard you've forgotten there was ever a door, or that on the other side of it is half of everything you love. Your boots. Your letters from home. Your horse, who has been trying to tell you for two days that her left hind shoe is loose, and who is going to throw you on the descent if you don't listen to her."

Vane's head snapped toward the white mare. The mare looked back at her with one liquid eye and lifted, pointedly, her left hind foot.

The ranks below made a sound that no doctrine had a name for. It might have been laughter. It might have been something older.

* * *

Zephyrine felt the moment tip.

It was a thing she could feel now, in the true tongue—the way a room tips, the way a held breath decides. Four hundred men had climbed this cliff expecting a witch, and instead they had been introduced to a horse's sore foot and a dead woman named Ede and a healer chained in the baggage train, and the wall of them had developed, all along its length, small hairline cracks of *maybe*.

Vane felt it too. Zephyrine watched her feel it—the commander's spine straightening, the ledger-hand flexing, the rut in her voice smoothing back over with fifteen years of doctrine.

"You speak beautifully," Vane said. "That's what they all say about your kind. Words like honey over a wasp's nest. But I've read the old accounts, Winterbourne. I know what the covenant cost the *last* time. Nine seal-children, and eight of them dead, and the ninth standing there weighing less than her own coat. You're not offering these men a door. You're offering them a fire, and calling the burning a warmth." She raised her voice, and it carried down the headland with the flat authority of a bell that has never once been wrong. "Zephyrine Winterbourne. I have offered you the law's mercy and you have refused it. Witnessed and recorded. At my signal, my men will take the altar, take the child, and shatter the ninth stone, and the long winter will be stopped the only way it can be stopped—by finishing it. By ending the conversation *cleanly*, on our terms, before it ends us on its—"

"Serah." Zephyrine's voice was quiet now, and somehow it carried further than the loudness had. "Look behind me."

Vane stopped.

Zephyrine turned. And along the cliff-top, out of the wind and the sea-haze, the wolves came.

Not charging. *Walking.* Dozens of them, then more, grey and white and winter-thin, pouring along the ridge on both sides until they ringed the altar in a great silent crescent, and behind them, impossibly, other things—stags with frost on their antlers, a bear the color of wet slate, hawks dropping out of the sky to perch on the altar stone itself, on the ninth stone's worn spiral, like a congregation taking its seats. The land that remembered had come to attend.

The soldiers below did not charge. Four hundred men stood in their squares and looked at the wild looking back at them, and no one breathed.

"The last time the covenant broke," Zephyrine said, "the wild came for your village mad with grief, and it took children, and you have hated it rightly ever since. So look at it now, Serah Vane. Look hard. Is this grief?" The wolves sat. All of them, at once, like a congregation. "This is a funeral procession and a wedding party and I honestly could not tell you which. But it is not madness. It came when it was *called*. It came for the child who is the last word of the old promise. And it will do what I do next."

She turned to Wren. The child's eyes were half-closed, burning, flickering like a lamp running low—but they focused, and they found Zephyrine's face, and they were not afraid.

"Are you ready?" Zephyrine asked her.

"You first," Wren whispered, and smiled, and it was the same smile that had wept with relief in a kitchen full of ash-spirals a whole life ago. "You always make it look easy."

Zephyrine stepped to the stone. Below, Vane's hand went to her signal-horn. The army tensed. The wolves rose.

And Zephyrine Winterbourne, who had spent thirty years learning to be silent, opened her mouth to speak her whole life into the covenant—and saw, past Vane's shoulder, down the long climb, a small grey figure on a lathered horse cresting the headland road at a desperate gallop, riding like the devil himself was the one chasing him.

Tobias Grell.

And he was shouting something, over and over, words the wind tore apart, words that were not for her.

They were for Vane.
