## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The ledgers never balanced on the first pass. That was the point.

Cyrus Eriksen ran his thumb down the column and let the numbers lie to him for a moment longer — the way the Compact liked its lies, neat, ruled, double-entered. Three vials of the River Vell, bottled at its source before it learned to be a sea. Fourteen borrowed years, drawn against a farmer's granddaughter who would not be born for another decade. One jar of thunder, cracked in transit, the loss written off against the estate of a man who had traded his own death to avoid paying for it.

He dipped his pen. He did not write anything yet.

Around him the guildhall of the Cartographers' Compact went about its evening business with the hush of a church that dealt in merchandise. Clerks moved between the shelves with lamps, tending the maps the way other people tended relics. And they were relics, some of them — charts of coastlines that had not existed since the Sundering, city plans for places that had never been built but might be, if someone paid enough. The Compact's whole trade was this: that somewhere, everything already existed, and all you needed was the right piece of paper to go find it.

Cyrus was good at his work because he was careful, and he was careful because he had something to hide.

He could read them. All of them. Not the way the senior cartographers read — squinting at scale bars and compass roses, arguing over projections. Cyrus looked at a map and it opened. Not visibly. Nothing so useful as that. But he'd look at a chart of the Amber Coast and suddenly know the smell of the fish markets there, the way the tide sucked at the pilings, the name of a woman he'd never met who sold oranges wrapped in damp cloth. The knowledge arrived like a memory of a place he'd never been, and it always left him slightly seasick, as if he'd been somewhere without his body's permission.

He had told no one. At fourteen, when the gift — curse, affliction, whatever the masters would have called it — first showed itself, he'd watched what happened to the boy who could sing any song after hearing it once. They hadn't made him a master. They'd made him an *asset*, locked in a room with foreign hymnals until his nose bled. So Cyrus kept his head down, balanced his ledgers, and read maps alone at night with the shutters closed, letting the world pour into him one coastline at a time.

"Ledger Nine is short again." Maren stood at his desk, arms folded, her gray braid looped through itself in the style she'd worn since before he was born. She was the archivist, which meant she was the only person in the building whose authority outranked her salary. "Page forty. The bottled river."

"It's not short. It's *misfiled*." He turned the page around. "Someone entered the third vial under Salvage instead of Acquisition. Which means someone acquired a river and didn't want it noticed."

Maren's eyes narrowed the particular way they did when she smelled a story. "Which someone?"

"If I knew that, I'd be selling the information, not balancing their books."

"You'd be reporting it."

"I'd consider it," Cyrus said, and Maren snorted, and the moment passed the way such moments always passed in the guildhall: noted, filed, forgotten. That was the arrangement. She suspected he was more than a bookkeeper; he suspected she knew; neither of them ever said it out loud, because saying it would make it real, and real things required decisions.

Outside, the rain had started. It came off the harbor in long gray sheets, and the lamplighters were giving up early, and Cyrus decided — reasonably, he'd have said later — that he would finish the column, lock the ledger, and go home to his narrow rooms where nothing was impossible except the rent.

He was blotting the last entry when the knock came.

Not a knock, really. A collapse against wood. He crossed the hall — past the map vaults, past the duty clerk's empty chair, past the great framed chart of the Known World that hung in the atrium like a saint's portrait — and pulled open the guild's side door onto the rain.

The man fell inward.

Cyrus caught most of his weight, which wasn't much. The stranger was soaked through, gaunt in the way of men who had stopped eating on purpose rather than from poverty, and his eyes had the fixed brightness of a fever burning through its final fuel. His right hand was pressed flat against his chest, and beneath it, dark liquid seeped between his fingers — too slow, too steady, and coming from nowhere Cyrus could see. There was no wound. There was just blood, arriving, as if summoned.

"Guild," the man rasped. "You're the guild."

"The Compact, yes — sit down, I'll fetch—"

"No." The grip on Cyrus's wrist was shocking, cold and strong. "No clerks. No masters. You." His gaze found Cyrus's face and held it with terrible precision, the way a dying man looks at the last thing he intends to see. "You're the one who reads them. I've asked in nine cities. *You.*"

Cyrus's mouth went dry. "I balance ledgers."

"Then balance this." The stranger fumbled inside his coat and brought out a roll of oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, stained dark along one edge. He pushed it into Cyrus's hands with the urgency of a man passing off a brand. "It has to be read. It has to be *carried*. It won't stop changing until someone reads it true."

"What is it?"

"A map." The man laughed, or coughed, or both; it came out wet. "A map of the road to King Aldric. What's left of him."

Cyrus knew the name the way everyone knew it — from history, from the old coins still turning up in market stalls. Aldric the Oathbound, crowned twenty years ago and deposed within the season by his queen-regent Veyra, who wore the crown herself now and had worn it, rumor said, without aging a day since. The Compact had a standing policy on royal politics, which was to price access to both sides and sell to neither.

"There is no road to Aldric," Cyrus said carefully. "There hasn't been since the coup. He's either dead or—"

"He's a *door*," the stranger said, "and she nailed it shut from the inside. Listen to me. Listen." He sagged, and Cyrus lowered him against the doorframe, kneeling in the wet. "I drew it. Every league of it. Sef Holm, royal cartographer, that's the name, hold onto the name, because the map will try to make you forget it—" His breath hitched. "It costs. Reading it costs. You'll understand soon. When you dream."

"Dream what?"

But Sef Holm — if that was who he was, and some cold part of Cyrus already believed it completely — had stopped looking at him. His eyes had gone past Cyrus's shoulder into the hall, to the great chart of the Known World, and something in his face broke open, grief and fury and homesickness all at once.

"The crown remembers," he whispered.

"What? What does that mean?"

"The crown *remembers*. Everything sworn on it. Everything eaten. It keeps the oaths, boy, it keeps them all, and it's *hungry*—" His hand found Cyrus's collar and pulled him close, close enough that Cyrus could feel the heat pouring off him like a banked forge. "Don't give it back to the guild. Whatever they offer. Whatever they threaten. The Compact sold the coronation once already. Don't let them sell it twice."

"I don't know what you're talking about—"

"I know." And the terrible thing, the thing Cyrus would turn over for years afterward, was how gentle the stranger sounded. How forgiving. "That's the mercy of it. You don't know yet. But you will, and then you'll hate me a little, and that's fair."

His grip loosened. His hand slid from Cyrus's collar, leaving the oilcloth bundle behind, and he tipped sideways against the doorframe with his eyes still open, still fixed on the middle distance, on some map only he could see.

By the time Cyrus thought to shout for help, there was nothing left to help. The rain came in through the open door and pooled around them both, and upstairs a clerk laughed at something, and the guildhall went on being a place where impossibilities were safely priced and filed away.

Cyrus knelt there a long moment, the bundle heavy in his lap, water soaking through his trousers, doing the arithmetic he was trained for. A dead man on the guild's doorstep. A stolen chart, by the sound of it — stolen *from* the guild, or from whoever the guild had sold it to. A standing order, delivered with a dying man's last strength: don't surrender it.

Every rule he had, every safe wall of his ordered life, said the same thing. Wake the duty clerk. Report it. Let the masters decide. It wasn't his to carry. Nothing was ever his to carry; that was why the ledgers balanced, because every weight belonged to somebody else.

But his thumb had already found the waxed cord, and the knot gave at a touch, as if it had been waiting nine years — though he couldn't have said why that number came to him, or why it arrived with the smell of pine resin and cold mountain air, or the image of a coronation tent seen from the back of a crowd, all of it sudden and specific and *not his*.

He unrolled the chart halfway. Just enough.

Ink crawled across the parchment like things waking. Coastlines shifted. A road drew itself westward, then north, then somewhere that made his eyes ache to follow — and underneath the ink, faint as a watermark, a second hand was writing, line after line, in a script that hurt to look at because some part of him understood it perfectly:

*Third day out of Halveth. The forest moved again. I have started naming the trees after what I've lost.*

Cyrus rolled the map shut so fast he nearly tore it.

For a while he just breathed. Rain. Lamplight. The dead man's cooling shoulder against his knee.

Then he did the only thing his life had prepared him for. He folded the stranger's coat closed with something like respect, wiped the blood from his own hands as best he could, and hid the map inside the ledger box — because if the Compact taught anything, it was that the safest place for an impossible thing was inside an accurate record of everything else.

Tomorrow, Maren would ask questions. Tomorrow, the masters would notice a body. Tomorrow, the arithmetic would come due.

Tonight, Cyrus Eriksen carried his ledgers home through the rain, one box heavier than it should have been, listening to the harbor wind for footsteps — and in the back of his skull, patient as ink drying, a voice that was not his began to hum a coronation hymn.

* * *

He dreamed, that night, of a crown.
