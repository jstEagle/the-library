## 3. Roads the Guild Never Drew

The road east should have taken eleven days. Cyrus knew because he had drawn it — or rather, the Compact had, in 1409, by a surveyor named Pell who'd died of a fever two towns later and whose careful hand Cyrus had traced a hundred times in the guildhall's archive. Eleven days. A river crossing at Brant's Ford, a toll bridge at Kessel, then the long gray climb through the Vharen Pass to the capital plain.

He was four days out of the city when the map took him off that road entirely.

It happened at dusk, at a crossroads so minor it appeared on no chart he'd ever inked. He'd stopped to check his bearings, unrolled Sef Holm's chart across a flat stone, and found the road he stood on simply *gone* — replaced by a line of fine red thread that hadn't been there that morning, running north into country marked only with the old cartographer's curse: *here be regret.*

"That's not a direction," Cyrus said aloud. "That's an apology."

The map said nothing, but the red line pulsed faintly, like something breathing under parchment.

He told himself he was following it because the main road was watched. The Compact would have sent men after him — quiet ones, patient ones, the kind who asked questions with their hands. That was true. It was also true that when he packed the chart away and turned north, his feet knew the way before he did, as if the map had already walked him there and was only waiting for the rest of him to catch up.

* * *

The forest began where the maps ended.

Cyrus noticed it first as a wrongness in the light — green too deep for autumn, shadows falling at angles the sun couldn't justify. Then the path behind him closed. Not overgrown: *closed*, the way a sentence closes when you decide not to say it. One moment there was a track; the next, unbroken trees, and the certainty that no path had ever run there at all.

"Right," he said, to the forest, to the map, to whatever was listening. "You're one of those."

He walked. The trees rearranged themselves at the edge of vision — never while he looked directly, always just after, like a word on the tip of the tongue. And the forest *knew things*. He passed a clearing where someone had built a fire ring from river stones, and the stones were warm, and he understood without knowing how that this was a fire Sef Holm had built, thirty years ago, with a woman whose name Cyrus suddenly ached with missing.

He sat down hard against a root.

"This is what you do," he whispered. "You rearrange yourself around what people can't let go of."

The forest rustled, which was not agreement but was not denial either. Somewhere ahead, the red thread on the chart — he could feel it now even rolled up, a warmth against his ribs like a second heartbeat — pulled gently eastward. Toward the capital. But not by any road a sane man would take.

He got up and followed it anyway.

* * *

Maren found the third body in the guildhall's lower stacks.

Not a body — she corrected herself, kneeling — a *record*. The Compact kept its failures the way other institutions kept their triumphs: carefully, alphabetically, in ledgers bound in gray calfskin that no one opened twice. This one covered the years of the succession crisis, and it fell open, as if it had been waiting nine years for her thumb, to a page headed **HOLM, SEF — ROYAL CARTOGRAPHER — STATUS: WRITTEN OFF**.

Written off. As though a man were a bad debt.

She read by candle stub what the Compact had known and buried: that Holm had drawn the true route of Aldric's coronation procession; that he had witnessed the oath; that three days after the usurpation he had requested leave and never returned it; that a recovery team dispatched after him reported finding his camp burned and his charts scattered, all recovered except one, described in a clerk's cramped hand as *"a working chart of the capital approaches, value negligible."*

Value negligible. Maren almost laughed. The Compact had put a price on everything for four hundred years, and the one time it mattered, they'd priced a kingdom's salvation at nothing because it wasn't for sale.

"You knew," she said to the ledger, to the dead clerk, to Archivist Corren upstairs who signed the writs for the retrieval teams. "You all knew."

Footsteps above. She closed the ledger, left it exactly as found, and climbed the stairs with her face arranged into the mild blankness the guild prized. In the corridor, Corren himself was giving instructions to two men she didn't recognize — not guild, not guild-adjacent, the kind of quiet who cost more than a senior archivist earned in a decade.

"The deserter went east," Corren was saying. "Our young cartographer will follow the chart, and the chart follows the crown. Let them lead us to it. Nothing leaves the Compact's books unpaid."

Maren bowed and walked on, counting her heartbeats until her hands stopped shaking.

Then she went to her rooms and began packing for a journey she would deny planning.

* * *

On the fifth day in the forest, Cyrus met the soldier.

He heard her before he saw her — the small honest sounds of someone making camp who expected no one: a tin cup set down, water poured, a blade being honed in slow, disciplined strokes. He came around a stand of birches whose bark had been carved, generations deep, with names worn to ghosts, and found a woman sitting on a fallen log with a sword across her knees and eyes that measured him before he'd finished deciding whether to run.

"Cartographer," she said. Not a question. She nodded at the tube slung across his back. "I've seen your kind before. You hold those like a priest holds relics."

"I hold them like debt instruments," Cyrus said. "That's what they are, where I'm from."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile. "And where are you from?"

"A place that sells impossible things and pretends the selling is neutral." He didn't sit, though every muscle wanted to. "You're a long way from any garrison."

"I'm a long way from everything I was in." She turned the blade, sheathed it, deliberate, letting him see her hands stay open. "Tessa Vael. Fourth Banner of the Queen's— " she stopped, jaw tightening. "Of *Veyra's* army. Formerly. Three weeks formerly."

"A deserter."

"A realist." She studied him with uncomfortable thoroughness. "You're going east. Everyone going east is going to the capital, and everyone going to the capital is either a fool, a spy, or a man carrying something the crown wants. Which are you?"

"All three, some days." He hesitated — and then, because the forest had spent five days teaching him that secrets were just debts accruing interest, he unslung the tube. "I'm carrying a dead man's map. It redraws itself. It's taking me somewhere I didn't choose."

Tessa went very still. "Describe the man."

"Tall. Left-handed, from the ink wear. Royal cartographer, once. His name was Sef Holm."

The sound she made was small and terrible — grief compressed into a single syllable, held down by discipline. "My father drank with Sef Holm," she said finally. "Before. My father said he was the only honest man at court, which is why he lasted exactly as long as he did." She looked up. "You have his memories in you. Don't argue — you flinched when I said his name like it hurt, and you've been talking to yourself since you entered this clearing. They all do that. The ones who carry him."

"How would you know—"

"Because I've seen what the crown does." She stood, and up close Cyrus could see the hollows under her eyes, the particular gauntness of someone who had stopped sleeping rather than keep dreaming. "I stood guard in the throne room for two years. I watched the king — the *real* king, Aldric — sit under that thing while it drank him. He used to try to speak. Some nights he'd get a whole sentence out before it took him back. Do you know what he said? Over and over?" Her voice didn't waver, which must have cost her enormously. "*'Tell them I remember.'*"

The forest had gone silent around them. Even the rearranging had stopped, as if it were listening.

"He's alive," Tessa said. "Whatever's left of him. Hollow-eyed behind the crown, but alive. And Veyra sits beside him getting younger every year, and everyone at court has decided not to notice, because noticing is the first thing the crown eats." She picked up her pack. "So. Are we walking east together, cartographer, or am I going to follow you at a distance and pretend that's safer?"

Cyrus looked at her — at the deserter's guilt worn like armor, at the sword she'd sheathed to prove a point — and felt the map warm against his ribs, patient as a creditor.

"Together," he said. "But I should warn you. I don't think I'm choosing this route. I think the map is. And I don't know yet what it wants in exchange."

Tessa shouldered her pack. "Nothing's free. My father taught me that, and he learned it from your dead man." She nodded east, into the trees that were already, subtly, rearranging themselves to make room for two travelers instead of one. "Then let's go find out the price."

They walked. Behind them, the clearing with its warm fire ring faded back into unbroken forest, keeping its memory, spending nothing.

Ahead, the red thread pulled toward the capital — and, Cyrus noticed with a chill he couldn't name, toward a fork the map had just drawn where no fork had been: one branch marked in Sef Holm's sure hand, the other in ink still wet.

As if someone else were drawing now.
