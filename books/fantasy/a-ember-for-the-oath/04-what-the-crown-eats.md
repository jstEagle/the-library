## 4. What the Crown Eats

Tessa built the fire small, the way you did when you didn't want to be found. She'd learned that in the army — Veyra's army, though she still caught herself calling it the king's army sometimes, out of habit, out of shame. Small fires. Cold camps. Names never spoken above a whisper.

The cartographer sat across from her with the map rolled shut against his chest like a child holding a stolen sweet. He hadn't let go of it since the forest. Since the trees had rearranged themselves around something he'd said — or something the dead man inside him had said — and opened a road that shouldn't have been there.

"You want to know what I saw," she said. "At court."

"I want to know if the king is alive."

"He's alive." She fed a twig to the flames and watched it catch. "That's not the same as living."

* * *

She'd been nineteen when she was posted to the capital, a spear-carrier in the palace guard, proud of it in the way you're proud of anything at nineteen. The usurpation was three years old by then, old enough that most people had stopped calling it that. Veyra ruled. Aldric sat beside her on a smaller throne, and everyone pretended this had always been the arrangement.

Tessa had stood post outside the great hall during the evening courts, which meant she'd watched hundreds of petitioners climb the dais and kneel before the crown and swear their little oaths. Loyalty oaths. Debt oaths. A miller swearing his grain weights were honest. A widow swearing her husband's last words named Veyra regent forever.

It was ceremony. Everyone knew it was ceremony.

Except she started noticing what happened after.

The petitioners would leave the hall looking — lighter. That was the only word for it. Lighter, and emptier, like houses after a moving cart. They'd smile too easily. They'd agree to anything. And they never came back to complain, because complaining required remembering what you'd lost, and they couldn't quite reach it anymore.

"The crown eats oaths," she told Cyrus now, poking the fire apart with a stick. "But that's not even the worst of it. It doesn't just take the oath. It takes everything braided into the oath. You swear loyalty to your city, it takes your love of your city. You swear on your mother's grave, it takes your mother. Not all of her. Just enough. Just the warm parts."

Cyrus had gone very still. "And the king?"

"Aldric swore first. Biggest oath there is." She made herself say it plainly, because softening it would be a lie. "The coronation oath. The whole realm poured into six sentences. The crown drank him down to the shape of a man. He sits, he nods, he signs what Veyra puts in front of him. Sometimes he weeps without knowing why. My sergeant said it was piety." Her laugh came out wrong. "Piety."

"And Veyra?"

Tessa looked at him across the fire. He was younger than she'd expected from his reputation — guild men usually looked older, calcified. This one looked like someone who'd had a safe life until recently and hadn't finished grieving it.

"She gets younger," Tessa said. "You understand what I'm telling you? When she took the throne she was forty if she was a day. Grey in her temples. Now she looks thirty. Every year she looks younger while everyone around her forgets more. She's drinking the years out of other people's lives through that thing on her head."

* * *

He asked about Sef Holm eventually. Of course he did. It was the map that made him ask — he'd unrolled it a handspan, just enough to check their position, and his face had done something complicated, like a man listening to two conversations at once.

"You know whose map this is," Tessa said.

"Sef Holm. Royal cartographer. Missing nine years." He said it like recitation. Then, quieter: "I dream in his voice sometimes. I know things I shouldn't know. The way the river bends below the capital before you can see it. The sound the crown makes when it feeds — he heard it once, through a wall. Like teeth."

Tessa's hands stopped moving.

"My father drew maps too," she said. "Not for the guild. For himself. Coastlines, mostly. He and Sef Holm were friends before any of this — before the plague of forgetting, before Veyra, before everything went sideways. Sef used to come to our house. He taught me to read contour lines when I was eight. He said a map was just a memory of the land, and land never lied, only people did."

"What happened to him?"

"Nobody knows. Nine years ago he walked into the palace with a commission from the king himself and never walked out. Officially he resigned and emigrated. Unofficially—" She shrugged. "Unofficially, my father spent four years asking questions and then stopped asking questions, and a year after that he stopped doing much of anything at all. Sat in a chair. Forgot how to be angry. The forgetting took the people who looked hardest. Maybe that's why Sef ran. Maybe that's why he carried that chart until his heart gave out at a stranger's door."

Cyrus was staring at the fire. "He gave it to me on purpose. He chose the door."

"Maybe." Tessa turned the stick over. "Or maybe the map chose you. I've watched it pick roads, cartographer. Forests opening where forests don't go. It knows where it's going better than you do."

"That's not possible."

"Neither is a man dreaming in a dead man's voice." She met his eyes. "His memories live in you now. Whatever bled off that chart when he died — it went somewhere, and it went into you. Which means part of Sef Holm has been walking east with us this whole time. Toward the palace he escaped."

* * *

They didn't talk for a while after that. The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

Tessa used the silence for what she always used silence for: the trial she conducted against herself nightly, verdict already written.

She had been there. That was the thing she couldn't get past. She'd stood post in the great hall the day Veyra's soldiers cleared the gallery and the crown was brought out and set on Aldric's head, and she'd felt the change come through the floor like a struck bell, and she'd known — known in her body, the way you know a load-bearing wall just cracked — that something monstrous had happened.

And she'd stood there. Held her spear. Saluted.

Desertion came later, two years later, the night her orders were to escort a wagonload of seized records to a burning pit outside the walls. Archives. Parish rolls. Letters. Everything written down about who people had been. She'd looked at the fire eating the paper and understood that the burning wasn't destruction, it was digestion — Veyra feeding the realm to the crown one page at a time — and she'd put down her spear and walked away from the column and kept walking for three days.

She should have fought. She knew that. One guard with a spear against a garrison wouldn't have changed anything, but it would have been true, and truth was getting scarce, and she'd chosen survival instead. Every day since, she'd been paying interest on that choice.

Now here was this cartographer with a dead man's memories in his skull and a chart that wanted to go to the capital, and Tessa looked at him and thought: *here*. Here is the fight I skipped. Here is the debt coming due.

"You're going to need witnesses," she said finally.

"Witnesses?"

"Of the coronation. The original oath." She poked the coals flat. "An oath can be broken by the ones who heard it sworn. That's old law, older than Veyra. But the witnesses have to speak it whole, and speaking it — near the crown, or near anything of the crown's — costs them. The crown takes back what it's owed. From whoever's closest."

"How do you know that?"

"Because my father told me. Because Sef Holm told my father." She looked up. "Because the crown remembers, cartographer. That's what the dying man said, isn't it? Well, remember this too: the crown doesn't just eat. It keeps accounts. And it collects."

Cyrus pulled his cloak tighter. In the firelight his face had the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like — counting miles, counting witnesses, counting whatever price waited at the end of the road.

"There are three left," Tessa said. "Three who stood in that hall and heard every word. A priestess gone blind. A knight stripped of his name. And a child who was there — a child who'd be old now." She stood, brushing ash from her knees, and pointed east, where the dark had begun to thin toward grey. "The map will know where. Ask it. Or ask him. Whichever of them answers."

She turned to bank the fire, and behind her she heard it — faint, half a breath, in a voice that wasn't quite the cartographer's:

*"I know where they are. I've always known."*
