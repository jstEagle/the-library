## 5. The Witnesses of the Oath

The map led them to a woman who had been blind for forty years and who, Tessa swore, had been the most beautiful thing at court the day Aldric was crowned.

"She sang the invocation," Tessa said as they climbed the last hill above the salt marsh. "Half the knights wept. My father said her voice could make a stone feel homesick."

"And now?"

"Now she keeps a lighthouse that hasn't guided a ship in a decade." Tessa adjusted the strap of her stolen army pack and didn't look at him. "The crown took her eyes, Cyrus. First payment on the oath she witnessed."

The lighthouse stood at the marsh's edge like a finger raised in warning, its lamp long dark, gulls nesting in the gallery. The door was unlocked. Everything about the place said: I have stopped defending myself against the world.

Her name was Sister Odile, and she was small and straight-backed in a chair by a cold hearth, and when they entered she said, without turning, "Three sets of boots. One drags — desertion does that to a march. One is new and guilty. And one belongs to a man carrying another man's memories like stones in his pockets."

Cyrus's hand went to the chart tube before he could stop himself.

"You can't see," he began.

"I can see what matters." She smiled, and it was a terrible, gentle smile. "Sit down, cartographer. You've come a long way to ask an old woman to forget her life."

* * *

She fed them barley soup first, because hospitality dies last even in broken women, and while they ate she told them what she remembered of the coronation — not the ceremony, which she had sung, but the moment after, when the crown touched Aldric's brow and every witness felt something *leave* them.

"It wasn't pain," she said. "It was subtraction. Like reaching for a word and finding the space where it lived swept clean. We all felt it. Nine hundred witnesses. And we all decided, together, silently, that it was worth it. A king for a kingdom. What's a memory or two against peace?"

Tessa set down her spoon. "What did you lose?"

"My mother's face." Odile said it the way you'd report the weather. "I had seen it that morning. By evening it was gone, and I told myself the blindness came later, from age, from grief. But I knew. I have always known. The crown doesn't steal randomly. It takes what holds you most tenderly, because that's where the strength is. That's the arithmetic no one teaches children: love is currency, and someone is always spending yours."

Cyrus thought of Sef Holm's memories bleeding into his dreams each night, the dead man's voice growing more familiar than his own father's. "The fragments," he said. "Maren's research says each witness carries a piece of the original oath. If spoken aloud—"

"If spoken aloud, the oath reasserts itself, and the crown must loosen its grip. Yes. I know what your archivist found; Sef wrote to me, before the end." She turned her blind eyes toward him with unnerving precision. "And I know what it costs. To speak my fragment is to unmake the last forty years of my devotion. I would forget why I stayed by this dead light. I would forget the vow itself. I would be a woman who kept a lighthouse for reasons she could not name, until she wandered off one winter and the sea took her."

"Then don't—" Tessa started.

"I didn't say I refuse." Odile folded her hands. "I said you should understand the price before you ask a stranger to pay it. That's the difference between you and Veyra, cartographer. She takes. You must learn to ask."

She asked for one night to decide. Cyrus lay awake in the lighthouse loft listening to the gulls and thinking about ledgers — how the Compact recorded every trade, every debt, every impossible thing bought and sold, but never once recorded what the seller lost. Somewhere below, Odile sat in the dark with her forty years, weighing them.

At dawn she was waiting with her traveling cloak already on.

"I've decided," she said. "I'll pay. But not here. At the place where the oath was sworn, so the memory goes home instead of into the wind. Take me with you."

* * *

The second witness was harder to find and easier to reach: Ser Brandt Ivell, once commander of the kingsguard, now a drunk in a river town that smelled of fish and regret, keeping a tavern he'd won in a card game he didn't remember playing.

He recognized the chart the moment Cyrus unrolled it on his sticky table.

"Sef Holm's hand." He touched the inked coastline with one thick finger, gentle as a man touching a sleeping child. "Nine years missing. They said he ran. They said he stole from the treasury." His jaw worked. "I said those things too. I signed the warrant."

"You were there," Cyrus said. "At the coronation."

"I was there." Brandt poured himself a drink with hands that shook only slightly, a veteran's tremor, controlled. "And I remember my fragment. Every word. Do you know why? Because I recite it every night before I sleep, like a prayer, like a penance. Twenty-two years saying it into my cup so I wouldn't lose it." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "And you want me to say it *out loud*. Once. Properly. And then it's gone, and with it everything I've got left of the man I failed."

"He begged you to end it," Tessa said quietly. "Even if it killed him. Lucid, for one breath."

Brandt's glass stopped halfway to his mouth. "How do you know that?"

"The map remembers."

For a long moment the only sound was the river slapping the pilings and a drunk singing two streets over. Then Brandt set the glass down, untouched, and pushed it away.

"No." He said it calmly, which was worse than shouting. "No. Find another way. Find nine hundred other witnesses. I carried this fragment through twenty years of shame — it's the only thing I did right, holding onto it. You're asking me to spend the one coin I have left on a king who's already dead behind the eyes." He stood, joints cracking. "Get out of my tavern."

Cyrus went. But at the door he turned. "Sef Holm didn't run. He died carrying this map to me — to anyone — because he believed the oath could still be honored. Your fragment isn't a coin, Ser Brandt. It's a debt. And debts don't stay hidden forever."

The drunk two streets over had stopped singing. In the silence, Brandt said nothing at all.

* * *

The third witness they almost missed, because no one expects a witness to be ninety-one years old and feeding chickens.

She had been nine at the coronation — a child lifted onto her father's shoulders to see the new king, close enough that when the crown descended she felt the subtraction pass through the crowd like a cold wind. Her fragment was small, a single line, the closing words of the invocation. She had carried it seventy-nine years without knowing what it was, only that certain words rose in her unbidden every autumn, and that she'd never told a soul, because telling felt like losing.

Her name was Maret. She laughed when they explained.

"So the words in my head belong to a king?" She scattered the last of the grain. "Seventy-nine years I thought I was going mad, a little. Every harvest, *there they are*, like birds coming back to the same roof." She studied Cyrus with bright, shrewd eyes. "And if I say them properly, I lose them. And what do I get?"

"A king unbound," Tessa said. "A realm freed."

"Hm." Maret gathered her eggs, unhurried. "My husband's been dead eleven years. My daughter writes twice a year. You know what I'd lose? I'd lose the autumns. The feeling of those words arriving, like birds. It's a small thing. Small things are all old people have left, and we guard them like dragons." She looked at the sky, judging weather or fate. "But I'll tell you what my father said, lifting me onto his shoulders. He said, *look hard, little one, because being a witness is a job, and jobs get called in.*"

She handed Cyrus the egg basket.

"I'll say my line. At the place, with the others. But I'm keeping the basket. A woman should get something out of a bargain, even a bad one."

* * *

They traveled north together, three witnesses and a deserter and a cartographer haunted by a dead man, toward the coronation site the map burned to reach: the old oath-stone on the plain of Aerlun, where nine hundred voices had once made a king.

On the third night, camped under a sky rinsed clean by rain, Odile spoke into the fire without being asked.

"The knight will come," she said. "Men like Brandt Ivell refuse right up until the moment they can't. Shame is a rope; it holds longer than you think, then it doesn't hold at all."

"And if he doesn't?" Cyrus asked. "If two fragments aren't enough?"

Odile tilted her head, listening to something beyond the fire — beyond, perhaps, the world.

"Then the oath stays broken, and the crown keeps eating, and Veyra marches on cities that trade their names for mercy." She paused. "You know that's happening. Halveth is negotiating even now. Your guild sold you, cartographer; my sources are older than their archives."

Cyrus's blood went cold. "How do you—"

"I was a priestess before I was a lighthouse keeper. The gods gossip." She smiled her terrible gentle smile. "Which means we're out of time. Which means, when we reach the stone, everyone pays at once — willingly or not."

Far to the east, past the dark hills, the plain of Aerlun waited. And somewhere between them and it, Cyrus felt the map shift against his back like a living thing, redrawing its final road — and heard, faint and clear, Sef Holm's voice speak a single word he had never heard in any dream before:

*Hurry.*
