## 6. Veyra's Arithmetic

The crown woke her before the bells did.

It did this some nights — not with sound but with pressure, a tightening at the temples like a band of cold iron being fitted anew. Veyra lay in the dark of the high tower and let it speak, because it only spoke when something was wrong.

*Counter-oaths,* it said, in the voice that was not a voice, the one that lived behind her eyes where the stolen years were stacked like cordwood. *Distant. Three. No — four.*

Four witnesses. She had counted nine, once, on the day she took the throne. Nine living souls who had stood in the coronation hall and heard Aldric swear himself to the realm, and whose hearing had stitched the oath into the world so thoroughly that no usurper could simply cut it out. She had spent nine years finding them. Six were silent now — not dead; dead would have been simpler. Silent, which meant their fragments had been taken from them, or given away, or buried so deep even the crown could not dig them out.

And now four again. Four faint lights guttering somewhere east of her maps, drawing together like embers rolled into a palm.

Veyra rose, wrapped herself in the grey robe she preferred to any crown's ceremony, and went to the window. Below, the capital lay under a thin fall of ash — not from any fire of hers, not tonight, but the wind carried smoke from the road east, where the Third Column had put the archive-city of Brenholt to the torch two days past. The ash fell soft as snow. It always looked gentle from up here. That was the trick of arithmetic: at distance, every cost rounded down.

She pressed her palm against the cold glass and did the sums she did every morning, the ones no minister saw.

The plague had come thirty-one years ago, into the western valleys first. Not a plague of flesh. A plague of *forgetting* — the Grey Quiet, the old texts called it, though there was nothing quiet about it. Villages woke and did not know their own names. Mothers looked at children and saw strangers. Whole towns stood in their squares, mouths moving, reaching for words that had been poured out of them like water from a broken jar. And then the people themselves thinned, because a person is mostly memory, and when the memory went, what remained forgot how to keep breathing.

She had been nineteen. She had watched her mother stand at the edge of a wheat field for three days, trying to remember why the rows mattered, and on the fourth day her mother had smiled at her — a warm, vacant, terrible smile — and asked if they had met.

That was the danger. That was the thing no one else alive understood, because everyone who might have confirmed it was either dead or emptied. The forgetting was not gone. It was *sleeping*. It slept in the low places of the world, in unmapped country, in the folds between realms where cartographers feared to draw. And it woke whenever oaths frayed — because an oath was a knot of memory tied between people, and knots were what the Quiet ate first.

Aldric's coronation oath had been the greatest knot ever tied. A king binding himself body and soul to his people, witnessed by nine, sealed in gold. For eleven years it had held the valleys shut. Then Aldric had grown old, and afraid, and had begun to wonder aloud whether a man should be bound forever to a crown he could not set down — and Veyra, who by then commanded the loyalty of half the army and all of its fear, had made him a offer he was too tired to refuse.

*Let me carry it,* she had told him. *You've carried it long enough.*

She had not lied to him. That was the part the histories would get wrong, if anyone ever wrote them. She had not lied. She had believed — still believed — that a single will, disciplined and unsentimental, could hold the knot better than a scattered college of witnesses. Witnesses died. Witnesses forgot. Witnesses could be turned. But a crown worn without pause, fed on oaths sworn in good faith across the whole realm — a crown that *remembered on behalf of everyone* — that was a fortress.

She had not understood, that first year, what the feeding meant. What it cost the ones who swore.

* * *

"Your Radiance." Captain Ilse Voss stood in the doorway of the map room, helmet under her arm, ash dusting her shoulders grey. "Brenholt report."

"Read it."

"Burned to the foundations. The scriptorium vaults opened cleanly — we recovered four hundred sealed oaths, marriage-bonds, guild charters, land-titles. All catalogued. Two archivists resisted." A pause. "Three resisted."

"And?"

"And they are ash with the rest." Voss's voice did not waver. It never wavered; that was why Veyra kept her. "The column marches for Halveth at dawn. Halveth's envoys have already sent terms."

"I know. I wrote the reply." Veyra traced a finger along the great table-map, over the eastern roads, to the free city with its famous founding-oath — the oldest continuously-sworn civic bond in the realm, renewed every generation for six hundred years. A knot like that could hold back the Quiet for a century on its own. It could also, unbound and brought here, feed the crown through a hard winter.

"Halveth will trade its name for mercy," she said. "They'll dress it up as diplomacy. They'll tell themselves they're saving lives. Let them. A city that sells its oath was going to lose it anyway — better it comes to me intact than scatters into the wind."

Voss said nothing. Veyra had learned to read the captain's silences the way other rulers read petitions.

"You think we burn too much," Veyra said.

"I think," Voss said carefully, "that the men who fired Brenholt's granary as well as its archives are having trouble sleeping. Archives don't feed anyone, Your Radiance. Grain does. When the soldiers start confusing the two—"

"They're not confused. They're *afraid*, and fear is correct." Veyra straightened. "Captain. Do you remember your mother?"

Voss blinked. "Yes, Radiance."

"What was her name?"

"Maren— " the captain stopped. Frowned. "Her name was—" Her jaw worked. Her hand tightened on the helmet until the knuckles whitened. "I know her face. I can't—"

"That," Veyra said quietly, "is what leaked through last spring, when the crown strained and I could not patch it fast enough. One village's worth of names, gone. Yours was in the path. I have regretted it every day since, and I have written the debt in the ledger myself, in my own hand, and I will pay it if the realm survives long enough for debts to mean anything." She met the captain's eyes. "But I will do it again tomorrow, and the day after, and as many tomorrows as it takes. Because the alternative is your mother's face going blank while you watch, and then yours, and then everyone's, everywhere, forever. Do you understand the difference?"

Voss stood very still. "Yes, Radiance."

"Liar," Veyra said, without heat. "Go. Dawn march. And double the escort on the oath-wagons. Something is pulling at my crown from the east, and I want no accidents on the road."

* * *

Alone, she went down into the vault beneath the tower — the room the servants called the Reliquary and she called, privately, the Pantry.

Shelves lined the walls, floor to ceiling, and on each shelf sat vessels: jars of river-glass, stoppered bottles, lead-sealed boxes, each holding an oath or a hoard of bound memory, catalogued in her own cramped hand. Four hundred from Brenholt alone, still being shelved. The crown drank from these when the distant counter-oaths pulled at it, and lately it had been drinking more, and she had begun to notice — in the mirror, in the way her hands did not ache anymore, in the strange smoothness of years that should have left lines — that she was drinking too.

She had stopped aging at forty-one. She knew exactly what that meant. Every year the crown kept her young was a year it had taken from someone who had sworn something true, and the ledger of those years ran to pages she could not bear to total.

*A ruler who keeps accounts is a steward,* her father had said, back before the Quiet took him mid-sentence, mid-life, leaving him a pleasant hollow man who whittled sticks by the window until his heart forgot its work. *A ruler who spends without counting is a thief.*

Then count, she told herself, as she did every night. Count everything. That is the whole discipline. That is the cure.

Because that was the truth she had built nine years of ash upon: the forgetting could be starved. Memory was finite, hoardable, governable. If every oath in the realm passed through one pair of hands — hers — then nothing could fray, nothing could leak, nothing could wake the thing in the low places. Total custody. Total control. It was not cruelty. It was quarantine.

The crown pulsed against her brow, and she let it show her what it had felt at dawn: four small flames in the east, converging. A blind woman's fragment, maybe. A knight's. And threaded through them, faint and wrong and familiar, something else — a dead man's handwriting moving across a chart. Sef Holm's hand. Sef Holm, who had mapped the low places, who had seen the Quiet sleeping and run west with what he knew, who had been missing nine years and was now, unmistakably, *active* — his memories riding in someone else's skull like a passenger.

So. Someone had inherited him. Someone was carrying his map and his ghosts toward her capital, gathering her scattered witnesses as they came.

Veyra stood among her shelves of bottled vows and felt, for one unguarded moment, something almost like envy. To inherit another's memories freely given — to carry them without ledger, without arithmetic — what a luxury that must be.

Then the moment closed, and she was the Regent-Protector again, and the sums resumed.

Four witnesses converging. One vessel carrying a dead cartographer. One free city about to sell its name. She returned to the map room and drew three circles on the eastern roads in red ink, and beside the third she wrote a single instruction for Voss:

*Halveth first. Take the oath whole. Burn nothing we can carry.*

And beneath it, smaller, for no one's eyes but her own, the oldest entry in her private ledger, the one she had written the night of the coronation and never crossed out:

*Nine witnesses. Cost: one king. Balance due: never.*

Far to the east, the counter-oaths flickered — and held.
