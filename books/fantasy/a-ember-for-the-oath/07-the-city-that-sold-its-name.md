## 7. The City That Sold Its Name

Maren had spent thirty years learning to read a city the way other people read a ledger, and Halveth was telling her it had already signed something away.

She saw it in the small things first. The oath-stones in the market square — granite pillars carved with the founding compact, each clause worn smooth by generations of touching hands — had been draped in gray cloth. Not removed. Covered. A city that meant to keep its word did not cover its word; it displayed it. Covering was what you did with furniture you'd already sold.

The harbor told her more. Veyra's ships rode at anchor beyond the chain, black-hulled and patient, flying no colors because they needed none. And on the quay, guild factors moved among the dockhands with their brass badges turned inward against their chests. She knew that gesture. It meant the Compact's business in Halveth was not public business. It meant she was, for the first time in her life, working against her own order on their own ground.

She had come north from the archive at Corren's Ford with two letters of transit, a forged factor's seal, and a single instruction burned into her memory: *the fragment leaves Halveth before the envoys do.* The Compact's directive had been characteristically precise about the object and characteristically silent about why. Maren had stopped asking why three weeks ago, when the second courier had arrived asking after Cyrus Eriksen by name, by description, and by the route he'd taken east — questions no archivist should have been able to answer unless someone upstairs had opened the tracking ledgers.

The Compact tracked its debts. It had never before tracked its people.

* * *

The bargaining hall of Halveth had been a salt exchange once, back when the city's wealth was measured in white gold instead of oaths. The vaulted ceiling still bore the old merchants' sigils, and beneath them, at a table too long for the room, sat the Council of Nine on one side and Veyra's envoys on the other, with a space between them exactly wide enough for a document.

Maren stood at the back among the clerks, her factor's badge correctly inverted, her ledger open to a page of plausible entries. She had bribed her way into the hall as an appraiser of written instruments, which was true enough; the Compact had trained her to value a contract the way a jeweler values a stone, by holding it up to the light and looking for flaws.

The document on the table was the flaw.

It lay open under a glass case, and even from thirty feet she could feel it — that faint pressure behind the eyes, like standing near a struck bell. The Founding Oath of Halveth. Not a copy. The original, inked nine hundred years ago by eleven families who had agreed, in writing, to be a city rather than a ruin. Every wall in Halveth stood on that sentence. Every free right the city claimed — its courts, its charter, its stubborn refusal to kneel — was collateral on that one page.

"Terms are unchanged," said the senior envoy, a woman named Lissane Vael whose voice had the flat pleasantness of someone reading terms aloud for the hundredth time. "The crown accepts the surrender of the founding-oath. In exchange, Halveth's walls stand, its council continues, and the imperial armies pass around your fields rather than through them."

"And the city keeps its name," said Councillor Brandt, and his voice cracked on the last word, which told Maren everything about how the vote had gone.

"The city keeps its name." Vael smiled without warmth. "Names are cheap, councillor. It's the promises attached to them that cost."

Maren wrote nothing on her ledger page. Her hand wouldn't obey. She kept thinking of the oath-stones in the square, covered in gray cloth like corpses, and of the eleven families, and of what happened to a city when the promise underneath it was pulled out — she had catalogued the results, once, in a folio the Compact kept locked: *Hollowtowns. Settlements whose charters were sold or seized. Population remains. Cohesion does not. Within a generation, nothing holds. The streets fill, the meaning empties.*

Veyra wasn't buying mercy for Halveth. She was buying the mold that shaped it.

And the Compact knew. The Compact had to know — you could not run an archive of impossible things and not know what a hollowtown was. Which meant the order to steal the fragment wasn't sabotage of the trade.

It was positioning. Whoever held the founding-oath of Halveth held a piece of leverage over nine thousand people, and the Compact had decided, quietly and in committee, that it wanted to be the one holding it.

Not to give it back. To keep it. An asset. A line in a ledger.

Maren closed her book.

* * *

She found the fragment's keeper by following the money, because in Halveth the money always knew.

The Council had hired a private strongroom — not the guild-vaults, notably, though the Compact's Halveth chapterhouse had offered, and been refused. Someone on the Council didn't trust the guild either. Good instincts, whoever they were. The strongroom belonged to a retired tide-priest named Odile Ferran who kept her rates high and her questions higher, and Maren got past her the only way that worked with careful people: she told her most of the truth.

"I'm here to steal it," she said, sitting in Odile's lamp-lit parlor above the strongroom stairs. "Before the trade closes tomorrow noon. And I need you to let me."

Odile Ferran was seventy if she was a day, with forearms like mooring rope and eyes that had watched forty years of tides come in carrying whatever the sea felt like sending. She poured tea without asking and studied Maren over the rim.

"The Compact sent you."

"The Compact sent me to secure it. I'm telling you what securing it actually means." Maren set down the cup. "You've heard of hollowtowns?"

"Everyone's heard of hollowtowns. Nobody talks about them."

"If your Council signs tomorrow, Halveth becomes one within twenty years. Walls up, name intact, and nothing inside it but weather. The envoy isn't lying to them — that's the worst part. Everything she promised is true. It just isn't the whole price." She leaned forward. "I came to take the oath out of this city so the Compact could hold it. I'm now telling you I intend to take it somewhere else entirely. You can call the chapterhouse and have me arrested, or you can help me, or you can go to bed and let me pick your lock. Those are your options."

Odile drank her tea for a long moment.

"Why?" she said. "Why turn? People don't. Institutions buy people the way your guild buys rivers in bottles, and they stay bought."

Because of a dying stranger at a door, Maren almost said. Because of a young cartographer with ink-stained fingers who looked at her across an archive table and asked whether the Compact had ever once forgiven a debt, and she hadn't been able to answer him. Because she had spent thirty years believing the ledgers balanced toward something, and had finally seen the column they all summed to, and it read *hold*, not *return*.

"Because I've read the accounts," she said instead. "All of them. Including the ones we don't show members."

Odile set down her cup. "The strongroom's below. The case is warded — good work, I paid for good work. But wards answer to intent, and my intent has been getting softer every day since those black ships anchored." She stood, joints cracking like rigging in a storm. "Come down before I change my mind. Old women are allowed one act of treason per decade. I'm due."

* * *

The fragment was smaller than Maren expected. The Founding Oath of Halveth, the sentence that had made eleven quarreling families into a city, fit in a case the size of a hymnal, and when she lifted it the weight of it ran up her arms like cold water — not heavy, exactly. *Present*. The way a person is present in a room.

She wrapped it in oilcloth, and then, because some habits are deeper than treason, she logged it. Drew a line in her own pocket-ledger, her private one, the one she'd kept since her apprenticeship: *One (1) founding-oath, Halveth. Removed from sale. Disposition: return pending.* She stared at the entry. *Return pending* was not a term the Compact used. Debts were collected, assets held, losses written off. Nothing was ever *pending return*, because nothing was ever meant to go home.

She wrote it anyway.

Odile watched from the stair. "Where will you take it?"

"To the king," Maren said, and heard how strange the words sounded — *the king*, a man most of the realm believed dead or worse, a rumor attached to a hollow-eyed figure behind a usurper's crown. "There's a counter-oath being assembled. Witnesses of the coronation. Fragments of the original vow. If enough of them are spoken together—" She stopped. She didn't fully understand it herself; she understood it the way she understood maps Cyrus had drawn, which was to say she trusted the hand more than the geography. "Cyrus Eriksen is gathering them. He'll know what to do."

Something crossed Odile's face. "Eriksen. The Compact's been asking after him. Quietly. Through factors, through couriers." The old woman's voice dropped. "They posted a retrieval notice three days ago. Not arrest — retrieval. There's a difference in the wording, and the wording is bad. Retrieval means bring back the item and dispose of the carrier."

The lamp flame stood very still between them.

"Dispose," Maren repeated.

"That's what I read. I read everything; it's a failing of mine." Odile's jaw set. "They've sold him, girl. Sold him the way this city's selling itself. Price undisclosed, buyer unnamed, delivery east."

Maren thought of Cyrus at the archive table, asking his quiet, dangerous question. Thought of the tracking ledgers opening. Thought of the second courier, and the third, and how she had filed her unease under *coincidence* because the alternative was unthinkable, and institutions counted on exactly that.

"They can't retrieve him," she said. "He's carrying memories that aren't his. If the Compact takes the map off him—" She stopped again, because she did know what happened then. She'd catalogued it. *Inherited memory, severed from bearer: degrades. Rapidly. Irretrievably.* They wouldn't be taking Sef Holm's memories into safekeeping. They'd be killing them a second time, and killing the man who carried them if he resisted, and the committee would minute it as *asset recovery, partial loss acceptable*.

Partial loss. Cyrus Eriksen was a partial loss now. He had been one since the night he walked out of the guildhall with a dead man's chart under his coat.

Maren slung her satchel across her body, the oath-fragment riding against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"East gate," she said. "Is there a road the armies haven't taken?"

"There's roads the armies can't find," Odile said. "Old salt-paths through the fens. My father ran contraband on them. I'll draw you a chart." She paused at the foot of the stairs, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and then some years besides. "You understand you're done, after this. No chapterhouse will hold you. No ledger will carry you except as a loss."

"Yes," Maren said. "I've read the accounts. I know how we write off people."

She climbed the stairs into the gray Halveth dawn, where the oath-stones stood shrouded in the square and the black ships waited beyond the chain, patient as arithmetic. Somewhere east, past the armies and the burning archives, a young cartographer was walking into a trap her own order had baited — and the only warning he would get was whatever she could carry to him faster than betrayal traveled.

Institutions fail, she thought, tightening the satchel strap. That was what they were built to do eventually, the way roofs were built to leak. The question had never been the institution.

The question was who walked out of it carrying what mattered.

Behind her, Halveth's bells began to ring the hour — noon approaching, the trade approaching, the signing. Ahead of her, the fen-road ran east into mist, unmapped by anyone except smugglers and deserters and one old woman's memory.

Maren took out her pen and added one final line to her private ledger, small and clear:

*Compact of Cartographers — account closed. Carrier: self. Disposition: hers.*
