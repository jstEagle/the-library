## 8. Throne of Borrowed Years

The last road the map drew was not a road at all.

It was a seam — a hairline crack in the parchment that appeared on their ninth night out from Halveth, running from the western margin toward a blank space where the capital should have been. Cyrus had stared at it for an hour before he understood. The map wasn't showing him a way in. It was offering to make one, and it wanted his permission.

"You're asking me," he said aloud, to no one, by firelight, while Tessa slept with her back to a dead oak and Maren kept watch with a knife across her knees.

The ink pulsed once, like a heartbeat.

"Fine." He pressed his thumb to the seam. "But I'm not drawing you anything else. You've taken enough of my hand already."

The forest around them exhaled. Trees leaned aside like courtiers. And where there had been a wall of thorn and dark, a path opened — narrow, pale, stitched together from every place Sef Holm had ever walked and never mapped, because he'd been saving them. A cartographer's hoard. A dead man's emergency exits.

They walked it until dawn, and at dawn the path ended at a drainage culvert beneath the capital's outer wall, and Cyrus understood that Sef Holm had always known he would die here, and had left a door anyway.

* * *

The culvert smelled of nine years of rain. They came up through it into a garden gone wild behind the old queen's aviary — a garden no one tended anymore because Veyra kept no birds. Caged things made her nervous, Tessa said. Things that remembered flight.

Above the walls, above the terraced roofs, the palace rose against the grey morning sky, and Cyrus felt the map against his chest go hot and then cold, hot and then cold, like something panting.

"It knows we're close," Maren whispered.

"It's not the map," Cyrus said. "It's him."

They crossed the city in ones and twos, Tessa leading, hooded, past bread lines and gallows scaffolds still hung with the morning's work. Veyra's soldiers moved through the streets in pairs, carrying ledgers. Not weapons — ledgers. Going door to door. Collecting names, birthrights, oaths of trade and marriage and guild fealty, stacking the city's memory into confiscatable form.

"She's archiving them," Maren breathed. "She's going to archive the whole city."

"That's what mercy looks like now," Tessa said flatly. "You keep your life. She keeps your past."

The palace gates were impossible, so they did not use the gates. The map opened a servant's stair that had been bricked over for sixty years, and Cyrus felt each step of it drawn out of him — a small toll of warmth behind his eyes, paid per stone — until they stood in a corridor smelling of dust and snuffed candles, at a door with the royal seal broken sideways, hanging by one nail.

Tessa put her hand on the latch. Looked back at him.

"Whatever's in there," she said, "it isn't a king anymore."

But when Cyrus pushed the door open, it was.

* * *

The throne room had been stripped. Banners gone, dais bare, the great windows shuttered so that the only light came through the gaps in the boards, falling in thin blades across the floor. Aldric sat not on a throne — there was no throne, Veyra had melted it down or moved it or eaten it, who knew — but in a plain wooden chair, set in the middle of the room like a man waiting out a storm.

He was younger than Cyrus expected and older than any living man. His face was unlined. His eyes were nine hundred years tired.

The crown sat on his head like a hand pressing him under water.

Cyrus had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road: the rescue, the revelation, some clean transactional thing. He had not imagined that the king would see him — truly see him, focus on him with the whole weight of a mind surfacing from deep water — and speak first.

"Cartographer," Aldric said. His voice was rust and honey. "Sef's map. I can smell his ink on you."

"Sef Holm is dead," Cyrus said. "Nine years. His memories came with the chart. Some of them are in me now."

"I know." Something moved in the king's face — grief trying to surface through the drowning. "I felt him die. He was the last thing the crown let me feel that wasn't hunger." Aldric's hands gripped the chair's arms. The knuckles were white as bone. "Listen to me. Listen, because I am lucid once a month if the moon is generous, and this is the breath I get."

Cyrus stepped forward. Behind him, Maren and Tessa fanned out along the walls, watching the doors.

"The crown eats oaths," the king said. "Do you understand what that means? Not loyalty. Oaths. The moment a promise is sworn, the moment two people bind themselves in good faith, it feeds. Every wedding vow in this kingdom is a meal. Every guild oath, every coronation — " he laughed, a terrible sound — "every coronation is a feast. It has been eating mine for nine years, slowly, because Veyra rations me. She keeps the kingdom afraid and half-sworn. Small promises. Controlled portions."

"Then we break it," Cyrus said. "We have witnesses. We have fragments of the original oath. We can unmake the coronation — "

"No." Aldric leaned forward, and the light-blades crossed his face, and for a moment Cyrus saw the young king from Sef's memories, standing in the rain swearing to protect people who could never repay him. "You cannot unmake it. It has to be *ransomed*. Fed something worth more than a kingdom's fealty. That is why Sef went looking for me and died for it. That is why you carry him." The king's voice dropped to almost nothing. "End it. Even if it kills me. Even if it kills you. Do you hear me? Nine years is enough. I have watched myself forget my mother's face and keep the hunger. I would rather be nothing than be this."

The breath ended. Cyrus watched it end — watched the lucidity drain out of the king's eyes like water through sand, watched the face smooth into vacancy, watched the mouth open and begin, softly, mechanically, to recite the oath of fealty owed to Queen Veyra. Over and over. A man made into a bell that someone else rings.

And then the doors opened, and it didn't matter what any of them said, because Veyra had heard all of it anyway.

* * *

There were no soldiers at first. There was just her, standing in the doorway with a lamp, wearing the plain grey of a woman attending to accounts, and behind her the room filled with crossbows until it was mostly crossbows.

"The drainage culvert under the aviary," Veyra said, conversational. "Sef Holm's little door. I sealed three of his. I wondered about the fourth." Her eyes found Cyrus, and he had the disorienting sense of being inventoried. "And you brought it. After nine years, the map walks itself back to me. Things want to come home, even maps."

"We're not here for you," Tessa said, and there was steel in it, and a crossbow stock met the back of her skull before she finished the next word. Maren reached for the satchel with the oath-fragments and was stopped by four spears and a shake of Veyra's head.

"Don't," Veyra told her. "I know what's in the bag. Why do you think I let Halveth's envoys leave?" She turned fully to Cyrus, and up close he saw what Tessa had described and failed to convey: the youth of her face, the age of her hands, hands that had held plague carts shut while the faces at the windows forgot the names of everyone inside. "Take the chart out of your coat. Slowly."

Every blade in the room waited. Cyrus thought about the fragments in Maren's satchel, the priestess's words, the knight's, the child-now-old's. He thought about the king reciting fealty to an empty room. He thought: *this is the part where cleverness fails and only payment remains.*

He took out the map. Unrolled it on the floor between himself and the throne.

Veyra looked at it. And frowned.

"That's not — " she began, and the map opened.

Not unfolded. Opened, the way a mouth opens, the way a wound opens. The seams between its regions split wide, and the blank spaces breathed outward, and the room's thin blades of light bent toward the parchment, and Cyrus felt the pull start behind his eyes like a hook finding purchase.

"What is it doing?" Maren shouted.

"It's not feeding," Veyra said, and for the first time her voice held something like awe, and something like fear. "It's *paying*. He's paying in advance."

The crown stopped eating Aldric.

Cyrus felt the transfer happen — felt the crown's attention swing to him like a searchlight, felt Sef Holm's memories begin to pour out of him, and not gently. This was not remembering. This was being *made to remember*, everything, in order, at speed, while the crown drank.

Rain on a coronation morning. The oath in three voices, then seven, then a city's worth. Then Sef's own memories, the private ones, the ones Cyrus had carried without examining because they weren't his to examine — his daughter's name, which Cyrus realized he had been able to say for weeks; the reason Sef really left the guild, a debt, a betrayal, a face Cyrus now saw clearly and recognized as a portrait hanging in the Compact's hall. And then the death. Of course the death. The crown saved the death for last the way you save the sweetest part of the plate.

An alley near the palace. Nine years ago. Soldiers who had been ordered not to kill him and did anyway, slowly. Sef Holm on the wet stones with his charts scattered around him like shed feathers, pressing the most important one into the hands of a stranger — a stranger with kind eyes and a guild badge and ink-stained fingers — and saying, with the last air in his lungs:

*The crown remembers. Make sure someone does too.*

Cyrus screamed. He didn't remember deciding to. He was on his knees on his own unrolled map, and the crown was drinking, and somewhere very far away Maren was shouting his name and Veyra was saying something quiet and horrified about arithmetic, about balances, about how much a soul weighed on her scales.

And underneath the scream, underneath the draining, one thought kept its shape:

*The ransom isn't finished. It's only begun. To unbind him fully, I have to give all of it — Sef entire — and my name beside his in the ledger, and I don't know if anything of me comes back after.*

The crown paused. Considered. Found the payment promising but incomplete.

And reached deeper.
