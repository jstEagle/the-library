## 10. A Map With Room to Draw

The crown lay on a cushion of grey velvet in the middle of the council table, and it remembered nothing.

Cyrus had checked. Every morning for three weeks he had come to the hall where Aldric held his court — a borrowed hall in Halveth, since the capital still smelled of smoke and siege — and every morning he had leaned over the thing and waited for it to speak. It didn't. It sat like any other circle of old gold, dull where the light touched it, hungry for nothing. Once it had eaten oaths the way a fire eats air. Now it was just metal that had forgotten how to be a crown, which, Cyrus thought, was probably the most honest thing a crown could be.

"You keep looking at it like it might apologize," Aldric said.

They were alone. The king had dismissed his stewards an hour ago and stayed behind to do the work usurpers never bother with — the work of grain tallies and bridge repairs and which village's well had gone foul. His hands were ink-stained. His eyes, which Cyrus had first seen hollowed out like rooms after a fire, had something in them again. Not brightness. Something more durable than that.

"It cost enough," Cyrus said. "I want to know what I bought."

Aldric turned the crown over with one finger. It rolled a quarter-turn and stopped.

"You bought a man his own name back," he said. "And a realm the right to forget things on purpose. Do you know what a luxury that is? Veyra was right about the danger, you know. The forgetting-plague is real. It took three villages in the eastern valleys before her armies ever marched. She just decided the cure was to own every memory in the world, and ownership rots faster than the disease." He looked up. "You gave people back the right to lose things naturally. That's not nothing."

Cyrus didn't answer, because the answer was that he hadn't done it for the realm. He'd done it because a dying man had pressed a chart into his hands on a cold morning and said *the crown remembers*, and Cyrus had spent a year learning what those words cost, and he was still paying.

He was missing things. He knew the shape of the missing the way you know a missing tooth — not the tooth itself, just the hole where your tongue keeps going. He had lost the name of the street he grew up on. He had lost his mother's voice entirely, though he remembered that she had one, and that it had been warm. He had lost, all of it, Sef Holm: the coronation, the nine years of flight, the forests that rearranged themselves around regret, the last breath in a doorway with a bloodstained chart under his fingers. Cyrus had carried a dead man for a year and then paid the dead man back with himself, and now when he reached for Sef Holm he found only a ledger entry: *received, one inheritance; returned, in full.*

The guild's ledgers would record it as a debt no one could collect. He'd asked Maren to make sure of that. She'd said she'd do better than record it. She'd said she'd forgive it, and he hadn't understood what she meant until later.

"I'm leaving," he said.

Aldric nodded as if he'd been expecting it for weeks, which he probably had. Kings, Cyrus was learning, were mostly people who'd gotten good at expecting things.

"Where?"

"Nowhere yet. That's the point." Cyrus took the roll of parchment from his satchel — good stock, heavy rag paper, and blank. Completely, perfectly blank. He'd bought it in the market three days ago and hadn't been able to stop touching it. "Everything I ever drew came from someone else's hand. Sef's maps. The guild's charts. Even the ones I made myself, I made to trade — impossibilities by the yard, bottled rivers, borrowed years, a map of a realm that only exists while someone regrets it. I was good at it because I never had to decide what was worth drawing. The map decided. The guild decided. The dead man in my head decided." He set the blank roll on the table next to the crown, and the contrast between them was almost funny: one thing that remembered everything and did nothing, one thing that remembered nothing and could become anything. "I'd like to find out what I draw when it's only me."

"And what's left of you?"

Cyrus smiled. It still surprised him when the smile came easily.

"Enough," he said. "I remember Tessa. I remember the forest that moved. I remember the priestess reciting her fragment of the oath and what it cost her, and the knight refusing, and the child grown old who paid anyway because she said a debt you won't pay isn't discharged, it's just moved. I remember you begging me to end it even if it killed me, and I remember that I said yes before you finished asking." He tapped the blank parchment. "I remember the important things. The rest was ballast."

Aldric studied him for a long moment. Then the king picked up the crown, crossed the room, and set it on a shelf behind his chair — not on his head, not in a vault, just on a shelf, in the light, where anyone could look at it and see that it was only metal.

"Come back in a year," Aldric said. "Draw me what you find. I'll pay you in the only currency I have left." He smiled, tired and real. "Oaths. Freely given, cheaply kept."

Cyrus took the parchment and left the crown to its remembering-nothing, and walked out into a morning that belonged to no one's map at all.

* * *

Maren had believed, for most of her life, that an institution was just a promise that had learned to survive its makers.

She had believed it in the guildhall's archive, among the ledgers of impossible things, where she had spent thirty years cataloguing trades no sane market should have permitted — a river in a bottle, a decade of someone's youth, a map of a forest that fed on regret. She had believed it when the guild sold out Cyrus, when the archivists' council weighed one cartographer's life against their charters with Veyra's envoys and found the arithmetic acceptable. She had believed it right up until the night she stood in the free city of Halveth with a stolen oath-fragment in her coat and understood that the promise had not survived its makers after all. It had simply outlived them, the way a debt outlives the debtor, accruing interest in the dark.

So she did what she had always done with records that were rotten at the root. She closed the ledger. And she started a new one.

The new compact met, at first, in a rented room above a chandler's shop in Halveth, because Maren refused to occupy any building the old guild had ever owned. There were nine of them to start: two defected archivists, a Tessa who was learning — slowly, angrily, with relapses — that desertion could be converted into something else, a Halveth scribe who had watched her city nearly sell its founding-oath and never wanted to watch anything like it again, and a handful of others who had each, in their own way, been a line item in someone else's accounting.

The first rule Maren wrote took her four nights to get right, and when she finally read it aloud the room was quiet for a long time.

*No debt of memory shall be collected from one who cannot spare it. Every debt may be forgiven by the one who holds the paper. No ledger of this compact shall record what a person has lost — only what they have chosen to carry.*

"It's not law," she said. "It's not even policy. It's a promise, and promises fail. I've watched them fail professionally for thirty years." She set the page on the table. "But the old guild's ledgers never once said *forgiven*. Not in nine hundred years of entries. Not once. So we'll start there."

They argued about the second rule for a week. They argued about fees and oaths and whether a compact that forgave debts could survive its own creditors, and Tessa stormed out twice and came back twice, and the chandler below them complained about the stomping. Maren loved every minute of it, in the way she had once loved a difficult folio: the sense of a thing being made correctly, against resistance, by hand.

Word spread the way word does. A woman came from the eastern valleys where the forgetting-plague had passed through, carrying her village's records in a wheelbarrow, asking if the new compact could hold memories *in trust* — not owned, not traded, just kept safe for people who were afraid of losing them naturally. Maren said yes before the woman finished asking, and then spent a sleepless night inventing the safeguards, because saying yes was the easy part and the old guild had been built by people who only ever did the easy part.

By the sixth week they had outgrown the chandler's shop. By the eighth, a letter arrived from King Aldric's court offering them a hall in the capital, and Maren wrote back that they would accept a *room*, unfurnished, and would furnish it themselves, because a compact that accepted gifts from kings would soon be keeping kings' ledgers, and she had buried one institution already and did not intend to dig a grave for a second.

It was on a grey afternoon in the ninth week that Cyrus walked in.

He looked thinner. He looked, Maren thought, like a man who had set down a weight and was still walking with the posture of it, his body not yet convinced. He carried a roll of good rag paper under his arm and nothing else — no satchel of charts, no guild seal, none of the cartographer's furniture she remembered.

"I hear you're forgiving debts," he said.

"Selective ones." She looked at him over the table where the first rule still lay, its ink long dry. "Yours was the first entry in the new ledger. *Received: one inheritance. Returned: in full. Forgiven.*" She turned the book so he could see it. "I thought you should know it's official. No one can collect from you now. Not the guild — what's left of it. Not the crown. Not the dead."

Cyrus read the entry twice. When he looked up, his eyes were wet and he seemed entirely unbothered by it.

"Then I've come to open a new account," he said, and unrolled the parchment on her table.

It was not blank anymore. It was a map — but unlike any chart Maren had catalogued in thirty years of cataloguing the impossible, because it did not show what *was*. It showed what could be. Roads not yet built, drawn in confident lines. A bridge across the river at Halveth where the ferry drowned three people every winter. The eastern valleys, marked not with the plague's old scars but with the names of villages planning to rebuild. In the corner, small and precise, a note in Cyrus's hand: *survey incomplete. room for more.*

"What is it?" Maren asked, though she already knew.

"A map with room to draw," Cyrus said. "I'm told your compact keeps things in trust. I'd like to deposit this — not to trade, not to own. Just so it exists somewhere other than my head, in case I lose any more of it." He shrugged, and the shrug was lighter than any she'd seen from him. "Turns out inheritance isn't what you're given. It's what you choose to carry. I'm choosing this."

Maren took out her pen. In a ledger built for forgiveness, under a rule written in four nights of stubbornness, she recorded the deposit — the first map in nine hundred years of the compact's history that pointed forward.

Outside, Halveth went about its evening. Somewhere east, a king shelved a crown that remembered nothing. Somewhere south, in the valleys, people wrote down their names and their mothers' names and the names of their streets, not because they feared a plague, but because they had learned what memory cost and had decided it was worth paying for willingly, a little at a time, by hand.

And in a rented room above a chandler's shop, a debt was forgiven, a map was trusted, and two people who had each lost pieces of themselves sat down together in the lamplight to decide what to carry forward — which was, Maren reflected, dipping her pen, the only inheritance that had ever been worth the paper.
