## 1. The Smuggler's Ear

There are three rules to moving contraband through Thornfell, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is to say: bleeding.

One — never take the same road twice. Two — never trust a man who whistles while he walks. Three — and this is the one that keeps you alive — never, ever let anyone see you talking to the animals.

I was breaking rule three at that very moment, crouched in the bracken above Miller's Cut with snow soaking through my left knee, negotiating with a raven who had the moral character of a tax collector.

"Two eggs," Kess said. Her voice arrived inside my skull the way it always did, dry as old paper, with the faint impression of feathers ruffling somewhere behind my eyes. "Three. It is cold, Wren-of-the-soft-hands."

"It's autumn," I said.

"It is cold *for me*. I am delicate."

"You ate a dead fox last week. Whole. You told me about it in detail."

"That was business. This is dinner." She hopped along the branch above me, head cocked, one black eye fixed on the wagon track below where my cargo sat under oilcloth in the bed of Tam Brell's cart. "Three eggs and the shiny thing in your pocket. The round one."

"The button? That's brass. It's worthless."

"Then you will not miss it."

I fished the button out anyway — salvaged off a dead soldier's coat years ago, kept because it was pretty and because I'd been nineteen and stupid about pretty things — and set it on a flat stone. Kess took it with the delicacy of a duchess accepting a compliment she knew was false.

"Road's clear," she said then, all business. "Two men at the tollhouse, both asleep, one of them drunk. A fox den under the third milestone, but the fox owes me and will keep her kits indoors tonight. And—" a pause, the particular pause I had learned to hate over six years of partnership, "—there are wolves on the high path. Four. They say they want to talk to you."

Wolves wanting to talk was not unusual. Wolves wanting to talk to *me* specifically, by name, was new.

"Did they say why?"

"They said the mountain is angry." Kess preened the stolen button into a fold of her wing, satisfied. "They said the paths taste wrong. They said—" and here her voice did something I'd never heard it do, something small, "—they said the old words are going out like coals, and they wanted to know if the soft-hands boy could read what's written on the stones."

I sat back on my heels in the wet bracken and felt the first cold finger of the evening slide down the back of my collar.

"I'm a smuggler," I reminded her. "I move cheese that isn't mine and brandy that's taxed twice. I don't read mountains."

"No," Kess agreed. "You don't. But you're going to talk to them anyway, because you're soft, and we both know it."

She was right, which was annoying, so I didn't answer. I whistled low — three notes, falling — and waited.

They came out of the treeline like the treeline had decided to stand up and walk. Four grey wolves, lean as winter, moving with that terrible economy wolves have, as though every motion costs them something they resent paying. The big one — scarred across the muzzle, one ear folded — stopped ten paces off and sat.

*Speaker.* His voice was nothing like Kess's. Hers was paper; his was stone rolling downhill. *You carry the listening blood.*

"I carry a wagonload of untaxed cider," I said. "That's all anybody's ever proven."

The wolf made a sound that might have been laughter, in the way an avalanche might be described as a light dusting. *The stones forget us, speaker. The boundary-stones on the ridge. Our grandsires marked them, and your grandsires answered, and now the marks go dark and no voice answers from the other side. The young ones grow strange. They bite things that are not food. They dream of a hunger under the mountain.* He leaned forward, and his breath smoked in the cold. *We do not blame you. Yet. But we remember whose blood held the bridge, and we are watching that blood.*

"I really think you've got the wrong Villalobos," I said. "My family farms turnips."

*Your family farmed turnips,* he agreed, *the way a riverbed farms stones.* Then he stood, shook himself once, and was gone between the trees with the other three flowing after him like water finding its level, and I was left alone in the bracken with my knee freezing and my heart doing something complicated against my ribs.

Kess dropped onto my shoulder. "Well?"

"Well what?"

"You heard them. The stones going dark. The young ones dreaming wrong." She tugged my ear, gently, for a raven. "You heard."

"I heard wolves being dramatic. It's what they do instead of singing." I got up, brushed off my knee, and started down toward the cart track, because there were forty wheels of smuggled cheese waiting on a schedule and dread doesn't pay debts. "The covenant's been dead for two hundred years, Kess. My grandmother used to tell stories about it. Nice ones. With music. Stories end."

"Mm," said Kess, in the tone of a creature who had lived through several endings herself and found none of them nice.

* * *

The run down through Thornfell went clean, which should have been my first warning. Nothing in my life went clean. We slipped past the sleeping tollhouse, forded the cold shallows at Miller's Cut, and handed the cider and cheese off to Tam Brell's cousin at the charcoal burner's clearing without so much as a raised eyebrow. By full dark I was walking home along the valley road with empty hands, an honest man's posture, and a pocket considerably lighter by one brass button.

That was when the stag came out of the woods.

He came badly. That was the first thing I noticed — no wild thing comes out onto an open road unless it has forgotten how to be wild, and this one staggered like a drunk leaving a funeral. His flank was torn, long parallel gouges crusted black, and his breath came in white rags. His antlers were magnificent and wrong, one tine snapped off clean, and when he saw me he didn't bolt.

He knelt. In the middle of the road, in front of me, a stag knelt, and spoke.

*Speaker.* His voice was thin as frost on a windowpane. *They are asking in the valley towns. Soldiers. Green coats, the king's mark, going door to door. Asking after speakers. After listeners. After anyone the beasts speak to.*

My mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with the cold. "Asking how?"

*By name. They have names. Lists.* He swayed. Blood dripped from his jaw, steaming faintly. *They caught a hare-speaker in Lowmarsh. She would not say who else. They burned—*

He pitched forward onto the road and did not finish, and I stood there in the dark with my heart hammering out some old rhythm my body remembered and my mind refused to name, telling myself the same thing I'd been telling myself since I was seven years old and my mother caught me trading gossip with the chickens.

Nobody knows. Nobody has ever known. The gift dies with the family line, everyone says so, the Villalobos beast-speech ended two hundred years ago with the breaking of the pact, you're just a smuggler with a strange talent for reading animals, that's all, that's all anyone will ever see.

I stepped over the stag — I'm not proud of that; I told myself I'd come back for him and I even believed it — and walked the last mile home fast, with my collar up and my eyes down, the way an honest man walks.

* * *

Marrow's Edge was a scatter of forty roofs tucked against the foothills, the kind of place where the news was whatever the miller's wife decided it was and the second-biggest building was the church and the biggest was Aldous Merrin's barn. I'd grown up three doors from that barn. I'd kissed a girl behind it. I'd hidden a barrel of untaxed gin in its hayloft more than once, and Aldous had pretended not to know, because that was the arrangement, that was how small places survived.

I smelled it before I saw it.

Smoke, but not hearth-smoke — burnt thatch and burnt hair and something underneath both that I knew from the abattoir and would spend the rest of my life trying to unknow. The lane was full of people standing very still, the way people do around a thing too large to look at directly. The barn was a black ribcage against the stars, still breathing smoke, and the snow around it was trampled to grey slush by boots. Soldier's boots. I knew the pattern of the hobnails; I'd spent years learning which prints to walk around.

Aldous Merrin sat in the slush in front of his ruined barn with his hat in his hands, and he wasn't crying, which was worse.

"Aldous," I said. My voice came out wrong. "What happened."

He looked up at me, and his face was the face of a man who has been made to watch something and knows he'll be made to watch it again in his sleep forever. "They said there was a speaker in the loft," he said. "They said someone had been seen *talking* to the animals near my land, and they'd trace it or burn it out." His hands closed slowly around the hat. "It was Pip. My sister's boy. Eleven years old. He used to sit up in the hay and whistle to the starlings, and I thought — I thought it was just a boy whistling."

The crowd murmured, low, the sound of forty people each deciding separately not to have heard anything.

"He ran up into the loft when they came," Aldous went on, in that same terrible level voice. "Wouldn't come down. Wouldn't say who taught him. So they—" He stopped. Started again. "So they burned it."

I looked at the black ribs of the barn, and somewhere inside my chest, beneath the dread and the horror and the cold, something older shifted its weight — something that had been sleeping in my family for two hundred years, patient as a stone, and had just opened one eye.

Because here is the thing I hadn't told the stag, hadn't told the wolves, hadn't told Kess, hadn't told anyone: I had taught Pip. Three weeks ago, in this lane, I'd shown him how to still his mind and listen, laughing, thinking it was harmless, thinking the gift was a dead ember that couldn't catch.

The soldiers had lists. The soldiers had names. And tomorrow morning, by the proclamation drum, they'd be in Millbrook — four miles down the valley — asking theirs.

"Wren," Kess said softly, from the fencepost behind me. "Wren, your hands are shaking."

"I know," I said. And I turned south, toward Millbrook, toward the drums I could already hear starting up in the dark, and began to walk.
