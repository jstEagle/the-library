## 2. Proclamation at Millbrook

I had a rule about towns: never be in one longer than it takes to sell what you're carrying and buy what you can't make yourself. Millbrook was the exception that proved the rule, because Millbrook had a woman named Odette Fenn who paid honest coin for mountain honey and asked no questions about where the wax came from. Honest coin and no questions. In my experience you could have one or the other, never both, so I'd learned to treat Odette like a religious experience — rare, brief, and not to be examined too closely.

I came down off the ridge two days after the stag died with his warning caught between his teeth. Two days of telling myself that dying animals say all kinds of things. That soldiers asking after *speakers* was probably just soldiers being soldiers — bored, underpaid, and looking for someone to frighten. That my gift had been a secret for twenty-six years and would stay one for twenty-six more.

The stag's blood was still on my hands when I said it. I washed it off in a stream and told myself that didn't mean anything either.

Millbrook smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, underneath it, something new. Something like hot iron. The market square was fuller than a market day warranted, and half the faces were wrong for it — hard faces, travel-worn, wearing the grey-and-crimson of the crown's service. A platform had been thrown up against the tithe-house wall, and on it stood a herald with a scroll he clearly intended to enjoy.

I should have turned around. I want that on the record. Whatever happens next, I *should have turned around.*

But Kess was somewhere over that square. She'd flown ahead an hour ago, black against the white sky, because ravens get restless when I spend too long being human, and if there was going to be trouble I wanted to know which direction it faced before I walked into it. So instead of leaving, I pulled my hood up, bought a meat pie from a vendor whose hygiene I chose not to investigate, and drifted toward the crowd like a man with nothing better to do than listen to a herald ruin his morning.

He unrolled his scroll with a flourish. "By order of His Majesty Aldric the Third, Protector of the Realms, Keeper of the Mountain Marches—"

*Keeper of the Mountain Marches.* The crown hadn't called itself that since my great-grandmother's day. Since the covenant broke and the mountains started dying and the crown decided pretending nothing had been lost was cheaper than admitting fault. My grandmother used to spit when she said that title. I'd always thought it was theater.

"—be it known that persons gifted with the abomination of beast-speech are hereby declared enemies of the realm," the herald read. "All such persons are to be taken alive for questioning by His Majesty's inquisitors. Should they resist—" he paused, savoring it, "—they may be put to death where they stand, and their households assessed for complicity."

The square went quiet in that particular way crowds go quiet — not silent, but hushed, everyone breathing carefully, everyone very aware of their neighbors. A woman near me pulled her child closer. An old man crossed himself with the wrong hand, old-country style, and someone behind me hissed at him to stop.

"Assessed for complicity," I murmured into my pie. "That's a polite way of saying burned."

"Keep your voice down," said the old man, without looking at me. He had the wrong-hand crossing and the accent of the high valleys, and his eyes stayed fixed on the platform. "They're counting who flinches."

He was right. Along the edges of the square, soldiers weren't watching the herald at all. They were watching us. Watching for flinches, for whispers, for people who left too quickly. I took another bite of pie and thought about the stag, and about the burned barn I hadn't gone to see yet because I'd been telling myself stories, and felt the last of those stories die quietly in my chest.

Then the huntress came out.

I knew she was a huntress before I saw the badge, the way you know weather is coming in your knees. She moved through the crowd the way a heron moves through water — no hurry, no wasted motion, everything parting around her without being asked. Tall, dark-haired, with a scar through one eyebrow that looked earned rather than decorative. She carried herself like someone who had never once needed to raise her voice, because the world generally arranged itself to hear her the first time.

She took the platform beside the herald and said nothing while he finished reading. That was the unsettling part. The herald performed; she simply stood, and the standing was worse than any speech. Her eyes went across the crowd in slow, even sweeps, like a lighthouse, and I had the distinct feeling that wherever that gaze landed, it lingered a half-second longer than chance.

"Sera Dain," breathed the old man beside me. "The king's own. They say she tracked the Vellmarsh bandits into the fens alone and brought back four."

"They say a lot of things," I said.

"She'll find them," he said. "The speakers." And then, quieter, almost to himself: "She'll find them all."

I finished my pie. It tasted like nothing. I began, casually, with the unhurried gait of a man whose errands were done, to work my way toward the lane that led north out of town.

That's when I heard Kess scream.

Ravens don't scream the way other birds do. Kess's cry is usually commentary — dry, ironic, delivered from a safe height. This was different. This was a sound ripped out of her, and it hit me in the sternum like a fist, because I'd known her eleven years and I'd never heard her afraid.

I found her at the well at the square's edge, and I will carry the sight of it to whatever grave I'm owed. There was a net strung between two posts — fine mesh, silvered thread woven through it in patterns that hurt to look at, charm-work stitched into every knot. A snare for speakers, or for anything a speaker loved. And Kess was in it.

She'd gone in after a scrap of bread some child had dropped. Or she'd gone in deliberately, because she'd seen the net and wanted to tell me about it, and raven sense isn't raven wisdom. The moment her wings touched the mesh the charms had lit up like frost-fire, and now she hung there tangled, thrashing, each movement wrapping her tighter, and the silvered thread was burning her. I could smell it. Feathers and something sweeter, like scorched sugar. She screamed again and the sound came out wrong, thinned, as if the net were drinking her voice.

And the square noticed. Of course it did. A trapped raven is a curiosity. A raven screaming in a charm-net is a spectacle, and spectacles draw huntresses.

Sera Dain was already turning toward the well.

There is a version of me — the smart version, the version who survived twenty-six years by being nobody, by keeping his head down and his gift buried so deep even I sometimes forgot it was there — who walks away. Who lets a bird die to keep a life. I've met that version of myself in dark moments and he's always seemed reasonable, cold and sensible, a man built entirely of exits.

Kess screamed a third time, and the smart version of me stopped existing.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the crowd, the posts, my hands on the mesh. The charm-work bit into my palms the instant I touched it — cold first, then hot, then a grinding pressure behind my eyes like something trying to read me. It was made for this. Made to catch the gift and hold it, and here I was, volunteering.

"*Hold still,*" I said to Kess, in the way I only ever spoke to beasts — not with my mouth, though my mouth said it too, but in the older grammar underneath, the language that isn't words. "*Hold still, love. I have you.*"

Her thrashing stopped. She looked at me with one wild eye, and I felt her recognition come through the pain like a hand gripping mine.

The net fought me. Charm-work is just a bargain somebody else made — a payment of pain for a payment of power — and I bargained back. I gave it what it wanted. I opened the door I'd kept shut my whole life and let the gift pour out of me into the silvered threads, let it taste everything I was, and the threads shuddered, confused, overwhelmed, and tore.

Not all at once. Knot by knot, as my fingers worked and my gift pushed, the net came apart like a rotted seam. Kess tumbled free into my arms — light, too light, feathers smoking, her heart hammering against my wrists faster than any heart should go.

And the square was silent. Truly silent this time. Because everyone had heard. Not my mouth — my *voice*, the other one, the one that isn't words, ringing off the tithe-house wall, rolling through the crowd, unmistakable as a bell. Beast-speech, spoken openly, in daylight, in front of forty witnesses and the king's own huntress.

I stood there holding a wounded raven, my hood fallen back, my palms blistered silver, and understood with perfect clarity that my life as I had built it was over. Twenty-six years of careful nothing, undone in ninety seconds because a bird I loved flew into a trap meant for me.

Sera Dain stepped down off the platform.

She didn't call for the soldiers. That was somehow worse. She walked toward me through the frozen crowd at that unhurried heron's pace, and her eyes — grey, level, terribly awake — were fixed not on my face but on my hands, on the fading charm-light still crawling over my blisters like frost melting off a window.

"Name," she said. Quietly. Just for me.

I could lie. Some distant part of me noted that lying remained technically available.

"Wren Villalobos," I heard myself say. "Beekeeper. Smuggler of honey, mostly." Kess shifted weakly against my chest. "And apparently that."

Something flickered across her face — not triumph. I'd expected triumph. It looked almost like regret, arriving a half-second late, and then it was gone, packed away behind the scar and the level stare.

"Wren Villalobos," she repeated, and raised her voice just enough to carry. "In the king's name, you'll come with me."

Behind her, at the far edge of the square, Kess lifted her head from my arm and screamed — not fear this time, but that dry, ironic commentary I knew so well, aimed past Sera Dain's shoulder at the northern road, where the snow was already beginning to fall.

I looked where she was looking. I thought about the stag, and the barn, and the ember of a story my grandmother used to tell about a mountain that remembered everything.

Then I ran.
