## 3. Run Through the White Pass

The snow started an hour after Millbrook, which I took as a personal insult.

Any decent storm would have covered my tracks by nightfall. This one came down fine and dry as flour, the kind that didn't bury a trail so much as decorate it, every bootprint crisply outlined for anyone with eyes and a grudge. And Sera Dain, I was learning, had both in generous supply.

I'd gone up through the birches behind the tannery, then cut west along the creek until the ice took my weight, doubling back twice and once walking backward in my own footprints like a fool in a mummer's play. Old smuggler's tricks. My father's tricks, mostly, and his father's before him — a whole family tree of people who'd gotten very good at not being found, for reasons my family had gotten even better at not discussing.

The reasons were currently gaining on me.

Kess found me at dusk, dropping out of the grey sky to land on a deadfall, feathers ruffled, outrage radiating off her like heat off a forge.

*Don't*, I said, before she could start. *Whatever you're about to say — don't.*

*You run like a man who has never been chased by anything that wanted to catch him,* Kess said. *Which is true, so I forgive it. But the huntress does not follow your trail, little speaker.*

I stopped. The cold got into the pause and made itself comfortable.

*She follows the ravens,* Kess went on, hopping along the deadfall, *because she is clever, and because the crown's charm-mongers paid certain of my kind in shining things to scream when they see you. I have persuaded two of them otherwise. There is a third, and he is stupid, and he is loud.*

*Can you—*

*I am going to defenestrate him socially, yes. It will take until morning. After that, you are on your own against her nose.* She fixed me with one black eye. *There is shelter above the treeline. The old shrine on White Pass. Go there. Stone remembers things, and some stones remember kindly.*

Then she was gone, a ragged shape swallowed by snow, and I stood alone in the birches understanding something I should have understood days ago: this wasn't going to blow over. Proclamations didn't blow over. Hunts didn't blow over. I had spent twenty-six years building a life with all the load-bearing walls hidden, and the crown had walked through the front of it whistling, and there was no rebuilding a house while someone watched you try.

So I ran north, toward the pass, and let the storm have my name.

* * *

Sera Dain had been hunting men since she was seventeen, and she had learned early that most of them ran stupid.

This one did not.

Wren Villalobos — smuggler, miller's odd-jobs man, the quiet Villalobos boy everyone in Millbrook swore couldn't so much as housebreak a cat — ran like a fox that had read every book ever written about foxhunting. He crossed water. He used game trails that no game used. At the creek he'd walked backward in his own prints, which was such an old trick that it circled around to being clever again, because she'd nearly dismissed it as too obvious to be real.

He was also, she noted, climbing, when every instinct said a fugitive should go down. Down was valleys, valleys were towns, towns were crowds to drown in. Instead he was heading straight for White Pass in a snowstorm, toward nothing, toward the ruined shrine the maps marked with a faded *caveat*.

Nobody climbed toward nothing unless the nothing mattered to them.

"Speaker," she said to the empty trees, tasting the word. In the briefings it came pre-loaded: beast-speakers were unstable, the pamphlets said. Beasts whispered in their heads until they couldn't tell their own thoughts from animal hungers. They were pitiable, dangerous, and best taken gently, for their own sake.

She had taken three already. Two had wept. One had begged her to shoot him instead, and she'd thought, at the time, that it was fear.

The third raven had come shrieking over the ridge twenty minutes ago, paid in polished tin, screaming directions. Sera had shot it down herself with an arrow she hadn't strictly needed to spend, and told herself it was because its screaming carried in this snow and would spook the quarry.

She was getting to be a very good liar, out here. It worried her more than the cold did.

She climbed.

* * *

By midnight I could no longer feel my feet, which I chose to interpret as the mountain accepting me as one of its own rather than as the early stages of losing toes.

White Pass lived up to the first half of its name. The wind came down off the peaks with the smell of deep ice and old stone, and somewhere under that, faint as a memory, the wrongness I'd been feeling since the stag died in front of me — the mountain's silence, like walking through a house where everyone you love is asleep and won't wake.

The shrine sat in a fold of rock below the saddle: a low archway of black stone, half-swallowed by drift, carved — I wiped the rime away with my sleeve — with beasts. Wolves, ravens, a stag with antlers like winter branches, and between them, human figures standing with open hands. Speakers. My family's mark was worn almost smooth on the lintel. I'd never known it existed until tonight, and touching it felt like pressing my thumb into a scar I'd had all my life without noticing.

Inside, out of the wind, I allowed myself ten minutes of shaking and one minute of thinking the future might hold something other than rope. Then I heard boots on scree, deliberate, unhurried, and my whole body turned into a single clenched knot.

There was exactly one way out of the shrine's hollow: up and over the saddle, across a ledge I knew from smuggling days — a goat-track above a cliff the locals called the Widow's Tooth, four hundred feet of air dressed in ice. Nobody sane would follow me onto it in a whiteout.

Which told me precisely what kind of person was following me.

* * *

Sera came around the shrine's arch with her bow strung and found him standing in the lee of the far wall, snow to his knees, hands raised in a gesture that was either surrender or theater. His raven — the unaccounted-for variable, the one her orders insisted was a familiar, a leash the beast kept on its pet — sat on his shoulder like a epaulette, watching her with an attention that made her skin crawl.

"Wren Villalobos," she said. "By order of the crown, you'll come with me."

"You've been following me since the creek," he said. Conversational. Teeth chattering only slightly. "Can I ask how?"

"The ravens."

Something crossed his face — not fear. Disappointment, maybe, or a bitter kind of amusement. "They sold me."

"They're beasts."

"So am I, apparently." He took one step back. Snow slid from the ledge behind him. "Huntress. Before you do whatever comes next — did you burn the Hollis barn?"

She hadn't. That had been the garrison at Ford's Crossing, overzealous, before the alive-if-possible order came down. "No."

"But it happened. A child." His voice cracked on the word and reassembled itself. "Because of what he was. Because of a proclamation your king wrote."

"My king wrote it to protect—"

"To protect whom?" The raven spread its wings, and Sera's finger found the string. "Look at me. Look at what I actually am. You've watched me for two days. What monster have you seen?"

And the raven looked at her.

That was the only way she could describe it afterward, though she never described it to anyone. The bird's eye caught the last grey light, and the light wasn't light anymore but something older, and Sera Dain saw — not images, exactly. Truths. A boy crouched in a hayloft talking a mare through a difficult foaling with his whole heart open. A wolf pack parted for him in the Thornfell dark because he asked, politely, in the tongue their mothers used. A dying stag spending its last breath on a warning for a stranger, because a stranger had knelt in the snow and *listened*. Not a man commanded by beasts. A man trusted by them, which was a different thing entirely, a thing her orders had no category for, a thing the pamphlets had never mentioned because it did not frighten anyone.

Her bow arm lowered an inch. She hated it for lowering.

"That's not—" she started.

"It's exactly what it is," Wren said quietly. "And you know it now. So. Huntress. Your move."

He stepped backward off the ledge.

* * *

I want it recorded that I didn't jump. I stepped. There's a difference, and I was the only one in a position to appreciate it, dropping sideways onto the goat-track I'd prayed was still there under the snow, hearing Sera shout something behind me that the wind shredded before it arrived.

The Widow's Tooth in a storm. Four hundred feet of nothing on my left, a shelf of ice-slick stone eighteen inches wide under my boots, and behind me, impossibly, deliberately, the measured footfalls of a woman following me onto it, because of course she was. I had met dogs with less pride than Sera Dain.

Halfway across, the mountain decided to comment. A crack like a rifle shot — the drift above the ledge letting go, snow pouring past me into the dark, and my footing went with it. I hit the rock face, arms spread, boots skating on nothing, and hung there by my fingernails over four hundred feet of white silence.

When I hauled my chin up, she was standing over me. Bow in hand. Perfectly steady. Snow gathering on her shoulders like ermine.

"Take my hand," Sera Dain said.

I stared at her. "Your orders—"

"Are to take you alive, which I cannot do if you're at the bottom of this mountain." Her jaw was tight enough to crack walnuts. "Take my hand, Villalobos. I'm not saying it twice; my arm's getting tired."

So I did. She hauled me up with a strength that suggested the crown trained its huntresses on siege engines, set me against the rock face, and stood there breathing hard, looking at me with the expression of a woman doing long division and disliking the remainder.

"I can't bring you in," she said finally.

"No," I agreed. "Please don't."

"The child in the barn." Her voice was flat, careful, each word laid down like a stone she didn't trust to stay put. "His name was Tam Hollis. I'm going to find out everything about that. And you're going to go up over this pass, and I'm going to go back down, and report—" she exhaled, slow, the breath of a woman stepping off a ledge of her own, "—that the Villalobos man broke his neck on the Widow's Tooth. Which will be nearly true. It'll be true enough."

"Why?" The question came out smaller than I meant it to.

She looked past me, at the storm, at the shapeless white immensity of the peaks, and for a moment something young and grieving showed through the huntress the way a candle shows through a shuttered window.

"Because I've killed for worse reasons than the truth," she said. "Go. Don't let me see you again."

I went. At the top of the saddle I looked back once — a small figure descending into the white, straight-backed, alone — and Kess landed on my shoulder, feathers crusted with ice.

*Well,* she said. *The mountain works strangely. It sent you a hunter and returned a doubt.*

*Is that good?*

*It's a beginning. Beginnings are fragile. Walk, little speaker — the shrine is close, and I think the stone has been waiting for you a long time.*

Below us, dwindling in the storm, Sera Dain raised one hand — not a wave, exactly. More like a salute. Or an apology. And turned her face toward the valley, where she would have to stand in front of her commanders and lie for the first time in a career built on truth, and where, though neither of us could know it yet, the price of that lie was already waiting for her in the form of a brother's name on a prisoner list.

But the mountain knew. I felt it in the stone under my boots, faint as a heartbeat through a wall.

Something down there, beneath the peaks, had begun to wake.
