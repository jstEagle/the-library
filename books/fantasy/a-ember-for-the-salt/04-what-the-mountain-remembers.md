## 4. What the Mountain Remembers

The shrine found me the way all the best hiding places do: by accident, at the point of total exhaustion, when my legs had filed a formal complaint and my lungs had begun drafting a resignation letter.

I'd run through the night and most of the morning, following a smuggler's track so old it had forgotten it was ever a road. The storm had chased me up out of the low passes, and the snow had finally driven me off the trail entirely, into a fold of the mountain where the pines grew thick and the silence grew thicker. Then the trees parted, and there it was.

A shrine. Or what was left of one.

It had been built into the hillside itself, which is the only reason it still stood. Half the roof had fallen in, and what remained was furred with moss and hung with icicles like the teeth of some patient, frozen beast. The walls were stone, and the stone was carved — I could see that even in the failing light — covered edge to edge in figures: stags and ravens, wolves and bears, and between them, human shapes, small and upright, their hands raised as if in greeting. Or in surrender. Hard to say, at that distance. Hard to say with my people's history, generally.

I stood in the doorway a long moment, snow melting down my collar, and I thought about turning back.

Here's the thing about growing up Villalobos in a valley that burns barns: you learn to feel for traps the way other people feel for weather. My mother taught me to read a room before I entered it, a road before I took it, a stranger's smile before I trusted it. And this shrine was reading *loud*. Old magic has a texture. It prickles at the back of the neck, like the moment before lightning. The whole ruin was humming with it, low and constant, a note held so long it had become part of the mountain's breathing.

But the storm was rising, the light was dying, and the alternative was freezing to death on an exposed trail with the crown's hounds somewhere behind me. So I did what my family has always done when the choice is between caution and survival.

I went in, and I told myself it was only for the night.

* * *

The interior was one round chamber, open to the sky where the roof had caved, and in the center stood the stone.

I'd seen covenant stones before — or I'd seen what the crown left of them. Stumps. Stubs. The mountain roads are dotted with them, broken pillars where the carvings have been chiseled off, left standing as a lesson. *This was, and is no more, and you had best forget it was.* My grandmother used to spit when we passed one. I used to think it was theater. I don't anymore.

This one had been broken too — I could see the old wound across its middle, a seam of pale scar-tissue where the stone had been split and badly mended — but it still stood taller than I did, and it was whole enough that the carvings survived. I circled it slowly, trailing meltwater, reading the figures the way I'd been taught to read tracks. Stags running. Wolves running *beside* them, not after. Ravens riding antlers. And the human figures again, everywhere, woven through the whole procession, hands raised.

Not surrender, I decided. Greeting. Or something better than either.

*You shouldn't touch it.*

The thought arrived in my own voice, which is how I knew it was mine, and I ignored it, which is also how I knew it was mine. I'm an expert at ignoring my own good sense. It's a family trade, like smuggling.

I pulled off my glove and laid my bare hand against the stone.

* * *

The mountain took me.

I don't have better words than that, and I've had three days and one very cold night to look for them. One moment I was standing in a ruined shrine with wet socks, and the next I was standing in the same place — but it wasn't ruined. The roof was whole, the walls were warm, the fire in the central hearth was burning a color I have no name for: not orange, not gold, something older, like the light of the world's first evening. And the stone was whole. Unbroken. Alive. It rose through the floor and up through the open smoke-hole and I understood, with the certainty of a dream, that it wasn't a monument to the covenant.

It *was* the covenant. A root. A taproot, driven down into the deep places, drawing up something the mountain made and giving it to the world above.

And the shrine was full of people.

They moved like memories move — I couldn't look directly at their faces, couldn't hold a name, but I knew them the way you know a song you heard before you could speak. My people. Villalobos jawlines and Villalobos hands, and others besides, families I'd never heard of, all of them gathered in the firelight, and all of them *talking* — not to each other. To the beasts.

A woman stood at the stone with a wolf the size of a pony, her hand on its neck, and I could hear both sides of it: her words in the common tongue, and the wolf's answer — not words exactly, but meaning, arriving whole in the mind like a stone dropped in still water. A man argued cheerfully with a raven perched on his shoulder, and lost. Children sat in a circle with a stag, and the stag was telling them something, and they were laughing, and the sound of it went through me like a knife because I had heard that exact laughter before — in my grandmother's kitchen, in my mother's voice, in my own, before I learned to keep it behind my teeth.

*This is what it was for.* The understanding settled over me like snow. *Not hiding. Not smuggling. Bridging.*

I saw the work of it, then, in flashes: speakers standing between a bear and a village that feared it, translating grief in both directions. Speakers walking the high passes with wolves as guides, warning both realms of avalanches, of hard winters, of hunger. The mountain's magic moving up through the stone and out through the speakers and into the world, and the world giving something back — I couldn't see what, exactly, only that the exchange ran both ways, that the mountain grew *richer* for it, that the forests in the vision were deep and loud and full of voices.

And then a king came.

I felt him before I saw him — a cold spot in the warm room, like a draft under a door. He arrived with soldiers and a crown that caught the fire's old light and gave none of it back. I couldn't see his face either. I'm not sure the memory could hold it. But I heard his voice, and it was a reasonable voice, a patient voice, the voice of a man who has never once been told no and finds it merely interesting.

He didn't demand gold. He didn't demand land. He demanded *kneeling*.

The beasts to kneel. The wild realms to come beneath the crown, to be counted, catalogued, commanded. The mountain's magic to flow one way only — up, into the throne — because a king had looked at a covenant between equals and seen, in the word *equal*, an insult.

I watched my ancestors refuse. I watched it with my whole heart, wanting to shout it with them. And I watched the king smile his reasonable smile, and I watched what came after: the fires. The hunts. The stone split with iron and malice. Speakers dragged from their homes — and the beasts, cut off from the voices that had spoken for them, turning confused and then afraid and then angry, the way anything turns when the hand that fed it becomes the hand that holds the knife.

The vision darkened. The fire guttered. And in the last of the light I saw the stone as it was now — broken, mended badly, humming its long held note in the dark — and I saw that the note was not strength.

It was *waiting*.

* * *

I came back to myself on the shrine floor, on my hands and knees, with frost in my hair and my heart going like a drum in a parade.

How long I'd been under, I couldn't say. The storm had buried the fallen roof; snow sifted down through the smoke-hole in a fine white veil. My hand was still on the stone. I hadn't let go. Some part of me, apparently, had more sense than the rest.

"Alright," I said out loud, because the silence was getting ideas. "That was — fine. That was fine. Visions are normal. Everyone has visions."

Kess would have had something to say about that. Kess, who was somewhere out there in the white dark, hopefully alive, hopefully having the sense to shelter, though she was a raven, and ravens have exactly as much sense as suits them. Thinking of her put a stone in my chest. Thinking of the barn — of the child — put a knife under it.

I tried to stand. My knees had other plans. And that was when I noticed the warmth.

My right hand — the one on the stone — was glowing.

Not blazing. Not burning. A small light sat in my palm like a coal, like a seed of the hearth-fire from the vision, that same unnamed color, the light of the world's first evening. It didn't hurt. It was warm the way a held hand is warm. And I could feel it *weigh* something, though it weighed nothing — a gravity of meaning, a small dense certainty, the way a promise is heavier than the breath that makes it.

I knew what it was the way I'd known the vision. Inheritance. The old fire, an ember of it, pried loose from the broken root by whatever was left of the covenant's will — or its hope, or its stubbornness, all of which are the same thing in my family.

"No," I told it. Firmly. The way you tell a raven not to steal your breakfast. "No. I'm a smuggler. I move cheese and untaxed brandy. I am not — I don't do *quests*. I don't do destiny. I once hid in a wine cellar for two days to avoid a wedding. This is not my—"

The ember pulsed. And the mountain spoke.

Not in words. Not in the beasts' way, either — not meaning dropped whole into the mind. This was older and vaster, a voice like the creak of glaciers, like the settling of stone, and it came up through my hand, through my knees, through the whole floor of the world, and it said what it had been waiting generations to say:

*Carry it home. Carry it down. To the Salt Court, beneath the peaks, before the last moon of winter — while there is still fire enough to wake the root. Bear it true, speaker, or the mountain dies, and everything on it.*

Then it was gone, and the silence came back in like a tide, and I was alone in a ruined shrine with a coal of the world's first evening sitting in my hand and a deadline.

The last moon of winter. I looked up through the smoke-hole at the storm-wrecked sky, at the thin ghost of the moon behind the clouds, and did the arithmetic every smuggler knows how to do — distance against time, risk against reward — and the answer came back the way those answers always come back for people like me.

*Impossible. Barely. Which means yes.*

I sat back against the broken stone, and I laughed — a small, cracked, unhinged sound that the storm politely ignored — because my grandmother used to say the gift skips no one and spares no one, and I had spent thirty years proving her wrong, and the mountain had just spent five minutes proving her right.

An ember. A dead court beneath the peaks. A kingdom hunting my kind, a huntress who'd seen my face and let me live, and a shadow — because there's always a shadow, there's always something in the dark that likes covenants broken — that I could feel, even now, even here, paying attention.

I closed my hand around the ember. It beat against my palm like a second heart.

"Alright," I said to the mountain, to the stone, to my grandmother, to nobody. "But I'm complaining the entire way."

Somewhere far below my boots, deep in the roots of the world, something heard me — and something else, colder and more patient, heard me too.

The storm began to lift. And on the wind, from the valley I'd left behind, came a sound I knew too well: horns. The hunt, moving into the high passes.

They weren't tracking me anymore.

They were tracking the light.
