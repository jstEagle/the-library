## 5. The Huntress Returns

The report I filed said Wren Villalobos was dead. Dead men, in my experience, have the decency to stay that way.

So when the summons came — not a request, never a request, the crown doesn't know how to ask — I knew my lie had been weighed and found wanting. Inquisitor Vael's clerks had gone to Millbrook. They'd asked their questions in that soft way clerks have, the way that makes people remember things they'd forgotten they saw. A raven freed from a charm-net by a man who spoke to it. A smuggler who vanished into Thornfell with soldiers a day behind him.

Dead men don't talk to ravens.

I rode back into the mountains with three troopers I didn't trust and a new set of orders I liked even less: *find Villalobos, alive this time.* The word *alive* was underlined twice in Vael's hand, which meant he wanted him breathing for reasons that had nothing to do with justice. I've read enough of Vael's paperwork to know what his underlines cost.

The troopers were named Brack, Odo, and a boy everyone called Finch because he looked like one — all elbows and nerves. Brack talked too much. Odo didn't talk at all, which was worse. Finch kept looking at the treeline like it owed him money.

"Story goes," Brack said around a mouthful of dried plums, "the beast-speakers can call a wolf right up to your throat. Look it in the eye and it just — lies down." He snapped his teeth at me, delighted with himself. "You ever seen one work, Captain?"

"Once," I said. "It wasn't like that."

It had been exactly like that, actually, on a cliff edge in a snowstorm, when a raven landed on my crossbow and showed me something no bird should be able to show anyone: a man's whole self, laid open like a gutted fish. What I'd seen in Wren Villalobos wasn't a monster. It was a tired smuggler carrying something old and heavy, and being hunted for it by people like me.

I'd let him go. Then I'd written the neatest lie of my career, and now here I was, riding back into Thornfell with my career hanging off my saddle like a gutted deer.

* * *

We found the burned barn first.

Brack wanted to make camp there — four walls, however charred, beat the open snow — but the smell stopped him halfway through the door. I stood outside and let the cold eat my face and thought about Tam.

My brother was taken in the first sweep. That's the sentence I keep having to think in full, because if I shorten it — *Tam was taken* — it stops sounding real and starts sounding like grief, and I don't have time for grief. Full version: my brother Tam, nineteen years old, who once spent an entire summer teaching a magpie to steal Father's pipe, was taken in the first sweep because he whistled and the crows answered, and he'd been doing it since he was six and never once thought to hide it, because why would you hide the only beautiful thing about yourself?

They took him alive. The proclamation said alive. Nobody would tell me where *alive* went after the wagons rolled east toward the capital, and every clerk I asked developed a sudden fascination with their own boots.

So yes. I had reasons. Reasons are just grief wearing armor, but they hold a line well enough.

Finch came out of the trees pale as milk. "Captain. Tracks. Wolf, big one, and — boot prints going the same direction. Fresh."

Of course they were fresh. Wren Villalobos had the survival instincts of a man who'd grown up knowing these woods better than the crown's maps did, which meant he moved like smoke and left tracks like a drunk. Either he was careless or he wanted to be found, and I'd learned enough about him on that cliff to bet on the second.

I told the troopers we'd found nothing and doubled back alone at dusk. They believed me. That's the trouble with being good at your job — nobody checks.

* * *

He was waiting at the ruined shrine, sitting on a fallen lintel like a man waiting for a ferry, Kess the raven hunched on his shoulder with her feathers puffed against the cold.

"You're late," he said.

"I had to lose three idiots." I kept my hand near my knife out of habit, not intent. Mostly not intent. "How long have you known I was coming?"

"Since the stag smelled your horses two days ago. She says you ride like a funeral."

"The stag and I agree on more than I'd like." I stopped ten feet from him. Snow was starting again, fat slow flakes that made the world quiet in a way I've never trusted. "Here's how this goes, Villalobos. My report failed verification. Vael sent me back with orders to take you alive, and three witnesses to make sure I do. If I come back empty twice, I stop being a huntress and start being a suspect, and then they send someone who won't sit on a wall and chat."

"So arrest me," he said mildly.

"I'm offering you the opposite of that."

Kess made a sound — half croak, half laugh, the way she does when she thinks humans are being dense. Wren scratched her neck absently, and I watched him do it, watched the small ordinary tenderness of it, and hated how much it reminded me of Tam feeding crumbs to gulls off the mill bridge.

"I heard about the sweeps," he said quietly. "The valley towns. I heard what they're doing."

"Then you know why I'm here, and it isn't the crown's business." I pulled off my glove and held out my hand, palm up, the way you do when you're trying to prove you're unarmed and mostly failing. "My brother. Tam Dain. Nineteen. Whistled to crows. Taken in the first sweep out of Harrow's Ford. No record of where they took him. No record of anything. You want to know why I lied in my report? There it is. That's the whole of it."

Wren was silent a long moment. Snow gathered on his shoulders and he didn't brush it off.

"They'll do the same to you," he said finally. "When they find out you let me go. You understand that. Whatever bargain you're building, it ends with you in a wagon too."

"Probably." I shrugged. "I've been thinking about what happens after 'probably,' and I've decided I'd rather hang for the right thing than get promoted for the wrong one. It's a recent development. Don't get used to it."

"What's the bargain?"

I'd rehearsed it on the ride up, all the sharp official phrasing, and it all fell apart the moment I opened my mouth, so what came out was just true: "There's a cordon across the Salt Road. Forty men, ward-chains, hawk patrols. You'll never get through alone, and whatever you're carrying—" I nodded at his chest, at the faint warmth I could feel from ten feet away, like standing near a banked fire, "—whatever the mountain gave you, it dies with you if you die in the woods. So. I guide you past the cordon. I get you to the Salt Court, or wherever it is you're going. And in exchange, you prove it."

"Prove what?"

"That it can be reforged." My voice cracked on the second word and I let it; some things you shouldn't say smoothly. "That the covenant isn't just an old story the priests use to scare children. That the beasts can be called back from whatever's turning them mad. Because if it can — if there's any chance it can — then maybe the gifted aren't criminals. Maybe they're necessary. Maybe the crown needs them alive and *whole*, and someone has to stand in front of the king and prove it before every speaker in the lowlands is in a wagon heading east." I swallowed. "Maybe someone proves it, and the wagons turn around, and one of them has my brother in it."

The snow filled the silence between us.

"You're asking me to save the world so you can save one boy," Wren said.

"Yes," I said. "Is that a problem?"

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but its cousin. "No," he said. "That might be the most honest reason anyone's ever had for anything." He stood, and Kess shifted her grip on his shoulder, and I felt the ember-warmth in his chest shift with him, patient as a coal. "But you should know what I know, Huntress Dain. The ember dims. Every mile, a little less. The stone that gave it to me said *before the last moon of winter*, and winter is already old. If we don't reach the Salt Court in time, there's no proof to give anyone. There's just a dead man and a disgraced huntress and a mountain that keeps dying."

"Then we'd best not be slow."

"There's one other thing." His jaw tightened, and for the first time since I'd met him, he looked afraid — not of me, which would have been sensible, but of something behind me, out in the dark trees. "The beasts that attacked me last week weren't just maddened. Something is *herding* them. Something that knows about the ember and wants it unlit. It's between us and the Salt Court, and it's been listening. Possibly it's listening now."

I turned slowly, scanning the treeline. Black trunks. Falling snow. Nothing.

"Comforting," I said.

"I thought you'd want the full picture."

"I want a drink and a different life. We none of us get what we want." I pulled my glove back on and turned north, toward the cordon, toward forty armed men and a shadow-thing and a moon that was running out of patience. "Keep up, Villalobos. And if your stag friend sees anything with more teeth than a rabbit, I expect to hear about it *before* it's chewing on Finch."

"He's one of yours?"

"He's nobody's. That's why I'm keeping him alive."

Wren whistled — low, three notes, nothing a human ear would mark — and somewhere upslope, a shape detached itself from the dark and paced us at fifty yards, gray on gray, gone when I looked directly at it.

*Escort,* Kess croaked, in a voice like a hinge.

I decided not to think too hard about the fact that I was starting to understand the bird.

We walked into the falling snow, the traitor and the smuggler and the ghost of a boy in a wagon heading east, and above us the last moon of winter hung thin as a paring, counting down.
