## 6. Teeth in the Dark

The forest went quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone you owe money to walks in.

Kess felt it first. She'd been riding the thermals above us all afternoon, lazy as a leaf, and then she wasn't — she dropped through the canopy and landed on Sera's shoulder, which she never did, because Sera was still technically the woman who'd hunted us across the White Pass and Kess held grudges the way rivers hold water: completely and without visible effort.

"Something's wrong," I said, and immediately felt stupid, because obviously something was wrong. The birds had stopped. The squirrels had stopped. Even the wind seemed to be holding its breath between the pines.

Sera's hand went to her knife without her seeming to notice it had moved. "Wolves?"

"Worse, maybe. Or just wolves having a very bad century." I crouched and pressed my palm flat to the frozen ground. This was the part of the gift I'd spent my whole life hiding, the part that felt less like a talent and more like pressing your ear to a wall and hearing the argument on the other side. The earth carried them. Many paws. Moving fast. Not hunting the way wolves hunt — patient, economical, taking the wounded one. This was a hunt with a grudge in it.

"They know we're here," I said. "They've known since the pass. They've been herding us."

Sera turned, and I watched her do the arithmetic she'd been doing for three days — the arithmetic of a woman who had reported me dead and then been sent back to verify the corpse. "Herding us where?"

"Doesn't matter. They're here."

They came out of the treeline like a decision already made. Six wolves, maybe seven — it was hard to count them because they weren't moving like a pack, they were moving like a fist. Big, gray, winter-lean, and wrong in a way I couldn't name at first. Then I could. Their eyes. A wolf's eyes are the eyes of something that knows exactly what it is. These eyes were the eyes of something that had been told what it was, over and over, for a hundred years, by a silence where a voice used to be.

The leader stopped ten feet away. Big male, scar across the muzzle, breath steaming. And when I reached for him with the gift — the way I'd reached for ravens and stags and half the contraband-bearing wildlife of Thornfell my whole life — what came back wasn't a voice.

It was screaming.

Not sound. Not yet. Just the shape of it, the pressure of it, a hundred years of a broken promise compressed into one animal skull. I'd felt grief through the gift before. I'd never felt it like this — grief that had been left out in the weather so long it had gone to rot, and rot, as my mother used to say, always finds something to do.

"They're not going to talk first," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted. "Sera, the knife. Put it down. Slowly."

"You're joking."

"The knife says *enemy*. Everything you carry says *enemy*. The crown put a bounty on their kind for sixty years and the metal remembers." I kept my eyes on the scarred wolf. "They can taste it. Iron holds a grudge like everything else out here."

She didn't put it down. She was a royal huntress; the knife was probably the last honest thing she owned. But she let it hang loose in its sheath, and that was something, and the pack's forward creep slowed by a hair.

The scarred one spoke, and it was like listening to a door that hadn't opened in a century.

*Speaker.* The word came through cracked and rusted. *We smelled you at the pass. We smelled the old fire on you. You carry what was stolen from us.*

"I carry an ember of the covenant," I said. "I'm taking it to the Salt Court. To reforge it. To fix this."

The pack made a sound then, all of them at once, low in their chests — and it wasn't a growl, exactly. It was laughter, if laughter could bleed. *Fix it. The two-legs says he will fix it. With a coal and a walk.*

"I know how it sounds—"

*You know nothing.* He came a step closer and the whole pack came with him, a tide of gray. *My mother remembered the speaking days. She told us the names of the first pact-brothers until she starved in a winter the mountain forgot to soften. My father died in a crown snare and no raven came to warn him, because the ravens had stopped answering. We waited. We starved. We went quiet. And now the quiet has a master, little speaker, and the master is hungry, and it tells us that the old fire is coming down the mountain in the hands of a thief, and that we should take it.*

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the season. "A master. What master?"

*The one beneath.* The scarred wolf's lips peeled back. *The one that grew in the wound your kings made. It eats what leaks from the broken pact. It has eaten so much of us that some of my own kin answer it now and call it the Shepherd. And it knows about your ember, Wren Villalobos. It has known since the shrine. It is not sending wolves like us, who are merely broken.*

He was close enough now that I could smell the wrongness on him — under the musk, something like ash and something like the inside of a locked room.

*It is sending the ones who are hungry.*

That's when the rest of them came.

Not the six in front of us. From behind — from the dark we'd walked through all afternoon, the quiet we'd mistaken for emptiness. Eight, ten, twelve, eyes like coals in a dead hearth, and their eyes were worse than the scarred one's, because his eyes still had grief in them and theirs had only appetite. They came out of the shadows the way shadows come out of shadows, and the scarred one's voice cracked through my skull one last time, ragged, almost human in its despair:

*Run, speaker. We were sent to hold you. I am old enough to choose how badly I hold.*

Then the world came apart into teeth.

Sera moved the way I imagine she moved in every hunt she'd ever won — no wasted motion, knife out and singing, and she put herself between me and the first leaping shape with a speed that made my chest hurt for reasons that had nothing to do with fear. Kess went up screaming, a raven's war-cry, dive-bombing eyes. I did the only thing I could do, the thing my family had bled for generations for me to be able to do.

I stopped fighting and I *spoke*.

I threw the gift wide, wider than I'd ever dared, not at one wolf but at all of them, and I didn't send words. Words were too small. I sent the shrine. I sent the vision — the covenant stone whole, the ancestors with their palms on it, the wolves of the first winter lying at their feet with their ears up, the two realms breathing through one throat. I sent it into the dark like a man throwing a lantern down a well.

And for one heartbeat, the forest stopped.

Wolves froze mid-leap. One hung in the air an arm's length from my throat, and in that suspended moment its eyes flickered — the coal-glow guttering, the old wolf looking out from behind the hunger, *seeing* it, the pact, the memory of being spoken to like a brother instead of a beast — and I heard it, faint as a voice through a wall:

*We were loved once. We were loved once. Why did you stop—*

Then the shadow behind its eyes slammed down like a shutter, and the wolf landed, and the moment was gone, and it came for me anyway.

Sera's shoulder hit me and we went down together into the snow, jaws snapping where my face had been. She rolled, came up slashing, bought us three feet of bloodied ground. "Wren!" she shouted, and it was the first time she'd said my name like it wasn't a report she was filing. "Whatever you're doing — do more of it!"

"I don't know how to do more of it!"

"Then do it louder!"

Kess shrieked from above — and I understood. The ember. It was against my chest, wrapped in wool, and it had been warming all day the way a banked fire warms, and I had been so busy carrying it I'd forgotten it was *alive*. I tore open my coat and the light spilled out, small and gold and stubborn, and the nearest wolves flinched from it like it was a brand.

But the scarred one didn't flinch. He stood at the edge of the ember-light, and the hunger in his pack-mates' eyes rolled around him like water around a stone, and he looked at me with his ruined, grieving eyes, and he said the thing that has not stopped echoing in me since:

*It knows where you are going, speaker. It was in the Salt Court before your grandmother's grandmother was born. You are not carrying the fire home. You are carrying it to the mouth of the thing that eats fire.*

And then the old wolf turned and threw himself sideways into his own pack — not attacking me, attacking *them*, buying seconds with his body, snarling *run* in every tongue the mountain had left — and Sera hauled me up by the collar and we ran, the ember burning against my heart, Kess wheeling above us, and behind us in the dark I heard the scarred wolf's voice go out like a candle in wind.

* * *

We didn't stop until the stars were out and our lungs were done arguing with us. Sera leaned against a boulder, knife still bare, breathing in the controlled way of someone who refuses to admit she's shaking. Blood on her sleeve. None of it hers.

"Your friend," she said finally. "The old one. He held the line."

"I know."

"You're going to tell me that's what the gift costs. That's the lesson, right? You speak, and the things you speak to die for it." She wasn't being cruel. She was being Sera — testing the weight of a thing before she decided what to carry.

I looked at my hands. They were still trembling, and not from cold. "I think the lesson is worse than that," I said. "I think the lesson is that something down there has been eating the pact for a hundred years, and it knows we're coming, and it's been *letting* us come."

Sera was quiet a long moment. Then she pushed off the boulder and looked down the slope, toward the drowned tunnels, toward the Salt Court, toward the mouth of the thing that eats fire.

"Then we'd better be worth the meal," she said. "Move. We've got a mountain to betray."
