## 7. The Crown's Long Arm

I want it on the record that I did not trip the snare.

The trap was a charm-line strung across a game trail — the same crown-work that had caught Kess in Millbrook, all silver thread and hungry whispering — and Sera saw it before I did. She said, "Down," and I went down, and then the trees themselves came down around us, because of course they did. Six soldiers in grey-and-white, rising out of the snow like the mountain had grown them. Crossbows. Charm-cuffs. The particular smile of men who have been promised a bonus per head.

"Beast-speaker Wren Villalobos," said their sergeant, reading off a slate like my name was a recipe. "And Huntress Sera Dain, wanted for falsifying a kill report." He looked at her with something worse than hatred. Pity. "They'll hang you higher than him, Dain."

"They'll try," she said, and I believed her, right up until a crossbow butt met the back of my skull and the world went white and quiet.

* * *

The camp sat in a bowl of the high forest where three ridges met, ringed with charm-stakes that made my teeth ache from thirty paces. They put us in separate cages — hers iron, mine wrapped in that silver thread — which told me everything about how the crown ranked its threats. A speaker was a curiosity. A traitor huntress was an infection.

I passed the first hours cataloguing my assets. One: I could still feel the ember through my shirt, a coal-warmth against my sternum, dimmer than it had been a week ago. Two: the wolves were somewhere out there in the dark; I could feel them at the edge of my gift like voices through a wall, angry and afraid to come closer because of the stakes. Three: Sera was alive, and had not yet tried to kill anyone, which for Sera constituted optimism.

Four: I was going to die here unless something changed.

The something arrived at dusk, carried on a litter by four silent men — a narrow figure in dove-grey robes, hooded, with hands folded like a man at prayer who has long since stopped believing anyone is listening. The soldiers straightened when he passed. Even the fire seemed to burn more carefully.

"Inquisitor Vael," Sera breathed through the bars beside me. They'd moved her cage next to mine, which I chose to interpret as arrogance rather than mercy. "He broke my brother's unit in the first sweep. He interrogated half the border garrison just to see if they'd flinch."

"He sounds charming."

"He once kept a man awake for nine days using only questions."

"Noted. I'll sleep defensively."

Vael stopped before our cages and lowered his hood. He had the kind of face you forget immediately, which is its own kind of horror — a face built for paperwork, for ledgers of the condemned. His eyes were pale as frost on a window.

"Wren Villalobos." My name in his mouth sounded like an entry in an inventory. "Do you know what your family was?"

"Smugglers, mostly. Good ones."

"Speakers." He smiled without warmth, the way a door opens without welcome. "Your grandmother spoke for the wolves of Thornfell. Her mother for the ravens. Back and back, to the first Villalobos who stood beneath the peaks and gave voice to both realms. You are not a smuggler, boy. You are a broken instrument of a broken treaty, and I have been sent to learn how much of the old music is left in you."

"And if there isn't any?"

"Then you're only meat, and meat is simpler." He turned to Sera's cage. "Huntress Dain. Your brother Tam is alive."

The change in her was total and terrible. She'd been coiled loose as a cat at rest, and now every line of her pulled taut as bowstring. I felt it through the bars, through the silver thread humming against my skin.

"You're lying," she said.

"I don't lie. Lies are inefficient." Vael folded his hands again. "Tam Dain is held at Greywatch, healthy, fed, and entirely unaware that his sister has committed treason. His continued health depends on your continued cooperation. Both of you have been offered a simple arrangement. The speaker tells me where he found the ember and surrenders it. The huntress confirms her report and testifies that she killed him as ordered. In exchange: full pardons. Tam goes free. You both walk away."

"And if we refuse?" I asked, though I already knew. Men like Vael always show you the other ledger.

"If you refuse, the speaker dies slowly enough to be instructive, and Tam Dain dies quickly enough to be deniable. And the ember comes out of your chest regardless, Wren Villalobos. We have surgeons who specialize in extraction. Most subjects survive the procedure." A pause. "Some even survive the questioning after."

Here is the thing nobody tells you about fear: it clarifies. All my life I'd hidden my gift because hiding was safe, because a secret kept small stays small. But standing in that cage, watching a man offer me my life priced in someone else's blood, I understood that the hiding had never been safety. It had just been a slower version of this — a slow surrender, bargain by bargain, until nothing of me remained but what the crown permitted.

Sera spoke first.

"No."

Vael tilted his head. "For your brother—"

"My brother joined the hunt because he believed we protected people from beasts. Not that we butchered children in barns and called it covenant-keeping." Her voice didn't shake. I would remember that. "You want me to testify Wren is dead? Fine. The woman who took that report is dead too. Tell Tam his sister died at White Pass. It'll almost be true."

Vael regarded her for a long moment, then turned those frost-pale eyes on me. "And you? I confess I expected the huntress to be difficult. But you — a smuggler. Smugglers are practical creatures."

"You're right," I said. "I am extremely practical. So let me tell you what I know that you don't." I leaned close to the bars, close enough to smell the charm-thread burning faintly against my skin. "The ember isn't treasure. It's a debt. Your king broke the covenant to hoard the mountain's magic, and the mountain has been dying ever since, and the beasts have been going mad ever since, and every burned barn and emptied valley town — that's not the beasts' cruelty. That's the interest coming due on what your crown stole. Carrying this ember isn't my crime, Inquisitor. It's my payment. And I don't default on debts."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not doubt. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a truth he'd already filed away and chosen to ignore.

"The covenant was an anchor," he said quietly. "It bound the mountain's power to beasts and peasants and talking ravens. The king cut it loose so the power could flow where it belonged — to the throne. What you call theft, history will call liberation."

"History will be written by whoever survives the winter," I said. "Ask the mountain which side it's on."

Vael sighed, and it was almost gentle. "Take the speaker to the interrogation tent at moonrise. Gag the huntress until then — she has a persuasive tongue." He drew his hood back up. "I did offer."

* * *

**SERA**

They gagged me with charm-silk, which was stupid of them, because charm-silk dampens speech and sharpens everything else. I heard everything. I heard Wren's heartbeat speed when they cut him down from the cage — I'd learned his rhythms on the road, the way you learn weather. I heard the tent flap close. I heard Vael's voice begin, low and patient, asking questions the way a surgeon asks permission.

And I heard the wolves.

Not with ears. With something older that I'd spent ten years pretending wasn't there — the same sense that had let me track Wren through a blizzard when no tracker alive should have found him. The pack had come to the edge of the charm-ring, and they were singing, low and grief-struck, and every note of it scraped against the stakes like a hand testing a lock.

*Hold,* I thought at them, absurdly, uselessly. *Hold, hold.*

The first stake went down at the third watch. I never saw how — one moment the ring was whole, the next there was a gap of darkness and cold air pouring through it, and then the camp was screaming. Wolves came through the gap like grey water through a breached dam, and the soldiers' charm-lanterns guttered, and somewhere a man shouted the word *speaker* as if it were an accusation and a prayer at once.

My cage was iron, locked, guarded by two men who were already running. I did the only thing available to a caged huntress: I threw myself against the bars, once, twice, and on the third time the lock — old, cheap, crown-issue — sheared, and I was out, and the night was mine.

The interrogation tent was burning. Of course it was. I found Wren twenty feet from it, on his knees in the snow, wrists still cuffed, laughing in a cracked, breathless way while a wolf the size of a pony licked the side of his face like he owed it money.

"You're bleeding," I said, hauling him up.

"Minorly. Kess pecked the guard holding the keys and it got emotional." He staggered upright, and his eyes found mine, and for a moment neither of us said anything, because there was a whole speech sitting between us — about brothers, about refusals, about the fact that we had each just chosen death over betrayal within an hour of meeting the choice — and neither of us knew how to carry it.

"We ride north," I said instead. "Greywatch is two days. If Tam's alive, I'm getting him out before Vael makes good on his threat."

"Sera." Wren caught my arm. His hands were shaking — cold, or fear, or the ember burning low. "The Salt Court is east. Before the last moon. If I turn aside now—"

"I know what you're carrying. I've watched it dim for a hundred miles." I looked back at the burning camp, at Vael's litter abandoned in the snow, at the wolves circling with their hackles up and their eyes on me like they were waiting for a verdict. "So here's the arithmetic, smuggler. We go to Greywatch, we pull out my brother, and we run east together — because Vael will send every blade he has after that ember now, and I will not bury another person I love because I chose the mission over the man. We do both. Or we do neither."

Wren stared at me. Then he laughed again, that same cracked sound, and pressed his cuffed wrists into my hands. "Break these, then, Huntress. And somebody find my raven before she finishes looting the quartermaster's tent."

Kess landed on his shoulder at that exact moment with a charm-netting spool in her beak and an expression of profound criminal satisfaction.

We ran north, into the dark, with the mountain singing behind us — and ahead, on the wind, faint but unmistakable, I caught the scent of woodsmoke from a direction where no fire should be.

Greywatch was expecting us.
