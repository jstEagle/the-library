## 8. Descent to the Salt Court

The wolves led us to a hole in the world that smelled like a dead sea.

"Charming," Sera said, peering down into it. "You get invited to a lot of places like this?"

"Only the best," I said. "Kess has standards."

The raven in question croaked from a ledge above us — a sound I'd learned over the years to translate as *stop wasting breath and move*. The pack ringed the opening: nine gaunt shapes, gray and white and one enormous black male with frost-scars across his muzzle, their eyes catching the last gray light of the afternoon like coals banked under ash. A week ago they'd tried to eat us. Now they walked for us. That was the thing about the gift nobody warned you about — reconciliation was heavier than enmity. An enemy only wants your throat. A friend wants you not to die, and follows you into hell to make sure.

The black wolf — Ashen, the others called him in the wordless way wolves have names — stepped to the edge and looked back at me.

*Down*, he said, in the language that isn't sound but pressure, weight behind the breastbone. *Down, speaker. The old road.*

"The old road is flooded," Sera said. She'd heard him too, these days. Whatever the ember was doing to me, it was leaking. She didn't like hearing wolves. She especially didn't like admitting she liked hearing wolves. "I've seen the survey maps. The lower galleries drowned when the crown dammed the Graywater."

*Drowned is not closed,* Ashen said. *For things that swim.*

"I don't swim in armor," Sera said.

"So take off the armor," I said, and got the flat look I deserved, and we went down.

* * *

The tunnels swallowed daylight within fifty steps, and after that there was only the ember.

It rode against my chest in its wrapping of shrine-cloth, and it had stopped being warm somewhere on the third day and started being *alive* — a small stubborn pulse, like a bird's heart, like my own heart answering it from the other side of my ribs. In the dark it glowed through the cloth faintly, gold shot through with salt-white, and by that light I watched stone beasts go past.

That was the first awe. The Salt Court did not begin at the Salt Court. It began here, miles out, in corridors carved with beasts the size of wagons — stags with crowns of branches, bears mid-stride, serpents winding up pillars — all of it cut by hands that must have worked for generations, all of it *listening*, somehow, even now. Stone shouldn't have faces. These did. They had expressions. And every expression, without exception, was grief.

"They're asleep," I said, the fourth hour in. My voice came back wrong off the wet walls, flattened. "Not dead. Asleep."

"You keep saying that like it's better," Sera said. Her boots splashed. The water had come up past our ankles an hour ago and hadn't stopped rising, because of course it hadn't. "Asleep means they can wake up wrong."

"Everything wakes up wrong eventually. The trick is being there when it wakes up right."

"That's not wisdom, Villalobos, that's a saying on a tavern sign."

"And yet." I touched a carved wolf's jaw as I passed. Cold stone. But under the cold, so faint I might have invented it, a slow tidal breathing. My gift reached toward it the way a hand reaches toward a hearth in a cold room, and the stone did not answer, and that frightened me more than anything the shadow-thing had thrown at us yet.

Because I'd been listening to the mountain since Thornfell. Even muffled, even buried under years of hiding, the mountain had always murmured. Down here it was silent. Not resting-silent. *Held-breath* silent.

And the ember was dimming.

I noticed it first as a change in the dark — the corridor ahead going from gold-lit to merely suggested — and then I made the mistake of checking directly, unwrapping the cloth while we waded a long gallery where the ceiling vanished into black water overhead. The flame inside had been the size of a thumbnail when the broken shrine gave it to me. It was the size of a grain of barley now. Maybe smaller. It burned salt-white at its core, and around that white, like a bruise, crept gray.

"What?" Sera said, because I'd stopped walking, and because she was the sort of person who read a face like a trail sign.

"It's dying."

"Then feed it."

"With what? It's not a campfire, Sera. It's a pact. It eats oaths, not kindling." I wrapped it again, hands steadier than I felt. "The stone told me to carry it to the Salt Court before the last moon of winter. It didn't tell me the carrying itself would spend it."

"Everything spends everything," she said, in the voice of someone who'd buried people. "Move."

We moved.

* * *

Kess found us at the drowned gate.

I knew something was wrong before she landed because Ashen's whole pack stopped at once, nine heads swinging east, hackles lifting off their spines like grass before a storm. Then the raven came out of a side tunnel fast and low, wings hard, and hit my shoulder already talking.

*They are behind. The hollow ones. Many. Fast in the water.*

"Hollow ones," Sera said. "Those are the beasts the shadow-thing wears?"

*Wears, empties, keeps.* Kess shifted on my shoulder, feathers rigid. *They do not speak, Wren. They only remember speaking. It is worse.*

Ahead of us the corridor ended in a wall — no, a gate, two slabs of black stone carved with intertwined serpents, pulled apart just wide enough for a determined person and a very reluctant huntress. Through the gap I could see it: open water, black and still, stretching back into the mountain further than the ember's light could push. And beyond the water—

"Oh," Sera said softly.

Beyond the water rose towers.

Stone, grown, not built — or built by hands that thought in roots — a city inside the mountain, pale spires streaked with mineral veins, terraces descending to the flooded streets like amphitheater seats. Beasts stood along every terrace. Hundreds of them. Bears and elk and cats and things I had no names for, all of stone, all of them facing inward toward a glowless center where something vast waited that my eyes kept sliding off of.

The Salt Court. Silent since the breaking. I'd imagined ruins. This wasn't ruin. This was a held note.

"It's beautiful," Sera said, and then, because she was Sera: "How deep is that water?"

"Ask the wolf."

*Deep,* Ashen said unhelpfully, wading in until the water climbed his chest. *But the hollow ones come faster than you swim. Choose.*

Behind us, far down the flooded gallery, the sound arrived. Not footsteps. Not splashing. A kind of wrongness in the water itself, a ripple moving against gravity, and beneath it — Kess was right, it was worse than silence — the memory of voices. Beasts' voices, saying beast things, but looped and flattened, like a song sung by something that had eaten the singer.

Sera drew her sword. In the ember-light the blade looked like a strip of dusk.

"How long to cross?" she asked.

"At a swim? Half an hour." I was already doing the arithmetic I hated. "At a fight, forever."

"Then we don't fight," she said. "We cross loud and ugly and we don't stop. You keep that ember lit, Villalobos. That's your only job. The rest of it—" she glanced at Ashen, at Kess, at the nine wolves already fanning into the water, "—the rest of it is ours."

Ashen swam first, cutting a long V through the black water. Two wolves flanked me, one on each side, and Sera fell in behind with her sword held out of the water, and Kess flew above us all, screaming — actually screaming, raven war-song, a sound I'd heard maybe twice in my life — down the drowned street of a dead city toward the silent court where everything ended or began.

Halfway across, the hollow ones reached the gate.

I didn't see them. I felt them. The gift that had been leaking into Sera for a week tore fully open, and what poured in was not pain exactly but the *absence* of pain, absence of everything, hunger wearing shapes that used to be animals — a stag's shape with nothing behind the eyes, a bear's bulk running on legs that bent the wrong way, dozens of bodies moving through the water without splash or breath, and every one of them turning, as one, toward me. Toward the ember. The shadow-thing knew about it. Of course it knew. It had probably known since the shrine, and everything since — the patrols, the pursuit, the patience — had been it herding me here, letting me carry the pact's last life straight to the place where it fed.

The ember flickered against my chest.

Not dimming this time. *Answering.* The gray in its heart spread another hair's width, and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives when you're treading black water between stone gods and the things that emptied them, that it wasn't spending itself on distance at all.

It was spending itself on *me*. Keeping the gift open. Keeping the bridge standing while the shadow pushed against it from both sides.

Every mile, a little less Wren.

"Wren!" Sera's voice, sharp, closer than I expected — she'd caught my arm, was hauling me forward. "Stay in the water, stay with me, look at me—"

"I'm here," I lied, because part of me was already elsewhere, stretched thin across a covenant, listening to the Salt Court begin — finally, faintly, terribly — to murmur. Stone throats waking. Hundreds of carved beasts on hundreds of terraces turning their heads, one degree, two, toward the water, toward the ember, toward me.

*Speaker,* said the city, in a voice like a mountain deciding to be an avalanche. *You are late.*

"I know," I gasped, swimming, dimming, the hollow ones pouring through the gate behind us like spilled ink. "Believe me. I know."
