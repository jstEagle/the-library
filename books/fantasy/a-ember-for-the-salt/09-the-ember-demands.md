## 9. The Ember Demands

The Salt Court did not want us there.

I don't mean it was hostile. I mean the whole cavern had a quality of *reluctance*, like an old dog that remembers being beaten and flinches even from kindness. The stone beasts lining the walls — bears the size of granaries, stags with antlers like winter branches, wolves carved so finely you could count their teeth — all of them faced inward, toward the heart of the court, and none of them had moved in three hundred years. Dust hung on them like snow that had forgotten how to melt.

Kess perched on my shoulder and did not speak. She'd barely spoken since the drowned tunnels. Even ravens know when to hold their tongue.

The ember rode in its iron lantern at my chest, and it was dying. I could feel it the way you feel a fever breaking — not gone, but going. Every mile since the shrine had cost it something. When we'd entered Thornfell it burned white; by the cordon it was gold; now, deep beneath the peaks where no sun had ever reached, it was the color of old coals in a banked hearth, and I found myself rationing my hope the way I used to ration contraband brandy.

Sera walked beside me with her bow strung and her jaw set, and if she was afraid she hid it better than I hid anything, which is to say completely and also not at all.

"It's quiet," she said.

"Too quiet," I agreed, because someone had to say it, and if neither of us said it we'd both be thinking it, which is worse.

Then the voice began.

It came out of the floor. Out of the walls. Out of the mouths of every stone beast at once, which should have been terrifying and was, but mostly it was *sad* — a chorus speaking in unison with the weariness of something that has waited so long to be asked a question that it has forgotten what answers sound like.

**WHO CARRIES THE EMBER?**

"I do," I said. My voice cracked on the second word. Very heroic. The smuggler who once talked down a bear over a disputed salmon would have been embarrassed for me.

**AND WHAT DO YOU BRING?**

"A piece of the old fire," I said. "From the broken stone at the shrine. And a charge: carry it home before the last moon of winter."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on your eardrums like deep water. Then:

**THE FIRE IS HOME, LITTLE SPEAKER. IT NEVER LEFT. ONLY THE WORDS LEFT. WORDS ARE CHEAP. FIRE IS NOT.**

I looked at Sera. She looked at me. Neither of us understood, and both of us pretended we did, which is the foundation of most partnerships.

"What does it mean?" she finally asked, breaking first. "Words are cheap?"

I think — I think it meant this. That the covenant was never really the stone, or the ember, or the magic hoarded up in crown vaults. It was the *speaking*. Two realms agreeing, out loud, generation after generation, to belong to each other. And when the king demanded the beasts kneel instead of converse, the words stopped, and everything built on them — the gift, the pact, the mountain's own living warmth — went out like a lamp in wind.

But the ember still needed something. The voice made that clear enough.

**A COVENANT IS SEALED IN BLOOD OR IN GIFT. THE KINGS CHOSE BLOOD, AND LOOK WHAT IT BOUGHT THEM. THERE REMAINS ONE PRICE THAT POWER CANNOT PAY, BECAUSE POWER WILL NOT PAY IT. A LIFE. FREELY GIVEN. BY ONE WHO CARRIES BOTH WORLDS AND BELONGS TO NEITHER.**

The stone beasts' mouths closed. The voice withdrew into the rock like water into sand, and left us standing in a cavern full of dust and dead grandeur and one small, guttering coal.

"Well," said Sera.

"Well," I said.

"That's you," she said. "That's obviously you."

And here is where I have to be honest, because I promised myself I would be, somewhere around the moment a royal huntress decided not to put an arrow through my throat at a cliff's edge. I had known. Since the shrine, some part of me had known the charge came with a bill attached. Charges always do. Nobody hands a smuggler something precious without expecting payment, and the mountain had been a better business partner than any man I'd ever dealt with — patient, fair, and absolutely merciless about debts.

I just hadn't expected her to say it out loud first.

"Sera—"

"Don't." Her hand came up. "Don't do the thing where you argue beautifully and wear me down. You're good at that. I've watched you talk a wolf-pack out of eating us. I'm not a wolf-pack."

"You're offering yourself," I said. "You don't carry both worlds. You carry one, and a grudge against the other half of it."

"I carry a brother who might still be alive because the inquisitor wanted leverage," she said. "I carry a crown I've already betrayed. I carry—" her voice did something complicated, "—I carried orders to kill you, and I didn't, and everything since has been me trying to find out what I am instead. Maybe this is it. Maybe this is the answer. A life freely given. Mine's as free as anyone's."

"It isn't," I said. "That's the whole point. Freely given means given *whole* — not spent to settle accounts. Not traded against your brother, or your guilt, or your crown. If you step into that flame to pay off a debt, it isn't a gift. It's just another transaction. And transactions are exactly what broke this place."

She stared at me. I stared back. And underneath all the words, both of us knew we were really arguing about who got to be brave, which is the stupidest argument two people can have, and also the only one worth having.

Then the floor shuddered.

Dust fell from the vaulted dark above in long grey sheets. Somewhere behind us, back up through the drowned tunnels, something vast had noticed where we were. The shadow-thing's servants — the maddened packs, the hollow-eyed things that had hunted us since Teeth-in-the-Dark — they had followed the ember's dimming light the way carrion birds follow a wound. We'd bought hours. We'd thought we'd bought more.

Kess lifted off my shoulder and flew a slow circuit of the cavern, screaming warnings in raven, and I understood maybe half of them, and the half I understood was: *they are coming through the water-gate.*

"They're coming through the water-gate," I translated, helpfully.

"How long?" Sera asked, nocking an arrow.

"Minutes. Maybe fewer."

We looked at each other again, and this time there was nothing clever left to say. The ember sat in its lantern, guttering. The price sat in the voice's words, unclaimed. The enemy sat at our backs, arriving. Three problems, one solution, and the solution was me, and I had run out of ways to pretend otherwise.

"Sera," I said. "Get back. Both of you — get back to the far wall."

"Wren—"

"If I'm wrong, you'll need to tell people what happened here, and you can't do that from inside a fire." I smiled, because I have always smiled at bad moments; it's a flaw. "Besides. Someone has to survive to blackmail the crown properly. You're much better at threats than I am. I always lead with the charm and forget the knife."

She laughed — actually laughed, once, sharp and awful — and then took Kess and retreated along the wall, arrow drawn, facing outward toward the tunnels while I turned inward toward the flame.

I opened the lantern.

The ember was smaller than I remembered. Smaller than a fist. Smaller than a heart. It hissed faintly in the cold air of the court, and it looked, in that moment, less like a great fire and more like the last coal in a hearth whose family had gone to bed — waiting, hoping, nearly hopeless, for someone to breathe on it.

I thought about my grandmother teaching me wolf-speech in the barn with the doors barred, telling me never, never to let anyone hear. I thought about the stag in Chapter One, dying on the smugglers' path, spending its last breath to warn a stranger. I thought about the child in the burned barn, whom I had failed. I thought about a huntress who chose a stranger over her crown, and a raven who chose me over every instinct her species ever had.

I thought: *this is what carrying both worlds means.* Not belonging to either. Belonging to the space between, and paying for the privilege.

**IT MUST BE FREELY GIVEN,** the voice said, one last time, gentle as frost forming. **NO ONE WILL FORCE YOU. NO ONE CAN.**

"I know," I said. "That's rather the point."

I stepped forward. Behind me, Sera shouted my name — I heard it, I'll always have heard it, that shout is stitched somewhere into whatever I am now — and ahead of me the stone beasts turned their heads, three hundred years of stillness ending in a single grinding motion, every carved eye in the Salt Court turning to watch one small smuggler walk toward a coal.

I lifted the ember in both hands.

It was warm. It was almost out. And it weighed nothing at all, which is the thing nobody tells you about sacrifices — everyone expects them to be heavy, and they aren't. They're light. They're the lightest thing you'll ever carry, right up until the moment you set them down.

I breathed on the coal, the way you'd coax a hearth back to life, and stepped into the flame.

* * *

Sera Dain had killed men before. She wanted to be clear with herself about that, standing at the far wall with an arrow nocked and tears freezing on her face, because clarity was the discipline she had left when everything else was stripped away. She had killed men, and she had disobeyed a direct order, and she had lied to an inquisitor, and none of it had hurt the way watching Wren Villalobos open his hands did.

He didn't hesitate. That was the unbearable part. She had rehearsed hesitation for him — she'd imagined arguing, wrestling the lantern away, tying him to a pillar if it came to it. Instead he'd made one joke about blackmail, kissed Kess's feathers with two fingers like a fool blessing a fool, and walked.

The flame took him the way night takes a candle. No scream. A light that swelled and swallowed and then—

Then the world changed key.

Sera felt it in her teeth before her eyes caught up. The dust on three hundred years of stone beasts shivered and slid away. The carved wolves flexed. The carved stags stamped. And the voice — the sad, weary chorus that had named the price — split into ten thousand voices, every beast in the court waking at once, and the sound was not triumph. It was grief and relief tangled together, the sound of a mountain remembering its own name.

The shadow-thing's servants poured through the water-gate into a court that was no longer sleeping, and the court looked at them, and the servants *stopped*. Some fled. Some simply lay down, hollow-eyed things surrendering to something older than the hunger that had ridden them. The shadow itself recoiled from the relit covenant like smoke from rain — Sera saw it happen at the tunnel mouth, a darkness with edges, folding inward, thinning, coming apart into nothing that had ever truly been alive.

And in the heart of the flame, where a smuggler had stood, there was — something else. Something standing. Shaped like Wren and not like Wren, lit from within, speaking in a voice that was somehow raven and wolf and human and stone all at once, saying words Sera couldn't translate but understood anyway, the way you understand a lullaby in a language you don't know.

He turned. He looked at her across the cooling fire, and his eyes were the same eyes, and they were not the same eyes at all.

"Sera," he said — and the name came out wrong, gentler, layered, as though he were borrowing it. "I believe I owe you a blackmail threat."

The stone beasts knelt. All of them. To her, to Kess, to the waking mountain — and beyond the cavern walls, far above, she could feel through the rock the wild herds turning homeward, the wolves calling in voices that carried meaning again, the whole frozen hierarchy of the broken world cracking like river ice in spring.

Her brother. The crown. Everything still waited above ground, unresolved, the king in his hall with his hoarded magic and his inquisitor and his lies.

Wren-not-Wren smiled at her, and held out a hand that had walked through fire.

"We should go up," he said. "They'll want to hear what the mountain has to say."
