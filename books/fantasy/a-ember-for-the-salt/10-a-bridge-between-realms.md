## 10. A Bridge Between Realms

I remember the moment of stepping into the flame the way you remember a door — not the room beyond it, but the threshold itself. The heat did not burn. That was the first surprise. The second was that I could still think, which I had rather counted on not being able to do.

The ember took my name first. *Wren Villalobos* peeled away like bark off a wet branch, and underneath it there was — nothing. A shape where a boy had been. Then it took the hiding, all those years of folding myself small in valley towns, of bargaining in whispers under my breath so no one would hear the wolf-words in my mouth. It took the secret and it did not destroy it. It turned it inside out. Every word I had ever swallowed came back up through me at once, in every voice I had ever borrowed: raven, stag, wolf, and something older that lived in the stone itself and had no name I knew until that instant, when the flame gave me one.

*Bridge,* it said. Not a name like Wren. A name like a job description. Like being told, mid-drowning, that you are actually a boat.

The shadow-thing hit the Salt Court at the same moment the flame finished with me. I felt it arrive the way you feel a storm front — pressure, cold, a wrongness in the teeth. It had worn the shapes of beasts, maddened ones, its servants' eyes gone black as tar pits. And then the covenant caught fire along every vein of the mountain at once, and I stood in the middle of it, and I was the middle of it, and the shadow-thing simply — came apart. No battle. No screaming. It had been feeding on the broken pact the way mold feeds on damp, and we had, all of us together, finally fixed the leak. It thinned, unraveled, went to smoke, and the smoke went out through the high chimneys of the cavern, and the stone beasts of the Salt Court opened their eyes.

All of them. At once. After however many generations of silence — the old voice of the court had said *since the breaking*, and I had stopped asking how long, because the answer was a number that made my bones ache — after all of it, they woke like a field of flowers after rain. Not gradually. All at once, with a sound like ten thousand joints unstiffening, and then the court was full of voices again, and I understood every single one, and every single one of them was saying some version of *at last* or *who are you* or, from one very old stone bear near the far wall, *I dreamed of honey. Is there still honey?*

There was not still honey. There were more pressing matters. But I liked him immediately.

Sera found me on my knees at the heart of the flame, which had gone out, which had become simply warm stone, and she was looking at me like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't enjoy. Because I was alive. She had watched me step in. We had both understood the price; the court's voice had named it plainly enough, and I had refused her offer with what I felt at the time was considerable eloquence, and she had called me a self-righteous smuggler's whelp even as she cried, and then I had walked into the fire, and now here I was, kneeling, smoking slightly, alive.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said.

"I have excellent news for you, then," I said, "about your report."

She laughed. It cracked in the middle, the way laughter does when it's carrying too much, and then she sat down next to me on the warm stone, and Kess landed on her shoulder, which surprised us both, because Kess had never once sat on Sera's shoulder before, and if that isn't an endorsement I don't know what is.

"I don't understand what happened," she said.

"Neither do I." That was true, and it stayed true for a long time, and I want to be honest about that. Everyone afterward — the wolves, the stone elders, eventually even the crown's own scholars — wanted a tidy account. Here is what I had: I had offered everything, and the covenant had taken the offering and spent it differently than any of us expected. A life freely given, yes. But the pact had never wanted a death. The kings who broke it thought power was something you hoarded, and the shadow-thing grew fat on exactly that thinking, and the original covenant — the one my ancestors spoke for, the one in the vision the broken shrine-stone gave me — had always been a bridge, not a wall, not a throne. So when I gave myself wholly, the covenant made me into what it had always needed: someone who belonged to neither realm and therefore to both. It didn't kill me because killing me wasn't the point. Making me *usable* was.

I am aware this sounds like I'm describing my own salvation as a bureaucratic convenience. Sit with that. I did, for several days.

* * *

From Sera's side of things, and she told me this later, slowly, over the course of the climb out, because she was not a woman who gave accounts easily:

She had watched him walk into the flame and had begun composing his eulogy in her head, because that is what huntresses do instead of screaming. She had gotten as far as *he was a smuggler, and a liar, and the best man I ever failed to arrest* when the light changed. The whole cavern went gold-white, then deep red, then a color she had no word for and resented having to see. The shadow-thing's servants dropped mid-lunge, their black eyes clearing like ice melting from a window, and the beasts that had been trying to kill them two nights ago in the deep forest — the same pack, she realized, scarred faces she remembered — were suddenly just wolves again, confused, hungry, and looking at Wren with an attention that made her skin prickle.

Because they weren't looking at him like a man. They were looking at him like a *door*.

That was the phrase she used. Like a door. Like something you could speak through but not possess, and every beast in that court seemed to know it instantly, the way animals know weather. The wolves pressed close and did not threaten. The stone elders inclined their great heads. Kess — her raven now too, apparently, ownership having dissolved along with so much else — landed on her shoulder and said, in perfectly clear common speech, *he lives, tell them he lives,* which was the moment Sera Dain, royal huntress, eleven years sworn, understood that the world had rearranged itself while she wasn't granted a vote.

Her brother was the other thing. Tomas. Taken in the first sweep, held since autumn in one of the crown's holding camps, and she had carried that weight up the drowned tunnels and through the pack-country and into the dark beneath the mountains without once setting it down, because grief is not something you get to put down just because the walking is hard. When the covenant reignited, she felt it — she swore this, and I believed her, because I'd felt it too — she felt it in her chest like a second heartbeat, brief and enormous, and somewhere above them, in a camp lit by torches, a boy in a cage lifted his head because the birds outside the palisade had all started singing at midnight in voices no bird should own.

She didn't know that yet. She learned it three days later. But the mountain knew it immediately, and the mountain, it turns out, is a gossip.

* * *

The climb out took four days. The wolves escorted us, which meant the wolves trotted ahead of us at a respectful distance and occasionally looked back to check we hadn't died of our own incompetence. The stone elders sent word ahead through channels I was only beginning to learn existed — roots, water, the slow speech of mineral and frost — so that by the time we reached the treeline, the forest already knew. Every stag on the south face was standing at the edge of the clearings watching us come. Ravens in numbers that suggested either a council or a conspiracy followed us from pine to pine, and Kess flew among them translating, or possibly negotiating, or possibly just bragging.

At the treeline, the world was different. I want to be careful with that sentence, because it sounds like poetry and I mean it as reportage. The trees were different. Not healthier, exactly — though they were — but *present*. For my whole life the forest had felt like a room with the furniture covered in sheets. Now the sheets were off. You could feel the attention of the place, benign and vast, the way you can feel a large dog lying quietly across your feet.

And above us, on the ridge, the mountain had sent a delegation. Three stags with winter-heavy antlers, a fox who I suspect was there purely for the drama, and Old Honey-Bear's living cousin, an actual bear, actual size, who sat down on the ridge like a magistrate and looked at the valley below with an expression I can only describe as *we need to talk about the last two hundred years.*

Below us lay Millbrook, and the king's cordon, and the camp where they kept the gifted in cages, and the garrison where Sera's report of my death had been disbelieved by men who had never once in their lives been right about anything.

"They'll come up," Sera said. "When they realize you're alive. They'll send soldiers."

"They'll try," I said.

"You're not worried."

"I'm a bridge, Sera. Bridges don't worry about traffic."

She gave me a look that contained, in equal measure, admiration and the urge to hit me, which I have since learned is simply her resting expression toward me, and I have made peace with it.

* * *

They came up, of course. Two hundred soldiers, an inquisitor — the same one from the border camp, the one who had told us the crown broke the covenant deliberately, who had known about the shadow-thing and let it feed, who had offered each of us our freedom priced against the other's betrayal — and behind them, eventually, because the situation had grown embarrassing enough to require supervision, the king's own envoy.

What they found at the treeline was not a fugitive and a traitor. It was a hillside full of witnesses. Beasts who could testify — actually testify, in words, in common speech, through me — to what the crown had done. Stone elders with two hundred years of memory. A huntress with a sworn oath and a brother in a cage and nothing left to lose, which is the most dangerous material a witness can be made of. And me, standing between the two realms, belonging to neither, which meant that when the inquisitor's men leveled crossbows at us, the wolves did not charge and the soldiers did not shoot, because everyone present understood simultaneously that shooting me would accomplish precisely nothing except making the entire mountain permanently unforgivable.

The negotiations were ugly, protracted, and conducted largely in bad faith on the crown's side, and I will spare you the details except for the terms that mattered: the cages emptied. The sweeps ended. The wild realms recognized as sovereign within their bounds, with the covenant — reforged, witnessed, written into law over the objection of three separate bishops — as the treaty's spine. The inquisitor was recalled to the capital, where I am told he has taken up gardening, bitterly. And Tomas Dain walked out of the holding camp at Millbrook on the fourth day, thin and squinting, met at the gate by his sister, who had ridden down alone, and by a raven who sat on the gatepost and said, *told you.*

He didn't remember being told. He remembered the birds singing at midnight. He remembered lifting his head. He remembered hoping, in the specific way you hope when hope is all they've left you. And then his sister came, and the gates opened, and the birds sang again, and this time it was morning.

* * *

Winter loosened. It does that slowly, in the high country, grudgingly, like a miser counting out coins. By the time the passes cleared, the Salt Court had sent up its formal greeting to the new order of things, delivered through me in a ceremony nobody quite knew the protocol for, least of all me. The stone elders asked questions. The crown's scholars took notes. The wolves ignored everyone equally, which the scholars eventually decided was also a form of answer.

And I learned what I was, day by day, the way you learn a new limb. I could stand in a valley town market and hear the ravens arguing over a dead lamb two ridges away. I could sit with a wolf-pack's elders while they deliberated whether the elk herds could spare a cull, and translate their reasoning to the human hunters whose snares had been cutting the herds the wrong way for generations, and watch both sides flinch, and then, slowly, stop flinching. I belonged nowhere. I had given away the small belonging I'd built — the double life, the careful anonymity, the smuggler's reputation, the name — and what came back was larger and lonelier and mine in a way nothing had ever been mine before.

Some nights it ached. I won't pretend otherwise. There are worse fates than being a bridge, but there are few lonelier ones, and there were evenings that first spring when I stood at the treeline and felt both realms pulling at me like children on opposite ends of a rope, and I missed being merely a man.

But then there were mornings like the one in late April, when a shepherd from Millbrook came to me — came to *me*, openly, in daylight, because the proclamation was ash and the fear was ash and somehow the town had decided that the beast-speaker was theirs now, theirs and the mountain's both — and asked if I could speak to her dog, who had gone silent since the fires two winters ago. I knelt in the road and asked the dog what was wrong, and the dog said, in the flat sad voice of a creature who has been waiting a long time, *nobody asked.* And I told the shepherd what her dog had seen, and why he had stopped barking, and what he needed, and she cried in the road, and the dog leaned against my knee, and I thought: this. This is what the gift was always for. Not power. Not secrets. This — a question nobody asked, finally asked.

* * *

Sera stayed. I want to record that accurately, because it was a choice, and choices deserve recording. She had a pardon waiting in the capital — the envoy had offered it, terms of the treaty, full restoration of rank, a commission, honors. She read the letter twice and burned it in the garrison hearth, and when the officer who brought it asked her what she would do instead, she said, "Live somewhere the orders make sense," and left, and did not look back, which for Sera constituted a farewell address.

She took a cabin at the forest's edge, half a day from Millbrook, on the seam between the realms — because of course she did, because she had become a seam herself, crown-born and mountain-sworn, and neither realm quite knew what to file her under, and she preferred it that way. She hunts now only what needs hunting, by covenant law, with the packs' leave, and the packs give it gladly, because Sera Dain is the only human they have ever known who keeps her word past the point where keeping it costs her.

We did not plan what we became to each other. I want that on the record too, because the ballads — oh, there are ballads already, the mill folk sing them badly — the ballads make it neater than it was. What it was: two people who had each tried, sincerely, to destroy the other, and failed, and then carried each other through the dark under the mountains, and came out the other side owing each other debts that couldn't be paid in any coin either of them recognized. What it became: mornings when she appeared at the treeline with bread and terrible coffee and news from the valley, and evenings when I brought her the mountain's gossip, and a slow accumulation of ordinary hours that neither of us named for the better part of a year, because naming things has consequences and we had both recently seen what happens when you say certain words out loud in front of old powers.

But I will say this, since the chapter demands an ending and honesty demands a confession: on the first warm night of the second spring, at the treeline, with the stags watching from the ridge and the fox presumably taking notes, she said, "You know you don't belong to either of them. Neither do I. Maybe that's the point of us." And I said, "That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," and she said, "It wasn't romantic, it was cartography," and I kissed her anyway, and the ravens, who have opinions about everything, held a brief and inconclusive vote on whether we were disgusting.

* * *

The mountain is awake. I check sometimes, the way you check on a thing you helped save — touch the stones, ask the wind, listen at the roots. The covenant holds. The ember is out; it lives in the pact now, distributed, everywhere, in every spoken bargain between shepherd and flock and hunter and herd. My gift isn't a secret anymore and it isn't a weapon and it isn't even, strictly speaking, mine. It's infrastructure. I am a public works project that occasionally has feelings.

Old Honey-Bear got his honey. A beekeeper from the valley carried a comb up the switchbacks in autumn, specifically for him, having heard about the dream through channels she declined to explain. He ate it in the Salt Court with his paws on the table like a senator, and the stone elders observed solemnly that the first gift freely given across the new covenant had been honey to a bear, and that this was correct and auspicious, and honestly? They're right. Of all the treaties and proclamations and sworn oaths of that first year, the thing that sealed it best was a pot of honey.

Kess nests above my door. She says she stays for the conversation. She is lying; she stays because I am the only person in two realms who takes her arguments seriously, and she knows it, and she is insufferable about it, and I would not trade her for anything.

And on quiet nights, when the wind comes down off the white pass and the whole forest breathes at once, I stand at the treeline where we stood when the soldiers came up and the mountain backed us, and I listen to both worlds talking — really talking, warily, then less warily, year over year — and I think about the boy I was, hiding in plain sight, whispering bargains under his breath, flinching from his own reflection because the reflection knew what he was.

He thought the gift was a thing to survive. It was a thing to give away. That was the whole lesson, and it cost everything I had to learn it, and I would pay it again tomorrow, and the day after, and every day the mountain asks — which, so far, is every day, and the asking sounds, more often than not, like a stone bear wondering aloud whether anyone up there might be thinking about honey.

Someone usually is. Word travels fast, these days, between the worlds.

Faster, lately, than anyone expects. Last week a rider came up from the capital — not a soldier, a scholar, young, nervous, carrying letters sealed with the king's own signet. The crown wants a speaker at court. An embassy of beasts and men, permanent, formal, the covenant written into the succession itself so no future king can break what this one was forced to keep.

They want me to come down and argue for it.

Kess has already packed.
