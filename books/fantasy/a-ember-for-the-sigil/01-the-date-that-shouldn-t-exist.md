## 1. The Date That Shouldn't Exist

The widow Maren Colm came to Wren's workshop at dusk, when the light through the horn-pane windows went the color of weak tea. She carried her grief the way other women in Hollowmere carried market baskets—carefully, close against the body, as though it might spill.

"He was forty-one years," she said, setting a small cloth bundle on his workbench. Inside lay a knucklebone, smooth and yellowed, from the foreleg of the ox her husband had raised from a calf. "Tam said you'd know what to do with it."

Wren did. He always did. Bone carving was the one trade Hollowmere permitted him without suspicion, because everyone in the valley carved something—spoons, yokes, buttons—and no one asked why Wren's carvings were different. No one asked why the dead spoke to him. In a valley warded so thoroughly that magic could not cross its borders, a boy who heard whispers from beyond was either a miracle or a malfunction, and Hollowmere had long ago decided not to decide. They simply brought him their dead and said nothing.

He took up the knucklebone and turned it in the lamplight, reading the grain the way a carpenter reads timber. There would be an oak leaf here—the tree Tam Colm had planted the year of his wedding—and beneath it, small enough that only a weeping eye would find it, the words *rest easy, neighbor*. The dead liked to be spoken to plainly. Grandmother had taught him that, back when she'd been alive to teach anything, back before her deathbed riddles had made him feel like a man trying to solve a lock whose key had been swallowed.

Maren sat by the window with her hands folded, watching him work, and for a while the only sounds were the whisper of the burin and the tick of bone dust settling on the bench.

Then the whispering started.

It came the way it always came—not through the walls or the window but through the bone itself, as if the material remembered everything it had ever been part of. Usually it was Tam Colm's voice, faint and familiar: *tell her the gate latch sticks, tell her I fixed the hinge before I went.* Small messages. Domestic ones. The dead of Hollowmere were a chatty lot, all unfinished errands and unspoken tendernesses, and Wren had spent twenty-three years relaying them like a ferryman who never touched water.

But tonight the voice was not Tam's.

Tonight it came from deeper in the bone, from somewhere older than the ox, older than the valley itself, and it did not ask him to pass along a message about hinges.

*The book has your name.*

Wren's burin slipped. A thin bright line scored across the oak leaf, ruining three hours of patience, and he set the tool down before his hands could shake any worse than they already were.

"Say that again," he murmured, knowing better than to speak aloud what only he could hear. Maren was ten feet away. She saw him pause, saw him press two fingers to the bridge of his nose the way he did when the whispers crowded, and she mercifully mistook it for grief-tiredness.

The voice did not repeat itself. It never did. It gave him instead something worse than repetition: a direction. A pull, faint and cold, like a fisherman's line hooked behind his sternum, drawing toward the east side of the valley. Toward the chapel. Toward the archive beneath the chapel, where Hollowmere kept the records nobody read because everybody believed they already knew their own history.

*The book has your name,* the dead had said. Not *a* name. *Your* name.

"Wren?" Maren asked softly. "Is something wrong?"

"No." He picked the burin back up. "Just a knot in the grain. I'll work around it."

He worked around it for another hour, because Maren needed the hour, needed to sit in a warm room while someone made her husband's absence into a thing she could hold. That was the other half of his trade, the half no one paid for: he was a place where grieving people could be near someone who understood that the dead were not gone, merely quiet. He finished the charm at last—a leaf like a held breath, the words hidden in the vein-work—and pressed it into Maren's hands, and watched her walk home through the blue dark with her bundle clutched to her chest, and only then did he let himself feel how badly his hands wanted to shake.

*The book has your name.*

He told himself it was a trick. Old bones sometimes carried old voices, echoes of echoes, words meant for someone else a hundred years ago. He told himself this all the way down the lane, past the shuttered smithy, past the well with its moss-slick stones, to the chapel where the verger had left a single lamp burning in the porch niche because Wren had once mentioned he preferred coming at night, when no one could see him do what he was about to do.

Which was: go looking for trouble.

* * *

The archive under the chapel was not grand. It was a stone cellar with cedar shelving and a smell of tallow and dust and time, and it held the valley's memory in ledgers and bound volumes that no one had opened since the last verger died. Wren knew the verger's old habit of leaving the door unbolted—he'd learned it young, sneaking down here as a boy to escape his grandmother's scolding, back when the archive had seemed like a cave full of sleeping dragons rather than what it actually was: a room where a whole valley had agreed to forget things on purpose.

He lit the lamp he'd brought and stood a moment, letting the flame steady, letting his eyes adjust. Then he began walking the shelves, running his fingers along the spines. Baptismal rolls. Tithe books. A fat volume titled *LAND DISPUTES, RESOLVED AND OTHERWISE*, which sounded like the most honest title in Hollowmere. And there, on the third shelf, wedged upright between a hymnal and a box of candle stubs, a book bound in grey leather gone soft as moth wing, its spine lettered in faded gilt:

*THE HOLLOW COVENANT: BEING A TRUE ACCOUNT OF THE VALLEY'S FOUNDING, WITH CERTAIN PROPHECIES APPERTAINING THERETO.*

Wren pulled it free. The cover fell open in his hands as though the binding had been waiting a century for exactly this grip, and the first page he saw was not the first page of the book.

It was a date.

*The ninth night of the Frostwane Moon,* written in brown-black iron-gall ink, in a hand so fine and certain it might have been engraved. Beneath the date, a line of prophecy in the same hand:

*On this night shall the keeper come to the book, and the book shall open to him, and he shall see himself as the valley has hidden him.*

And beneath that—beneath the date, beneath the prophecy, in a space left deliberately blank by whatever careful hand had written the rest—

A sketch.

Charcoal, smudged at the edges, fixed with some wash that had kept it from fading. A face. Young, sharp-boned, heavy-browed, with a jaw that shadowed deep in the cheek hollows and eyes that the artist had rendered as two dark smears, as though the person who drew it had known the eyes were the point and could not risk getting them wrong.

Wren stared at it the way a man stares at his own reflection when the reflection is doing something he isn't.

Because it was his face. Not a likeness. Not a family resemblance, not a cousin, not a type. It was *his* face—the slight asymmetry of his nose, broken once when he was nine and never set quite true; the scar through his left eyebrow from a fall off the millrace wall; the way his hairline dipped lower over the right ear. Details no artist could guess. Details only someone who had *seen* him could have drawn.

Except the sketch was a hundred years old. The ink said so. The hand that drew it had been dust for longer than Wren's grandmother had been alive.

He turned pages with the care of a man defusing something. The volume was a founding chronicle, dense and dry, all about boundary stones and water rights and the first winter, and threaded through it, at intervals, prophecies—dated, specific, unnervingly precise. One described a fire in the mill district, dated to a night sixty years past; the mill had burned, the verger's marginalia confirmed, on exactly that night. Another named a flood. Another named a death. Each prophecy sat beside its date like a promise beside a receipt, and every receipt had been honored.

Every one except the last.

The last entry was tonight's. Tonight's was the only one still unproven, the only one addressed to a living reader, the only one with a face drawn in the margin—and the face was his.

His lamp guttered. His breath went shallow. Somewhere above him, through the ceiling stones, the chapel bell hung silent in its frame, and Wren found himself listening past it, listening the way he listened to bone, straining for the dead who had sent him here. They were quieter now. Almost absent. As though even they weren't sure what happened next.

He found the passage that explained the sketch, near the end of the chronicle, in a section the original scribe had titled *ON THE HIDING OF THE HEIR*. He read it twice, three times, mouthing the archaic phrasing until it gave way to meaning, and with each reading the floor of his life tilted further beneath him.

*A child shall be born to the covenant-line, marked to hear the dead. The valley shall take his name and give him silence in exchange, that the wards may hold. He shall live unseen, unknowing, until the night appointed, when the book shall show him his own face and he must choose.*

*Choose,* the scribe had written, and then, in smaller letters, cramped into the margin as though the thought had frightened even the pen that wrote it: *choose what?*

Wren closed the book. Held it against his chest. Stood alone in a stone cellar under a chapel in a valley that had been his entire world, and felt the carefully invisible life he had built crack straight down the middle like bad ice.

He had always known he was strange. He had made a craft of strangeness, a livelihood of it, a quiet dignity of it. He had thought the whispers were a gift the valley tolerated. He had thought his grandmother's riddles were an old woman's games. He had thought he was Wren Underhill, carver, orphan, neighbor, and that this was the whole of it.

Now a dead man's handwriting told him he was a hiding place. That his name—the name he answered to, the name carved nowhere because he'd assumed orphans went unrecorded—had been taken from him before he could speak, fed into the wards like coal into a furnace, and that everything he loved about Hollowmere, every kindness ever shown him, had grown in soil fertilized by his own stolen self.

The lamp flickered again. This time it didn't recover properly; the flame shrank to a blue bead and the cellar dimmed around him, and in that dimness Wren heard the dead return—not the chatty domestic dead of the valley, but something else, something at the edge of hearing, many voices braided into one low hum, like wind through a keyhole.

They were not giving him messages anymore.

They were counting down.

He climbed the stairs with the book under his arm, because leaving it felt impossible and taking it felt like theft and he did both anyway, choosing theft, choosing the book, choosing to know. At the top of the stairs he paused in the chapel porch, under the lamp the verger had left burning, and looked out across the sleeping valley—at the dark fields, the shuttered houses, the ward-stones standing sentinel along the ridgeline like teeth—and for the first time in twenty-three years, Hollowmere did not look like home.

It looked like a cage with the door beginning to swing.

Behind him, in the cellar he had just left, the lamp he'd forgotten to snuff went out on its own.

And in the dark it left behind, very softly, the dead finished what they'd started saying in the ox bone:

*Tomorrow, keeper. Tomorrow they come.*
