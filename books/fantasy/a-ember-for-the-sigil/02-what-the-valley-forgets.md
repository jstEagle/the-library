## 2. What the Valley Forgets

The archive beneath the chapel smelled of tallow and cold stone, of paper that had given up pretending to be alive. Wren had been down here a hundred times—fetching candle-boxes for Father Aldous, hunting a hymnal that had wandered off—but never past the second shelf, never into the locked cabinet at the back where the founding records slept under a wool blanket of dust so thick it looked like snowfall.

He had not come to read history. He had come because he could not stop shaking.

Last night, in the hour when the house went quiet and the coals settled low, the dead had spoken again. They always spoke—he had learned to live with them the way one lives with a creaking stair or a drafty window, half-heard, half-ignored. But last night they had not murmured. Last night they had *insisted*, a dozen voices braided into one thin, urgent thread: *the book has your name. The book has your name. Look.*

So he had looked. And what he'd found was worse than anything he'd imagined: a prophecy volume older than the chapel itself, its vellum gone the color of weak tea, and there on the page—inked by a hand dead a century before his birth—tonight's date. Beside it, a sketch. A young man with a carver's calluses and eyes too large for his face.

His own face.

He had sat up all night with the book open on his chest like a stone, afraid to close it, afraid not to. When dawn finally bled through the shutters he had hidden it again, told himself it was coincidence, told himself harder. Then he had walked to the chapel anyway, because the dead do not let you rest once they've decided you should know something.

* * *

Father Aldous was at morning prayer, so Wren let himself into the archive alone. The lock on the cabinet was old brass, older than sin, but the key hung on a nail beside it—Hollowmere trusted everyone, because Hollowmere had nothing worth stealing. Or so Wren had believed for twenty-three years.

He pulled the records out one by one and laid them on the reading table: the land grant, the warding charter, a ledger of names, a bundle of letters tied with string gone brittle as straw. He started with the charter because it was thinnest, because starting small felt safer.

It was not safe.

*On this day,* the first line read, in a hand as neat as stitching, *the undersigned do covenant to raise about the valley of Hollowmere a veil, that no working of the outer realm may enter herein, nor any soul depart beyond the bounds of the wards.*

Wren read it twice. Three times. His lips moved without sound.

*No soul depart.* Not no magic in. No soul *out*.

He had grown up on the story every child in Hollowmere grew up on: the valley was warded against sorcery, blessedly dull, safely gray, because the founders had loved their children too much to let the wild magics touch them. A shield. A blessing. The reason nothing ever happened here, and why that was good.

But shields did not keep things *in*. Shields had no need of clauses about souls.

His hands were steady—he was a carver; his hands were always steady—and somehow that made it worse, watching them turn the pages with the same calm they used on bone, while everything behind his ribs came loose.

* * *

The ledger came next. It was a census, or something shaped like one: every soul registered in Hollowmere since the founding, columns of names, dates of arrival, dates of death. And beside each death-date, in a column someone had tried to scrape away and failed, a single word repeated down the years like a heartbeat:

*Retained. Retained. Retained.*

Not *passed*. Not *departed*. *Retained*.

Wren's mouth went dry. He thought of the voices. Of course he thought of the voices—the whisperers in the hedgerows, the hummer in the well, the old man who sang harvest songs through the cracks in the meetinghouse wall. All his life he had assumed they were echoes drifting in from somewhere else, from the realm beyond the wards, faint as light through heavy cloth. That was the story he'd built to hold his gift: the valley kept magic out, but sound slipped through, and Wren's ears were simply strange enough to catch it.

But if no soul departed—if the wards were not a wall but a lid—

Then the dead of Hollowmere had never left.

They were not visitors. They were prisoners. Every voice he had ever heard, every murmur and song and warning, belonged to someone who had died inside the valley and *stayed*, snagged on the wards like burrs on wool, severed from whatever waited beyond, unable to move on, unable even to properly haunt. Just—held. Thinned. Echoes of themselves, pressed flat between the living world and a door that would not open.

And he was the one who heard them. Which meant he was the one they could reach. Which meant—

*The book has your name.*

He set the ledger down and did not pick it up again for a long moment. He stood in the cold air of the archive and felt the floor tilt, gently, the way a boat tilts when you realize the water is deeper than you thought. Not drowning. Just—depth. All around him, everywhere, all his life, depth he had never suspected.

* * *

The letters were the worst. Letters always are; ledgers tell you what happened, letters tell you who it happened *to*.

There were eleven of them, tied in their rotten string, written across three generations by a woman named Maren Cole, who signed herself *warden of the veil* and whose handwriting deteriorated over the decades from confident loops to a cramped, frightened scrawl. Wren read them in order, kneeling on the cold flagstones because standing suddenly felt presumptuous.

The early letters were administrative. Repairs to the northern anchor stone. A dispute over tithes. Tedium.

The middle ones changed.

*I have begun to hear them,* Maren wrote, in the year the wards turned forty. *At the stones, mostly. Voices with no bodies. I told myself it was wind in the hollows. But they say names. Names of the buried. Names I entered in the ledger myself.*

The next letter, two years later: *I petitioned the other signatories to open the veil, even a crack, even an hour, that the retained might go free. They refused. They said the covenant must stand whole or fail whole, and that a failing covenant would take the valley down with it. They said the dead are at peace. I stood in the burying ground all night and listened to them scream, and I do not call that peace.*

Wren's throat closed.

The last letter was short. The handwriting had gone to threads.

*I was wrong to sign. I have spent forty years telling myself we built a shelter. We built a jar. God forgive us, we built a jar, and we put our own dead in it, and we called it love because the alternative was to look at what we'd done. If anyone reads this: the veil does not protect the living. It feeds on the dead. Find the heir. The covenant can be reforged, but only by one who carries both sides of it. I did not understand what that meant until it was too late to matter.*

*Find the heir.*

Wren knelt there a long time. The chapel bell rang the hour above him and he flinched as though struck.

* * *

He walked home the long way, along the river path, because he could not bear to pass the burying ground. Not yet. Not today. Tomorrow he would go and listen properly, listen the way he'd never dared, knowing now what he was listening *to*. Today he needed sky.

The valley lay green and gold in the late light, barley moving in waves, smoke rising straight from chimneys, everything exactly as it had always been—and that was the vertigo of it. Nothing had changed. Everything had changed. Hollowmere looked precisely the way it had looked yesterday, and yesterday he had thought it was home, and today he knew it was a jar, and the terrible thing was that it was still home, still beautiful, still the only place his hands knew how to be. You cannot unlearn the shape of your cage and remain comfortable inside it. But where else was there? The wards had been his whole sky.

His grandmother's voice rose in him unbidden—Maren's riddles, passed down through his mother to Nan Underhill, who had fed them to Wren across a hundred winter evenings by the fire, laughing at her own nonsense:

*The valley keeps what it loves, and loves what it keeps.*
*A door with no handle is still a door.*
*When the bones speak clear, the walls are near their end.*
*You'll know the keeper by the listening.*

He had thought they were old-woman whimsies. Firelight games. He had carved little jokes of them into spoon handles and forgotten them.

Now they landed one by one, like stones dropped into still water, each ring widening.

*Keeps what it loves.* The ledger. *Retained.*

*A door with no handle.* The wards. Sealed from within, sealed from without, opening for no one.

*When the bones speak clear.* He was the bones-speaker. He had always been the bones-speaker. And if clarity meant the walls were failing—

*You'll know the keeper by the listening.*

Keeper. Not hearer. Not listener. *Keeper.* As if the voices weren't an affliction he suffered but a charge he held. As if someone, a century ago, had known a child would be born in this valley with ears that reached through the veil—and had left instructions for him in the only language guaranteed to survive: a grandmother's bedtime riddles, worn smooth by repetition, dismissed by everyone, including him.

Nan had died holding his hand. Her last words had been almost too soft to catch: *"Don't mend the quiet, love. Mend the breaking."*

He had thought she meant a chair. She had meant everything.

* * *

Dusk came down blue and slow. Wren sat at his workbench with a blank piece of sheep bone in his hands and could not carve. The knife stayed lifted, hovering, useless. For the first time in his life the work refused him—not his skill, but his attention; his mind kept sliding off the bone and back to the archive, back to Maren Cole's failing hand, back to the word *retained* marching down the ledger like soldiers.

The dead noticed. They always noticed when he faltered; his silence was their silence too.

*You read it,* they whispered, closer than usual, clearer than usual, urgent. *You read what she wrote.*

*I read it,* Wren said aloud, to the empty room, because saying it out loud was the only way to make it real enough to survive.

*Then you know what comes,* they said. And then, softer, sadder, in a voice he recognized—one of the harvest singers, the old man from the meetinghouse wall, dead sixty years and still humming—*and you know what you are.*

He set the knife down. He lit a candle. He took out the prophecy book from where he'd hidden it under his spare shirts, and opened it to the page with his face on it, and read the lines beneath the sketch all the way through for the first time, slowly, letting each one strike:

*When the veil grows thin and the retained grow loud, the heir will rise who hears both sides. The Court of Ash will come for him wearing borrowed faces. Let him not trade his name for peace, for his name is not his own to sell—it is the valley's, and the valley's is his. On the night the stars mark [the date], the door shall knock.*

Tonight was the date.

The candle guttered in a draft that had no source. Outside, far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the wards, something knocked—once, twice, patient as tide against a door that had been shut for a hundred years.

The dead in the room with him went very still.

*That's not one of ours,* the old man whispered. *That's not one of ours, keeper. That's come from outside.*

Wren blew out the candle and stood in the dark, listening to the knocking, and understood that whatever came through that door next, his invisible life was over.
