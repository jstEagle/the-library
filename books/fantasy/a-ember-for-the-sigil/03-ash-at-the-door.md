## 3. Ash at the Door

The knocking did not come again.

That was somehow worse. Wren lay awake in his narrow bed above the workshop, staring at the ceiling beams, listening for a sound that refused to repeat itself. The dead had dispersed at dawn the way they always did, thinning back into the walls of Hollowmere like breath fading from a mirror, but their unease stayed with him, a cold weight behind his sternum.

He went through the motions of his morning because motions were all he had. He banked the coals. He fed the fire. He took down the half-finished memorial charm for Widow Fairlight—rowan wood this time, though she'd asked for bone, because her husband's ghost had asked *him*, quietly, for something living—and set it on the bench beside the window.

The knife hovered over the first groove and did not move.

Outside, the lane was waking. Old Marta sweeping her step with the same three strokes she'd used for forty years. The miller's boy hauling pails. Smoke rising straight from every chimney into windless air. Hollowmere doing what Hollowmere had always done: nothing. Everything here was nothing, and that was precisely how he had liked it.

*Except you didn't,* said a voice that might have been his own or might have been the dead man's. *You carved doors for souls to pass through. You don't build doors for a house you love.*

He made the first cut anyway. And the second. His hands knew their work even when his mind was elsewhere, and by midmorning there was a charm taking shape under them: a gate, a road, a lantern. Safe passage. That was all a memorial ever said, when you boiled it down. *You went somewhere. We hope it was better than here.*

He was sanding the grain smooth when he heard the footsteps on the lane—the wrong ones.

Not Marta's shuffle, not the boy's clatter. These were measured, unhurried, the walk of someone counting the houses as they passed. Wren's hands stopped. In the corner of the room, where the morning light pooled thinnest, one of the dead—a girl who had died before his grandmother was born and never told anyone her name—rose up out of the floorboards like frost forming on glass.

*Don't answer it,* she said.

Nobody knocked. But the latch lifted anyway, and Tam Broadhurst stood in the doorway of Wren Underhill's shop.

Tam, who had broken his ankle falling off the granary roof eleven years ago and let Wren splint it because Hollowmere had no healer worth the name. Tam, who traded him mutton bones for charms and complained about the weather the way other men complained about their wives. Tam stood in the doorway smiling, and everything about the smile was correct, and nothing about the eyes above it was Tam's at all.

They were gray. Not the gray of age or weather—the flat, ash-gray of a fire burned down to nothing, and in the center of each iris a fleck of ember-red that pulsed very slightly, like something breathing.

"Afternoon, carver," the thing wearing Tam said. Its voice was Tam's voice, pitched a half-tone too smooth, like a copy taken from a copy. "Mind if I come in? Cold out."

It wasn't cold out. The morning had been mild, still, almost warm for the season. Wren did not move from behind his bench. Between them, on the scarred wood, the bone knife lay exactly where his hand could reach it, and the thing's eyes flicked down to it and back up, and its smile widened by a fraction of a tooth.

"Suit yourself," it said. "I'll stand."

*That's not Tam,* whispered the nameless girl, pressing close against Wren's shoulder. Her presence felt like standing in a doorway in winter. *Tam's been dead two days. Don't you remember? Fever. You carved his charm Tuesday.*

And he did remember, with a lurch like a stair missing in the dark—he'd carved it, hadn't he? A small one, quick work, and Tam's widow had wept on his step. Two days. He had known Tam Broadhurst for thirty years and watched him buried two days ago, and he had looked at the thing in the doorway and thought only *Tam, wrong, wrong* —because grief had blunted the sharper thought.

"You've been reading things you shouldn't," the emissary said conversationally, strolling along the wall, trailing gray fingers across the hanging charms without touching them. Each one it passed gave a small shudder, like a dog flinching from a hand. "Old books. Dead men's papers. That's not healthy, carver. That's not *restful.*"

"Who are you?" Wren kept his voice level. It cost him more than he showed.

"A friend. An advocate. Call me what you called the knocking last night, if you like." The ember-flecks pulsed. "I've come with an offer. The Court extends peace—real peace, not the paper kind your founders signed and then hid from—to any soul within these walls who wants it. All you have to do is give up what isn't yours."

"It is mine." The words came out before he could weigh them.

"Is it?" The emissary tilted Tam's head, birdlike, curious. "A gift, freely given, leaves no ache. You hear them day and night, every trapped whisper in this valley, and you call it gift? We know what you are, Wren Underhill. What you're *for*. Surrender it. Let us carry it home to the Court, and you'll sleep. Oh, you'll sleep like the dead—" the smile again, savoring its own joke "—and Hollowmere can go on being nothing, forever. Isn't that what you wanted? To be invisible?"

The silence stretched. Outside, Marta's broom scraped. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped, as if embarrassed.

"I keep them," Wren said quietly. "The whispers. Someone has to."

"They're *echoes.* Broken things. Severs, we call them—souls cut off at the ward-line, half-crossed, half-held. Your valley is full of them, carver. Your valley was built out of them." The emissary leaned forward over the bench, and up close Wren could smell it—not Tam's sheep-and-tobacco smell but something like a cold hearth, like ash stirred after rain. "The Court only asks for the tool. Keep your memories, your mother's face, whatever else you imagine you are. Hand over the hearing, and live out your little life in peace until you die like a man instead of leaking out of the world like the rest of this valley's dead."

Wren looked at the bone knife. He looked at the rowan charm between them—the gate, the road, the lantern, safe passage—and found, to his own mild surprise, that his fear had gone quiet. Not gone. Just settled, the way water settles when it freezes into something you can stand on.

"No," he said.

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

"No?" The emissary's voice lost its smoothness for just a moment, showing rough edges beneath.

"You heard me. I carve doors for souls to pass through. That's the work. It was the work before I knew whose work it was, and it'll be the work after you leave." He picked up the rowan charm and turned it in his hands, once, deliberately. "Tell your Court the keeper says no. Tell them the covenant isn't theirs to hold broken anymore."

For a long moment the thing wearing his neighbor's face simply looked at him, and the ember-flecks in its eyes flared, dimmed, flared—calculating, he realized. Weighing him. Then it straightened, smoothed Tam's coat with gray fingers, and walked to the door.

"The wards are old," it said from the threshold, without turning around. "Older than your founders knew, and thinner than they dared write down. When they go—and they will go, carver, soon, and messily—you'll wish you'd sold your hearing while it still had a buyer."

The door closed. Through the window Wren watched it walk down the lane at that measured pace, past Marta, past the miller's boy, and neither of them so much as glanced at it. As far as Hollowmere was concerned, Tam Broadhurst had simply come calling.

But Tam Broadhurst was two days buried, and the grave, when Wren checked at dusk, had not been disturbed. Whatever had worn his neighbor's face had needed no body to wear it. It had simply reached through the ward-line and put on the shape of a dead man like a borrowed coat.

*

He did not sleep. He sat up with every lamp in the workshop burning and all his charms laid out around him in a ring, which was superstition and he knew it, and he listened to the valley breathe.

Past midnight, the knocking started again.

Not at his door. At the edges—far off, at the rim of hearing, at the rim of the wards themselves, all the way around the valley at once. Knocking like tide. Knocking like knuckles on a coffin lid from the inside. And underneath it, fainter and fainter, a sound Wren had lived with all his life and never named: the hum of the wards, the deep low note that sat under everything in Hollowmere like a held breath.

The note cracked.

It was not loud. It was worse than loud—it was *thin*, a hairline fracture running through a sound that had been seamless for a hundred years, and every ghost in Hollowmere heard it at once. The dead rose up around him in the lamplight, dozens of them, the whole silent congregation he'd grown up among, and for the first time in his life they were all looking in the same direction. Outward. Toward the hills.

Then came the tearing.

He felt it in his teeth, in the roots of them, a wrongness like a chord resolving badly. Somewhere north of the mill, along the ward-line where the standing stones ran up into the black trees, something that had been shut for a century swung open—and through the gap came the first of them.

Not one of the valley's trapped echoes. Those he knew; those drifted and murmured and pressed at him gently, familiar as weather. This was different. This came *through*, crossing some distance that had distance in it, arriving rather than merely persisting.

It was a child.

She stood in the middle of his workshop as if she'd walked there, though no door had opened. Small—seven years old, maybe eight—with mud on her hem and a wilted flower pinned to her dress, the way the outer-realm folk dressed their dead for burial. She was translucent as smoke and she looked up at him with eyes that held the whole cold dark of wherever she'd come from, and she was not afraid.

She was *relieved.*

"Keeper," she said. Her voice was small and clear, a bell rung once. "You're the keeper. Mama said. She said follow the knocking to the keeper, and he'd know my road."

Wren knelt. His knees hit the floorboards and he hardly noticed, because the ring of charms around him had begun to glow, faintly, all of them at once, rowan and birch and bone answering something in the child's presence like iron answering a lodestone. The other dead drew back to make room for her, making way, and the nameless girl whispered at his shoulder:

*The wards are failing. More will come. They'll all come now, keeper—all the ones outside, all the ones the Court has been starving on. They'll come to you.*

The child waited, patient as the dead, holding out one small translucent hand.

Wren looked at her. He looked at his hands, ink-stained and bone-dusted, a carver's hands, a *keeper's* hands. Fear was still there—it would be there tomorrow, and the day after—but it had changed shape overnight, the way ore changes in the fire. It had become something he could carry. Something he could work with.

"Yes," he said to the child, and took her hand, cold as river-water in October. "I know your road. And come morning, I'm going to need help drawing it—because I don't think I'm staying in Hollowmere."

Far to the north, through the crack in the world, he heard hoofbeats on the corpse-road, riding hard toward the valley. Whoever they were—Court hunters, or something else entirely—they would reach the ridgeline by dusk.

And Wren intended to be there first.
