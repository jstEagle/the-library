## 4. Outcasts on the Ridgeline

**Wren**

The wards broke like ice over a pond—slowly at first, in hairline whispers only Wren could hear.

He stood at the eastern fence of his mother's old plot, bone knife idle in his hand, a half-finished charm cupped in his palm. Three nights since the child had called him *keeper* and crossed over. Two since the emissary wearing Mrs. Hallow's face had smiled at him with too many teeth. The valley's silence, the deep magic-dead hush he'd lived inside for twenty-six years without ever knowing it was a silence, was coming apart. He could hear it fraying thread by thread: little pops of pressure, like ears clearing after a long descent.

And underneath that, footsteps. Many of them. Coming down the ridgeline.

Wren did not run. Running, his grandmother had said in one of her riddling moods, is just arriving somewhere else with all your fears packed neatly. Instead he tucked the charm into his coat, took up his walking stick—the good one, hazel-wood, iron-shod—and walked out to meet them.

There were three. They came down the sheep track from Grey Tor in the grey hour before full dark, when the valley always looked like a memory of itself. The tall one in front moved like a blade being carried in a cloth: a woman, sharp-faced, ash-pale hair cropped close, wearing a traveling coat stitched all over with sigils so dense they hurt to look at. Behind her, a smaller figure in layered shawls—a hedge-witch by the smell of her, dried herbs and river clay and something burnt. And last, a mountain of a man carrying a broken ferry-pole across his shoulders like a yoke, who watched the ground and never once looked up.

The tall one stopped ten paces off. Her eyes found Wren's face the way a key finds a lock, and Wren felt it—the horrible rightness of being recognized.

"So it's true," she said. Her voice was low and roughened, an accent from far outside the valley, vowels worn smooth by distance. "The heir exists."

"I'm sorry," Wren said, "I think you've mistaken me for someone important."

"Have I." She pulled something from her coat. A book. Wren's stomach dropped through the frozen ground before she even opened it—he knew that binding, that cracked leather, the sigil pressed in tin foil gone brown with age. A copy. Not his copy, but a sister to it, and on the open page, faint as breath on glass: a sketch of his own face, and beneath it, inked a century past, *the keeper comes in the month of falling leaves.*

"You have that same book," Wren said stupidly.

"We have *every* such book," the woman said. "We burned most of them. This one we kept." She closed it. "My name is Sable Vex. Formerly of the Guild of the Sigil-Sworn, formerly of anything at all. These are Tamsin—" the small witch raised two fingers in a lazy salute "—and Brannoc." The big man didn't move. "He doesn't speak. Don't ask why. It's rude."

"You've come to kill me," Wren said. It wasn't a question. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Perhaps there was only so much dread a body could hold before it ran out of room.

"Kill you?" Sable's mouth twisted—not quite a smile, more like a scar forming. "If the Ashen Court wanted you dead, carver, you'd be ash. They want you *whole*. Which is worse. No—we came to find you first." She looked past him, at the failing wards, at the hairline shimmer along the eastern sky where the veil hung like torn curtain. "And we're a bit late."

"The Court knows," Wren said.

"The Court has known since the night the child got through." Tamsin's voice was surprisingly warm, a hearth-voice, entirely wrong for the words coming out of it. "You let her cross, didn't you? Held the seam open?"

"I didn't hold anything. She just—" Wren stopped. "She called me keeper."

Tamsin and Sable exchanged a glance that carried whole paragraphs.

"Then here's the state of things," Sable said, and folded her arms. "In three days, maybe four, these wards fail completely. When they do, everything the valley has been swallowing for a hundred years comes out at once—and everything outside comes *in*. Including the Ashen Court, who will take you, crack your gift out of you like a walnut, and feed your severed soul to their names. You will wish very hard for death, and you will not get it." She let that sit. "Or. You come with us. Out of the valley, on the corpse-road, to the Sigil's heart, and we see if the prophecy's end can be written differently than the Court intends."

"Why?" Wren asked. "Why would exiles cross the wards hunting a stranger? What do you get?"

Sable was quiet a moment. Wind came down off Grey Tor and moved through the dead bracken, and somewhere behind Wren, faint as a coin dropped in a well, he heard the trapped dead stir.

"Atonement," Sable said at last, flat, like a price quoted reluctantly. "And passage. We can't reach the Sigil without you—the corpse-road opens for the keeper or no one. So it isn't charity, carver. It's a bargain. Do we have one?"

Wren looked back at Hollowmere. Lamplight in the windows. The chapel where he'd found his own face in a dead man's book. Neighbors he'd spent a lifetime being invisible among, kind in the shallow ways people are kind to those they've decided not to see. He thought of the widow's memorial charm still unfinished on his workbench, and understood, with a strange calm, that he would never finish it—that the life he'd carved so carefully around himself was already broken, and all that remained was to decide what to build in its place.

"One condition," he said. "The dead this valley holds. The trapped ones. Whatever happens at the Sigil—they go free. Swear it."

Something flickered in Sable's eyes. Respect, maybe, or recognition.

"Sworn," she said.

**Sable**

Sable had imagined the heir a hundred times on the road across the outer realm, and never once like this.

She had imagined thunder, a warrior, someone marked—eyes like coals, a birthright burning on their skin. Instead she got a bone-carver in a patched coat with sawdust on his sleeves, who bargained for ghosts before he bargained for himself, and who now walked ahead of them through his own darkening village gathering supplies with the brisk unhappiness of a man locking up a house forever.

"Stop hovering," Tamsin murmured at her shoulder. "You're doing the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have the face you make when you've decided not to hope about something. You've been making it at him for an hour."

Sable scowled at the mud. The truth was the boy unsettled her—not because of the prophecy, but because of how ordinary he was. She'd built her whole ruinous life around the guild's doctrine: power pools in the marked, the great, the named. And here was the covenant's heir, kneeling in a stranger's garden to leave a carved token on a doorstep, some small kindness for a neighbor who would wake and never know. That kind of person was either a fraud of historic proportion or exactly what the old books said the covenant needed. She genuinely could not tell which frightened her more.

Brannoc rumbled. One low note, wordless—but Sable had learned his grammar over three hard years, and this note meant *look*.

At the valley's western gate, where the ward-stone stood in its collar of moss, the air had begun to shine. Not the clean shine of healthy magic—the wet, split-mussel shine of something dying. Through the tear, Sable could see the corpse-road unspooling into the outer dark: a pale ribbon stitching hill to hill, dotted with waystation ruins, running west toward the horizon and the shattered thing waiting at its end.

The heir shouldered his pack. He'd brought almost nothing, she noticed. Tools. A book. A bundle of blank bone charms. As if he already knew what mattered.

"Which way do we walk?" he asked.

"West, until the road decides otherwise," Sable said. "Corpse-road rules are simple. Don't step off the stones. Don't answer voices using the names of anyone you loved. And when you meet the tollman—" she glanced at Brannoc "—let Brannoc do the talking."

"He doesn't talk."

"He talks fine," Sable said. "Just not to us."

They passed under the ward-stone together, four shadows in single file, and Sable felt the failing wards brush over her skin like cobwebs, like fingers checking her against a list. For a heartbeat the whole lattice flared—hundred-year-old work, she could feel the craftsmanship even now, and beneath it the wrongness at its core, the stolen name humming in the stone like a fly in amber. *One name,* the guild records had said. *A living name freely surrendered, or taken.* Taken, then. That was the crime. Hers too, in the end; she'd mixed the mortar of her silence into it for years without asking what held the walls up.

Behind them, Hollowmere's lights went on one window at a time, unaware. Ahead, the corpse-road breathed.

"Vex," the heir said suddenly, turning. He'd been quiet a long while, working at something. "That mark on your coat. The broken circle with the bar through it. My grandmother drew it in the margins of her recipes. Over and over, near the end. I thought it was a flour brand."

Sable stopped walking. The road hummed beneath her boots.

"It's not a flour brand," she managed.

"No," he said. "I didn't think so anymore. Not after tonight." And then, unbearably gently, the way you'd speak to a wounded animal: "Whatever it means—you don't have to tell me yet. But you'll tell me before the Sigil. I think I'm going to need to know who's standing next to me when we get there."

No one had spoken to her like that in eleven years. Not accused her, not pleaded, not sentenced—just *asked*, as if her honesty were a thing worth having.

"Before the Sigil," Sable agreed, and heard her own voice come out lower than intended.

They walked. Tamsin began, softly, to sing one of the endless nonsense songs she used to keep despair at bay, and to Sable's astonishment Brannoc's ferry-pole creaked in rhythm, which was his version of keeping time, which was his version of joy. The heir glanced back at them all—witch, mute ferryman, disgraced smith, himself—and something in his face eased, the way a knot eases just before it slips loose.

That was when the lights went up behind them. All of them. Every window in Hollowmere at once, and rising over the eastern ridge, thin and bright and wrong, a column of grey fire.

"They're early," Tamsin said, song dying in her throat.

Sable looked at the heir, who was looking back at his burning valley with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life—grief and fury and, worst of all, resolve.

"Then we run," she said, and the corpse-road opened ahead of them like a throat.
