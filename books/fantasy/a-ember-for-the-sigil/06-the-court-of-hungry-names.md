## 6. The Court of Hungry Names

They took him at the ford, where the corpse-road dipped into gray water and the willows hung like mourners who had forgotten whom they wept for.

Wren never saw the ambush coming. One moment Sable was ahead, testing the shallows with a staff; the next the air folded like wet paper, and hands—too many hands, all of them cold as riverbed stones—closed around his arms. He heard Tamsin shout. He heard Brannoc's whistle-birds scatter shrieking into the dark. Then a hood of woven ash came down over his face, and the world smelled of burnt letters and old smoke.

When the hood came off, he was standing in a hall made entirely of mirrors.

Not glass—Wren knew glass, had cut it and set it in widow's windows. These were mirrors the way a scar is skin: surfaces grown over wounds. They rose from floor to vaulted dark in crooked colonnades, thousands of them, each reflecting not the viewer but something *adjacent* to the viewer—a version slightly wrong, slightly hungry. In one, Wren saw himself older, hollow-cheeked, mouth moving around words no one bothered to hear. In another he saw himself as a child, reaching for a woman's hand that dissolved even as he grasped it.

Before him, on a dais of fused bone-white stone, sat the Ashen Court.

He had imagined monsters. What he saw instead was worse: people. Or the shapes of them. Seven figures robed in gray so fine it seemed spun from the memory of gray, their faces smooth as uncarved bone—not featureless, but *unfinished*, as if they had been interrupted mid-sculpture and never completed. Only their eyes were done, and their eyes were patient.

"You are smaller than the prophecy promised," said the one at the center. Its voice was the sound of a page turning.

"I get that a lot," Wren said. His voice shook. He let it shake. Fear that was allowed to exist was fear he could carry.

The Court did not laugh. He suspected they had forgotten how.

"We are not your enemy, carver." The speaker rose, robes whispering like ash sliding off a log. "We are the consequence of other people's enemies. Come. There is something you must see before you decide anything."

"I haven't agreed to decide anything."

"Everyone decides," the Court said gently. "Some simply do it slowly."

* * *

They walked between the mirror-columns, and Wren understood slowly what this place was. Not a throne room. A *larder*.

Each mirror held a name.

He felt them before he could articulate it—a pressure behind his sternum, the same pressure he'd felt his whole life when the dead pressed close, but inverted, refined, concentrated. Every surface hummed with a captured identity. He stopped before one mirror and saw reflected in it not a person but a single word, glowing faintly, coiled like a sleeping adder: *Merridale*. Below it, scratched by some desperate finger from the inside: *let me go home*.

"What are they?" he whispered.

"The severed," the Court said, without shame. "When Hollowmere's founders broke the covenant, souls could no longer pass whole across the veil. The names tore free—the oldest part, the truest part—and drifted. We gathered them. Someone had to. Otherwise they would have scattered into nothing, and nothing is a cruelty even we do not practice."

"And you feed on them."

"We *sustain* ourselves on them, as any creature sustains itself on its food. You do not apologize to the bread."

Wren's hands curled into fists. He thought of every whisper he'd ever heard in Hollowmere, every trapped echo scratching at the wards, and wondered how many of those voices had names hanging here in the dark, screaming silently into silvered glass.

The Court led him to the deepest part of the vault, where a single mirror stood apart from the rest, larger than a doorframe, framed in black iron carved with valley-oak leaves.

Hollowmere oak. He knew the grain of it. He'd carved it a hundred times without knowing why his hands loved it so.

"Look," the Court said.

Wren looked.

The mirror showed him a name. Not his face—his face he knew, plain and ink-stained and ordinary. This was beneath the face, or above it, or threaded through it: a word of light written in an alphabet he somehow recognized the way you recognize your mother's handwriting without ever studying it. It pulsed softly, like a heartbeat seen through skin.

And it was *him*. Not Wren Underhill, the carver who kept to himself, who mended fences and avoided the market on busy days. This was the self that had been taken out of him before he could walk—an identity stripped away as an infant, carried across the veil, and buried here in the Court's keeping, because the founders of Hollowmere had needed fuel for their walls.

"This is why the dead speak to you," the Court murmured. "You are a hole shaped like a soul. Things fall into you. That is not a gift, carver. It is a wound that never closed."

Wren touched the glass. Cold. But under the cold, faintly, the name leaned toward his fingers like a plant toward sun.

His eyes were burning. He hadn't noticed.

"Give us the covenant," the Court said, "and we give you back yourself. Whole. Complete. You would walk out of here knowing your own name, feeling your own edges, deaf at last to the dead who have haunted you since childhood. No more whispers. No more being a door others push through. Just a man, entire, alone in the best possible way."

The silence stretched.

And Wren, to his own horror, wanted it.

Gods, how he wanted it. Sixteen years of hearing grief that wasn't his, of carving charms through the night because some grandmother's echo wouldn't stop weeping in his rafters. Sixteen years of flinching at churchyards. He could be *quiet*, inside his own skull, quiet the way other people got to be quiet. He could sleep.

"You're lying," he said hoarsely. "About something. Maybe everything."

"The offer is sincere. Our hunger is real, but our arithmetic is honest. Without the covenant reforged, the realms bleed into each other and everything starves—including us. We would rather eat slowly forever than gorge once and end." The unfinished face tilted. "But know this, carver: if you reforge the covenant, that name dies. Truly dies. It cannot return to you and cannot pass on. It becomes mortar. You will spend the rest of your life wondering what it would have said."

"That's cruel."

"That is *price*. Cruelty would be pretending there isn't one."

Wren stood before the mirror a long time. In its depths, the stolen name pulsed, and pulsed, and he realized with a lurch that it wasn't calling to him.

It was waiting for him. Patiently. The way his grandmother used to wait at the window, trusting he'd come home when he was ready.

*Trust the hands,* she'd whispered on her deathbed. *Not the wanting.*

He stepped back.

"No," Wren said.

The word fell into the mirrored hall and echoed strangely, off surfaces that should not echo.

"The covenant gets reforged. And I'm sorry—" his voice cracked, and he let it, "—I'm so sorry it costs you. But you don't get to keep a larder of screaming people and call it mercy. Find another way to live. The dead deserve their names back."

For the first time, something moved across the Court's unfinished faces. Not anger. Something older and sadder.

"So the heir chooses the wound," the center figure said. "As the prophecy insisted you would. Very well."

The mirrors began to darken, one by one, like eyes closing.

"But choosing has consequences, little keeper. The vault knows you now. And the vault does not forget the taste of what it almost had."

* * *

The darkening mirrors were not a dismissal. They were a closing jaw.

Wren ran. Colonnades that had been straight now bent; the reflections showed him versions of himself being caught, being opened, being *eaten*, and the images bled at the edges like wet ink. Behind him came a sound like a library burning—pages, thousands of pages, all turning at once.

A side passage gaped open—too convenient, surely a trap—but a shape slipped out of it and grabbed his wrist, and the grip was small and fierce and smelled of hedge-herbs and woodsmoke.

"Tamsin."

"Shut up and run." Her focus-stone—the cracked one, the one he'd mended on the road with bone-dust and patience—blazed in her fist, light pouring through the healed fracture in a jagged white seam. "Your mending holds, by the way. Better than my original work ever did. Remember that when you're feeling sorry for yourself."

They ran. The corridor behind them filled with turning pages and unfinished faces, and ahead the passage narrowed to a crack of gray pre-dawn light, and Tamsin shoved him through it bodily, then followed, slamming her palm against the stone. The crack sealed with a sound like a held breath released.

They were outside. Actual outside—scree slope, thin wind, the corpse-road winding below toward a horizon going pink at the seams. Somewhere downslope, Brannoc's birds were circling, calling, calling, and Sable's tall silhouette stood against the brightening sky like a drawn blade.

Wren sat down hard on the scree and shook. Not from fear anymore. From the other thing.

"He showed me my name," he said. "My real one. The one they took." He laughed, and it came out broken. "It was right there. All I had to do was say yes."

Tamsin sat beside him, knees up, stone dimming in her lap.

"Do you regret saying no?"

"Yes," Wren said honestly. "Every hour for the rest of my life, probably. Some part of me is going to stand at that mirror forever, with my hand on the glass."

Tamsin nodded slowly, as if he'd passed a test she hadn't warned him about.

"Good," she said. "Regret means the price was real. People who feel no regret about their choices just haven't looked closely enough yet." She bumped her shoulder against his. "Sable needs to hear what the Court told you. All of it. Because when I went in after you, I saw the way their unfinished faces turned when they spoke of the Sigil's anchor stone—and Wren, they weren't threatening it."

She met his eyes, and hers were grim in the growing light.

"They were grieving it. Like people grieving a grave they dug themselves. Come on. There's something Sable's been hiding from us, and I think the Court just told us what it is."

Below them, Brannoc's birds had gone suddenly, unnaturally silent—all of them facing the same direction, down the corpse-road, toward the shattered heart of the Sigil.

Where something was already waiting.
