## 8. The Breaking of the Sigil

The Sigil announced itself before they saw it—a pressure behind the eyes, a taste like struck flint on the tongue, the sense of walking into a room where someone enormous had just stopped speaking.

Sable felt it first, as they had always felt sigil-work first, in the marrow. They raised a hand and the company halted on the corpse-road's last bend, where the pines thinned to wind-scoured scrub and the mountain opened like a wound.

"There," they said.

The Sigil's heart lay in a bowl of shattered stone a half-mile below, ringed by the broken ribs of an ancient amphitheater. Even from the ridge, even through the gray veil of failing wards, Sable could see what remained of it: a great circle of standing stones, most of them toppled, and at the center one anchor stone that should have blazed with the covenant's light like a hearth in a dark house.

It was dark. And it was wrong.

Tamsin came up beside them, her focus-stone—mended now, mended weeks ago by a carver's patient hands—glowing faintly against her throat. "It's still there," she murmured. "Underneath. Like a pulse under a bandage."

"Then the covenant isn't dead." Sable started down the scree. "Only silenced."

Wren fell into step at their shoulder, and Brannoc behind him, and Sable was aware—as they had been aware for weeks now, with an unease that had nothing to do with the Ashen Court—of how naturally the four of them moved as one body. How they had stopped being four travelers and become, without anyone deciding it, a company.

They had no right to that word. Not yet. Perhaps not at all.

* * *

The amphitheater floor was littered with a century of weather: lichen the color of old blood, drifts of ash that had blown in from the outer realms, the small bones of birds who had flown too close to the wards and fallen. Sable led them between the toppled pillars, reading the old sigil-cuts as they went—ward-marks, boundary-rites, the deep grammar of the covenant—and every step made their stomach colder.

Because they recognized the hand.

Not the individual masons. The *school*. The angled terminals on the ward-marks, the double-struck foundation lines, the way the binding glyphs were cut shallow and wide to drink light rather than reflect it. That was Guildwork. That was the Sigil-Smiths of Vael Corath, the order that had raised Sable from an apprentice with bleeding hands to a master with a name spoken in three realms.

That was Sable's own guild, and their work was all over this place like fingerprints on a knife.

They stopped walking. Wren noticed—of course he noticed; the man had eyes that caught everything and a carver's patience about what he did with what he saw—and stopped too.

"Sable?"

"Keep going," Sable said. "The anchor stone. I'll catch up."

"You've gone the color of tallow."

"*Keep going.*"

The words came out with an edge they hadn't intended, and they watched it land—watched Wren's face close a little, watched him exchange a glance with Tamsin, watched the company move on without them and the space where Sable had been part of it widen by a single, terrible degree.

They stood alone among the fallen pillars for a long moment. Then they followed, because there was nothing else left to do, and because the truth was waiting at the center of the circle whether they carried it there or not.

* * *

The anchor stone was the height of two men and shaped like a flame frozen mid-leap. Once it must have been beautiful; even defaced, even dark, it held a gravity that made the air lean toward it. The covenant's anchor—the hinge-point where the living world and the realm beyond had once been bound together, each promising the other passage, remembrance, rest.

Someone had cut it apart.

Sable circled it slowly, and the cold in their stomach became a thing with teeth. The defacement wasn't vandalism. It was *surgery*—precise, deliberate, masterful. The covenant's binding lines had been severed at their junctions and re-cut into a different pattern entirely: a pattern Sable knew intimately, because they had cut it themselves a hundred times in a hundred lesser works.

A severance pattern. A ward that didn't keep danger out, but kept souls *in*—or rather, kept them from ever passing on, from ever crossing, from ever completing themselves. The dead of Hollowmere, trapped in echo, their unfinished selves bleeding slowly into the Ashen Court's hungry hands.

The guild hadn't failed this place. The guild had *built* the cage. A hundred years ago, the Sigil-Smiths of Vael Corath had come to Hollowmere and broken a covenant older than their order, and carved the pieces into a prison, and called it protection, and been paid—Sable knew it the way you know a scar—for their silence in secrets and standing.

And when one of their own had found out—when a journeyman named Sable, twenty-six years old and arrogant as a blade, had uncovered the ledger of it in the guild archive and carried it to the magistrates—no one had listened. The guild had moved faster than truth always moves. Sable had been tried for theft of guild property and heresy against the craft, and stripped of their marks, and cast out with their tools broken and their name struck from the rolls.

Exile. They had called it punishment. Sable had spent thirty years learning to call it penance—walking the corpse-roads, hunting the prophecy, hunting the heir, telling themselves that if they could help reforge what their guild had broken, the ledger would balance.

Now they stood at the broken thing itself, and their guild's chisel-marks were under their fingers, and the company was coming back around the anchor stone with questions in their faces, and Sable understood that the ledger did not balance. Ledgers never did. There was only the debt, and the debtor, and the moment when you finally said the number aloud.

Wren was standing at the stone's base, his palm flat against the defaced surface, his eyes closed. "There are names in here," he said quietly. "Hundreds of them. Under the cutting. They're still— they're not gone, Sable. They're *pressed*. Like flowers in a book." He opened his eyes. "Who did this? Who could cut like this and leave the souls still alive underneath?"

Sable looked at the mark at the stone's base—the guild-sigil, half-effaced but legible to anyone trained to read it, which meant legible to everyone in this company except the ones who mattered most.

Tamsin saw where they were looking. Her hand went to her mouth.

"Sable," she said. "Whose mark is that?"

* * *

They told it standing, because sitting would have been surrender, and they told it plainly, because ornament would have been worse than cowardice. The guild. The commission. The severance pattern. The trial, the stripping, the exile. The thirty years. The prophecy. The heir.

"You *knew*," Tamsin said, when it was done. Her voice was very quiet, which was how Sable knew it was very bad. "The whole road, you knew what we were walking toward, and you knew your people made it, and you said nothing."

"I was going to tell you—"

"When? At the stone? After? On the road home, if there was a road home?" She was shaking. The focus-stone at her throat had gone dark, and Sable realized she had closed her hand around it without knowing, the way a person guards a wound. "My sister's soul went into a Court's keeping when the wards near our village failed, do you remember? Do you remember what you told me? *The Sigil can be reforged, Tam. The covenant can be mended.* You let me walk beside you for six weeks on the strength of that, and the whole time the hands that broke it were—were *yours*. Were your teachers. Were your—"

"Tam." Wren's voice, low.

"No." She turned on him, and then turned back, and Sable stood and took it, because taking it was the only thing left that they were qualified to do. "You should have told us at the ridge. You should have told us the day we met. You let Wren bargain his life for your passage and never said—"

"Tam." Wren again, sharper now. He had not moved from the anchor stone. "Look at the cutting again."

She didn't. Brannoc did—the mute ferryman, who had not spoken a word since the waystation, who communicated now in gestures and carved whistles and the small eloquent language of his hands—stepped past her and laid his palm beside Wren's on the stone, and then looked at Sable with an expression Sable had spent a lifetime learning to dread: the look of a man who has read a thing and is waiting to see if you'll confess it before he names it.

"The terminals," Wren said. "The double-struck lines. The way the binding glyphs are cut to drink light." He recited it back to Sable like an indictment, because it was one. "I've been reading your mending-work for six weeks. Your hands are all over the things you've repaired. And they're all over this." His voice didn't rise. That was the worst of it. It stayed level, and gentle, and it cut deeper than Tamsin's fury ever could. "You didn't just know, Sable. You *recognized* it. The moment we crested the ridge. Maybe before."

The wind moved through the amphitheater, and the ash stirred, and somewhere far above them the failing wards made a sound like a held breath.

"Yes," Sable said.

"Why didn't you tell us?"

"Because I was afraid that if I said it aloud, you would leave. And I—" The truth came up like a splinter, all at once, taking skin with it. "I have been alone for thirty years. I told myself it was penance. It was easier than admitting it was just exile. And then there was a hedge-witch who argued with me about the right way to mend a focus-stone, and a ferryman who gave me his ration when mine ran out, and a bone-carver who mended things no one asked him to mend, and I thought— I thought, *if I can just get us to the stone, if I can just balance the ledger, then I'll have earned them. Then I'll be allowed to keep them.*" They laughed, and it came out broken. "As if belonging were a debt you could pay. As if any of you were *payment*."

Silence. Tamsin's hand was still closed around her stone. Brannoc had not moved. Wren was looking at Sable with an expression they couldn't read, and Sable had read a thousand faces across a thousand corpse-roads, and this one defeated them.

Then Wren turned back to the anchor stone and put his hand on it again.

"The souls are still here," he said. "Pressed, not dead. Whatever your guild did, they couldn't kill a covenant this old—they could only bury it. Which means it can be dug out." He looked up. "And you're the only one alive who can read the cutting well enough to undo it."

"Sable," Tamsin said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Don't. Don't ask us to trust you again. Not after—"

"I'm not asking." Sable knelt at the stone's base, in the ash, in front of all of them, and set their bare hands flat against the guild-mark that had cost them everything twice over. "I'm telling you what's true. I broke your trust, and I can't mend that, and I know better than anyone that some cuttings can't be undone—only outworked. So here is my outworking: I will cut this stone apart down to the covenant's living lines, and I will do it with you watching every stroke, and if I cut one line wrong—one—you walk away, and I won't follow."

"And if the Court comes while you're working?" Tamsin asked.

Sable looked up at the ridgeline, at the darkening sky, at the first threads of ash-gray light beginning to gather at the amphitheater's rim like smoke deciding to become a shape.

"Then you'll know soon enough what I'm worth," they said.

* * *

They worked as the light died.

Sable's chisels were broken ones—thirty years broken, mended now with Wren's bone-pins and Tamsin's binding-salve—and they sang against the anchor stone in the gathering dark, each stroke a small argument with a dead master's work. Wren stood at their shoulder translating the pressed names, calling the living lines out of the buried covenant the way a man calls a drowning swimmer toward shore. Tamsin held light in her mended focus-stone and did not speak to Sable, and did not leave, and Sable understood that the not-leaving was its own sentence, still being written. Brannoc stood at the amphitheater's rim with his carved whistle at his lips, and the birds that answered him had begun to go silent, one by one, from the east.

The east. Where the Court would come from. Where the ash had always come from.

"Sable." Wren's voice, tight. "The last junction. When this line comes free, everything buried here wakes up at once. Every soul. All of them, all at once, and they'll be *hungry* for a hundred years of missed crossing, and the anchor will be open, and—"

"And the Court will feel it from a hundred miles," Sable finished. "I know."

"Then say it plainly. What happens when I cut this last line?"

Sable set the chisel against the final junction, and looked at the three people who had every right to abandon them on this mountain, and who had not.

"What happens," they said, "is that the covenant reopens, and the dead begin to cross, and the Ashen Court comes to stop them—and the only thing standing between a hundred trapped souls and the things that feed on them will be a disgraced smith, a hedge-witch, a mute ferryman, and a bone-carver who was never supposed to exist."

Above the eastern ridge, the ash-light gathered, and took a shape, and began to descend.

"Cut it," Tamsin said.

Sable struck the line.
