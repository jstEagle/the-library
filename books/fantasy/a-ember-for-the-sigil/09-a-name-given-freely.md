## 9. A Name Given Freely

The Sigil's heart was a wound that had learned to sing.

Wren felt it before he saw it—a low, broken harmonic thrumming up through the soles of his boots as the company picked their way down the shattered stair. The anchor stone rose from the center of the ruin like a black tooth: a monolith the height of three men, its surface carved over with a century of covenant-script, and across all of it, one long gouge where someone had dragged a blade of guild-steel and unmade the words.

Sable stood at its base with her head bowed, ash from her ruined guild-sigils still dusting her knuckles. She had not spoken since her confession. She did not need to. The stone said it for her.

Around them, the air was coming apart.

Not violently—worse, gently. Threads of the veil hung loose like torn gauze, and through them Wren could see the far country: meadows the color of remembered summers, roads that walked themselves, shapes moving with the unhurried patience of the dead. And pressing against those threads from the other side, hungry and patient, came the Ashen Court—gray figures in gray robes, faceless until they wanted faces, wearing borrowed expressions like coats taken off hooks.

"They're waiting," Tamsin murmured. Her focus-stone—the one Wren had mended on the road—glowed fitfully at her throat. "They want you to do it badly, Wren. They want a botched binding. A covenant reforged in fear makes a better harvest than no covenant at all."

"Then I won't be afraid," Wren said, and was surprised to find it mostly true.

He climbed the broken dais alone. The anchor stone hummed under his palm, cold as a grave-marker in winter, familiar as the bones he'd carved all his life. He understood now what it asked. Brannoc had shown him, there at the waystation, pressing Wren's hand flat against an old ferry-oath scratched into wood: *a name given freely, held by stone, witnessed by both realms.* The covenant had been forged the same way a hundred years ago—and broken when the guild decided that taking was faster than asking. They had taken a name from an infant instead. They had built Hollowmere's walls out of a stolen self.

And here was the mirror-vault's truth, still lodged under Wren's ribs like a splinter of ice: somewhere in the Court's keeping lay his birth-name. The name his mother surrendered to hide him. The name he had never once spoken because he had never once known it. He could feel its absence the way you feel a missing tooth—with the tongue always returning, always probing the gap.

*You could have it back,* the Court had whispered through the vault-glass. *Say the word. Walk away. Be whole.*

Brannoc touched his elbow now. The big ferryman could not speak, but he did not need to; his hands said everything his throat could not. *Which name?* those hands asked. *The one they kept, or the one you made?*

Wren looked at his own palms—carver's palms, scarred and nicked, smelling faintly of bone-dust and linseed oil. Hands that had made two hundred memorial charms for two hundred grieving neighbors. Hands that had mended a witch's focus-stone by firelight and whittled a whistle so a mute man could speak in birdsong.

"I know which one is mine," he said.

He drew his carving knife.

The Court moved then—all along the rim of the ruin, gray shapes descending the stair with the smooth wrongness of smoke pouring downhill. Sable planted herself at the dais's foot, sigil-blades drawn, burning with the last light of her disgraced craft. Tamsin's voice rose in a hedge-witch's warding, ragged and fierce. Brannoc simply stood in their path, vast and silent, the way a riverbank stands against a flood.

And Wren pressed the knife's point to the back of his own hand.

"This is how my grandmother taught me," he said aloud, to the stone, to the dead, to whatever listened. "You don't take a mark. You give one."

He carved—not deep, not cruel, just true—a single line into his skin, and spoke as the blood welled:

"My name is Wren Underhill. Son of a woman who loved me enough to go nameless. Grandson of a woman who riddled me toward this day. Carver of charms in Hollowmere. Keeper of the door between. Friend to a sigil-smith who fell, a witch who mends, and a ferryman who sings in sparrows." His voice cracked; he let it. "I give this name freely. Not the one they stole—I never owned that. This one. The one I built. Take it into the stone, and let the covenant stand on what I chose, not what was taken."

He laid his bleeding hand against the anchor.

For one heartbeat, nothing.

Then the stone drank.

Light ran up the gouged channel like water finding an old bed, filling the defacement, tracing every broken letter, and the covenant-script blazed white down the monolith's face. The harmonic that had been broken for a century snapped whole—one clear note that passed through flesh and stone and veil alike, and Wren heard it in his teeth, in his scars, in the hollow place where his stolen name used to ache.

The dead rose to witness.

They came up through the torn veil in their hundreds—not the trapped echoes of Hollowmere, severed and starving, but the true dead of the far country, called by the note, arriving like guests to a wedding. Meadows emptied. Roads that walked themselves walked here instead. They ringed the ruin in ranks of soft light, and among them Wren saw faces he knew: the widow's husband, nodding to him kindly; old Tam Fletcher, whose charm Wren had carved at fourteen; and at the very front, small and bright-eyed, the child who had crossed the failing wards and called him keeper.

The Ashen Court stopped.

They stood at the edge of the witnessing host, gray robes stirring in a wind that did not blow, and Wren watched them understand. Their whole existence was appetite for the severed—for souls cut off from both realms, drifting, harvestable. But the covenant was whole again now. Nothing was severed. Nothing would be severed. There was nothing left to eat.

One of them reached toward the anchor stone anyway—reached for the last severed thing in the valley, the stolen infant-name still locked in the Court's keeping—and the stone answered. Light took the reaching hand like dawn takes frost. The figure unraveled, quietly, almost gratefully, into ash that the wind carried away. Then another. Then another. Not slaughtered—starved. Unraveled for lack of anything left to hunger for. The Court thinned like fog at morning, and the last of them, the one who had worn a neighbor's face at Wren's door, paused before the dais.

It wore his mother's face then.

It said, in a voice like paper settling, "She gave hers up willingly. Willingly is stronger than stolen. We should have remembered." And it came apart, gently, into motes that drifted upward through the veil instead of down.

Silence, then—but a living silence, full of birdsong and breath and the far-off murmur of the dead conversing among themselves, unhurried, unafraid, no longer prisoners or prey. Just neighbors, on the other side of a door that was now a window.

Sable lowered her blades. Tamsin sat down abruptly on a fallen pillar and laughed until she cried. Brannoc lifted the bone whistle to his lips, and a sparrow landed on his shoulder and sang something that needed no translation.

Wren stayed with his hand on the stone a moment longer. The name he had given lived in it now—he could feel it, held safe, held *given*, and the strange thing was that he did not feel diminished. He felt, for the first time in his life, entirely present. A man does not lose what he has chosen to hand over. He becomes the handing.

The child-dead tugged his sleeve. Behind her, stretching away down the ruined stair and out into the world, the dead were already turning toward the valleys and cities of the living, drawn by the new-opened way—and among them, Wren glimpsed a woman with his own eyes, standing at the veil's edge as if she had been waiting a hundred years for the door to open.

She smiled, and stepped through.

* * *

They buried no one and mourned no one that night. They built a fire in the Sigil's heart, in a ruin that was becoming, minute by minute, something more like a hearth. Tamsin brewed hedge-tea. Sable traced new sigils in the ash—her own designs, unblessed by any guild, and they held. Brannoc's sparrow sang three songs about soup.

Wren turned his carving knife over in his fingers, watching the firelight run along the blade, and found he was already thinking about the next charm he would make. There would be so many now. Doors opened both ways, and both sides would need gifts.

Far off, beyond the firelight, Hollowmere lay quiet under wards that were becoming windows—and somewhere in the chapel archive, a prophecy book waited on its shelf with its final page still blank, its ink drying mid-sentence, as if even prophecy had finally understood that some things are not foretold.

Some things are built.
