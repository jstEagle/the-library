## 10. Hollowmere, Unhidden

The first frost of the season came on a morning when the dead were already awake.

Marta Underhill—no relation to Wren, though she'd have argued the point by now—stood at her kitchen window and watched old Tam Vess's ghost lean over his own garden fence, inspecting the turnips he'd planted sixty years ago in a body that had been ash since before she was born. He looked up, caught her watching, and tipped his hat the way he always had. Marta raised her teacup in answer.

A year ago she would have screamed. A year ago Hollowmere didn't let you see them at all—the wards had ground every whisper of the beyond into silence, and the valley had slept inside that silence like a stone at the bottom of a well. Now the wards were windows. You could look through them either way. Some mornings, if the light came slantwise off the eastern ridge, you could even see the other realm shimmering behind the mist like a second valley laid over the first, its hills greener, its rivers running silver with names.

Tam drifted along the fence line, checking turnips that existed only as memory, and hummed a harvest song that three generations of Hollowmere children had learned without knowing where it came from.

Marta drank her tea and thought: *this is what peace sounds like.* Not silence. Company.

* * *

Wren Underhill's workshop smelled of bone dust, beeswax, and woodsmoke, and on this particular morning it also smelled of porridge, because four children had arrived before dawn and one of them had brought breakfast as tribute.

"Sit," Wren said, not looking up from the charm blank in his hands. "Sit *down*, Pip."

Pip Osgrey sat down. She was nine years old, ghost-touched since birth—the first child conceived in the valley after the reforging, which meant the dead could see her clearly, and she could see them right back, and nobody had yet decided whether this was a blessing or a very great deal of trouble. She set a clay pot of porridge on the workbench between two half-finished memorial charms, daring him with her eyes to object.

"You'll get grease on the bone," Wren said, but he was already reaching for a bowl.

The other three children arranged themselves on the long bench: Ferren, who carved like he was fighting the material and lost every time; little Sela, whose brother had crossed the veil last spring and who came every week now to make things she could give him through the window; and Doss, the ferryman's grandson, who said almost nothing but watched Wren's hands the way Brannoc once had—like language lived there instead of in the throat.

"Today we do hinges," Wren told them. "A charm is only as honest as its hinge. Anyone can carve a pretty face into bone. The question is whether it opens."

He passed out the blanks—sheep bone, cured soft—and moved down the bench correcting grips, turning blades, steadying elbows. Outside the window, the mist off the ridge was doing its slow silver shimmer, and Sela pressed her palm flat against the glass for a moment, toward something only she could see, before picking up her knife again.

This was the thing no prophecy had ever said, Wren thought, watching them work. That the gift would come to feel less like hearing ghosts and more like teaching children to hold a knife properly. Less like a wound and more like a trade. He had spent twenty years carving charms so the living could say goodbye. Now he taught the living to say *hello*, too, and some mornings the difference between those two words felt like the whole width of the world he'd crossed to learn it.

Pip's hinge snapped on her third attempt. She swore—an impressive vocabulary for a nine-year-old, harvested wholesale from the tavern ghosts—and started over without being told.

Wren smiled and went back to his own blade.

* * *

Half a mile down the lane, where the corpse-road met the old mill track, Sable Ashvane stood in the skeleton of a house they were building with their own hands and discovered that the roof beam was a finger-width short.

"It's fine," Tamsin called from atop the ladder, hair full of straw, hedge-witch's robes traded months ago for a carpenter's apron that suited her disgracefully well. "We'll shim it."

"We will not *shim* it. This is a load-bearing—" Sable stopped. Closed their eyes. Breathed the breath of a person choosing patience over perfection for the ten thousandth time. "Shim it."

Below them both, Brannoc whistled.

It was a thrush's phrase, three notes falling like water off a ledge, and it came from the man's own lips these days—not from the carved whistle, though he still wore it on its cord, worn smooth as sea glass. The whistle had been the door; what waited behind it had been Brannoc all along, patient as bone. His voice had returned the way spring returned to the valley: unevenly, in patches, a word here and a phrase there, until one evening in midsummer he'd sung an entire verse of the harvest song and then looked profoundly embarrassed about it. The whole of Hollowmere had pretended not to notice. It was, everyone agreed afterward, the kindest thing the village had ever done.

*Roof,* he whistled now. *Rain coming.*

Tamsin squinted west. "He's right. Clouds over the Sigil ridge." She climbed down, wiping her hands. "Do you ever get used to that? Looking up there and feeling it hum?"

Sable followed her gaze. On the eastern heights, the shattered heart of the Sigil caught the grey morning light, and beneath it—if you knew how to listen, and Sable was learning—you could feel the covenant turning over like a sleeper: vast, slow, alive. The anchor stone Wren had given himself to. The name *Wren Underhill* still sat in it, bright as a coal, feeding the wards the way the stolen infant-name once had—but fed freely now, renewed each season by choice, and everything built on choice held differently than anything built on theft.

"No," Sable admitted. "You don't get used to it. You just stop flinching."

Their guild sigil-ring was gone—melted down in spring, the metal folded into the hinge of the chapel door, so that everyone who entered pushed against a piece of their penance and never knew it. Their exile papers were gone too, burned the night Tamsin declared the guild's judgment void by authority of "one witch, one ferryman, and one extremely stubborn carver." What remained was sawdust, splinters, a house going up crooked but sound, and neighbors who waved.

Sable picked up the shim Tamsin had already cut and hammered it home.

From the ridge road, a cart creaked—new arrivals, the third family this month, displaced folk from the outer realm following rumors of a valley where magic didn't bite and the dead paid social calls. Sable watched the cart come down through the birches and felt the old reflex rise, the guild-trained instinct to assess strangers as threats, and set it aside like a tool no longer needed.

"Hollowmere," they said to the cart as it passed, and pointed them toward the inn, and the widow Marta, and the spare rooms that were somehow always ready.

* * *

The prophecy book lay open on the chapel archive table, because Wren had taken to leaving it there—face-up, unguarded, available to anyone who wanted to read what could no longer hurt anyone.

It ended mid-sentence.

That was how it had been since the night of the reforging. The final page carried the last lines of the old prophecy—*and the heir shall choose, and the choice shall be the binding, and the binding shall be*—and then nothing. Not torn away, not inked out. Simply unfinished, the vellum blank past that point, as though the book itself had understood that a covenant remade by free will could not be described in advance. Every scribe who had examined it since—two from the outer realm, one very old ghost who'd been a librarian in life—had confirmed the same strange truth: the pages would take new ink now. They were waiting.

Children dared each other to write in it. So far none had. It seemed to everyone, without discussion, like something that ought to be written by someone who'd earned a sentence worth keeping.

Wren visited it most evenings. Tonight he brought a lamp and sat across from it the way you'd sit across from an old adversary who had become something more complicated—an accuser, maybe, or a mirror. Beside tonight's date, a century-old hand had inked a sketch of his face and the words *the keeper comes*. He touched the edge of the page without reading further. He didn't need to anymore. The book had been right about everything except the ending, because the ending hadn't belonged to it.

Behind him, the archive door opened on cold air and candlelight.

"You're late," Wren said, without turning. "Dinner's cold. Marta will be insufferable about it."

"I brought bread," Tamsin said, "which cancels the lateness. Brannoc brought cider, which cancels the bread being slightly burnt. And Sable brought—" she stepped aside, revealing Sable holding a small wrapped bundle with the expression of someone delivering a summons, "—Sable brought news."

Sable set the bundle on the table beside the open book and unwrapped it. Inside lay a letter, sealed with grey wax bearing a sigil Wren recognized and wished he didn't: the mark of Sable's former guild, the sigil-smiths of the outer realm.

"They've read the reports," Sable said. "The reforging. The windows. The haven you're building here. They're requesting—" a pause, and something old and bitter moved behind their eyes before honesty won, as it had learned to do, "—*asking*. Formally. For permission to send observers. To study what we did."

"What we did," Wren repeated.

"What *you* did. They want to know if it can be done elsewhere. Other valleys. Other severed places." Sable looked at the letter a long moment. "There are more Hollowmeres out there, Wren. Places walled off by people who were afraid, or hungry, or both. If the covenant can be rebuilt—"

"Then someone has to go show them how."

"Someone," Sable agreed quietly, "who knows both sides of the wall."

The lamp flickered. Out in the dark, somewhere between the chapel and the ridge, a thrush sang three falling notes in a voice far too human, and Wren heard beneath it the faint, patient chorus of the valley's dead—not trapped now, not echoing, simply *present*, the way neighbors are present, keeping their lamps lit on the far side of the window until the rest of the world learned what Hollowmere had finally learned:

That belonging was never found. It was built. Beam by beam, charm by charm, name by freely given name.

He picked up the letter and broke the seal.

"Well," he said, and outside the archive window the wards shivered silver, listening. "I suppose we should start with the roof."
