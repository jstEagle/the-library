## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The stew wanted killing.

Edmund Marchetti knew this the way other men knew rain was coming—in the teeth, in the back of the tongue, in a small cold bell that rang somewhere behind his eyes. He had been standing at his station beside the king's high table for eleven minutes, hands folded, face arranged into the pleasant blankness of furniture, when the bell rang.

Henbane. Not much of it. A whisper of it, folded into the rosemary like a secret told to someone who wasn't listening.

He did not sigh. Sighing drew attention, and attention, in the palace of King Aldric the Second—Aldric the Usurper, if you asked anyone who dared ask anything—was a thing that got people opened up and examined and occasionally buried. Instead Edmund did what he always did with bad news: he swallowed it first.

The serving girl came down the line of dishes with the king's portion, and Edmund stepped into her path with the smooth, apologetic shuffle of a man who has spent nine years perfecting the art of being slightly in the way.

"Master Taster," she said, in the tone of someone addressing a doorstop that had learned manners.

"Just checking the rim," he murmured, and lifted the bowl's edge to his nose as though this were ritual rather than rescue. Beneath the rosemary and the mutton fat and the honest brown smell of a kitchen doing its best, there it was again—that faint green bitterness, sweetish at the back, like a lie told by a flower. Henbane didn't announce itself. That was its charm. It arrived politely, sat down quietly, and stopped your heart somewhere between the second course and the toasts.

Edmund took the ladle, dipped it, and drank.

* * *

Here is what nobody at court understood about food-tasting, and what Edmund had long ago stopped trying to explain: it was not bravery. Bravery implied a choice made fresh each time, a white-knuckled moment at the lip of the spoon. What Edmund had was something more like arithmetic. He knew forty-one poisons by taste, nine by smell alone, and three—he was proud of these—by the way they changed the sound of a simmering pot. He knew which ones killed fast and which killed slow and which merely ruined your fortnight while you wished, sincerely and at length, for death instead.

And he knew, above all, how much of each one a body could be given before the body filed its complaint.

The dose in the king's stew would have been enough to make Aldric clutch his chest during the blessing and die with everyone watching, which was, presumably, the point. The dose Edmund took was enough to make him sweat through his collar by the third course and spend the evening feeling as though his heart were a bird trying to escape through his ribs—but not enough to kill him. He had spent years calibrating himself against the common poisons the way a sailor calibrates against weather. It was not immunity. There was no such thing, whatever the gossip in the servants' hall said. It was tolerance, built drop by drop over nine years, and it meant that when the palace's enemies reached for their little vials, Edmund could drink their work down and keep standing.

His only value, as he had once put it to himself on a bad night, was that he could die well. Slowly, expensively, and on schedule.

The trick now—the part that required actual craft—was making sure no one noticed he'd just done it.

He set the ladle down, nodded at the serving girl with grave satisfaction, and announced, in the flat voice he used for such announcements: "The stew is fit."

Which it was. For the king. That was the whole joke of his profession, the one he kept locked in his chest where it rattled around at night: *the stew is fit* meant *the stew will not kill you today*, and never meant *the stew is good*. Nobody thanked the taster. Thanking the taster would mean admitting there had been something to thank him for, and the palace did not admit to poisonings. Poisonings were bad for trade, bad for morale, and very bad for whoever was supposed to have prevented them. So the pattern, established over nine years and some forty-odd interventions, was always the same:

Edmund tasted. Edmund lived. The cook was quietly dismissed—or worse, depending on the season and the king's mood—and a new cook arrived within the week, and within the month someone tried again, because a usurper who had taken a throne over the bodies of the old royal family tended to collect enemies the way a gutter collected leaves.

Tonight's cook was a round, anxious woman named Besset who had been in the kitchens exactly nineteen days and who was, Edmund was nearly certain, entirely innocent. Which meant the henbane had come from someone else along the way, which meant someone inside the palace, which meant tonight was going to be an unpleasant night for somebody, and Edmund intended with his whole heart for that somebody not to be him.

* * *

King Aldric ate his stew.

This was, in fairness, the entire job description, but Edmund still felt the small private click of a task completed—a coin dropped into a jar he kept somewhere behind his sternum. Forty-two. He watched the king eat with the detached attention of a man watching weather. Aldric was fifty-ish and going soft, with the flushed, overfed look of a man who had won everything he wanted and discovered that wanting had been the interesting part. He laughed too loudly at his own jokes. His crown sat on him like a hat borrowed from a larger man.

Around the high table, the court performed loyalty. Edmund had a professional appreciation for the performance. He knew every variation: the chancellor whose smiles came a half-second late, the general who drank only from cups he'd poured himself, the young countess who watched the king the way a cat watches a bird it has decided not to pounce on yet. Nine years of tasting had taught Edmund that everyone at court was swallowing something.

He stood through the courses with his hands folded, sweating gently, heart hammering its henbane rhythm, face blank as fresh paper. By the fish course the worst had passed. By the sweets he felt almost human, in the way of a man who has recently been extremely not.

It was Master Corvel, the taster-master, who found him afterward in the corridor outside the banquet hall, holding a candle and wearing the expression of a man about to deliver either a compliment or a summons.

"You looked pale tonight," Corvel said.

"I'm always pale," Edmund said. "I'm a taster. We're bred in cellars."

Corvel did not smile. Corvel had been taster-master for twenty years and had survived them, which meant he had no sense of humor left whatsoever—it had been tasted out of him, drop by drop, like everything else. "Forty-two," he said instead, quietly.

Edmund went carefully still. "Sir?"

"The stew." Corvel's eyes, grey and patient as slate, held his. "You said 'fit' on the nose. You always say 'fit' on the nose. But tonight you checked the rim first, and you stepped into Hessa's path before she reached the table, and you've been sweating since the second course. I counted." He raised the candle slightly, illuminating nothing so much as his own calm. "Forty-one was the salted almonds at midwinter. Forty-two is tonight. You keep better books than you think I keep, boy."

There was a long moment in which Edmund considered several replies and discarded all of them. Lying to Corvel was possible—Edmund lied beautifully, it was practically his second profession—but lying to Corvel about *this*, to a man who had probably drunk worse things than henbane in his day and walked away whistling, felt like cheating at cards with someone who owned the deck.

"It was mild," Edmund said finally. "Well-dosed. Someone with a careful hand."

"Mm." Corvel turned the candle slowly. Somewhere down the corridor, a door closed; both men ignored it with the practiced deafness of the palace's permanent staff. "Besset will be gone by morning."

"She's innocent."

"Innocent cooks are still cooks who served poisoned stew." Corvel said it without malice, the way one notes that wet wood doesn't burn. "That's not cruelty, Marchetti. That's arithmetic. Yours, actually—you taught the palace that rule. Every time you catch something and say nothing louder than 'fit,' you save the king and cost a stranger. Have you ever added up the column?"

Edmund had. That was the trouble. He lay awake some nights doing precisely that arithmetic, in the dark of his garret under the eaves, adding up cooks and cupbearers and a carver who'd been sent to the border garrisons and never wrote again. The column had a great many entries, and none of them were his name, and he had never decided whether that made him the palace's best servant or its most successful coward.

"I do my work," he said.

"You do," Corvel agreed. And then, in a different voice—lighter, almost gentle, which from Corvel was more alarming than suspicion—"You know what I was doing at your age? I was drinking for old King Vessany's grandson. Last of that line. Sweet boy. Dead within the year, along with everyone who remembered his name." He smiled without warmth. "Names are dangerous things in this country, Marchetti. Be glad yours is forgettable."

He handed Edmund the candle, which was somehow both a kindness and a dismissal, and walked away, his footsteps even and unhurried, a man who had made his peace with every column in his own ledger.

Edmund stood in the corridor a while, sweating out the last of the henbane, thinking about names.

He had one, technically. Edmund Marchetti. But it was a thin name, a name like a rented room—no family crest, no village, no grandmother's recipes, no stories. The guild records said he'd come from the coast. The coast was large. When colleagues asked—rarely; tasters were not a sociable guild—he said his parents had been merchants, dead of fever, nothing worth remembering. People's eyes always slid off the answer, the way eyes slid off Edmund generally. He had cultivated that slide the way other men cultivated mustaches. Average height, average brown hair, a face that mirrors forgot. In nine years he had learned that invisibility was not the absence of being seen. It was a skill, practiced daily, and he was very, very good at it.

So why did Corvel's parting words sit in his chest like a swallowed stone?

*Be glad yours is forgettable.*

He was glad. Of course he was glad. Gladness was the sensible response. He climbed the four flights to his garret, past the sleeping household, past the scullery boy who snored in a nest of onion sacks, and let himself into his narrow room with its narrow bed and its single window that looked out on nothing but roof slates and a slice of grey sky.

He should sleep. Tomorrow there would be a new cook to break in, and the usual quiet investigation into the henbane—which he would stay carefully clear of, because investigations, unlike stews, could not be tasted safely—and the ordinary business of remaining alive and unremarkable.

Instead he sat on the edge of the bed and did the thing he did perhaps twice a year, the thing that was, in its way, more dangerous than any poison: he wondered.

Who had dosed the stew? Someone with access between kitchen and table. Someone with a careful hand and a grudge. The list was short and well-dressed, and every name on it frightened him in a distant, theoretical way, the way wolves frighten you from a picture book.

And underneath that question, older and quieter, the one that never quite went away: *who had I been, before I was this?* Because everyone was someone before. Even the most forgettable man in the palace had been born somewhere, to someone, with a name that somebody had once spoken aloud in love rather than in record-keeping. His memory of before was a hallway with all the doors closed. He had stopped knocking years ago. Knocking hurt.

Outside, the city murmured its night noises. Somewhere below, guards were escorting poor innocent Besset toward whatever happened to dismissed cooks on unlucky nights. Somewhere across the roofs, the king slept in the bed Edmund's tongue had cleared for him, ungrateful and unaware, and that was fine—that was the arrangement, that had always been the arrangement. He saved lives the way a wall keeps out weather: silently, structurally, without thanks.

Edmund blew out his candle and lay down.

He was nearly asleep—heart finally steady, the henbane's last green ghost fading from his tongue—when he heard it. Footsteps in the yard below, wrong for the hour: quick, uneven, dragging. Then a thud against the outer wall of the kitchens, down by the old smuggling door that officially did not exist and unofficially never locked.

Then silence, of the particular quality that makes a man who tastes poison for a living sit bolt upright in the dark, because he knows silence when he hears it, and this silence was bleeding.

* * *

Edmund told himself he was going down to check on the wine deliveries. He told himself this with the fluency of long practice, because lying to himself was the one lie he'd never managed to make convincing, and he descended the stairs anyway, barefoot, taking the last flight by memory.

The smuggling door stood ajar. Cold air moved through it, smelling of river mud and, faintly, of blood.

A man lay crumpled against the doorframe—a courier by the dress, road-boots and a riding cloak gone dark with wet. Young, maybe younger than Edmund. His breath came in shallow pulls, and there was an arrow shaft broken off high in his shoulder, snapped short by a fall or a crawl. His eyes found Edmund in the doorway and fixed on him with the terrible urgency of a man spending his last coins.

"They're coming," the courier whispered. "Behind me. Grey cloaks. Listen—" A hand, slick and shaking, closed around Edmund's wrist and pressed something into it: a scroll, small, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with dark wax stamped with a sigil Edmund did not recognize and yet, absurdly, felt he ought to. "Take it. Not to the king. Never the king. Find—" The man coughed, wetly. "Find the heir. The map remembers. The seal knows its blood. Tell them Vessany—"

Torches, far off in the yard. Voices. The scrape of boots on gravel.

Grey cloaks.

Every instinct Edmund had honed over nine years said *leave him, leave it, walk away, be furniture.* Furniture survived. Furniture did not get dragged into courtyards and questioned by men whose questions were famous in the way plagues are famous.

But the courier's hand was still on his wrist, gripping with the last of everything, and his eyes were not afraid of dying—they were afraid of *failing*—and Edmund, who had spent his whole adult life being chosen by no one for nothing, heard himself say, "Give me your arm," and hauled the stranger into the dark of the buttery, pulling the door shut behind them as the torchlight swung into the yard.

In the blackness, the courier breathed one word against Edmund's shoulder, soft as ash settling:

"Vessany."

And the wax-sealed scroll in Edmund's hand grew, impossibly, warm.
